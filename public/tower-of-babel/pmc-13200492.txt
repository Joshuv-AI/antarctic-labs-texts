Combined mode-kinetic chain exercise with and without core stability exercises on patients with knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Maryam Maqsood, Jannat Sohail, Syeda Aiman Batool
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong adult population, the osteoarthritis (OA) of knee is the major issue that can lead to pain, functional limitations, and reduced quality of life. Exercise therapy is a core component of management, yet the added value of core stability training in conjunction with kinetic chain exercises remains underexplored.ObjectiveTo determine the effects of combined mode kinetic chain exercise with and without core stability training on pain, range of motion and quality of life in patients with knee OA.MethodsA total of 48 patients with osteoarthritis in their knees were the subjects of a randomized controlled experiment at PSRD Hospital in Lahore were randomly allocated to either Group B (kinetic chain exercise only) or Group A (kinetic chain exercise with core stability training) with 24 participants each. For 12 weeks, interventions were carried out three times a week. Quality of life (KOOS), knee range of motion (goniometry), and pain (NPRS) were evaluated at baseline, six weeks, and twelve weeks.ResultsGroup A and B both groups showed significant improvements in NPRS, ROM, and KOOS scores over time (p < 0.05). At 12 weeks, Group A demonstrated a greater reduction in pain compared to Group B (p = 0.002). Flexion ROM improved by 19.6° ± 3.2° in Group A versus 12.4° ± 2.9° in Group B (p < 0.001). KOOS total score increased by 34.8 ± 5.6 in Group A and 25.7 ± 5.1 in Group B (p < 0.001).ConclusionIncorporating core stability exercises into a kinetic chain exercise program yields superior improvements in pain reduction, knee range of motion and quality of life in knee osteoarthritis patients compared to kinetic chain exercises alone.Trial registrationThe study was registered at Clinical trial.gov and was submitted on 2024–04-18. Trial Registry number was NCT06377813.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Knee, Exercise Therapy, Core Stability, Pain, Range of Motion, Quality of Life

Introduction and literature review

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common condition affecting people in their 40’s or later in life [1]. Pain, joint stiffness, muscular weakness, altered proprioception, and decreased joint motion are common clinical signs of knee OA that can interfere with daily activities of living [2]. In addition to the articular cartilage involvement the disease processes affect the entire joint, including the periarticular muscles, ligaments, joint capsule, synovium, and subchondral layer of bones [3]. Osteoarthritis is the aging process that results in various degenerative changes in the articular cartilage. These changes may occur for a variety of reasons, such as genetic, biochemical, metabolic, or traumatic [4].

The most frequent kind of arthritis identified is knee osteoarthritis, which will become more widespread as life expectancy and obesity increases. Approximately thirteen percent of women and 10% of men within 60 years of age and older have this problem i.e. Symptomatic knee osteoarthritis. The incidence increases to 40% in those more than the age of 70 years. Males are also less likely than women to be affected by osteoarthritis in their knees [5]. In normal joint Type 2 collagen fibers, chondrocytes, water and proteoglycans make up the majority of articular cartilage, which is made up of these constituents in a way that ensures equilibrium between them all and ensures that any cartilage breakdown is balanced by synthesis. Degradative enzymes called matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) are overexpressed during the beginning of osteoarthritis, disturbing homeostasis and leading to damage of collagen and proteoglycans [6].

Clinical recommendations promote exercise as a critical non-pharmacological treatment for osteoarthritis (OA) of the knee. Before there is noticeable structural deterioration, exercise intervention seems to be more beneficial in the early stages of knee OA [7]. Open and closed kinetic chain (OKC and CKC) exercises have been suggested for patients with osteoarthritis (OA). Straight leg rises in supine laying and knee extension activities in various sitting positions are examples of OKC exercises [8]. On the other hand, during CKC exercise, the distal portion of the body remains stationary on the ground. CKC workouts include the leg press and the squat [9]. Both exercise modalities had comparable effects on improving functional scores, strength, and pain levels [8].

Closed Kinetic Chain (CKC) exercises are known to have a stronger impact than OKC workouts on enhancing muscle coordination patterns and functional status. This can be explained by the need for increased skeletal stability and a greater range of muscle groups to be activated. CKCE improves proprioceptive function and increases muscle strength by activating more spindle and joint proprioceptors. As a result, by mimicking movements like walking, ascending stairs, and getting out of a chair, the patient is more equipped for daily duties [9].

In patients with knee osteoarthritis, Combined Kinematic Chain Exercise (CCE) promises substantial changes in average daily pain, discomfort while walking, thus allowing for an early return to normal routine. Previous studies have shown that CCE also strengthens the tendons and proprioceptive muscles damaged after surgery, as well as the knee, hip, or ankle joints, increasing range of motion and facilitating the proper type of physical activity. [10]. Core stability exercises can enhance overall functional performance, which is beneficial for those with knee OA since it reduces the chance of falls and accidents and makes daily tasks easier. While some research shows that workouts for core stability alone can directly reduce knee pain, improved posture, muscular support, and joint stability can also indirectly reduce pain for some people [11].

It is crucial to treat osteoarthritis in the knees by using Combined Mode-Kinetic Chain Exercise, both in conjunction with and apart from core workouts. This strategy might improve muscular strength, encourage an early recovery, shorten hospital stays, and result in a better prognosis for the patients. This study had a significant economic impact, especially when it came to shorter hospital stays. This work improves overall quality of life by reducing the risk of age-associated depression associated with immobility and inadequate physical exercise.

Data collection tools

The data from participants in both the groups were calculated by the following questionnaires;

Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS): This questionnaire assesses both short and long-term symptoms and functional limitations in individuals with knee injury and osteoarthritis. Researchers developed KOOS as an extension of the WOMAC Osteoarthritis Index. The questionnaire evaluates five subscales: pain, other symptoms, activities of daily living, sport and recreation function, and knee-related quality of life [13]. This scale demonstrates strong construct validity, with values ranging from 0.84 to 0.97 across all subscales. Furthermore, KOOS exhibits outstanding test–retest reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.99 for all subscales [14].

Data collection procedure

The study recruited participants based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with eligible individuals invited to participate after providing written informed consent. Participants were randomly allocated to groups through the lottery method, by drawing either the number one or two from a box, with number one indicating Group A and number two indicating Group B. Due to the nature of the exercise interventions, neither the researcher nor the participants could be blinded; however, the outcome assessor remained blinded to group allocation.

Intervention

Experimental Group or Group A (Combined Kinetic Chain Exercise & Core Strengthening Exercises): Group A received Core Strengthening Exercises with Combined mode kinetic Chain Exercises as a baseline. A daily session of 45 min of Core Strengthening Exercises, patients performed Kinetic Chain exercises plus exercises aimed at the activation of the muscles considered important for core stability. The amount of exercise was approximately 30 min each time, 5 times per week, depending on the individual’s fitness level. The physiotherapist, used clinical evaluation to ascertain the participants' baseline level of fitness. Pain intensity (NPRS), knee range of motion (goniometry), body mass index (BMI), medical history, previous physical activity levels were all assessed. Exercise intensity and progression were customized based on these factors. While those with more discomfort or restricted mobility engaged in lower-intensity exercises, those with improved tolerance, less pain, and increased joint mobility were advanced to higher exercise levels. Throughout the intervention period, safety and a suitable workload were guaranteed by this customized strategy. Treatment protocol for Group A workouts include: Leg raises in a straight posture; Quadriceps setting exercise. Wall slides Complete exercise of arc extension. There was a standard set of 10 repetitions for each exercise. The goal of this regimen is to improve kinematic chain function and support both groups' members' recuperation. There are three levels of difficulty for the core stability exercises that the patients will get. The following exercises would be established during Level 1: (1) Quadruple arm raise; Lateral step up/step down; and (3) Back bridge. Compromises at Level 2: (1) Unilateral Bridge Step up/step down lateral movements (3) Quadrupled lower extremity lift. Exercises at Level 3 include: (1) Quadrupled arm and lower extremity lift; Lateral step up/step down; and (3) Dynamic unilateral bridge. For six weeks, each exercise would be done five times a week, both isotonically and isometrically. There would be a 5-second rest interval between isometric contractions, a 30-second rest interval between sets of isotonic contractions, and a 1-minute rest period in between each exercise. During the course of 12 weeks, each participant adhered to the same standardized exercise training and intensity regimens five days a week. Every workout started with an active warm-up that lasted five to ten minutes and included flexibility exercises, 20–30 minutes combined mode kinetic chain exercises; 10–15 mins of core stability exercises [17].

Data collection procedure

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25, with statistical significance set at p = 0.005. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated non-parametric data. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum) were calculated for age and BMI in both intervention and control groups. Within-group differences between pre- and post-treatment readings were evaluated using the Friedman test, while between-group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Results

The results of this study shows that the mean age of participants was 43.68 ± 1.17 years in the Control group and 42.91 ± 1.92 years in the Experimental group, while the mean BMI was 28.64 ± 1.78 kg/m2 and 29.05 ± 2.17 kg/m2, respectively. In the Control group, 72% of participants had right-sided knee osteoarthritis (OA) and 27% had left-sided OA, whereas in the Experimental group, 77% had right-sided OA and 22% had left-sided OA. The gender distribution included 15 females and 7 males in the Control group, and 14 females and 8 males in the Experimental group. More than 90% of Control group participants and over 60% of Experimental group participants had no prior history of intra-articular steroid use. In both groups, 5 out of 22 participants reported a history of road traffic accident, while the remainder had none. The Shapiro–Wilk test results (p < 0.05) indicated that none of the variables were normally distributed; therefore, nonparametric statistical tests were used.

Both the Control and Experimental groups in Table 1 demonstrated significant improvements (p < 0.05) in all outcome measures from baseline to 6 weeks and 12 weeks. NPRS scores decreased progressively in both groups, indicating effective pain reduction. Knee range of motion in both flexion and extension increased significantly over time, with gains observed at both follow-up points. Similarly, all five components of the KOOS that are pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sport/recreation function, and knee-related quality of life showed consistent improvement within each group. Pre-intervention differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), whereas post-intervention scores were significantly improved across all measures (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined the effects of mode-kinetic chain exercises with or without core stability exercises on knee osteoarthritis (OA) patients' pain, range of motion, and quality of life (QOL). The findings have important ramifications for the treatment of osteoarthritis in the knee, indicating that a combined strategy including kinetic chain and core stability exercises can be quite beneficial. Age, BMI, gender distribution, affected side, and clinical factors were among the baseline parameters that were similar between groups (p > 0.05). From baseline to 6 and 12 weeks, both groups significantly improved in knee range of motion, pain (NPRS), and all KOOS subscales (p < 0.05), according to nonparametric analysis. At both follow-up points, however, between-group comparisons showed that the experimental group had considerably bigger improvements (p < 0.05). In all KOOS areas, including activities of daily living, sport and leisure function, and quality of life, participants who received additional core stability training had superior pain reduction, higher increases in knee flexion and extension, and improved functional outcomes.

The present study demonstrated a significant reduction in pain in both groups, with superior improvement in the group receiving additional core stability training. These findings are consistent with Arjunan (2023), who conducted a study on 44 patients with knee osteoarthritis aged 45–60 years. Pain was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and the results showed a highly significant reduction after four weeks of intervention (p < 0.001). The author reported comparable baseline characteristics between groups, with mean ages of 51.2 ± 3.9 years and 52.6 ± 3.8 years, respectively. The significant post-intervention improvement supports the present findings and highlights the effectiveness of structured exercise programs in reducing pain among individuals with knee osteoarthritis [19].

The improvement in knee range of motion observed in the experimental group was particularly noteworthy. In the present study, pre-treatment values were statistically insignificant (p > 0.05), whereas post-treatment values at 6 and 12 weeks showed significant improvements (p < 0.05) in both knee flexion and extension. These findings align with the study by Flowers et al. (2022), who used the KOOS questionnaire and an electro-goniometer to assess knee range of motion. Their results demonstrated significant improvements in knee mobility (p = 0.001), pain intensity (p = 0.001), and overall function (p = 0.001). The authors concluded that cognitive-motor training approaches are beneficial for individuals with knee osteoarthritis, supporting the role of neuromuscular control in enhancing joint mobility [20].

Quality of life, as assessed through standardized questionnaire the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score that showed significant improvements in the group receiving additional core stability training. KOOS all five components i.e. pain, Symptoms, Activities of daily living, Sport and recreation function and Knee-related quality of life improved in both groups. The finding showed that pretreatment p value > 0.05 which is insignificant and posttreatment p value < 0.05 which is significant for both groups at 0 week, 6 weeks and 12 weeks. This is consistent with literature indicating that core stability exercises in addition to Proprioceptive Exercises can lead to better outcomes in term of Pain and Quality of Life (Richhariya, et al., 2023). 40 male and female participants, ages 45–55 years, with knee pain and first and 2nd grade OA were studied. The study tools was the KOOS scale, which focused on pain and quality of life [21]. Enhanced stability and reduced pain likely allowed patients to engage more fully in daily activities, contributing to a better quality of life. Wisnubrata, (2020) Exercises for core stability may be useful in the treatment of osteoarthritis in the knee. Enhancing muscular strength, enhancing posture, decreasing discomfort, raising capacity, and promoting functional mobility are among the objectives of core stability exercises for patients. Core stability and the hip, knee, and ankle are related to the use of core stability exercises. This is due to the direct and indirect connections that exist between every bodily part. The hip, knee, and ankle muscles will all be powerful if the core muscle is strong [22].

The study faced limitations due to variability in exercise protocols, patient characteristics, and outcome measures, making it challenging to compare findings with other researches. Additionally, low long-term adherence to exercise programs may reduce the sustainability of benefits. It is recommended to develop standardized exercise protocols to enhance comparability across studies, combine exercise therapy with complementary interventions such as education and weight management, and educate patients on the importance of adherence for lasting outcomes. Exercise programs should be tailored to individual needs, considering OA severity, fitness levels, and comorbidities, with a focus on integrating core stability exercises for comprehensive management of knee osteoarthritis.

Conclusion

Study concluded that both group the experimental group in which core strengthening exercises with combined mode kinetic chain exercises and the control group in which combined kinetic chain exercise only, were given to patients reduced the pain, improve knee flexion and extension and improved the overall quality of life of patients with knee Osteoarthritis but experimental group had better results.
