Using machine learning to construct the diagnosis model of female bladder outlet obstruction based on urodynamic study data
Authors: Quan Zhou, Guang Li, Kai Cui, Weilin Mao, Dongxu Lin, Zhenglong Yang, Zhong Chen, Youmin Hu, Xin Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo intelligently diagnose whether there is bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) in female with decent detrusor contraction ability by focusing on urodynamic study (UDS) data.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the UDS data of female patients during urination. Eleven easily accessible urinary flow indicators were calculated according to the UDS data of each patient during voiding period. Eight diagnosis models based on back propagation neural network with different input feature combination were constructed by analyzing the correlations between indicators and lower urinary tract dysfunction labels. Subsequently, the stability of diagnostic models was evaluated by five-fold cross-validation based on training data, while the performance was compared on test dataset.ResultsUDS data from 134 female patients with a median age of 51 years (range, 27–78 years) were selected for our study. Among them, 66 patients suffered BOO and the remaining were normal. Applying the 5-fold cross-validation method, the model with the best performance achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) value of 0.949±0.060 using 9 UDS input features. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for BOO diagnosis model in the testing process are 94.4%, 100%, and 89.3%, respectively.ConclusionsThe 9 significant indicators in UDS were employed to construct a diagnostic model of female BOO based on machine learning algorithm, which performs preferable classification accuracy and stability.Keywords: Back propagation neural network, Bladder outlet obstruction, Feature selection, Machine learning, Urodynamic study

INTRODUCTION

Urodynamic study (UDS) is a primary clinical method used to replicate the entire process from bladder filling to micturition in patients, with the aim of diagnosing lower urinary tract symptoms [1]. During the process of UDS, bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) is a crucial physical concept utilized to assess whether there is abnormal increase in urethral resistance during urination, which is essential in determining the smoothness of urination in patients [2].

The identification of BOO in patients is typically clinically determined by simultaneously examining the detrusor pressure and urinary flow rate curves during UDS. This approach heavily relies on the expertise and experience of urologists. The Schäfer nomogram and the International Continence Society nomogram (ICS nomogram) provide additional diagnostic criteria for BOO in male patients by analyzing detrusor pressure and flow rate curves during micturition [3,4]. However, due to anatomical differences in the female urethra, UDS data for women lacks obvious specificity [5]. The Blaivas and Groutz nomogram (B-G nomogram) has been utilized for diagnosing BOO in women since 2000 [6], but Akikwala et al. [7] discovered in their research that it tends to overestimate the level of obstruction. The Solomon and Greenwell nomogram (S-G nomogram) [8] was the first clinically validated chart according to the European Association of Urology Guidelines 2021. In 2023, the most recent International Continence Society-Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction (ICS-SUFU) standard documents for pressure flow study were published [9]. By refining the S-G nomogram, a standardized reference for diagnosing BOO in women was established. However, the current method still rely on predetermined formulas for diagnosing female BOO based on historical data, lacking adaptability and flexibility.

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has facilitated the widespread utilization of machine learning in various fields, including medicine. Despite its extensive application, the exploration of machine learning in urodynamics is still in its nascent stages. For instance, Wang et al. [10] employed the k-means algorithm to construct a detrusor overactivity (DO) library and identified DO by assessing the resemblance between test samples and samples in the DO library. Similarly, Szmydki et al. [11] extracted the key characteristic parameters from UDS tracing and employed a decision tree algorithm for diagnosing detrusor underactivity. Nevertheless, the integration of machine learning in BOO remains unexplored in current literature.

To address the aforementioned issues, this study extracts the crucial indicators of urination period in UDS tracing of female patients, develops a machine learning diagnosis model for the detection of BOO, and subsequently assesses the model’s performance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data acquisition and pre-processing

All data were collected from the Department of Urology, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, during the period of 2018 to 2021. The detrusor pressure, infused volume, urinary flow rate and urine volume curve of 134 female patients during the voiding phase were extracted from the UDS software from Laborie Co., Canada. It should be emphasized that all patients have a certain degree of detrusor contraction ability. Among them, the median age was 51 years (range, 27–78 years). The number of patients with and without BOO were 68 and 66, respectively. All diagnostic conclusions of BOO were based on cystoscopic findings, and the diagnosis of BOO is illustrated in Supplementary Material 1 using a specific patient as an example. For patients without BOO, some of them exhibited symptoms of urinary retention, such as bladder sensitivity, detrusor overactivity, and urinary incontinence. In this study, 80 samples were allocated as the training set, and the remaining 54 samples were designated as the test set. To ensure consistent sample distributions in the training and test sets, we employed the strategy of stratified sampling as described in section 2.4. It is important to highlight that test set was not involved in the model training process. The specific sample description is provided in Table 1. To facilitate the training of the model, the non-BOO samples were labeled as 0, and BOO samples were labeled as 1.

2. Correlation analysis and feature selection

Correlation is a statistical method used to measure the potential linear relationship between two variables [12]. To gain a deeper understanding of the significance of each urinary flow index in the final model, we utilized the classical Pearson correlation coefficient formula [13] (refer to the Pearson correlation coefficient section in Supplementary Material 2) to examine the linear relationship between each urinary flow index and its corresponding symptom label (0 and 1).

Generally, the value of a correlation coefficient falls between -1 and 1. The closer the absolute value of the correlation coefficient is to 1, the stronger the linear relationship between the two variables. The 5-fold cross validation (which will be detailed in section 2.4) was used to demonstrate the correlation coefficient between each index and label in the dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 1. It should be noted that all correlation coefficients are absolute values.

3. Construction of the machine learning model

As one of the classical methods in the field of machine learning, neural network establishes a multi-layer and multi-node network structure by simulating human brain to establish the nonlinear mapping relationship between input and output [14]. The back propagation neural network (BPNN) is the most representative network structure in neural networks, and it is also the core of enabling neural networks to have strong nonlinear mapping ability and self-learning ability [15]. Generally, a three-layer structure consisting of an input layer, hidden layer and output layer is sufficient to approximate a continuous function with arbitrary accuracy. The structure diagram of BPNN is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Since only two categories need to be diagnosed in this study, the number of output layer nodes is set to 2. The detailed principle of BPNN is shown in Supplementary Material 2.

4. Model performance validation

Due to the limited samples used for the training model, the dataset was divided using 5-fold cross validation [16] (CV) for model validation. Specifically, all samples were divided into five equal parts, with one part used as the training sample and the remaining parts as the test sample to train the model. Five experiments were conducted to ensure that each sample served as a test sample. Finally, the mean value of the five model validation results was considered the final evaluation result. However, the above method had a flaw, i.e., the distribution of different sample types in each fold of data was uneven, potentially impacting the classification results negatively. Therefore, this study integrated a stratified sampling strategy with 5-fold CV to ensure an equal sample proportion of the two categories in each dataset. The schematic of 5-fold CV with a stratified sampling strategy is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

During the model training period, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model for positive and negative samples [17]. Compared with the accuracy evaluation index, AUC can more objectively and comprehensively reflect the classification ability of the model for unbalanced datasets. Additionally, sensitivity and specificity were utilized to evaluate the classification ability of the trained model. All the model construction and performance analysis mentioned above were conducted using Python v3.8.5.

RESULTS

1. Model training results

In order to determine if the core indicators extracted were beneficial for enhancing the model’s performance, this study compared the model’s performance with different sets of urinary flow indicators as inputs. Initially, the model with all 11 urinary flow indicators as inputs was served as the baseline for performance assessment. Eight diagnostic models were developed with varying numbers of urine flow indicators as model inputs. The urine flow index was chosen based on the correlation coefficient’s absolute value, from high to low. In the eight comparative experiments, the dataset was divided using 5-fold CV combined with a stratified sampling strategy, and the average AUC was used to assess the model’s performance. It should be noted that in the comparative experiment, the different number of input features led to variations in the corresponding BPNN model structures. Across these experiments, the BPNN hierarchical structures of the eight models were 11-8-2, 10-7-2, 9-6-2, 8-5-2, 7-5-2, 6-4-2, 5-3-2, and 4-3-2, respectively.

From the Fig. 2, we can see that the average AUC of the model is only 0.815±0.132 when four features are used as inputs. As the number of input features increases, the AUC of the model also increases. The model’s AUC reaches its highest value 0.949±0.060 when the number of input features is 9. However, as the number of input features continues to increase, the AUC of the model begins to decrease. When all 11 features are used as inputs to the model, the AUC drops to 0.930±0.074.

2. Model testing results

For the aforementioned models, we assessed their performance using test data. Additionally, we classified the test data using the B-G nomogram, the S-G nomogram and ICS-SUFU standard, which are commonly used in clinical practice. The test results are shown in the confusion matrix in Fig. 3.

As we can see, the machine learning-based model’s classification accuracy for the test data (Fig. 3D-K) is overall better than the three clinically used nomograms (Fig. 3A-C). The ICS-SUFU standard, improved from the S-G nomogram, possesses higher sensitivity but lower specificity. The overall classification accuracy (81.5%) of the ICS-SUFU standard is lower than that of the S-G nomogram (85.2%). The results of the machine learning model showed that the BPNN with a 4-feature input is comparable to the S-G nomogram in terms of total classification accuracy (85.2%), with higher sensitivity (92.3%>88.5%) and lower specificity (78.6%<82.1%). As the number of input features increases, the advantage of BPNN becomes more evident. When the number of input features reaches 8 and 9, the overall classification accuracy reaches 94.4%, with sensitivity even at 100% and the specificity at 89.3%. However, as the input features continue to increase, the sensitivity of the BPNN remains at 100%, but the specificity sharply drops to 71.4%.

DISCUSSION

Despite UDS currently being the gold standard for studying lower urinary tract function during bladder filling and emptying, there is no consensus on the interpretation of UDS tracings [10]. Clinically, there is no universally acknowledged diagnostic standard for diagnosing BOO. This lack of consensus is mainly due to the absence of a consistent signal and feature pattern in UDS tracings [18]. While the B-G nomogram, the S-G nomogram and the ICS-SUFU standard are effective for diagnosing female BOO, they are based on fixed formulas derived from historical samples, which limits their flexibility and adaptability to new data.

The main purpose of this study is to propose a new approach for developing a widely applicable and more flexible diagnostic method for female BOO. To achieve this, an intelligent diagnostic model was constructed using machine learning techniques. The aim is to introduce an innovative concept for enhancing the standard of diagnosis for female BOO diagnosis through advanced computer technology. However, there are two primary challenges that need to be addressed. Firstly, the machine learning model typically requires consistent input dimensions for each sample [19]. Given that the urination time varies among patients, harmonizing the input dimensions of the model poses the initial challenge. Secondly, a contentious issue is that machine learning models are often perceived as black boxes [20]. Hence, it is crucial to explore methods for selecting more transparent and convincing model inputs.

During the process of data acquisition, we observed that some female patients failed to complete the urination process due to environmental and psychological factors. However, the UDS software still recorded the data of these incomplete examinations, which were subsequently excluded from our study. Following data collection, we extracted 11 common indicators of the urination process in female patients from the UDS tracings, based on medical expertise and clinical experience. These indicators were then utilized as inputs for a machine learning model. This approach resolved the issue of inconsistent input dimensions for the model and integrated expert knowledge into the selected input features, enhancing the credibility of the study. Nevertheless, the average experimental results prompted us to reconsider on whether these eleven indicators represent the optimal model inputs.

To this end, we calculated the correlation coefficients between the eleven features and their corresponding labels. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the two indicators with the largest absolute correlation coefficients are detrusor pressure at the beginning of urine flow (P.Qbegin) and detrusor pressure at maximum urinary flow rate (P.Qmax), which are also the primary indicators of concern for doctors when analyzing UDS tracing. Moreover, total urine volume, maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax), average urinary flow rate (Qave), and maximum detrusor pressure (Pmax) are closely related to labels, with their correlation coefficients having a mean absolute value exceeding 0.2. In addition, it is important to highlight that the standard deviation of Pmax is notably high, which is linked to the urethral structure and urination pattern in women. Due to the short and wide urethra, some women can complete the urination process smoothly without requiring significant detrusor pressure. Conversely, certain female patients may experience post-voiding urgency due to their heightened bladder sensitivity, resulting in a substantial increase in detrusor pressure, resulting in significant fluctuations in maximum detrusor pressure. Ultimately, the selection of input features should be determined by the model training results.

The results of model training and testing show that the machine learning-based model outperforms the B-G nomogram, the S-G nomogram and the ICS-SUFU standard in terms of overall diagnostic performance. This superiority is primarily attributed to the fact that traditional nomograms rely on only two indices, P.Qmax and Qmax, which are easier to visualize in a two-dimensional coordinate system. In contrast, the approach proposed in this paper integrates a broader range of urinary flow metrics to develop the classification model, thereby incorporating more comprehensive disease information. Additionally, the BPNN is not a simple linear classifier; it nonlinearly characterize high-dimensional input features to achieve classification. This also makes it difficult to visualize the classification results in a two-dimensional coordinate system. Furthermore, this study also highlights that having more input features does not necessarily translate to the better model performance. It is suggested that an increase in input features may introduce redundant information, potentially impacting the model’s recognition accuracy. On the contrary, too few input features may lead to underutilization of crucial information. Therefore, selecting the optimal number of input features is also essential for enhancing model performance.

Although the performance of the model constructed in this study is the best in the comparative experiment, this diagnostic model cannot be directly applied to practical clinical diagnosis due to its lack of medical interpretability. In the process of UDS, obtaining patients’ medical history and understanding their feelings during urination are also crucial factors for doctors’ diagnosis [21]. However, due to the subjective nature of doctors’ diagnoses, a set of clear standards has not yet been established. In the future, if medical history and patients’ feelings can be integrated as part of the model input, it is likely to significantly enhance the model’s performance.

We believe that the significance of this study lies in providing a new idea for female BOO diagnosis standard. Although machine learning-based diagnostic model has some limitations, we aim to enhance the model’s performance gradually and conduct additional research on the interpretability and standardization of the diagnostic model. Simultaneously, we also hope that researchers in this field to contribute new perspectives and insights on this issue to collectively advance the field of urodynamics.

CONCLUSIONS

This study analyzed the UDS tracings of female patients during urination, screened out the key characteristic indexes by correlation analysis, and developed a diagnostic model using machine learning. The model demonstrated in high accuracy in identifying both positive and negative samples. The significance of this study lies in offering a novel approach to diagnosing female BOO and reducing reliance on doctors’ expertise and clinical experience to some extent.
