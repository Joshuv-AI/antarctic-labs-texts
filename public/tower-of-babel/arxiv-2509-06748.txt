Differential Geometry on Pointwise Affine Spaces
Dan Jonsson

Abstract
. We introduce an alternative formalization of curved spaces in which the concept of a pointwise affine space, as defined here, replaces that of a manifold. New or modified definitions of familiar notions from differential geometry such as connections, torsion, Riemann curvature, vector fields and differentiation of vector fields are presented, and examples of applications of the resulting framework are given. In general, the notions and results considered receive simpler and more transparent forms than in conventional approaches.



1.
Introduction

It may be said that there are two mathematical languages for differential
geometry: one coordinate-oriented and one coordinate-free.

The coordinate-oriented language is the oldest one, and bears evidence
of its origins. During the early development of differential geometry,
modern notions of abstract spaces had not yet crystallized. The first
axiomatic definition of affine spaces was given by Weyl in 1918
[
1
]
,
and in spite of early contributions by Grassmann
[
2
]
and
Peano
[
3
]
abstract vector space theory ”was actually not
developed before 1920”
[
4
, p. 228]
. By then, differential
calculus on curved spaces, following Ricci and Levi-Civita
[
5
]
,
had already been used in the theory of General Relativity. Differential
calculus on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
regarded as a concrete flat space had
morphed into a more modern differential calculus when
\mathbb{R}^{n}

came to be regarded as a possibly curved space. To clarify this point,
we give a brief and simplified account of generalized differential
calculus on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be regarded as a space where points, vectors
and their coordinates live. We define a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

as a tuple
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}}\right)
, a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

as a tuple
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{1},\ldots v_{n}}}\right),
and denote their their
coordinates by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n}}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v^{1},\ldots v^{n}}}\right)
,
respectively. A coordinate system is a bijection

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}}\right),\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}}}\right)}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n}}}\right),\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v^{1},\ldots,v^{n}}}\right)}}\right)

such that
x^{i}=f^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
and
v^{i}=f^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. A Cartesian coordinate system is a coordinate
system such that
x^{i}=f^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}}\right)=x_{i}

and
v^{i}=f^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}}}\right)=v_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
In particular, we let

v\thicksim_{\mathsf{C}}v^{k}

describe a situation where the vector
v
has coordinates
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v^{1},\ldots,v^{n}}}\right)

relative to
\mathsf{C}
.

A vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a function

\boldsymbol{v}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad x\mapsto v.

Using the Einstein summation convention, we can define the directional
derivative
\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
of the differentiable
vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
with respect to the vector
u
at
the point
x
by requiring that

(1.1)

\nabla{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\sim_{C}u^{i}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{k}}{\partial x^{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),

where
C
is a Cartesian coordinate system. However, if we substitute
coordinates relative to a curvilinear (that is, not necessarily Cartesian)
coordinate system
\overline{C}
in (
1.1
) then the
definition of
\nabla{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
may need
to be given another form since, in general,

\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\nsim_{\overline{C}}\,\overline{u}^{i}\frac{\partial\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{k}}{\partial\overline{x}^{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right).

We thus need to complement (
1.1
) by a correction term,
which we express by means of a system of scalars
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}

for each point
x
, so that we obtain a coordinate-system independent
definition

(1.2)

\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\sim_{\overline{C}}\,\overline{u}^{i}\frac{\partial\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{k}}{\partial\overline{x}^{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+\overline{u}^{i}\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right).

By the construction of
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
, we have
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=0

for all
i,j,k,x
if
\overline{C}
is Cartesian, so
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}

measures the discrepancy between
\overline{C}
and a Cartesian coordinate
system.

We have thus generalized (
1.1
), a formula for calculating
a directional derivative in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, valid only for a Cartesian
coordinate system, to a formula (
1.2
), valid for a curvilinear
coordinate system. There is, however, another way to look at (
1.2
).
In Einstein’s and Grossmann’s
Entwurf
article
[
6
, p. 23]
,
the latter makes a profound observation:

Ricci und Levi-Cività haben, ausgehend von den Christoffel’schen
Resultaten, ihre Methoden der absoluten, d.h. vom Koordinatensystem
unabhängigen Differentialrechnung entwickelt, die gestatten, den Differentialgleichungen
der mathematischen Physik eine invariante Form zu geben. Da aber die

Vektoranalysis des

auf beliebige
krummlinige Koordinaten bezogenen euklidischen Raumes

identisch ist mit der
Vektoranalysis einer beliebigen

[—]
Mannigfaltigkeit
, so bietet
es keine Schwierigkeiten, die vektoranalytischen Begriffsbildungen
[---] auszudehnen auf die vorstehende allgemeine Theorie von
Einstein. [emphasis supplied]

Starting from the results of Christoffel, Ricci and Levi-Cività
have developed their methods of absolute, i.e., coordinate-system
independent, differential calculus that allows the differential equations
of mathematical physics to be given an invariant form. However, as

vector analysis in a Euclidean space with arbitrary curvilinear
coordinate
s is formally identical to
vector analysis
in an arbitrary manifold
, it does not present any difficulties
to extend the conceptual apparatus of vector analysis [so as to
apply to] Einstein’s general theory presented above.

In other words, the change of coordinates of
u
and
v
can depend
on a change of the coordinate system (”passive transformation”),
or it can depend on a change of the vectors
u
and
v
themselves
(”active transformation”). These are interchangeable points of
view. In the latter case, the vectors are transformed as a consequence
of a deformation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to a curved space. Thus, in
the first case,
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
describe the ”curvature” of
the coordinate system; in the second case
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
describe
the curvature of the space itself.

Thus, early differential geometry was characterized by

•

a coordinate-oriented mathematical language,

•

a setting for differential calculus in the form of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

regarded as a possibly curved space,

•

a tool
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
, nowadays known as a connection, for describing
the curvature of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The coordinate-oriented approach, as amplified by the tensor formalism

[
5
]
, is quite powerful, but has two significant weaknesses.
The first one is that
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a globally trivial topology
(shape): it cannot naturally represent a sphere, a torus etc. The
second weakness derives precisely from the fact that a coordinate-oriented
mathematical language is used. For example, in modern algebra a vector
is conceived as simply an element of a vector space, not, in a more
convoluted way, as an object the coordinates of which change in a
particular way when the coordinate system changes in a particular
way. A related reproach is that the ”débauches d’indices”
(Cartan) used in the coordinate-oriented language tends to obscure
the geometric meaning of objects and operations.

Thus, the emphasis in the further development of differential geometry
has fallen on elaborating an appropriate notion of a manifold that
generalizes
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so as to accommodate spaces with more
general shapes, and on proposing coordinate-free formulations whenever
feasible. Several sophisticated frameworks have been developed, characterized
by

•

an abstract, algebraically oriented mathematical language,

•

a setting for differential calculus in the form of a fiber bundle
(such as a tangent bundle, a vector bundle, a frame bundle or a principal
bundle) the base space of which is a differentiable manifold, a generalization
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(sometimes
\mathbb{C}^{n})
that is locally
but not necessarily globally similar to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
\mathbb{C}^{n})
,

•

an abstract notion of a connection on the fiber bundle involved.

The price in the form of increased complexity paid to generalize
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and reduce the use of coordinates is, alas, high. While this increased
complexity serves a purpose for advanced applications, there are many
simpler applications where it is redundant. Worse, a significant disadvantage
of the coordinate-oriented approach persists: the geometric meaning
of abstract algebraic expressions is often as obscure as that of coordinate
expressions. Is it really not possible to find a simpler and simultaneously
more transparent coordinate-free formulation of differential geometry?

Following up on previous work
[
7
]
, where the key notion
of a pointwise affine space was introduced, this is the question that
will be addressed in this article. In the approach proposed here,
the main elements are

•

a coordinate-free, geometric-algebraic mathematical language,

•

a setting for differential calculus in the form of a pointwise affine
space,

•

a geometrically meaningful connection, defined via a pseudo-derivative

\Delta_{u}v
on the pointwise affine space.

The price paid for simplicity and transparency is that the present
formulation again applies only to spaces with a simple global topology,
although a generalization to more general cases may be possible (see
Section 10).

In Section 2, we review affine (flat) spaces, and then generalize
to pointwise affine (flat and curved) spaces in Section 3. Using notions
from Section 3, we introduce tools for analyzing discrete and infinitesimal
curvature in Section 4. In Section 5, we then show how torsion and
Riemann curvature can be defined and interpreted in terms of the new
notions, in particular the coordinate-free pseudo-derivative
\Delta_{u}v
.
In Sections 6 – 8, we define and investigate differentiation of
functions that connect pointwise affine spaces; this includes differentiation
of vector fields. Section 9 contains applications of the theory developed
to some topics of interest in differential geometry. In Section 10,
we make some final remarks, providing a perspective on the theory
of pointwise affine spaces.

2.
Affine spaces

Recall that there are two equivalent ways to define an action of a
group
G
on a set
X
, in particular, an action of the additive
group of a vector space
V
on an affine space. We can use a function

\upphi:G\times X\rightarrow X
such that
\upphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e,x}}\right)=x

\upphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g,\upphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h,x}}\right)}}\right)=\upphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(gh,x}}\right)

, or

of a vector space. Alternatively, we may use a group homomorphism

\phi:G\rightarrow\mathcal{S}{}_{X}

\mathcal{S}{}_{X}

is the group of bijections on
X
. Following Berger
[
8
]
,
we use the second approach here.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

be a non-empty set,

of all bijections on

and

a vector space over a field

.
Also let

(1)

\mathfrak{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)=\mathfrak{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

As the additive group of

\mathfrak{a}

Below,

3.
Pointwise affine spaces

Consider an affine system
\mathcal{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\mathtt{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)

2.3

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\vec{u}}}\right)+\vec{v}}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{u}+\vec{v}}}\right)}}\right)=\vec{0}

Definition 3.1

An

For each

For convenience, we may use the notation

(3.1)

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right),\qquad p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{v}}}\right)\equiv\mathfrak{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{v}}}\right)

when it is not necessary to reference
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}

explicitly. Employing this simplified notation, we have

\displaystyle p\circ p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{v}}}\right)=\vec{v},\quad p^{-1}\circ p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=v,

\displaystyle p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda u+\kappa w}}\right)=\lambda p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+\kappa p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right),\quad p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\vec{u}+\kappa\vec{v}}}\right)=\lambda p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{u}}}\right)+\kappa p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vec{v}}}\right),

where
u,v\in V
and
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\vec{V}.
We will also use
the notation

(3.2)

p+\overline{v}\equiv p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right).

Thus,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

etc.

Definition 3.2
.

Consider a pointwise affine system
\mathscr{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
,
We say that
A
is
affine flat
when
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

for any
p,q\in A
and
v\in V
.

An affine flat pointwise affine space
A
is effectively an affine
space. Using the notation introduced in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
),
we can form an expression that reflects to what extent and in what
way
A
is curved at
p
, and vanishes everywhere if and only if

A
is affine flat.

Proposition 3.3
.

A pointwise affine space
A
is affine flat if and only
if

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)=\vec{0}

for all
p\in A
and
u,v\in V
.

Proof.

If
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)
for every
p,q\in A
and
v\in V

then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}

\displaystyle=p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right).

Conversely, if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}=p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)

then
p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)
,
so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

for all
p\in A
and
u,v\in V
, and setting
u=p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q\leftarrow p}}\right)

we obtain

q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q\leftarrow p}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right).\qed

Corollary 3.4
.

A pointwise affine space
A
is affine flat if and
only if

(3.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=\vec{0},

or equivalently

(3.4)

p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v=0,

for all
p\in A
and
u,v\in V
.

4.
Discrete and infinitesimal curvature

Throughout this section, we will consider a pointwise affine system

\mathscr{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
. We first
consider curvature from the point of view of discrete differential
geometry, where we do not yet employ the notion of a limit.

4.1.
Deviation, dissociation and displacement

Definition 4.1
.

A
(vector)

deviation
is a function

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow V,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right),

or a value of such a function. A
(vector) dissociation
is a
function

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow V,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-v=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v,

or a value of such a function. Variants of these functions are

\displaystyle\vec{\mathrm{G}}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow\vec{V},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\vec{\mathrm{G}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right);=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\vec{\mathrm{D}}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow\vec{V},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right).

Remarkably,
\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
is precisely the expression
(
3.4
) that measures non-flatness of a pointwise affine
space.
\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and its infinitesimal counterpart

\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
feature prominently in the remainder
of this article.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider a pointwise affine system
\mathscr{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
.
The function

\mathrm{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v}}\right)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v

is the
discrete affine curvature
of
A
at
p
, and a function

\mathbf{C}:p\mapsto\mathrm{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

is a
discrete affine connection
on
A
.

Intuitively, a deviation
\mathrm{G}
in a curved space is, in general,
a rotation and rescaling of a vector bound to a fixed point, due to
a deformation of the space, while a dissociation
\mathrm{D}
is
the discrepancy between the original vector and the rotated/rescaled
vector.

It follows directly from the definitions of
\mathrm{G}
and
\mathrm{D}

that
\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,

\vec{\mathrm{G}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and
\vec{\mathrm{D}}{}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

are linear in
v
for all
p\in A
since
\mathfrak{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

and
\mathfrak{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)^{-1}
are linear functions. Hence,

\mathrm{D}_{u}0\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0
and
\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{u}0\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\vec{0}
.
However,
\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,

\vec{\mathrm{G}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and
\vec{\mathrm{D}}{}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

are not necessarily linear in
u
, although clearly
\mathrm{D}_{0}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

and
\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{0}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\vec{0}
.

Deviation and dissociation functions can be composed. Specifically.

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=\mathrm{G}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{G}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+u}}\right)\circ p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)-w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)-w}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)-w}}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\circ p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)-p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)-p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)+w

and so on for more than three vectors. Note that hence

(4.2)

\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{G}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\mathrm{G}_{u}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\mathrm{G}_{v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+w.

\vec{\mathrm{G}}{}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{u}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,

\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

etc. can be defined analogously.
\mathrm{G}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,

\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
\vec{\mathrm{G}}{}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

and
\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

are clearly linear in
w
for all
u,v\in V
and
p\in A
. It also
follows from
\mathrm{D}_{0}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{D}_{u}0\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

that
\mathrm{D}_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{0}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

etc.

Definition 4.3
.

A
(point) displacement
is a function

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow A,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\mathrm{M}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\vec{\mathrm{G}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}.

A (once) iterated displacement is given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{uv}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)+\vec{\mathrm{G}}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{G}_{v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\bar{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\circ p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\bar{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right).

\mathrm{M}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
corresponds to an
interval
displacement

of
p\cdots p+\overline{v}
along
p\cdots p+\overline{u}

p\cdots p+\overline{v}\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v},

or a
parallel transport of a bound vector
v_{p}
along
u_{p}

\displaystyle v_{p}\mapsto p^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)}}\right)_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)}=\mathrm{G}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)}.

Thus, the deviation
\mathrm{G}
is the key to the dissociation
\mathrm{D}
,
the point displacement
\mathrm{M}
, and the classical notion of
parallel transport of vectors.

4.2.
Infinitesimal dissociation

In proper differential geometry, we have notions of differentiable
manifolds and curvature. We can similarly introduce notions of differentiability
and infinitesimal curvature in a pointwise affine space. For this
purpose, we define an operator
\Delta
in terms of the dissociation

\mathrm{D}
by forming a natural limit.

Definition 4.4
.

A
differentiable
pointwise affine space is a
pointwise affine space
A
such that the limit

\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{D}{}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau u}}\right)}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v}{\tau}

exists for all
p\in A
and
u,v\in V
, and is linear in
u
and

v
.

We call
\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
the
infinitesimal dissociation

of
v
with respect to
u
at
p,
or
the pseudo-derivative

of
v
with respect to
u
at
p
.

It follows from Definition
4.4
that if
A
is a differentiable
pointwise affine space then

(4.4)

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v=0

for all
u,v\in V
and
p\in A
.

It is obvious how to extend Definition
4.2
to the infinitesimal
case.

Definition 4.5
.

Consider a pointwise affine system
\mathscr{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
.
The function

C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v}}\right)\mapsto\Delta{}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

is the (infinitesimal)
affine curvature
of
A
at
p
, and
a function

\boldsymbol{\mathit{C}}:p\mapsto C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

is an (infinitesimal)
affine connection
on
A
.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
is additive in
u

then
\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
is linear in
u
and
v
.

Proof.

It suffices to note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{cu}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cu}}\right)}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v}{\tau}=c\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c\tau}}\right)u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v}{c\tau}=c\,\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

for all
c\neq 0
, and that
\Delta_{cu}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=c\,\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

if
c=0
.
∎

A twice differentiable pointwise affine space is a differentiable
pointwise affine space such that the limit

\mathrm{\Delta}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{\Delta}_{v}w}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{\mathrm{D}}{}_{\tau u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta_{v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{\mathrm{D}}{}_{\tau u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{D}{}_{\tau v}w\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau}

exists and is linear in
u,v,w
, and so on for three or more times
differentiable pointwise affine spaces.

Note that differentiability of a pointwise affine space is an intrinsic
property of the space, independent of differentiability of functions.
Differentiation of functions will be considered in Section
7
.

5.
Skew-curvature

Below, we will again consider a pointwise affine system
\mathscr{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
.
The functions
\mathrm{D}_{u}v
and
\Delta{}_{u}v
discussed above
account for the affine curvature of
A
. The ”tonal amount” of
curvature can be subdivided into components in various ways. In this
section, we will consider two curvature components frequently encountered
in the literature, obtained by forming the difference
\mathrm{D}_{u}v-\mathrm{D}_{v}u

(torsion) anf the difference
\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{v}w}}\right)-\mathrm{D}_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{D}_{u}w}}\right)

(affine Riemann curvature), as well as their infinitesimal analogues.
A combination of torsion and affine Riemann curvature will also be
discussed.

5.1.
Torsion (first-order affine skew-curvature)

Definition 5.1
.

The
discrete torsion
\mathrm{T}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

at

p

for

u,v
is given by the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}:V\times V\times A\rightarrow V,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v,p}}\right)\mapsto\mathrm{T}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=\mathrm{D}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\mathrm{D}_{v}u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)-u}}\right).

A variant of
\mathrm{T}
is
\vec{\mathrm{T}}
, given by

\vec{\mathrm{T}}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{T}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)=\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\vec{\mathrm{D}}_{v}u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right).

Clearly,
\mathrm{T}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\mathrm{T}_{vu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

and
\vec{\mathrm{T}}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\vec{\mathrm{T}}_{vu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\vec{0}
.
Furthermore, it follows from
\mathrm{D}_{0}u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{D}_{u}0\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

that
\mathrm{T}_{0v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{T}_{u0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=0

and
\vec{\mathrm{T}}_{0v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathrm{\vec{\mathrm{T}}}_{u0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\vec{0}

for all
u,v\in V
and
p\in A
.

Proposition 5.2
.

\vec{\mathrm{T}}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{M}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right]
,
where

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{M}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right]=\mathrm{M}_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\leftarrow\mathrm{M}_{v}u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)+\overline{u}}}\right).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\vec{\mathrm{T}}{}_{uv}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)-p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{u}}}\right)+\overline{v}}}\right)\leftarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{v}}}\right)+\overline{u}}}\right).\qed

As shown in Figure 5.1, Proposition
5.2
provides a simple
geometric interpretation of torsion, motivating Definition

.

Lemma 5.6

Let

6.
Fields

We have already encountered an example of a field, namely an affine
action field. In this article, an
S
-field on
A
is simply
a function
f:A\rightarrow S
, where
A
is a point space and
S

is any set. We denote the set of all fields of the form
A\rightarrow S

by
A^{S}
.

If
S
is a vector space
V
over
K
then
A^{V}
is also a vector
space over
K
with operations defined by

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varPhi+\varPsi}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\varPhi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\varPsi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\varPhi}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varPhi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right).

More generally, a set
A^{V}
of functions closed under the displayed
operations is also a vector space.

In the expression
\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
u
and
v
are
vectors in a given vector space
V
. We can replace these vectors
by vector fields
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in A^{V}
by substituting

\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
for
u
and
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

for
v
. Specifically, we set

\displaystyle\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}v}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)-v}{\tau},

\displaystyle\Delta_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)-\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)-\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}{\tau}

for all
p\in A
. Note that then
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
do not
depend on the function
\boldsymbol{u}
but only on its value
\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.

We can thus speak about an infinitesimal dissociation, or pseudo-derivative,
of a vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
, and along a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
.
As a consequence, the vectors
u
,
v
and
w
in
T_{uv}
,
R_{uvw}

and
C_{uvw}
can be replaced by the vector fields
\boldsymbol{u}
,

\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
.

A natural operation for fields on point spaces, especially vector
fields, is differentiation. In the derivative
\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
discussed below, the vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
cannot be replaced
by the vector
v
;
a derivative is a derivative of a field.
On the other hand, we can replace
u
by the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
,
setting

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

for all
p\in A.

Below, we will define several derivatives of point fields and vector

7.1.

We first consider differentiation of mappings between point spaces

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,\Phi,p}}\right)\mapsto{\textstyle\dot{\overline{\mathrm{D}}}}_{u}\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right):=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{b}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\leftarrow\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right).

If

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,\Phi,p}}\right)\mapsto\dot{\mathsf{D}}_{u}\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\leftarrow\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right).

7.5.
Linearity properties

Recall from Section
4.2
that
\Delta_{u}v
is linear
in the vectors
u
and
v
,

partly by assumption and partly
by calculation. The derivatives
\overline{\nabla}{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
,

\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\delta{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
, and
variants thereof, have similar linearity properties. For example,

(7.19)

\nabla_{\lambda u+u^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\lambda\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\nabla_{u^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right),\qquad\nabla_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)=\lambda\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

for any
\lambda\in K
, so
\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
is linear
in
u
and
\boldsymbol{v}
with scalar multiplication by elements
of
K
.

Derivatives with regard to vector fields have analogous linearity
properties. Thus, if we set
\nabla_{\phi\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\nabla_{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

and

then (

) generalizes to

\nabla_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\phi\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right),\qquad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}}\right)=\lambda\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

for any
\phi\in A^{K}
and
\lambda\in K
, so
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

is linear in
\boldsymbol{u}
with scalar multiplication by elements
of the ring
A^{K}
, and in
\boldsymbol{v}
with scalar multiplication
by elements of
K
.

8.
Product rules

There are well-known rules for differentiation of products of vector
fields. In this section, we formulate such rules for the somewhat
more general case of bilinear maps of vectors or vector fields.

Recall that if
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)
exists
and
f:X\mapsto f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
is continuous then
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)}}\right)
.
More generally, if
f:X,Y\mapsto f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X,Y}}\right)
is continuous
and
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)
and
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)

exist then

(8.1)

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right),Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right),\lim_{\tau\rightarrow a}Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)}}\right).

8.1.

Proposition 8.1
.

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v,w}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,w}}\right\rangle\!}}\right\rangle

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,w}}\right\rangle\!}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim\nolimits_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\tau}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P_{\tau}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right),P_{\tau}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)}}\right\rangle\!}}\right\rangle-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,w}}\right\rangle\!}}\right\rangle}}\right)

8.2.
Coherent derivatives (uniform vector fields)

If
\mathscr{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U,A,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}}}\right)
,
\mathscr{B}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,V,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{b}}}}\right)

and
\mathscr{C}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W,W,\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{c}}}}\right)
, where

, then
\boldsymbol{v}:A\rightarrow V
and
\boldsymbol{w}:A\rightarrow W

are said to be uniform vector fields and a derivative of a bilinear
map

coherent

Lemma 8.2
.

If the pointwise affine space considered is differentiable

\lim\nolimits_{\tau\rightarrow 0}p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)}}\right)=\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right).

Proof.

Set
X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)=p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)}}\right)-\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)

4.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}p^{-1}\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+\overline{\tau u}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau}}\right)=\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right).\qed

Proposition 8.3

We note some concrete applications of Propositions

and

.

•

If

and

are scalar fields then

,

If
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are vector fields then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}}}\right\rangle
,

•

If
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are vector fields then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}}}\right)\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{w}
,

\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}

9.1.
The gradient

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
V
be a vector space with an inner product (non-degenerate real
bilinear form)
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle-,-}}\right\rangle
, and consider a scalar
function
\phi:V\rightarrow K
. Then there is a unique function

\nabla\phi:V\rightarrow V,\qquad x\mapsto\nabla\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),u}}\right\rangle=\partial{}_{u}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

for all
u,x\in V
.

Proof.

(Existence). (i) If
u=0
then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle=0

and
\partial{}_{u}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=0
, so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle=\partial{}_{u}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

for all
v\in V.
(ii) If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle=0
for
all
v\in V
then
u=0
since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle-,-}}\right\rangle

is non-degenerate. Equivalently, if
u\neq 0
then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle\neq 0

for some
v\in V
, and using such a vector
v
we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\frac{\partial{}_{u}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle}v,u}}\right\rangle=\partial{}_{u}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right).

(Uniqueness). If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v,u}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v^{\prime},u}}\right\rangle

for all
u\in V
so that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle v-v^{\prime},u}}\right\rangle=0
for
all
u\in V
then
v=v^{\prime}
since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle-,-}}\right\rangle

is non-degenerate.
∎

We call
\nabla\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
the
gradient
of
\phi

at
x\in V
.

9.5.
The Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

and torsion revisited

By Definition
5.3

T_{uv}(x)=\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\Delta_{v}u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
,
and replacing the vectors

gives
T_{\boldsymbol{uv}}(x)=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

As an
Ansatz
, we define the

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

of
\boldsymbol{v}
along
\boldsymbol{u}
at

(9.7)

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial{}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right).

In view of (

),

depends on
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
, not only on
\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

and
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

By Proposition (

so it follows from (

On the other hand, this result can be used to define torsion in terms

We have thus recovered the usual definition of torsion by defining

However, this definition is somewhat unnatural, since it gives the

\boldsymbol{u}
,

\boldsymbol{v}
and
x
, whereas Definition

makes

depends only on
\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
,

\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
and
x
.

9.4
implies that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\delta{}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

for every
i,j=1,\ldots,n
since each
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
is a
constant vector field. This equality shows that the derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

carries information about curvature, similar to
\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

(and
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
), motivating the term ”connection”.

By Proposition
8.9
, Corollary
8.10
and other
results in this article, (1) – (2) below hold when interpreting

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
as
\nabla{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
,

\triangledown{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
,
\delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

or
\partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
, and (3) holds at least
when interpreted as
\nabla\delta{}_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta{}_{u}\phi}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)

or
\triangledown\partial_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\triangledown{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial{}_{u}\phi}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.

(1)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}=\phi\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}

for any scalar function
\phi
(see Section
7.5
).

(2)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}}\right)=\lambda\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}

for any
\lambda\in K
(see Section
7.5
).

(3)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\phi}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}

for any scalar function
\phi
.

Conversely, it has been shown that there is only one function
\nabla:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mapsto\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

satisfying these conditions, so a connection can also be defined as
a function
\nabla:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}}}\right)\mapsto\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

satisfying (1) – (3). This is the so-called Koszul connection.

Note, though, that we cannot equate the Koszul connection
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

with the pseudo-derivative
\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

or the corresponding affine connection
\boldsymbol{C}
(Definition

4.5
) since we do not have
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}=\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

unless
\boldsymbol{v}
is a constant vector field so that
\delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}=0
.
This is related to the fact that
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)

for all
x
, meaning that
\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}

does not depend on
\boldsymbol{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)
or
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\prime}}}\right)

for any
x^{\prime}\neq x
. In fact, we do not need the notion of a vector
field to define a connection and specify the curvature of a pointwise
affine space since we can use
\Delta_{u}v
instead of
\Delta{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
,

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{u}}}\boldsymbol{v}
etc.

10.
Final remarks

For practical purposes, the crucial innovation in the approach to
differential geometry suggested in this article is the pseudo-derivative

\Delta_{u}v
. As suggested by the similarity of relation (
1.2
),

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\sim_{\overline{C}}\>\overline{u}^{i}\frac{\partial\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{k}}{\partial\overline{x}^{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+\overline{u}^{i}\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),

and equation (
9.3
),

\displaystyle\triangledown{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\partial{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)+\Delta{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right),

\Delta_{u}v
is a coordinate-free counterpart to
u^{i}v^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
.
Simultaneously,
\Delta_{u}v
is closely related to the Koszul connection

\nabla{}_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
as discussed in Section
9.6
.
Note that
\Delta_{u}v
allows simple, natural definitions of several
central notions because
\Delta_{u}v
measures the curvature of a
space directly, not indirectly via its effect on vector fields on
that space. For example, torsion is defined by
T_{uv}=\Delta_{u}v-\Delta_{v}u

rather than the usual
T_{\boldsymbol{uv}}=\nabla{}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}-\nabla{}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
.

From a more abstract point of view, pointwise affine spaces generalize
affine spaces, while manifolds generalize tuple spaces such as
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Accordingly, the two approaches employ different strategies to specify
curvature and derivatives.

Specifically, for each point
p
of a manifold
M
there is a vector
space
T_{p}M
, the tangent space at
p
. All tangent spaces for
a particular manifold are isomorphic, but not canonically isomorphic;
instead we equip
M
with a choice of isomorphisms
\mathscr{I}{}_{\!pp^{\prime}}:T_{p}M\rightarrow T_{p^{\prime}}M

for certain pairs of points
p,p^{\prime}
. While we cannot subtract vectors
in different vector spaces, the difference

\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\mathscr{I}{}_{\!pp^{\prime}}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)

exists for any vector field
\boldsymbol{v}
provided that
\mathscr{I}{}_{\!pp^{\prime}}

exists, and we can use expressions of this form to define a derivative

\nabla_{u}\boldsymbol{v}
of
\boldsymbol{v}
when a choice of

\mathscr{I}{}_{\!pp^{\prime}}
has been made for each point pair
p,p^{\prime}

such that
p^{\prime}
is sufficiently close to
p
. Such derivatives can
be used to specify the curvature of a manifold.

For pointwise affine spaces, there is a single group
V
that acts
on the point space
A
. For each point, there is now a particular
way in which
V
acts on
A
rather than a particular tangent space.
In this case,
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{\prime}}}\right)

belong to the same vector space, isomorphic, in possibly different
ways for different points of
A
, to the vector space of translations
on
A
. Thus, the difference
\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)-\boldsymbol{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{\prime}}}\right)

always exists and can be used to define derivatives, so constructions
such as tangent spaces, tangent bundles and sections of tangent bundles
are redundant in this approach. For example, a vector field is defined
simply as a function on a point space with values in a fixed vector
space. Moreover, an automorphism on
V
of the form

v\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{\prime}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)

can be used to define
\Delta_{u}v\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and specify the
affine curvature at
p
of a pointwise affine space.

The simplicity of pointwise affine spaces is partly a consequence
of reduced scope and generality rather than of simplification of unnecessarily
cumbersome definitions. We obviously obtain a more complicated space
by adding a metric to a pointwise affine space, and we nave not considered
general tensors. Moreover, a pointwise affine space is a special,
uncomplicated manifold. A general manifold has a global ”shape”
which cannot be changed by changing its curvature locally: it may
be a surface, a sphere, a torus etc. A pointwise affine space is a
manifold with a trivial shape. To obtain a manifold with a non-trivial
shape such as a sphere we can ”glue together” more than one trivial-shape
manifolds.

Generalizing the notion of a pointwise affine space with the definition
of a manifold or a Cartan geometry as a model, we first note that
an open subset of a topological space can be equipped with the structure
of a pointwise affine space. Given a vector space
V
and a point
space
A
with a suitable topology, we can thus assign to every
p\in A

a pointwise affine system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,U_{p},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{p}}}\right)
,
where
U_{p}
is an open neighborhood of
p
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{p}

is an affine action field on
U_{p}
. Thus, for every
p\in A
it
is possible to obtain a pseudo-derivative or derivative at
p
by
using
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{p}
. It is not immediately clear,
though, what compatibility conditions, if any, that would have to
be imposed on the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{a}}_{p}\mid p\in A}}\right\}

to make a definition along these lines work. This problem will be
left open here.

Anyhow, while a pointwise affine space corresponds only to a special
manifold which can be covered by a single coordinate chart, initially
regarding curved spaces as pointwise affine spaces may make them easier
to understand. Also, in many applications the general theory is not
needed.
