Nontraumatic shoulder pain in a 23-year-old man with primary synovial chondromatosis
Authors: Jiebo Chen, Jinzhong Zhao, Xueying Zhang
Published: 2026

A 23-year-old man was referred to orthopedics with a 3-year history of progressive, nontraumatic right shoulder pain. He said he had no radiating pain, fever, or other joint involvement. He was otherwise healthy. Physical examination revealed right shoulder swelling and reduced range of motion in all planes, with normal sensation and strength in the limb. Blood work showed normal inflammatory markers and negative rheumatoid markers (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251762/tab-related-content). Radiography revealed numerous calcified bodies surrounding the glenohumeral joint (Figure 1A and Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251762/tab-related-content). Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed multiple loose bodies with specific ring-shaped enhancement surfaces (Figure 1B). We diagnosed Reichel syndrome, or primary synovial chondromatosis. The patient underwent arthroscopic synovectomy and removal of loose bodies. Histopathologic examination showed synovial tissue, clustered chondrocytes, and no malignancy. At 6 months’ follow-up, he had fully recovered, aside from a slightly decreased range of motion. Imaging showed no recurrence of radiopaque lesions.

(A) Anteroposterior radiograph and (B) sagittal plane of enhanced magnetic resonance imaging scan of the right shoulder of a 23-year-old man, showing multiple calcified intra-articular loose bodies (white arrows).

Shoulder pain accounts for 1.3% of all general practice consultations, the third most prevalent musculoskeletal complaint after spine and knee complaints.1 In young adults, the primary differential diagnosis of chronic, nontraumatic shoulder pain includes overuse (tendinitis or bursitis), impingement, biceps and labral pathology, synovial pathology, and cervical radiculopathy.1,2

Plain radiography is the first-line investigation after history and physical examination.1 Clinical suspicion of an inflammatory or rheumatoid cause requires targeted blood testing. Ultrasonography, computed tomography, or MRI may be required to confirm a diagnosis.1,2

Primary synovial chondromatosis is rare and occurs primarily in males aged 20 to 40 years.2 Plain radiographs show nodular calcification in 54% of acute-, 88% of transitional-, and 100% of mature-phase chondromatosis.3 Substantial interindividual variability exists in disease progression. Surgical management results in nearly complete symptom resolution, although 15% to 25% of patients have recurrence.2 Chronic or recurrent disease may lead to asymmetric joint space narrowing, osteoarthritis, or malignant transformation to secondary synovial chondrosarcoma (1% to 10%).2
