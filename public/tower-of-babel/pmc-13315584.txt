Independent roles of autophagy and apoptosis in post-apocrine-secretion cell death of salivary glands during Drosophila metamorphosis
Authors: Lucia Mentelová, Denisa Beňová-Liszeková, Milan Beňo, Bruce A. Chase, Robert Farkaš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Drosophila larval salivary glands (SGs) serve as an outstanding model to understand developmental transitions in secretory epithelial cells. They have been instrumental in understanding the regulatory mechanisms that lead to the exocytosis of SG glue (Sgs) just prior to pupariation during the late larval period, and gaining insights into apocrine secretion (AS), a non-canonical, non-vesicular transport and secretion, which occurs later in the late prepupal period to provide the immunoprotective exuvial fluid that surrounds the pupae during its metamorphosis into an adult fly. AS is the SGs major, evolutionarily conserved secretory function, as unlike Sgs exocytosis, AS is found across the Diptera. Since the SGs undergo hormonally controlled programmed cell death (PCD) shortly after they complete AS, a key question is how the regulation of AS and PCD are related. To address this, it is essential to clarify the mechanism of PCD. Earlier proposals that it occurs via autophagic cell death (ACD) or autophagy dependent cell death (ADCD) did not incorporate or explain any of the dynamic post-Sgs cellular processes that are associated with AS. Here we present three lines of evidence that the SGs die using an apoptosis-prone mechanism during which the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) becomes fragmented into vesicles that wholly fill the cytoplasm. First, although a low level of autophagy can be seen in the SGs, inducing autophagy with rapamycin does not accelerate PCD. Second, while the genetic removal of Atg1 or RNAi silencing of the Atg3, Atg5, Atg6, Atg7, and Atg12 genes inhibits the development of autophagy, this does not prevent vesiculation and subsequent SG-cell death. Third, the Drosophila SGs exhibit externalization of phosphatidylserine and attract phagocytic macrophages, both of which are pivotal hallmarks of apoptosis. Therefore, we propose that the entire Drosophila SG organ dies using a non-autophagic mechanism which utilizes key features of the apoptotic pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46472-3.

Introduction

In all metazoans programmed cell death (PCD) is a genetically encoded form of cell suicide that results in the orderly death of excessive, damaged or dangerous cells during normal development and adult life and thereby contributes to the maintenance of body homeostasis1–6. In addition, the inappropriate regulation of cell death is associated with diseases of very different etiologies including cancer, AIDS, neurodegeneration and ischemic stroke7–11. Because PCD is an active, gene-directed mechanism, it is amenable for therapeutic targeting once there is knowledge of the detailed, cell-specific mechanisms by which particular types of cells undergo suicide.

Although there are numerous minor variants, the major form of PCD recognizable based on morphological criteria, is apoptosis. During typical apoptosis there is an initial condensation of the nuclear chromatin accompanied by nucleosomal fragmentation of DNA, nuclear blebbing and pyknosis, externalization of phosphatidylserine, activation of caspases, and the formation of apoptotic bodies followed by their phagocytosis, mostly by macrophages12–14. By contrast, cells undergoing so-called autophagic cell death (ACD) or autophagy dependent cell death (ADCD) are thought to digest themselves by forming autophagosomes that transport dying cell cargo to the lysosome15–17. By definition, ACD is independent of caspase activity, does not involve the phagocytic engulfment of apoptotic bodies, and is morphologically distinct from apoptosis18,19. Indeed, autophagy is regarded as a process where there is bulk catabolic degradation of cytoplasmic components by the lysosomal/vacuolar system that allows for the efficient turnover and recycling of proteins or redundant organelles. It is thought to have a strong pro-survival role, allowing for survival during starvation or other adverse conditions20–23. During autophagy, the cytoplasm is engulfed within double-membraned structures known as autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes, resulting in the degradation of their cytoplasmic cargo24–26.

The plethora of molecular genetic tools available in Drosophila melanogaster have provided an ideal model to investigate the mechanisms underlying developmentally controlled cell death in multicellular organisms. In Drosophila, metamorphosis is controlled by a series of pulses of the steroid hormone ecdysone27,28. At the end of larval development, an ecdysone pulse signals the onset of metamorphosis, initiating puparium formation and the prepupal stage. About 11–12 h after this, another ecdysone pulse signals the prepupal-pupal transition. The response to these sequential ecdysone pulses is to destroy obsolete larval tissues in a stage-specific manner. This occurs prior to the transformation of the larva into an immature fly during the pupal period, when adult tissues and structures will develop from small clusters of progenitor cells29. Different larval tissues initiate cell death in response to the different pulses of ecdysone. The late larval pulse of ecdysone triggers the initiation of cell death in the larval midgut at puparium formation, while the prepupal pulse of ecdysone triggers the initiation of cell death in the salivary glands about 2–4 h after pupation30–32. Since PCD is steroid-hormone induced, studying PCD in the larval SGs provides a compelling model for defining the molecular mechanisms underlying steroid-regulated senescence.

The larval Drosophila SGs are composed of a terminally differentiated and polarized secretory epithelium and so have served as an excellent model system to address cellular mechanisms in secretory epithelia. As in mammals, they serve as an epithelial barrier, and they undergo fast switching of developmental programs that include exocytosis of the glue, endocytic vacuolation, membrane consolidation, and apocrine secretion (AS). AS, which includes transudation and calcium oxalate excretion, appears to be a much more widespread externalization pathway than has been so far appreciated. It is used by more than 10 different types of cells/tissues in humans and is a highly evolutionarily conserved mechanism characteristic of only eukaryotic metazoans (see e.g.33). Currently, the Drosophila SGs are the only model system that is accessible for elucidating the molecular mechanism underlying this type of non-canonical secretion. SGs are also an ideal model to study the evolution of the salivary and labial glands, which phylogenetically are originally head-borne glands in the especially older phylogenetic groups of insects, and discover what underlies the developmental switch for the transformation of labial glands to SGs.

Most germane to this work is that cell-death signaling has been studied in the Drosophila SGs. Four cell-death activators have been identified: reaper (rpr), head involution defective (hid), grim and sickle (skl)33–37. Removal of first three genes by use of the Df(3L)H99 deletion effectively blocks PCD in Drosophila34. All four genes are strongly expressed during the execution phase of the SG death program, two (rpr and hid) very strongly30,31. PCD in the SGs can be inhibited by the ectopic expression of either the p35 baculoviral antiapoptotic protein or diap1 and diap2 inhibitors of apoptosis30,38,39, implicating a role for caspases in the death response31,40,41. This is consistent with the induction by ecdysone of the Dronc caspase in both the larval midgut and the SGs before the onset of cell death42,43. Thus, the larval midgut and SGs appear to be destroyed by a steroid-triggered PCD pathway that has hallmark features of apoptosis.

Interestingly, autophagosomes also appear in both the salivary glands and the midgut, and autophagy-related genes (Atg) are expressed in these tissues. While these might be expected to serve a pro-survival or another active role in cell metabolism, their temporal proximity to PCD led to the proposal that these polytenized tissues die via a non-apoptotic, ACD mechanism44–50. However, several independent data sets have suggested that autophagy may not be the crucial process underlying PCD, at least in the prepupal/pupal SGs31,51,52. To provide a context for reconciling these alternative views of the mechanism of PCD, it is useful to consider the events that transpire following the Sgs, which occurs at the end of the third larval instar just prior to pupariation, until the time of SG PCD in the late prepupal period.

Following the Sgs secretion, dynamic processes within the SGs lead them to become heavily loaded with immunocompetent proteins and numerous enzymes. These are released into the SG lumen via a massive AS roughly 15 h after Sgs-glue secretion, producing a luminal fluid that will later be released into the space between the metamorphosing pupae and its case to serve as the exuvial fluid53–56. This fluid provides innate immunity to the organism during pupal development57. AS is therefore a key event that occurs just prior to PCD in the SGs. Recently, we established that it is the main, evolutionary conserved, secretory function of the Drosophila larval SGs58. This contrasts to the long-held assumption that their main function was the exocytosis of Sgs-glue.

Starting immediately after pupariation and continuing for 6 to 7 h, the SGs undergo heavy vacuolation due to ambipolar endocytosis59. Then, AS into the SG lumen is initiated and continues for about 2–3 h (8 to 10 h APF)53. During AS, many basally endocytosed proteins are apically rereleased in a process of transudation55. We recently found that even autophagosomes are actively released during AS. AS is followed by an abrupt excretion of calcium oxalate and other organic compounds that appear to contribute to formation of exuvial fluid, thus allowing the SG to act as a labial gland60. Between the 14th and 15th hour APF, the SG develops signs of PCD and becomes completely histolyzed by 16 h APF. Hence the developmental transition from AS activity to the initiation of PCD is very short, only 3 to 4 h. This suggests that the regulation of PCD may be linked to AS more than the much earlier release of the Sgs. Since addressing this regulation requires elucidation of the mechanism of PCD, we decided to reappraise the role of “autophagic cell death” in SG PCD.

This is also an important issue because understanding PCD in this tissue has implications for thinking about the mechanisms of PCD in general, including in humans, and in cancer61–66. Furthermore, the Drosophila SGs have been and can be invaluable to understand the molecular events that underlie several key developmental and cellular processes. These include tubulogenesis, a principal morphogenetic event in the formation of both invertebrate and mammalian SGs67–73, and vacuolation, which occurs robustly following Sgs-glue exocytosis due to endocytosis from both basal and apical poles, to support anabolic activities by providing ferrous iron to mitochondria54,59. The SGs can also serve as a model to investigate the regulation of AS33,56, starting with insights from the chromosomal puffing patterns, that as observed long ago74–80, vary during the late prepupal and early pupal periods so may identify genes that are differentially expressed during AS and PCD. Hence, the Drosophila SGs are emerging as an excellent model system for understanding developmental transitions in a secretory epithelium that moves from exocytosis at an early stage to apocrine secretion (via vacuolation) at a later stage and then to PCD as a final developmental program.

When SG ACD models were proposed, they did not consider AS, calcium oxalate formation and extrusion, or transport of the exuvial fluid, all of which occur after Sgs exocytosis in prepupal and early pupal SGs and just prior to PCD. Therefore, we sought to gain insights into autophagic processes and subsequent PCD. We used rapamycin to induce autophagy and genetic tools to block autophagy to test the hypothesis that while autophagy requires the function of individual autophagy genes (Atg genes), AS and its associated processes, as well as PCD do not.

Since the discovery and definition of apoptosis, ultrastructural analyses using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) remains one of the most validated methods to unambiguously recognize the process by which a cell dies and to characterize the type of PCD81–85. Therefore, we asked whether hallmark morphological criteria of PCD, using TEM and additional techniques, were affected by eliminating the function of the Atg genes. We found that induction of autophagy with rapamycin does not lead to accelerated cell death, and that, though the genetic removal of Atg1 function or the RNAi silencing of the Atg3, Atg5, Atg6, Atg7, and Atg12 genes inhibits the development of autophagy, these treatments do not prevent the vesiculation of the SGs and their subsequent PCD. Furthermore, we show that Drosophila SG cells exhibit externalization of phosphatidylserine and attract phagocytic macrophages, both pivotal hallmarks of apoptosis. These findings provide evidence that the Drosophila SG, as an entire organ, can die in the absence of the main Atg genes using a non-autophagic mechanism that utilizes key features of the apoptotic pathway.

Results

Salivary glands display ER vesiculation immediately prior to cell death

As a prerequisite for defining the molecular nature of the of cell death program in the Drosophila SGs we undertook a detailed characterization of its morphological features, searching for characteristics that could provide for a clear, tissue-specific definition of the cell suicide process. SGs from wild-type animals were dissected hourly APF and examined using electron microscopy. SGs were found as late as 16 h APF, at the stage corresponding to about 3 h after pupation. At later stages no SGs were present in the pupal body. We initiated a detailed ultrastructural documentation of the cell death program 2 h earlier, i.e., in 14 h prepupae (Figs. 1a–f, and S3a). At this time, SG cytoplasm containing occasional vesicles becomes filled with reticular whorls (Fig. 1a) that in cross-section appear as concentric circles of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Fig. 1b). The cytoplasm of SGs dissected from 16 h APF old animals is filled with numerous small vesicles, and only remnants of mitochondria or granules, but no other organelles, can be identified. We consider this morphological characteristic to mark the very last stage of SG cell death (Fig. 1c). To monitor the development of the death process between 15 and 16 h we examined the SGs in 20 min intervals. This revealed a sequential, gradual disintegration of ER. At the beginning of the last hour, the ER becomes dilated and forms a few small vesicles garlanded with ribosomes (Fig. 1d). After 20 min, the typical lamellae of the ER disappear, previously small vesicles become larger and some of them become free of ribosomes (Fig. 1e). After an additional 20 min, the cytoplasm becomes filled with numerous vesicles and is indistinguishable from structures we observed in 16 h old SGs (Fig. 1f). Quantification of the dramatic increase in vesicles and decrease in undegraded ER following puparium formation is shown in Fig. 2, Section I (discussed further below) and Supplemental Fig. S1a. To avoid technical errors and misinterpretation of these observations, we sought to verify the authenticity of this cell death process by asking whether similar observations would be found when the caspase inducers encoded by the rpr, hid, grim and skl genes were ectopically activated in earlier developmental stages in otherwise wild-type animals. We examined animals at pupariation, i.e. 16 h prior to the time when SGs normally die, that had ectopically driven expression of the rpr, hid, grim or skl genes. As illustrated in Fig. 1g–j, within 3 h, all four “death genes” induced fast cell suicide of the early prepupal SGs. In all cases, we observed the same outcome: massive vesiculation of the ER system. Secondly, we verified the role of Drosophila caspases in this process by ectopically driving expression of the proximal caspases encoded by the dronc and dredd genes. After 2 h, this too led to massive vesiculation of the ER. However, in addition to what we observed when the rpr, hid, grim and skl genes were ectopically activated, both proximal caspases induced detachment of the normally hexagonal SG cells. They adopted a round, ball-like shape that markedly resembled the apoptotic bodies of diploid cells (Fig. 1k, l). We did not observe the formation or increased occurrence of autophagosomes. Quantification of vesicles under these conditions is shown in Supplemental Fig. S1b.

To reappraise the potential role of an autophagic process in the cell death program of the SGs, we undertook a detailed morphometric analysis of cytoplasmic organelles during the entire prepupal period. We compared and evaluated the relative occurrence and number of autophagic vacuoles, mitochondria, ER areas, lipid droplets and vacuoles in white 0-h prepupae, 7.5-h and 15-h prepupae. We also counted electron-transparent vesicles, but only in 15-h old prepupae, as they were not present prior to this period. As documented in Fig. 2a, the number of autophagosomes, mitochondria and ER, and the area covered by them becomes significantly diminished. The number of autophagic figures peaks in the 0-h prepupae, reaching 2% of the total ultrastructurally evaluated area, drops to 0.78% in the 7.5-h old prepupae, and becomes reduced to 0.45% in the 15-h old prepupae. The rough ER has the largest area of all the evaluated structures, occupying 90% of evaluated areas in the 0-h prepupae, 83% in the 7.5-h prepupae, and dropping to 15% in 15-h old animals. Interestingly, the area of lipid droplets reaches 3.9% in the 0-h prepupae, grows threefold in the mid-prepupa and drops again to 3.8% in the 15-h prepupae. Strikingly, the only structures showing continuous growth are vesicles, which increase from 1.5% in the 0-h prepupae to 27% in the 15-h old prepupae. In 15-h old prepupae, we found that, suddenly and in contrast to what we observed in the 7.5-h old prepupae, more than 28% of the cytoplasm was now filled with small vesicles. This ultrastructural analysis carefully distinguished between autophagosomes, which are marked by a double-walled membrane, endosomal vacuoles associated with multivesicular bodies, like those whose abundance increases much earlier following Sgs exocytosis54,59, and the single-membrane vesicles that appear to derive from the ER as their abundance increases dramatically at the same time that ER-area decreases. These occupy most of the cytoplasm less than 1 h later.

Autophagy is known to play a crucial role in the fat body, where it is used to generate immediate energy for growth and tissue proliferation during non-mobile, non-feeding metamorphic phase from stores deposited during larval feeding. To compare our observations in the SGs to a well-established tissue where autophagy is known to play a crucial role, we assessed autophagy in the fat body during Drosophila late larval development. As shown in Fig. 2 (panel II. second row of graphs), autophagy in the fat body, as assessed by the area occupied by autophagosomes, increases over the 48-h period of the last instar whereas the area occupied by mitochondria and ER gradually decrease from 1.5 to 0.2%, and 7.6 to 0.5%, respectively. Over the same timeframe the presence of multivesicular bodies and protein granules grow substantially from 0 to 4% and from 0 to 8.9%, respectively. Despite increased autophagy, fat body cells remain viable and are actively engaged in the production of protein granules. Hence, in this tissue even strong and widespread autophagy has a pro-survival function. In summary, these data compromise the role of autophagy in the PCD of the SGs. They also indicate that the ER may play a much more important role in PCD than previously appreciated.

Removing/silencing pivotal autophagy genes do not prevent cell death program

The above results challenged us to reappraise the role of autophagy in Drosophila SG PCD in more detail. First, we manipulated a key nutrient-associated regulator of autophagy, the target of rapamycin (Tor)86, an evolutionary conserved protein kinase that interacts specifically with the rapamycin receptor prolyl isomerase termed FKBP12 (small ubiquitous FK506-binding protein). It has been well established in yeast and mammalian systems that inactivation of Tor by mutation or by treatment with the lipophilic macrolide drug rapamycin induces autophagy despite the presence of ample nutrients87–90. Hence, treatment with rapamycin provides a means for us to induce autophagy prior to when PCD would normally occur and ask if this also leads to early SG PCD. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, and quantified in Supplemental Fig. S2 (for controls, see Supplemental Fig. S3), treatment of early- or mid-prepupal Drosophila salivary glands with rapamycin for 4 h induces substantial autophagy in their cytoplasm but does not stimulate or accelerate the appearance of features of cell death even after prolonged exposure (8 h to 10 h in the case of early prepupal glands). In contrast, overexpression of wild-type Tor using a UAS-tor transgene, which is expected to prevent the development of autophagic structures, clearly did not prevent normal, timely SG PCD via vesiculation (compare Fig. 3c to similar observations in the wild-type in Fig. 1c–f; Supplemental Fig. S2). Though control glands had more vesicles than the UAS-Tor animals (Supplemental Fig. S2), they nonetheless had abundant vesicles consistent with PCD via vesiculation. Differences in the quantity of vesicles likely reflect differences in the developmental rate of UAS-Tor animals, as vesicle formation increases very rapidly and then drops very rapidly in the period 15–16 h APF (Fig. S3a). We staged animals precisely based on developmental timing in the wild type. If UAS-Tor animals have somewhat slower developmental rates, fixation at 16 h APF may capture the process prior to its peak and so fewer vesicles would be observed. To confirm our inference that autophagy and PCD seem to be independent processes and address the possibility that the timing of PCD is independent of the early induction of autophagy, we investigated whether the normal program of cell death in the late prepupae and early pupae would be affected by silencing individual autophagic functions in a temporally specific manner. To this end, we employed an Atg1 mutation and a series of UAS-driven RNA interference (RNAi) transgenes for five Drosophila autophagy (Atg) genes, specifically targeting the Atg3, Atg5, Atg6, Atg7 and Atg12 genes. To achieve fully effective silencing, either tissue-specific Sgs3-Gal4 or strong hs-Gal4 drivers were used. For hs-Gal4 two 45-min, 35 °C heat shocks (separated by a 2-h resting period), were administered. Early and late autophagosomes were absent in knockdowns for individual Atg genes (Supplemental Fig. S4), validating the effectiveness of the individual knockdowns in preventing the formation of autophagosomes.

First, we examined animals that completely lack Atg1 function (Atg1 hemizygous mutants, Atg1Δ3D/Df(3L)Bc10) and which exhibit pupal lethality. Among animals dying as pupae, 17% had SGs persisting more than 24 h after pupariation. This posed a very interesting question: why do these SGs persist past the normal time of PCD ? A closer morphological examination addressed this while providing insight into the function of autophagy in SG PCD. As shown in Fig. 3d, the SGs of Atg1 mutants die in 16 h old animals, the same stage as those of wild type, and they develop identical vesiculation. However, in contrast to wild type animals, the SGs, as assessed by a trypan blue dye exclusions test, persist in pupa as dead tissue for another 8 to 12 h (Fig. 4a–h, Supplemental Fig. S5). This suggests that autophagy is used in the process by which histolytic tissue is cleared. The Atg3 gene is a Drosophila homologue of yeast and mammalian Aut1 that encodes an authentic E2 enzyme for multiple substrates (GATE-16, GABARAP, and MAP-LC3) and facilitates conjugation of the Atg12 and Atg5 proteins in the early phases of autophagosome formation91. RNAi-mediated silencing the Atg3 function using a UAS-Atg3RNAi transgene during the late larval and/or early prepupal period resulted in the disappearance of young autophagosomes but did not prevent a normal program of cell death with typical vesiculation of the cytoplasm in the early pupae (Fig. 3e).

The Atg5 gene codes for a nascent autophagic membrane protein involved in early autophagosome formation92,93. When Atg5 function was removed during the late larval and/or early prepupal period by RNAi through activation of the expression of a UAS-Atg5RNAi transgene, de novo initiation of autophagosome assembly was prevented: only rare, mature and old autophagolysosomes were seen. However, this treatment had no effect on the normal timing of the SG cell-death program in the early pupae, and we observed the typical vesiculation of cytoplasm (Fig. 3f).

The Atg6 gene is a homologue of yeast Vps30 that functions in autophagosome formation and is essential for the pathway that selects cytoplasmic cargo that will be targeted to the vacuole94–96. As we found for Atg5, silencing of Atg6 via the expression of a UAS-Atg6RNAi transgene during the late larval and/or early prepupal period prevented the de novo initiation of autophagosome assembly, so that only rare, mature and old autophagolysosomes were seen—early autophagic vacuoles completely disappeared. Silencing Atg6 also had no discernable effect on either the timing or morphology of the SG cell-death program in the early pupae (Fig. 3g).

The Atg7 gene is a homologue of the yeast gene Cvt2 that encodes a protein-activating enzyme essential in the cytoplasm-to-vacuole targeting process and acts on multiple protein substrates essential for autophagy including Atg12, GATE-16, GABARAP, and MAP-LC397,98. As would be expected, removing Atg7 function by expressing a UAS-Apg7RNAi transgene during the late larval and/or early prepupal period blocked the formation of autophagosomes. However, this did not interfere with the normal cell-death program of SG cells (Fig. 3h).

The Atg12 gene is the Drosophila orthologue of the yeast and mammalian Atg12 genes and codes for a small ubiquitin-like protein that becomes covalently attached to Atg5, to facilitate Atg8 (LC3) processing99–101. Here too we found that silencing Atg12 function by expressing an Atg12RNAi transgene during the late larval and/or early prepupal period resulted in the disappearance of young autophagosomes and an apparent reduction in the numbers of old autophagosomes and autophagolysosomes, but that it did not prevent the normal program of cell death in the early pupae and the associated vesiculation of the cytoplasm (Fig. 3i). We did not observe an extended persistence of SGs in any Atg knockdowns. Quantification of vesicles observed in the absence of Atg1 function and following knockdown of other Atg functions is shown in Supplemental Fig. S2. Some lines differed from controls in vesicle number, though not the quality of the vesiculation process. As for the quantitation of vesicles in UAS-Tor animals, this difference likely reflects capturing the vesiculation process prior to its peak due to small differences in the timing of vesiculation between cells or slower developmental rates in some lines.

In conclusion, these data indicate that autophagy does not appear to be required for the commitment, regulation or execution of the steroid-hormone triggered PCD of the SGs during Drosophila metamorphosis. Rather than being involved in cell death, per se, autophagy appears to be used in the process of self-clearance following PCD, the cleaning up and removal of dead tissue.

The final phases of SG cell death are associated with extracellular exposure of phosphatidylserine and evoke phagocytosis by macrophages

These residues act to attract macrophages or similar phagocytic cells to facilitate the removal of cell corpses and remnants102–105. This hallmark is also considered to be an important demarcating feature that differentiates apoptosis from ACD. In the latter process, the cell does not need to present “eat me” ligands as it is eaten from the inside by its own autophagolytic process18,106. We therefore investigated whether this key feature of apoptotic cell death is also found in the Drosophila SG model. Discussion

Vesiculation of the ER without autophagy as a means of programmed cell death

These experiments provide evidence that the PCD in the Drosophila salivary glands is a non-autophagic process that displays many of the typical features of apoptosis (see timeline in Supplemental Fig. S7). Neither the induction of intense autophagy nor the inhibition of autophagosome formation influenced any aspect of the cell death program, demonstrating that autophagy and this apoptotic process utilize independent signaling pathways that must coexist at the same time in these cells. In different insect orders ecdysone does appear to be general inducer of autophagy in many, mainly polyploid tissues15,16,108,109, including the larval and prepupal SGs. This raises the possibility that earlier data on hormonally induced PCD in Drosophila melanogaster may have been misinterpreted as ACD43–50. The biological function of autophagy is well illustrated by considering its role in the fat body, the major tissue where intense autophagy occurs. Rather than resulting in cell death110,111, autophagy is used there to mobilize nutrient reserves in the pupa to fulfill the energy demands of adult morphogenesis during a developmental stage that is incapable of feeding109,112,113. Consistent with this role, the level of autophagy in the SGs is very low in comparison to that seen in the fat body or midgut. In addition, fat body cells, despite a high level of autophagy, persist during entire metamorphosis and become eliminated by apoptosis two days after eclosion of an adult114–116. In the SGs, we observed an increased but still low level of autophagy prior to pupariation. However, it was at its lowest level during the prepupal-pupal transition when PCD is being activated. Moreover, although ACD appears to provide a compensatory mechanism for cell suicide when apoptosis players such as the caspases or Apaf1 are compromised117,118, we showed that in Apaf mutants which fail to undergo SG cell death, there is no increased activation of autophagy51. Thus, cell death and autophagy are unlinked in doomed Drosophila SG cells.

This study indicates that the Drosophila SGs employ the disintegration of the ER via vesiculation to execute their suicide program. A biological rationale for why this and not an ACD mechanism underlies PCD follows from considering that the larval SGs are a terminally differentiated tissue with two major secretory functions. The first is the exocytotic secretion of the Sgs glue during the last larval instar, during which more than 30% of SG protein mass is composed of Sgs proteins51,56,119–121. The second follows this and requires a series of dynamic events that culminate in the apocrine secretion of the SG cell contents into the SG lumen prior to release as exuvial fluid. Given these functions, it is not surprising that 90% of SG cytoplasmic organellar area prior to PCD, as judged by morphometric analysis, belongs to ER system. After the gland’s secretory functions are fulfilled, its cell’s most abundant organelle, the ER, is used to commit suicide in a fast and highly efficient manner. Delayed removal of dead SGs in Atg1− mutants argues for a role of autophagy in the “clean up” process and may indicate that effective phagocytosis of dead tissue by macrophages can require earlier cell autonomous activity of Atg gene machinery or its selected members.

Apoptosis underlies cell death in the Drosophila salivary glands

In Drosophila, independent observations have documented that the expression of the caspase inducers rpr, hid, grim and skl is a hallmark of apoptosis during embryonic and postembryonic development in many tissues, including the SGs30,31,34–37,46,47. However, there are no data on the involvement of these genes in autophagy. The same holds true for the activity of caspases involved in PCD. Because of the clear association of the rpr, hid, grim and skl activators as well as the dronc and dredd caspases with apoptosis, we compared the normal development of cell death features in SGs with those when each of these genes is ectopically expressed. The morphological phenotypes found in wild type animals were identical to those seen in animals following ectopic expression of rpr, hid, grim, skl, dronc and dredd. This provides strong evidence that the apoptotic machinery is a key player in executing the cell death program in the Drosophila SGs.

The clearance of dying cells is critical to tissue homeostasis and resolution of inflammatory lesions. In typical apoptosis phagocytic macrophages accomplish this. Hence, macrophage recognition of phosphatidylserine (PS) on the surface of an apoptotic cell is considered a clearance key event102,105,122–124. During apoptosis, phosphatidylserine, which is normally restricted to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, is exposed on the surface of apoptotic cells and has been suggested to act as an “eat-me” signal to trigger phagocytosis125. Our findings provide definitive evidence that both phosphatidylserine externalization and active phagocytosis are a normal part of SG PCD. In C. elegans and mammals, phosphatidylserine recognition is mediated by specific receptor, PSR-1, which regulates members of apoptotic signaling pathway containing CED-2, CED-5, CED-10, and CED-12126. In Drosophila there are several PS receptors including draper, Nimrod, snazarus, synaptotagmins 1 and 4127–132. At least draper, Nimrod and snazarus are known to be expressed in larval hemocytes133–135. These and the above discussed data indicate that apoptosis governs the process of ecdysone-triggered senescence in the obsolete larval SGs during Drosophila metamorphosis. In particular, our study shows that an apoptotic pathway can be used both to remove individual cells within SG tissue in an apoptotic context as it is usually understood as well as to completely and synchronously eliminate the entire SG organ. This opens a new line of research in apoptosis that can provide clues as to how apoptotic signals in the separated cells of an organ are integrated and communicated so that cell death is fully synchronized in an entire tissue or organ.

Understanding the relationship between autophagy, autophagic cell death and apoptosis

Strong ectopic overexpression of Atg1 in Drosophila fat body cells did not result in ACD, but to inhibition of cell growth followed by apoptotic cell death136. This led to the suggestion that, even in the excellent model for autophagy provided by the fat body, the ectopic induction of autophagy provides an alternative means to trigger apoptosis, rather than a distinct form of cell death. This also supports the view that autophagy plays a role in the process of cleaning up and removal of dead tissue rather than in cell death per se. As documented in Fig. 4h, autophagy serves to support the self-clearance of histolysed tissue rather than suicide itself. A similar role for the autophagy in relation to PCD has been previously suggested by several authors137–139.

Strikingly, the very first authors who observed signs of degradation or histolysis in the SGs of Drosophila or in closely related non-Drosophila cyclorrhaphous dipteran species characterized the destruction of the SGs as phagocytosis29,140,141. These early observations, in the context of our current understanding of morphological landmarks of cell death, more than strongly suggests an apoptotic type of cell death. Indeed, more advanced studies using TEM142–145 all described the presence of phagocytes or hemocytes on the basement membrane of degenerating SGs. Furthermore, more recent and independent studies addressing the metamorphic cell death of SG in Drosophila species, including D. arizonae and D. mulleri, concluded that their histolysis takes place by apoptosis146, and the role of autophagy, if any is secondary. It may be that the speed and dynamic nature of the developmental events that occur in the SGs after Sgs-glue expectoration and prior to PCD obscured the observations of apoptotic hallmarks by the authors of previous studies on ACD.

A separate, but key argument, that PCD in the SGs occurs by an apoptotic mechanism rather than by ACD can be made by considering the context of how SGs become developmentally committed. The prepupal or larval SGs are de facto embryonic SGs71,73,147. The SGs and many other larval organs are formed during embryogenesis and maintain their histogenetic origin and function throughout the larval period. These polytenized tissues grow by expanding their volume, not by mitotic cycles as in diploid tissues. Furthermore, larval tissues become committed to their larval function during embryogenesis and retain this commitment until their destruction by PCD in the prepupal/pupal period. Multiple authors have shown that mutants whose SGs undergo earlier, embryonic PCD use an apoptotic mechanism. Fox et al.148 when studying the SG-specific ER proteins PH4αSG1 and PH4αSG2 in homozygous sageko embryos, observed SGs undergoing apoptosis. High-magnification images revealed punctate staining of PH4αSG1 and PH4αSG2 proteins in large macrophage-like cells documenting the phagocytic engulfment of SG‘s apoptotic bodies. In addition, consistent with the apoptotic death observed at these stages, the proapoptotic genes reaper (rpr) and head involution defective (hid) were expressed to high levels in the sage mutants but were undetectable in wild type SGs. Similar observations of embryonic SG apoptosis were made independently for mutations in the gene sens149. This prompts the question why embryonic SGs would switch their inherent (original) apoptotic program to autophagic PCD during late larval or prepupal development. It seems more reasonable for them to maintain their developmental commitment until histolysis, as switching commitments would require complicated and energy-costly reprogramming.

In this context, we previously provided clear evidence that the metamorphic PCD of Drosophila SGs requires the expression of apoptosis inducers such as rpr, hid, grim and skl in the late prepupal period which is consistent with an apoptotic mechanism of cell death31. At the the same time we found that ectopic expression of antiapoptotic baculoviral protein p35 or the treatment with synthetic caspase inhibitors (YVAD-CHO, Z-VAD-FMK and DEVD-CHO) very efficiently prevented cell death in the SGs—they survived at least 12 to 14 h past 16 h APF31. Moreover, we then showed52 and it was indepedently confirmed150 that PCD in the prepupal SGs requires an active form of caspase 3 and the activation of apoptosome, a key supramolecular structure involved in the early phases of apoptotic execution. The early effector caspase Dronc is a part of the apoptosome, and as such is essential for the implementation of cell death via the apoptotic machinery52. Interestingly, Berry and Baehrecke151 also found caspase-3 to be active during the process they call ACD in SGs. Later, these authors recognized that apoptotic caspases are also required for destruction of the SG152, and subsequently Liu and Levine153 offered an explanation for this observation that autophagy is merely an innocent bystander or a well-intentioned “Good Samaritan” trying to prevent inevitable cellular demise. While this may not fully capture, and we may not yet fully appreciate the role of autophagy near the time of PCD, all of these findings and observations strongly argues for an apoptotic, not autophagic, cell death mechanism in the early pupal SGs of Drosophila.

Autophagy was initially discovered and has since been described primarily as a life-saving mechanism, protecting cells from starvation154–156, and not as the basis of a cell death program. Later, autophagy was proposed to also play a role protecting cells from the formation of aggregates of misfolded proteins, organelle damage and microbes21,157. The autophagy that occurs in larval (prepupal) SGs, and also in many other tissues and organs such as fat body, muscles, and the CNS110,111,113,158,159 is a continuous slow process, whereas apoptosis as seen in this work and our previous papers is a relatively fast process that normally takes no more than 2–3 h. It is even faster under ectopic expression of rpr, hid, grim and skl, or caspases, where it can culminate within 1 h. These and similar data led Kroemer and Levine157 to critically review ACD and conclude that ACD is a misnomer. The description of cell death with autophagy is more appropriate than cell death by autophagy. In other words, autophagy is taking place in the background in the same way as are other cellular processes: respiration and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, transcription and ribosomal protein synthesis, vesicle formation and intracellular trafficking etc. In fact, it appears that autophagy is rarely, if ever, the mechanism by which cells actually die4,12,15,16,157.

Nevertheless, Baehrecke et al.44–50,193–195 have identified an existence of an interesting and independent pathway that suggest that there can be interactions between some elements of the autophagic and apoptotic machineries. This may represent a novel, yet uncharacterized complex network for the efficient and coordinated elimination of obsolete tissues and organs during metamorphosis. Further studies along these lines, specifically using the Drosophila SGs as a model system, should shed more light on how cooperation between individual components of the autophagic and apoptotic pathways achieve the near-synchronous elimination of this organ.

Finally, we recently observed, that numerous autophagosomes are released via apocrine secretion into the lumen and then delivered to the exuvial fluid (Supplemental Fig. S8). The observation that autophagosomes are removed from the cytoplasm shortly prior to execution of cell death does not support a role for them in the cell death process per se. Indeed, though their role in exuvial fluid needs to be further investigated, this observation opens the question of what purpose autophagosomes serve in the SGs. Furthermore, our observations in animals with a loss-of-function Atg1 mutation or where other Atg-gene functions were downregulated using RNAi provide evidence that AS takes place normally in an Atg− background for all of the Atg genes used in this study (Supplemental Fig. S9).

Materials and methods

Fly culture and genetics

The wild-type Oregon R strain of Drosophila melanogaster (Meigen), originally obtained from Umea Drosophila Stock Centre, Umea, Sweden, was used as standard reference control156. Transgenic y w; hs-Gal4/CyO or y w; hs-Gal4/TM3 and Sgs3-Gal4 drivers came from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center, Indiana, USA. The transgenic animals UAS-rpr, hs-grim, UAS-grim, UAS-skl and UAS-Dronc were generous gifts of John Abrams, hs-hid and UAS-Dredd of Hermann Steller, and UAS-p35 from Bruce Hay160,161. Stocks of dsRNA transgenic lines for the autophagy genes UAS-Atg3RNAi, UAS-Atg5RNAi, UAS-Atg6RNAi, UAS-Atg7RNAi, UAS-Atg12RNAi, were provided by Oren Schuldiner119. Stocks with UAS-Tor and Atg1Δ3D were gifts of Tom Neufeld111,162. All stocks were maintained using SM1, SM5, CyO, TM3, TM6 or other commonly used balancer chromosomes described in Lindsley and Zimm163. Empirically we found that both the hs-Gal4 driver as well as the Sgs3-Gal4 driver are very similar in terms of their efficiency to show Atg-related phenotypes. There can be at least two reasons for this. First, the Sgs3 gene is highly active until the end of the 3rd larval instar and continues to be active at a reduced level at least 2–4 h APF164. Second, the Atg protein products have a generally longer half-life than other proteins24,165–169. Hence, even when they are expressed under Sgs3 control, the Atg proteins can survive and function longer during the prepupal stage and thereby produce a targeted phenotype. For the hs-Gal4 driver, its first expression following a 45-min pulse at 35 °C was done in the white puparium stage (0 h prepupa), followed by a second pulse as described in M&M after a 2-h lag period. Light microscopy and EM images presented in this paper were all collected using hs-Gal4 drivers. As controls to transgenic animals, we used either wild type flies (Oregon R) or uncrossed parental stocks (Sgs3-Gal4, hs-Gal4 or UAS-target) that were treated in the same way (raised at normal temperature for Sgs3-Gal4-based crosses and heat shocked at 35 °C twice for 45 min each for hs-Gal4-based crosses). The efficiency of RNAi in all UAS-Atg stocks was previously confirmed by RT-PCR57,170–178. To cell-specifically detect hemocytes, we used the He-Gal4 > > UAS-GFP.nls transgenic line, a gift from István Andó and Viktor Honti107.

In vitro treatments

Dissected SGs were cultured in a sitting drop of Grace’s insect tissue medium (Gibco Invitrogen Inc.) diluted 5:1 as described previously51. Rapamycin (ICN) was dissolved and then diluted in Grace’s medium to 1 μM. Control glands were treated with dilutions of solvent alone for the same time as experimental glands.

Annexin-V detection of phosphatidylserine and confocal microscopy

Immediately upon dissection, SGs were incubated for 15 min with FITC-annexin-V (Roche Biochemicals) diluted 1:50 in 50 mM Hepes and 20 mM CaCl2 buffer (pH 7.2) at room temperature, washed in the same buffer and immediately fixed for 1 h in 4% paraformaldehyde in 10 mM Pipes, 75 mM L-lysine, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM sodium-m-periodate (pH 6.9)179. After extensive destaining and rinsing in PT solution (10 mM Pipes + 0.3% Triton X-100), glands were mounted in Elvanol and scanned under a Zeiss LSM-510 META laser-scanning confocal microscope using a 40 × /NA 1.30 oil lens (Carl Zeiss MicroImaging GmbH.). The bitmap images obtained were converted in Zeiss LSM 5 Image Browser version 3.2.0 and processed with Adobe PhotoShop software.

Cell viability assessment

Cell viability in SGs was measured by dye exclusion of 0.2% trypan blue180 dissolved in a Drosophila ringer solution as described previously31.

Immunohistochemistry and laser confocal microscopy

Dissected SGs were fixed in PIPES-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (20 mM PIPES, 60 mM sucrose, 1 mM EGTA, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.2). In order to stain SG tissue with antibodies the SGs were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (PT) and then blocked with PT containing 2% fraction V of bovine serum albumin (Serva) (PBT) and 2% goat serum (Sigma) (PBTS). To detect nuclear, cytosolic and cytoskeletal proteins, the SGs were incubated overnight at 4 °C in PBTS in the presence of primary mouse anti-BR–C monoclonal antibody181–183 diluted 1:10 and primary rabbit anti-p127l(2)gl polyclonal antibody184,185 diluted 1:200. Hemocytes were detected using anti-Hemese MoAb (H2) from István Andó186, usually in combination with rabbit anti-β-tubulin polyclonal antibody (Sigma # T-3526) diluted 1:200. After extensive washing of the SGs in PT, goat affinity purified and preadsorbed Cy5-anti-mouse and Cy3-anti-rabbit F(ab)2-specific secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs.) were added along with 0.04 nM AlexaFluor488-Phalloidin (Molecular Probes/Invitrogen) to detect filamentous actin, and 5 μg/ml Hoechst-33258 (Calbiochem) to visualize DNA, and the SGs were incubated for 2 h at room temperature (23 to 25 °C). After extensive washing in PT, the SGs were mounted in Elvanol under a Schött high-performance No 1.5H coverslip and scanned under a Zeiss LSM-510 META laser confocal microscope using a 40 × (NA 1.3) oil objective. Most of these techniques are described in more detail in our previous papers54,57,59,60. The obtained bitmap images were processed using Zeiss AIM LSM5 software (v. 4.2.0.121) and Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 (v. 20).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Whole SGs were fixed immediately after dissection in 4% paraformaldehyde (Polysciences) + 2% glutaraldehyde (Ted Pella) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (Serva) (pH 7.2) for 1 h at 23 °C. To ensure they were fully immersed in fixative, the SGs were placed in inverted caps from 1.5 ml eppendorf tubes and ~ 100 μl of fixative was used per 10 pairs of glands. The SGs were then rinsed 5 × in 100–200 μl of fresh 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (pH 7.2) for 5 to 10 min each at 23 °C. The samples were postfixed in 0.5% osmium tetroxide (EMS) in H2O for 60 min, and then extensively rinsed in H2O (minimum 6 times for 10 min each). The SGs were dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanol (Merck) (30%, 50%, 70%, 96% and 100%); the dehydration step with 100% ethanol was repeated twice before applying a mixture of 100% ethanol + 100% acetone (Merck) (1:1) twice, followed by three changes of absolute acetone. At this step care was taken to prevent the sample from drying out during the exchanges of absolute ethanol or acetone, as well as at all subsequent times before critical-point drying. For this reason, the addition of hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS; Sigma-Aldrich), in place of Peldri II187–189 to facilitate critical point drying190,191, was done carefully in several steps: the first volume of HMDS was applied in the presence of small remnants of acetone; then, after 5 to 10 min, the HMDS was quickly removed and fresh HMDS added taking care that the previous HMDS did not completely evaporate; finally, SGs were then kept in the HMDS for 30 min, and then the remnants of HDMS were allowed to evaporate completely in a dust-free space.

Dried SGs mounted on pieces of Scotch double-sided tape on 16 or 24 mm aluminum SEM stubs were sputter coated for 2.5 min with gold–palladium using a Balzers sputter-coater device SCD-030 at 35–40 mA per stub and at a pressure of 0.05 to 0.1 mbar to produce a 40–50 nm continuous alloy layer. The samples were viewed and photographed on an FEI Quanta FEG250 scanning electron microscope with the emission field cathode at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The bitmap images obtained were processed and labeled using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 (v. 20) or Corel Draw 10 (v. 10.410) software and assembled into figures using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 (v. 20).

Morphological observations of whole animals

For basic morphological observations of the He-Gal4 > > UAS-GFP transgenic line puparia, precisely aged animals (starting from the point of white puparium formation = 0 h prepupa) were gently removed from the wall of glass vial, extensively washed in tap and then dd H2O, dried on the piece of filter paper, and then placed on a cleaned microscope glass slide. The GFP signal from the He-Gal4 > > UAS-GFP transgenic construct was examined using Leica MZ16F-A/X stereomicroscope with a GFP1 filter and images were taken using Leica DFC480 digital camera, which was operated with Leica LAS 3.6.1 software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and immuno-TEM

TEM was performed as previously described50,128. Briefly, upon dissection or after an in vitro treatment, the SGs were immediately fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde + 4% paraformaldehyde (PolySciences Europe GmbH., Eppelheim, Germany) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer containing 0.25 M sucrose (pH 7.2) for 2 h at room temperature, postfixed in 1% OsO4 (Serva Feinbiochemica GmbH., Heidelberg, Germany) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, dehydrated in ascending series of ethanols, infiltrated in propylene oxide, and embedded in Durcupan ACM resin (Fluka AG, Buchs, Switzerland). Ultrathin sections were contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and electron micrographs were collected by a Jeol JEM100 CX II electron microscope operating at 60 kV.

For immunodetection of phosphatidylserine-bound annexin-V, SGs were fixed and processed as described above. Postfixation with OsO4 was omitted and SGs were embedded in Durcupan resin (Fluka AG, Buchs, Switzerland). To detect biotinylated annexin-V, ultrathin sections were collected on nickel grids, briefly washed in sterile filtered nanopure water then in TBS (50 mM Tris–HCl, 140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl pH 8.0), incubated overnight with 10 nm colloidal gold-conjugated streptavidin (British BioCell International Ltd.), and examined in a Jeol JEM100 CX II transmission electron microscope.

Morphometric quantitation of structures

Detailed morphometry of structures in the salivary gland at 0, 7.5 and 15 h after puparium formation (APF) and fat body at 16, 24, 32, 40 and 48 h of the third larval instar was carried out by the point counting method as described192. For each set of measurements, six parallel tissue samples were prepared and specific surface was quantified in 30 electron micrographs taken from eight sections of each preparation. The cytoplasmic volume fraction and relative surface of the various organelles were measured on the electron micrographs at 15,000 × final magnification. The specific surface of the autophagosomes and the other organelles was determined as Sv/Vv. After confirming normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test, differences in the specific surface area of an organelle across developmental stages were evaluated by ANOVA. If group differences were significant, pairwise differences were evaluated using a Tukey HSD post-hoc test. Separate quantitation of organelle density was carried out in late prepupal salivary glands from wild-type animals at 14–16 h APF, and following the ectopic expression of genes integral to apoptosis or treatment with rapamycin or RNAi-inhibition of autophagy genes. The number of organelles/μm2 was quantified in micrographs from 2 to 7 preparations and differences from controls evaluated using non-parametric tests as described in the legends to Supplemental Figs. S1 and S2.
