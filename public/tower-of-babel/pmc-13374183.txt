Clustering longitudinal data: comparison of model-based and distance-based approaches using simulated and real-world data in psychiatric research
Authors: Laura Julià, Ana M. Sánchez-Torres, Jessica Merchan, Silvia Amoretti, Josep Lluis Carrasco, Sergi Mas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClustering longitudinal symptom trajectories is increasingly used to characterise clinical heterogeneity in psychiatric disorders. However, the relative performance of distance-based and model-based approaches remains insufficiently studied in psychiatric settings, where limited follow-up waves, measurement variability, and heterogeneous patient responses may affect clustering reliability. We aimed to systematically compare distance-based and model-based (Latent Class Mixed Models, LCMMs) methods, evaluating their performance in recovering the number of groups, classification accuracy, and clinical interpretability.MethodsWe analysed longitudinal symptom data from 237 first-episode psychosis patients (PEPs cohort). We also performed simulations (500 replicates per scenario) with known group structures (3 or 4), varying group-size distributions (balanced/unbalanced) and levels of group separation (well-separated, partially overlapping, strongly overlapping). Recovery of the true or optimal number of groups was assessed using multiple validity indices. Classification accuracy was measured by weighted Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\kappa\:$$\end{document}. Clinical interpretability was evaluated in terms of within-group homogeneity, stability across replications, and minimum group size.ResultsIn the PEPs cohort, distance-based methods consistently identified two stable and clinically interpretable trajectory groups, whereas LCMMs frequently suggested more complex structures, often including very small classes. Concordance between methodological families was low, indicating that patient stratification was strongly method-dependent: distance-based methods grouped patients primarily by overall symptom severity, whereas LCMMs distinguished groups according to trajectory slope. In simulations, both approaches performed well when groups were clearly separated, but performance declined with increasing overlap and group-size imbalance. Distance-based methods generally produced more stable and homogeneous partitions, whereas LCMMs showed greater variability across replications.ConclusionsIn psychiatric longitudinal studies with few assessment waves and moderate sample sizes, distance-based clustering methods may provide a robust strategy for trajectory-based partitioning. Introduction

Characterising clinical heterogeneity in the course of psychiatric disorders remains a central challenge in advancing precision medicine [1, 2]. Increasing attention has been directed toward identifying clinically meaningful subgroups of patients based solely on the longitudinal evolution of their symptoms, independent of baseline predictors or external covariates [3–5]. By stratifying individuals according to their symptom trajectories, researchers aim to refine prognosis, anticipate treatment response, and guide more personalised interventions. Such trajectory-based phenotyping underpins current initiatives in psychiatric precision medicine, including projects such as FarmaPRED-PEP, which seek to identify clinically actionable response patterns in first-episode psychosis [6].

A wide range of statistical methods are available for clustering longitudinal data [7], broadly including distance-based techniques (grouping individuals according to similarity in observed trajectories) and model-based approaches (relying on probabilistic assumptions to classify individuals into latent classes with distinct trajectories) [8]. Latent Class Mixed Models (LCMMs) are widely used in psychiatric longitudinal research for their flexibility in modelling subject-specific random effects and software availability [9–11]. Accordingly, we selected LCMM as a representative model-based approach for this study. Despite their widespread use in medical research, the choice between these methodological families often relies on convention or convenience rather than empirical evidence [12].

Psychiatric longitudinal data present methodological challenges that may affect differently the performance of these approaches. Symptom trajectories rely on subjective scales, introducing measurement variability, ceiling or floor effects, and potential reporting bias. Illness courses are frequently episodic, follow-up is often limited to a small number of assessments. Moreover, because psychiatric phenotypes lack stable biomarkers, uncertainty in trajectory estimation increases. Thus, methods that perform well in other medical contexts may lack stability and interpretability in psychiatric longitudinal settings.

Previous reviews have described and summarised available clustering techniques [9, 12–14]. Comparative studies specific to psychiatry have rarely focused on longitudinal data [15–17], and those addressing longitudinal clustering have not considered the particular challenges of psychiatric research [18, 19]. Importantly, these reviews primarily summarise methodological options and statistical criteria without empirically examining performance under domain-specific constraints. In particular, prior work has rarely combined recovery of the true number of groups, classification accuracy and clinical interpretability within a unified design. This leaves a methodological gap between statistical optimisation and clinical applicability.

Evaluation of clustering performance is inherently multidimensional. From a statistical perspective, two core aspects are typically considered: (i) the ability to correctly identify the number of groups present in the data; and (ii) the accuracy with which individuals are classified into those groups. Numerous criteria exist to guide model selection, including information criteria [20], internal validity indices [21], likelihood ratio tests [22], posterior classification probabilities, and minimum cluster size requirements. Several studies recommend combining multiple indices [23, 24], as different metrics capture distinct aspects of model performance and may lead to divergent solutions [25]. This variability highlights the absence of a clear gold standard in longitudinal clustering evaluation. With respect to classification accuracy, concordance measures are often employed [26].

Beyond statistical adequacy, medical research requires attention to the clinical meaning and practical utility of derived subgroups [27, 28]. Statistically optimal partitions may not correspond to clinically coherent or actionable phenotypes [12]. Clinical interpretability involves assessing whether groups are sufficiently distinct and internally homogeneous enough to represent useful patient profiles, whether they align with clinical knowledge, and whether they provide added value in guiding prognosis or treatment.

The aim of this study is to conduct a systematic comparison of distance-based and model-based clustering methods for longitudinal data, with a specific focus on psychiatric research. Specifically, we compared methods within a structured three-domain evaluation framework: (i) recovery of the true or optimal number of groups, (ii) accuracy of individual classification, and (iii) clinical interpretability in terms of within-group homogeneity and between-group separation. By jointly evaluating statistical and clinical performance dimensions, this framework extends prior comparative work and provides a more comprehensive assessment of methodological utility in psychiatric research.

To achieve this, we combine real-world data from the PEPs cohort, one of the largest Spanish studies of first-episode psychosis (FEP) patients [29], with simulated scenarios varying in the numbers of groups and degree of separation, where the true group structure is known. The simulation design was informed by empirical characteristics of psychiatric longitudinal data, including realistic trajectory patterns, incomplete follow-up, and the potential emergence of unbalanced subgroup structures. By integrating empirical and simulation-based evidence under conditions representative of psychiatric longitudinal research, this study aims to provide methodological guidance grounded in both statistical rigour and clinical relevance.

Case example: the PEPs cohort

To illustrate the application of longitudinal clustering methods in psychiatry, we used real-world data from the PEPs cohort (“Genotype-Phenotype Interaction and Environment: Application to a Predictive Model in First Psychotic Episodes”) [29]. This is one of the largest longitudinal cohorts of FEP patients in Spain, comprising 335 individuals recruited between 2009 and 2011 across 16 clinical centres nationwide. The inclusion criteria were age 7–35 years, duration of psychotic symptoms less than 12 months, fluency in Spanish, and written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included intellectual disability (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, DSM-IV), a history of head trauma with loss of consciousness, and medical/neurological conditions affecting cognition. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committees of all participating centres.

For the present analysis, we focused on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), a widely used and validated instrument for assessing psychotic symptomatology [30]. Specifically, we analysed the positive and negative subscales, which capture the two core symptom dimensions of psychosis. Higher values indicate greater symptom severity, with minimum and maximum possible scores of 7 and 49, respectively. Patients were assessed at baseline, and at 2, 6, and 12 months, thus providing a longitudinal profile of symptom evolution over the first year of treatment.

After data pre-processing, 237 individuals were retained for analysis. Participants with missing baseline PANSS scores or with clinical characteristics that made joint analysis inappropriate, such as age below 16 years or affective psychosis diagnoses, were excluded to ensure a homogeneous and clinically interpretable sample. In the context of clustering, the aim was to identify subgroups of patients who share similar longitudinal symptom trajectories, which can be interpreted as clinical phenotypes of treatment response. Identifying such subgroups is highly clinically relevant, as they may correspond to patterns such as rapid responders, slow responders, or non-responders. Thus, characterising longitudinal trajectories of treatment response in FEP patients is essential to better understand heterogeneity, inform prognostic models, and guide personalised care strategies.

Methods

Longitudinal clustering

We applied two main families of clustering approaches to identify subgroups of patients with similar longitudinal symptom trajectories.

Distance-based methods group patients into clusters by maximizing between-cluster separation and minimizing within-cluster variability, requiring a dissimilarity matrix as input. In our analysis, pairwise dissimilarities were obtained from the observed symptom trajectories of each individual. For distance-based methods, each individual trajectory was represented as a vector of PANSS scores across the scheduled follow-up visits. Pairwise dissimilarities were computed as the Euclidean distance [31] between these vectors. Thus, a single scalar distance was obtained for each pair of individuals, rather than computing separate distances per visit. When data were missing at specific visits, distances were rescaled to the number of shared observations so that individuals with incomplete follow-up were not systematically penalised. This procedure results in a symmetric dissimilarity matrix where each entry reflects how dissimilar two patients are in terms of their symptom evolution.

Three widely used distance-based algorithms were applied: agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward’s linkage [32], k-means [33], and Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) [34]. Hierarchical clustering builds nested partitions and is robust to noise, whereas k-means and PAM partition individuals into a pre-specified number of clusters, minimizing within-cluster variability. K-means is computationally efficient but sensitive to outliers, whereas PAM is more robust, particularly for small to moderate samples [12, 35].

Latent Class Mixed Models (LCMMs) were also implemented as a parametric model-based approach that extends linear mixed models by assuming population heterogeneity and classifying individuals into latent classes with distinct temporal trajectories [8]. PANSS scores were the dependent variable. Time was included as both a global fixed effect and a class-specific fixed effect. Random intercepts were included to capture inter-individual variability in baseline PANSS scores. Three functional forms were explored: linear, quadratic, and splines. Spline-based LCMMs were fitted using a natural cubic spline with internal knots at 2 and 6 months. Because only four observation times were available, the spline specification was intentionally kept low-dimensional. To improve convergence and reduce the risk of local optima, LCMMs were fitted using a grid search with 100 random initializations generated from perturbations of the parameter estimates obtained from the single-class model. Further details are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.

Identification of the number of groups

To evaluate each method’s ability to identify the number of groups, we used common clustering evaluation metrics.

For distance-based clustering methods, internal validity measures, including the average silhouette width [36], Dunn index [37], Calinski-Harabasz index [38], and Goodman and Kruskal’s gamma coefficient [39], were computed. These indices quantify different aspects of clustering performance, such as within-cluster cohesion, between-cluster separation, explained variance, and the consistency of cluster assignment relative to dissimilarities. In all the cases, higher values indicate better clustering performance.

For LCMMs, model fit was assessed via the following information criteria: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [40], Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [41], and Sample-Size Adjusted BIC (SABIC) [42], as well as entropy [43]. Lower values of the information criterion correspond to better model fit, whereas entropy values closer to 1 indicate greater classification certainty.

Full descriptions of all the cluster evaluation metrics are available in the Supplementary Methods 2.

Cluster concordance and homogeneity

Classification concordance was quantified using weighted Cohen’s kappa [44, 45] with quadratic weights, which penalizes disagreements proportionally to their squared distance from the diagonal.

Cluster homogeneity was assessed via the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) [46] computed from the pairwise distances between individual longitudinal trajectories. The ICC quantifies the proportion of variance attributable to differences between groups relative to the total variance (both within- and between-cluster). Higher ICC values indicate greater similarity of longitudinal trajectories within the same group and better separation across groups.

Software and reproducibility

All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.0 [47] using the packages cluster [48], fpc [49], lcmm [50] iccTraj [51] and psych [52]. The model fitting, bootstrap simulations, and related computationally intensive procedures were conducted on a high-performance computing server, equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 5318Y CPU @ 2.10 GHz and running a Linux-based operating system (kernel 5.4.0-216-generic, x86_64). Subsequent data processing, result analysis, and figure generation were performed on a 13th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i5-1335U (1.30 GHz) computer. The full analysis code is publicly available at Zenodo [53]: 10.5281/zenodo.18980293.

Finally, within the statistically optimal solutions, we assessed concordance across methods via weighted Cohen’s kappa and the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), and evaluated cluster differentiability via the ICC. Real-world data

Discussion

This study systematically compared the performance of longitudinal clustering methods –distance-based (hierarchical, k-means, PAM) and model-based (LCMM) – using real-world data from first-episode psychosis and a variety of simulated scenarios. Performance was evaluated using a structured three-domain framework encompassing: (i) recovery of the true or optimal number of groups, (ii) classification accuracy and (iii) clinical interpretability in terms of their homogeneity and separation.

In the PEPs cohort, two groups consistently emerged as a robust and clinically meaningful solution, particularly for negative PANSS trajectories. Distance-based methods showed high stability across bootstrap replicates and produced balanced, moderately homogeneous clusters. LCMMs often favoured more complex latent structures, some of which were unbalanced and showed lower within-class homogeneity. Although class imbalance may reflect the true distribution of response patterns, the combination of small class sizes, lower homogeneity and reduced bootstrap stability suggests limited robustness of some LCMM solutions in this dataset.

Concordance analyses showed low agreement between methodological families, indicating that patient stratification was strongly method-dependent. Distance-based clustering mainly separated patients by overall symptom burden (trajectory level), whereas LCMMs more often distinguished groups according to change over time (trajectory slope). This difference is expected: Euclidean distance emphasises magnitude differences across the full trajectory vector, while LCMMs explicitly model time and can identify classes defined by distinct longitudinal trajectories.

Clinically, both perspectives may be meaningful: slope-based groups may reflect treatment response dynamics, whereas level-based groups may capture persistent symptom severity, particularly relevant for negative symptoms and functional prognosis. However, these alternative partitions may lead to different conclusions in downstream analyses. Prognostic modelling, biomarker association studies, or treatment effect comparisons could yield distinct results depending on whether stratification is driven by baseline severity or by trajectory slope. In our setting, slope-driven LCMM solutions frequently resulted in very small classes (< 5%) and showed lower bootstrap stability, limiting their practical utility for subgroup stratification.

In simulations designed to approximate common characteristics of psychiatric longitudinal studies, including unbalanced group sizes and missing data patterns similar to those observed in the PEPs cohort, both families of methods performed well when the groups were clearly separated, recovering the correct number of groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k$$\end{document}) in > 80% of replicates and achieving high classification accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\kappa\:\approx\:1$$\end{document}). However, as group overlap increased, performance declined, reflecting a reduction in the signal-to-noise ratio of the data-generating mechanism. When residual variability approached or exceeded between-class separation, discrimination was difficult and class recovery deteriorated across all methods. In LCMMs, this residual variability is explicitly modelled through the error structure of the mixed model, whereas distance-based approaches operate directly on the observed trajectories without an explicit measurement error component. This effect was particularly evident in four-group for both balance and unbalanced settings, where smaller effective separation further compromised the recoverability of the underlying structure.

The simulations further showed that group-size imbalance substantially increased the difficulty of recovering the true structure, particularly when four groups were present. In these settings, smaller clusters were more easily absorbed into larger ones, leading most indices to favour solutions with fewer groups. This behaviour was especially evident for information criteria in LCMMs, which frequently selected three classes even when four were simulated. This effect was likely amplified by the presence of a very small subgroup in the four-class scenarios, which reduced the effective separability of the data and made the underlying structure harder to detect.

When comparing the two methodological families (as represented here by Euclidean distance-based methods and LCMMs), similar overall performance patterns emerged across balanced and unbalanced simulation scenarios. Both approaches tended to underestimate\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:k$$\end{document}, although the underlying mechanisms differed. Distance-based methods generally selected fewer but more stable and homogeneous clusters, whereas LCMMs occasionally recovered the correct \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k$$\end{document} but yielded poor classification accuracy in challenging scenarios. These findings are consistent with previous work [54, 55] and illustrate that identifying the number of groups does not guarantee a meaningful classification [56].

Moreover, LCMMs were more variable across replications, likely reflecting sensitivity to modelling assumptions such as normality, variance homogeneity, and correct distributional form and functional specification [9, 57]. This sensitivity is particularly relevant when flexible trajectory specifications are used and is compounded by the computational demands of LCMM estimation as model complexity increases. In our setting, spline-based LCMMs should therefore be interpreted cautiously: with only four distinct observation times, the estimation of nonlinear curvature is intrinsically unstable. Penalized spline formulations would likely be preferable when a larger number of repeated measurements is available.

Several patterns observed in the empirical analysis closely paralleled those identified in the simulations. In the PEPs cohort, the negative PANSS trajectories resembled simulations under partially overlapping groups, where distance-based methods prioritised stable and homogeneous partitions (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k=2$$\end{document}), whereas LCMMs identified more complex but less stable structures. In contrast, positive PANSS trajectories showed greater disagreement between methods than in any simulated setting, suggesting weaker or more heterogeneous underlying structure. Convergence failures and the occurrence of very small classes showed similar patterns across real and simulated data.

Across real and simulated data, discrepancies between statistical indices were also observed. No single criterion consistently identified the same optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k$$\end{document}. The Gamma coefficient tended to overestimate k, whereas other indices behaved more conservatively. In line with previous methodological work [7, 23, 24], these results reinforce the need to combine multiple fit indices and interpret them within a clinical and theoretical framework rather than relying on any single metric.

Our findings support the use of multiple performance evaluation domains (cluster recovery, classification accuracy, and interpretability) as part of the clustering workflow. In empirical settings without ground truth, different methods may reveal distinct yet clinically meaningful aspects of patient heterogeneity, and their value ultimately depends on interpretability and intended application.

Strengths and limitations of this study

A major strength of this study is the integration of real-world clinical data from the PEPs cohort, one of the largest Spanish FEP samples, with a comprehensive simulation study. Unlike previous work, our simulations were explicitly designed to reflect the challenges of psychiatric research by incorporating balanced and unbalanced group sizes, realistic missing data patterns derived from the empirical cohort, and varying levels of group separation (well-separated, partially overlapping, and strongly overlapping). This design allowed us to evaluate method performance under controlled yet clinically representative conditions.

Furthermore, we introduced a structured three-domain evaluation framework that jointly considers statistical adequacy (recovery of the number of groups), classification accuracy, and clinical interpretability (homogeneity, separation, and group size). By systematically combining multiple fit indices, bootstrap resampling, and concordance analyses across methods, this study provides a more comprehensive assessment of methodological utility than previous comparisons. To our knowledge, it is also the first to systematically document how statistically optimal but small groups can undermine clinical applicability in psychosis research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our simulations were informed by real PANSS trajectories, they cannot fully capture psychiatric complexity of psychiatric data. We did not systematically vary factors such as sample size, number of time points, or deviations from normality, as our aim was to reproduce conditions representative of typical FEP cohorts. Moreover, our conclusions are empirical and derived from Euclidean distance-based methods and LCMMs as a representative model-based framework, and should not be generalised to other model-based longitudinal clustering approaches, whose performance may differ under similar conditions.

Future research

Future research should expand this comparative framework to other psychiatric conditions and longitudinal measures, and to additional experimental conditions such as varying numbers of time points or sample sizes. Another important methodological extension would be to examine whether the performance patterns observed here generalise to data generating mechanisms that depart from the Gaussian error assumption. Furthermore, future methodological work could extend this comparison by incorporating LCMM implementations that support penalized splines, particularly in datasets with more follow-up waves.

It should also extend this evaluation to encompass a broader range of methodological frameworks, including functional data clustering approaches. Finally, theoretical mathematical analyses would complement simulation-based findings by delineating the fundamental conditions under which different clustering paradigms can be expected to succeed or fail.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first systematic comparisons of distance-based clustering algorithms and LCMMs using a unified three-domain evaluation framework. Our findings demonstrate that both methodological families perform well when groups are clearly separated, but their performance diverges under realistic, noisy conditions. Crucially, our results showed that patient stratification is strongly method-dependent and suggest that that the choice of method should be guided by the intended interpretation of the resulting subgroups.

In our setting, LCMMs yielded solutions with lower robustness across replications and a tendency to produce very small classes. This, combined with their higher computational demands and sensitivity to modelling assumptions, suggests that LCMMs may be better suited to larger samples with more assessment waves, where complex nonlinear trajectories can be reliably estimated.

On the basis of our findings, we suggest that in psychiatric longitudinal studies with few assessment waves and moderate sample sizes, distance-based methods represent a robust and interpretable approach. Importantly, these conclusions pertain to the specific modelling frameworks evaluated in this study and should not be extrapolated to alternative model-based longitudinal clustering approaches without further investigation. We further advocate for the use of multiple fit indices, bootstrap resampling to assess stability, and reporting of within-cluster homogeneity. Finally, clinical expert review is essential to ensure that the derived subgroups represent meaningful phenotypes.
