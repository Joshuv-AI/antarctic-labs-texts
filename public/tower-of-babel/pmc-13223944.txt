Family caregiver education programmes for safe home care: a scoping review
Authors: Valerie Matarredona Pérez, Mercedes Guilabert, Cristina Navarro Maciá, Nida Abed, Reinhard Strametz, Susanna Tella, Einav Srulovici, José Joaquín Mira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing and the rise in chronic diseases are driving a shift from residential models to home care where family carers play a key role. Although education programmes have shown benefits, limited attention has been paid to how family caregivers can be effectively trained to prevent unintentional mistakes in home care and to how they should be prepared to respond to them related to the care they provide.ObjectivesThis scoping review aims to examine education programmes that enhance family caregivers’ knowledge and skills to promote safe home care, prevent unintentional mistakes and mitigate their impact on both care recipients and family caregivers.DesignThis study follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) methodology for scoping reviews.Data sourcesComprehensive searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Embase in April 2024.Eligibility criteriaThe review included studies on family caregivers in home-based care, focusing on education programmes promoting safe practices; quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods designs were eligible while studies on professional care or unrelated to home safety were excluded.Data extraction and synthesisExtracted data included study characteristics, education type, target population and safety-related outcomes, which were summarised descriptively to map the available evidence.Results31 studies on home safety education programmes for family caregivers across oncological, neurological, chronic and paediatric conditions were identified, mostly from the USA and conducted in hospital, home and other health and care facilities. Randomised controlled trials predominated and were generally high quality. Education programmes targeting technical, daily living and self-care skills improved caregiver burden, knowledge, preparedness and quality of life although physical health outcomes showed limited change.ConclusionsEvidence indicates that structured education programmes enhance family caregivers’ knowledge, preparedness, competence and psychological well-being, supporting safer home care. While current education programmes are mostly short-term and caregiver-centred, this review identifies core technical, daily life and self-care competencies to guide future education programmes, highlighting the need for co-designed, rigorously evaluated education programmes that include patient safety outcomes and caregiver emotional support.Keywords: Caregivers, Safety, Chronic Disease, EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training)

Introduction

The increase in the life expectancy of the population is leading to an increase of chronic diseases and people with specific care needs profiles, often associated with aging.1 Residential care models, where social and healthcare professionals work, are declining, while home care is gaining importance, as both older individuals and those with social and/or healthcare needs now require this type of support.1

Addressing patient safety at home is more challenging than in hospital settings, as research on safety in home environments is limited.1 Home care involves several factors, including environments that are typically not adapted, increasing risks.2 Multiple care providers are involved, such as healthcare professionals and also family caregivers.3

Family caregivers are individuals without specific training on health skills who assist known individuals.4 Their responsibilities include medication management, providing care; for example, to make wound dressings, prevent falls, among others. Those receiving care may require healthcare (such as medication management, symptom monitoring or wound care) and day-to-day support (such as patient transfers, hygiene, feeding).5 Family caregivers play a key role in ensuring safety but can also be involved in adverse events due to their close involvement, but few studies focus on the role of family caregivers in providing safety in home-based settings.6 In the context of informal caregiving, an adverse event is an unintended injury or harm affecting the care recipient that is associated with care provided at home, rather than the underlying condition and may result in additional health complications, increased care needs, hospitalisation or death.6

The occurrence of adverse events in the home has been studied.7 However, how to implement safe practices at home within caregiver education programmes, and how to provide support following an unintentional mistake, remain issues that have to be addressed, beyond the concept of caregiver burden.8 In the context of family caregiving, an unintentional mistake is an unplanned or inadvertent error occurring during the provision of care at home, without malicious intent, that may compromise the safety of the care recipient or lead to potential harm.8

Studies indicate that the occurrence of unintentional mistakes in this context is high,1 3 by commission (eg, giving more medication than necessary) and by omission (eg, the cared-for falling while the carer is not present). Some of these unintentional mistakes may cause harm to the patient, resulting in an adverse event,9 which can also have a disturbing emotional impact on family caregivers,10 who may feel responsible for that harm.

The most frequently occurring adverse events at home were related to medication errors, infected wounds, injuries from falls and mental health problems.10 Family caregivers involved in adverse events may experience what is known as the ‘double victimisation’ phenomenon: on the one hand, the impact of being involved in an adverse event, and on the other hand, the cumulative burden associated with the caregiving role.11 This highlights the importance of promoting safe home care by empowering the caregiver and strengthening their capacity to respond and adapt following such events.12

Training family caregivers has proven to be an effective resource for preventing risks and improving safety.13 Effective education programmes could increase the abilities of caregiving, medication management and symptom monitoring could reduce caregiver burden and improve health outcomes.10 14 However, more structured support and research in this area are needed.10

This study was part of the Better Care project, a Cost Action CA22152, which focuses on supporting emergency care economies and empowering caregivers to provide safe care at home. The primary aim is to reduce unintentional mistakes by family caregivers through education and training programmes that enhance the safety of care receivers. The initiative encourages open dialogue about error consequences by integrating citizens, end-users and various disciplines. This study reviews current efforts to support family caregivers and promote safe home environments.

Objectives

This scoping review aims to examine education programmes that enhance family caregivers’ knowledge and skills to promote safe home care, prevent unintentional mistakes and mitigate their impact on both care recipients and family caregivers.

The secondary objectives are the following: to determine which educational programme modalities are perceived as attractive, useful and easy to implement for promoting safe home environments; to identify the essential skills required by family caregivers to establish a safe home environment and reduce the occurrence of unintentional mistakes; and to explore the knowledge gained, learning outcomes and the tools used to assess educational programmes for safe home environments.

Methods

This review is guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) to ensure transparency in the obtained results.15 The study protocol is available from the corresponding author and was preregistered in OSF at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/2YHZA.16 The SPIDER (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, and Research type) approach was used for constructing a research question and formulating eligibility criteria (see at online supplemental material 1).

As there has been little research on programmes and education for family caregivers focused on home safety, there is a need for this scoping review to shed light on this issue.

Patients and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of the research. The methodology focused exclusively on reviewing and synthesising existing literature without active participation from external groups.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

As inclusion criteria, the target population was family caregivers of individuals with health problems or some dependency requiring home-based care. Articles describing education programmes were considered, covering medication management, nutrition, mobility, infection prevention, caregiver emotional well-being, communication, conflict resolution, symptom management and/or hygiene. Articles also needed to mention caregiver skills and include some knowledge or learning acquired in these educational programmes and what measurement tools are provided.

This study understands ‘education program for family caregivers’ as a structured training and support strategy, offered in face-to-face, online or hybrid mode, aimed at reinforcing the knowledge, practical skills and psychosocial competencies necessary to guarantee safe care at home. These programmes should address key aspects (such as safe medication management, risk identification, basic techniques for safe care and caregiver self-care) and incorporate knowledge or learning acquired in these educational programmes and what measurement tools are provided.

Accepted study types included qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, experimental, clinical trials and pre–post studies and grey literature that meets the inclusion criteria, with no restrictions by publication year. Articles in Spanish, German, Hebrew, Finnish or English were included.

Studies were excluded if they did not address care recipient safety in the home setting or if they focused on populations outside the scope of family caregiving for dependent persons, including parents of healthy children, pregnancy-related education or non-dependent paediatric care. Studies centred on formal or professional caregivers were also excluded. Non-citable publications, such as editorials, letters, case reports, case series, narrative reviews and commentaries, were not considered.

Data sources and search strategy

After tentative searches in various meta-databases and databases (Scopus, Embase, ProQuest, PsycINFO, PsychArticles, Google Scholar, OVID) and systematic review protocol registries (PROSPERO, OSF), it was decided to conduct a scoping review, as the proposed topic had been little studied, mainly due to the outcomes sought in relation to how education programmes could promote both the safety of home care and the resilience of carers. This database search was complemented by a search of grey literature, mainly websites of associations of patients and carers of different pathologies, to review what support was being provided in terms of home care. Websites of caregiver and patients associations within the national and international sphere were also reviewed, but they did not provide information on the outcomes of the education carried out or their knowledges or learnings acquired by the participants, or their results, which is why it was decided to discard them. Given these initial exploratory searches, it was decided to conduct an efficient search by selecting those databases that actually yielded results in line with the research objectives, hence the selection of these three databases. The rest focused on many aspects related to caregiver overload and did not take the step of focusing on safe practices in the home that strengthen the autonomy and resilience of the caregiver. Although safety-related terms were initially considered, they were not included in the final search strategy because their use substantially limited retrieval across the selected databases. A broader search approach was therefore adopted to maximise sensitivity and avoid the exclusion of potentially relevant studies.

Searches were conducted in April 2024. In this way, this scoping review was made as a preliminary approach to the subject, to form a possible basis of future studies.

A combination of Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms were used. The search strategies were defined based on the combination of the following descriptors: “training”, “preventive programs”, “education programs”, “informal caregiver”, “family caregiver”, “non-professional caregiver”, “informal carer”, “patients safety”, “medication safety”, “safety management”, “home safety”, “home care safety”, “safe home care”, “home-based care safety”. Boolean OR and AND indicators were used in all cases (online supplemental material 1).

Selection of studies

To determine article eligibility, a two-stage screening process was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR recommendations. First, two independent reviewers (VMP and CNM), both trained in systematic database searching, screened titles and abstracts using the Rayyan platform to identify potentially relevant studies. The platform Rayyan17 is a digital tool designed to support the study selection process in systematic and scoping reviews. It allows researchers to import references from bibliographic databases, remove duplicates and screen titles and abstracts efficiently and independently. Additionally, it provides features such as blinding between reviewers, customisable tags, advanced filtering options and tools to resolve disagreements, thereby enhancing transparency, organisation and efficiency throughout the review process.17

In the second stage, the full texts of the selected articles were independently assessed by the same reviewers. Inclusion was based on the following criteria: (1) The study described an educational programme aimed at improving safety in the home environment, aligned with the objective of exploring educational programme modalities perceived as attractive, useful and easy to implement for promoting safe home environments; (2) The target population included family caregivers, directly supporting the objective of identifying essential skills required by family caregivers to establish a safe home environment and reduce the occurrence of unintentional mistakes; and (3) The educational programme focused on enhancing the knowledge and skills of family caregivers, which is consistent with the goal of exploring the knowledge gained, learning outcomes and tools used to assess educational programmes for safe home environments.

Any discrepancies at either stage were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (MG). No authors were contacted for additional information regarding their articles.

Data extraction

Two authors (VMP and CNM) independently and blindly extracted data from each study using a standardised Excel sheet, with included parameters such as the first author’s name, publication year, study location country, aims, study design, settings, participants, sample size, quality assessment results and the number of citations to gauge the study’s relevance. Additionally, data on programme descriptions and their effectiveness were also extracted.

Data synthesis

What is known about the review topic: A descriptive and comparative analysis approach was used to synthesise the findings of the included studies. Data synthesis and categorisation were conducted independently by two reviewers (VMP and CNM), with discrepancies discussed and resolved by a third one (MG), following the methodological guidance of the PRISMA-ScR checklist. Extracted data were charted and summarised in tables to facilitate comparison across intervention education programmes. An iterative categorisation process was applied to organise the data in relation to the review objectives.

Quality evaluation

The quality and validity of the studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Tool,18 a framework for conducting systematic reviews or critically appraising the literature. The Critical Appraisal Tool is a structured checklist designed to assess the methodological quality and potential risk of bias of the included studies according to their research design. It comprises 11 items that assess key aspects of the review process, such as the clarity of the research question, the appropriateness of the inclusion criteria, the comprehensiveness of the search strategy, the methods of critical appraisal, the minimisation of errors in data extraction and the adequacy of the synthesis of results. Verification involved checking compliance with predefined criteria, with response options of ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’.18 Two authors independently conducted data synthesis without seeing each other’s results, with a third author resolving discrepancies. The results of this assessment make it possible to determine the overall quality of the included reviews and to inform the interpretation and synthesis of evidence in umbrella reviews or systematic reviews of reviews.19

The quality results are presented as percentages based on ‘yes’ responses. Studies with over 80% are deemed high quality, those between 60% and 80% are good quality, and those below 60% are low quality and at risk of bias. The number of citations for each article was also recorded to assess its quality.

Results

Synthesis of results

1941 records were identified, of which 114 were identified as duplicates and excluded. After the title-abstract review, 175 articles were assessed as full text review. 31 studies were included in the scoping review. Full selection process is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1. PRISMA flow diagram, at online supplemental material 1).

The articles included in this review are described in online supplemental material 2. Most of the studies were from the USA20–35 (n=16), Iran36–40 (n=5), Netherlands41 42 (n=2) and Taiwan43 44 (n=2). Also UK,45 Hong Kong,46 Spain,47 Germany,48 Colombia49 and Indonesia.50 The studies found were published between 2001 and 2024, the peak of publications was between 2014 and 2023.

Regarding the intervention settings, education programmes were delivered across a variety of healthcare and community-based environments. Most were conducted in hospital settings25 27 36–40 42 47 (n=9), home-based settings21 22 28 31 41 44 50 (n=7), outpatient healthcare facilities20 23 24 33 34 40 43 (n=8). A smaller number of programmes were implemented in community-based support services,29 private healthcare facilities.46 In two studies, the intervention setting was not clearly specified.35 48

As for the types of studies, the most prevalent were randomised controlled trial20 23 26–28 30 37–40 44 48–50 (n=17), mixed-methods study21 22 24 43 (n=4), descriptive pre–post study25 29 31 47 (n=4), experimental study33–35 (n=3) and quasi-experimental study,37 prospective cohort study42 and qualitative study45 (n=1, respectively).

The most cited articles were Cheng et al, Hendrix et al and Hepburn et al, with 69, 113 and 219 citations, respectively.30 35 46

The results of the quality study conducted indicated that most of the studies related high quality20 22–24 27 30–34 38 41 42 46 47 49 50 and good quality.21 25 26 37 40 45 Three studies included in the review were considered to be at high risk of bias.28 34 48 See online supplemental material 2.

Modalities of programmes to ensure safe home environments

The studies identified were grouped according to the type of disease addressed, as safety risks in caregiver education are closely linked to the patient’s condition, medication and care practices. These factors influence both the content and priorities of educational interventions. Therefore, grouping programmes by disease allowed for better contextualisation of the educational content and the specific safety issues addressed.

According to the type of disease, the studies were classified into oncological diseases, programmes for family caregivers of patients with cancer20 23 27 30 32–34 36 38 41; neurological diseases, programmes for family caregivers of patients with neurological conditions such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s or psychological conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder, among others22 26 28 31 35 37 41 48; chronic diseases, programmes for family caregivers of patients with long-term health conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and others21 24 25 29 39 42 44 46 49 50; and paediatric diseases, programmes for family caregivers of children with serious medical conditions.47

Furthermore, 52% of these education programmes were carried out individually,21–23 26 27 30 32–34 38 41 44 49 50 while 42% were in groups20 24 25 28 31 35–37 39 40 45 47 49 and only 6% combined both modalities.26 42 On the other hand, 68% of the education programmes were face-to-face20 23–25 27–31 33–37 40–44 46 and 22% were distance-based,21 22 29 31 44 46 47 although only 10% of the education programmes presented both types of intervention,26 38 and only 10% of the education programmes had a postintervention follow-up.20 23 27 28 33 36–38 43 44 In terms of programme content, 45% were knowledge-based interventions,20 22–25 27 29 30 33–35 39 based on the acquisition of knowledge, 13% were training sessions41 43 47 49 and 42% combined both types of content,20 21 23 25 27 28 30 31 33–35 39 42 50 based on the acquisition of practical skills. Essential skills for promoting safe home environments

Establishing a safe home care environment is closely linked to patient safety, particularly when care is provided by informal or family caregivers. The skills addressed were grouped into three categories: technical skills, which encompass practical skills and knowledge enabling family caregivers to perform personal care and health management tasks safely and efficiently; daily life skills, which involve tasks and responsibilities that family caregivers undertake to support individuals with physical, cognitive or health limitations, including decision-making, communication, care coordination and environmental risk management that promote safe care practices; and self-care skills, which include practices and strategies for managing their own physical, emotional and mental well-being to ensure effective and sustainable long-term care, to reduce the risk of care-related errors and adverse events.

Knowledge or learnings outcomes acquired during the educational programmes and measurement tools used for assessing them

Across the included programmes, outcomes related to knowledge and skills acquired during educational interventions, as well as reported outcome measures, were mainly assessed through indicators of caregiver burden, psychological well-being, caregiving competence and knowledge acquisition. Most education programmes evaluating burden23 24 36 37 41 reported significant reductions, although one study showed no improvements and even declines in caring-ability dimensions.41 Psychological outcomes (including anxiety, depression, distress, resilience and coping) generally improved across interventions using validated measures.20 23 29 30 42 45 46 Programmes assessing knowledge and caregiving skills28 31 35 43 47 consistently reported increased competency, preparedness and understanding of care tasks. Additional outcomes, such as decision-making readiness,20 quality of life,42 45 communication behaviours,48 programme satisfaction21 22 26 34 and fall-prevention behaviours,40 also showed favourable changes. Overall, the acquisition of caregiver knowledge and skills was reflected in improved caregiver knowledge and self-efficacy, reduced emotional burden and consistently positive perceptions of programme usefulness and acceptability.

Discussion

To enhance patient safety and care outcomes in home settings, developing and implementing comprehensive education programmes tailored to family caregivers is essential. The need for such support is highlighted by the high levels of burnout and physical injuries experienced by caregivers.51 52

The shift from long-term residential institutional care to home care reflects deinstitutionalisation policies in developed countries, people’s preference to remain in their homes as long as possible and the limited availability of institutional care facilities and resources. In this context, the increasing reliance on home care highlights the need for accessible, adaptable educational programmes that integrate both theoretical and practical training.

Additionally, fostering collaboration between healthcare professionals and family caregivers is key to creating a supportive, mutual learning environment.51 53 Beyond education, formal support services for caregivers (such as counselling, respite care and structured support programmes) are also essential to reduce caregiver burden and sustain safe care in the home environment.53

Although most programmes identified in this review aim to enhance the quality of life and well-being of family caregivers, the issue of double victimisation among them needs more attention.54 This term describes the trauma caregivers experience after adverse events affecting their care recipients, such as medical errors, unexpected complications or worsening health conditions.55 These events can lead to guilt, post-traumatic stress and emotional distress, which can significantly impact caregivers’ well-being and their ability to provide quality care.54

Feelings of guilt can be particularly devastating for caregivers, as they often blame themselves for not preventing adverse events, even when these were beyond their control. This guilt can lead to self-criticism and despair, increasing stress and anxiety.56 Post-traumatic stress may manifest as flashbacks, nightmares and constant hypervigilance, making relaxation and emotional balance challenging. Prolonged distress can result in depression, social isolation and a significant decline in the caregiver’s quality of life.56 It is therefore crucial not only to provide educational interventions but also to develop structured support systems and services to help caregivers manage and overcome these traumatic experiences.56

The BetterCare project has carried out a number of studies and activities to promote safety in home care and improve support for informal carers. Key initiatives include defining the role of informal carers in Europe, identifying key challenges relating to safety and training, and conducting surveys on informal care practices and home safety in 28 countries. These efforts make a significant contribution to improving carers’ preparedness and addressing gaps in emotional and psychological support, particularly in the context of long-term care and the care of people with chronic conditions.57

Caregivers require not only specific caregiving skills but also adequate resources and professional support to safely implement home care models.57 Educational programmes should cover technical care tasks, coping strategies and self-care practices, which are essential for improving home safety.58 Ongoing professional and social support is vital for caregiver well-being and effectiveness.59 Using various delivery methods (such as in-person, remote, individual and group sessions) can enhance the reach and impact of these programmes and multidisciplinary teams provide a comprehensive understanding of care practices.51

Integrating caregiver education with accessible support services within healthcare and community systems may therefore be key to strengthening patient safety and improving the sustainability of home care.

Further research is needed to evaluate the long-term impact of education programmes on patient safety and caregiver well-being. Assessing different training modalities and understanding the barriers to implementation can help create effective and sustainable interventions.60 61

Conclusion

Available evidence suggests that structured education programmes for family caregivers are a promising strategy to support safer home care, as they consistently improve caregivers’ knowledge, preparedness, perceived competence and psychological well-being. However, most interventions are short-term, disease-specific and evaluated through caregiver-centred outcomes, so direct evidence that these programmes reduce medication errors, infections or falls at home remains limited. By mapping the content of existing programmes, this review highlights a core competency set (technical skills, self-care skills and daily life skills) that should guide the design of safety-oriented training. Future research and policy should prioritise the co-design and rigorous evaluation of comprehensive programmes that include postintervention follow-up, explicitly incorporate patient safety outcomes and address the emotional impact and potential double victimisation of family caregivers.
