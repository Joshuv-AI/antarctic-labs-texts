Unraveling biomarkers of exposure for tenuazonic acid through urinary metabolomics
Authors: Lia Visintin, María García Nicolás, Pablo Vangeenderhuysen, Tess Goessens, Eugenio Alladio, Beata Pomian, Lynn Vanhaecke, Sarah De Saeger, Marthe De Boevre
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycotoxins are secondary metabolites produced by fungi such as Aspergillus, Alternaria, and Penicillium, affecting nearly 80% of global food crops. Tenuazonic acid (TeA) is the major mycotoxin produced by Alternaria alternata, a prevalent pathogen affecting plants, fruits, and vegetables. TeA is notably prevalent in European diets, however, TeA biomarkers of exposure and metabolites remain unknown. This research aims to bridge this knowledge-gap by gaining insights about human TeA exposure and metabolization. Nine subjects were divided into two groups. The first group received a single bolus of TeA at the Threshold of Toxicological Concern (TTC) to investigate the presence of TeA urinary biomarkers, while the second group served as a control. Sixty-nine urinary samples were prepared and analyzed using UPLC-Xevo TQ-XS for TeA quantification and UPLC-Orbitrap Exploris for polar metabolome acquisition. TeA was rapidly excreted during the first 13 h and the fraction extracted was 0.39 ± 0.22. The polar metabolome compounds effectively discriminating the two groups were filtered using Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis and subsequently annotated (n = 122) at confidence level 4. Finally, the urinary metabolome was compared to in silico predicted TeA metabolites. Nine metabolites, including oxidized, N-alkylated, desaturated, glucuronidated, and sulfonated forms of TeA were detected.Keywords: Mycotoxin, Tenuazonic acid, Toxicokinetic, Machine learning, Metabolomics, Risk assessment

Introduction

Mycotoxins, harmful natural contaminants, are secondary metabolites produced by specific fungal genera such as Aspergillus, Fusarium, Alternaria, and Penicillium. Eskola et al. (2020) estimated that nearly 80% of global food crops have some level of mycotoxin contamination. Additionally, Moretti et al. (2019) predicted that due to climate change, some mycotoxins will become even more widespread in Europe, posing a significant health threat. The most recent annual report from the European Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF) highlights mycotoxins as a key hazard category in foods, representing a significant cause of economic loss (European Commission, 2022). Tenuazonic acid (TeA) is the major secreted mycotoxin of Alternaria alternata, a species that predominantly affects numerous plants, fruits, and vegetables. As reported by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the European population’s dietary exposure to TeA significantly outpaces that of other Alternaria toxins (Arcella et al., 2016). For adults, the estimated exposure levels do not exceed the Threshold of Toxicological Concern (TTC) i.e. 1500 ng/kg BW per day for TeA, hence not posing a health concern (Arcella et al., 2016). However, for infants and toddlers, exposure levels were found to present a potential health risk, with exposures ranging between 37 and 1614 ng/kg BW per day. Studies have demonstrated that TeA has acute and chronic toxicity effects against microbes, plants, humans, and animals (European Commission, 2006a, European Commission, 2006b; Kumari and Tirkey, 2019). In vitro models have shown cytotoxic properties in human intestinal epithelial barrier (Caco-2 cells) and hepatocytes (HepG2 cells) when exposed to TeA, with the cytotoxic effects being even stronger when TeA is combined with alternariol monomethyl ether (AME, another Alternaria toxin) (den Hollander et al., 2022). Despite these findings, Alternaria toxins are not currently regulated in food in any country. Recently, the European Commission introduced indicative alert levels for alternariol, alternariol monomethyl ether, and TeA in specific food commodities (European Commission, 2022), based on the Scientific Opinion issued by the EFSA in 2011 (Arcella et al., 2016). Above these levels, investigations should be performed, specifically concerning the factors contributing to the presence of Alternaria toxins and the impact of food processing. Investigation into the metabolism and excretion profile of TeA in animals and humans has been limited so far (Asam et al., 2013; Puntscher et al., 2019). One study on rats found that the urinary excretion rate of TeA is as high as 55% (Puntscher et al., 2019). A separate study conducted on two human subjects reported a urinary excretion rate of 87–93% after consuming TeA-contaminated food (Asam et al., 2013). Currently, there is a lack of understanding of exposure biomarkers and/or metabolites of TeA (Martins et al., 2020). Assessments of dietary TeA exposure are based solely on biomonitoring the mycotoxin itself, making a comprehensive risk assessment almost impossible due to the absence of data on potential metabolization pathways. Understanding the potential health consequences in humans necessitates the capability of conducting accurate mycotoxin exposure assessments at the individual level. More research is needed to understand the fate of TeA after human consumption, specifically regarding its excretion profile, potential biomarkers, and associated health risks. The field of mycotoxicology has started to apply human toxicokinetic trials, with the first such trial conducted by Vidal et al. (2018) on deoxynivalenol (DON). This trial provided insights into the metabolism and excretion of DON, facilitating the estimation of DON intake using urinary biomarkers (Mengelers et al., 2019; Vidal et al., 2018). Metabolomics is becoming increasingly important in identifying biomarkers for disease diagnosis, prognosis, and risk prediction (Xia et al., 2013). The process of biomarker discovery involves creating a mathematical model, based on selected features, to predict a defined clinical outcome, given measured biomarker responses from a target population (Wishart, 2010; Xia et al., 2013). Chemometric approaches, such as principal component analysis (PCA) or partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), are also used to identify statistically significant differences between groups of spectra or groups of chromatograms collected on different samples or under different conditions (Lavine and Workman, 2004; Trygg et al., 2007; Wishart, 2010). In case of high-dimensional data, such as metabolomics spectral signals, orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) is frequently used to separate group-predictive and group-unrelated variation (Bylesjö et al., 2006; Trygg and Wold, 2002; Worley and Powers, 2016).

Using targeted and metabolomic approaches, this study aims to investigate the human excretion of TeA. The objectives are to determine the influence of TeA on the urinary polar metabolome of subjects exposed to it and to detect its possible metabolites previously predicted in silico.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Five milligrams of TeA standard (purity >98%) was purchased from Fermentek (Jerusalem, Israel), while 100 μg of isotopically labelled 13C-TeA (purity >99%) was purchased from HPC Standards GmbH (Cunnersdorf, Germany), and dissolved in acetonitrile (CH3CN) to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL and stored at −20 °C, according to the certificate of analysis. A stock solution of TeA was prepared by dissolving the mycotoxin solid standard in methanol (CH3OH) according to the certificate of analysis. The stock solution had a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and was stored at −20 °C. The bolus for ingestion was prepared in water upon consumption by diluting the TeA stock solution after complete evaporation of the CH3OH. Ultrapure water was obtained using a purified-water system (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany). CH3OH absolute LC-MS grade, CH3CN absolute LC-MS grade, glacial acetic acid (99.95%, CH3COOH), and formic acid (99.5%, HCOOH) were purchased from BioSolve (Valkenswaard, The Netherlands).

Ethical approval

The human intervention study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Ghent University Hospital (B670201630414). Informed consent was obtained from all the volunteers before participation in the study.

Volunteer recruitment and sample collection

The invitation to participate to a human intervention study was addressed to healthy adults between the age of 20 and 65 years, not pregnant or breastfeeding. Subjects who suffer from pathologies involving liver, gastrointestinal tract, or kidneys were not included in the study, as well as subjects who were taking medications that could potentially affect the functionality of the same organs. Nine subjects were recruited and divided in intervention and control groups consisted of 4 and 5 subjects, respectively. The study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ghent University Hospital (UZGent, Gent, Belgium) through an amendment to the original dossier B670201630414. Informed consent was obtained from the volunteers before the participation. In Table S1 of the supplementary material, the average sociodemographic profile of the volunteers included in the intervention and control group are reported. All medical aspects of the study were supervised by a medical doctor of the Academic Hospital UZ Gent. All volunteers were divided into two groups, i.e. an intervention group and a control group, and were asked to follow a specific diet developed to minimize the dietary intake of Alternaria toxins during the 5-days trial. The diet consisted in avoiding foods commonly highly contaminated by Alternaria toxins (Arcella et al., 2016; European Commission, 2022) such as seeds and derivates, tomatoes and derivates, whole grains, spices, apples, pears. On the third day of the trial, and before breakfast, the intervention group ingested 5 mL of an aqueous bolus containing TeA at the TTC of 1500 ng/kg body weight per day, as proposed by EFSA (Arcella et al., 2016). The control group received 5 mL of water. Urinary samples were individually collected for 48 h after the bolus or water intake, and the participants were asked to record the void volume and the collection time point of each sample. A total of 69 samples were collected from the 9 volunteers during the 48 h: 34 samples from the intervention group and 35 samp1les from the control group. Every subject collected on average 7–8 longitudinal replicates. Samples were stored in traditional polypropylene sample containers with a screw cap at −80 °C until the day of the analysis to ensure the stability of the urinary metabolome (Laparre et al., 2017). Targeted UPLC-MS/MS analysis

The quantification of TeA in urinary samples was carried out using a targeted analysis with matrix-matched calibration and internal standard correction to compensate for the ion suppression of the matrix and for the multiple sample preparation steps, respectively. The method was previously validated in-house, in accordance to SANTE/10672/2021 and the Decision of the European Commission 2002/657/EC (European Commission, 2002). The calibration curve was built by spiking 2 mL of blank urine with the TeA standard to reach final concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 and 10 ng/mL.

The samples were extracted using the QuEChERS (Quick Easy Cheap Rugged Effective and Safe) technique: 2 mL of defrosted urine were spiked with 13C2-TeA, diluted in 18 mL of extraction solvent (CH3CN/H2O/HCOOH, 52/45/3, v/v/v), and finally added to 4 g of anhydrous magnesium sulfate and 1 g of sodium chloride. Next, the samples were shaken for 30 min at room temperature using an overhead shaker (Agilitec, J. Toulemonde, and Cie, Paris, France). Then, samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 4000 g and the supernatant was evaporated until dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream (40 °C). Finally, reconstitution was performed in 250 μL injection solvent (CH3CN/H2O, 30/70, v/v) before transferring into the appropriate vials.

Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Acquity ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Waters®, Manchester, UK) equipped with an Acquity HSS T3 100 × 2.1 mm UPLC column (1.8 μm particle size) and Acquity Vanguard HSS T3 10 × 2.1 mm UPLC pre-column (1.8 μm particle size), both from Waters® (Manchester, UK). Column and autosampler temperatures were set at 35 °C and 10 °C, respectively. The injection volume was 10 μL. Gradient elution was established with a watery mobile phase consisting of 1% acetic acid in Milli-Q Ultrapure water (mobile phase A) and an organic mobile phase consisting of pure CH3CN (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The initial condition of the gradient program was 75% A and 25% B. In 3.0 min a concave gradient increased the %B up to 85%, subsequently, the %B was increased instantly at 100% and kept in this condition for 1.5 min. At 4.5 min the %B started to decrease linearly until the initial condition that was kept constant from 6.0 min to 7.0 min to re-equilibrate the column. The total run time of the UPLC method was 7.00 min. Analytes were detected using a Waters Xevo® TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface. The desolvation gas flow rate and temperature were 1000 L/h and 650 °C, respectively. The cone gas flow rate was set at 150 L/h, and the source temperature was kept at 150 °C. The capillary voltage was optimized at 0.50 kV for ESI negative mode. The Xevo® TQ-XS mass spectrometer was operated in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The MS tune and detection parameters were optimized for TeA and its isotopically labelled analogue (13C-TeA) to maximize the sensitivity of the method. The compounds' specific MS/MS parameters are presented in Table 1. The data were acquired using the software MassLynx (version 4.2, Waters®, Manchester, UK). Eventually, the quantification was carried out using TargetLynx (version 4.2, Waters®, Manchester, UK). The method was validated for TeA to meet the criteria of European Commission (EC) decision No. 2002/657 (European Commission, 2002) and Commission Regulation (EU) No. 401/2006 (European Commission, 2006a, European Commission, 2006b) as guidance, Table 2 resumes briefly the validation parameters.

Untargeted UPLC-HRMS analysis

For untargeted analysis, the sample preparation consisted of a dilute-and-shoot approach to prevent the loss of analytes and avoid potential contaminations. Firstly, urinary samples were defrosted and allowed to acclimatize to room temperature. An aliquot of 300 μL was added with 600 μL of injection solvent (H2O/CH3CN, 95/5, v/v; 0.1% HCOOH) to reach a dilution of 1:3. Samples were vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged for 5 min at 4000×g. Finally, an aliquot of 200 μL was transferred in vials for the UPLC-HRMS analysis.

All samples were subsequently analyzed to record the complete urinary polar metabolome. Diluted urine samples were analyzed using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 XRS UPLC system, coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific®, Waltham, MA USA). The ionization was performed through heated electrospray ionization (HESI) operating in both positive and negative modes (HESI-, HESI+). The software used for data acquisition was XCalibur® (version 4.4 ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC®, Waltham, MA USA). Separation of analytes was carried out with an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) (Waters®, Manchester, UK). The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C, and the temperature of the samples was kept at 10 °C. Gradient elution was established with a mobile phase consisting of 0.1 % HCOOH in ultrapure H2O (mobile phase A) (99.9/0.1, v/v) and 0.1% HCOOH in CH3CN (mobile phase B) (99.9/0.1, v/v), at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient started at 2% B and was kept at this condition for 1.5 min. After the first isocratic step, three linear gradient steps followed. First, the % B increased to 25% at 7 min. Second, the % B increased to 60% at 8 min. Finally, the % B increased to 95% at 12 min. The latest condition was maintained isocratically until 14 min. At 14.1 min, the mobile phase B was immediately changed to the initial condition, and the column was allowed to equilibrate until 18 min. The volume of the sample injected into the UPLC system was 10 μL. The capillary voltage of the HESI source was set at 2.90 kV, and the ion transfer tube temperature and vaporizer temperature were both 300 °C. Nitrogen was applied as sheath gas (55 Arb), auxiliary gas (25 Arb) and sweep gas (3 Arb). The HRMS analyser was operating in full scan mode from 50 to 800 m/z to obtain profile data. The Orbitrap analyser was calibrated, requesting a mass error <1 ppm and a resolution of 120,000 (FWHM) at m/z 200. All the samples were analyzed randomly in a single run sequence to minimize the variability of the results. The consistency and goodness of UPLC separation and HRMS system calibration were assessed by analysing the TeA standard diluted in injection solvent to a final concentration of 50 ng/mL every 10 samples (quality control). The absence of carry-over was assessed during the run by injecting pure injection solvent every 10 samples and after the standard.

Data pre-processing of HRMS data

The raw data, obtained as. raw files, achieved from the analysis of samples, analytical standards, and blanks, were pre-processed using the software XCalibur (version 4.4, ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC®, Waltham, MA USA) to assess the presence of TeA in both HESI+ and HESI- data. The tolerance on the mass error was set at 1 ppm. Subsequently, two datasets, one for each HESI polarity, were built by importing the raw profile data of the real samples in Progenesis QI version 3.0 (Waters®, Manchester, UK). The alignment was performed upon automatic assessment of the most suitable reference run and ignoring peaks detected before 0.5 min and after 16 min. The peak picking sensitivity was set at 4, and no limits on the peak’s width were set. The two datasets were created by exporting the chromatographic areas of each feature recorded in the samples (in.csv files). The features were named based on retention time and accurate mass. The final dimensions of the two datasets were 69 × 22,168 (samples × variables) for the HESI+ and 69 × 30,508 for the HESI-. Finally, the two datasets were imported in R (R Core Team, 2022) (version 4.1.2, 2021-11-1) using Rstudio (RStudio Team, 2020). StructToolbox (Lloyd et al., 2021) (version 1.8.0) and in-house R codes were used to perform the statistical elaboration.

Chemometric data elaboration

The peak tables obtained in HESI+ (69 × 22,168) and HESI- (69 × 30,508) modes, which included the chromatographic areas of the peaks detected, were imported into RStudio. All the following steps were performed separately for the two datasets, and all data underwent a logarithmic transformation and mean centering data pre-processing steps. A PCA model was initially built to identify the potential occurrence of trends related to belonging to the two groups in an unsupervised way.

Specifically for metabolic data with multi-collinearity among variables, ropls package (Thévenot et al., 2015) was developed and tested. The OPLS-DA algorithm was applied to select the most effective classification variables and calculate the classification efficiency of the PLS-processed and reduced datasets. OPLS-DA was chosen over PLS-DA since it enables to model separately the variation correlated (predictive) to the factor of interest and the uncorrelated (orthogonal) variation (Thévenot et al., 2015; Westerhuis et al., 2010). The orthogonal distance (OD) and score distance (SD) (Hubert et al., 2005) were evaluated for each sample. Samples were stated as outliers when presenting high values for both distances (Hubert et al., 2005) and were consequently omitted from the datasets. A quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot was built to evaluate whether the data distribution was normal. For each model, both R2Y and Q2Y values were calculated as diagnostics for the variance of the class response explained and predicted, respectively (Mazzara, 2011). The closest the Q2Y and R2Y are to 1, the better the performance. R2Y is inflationary and rapidly approaches unity as model complexity increases. On the contrary, Q2Y at a certain degree of complexity, will not improve any further and then degrade (Mazzara, 2011). Models trained on datasets with a larger number of features compared with the number of samples can be prone to overfitting: in that case, high Q2Y values are obtained by chance only. A model can be considered robust and reliable when performances obtained from the test and training set are comparable, and performances obtained from the permuted class labels are significantly and systematically lower than the original model. The cross-validation (CV) approach was applied to assess the predictive performance of the models using the stratification by factor level to split the dataset in training (50% of the samples) and test set (50% of the samples), i.e. dividing the dataset into two subsets that have a similar proportion of samples for each class as the original dataset. As a further validation, an iterative permutation test (Szymańska et al., 2012) (with 20 iterations) was performed, and the validation parameters of the permuted and original models were compared to reject the hypothesis of overfitting. The p-value obtained from the permutation test is equal to the proportion of Q2Y-permuted above Q2Y-unpermuted (Hubert et al., 2005; Thévenot et al., 2015). The cumulative R2Y and Q2Y were also used to determine the optimal number of orthogonal components to consider while building and cross-validating the OPLS-DA models (Hubert et al., 2005). The performance of the models in classification was evaluated on the basis of the cumulative R2X (explained variance of the descriptor matrix), R2Y, Q2, and the root mean square error in estimation (RMSEE) (Hubert et al., 2005; Mazzara, 2011).

A volcano plot was built for each dataset after calculating the log2 fold-change, and p-value of each feature were corrected for the false discovery rate (Bhattacharjee et al., 2011). The top 100 features were selected and used to build and cross-validate the final OPLS-DA models. The final dimension of HESI- and HESI + datasets were 68 × 100. The performances of the models in classification were evaluated once again by computing the cumulative R2X, R2Y, Q2, and RMSEE (Hubert et al., 2005; Mazzara, 2011) and visually represented using confusion matrices.

Feature annotation

The identification of potential biomarkers of exposure was conducted through a variable selection strategy based on filtering the features by their p-value and fold-change. The top 100 features defined by the two volcano plots were tentatively identified by formula or mass. The formula was predicted based on the accurate mass and isotopic cluster of one or more adducts. From the two reduced datasets, features were compared with the data collections from ChemSpider and MassLists, using Compound Discoverer™ (version 3.3, ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC®, Waltham, MA USA). The databases selected for the ChemSpider search were KEGG (Kanehisa Laboratories, 2023), Human Metabolome Database (Wishart et al., 2022), FDA UNII – NLM (U.S. Food & Drug, 2018), BioCyc (SRI International, 2022), DrugBank (Wishart et al., 2017), EAWAG biocatalysis/biodegradation database (Gao and Ellis LBM, 2010), EPA DSSTox (NCCT, 2023), and EPA Toxcast (U.S. EPA, 2015). The mass error allowed in annotation was 5 ppm. Moreover, a suspect screening was performed. Potential phase I and II metabolites were generated in silico by the use of GLORY (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2019) for the cytochrome P450 phase I metabolism, and GLORYX (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2021) for the phase II metabolism, and are presented in Table S2 of supplementary material. GLORY (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2019) and GLORYX (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2021) are provided as open access on the web portal NERDD (Stork et al., 2020). The. mol file input used to compute the metabolites in Compound Discoverer™ and GLORY (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2019) contained TeA exclusively. To predict phase II metabolites with GLORYX (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2021), the. mol files of the most probable phase I metabolites of TeA were used. Specifically, the files of products of a single reduction or oxidation reaction at various positions were uploaded, Table S2 of supplementary material. The retention times of features matching with a possible metabolite were compared with the retention time of TeA, and other metabolites, to exclude in-source fragmentation, isotopes, and/or adducts.

Results

Tenuazonic biomonitoring

A human intervention trial was set up with 9 subjects to determine TeA’s biomarkers of exposure in urine. The intervention and control groups consisted of 4 and 5 subjects, respectively. The average age and BMI were 29.8 years ± 3.56 and 22.55 kg/m2 ± 3.03, respectively. The samples were screened for TeA based on accurate mass and retention time, with a limit of detection = 0.054 ng/mL and a lower limit of quantification = 0.10 ng/mL. TeA was detected for 13 h after ingestion in all participants, with maximum concentrations ranging between 19.31 ng/mL and 104.05 ng/mL, and no TeA was detected in samples originating from the control group. Chemometric data treatment was employed on the datasets obtained using Progenesis QI (69 × 22,168 HESI+, 69 × 30,508 HESI-) after logarithmic transformation and mean centering. During the first preliminary computation, one sample belonging to the intervention group was stated as an outlier and omitted from the datasets due to its OD and SD values, thus leading to the final dataset dimensions 68 × 22,168 for HESI+ and 68 × 30,508 for HESI-.

The PCA models computed on the HESI+ and HESI- original datasets showed a slight separation between the intervention and the control group. In Fig. 7, the loadings and scores plot of the resulting models are reported for the two reduced datasets. In Table 3, the validation parameters for the two OPLS-DA models are reported. As displayed in Fig. 7B, the OPLS-DA models built on the HESI- reduced dataset showed, in both cases, a polarization of the most significative features along the first predictive component (p1) with most of the features characterizing the control group. In Fig. 7D, the OPLS-DA models built on the HESI + reduced dataset showed a more balanced distribution of features along p1 and the first orthogonal components (pOrtho1). Still, both the OPLS-DA models exhibited good predictive performances, as confirmed by the validation parameters (Table 3) and the clear separation of the two groups in the scores plot (Fig. 7A and B). Moreover, the performances in the prediction of the CV models are checked separately on the training and test sets; the confusion matrices are reported in Fig. 8. Feature annotation

Data from a total of 200 HESI- and HESI + features were compared with data collections from ChemSpider and MassLists by the use of the software Compound Discoverer™ (version 3.3, ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC®, Waltham, MA USA). Through a systematic search in PubChem, Human Metabolites Database, and ChemSpider, all the exogenous compounds were excluded to circumvent the influence of the exposome. The chemometric filtering of the original UPLC-HRMS data and the consequent comparison with the data collections led to the annotation of 92 (HESI+) and 91 (HESI-) candidate biomarkers. Annotation data regarding the candidates are reported in Tables S3 and S4 (supplementary material) for HESI+ and HESI-, respectively. The features were identified at level 4 of confidence, according to Schymanski et al. (2014). These compounds are shown to be effective in the discrimination of urinary samples originating from people exposed to TeA compared with the ones from the control group.

The suspect screening was carried out on a shortlist of possible TeA metabolites generated in silico by the use of Compound Discoverer™ (for phase I and II metabolisms), GLORY (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2019) for the cytochrome P450 phase I metabolism, and GLORYX (de Bruyn Kops et al., 2021) for the phase II metabolism. The research of the metabolites generated in silico led to the detection of 9 features of the experimental data that were present only in the samples of the intervention group and matched with possible metabolites of TeA. Annotation data regarding the 9 metabolites are reported in Table 4 and the corresponding chromatograms are reported in Fig. 9.

Discussion

Tenuazonic acid biomonitoring and OPLS-DA models

TeA was detected only for 13 h after a single dose intake and the average fraction extracted was only 0.39 ± 0.22 in urine, thus leading to a substantial underestimation in assessing and reporting the exposure. The value obtained for the fraction excreted is in line with previous studies conducted on rats (Puntscher et al., 2019). However, the value is significantly lower than 87–93% reported for a study conducted on two human subjects after consuming TeA-contaminated food (Asam et al., 2013). The discrepancy between the results may be attributed to the different bioavailability of TeA when ingested alongside food compared to when it is ingested as pure watery bolus (Polaka et al., 2022). Nevertheless, the urinary excretion pattern reported by Asam et al. (2013) is consistent with the elimination profile reported in Fig. 2 that shows the major part of TeA being eliminated in the first 6 h.

The HESI+ and HESI- untargeted UPLC-HRMS analyses and following chemometric filtering resulted in the detection of 200 discriminant candidates. PCA was used as the exploratory data analysis to highlight the presence of grouping or patterns in the distribution of the samples that could be linked to belonging to one of the two groups. The PCA scores plot, reported in Fig. 3, showed that the intervention and control group samples exhibited differences in position and dispersion in the PCs space. Subsequently, OPLS-DA models were built to successfully demonstrate the features' ability to discriminate the samples between the two groups. Metabolomic studies are characterized by disproportionate datasets presenting a high number of features extracted from the chromatograms. The use of disproportionate or imbalanced datasets increases the risk of overfitting (Amante et al., 2022). CV and permutation tests (Szymańska et al., 2012) were performed to avoid overfitting and demonstrate the robustness of the models. The CV applied consisted of splitting the dataset into a training dataset, containing 50% of the original dataset, and a test dataset, with the remaining 50%. A new model is built on the training dataset, and the performances are verified on the test set, multiple iterations of CV are performed using different partitions. The samples were randomly labelled In an iterative permutation test, and a new classification model was calculated each time (Szymańska et al., 2012).

The cumulative Q2Y and R2Y metrics assessed the predictive performance of the whole model. The diagrams in Fig. 5 demonstrate that, for both HESI- and HESI+, the performances of the models with permuted labels are not comparable to those of the original models. In fact, despite the high values for R2Y, all the permuted models had low Q2Y and similarity values confirming that OPLS-DA can specifically model the y response with the X predictors, as expected.

From the comparison between the OPLS-DA scores plots built on the original (Fig. S1, supplementary material) and reduced datasets (Fig. 7), it can be seen that the samples of the two classes are more focused when filtering only the 100 most important variables, thus leading to a higher performance of the model. The results regarding the CV reported in Table 3 confirm the robustness of the four models. It can be noticed that the models built on the reduced datasets containing the top 100 features identified from the volcano plot exhibit better performances in terms of R2X(cum) and Q2Y(cum) than the original ones. The improvement may be attributed in the first place to the reduction of complexity of the models (Q2Y(cum) value) and, in the second place, to the depletion of not informative features (R2Y(cum) value).

The importance of building a multivariate model can be highlighted when evaluating the efficacy of the OPLS-DA models constructed using HESI+ and HESI- variables, in comparison to the outcomes from TeA biomonitoring. The CV OPLS-DA model demonstrated its capability in distinguishing individuals exposed to a singular dose of TeA within the previous 48 h, thereby extending the TeA exposure detection window to a minimum of 35 h. The mycotoxin was detected in urine only during the first 13 h post-intake, resulting in nearly half (49%) of the samples from the intervention group being negative. On the contrary, the models built on the two datasets can determine the state of exposure of a subject with high accuracy (0.88 for the HESI+ and 0.85 for HESI- on the test set). Metabolic fingerprinting uses multivariate analysis methods to identify those biologically relevant spectral features that characterise a perturbed system for further targeted analyses (Dettmer et al., 2007; Han et al., 2009; Worley and Powers, 2012). Multivariate methods have shown to facilitate the discovery of candidates characterized by enhanced specificity and sensitivity, especially in scenarios where the number of metabolites is substantial (Tyagi et al., 2020). The models obtained in this study presented an innovative way to distinguish a subject whose body is perturbed by the presence of TeA. This represents a significant achievement since it demonstrates that metabolic pathway(s) are influenced by TeA presence in the human body and that TeA is probably metabolized, thereby opening opportunities for further research and exploration in this domain.

Annotation of potential metabolites

To date, metabolites and biomarkers of exposure to TeA are unknown. However, the OPLS-DA models built on polar metabolomic data demonstrated that the metabolism is influenced and, therefore, metabolization of TeA may occur. Starting from the two original HESI+ (68 × 22,168) and HESI- (68 × 30,508) datasets, the application of OPLS-DA and the computation of the log2 fold-change and p-value permitted to filter out the most promising candidates leading to the creation of two reduced HESI+ (68 × 100) and HESI- (68 × 100) datasets. The shortlist of 200 candidates was processed in Compound Discoverer™ leading to the annotation of 193 compounds at a confidence level 4 according to Schymanski et al. (2014). Through a systematic PubChem (Kim et al., 2023), Human Metabolome Database (Wishart et al., 2022), and ChemSpider (Royal Society of Chemistry, 2007) search, all the exogenous compounds were excluded to circumvent the impact of the individual external exposome of the volunteers. The final 122 filtered compounds, reported in Table S3 (HESI +) and Table S4 (HESI -) of the supplementary material, showed to be systematically influenced by the exposure to TeA. The over- or underexcretion in the urine of these polar metabolites suggests that TeA influenced the metabolism and may be involved in metabolic pathways. The compounds belong to different chemical classes such as amino acids, sugars, peptides, hormones, and their derivates. Several compounds reported in Tables S3 and S4 are already known to have some important biological roles. N-myristoyl aspartic acid (row 37, Table S3) belongs to the class of N-acylamides that has a variety of signaling functions (Bradshaw and Walker, 2005) in physiology, including in cell migration, inflammation (Raboune et al., 2014) and certain pathological conditions such as diabetes (Grapov et al., 2012), cancer, neurodegenerative disease, and obesity. Indole-3-acetic-acid-O-glucuronide (row 38, Table S3 and row 40, Table S4) is the glucuronidated form of a terpene that exhibits a role in chronic kidney disease(Miao et al., 2020), metabolic syndrome (Esperanza et al., 2020), and breast cancer (Li et al., 2019). In Table S4, dimethyl itaconate and cortisol sulfate are reported (rows 15 and 56). They both act as inflammatory suppressors (Kuo et al., 2020; Mavroudis et al., 2015), nevertheless, cortisol sulfate, which is obtained by conjugation of cortisol and sulfuric acid, is a hormone influenced also by the stress level.

The detection of 9 of the in silico-predicted metabolites confirmed that TeA is metabolized in the body as expected after the OPLSDA modelling and the fraction excreted unmodified (0.39 ± 0.22). The molecule is susceptible to both phase I and II reactions, in some cases in combination with N-dealkylation (Table 4). Specifically, dehydration, desaturation, oxidation, and acetylation products were detected, as well as products of conjugation with glucuronic and sulfuric acid. The N-alkylation is not influencing the molecular formula of the molecular ion since it is causing the opening of the 1H-pyrrol-2-one ring. Several features are matching with more than one possible positional or structural isomer. As pinpointed in Table S2 (supplementary material), each phase I and II reactions can occur in different positions on the molecule of TeA leading to different positional isomers that, unfortunately, HRMS cannot distinguish. These findings put the base for further investigations about the enzymes involved in the transformation of TeA and the organ(s) where the transformations take place. The enzymes responsible for the conjugation of substrates with glucuronic acid and sulfuric acid are mainly belonging to the classes of UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs) and sulfotransferases (SULTs) that are found in liver, but also in the intestines and kidneys (Chapman et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2017). TeA possibly acts as cytochrome P450 substrate, such as other Alternaria toxins (Pfeiffer et al., 2008). Nevertheless, further investigations are necessary to confirm this hypothesis, and identify the individual CYP, UGT, and SULT isoforms involved, eventually providing a rationale for organ-specific metabolism (Pfeiffer et al., 2008).

Limitations and strengths

The main limitations of the chemometric filtering approach applied in this study are represented by the low sample size that limits the generalizability of the findings and the statistical power of the analyses. The present study involved a toxicokinetic trial and this inherently pose certain limitations on sample size, particularly when it relates to human subjects. These studies often impose a smaller sample size due to ethical, logistical, and practical constraints. The sample set was kept as general as possible including healthy subjects of both gender and from 20 to 65 years old. In other comparative studies investigating biomarkers of exposure (Maruvada et al., 2020), the general nutrition of all participants is regulated during feeding studies to minimize the differences that might be attributed to different nutritional habits. However, due to the necessity of collecting samples for up to 48 h, a less invasive and time consuming asset that could be performed at home was prioritized – which is a considerable advantage of this study. Through a systematic search in PubChem, Human Metabolites Database, and ChemSpider, all the exogenous compounds were excluded from the filtered features to circumvent the possible influence of the exposome. Metabolic fingerprinting experiments aim to determine the relative differences between the metabolomes of two or more systems to hypothesize a biological relationship. Metabolic fingerprinting uses multivariate analysis methods to identify those biologically relevant spectral features for further targeted analyses (Worley and Powers, 2012). Techniques such as PCA and PLS-DA provide an essential platform for rapidly interpreting of information-rich spectral datasets. Partial Least-Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) is a commonly used machine learning tool that represents a useful approach for feature selection and supervised classification purposes (Trygg et al., 2007). Studies recommend a minimum sample size of 200 cases (Marsh et al., 1998). PLS-DA, on the other hand, is applicable to very small sample sizes. In fact, a Monte Carlo simulation conducted by Chin and Newsted (1999) showed that PLS-DA can be performed with a sample size as small as 50, and Wold (1975) even analyzed 27 variables using two latent constructs with a data set consisting of ten cases. However, taken consistency into account, no rule regulates the proper amount of data to be analyzed. Metabolomics datasets are usually characterized by large volume, large number of features, noise, and, eventually, missing data, due to the nature of the metabolomics spectral signals. In case of highly dimensional data, PLS-DA strives to discern the variation correlated with the clinical outcome by building overly complex models (Bylesjö et al., 2006; Trygg and Wold, 2002). Therefore, in this study orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were chosen over the classic PLS-DA to disentangle group-predictive and group-unrelated variations in the measured data (Worley and Powers, 2016). OPLS-DA was applied, through the ropls package (Thévenot et al., 2015), since it was specifically developed for metabolic data characterized by multi-collinearity among variables. The data elaboration is an exploratory and qualitative approach, emphasizing in-depht understanding of the fate of TeA. The results obtained are the first steps towards the complete understanding of TeA toxicokinetics and metabolization. Nevertheless, the results must be necessarily confirmed in the framework of a large-scale biomonitoring study and the validation of potential biomarkers of exposure must be performed. The trial’s design proposed in this study was applied for the first time by Vidal et al. (2018) and provided pivotal insights into the metabolization of DON and its toxicokinetic properties (Martins et al., 2019; Mengelers et al., 2019). The insights generated for TeA provide a foundation for subsequent research, including biomonitoring studies on different cohorts (Gerding et al., 2015; Martins et al., 2022; Tesfamariam et al., 2022) and population based pharmacokinetic modelling (Deepika & Kumar, 2023; Mengelers et al., 2019). The results obtained combined with population exposure data, offer a comprehensive toxicokinetic description that will set the basis for more accurate quantitative risk assessment (Arcella et al., 2016; De Ruyck et al., 2020).

Conclusions

TeA is a mycotoxin that has bioactivity against microbes, plants, humans, and animals (Kumari and Tirkey, 2019), and showed cytotoxicity against human enterocytes and hepatocytes (den Hollander et al., 2022). To date, biomarkers of exposure and/or metabolites of TeA are unknown (Martins et al., 2020). Therefore, the exposure assessment of TeA is achieved by biomonitoring the unmodified molecule. TeA was detectable only for 13 h after a single dose intake and the average fraction extracted was only 0.39 ± 0.22 in urine as demonstrated by our research findings, thus leading to a substantial underestimation in assessing and reporting the exposure. The UPLC-HRMS approach, data treatment, and chemometric model proved to be able to extend the detection window up to 48 h from the exposure by considering the metabolome. The performances of the models in discriminating subjects exposed to TeA demonstrate that metabolic pathway(s) are influenced by the exposure to TeA and suggests that TeA may be metabolized. Moreover, the chemometric filtering was demonstrated to be an efficient method to state 122 potential biomarkers of exposure that showed to be effective in discriminating the group exposed to TeA from the control group. Furthermore, we predicted in silico possible metabolites and subsequently detected 9 of them in human urine, including oxidized, N-alkylated, and desaturated, glucuronidated, and sulfonated forms of TeA. As previously reported, HRMS cannot distinguish the different isomers leading once again to a confidence level 4 of annotation. However, to the authors knowledge, this is the first time that metabolites of TeA are detected in humans. Other Alternaria toxins acts as cytochrome P450 substrate (Pfeiffer et al., 2008) and the identification of oxidated, N-alkylated, and desaturated forms of TeA suggests that also TeA may act in a similar role. Nevertheless, the identification of the individual CYP isoforms and other enzymes involved in TeA transformation and transformation sites requires additional studies. As discussed, the obtained results need to be confirmed on a larger cohort and differences in metabolic rate depending on gender and age should be investigated.
