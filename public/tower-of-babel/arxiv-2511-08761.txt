Average density of Bloch electrons in a homogeneous magnetic field: a second-order response
Benjamin M. Fregoso

Abstract
We compute the average density of a three-dimensional multiband crystal of arbitrary symmetry, metal or insulator, to first and second order in a weak static magnetic field. To linear order and for insulators, the density follows the well-known Streda formula, but for metals there is an extra contribution from the orbital magnetic moments at the Fermi surface. To second order, we find that the average density depends on several microscopic processes. Among these, the quantum metric tensor plays an important role by generating a pseudo-magnetic moment resulting from the rotation of the Bloch wave functions in the complex projective plane. We also discuss the implications of our results for the volume and pressure. The method we develop is explicitly gauge invariant, considers intraband and interband processes on equal footing, accommodates relaxation processes, and can be readily extended to other observables.

Generally, the average density can be expanded in powers of the magnetic field as

\displaystyle n=n^{(0)}+\varsigma^{(1)a}B^{a}+\varsigma^{(2)ab}B^{a}B^{b}+\cdots.

(1)

The first term on the right-hand side represents the density in the absence of magnetic field, the second term is the linear response, and the third is the nonlinear response. Since the density is even under time reversal while the magnetic field is odd, the linear response vanishes if time reversal symmetry (TRS) is present.

If TRS is broken, the linear response is dominant and we expect the (pseudo) tensor
\varsigma^{(1)}
to follow the well-known Streda formula
Widom 1982
;
Streda 1982
;
MacDonald and St

r

eda 1984

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mathbf{B}}\right)_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{e}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{H},

(2)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{H}
when the chemical potential lies within an energy gap. This relation was originally derived in the context of strong magnetic fields but later found to apply across a wide range of field strengths, even in the presence of electron interactions. It predicts that the average density changes in discrete steps when the anomalous Hall conductivity is quantized with nonzero Chern number. Indeed, it has been used as a diagnostic tool to measure the Chern numbers
Xu et al.
;
Huang et al.

Xiao et al. 2005

\varsigma^{(1)}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mathbf{B}}=-\frac{1}{e}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{H}+\frac{1}{V}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}f^{\prime}_{n}\mathbf{m}_{n}.

(3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{n}^{(2)zz}\sim e^{2}g_{n}^{xx}v_{n}^{y}v_{n}^{y},

(4)

1

n^{(2)}/n^{(0)}\sim 10^{-4}\%

In Sec.

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=V^{(0)}+v^{(1)a}B^{a}+v^{(2)ab}B^{a}B^{b}+\cdots,

(6)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=P^{(0)}+p^{(1)a}B^{a}+p^{(2)ab}B^{a}B^{b}+\cdots,

II
Particle density

We consider non-interacting electrons on a 3D lattice whose single-particle Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle h=\frac{1}{2m}(\mathbf{p}-e\mathbf{A})^{2}+U,

(8)

where
e<0
is the electron charge, and
U
is the ionic periodic potential. Without loss of generality, the magnetic field is taken along the
z
-axis and represented by the vector potential
\mathbf{A}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}(B^{z}/q)\sin(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{r})
. The magnetic field is spatially modulated by a wavevector
\mathbf{g}=q\hat{\mathbf{x}}
. We introduce the parameter
q
to regularize the singular nature of the position operator in the Bloch basis
Shi et al. 2007
. Since we are interested in the response to a homogenous magnetic field, we take the
q\to 0
limit at the end of the calculation. In this limit, the magnetic field is homogeneous:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}B^{z}.

(9)

In addition, at every step of our calculation our expressions are invariant under the phase transformation of Bloch states,
|n\mathbf{k}\rangle\to e^{i\alpha(\mathbf{k})}|n\mathbf{k}\rangle
(we also call it gauge invariance).

The particle density operator

\displaystyle\hat{n}=\psi^{\dagger}\psi,

(10)

can be expanded in Bloch operators
a_{n\mathbf{k}}
as
\psi=\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{n\mathbf{k}}a_{n\mathbf{k}}
, where
\varphi_{n\mathbf{k}}
is a Bloch wave function with band
n
and crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
. Its expectation value is then

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\mathbf{k}m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\varphi^{*}_{n\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rho_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}n\mathbf{k}},

(11)

where
\rho_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}n\mathbf{k}}\equiv\langle a_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rangle
is the density matrix in the Bloch basis (see Appendix
A
). Because the magnetic field varies spatially, it is convenient to expand the density in its spatial Fourier modes as

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=n_{0}+n_{\mathbf{g}}\cos(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{r})+n_{2\mathbf{g}}\cos(2\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{r})+\cdots,

(12)

where

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{G}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{V}\int d\mathbf{r}~n\cos{(\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r})}.~~~~~(\mathbf{G}\neq 0)

(13)

The
\cdots
represents higher order Fourier modes. The expansion also contains
sine
modes, but, as shown in Appendix.
C
, these vanish. Here,
n_{0}
is the density in the absence of a magnetic field,
n_{\mathbf{g}}
corresponds to the linear correction, and
n_{2\mathbf{g}}
to the quadratic correction. Substituting (
11
) into (
13

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{G}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{V}\textrm{Re}\sum_{nm\mathbf{k}}\langle u_{n\mathbf{k}}|u_{m\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{G}}\rangle\rho_{m\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{G}n\mathbf{k}},

(14)

which is valid to all orders in
B^{z}
and for arbitrary
\mathbf{G}
. The average density is then

\displaystyle n-n_{0}=\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}n_{\mathbf{G}},

(15)

where the sum runs over
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{g},2\mathbf{g}

VI
Example: intraband contribution

We explicitly calculate the first term in (

\displaystyle h_{0\mathbf{k}}=v\hbar(k^{x}\sigma^{y}-k^{y}\sigma^{x}).

\displaystyle n^{(2)}_{11+21}

\displaystyle\approx n^{(0)}(\chi_{F}B^{z})^{2}4\pi^{2}\alpha

is a dimensionless measure of the geometric

VII
Example: magnetovolume effect

From semiclassical dynamics, it is known that the k-space volume of a wavepacket changes in the presence of a magnetic field
Xiao et al. 2005
. A similar effect occurs in crystals. The particle number is fixed by the background ionic charge, but the crystal must maintain its infinite, periodic electronic density. Here, we assume an ideal infinite crystal with no boundaries; the case with boundaries will be analyzed elsewhere. In other words, the electronic density cannot accumulate in a particular region of the crystal. This means that the Brillouin zone volume must adjusts its size (perhaps its shape too) to comply with the change in average density. We can expand the volume in powers of the field as

\displaystyle V=V^{(0)}+v^{(1)a}B^{a}+v^{(2)ab}B^{a}B^{b}+\cdots,

(38)

and since
n=N/V
, the fractional change in volume is

\displaystyle\frac{V^{(1)}}{V^{(0)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n^{(1)}}{n^{(0)}},

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{V^{(2)}}{V^{(0)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{V^{(1)}}{V^{(0)}}\right)^{2}-\frac{n^{(2)}}{n^{(0)}}.

(40)

If TRS is present,
V^{(1)}=0
, but if TRS is broken

\displaystyle\frac{n^{(0)}}{V^{(0)}}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathbf{B}}\right)_{\mu}=\frac{1}{e}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{H},

(41)

suggesting discontinuous jumps in the volume as a function of the magnetic field in the quantum Hall regime (assuming this expression remains valid in that limit). As the volume attempts to change, the elastic degrees resist, generating a pressure differential. The pressure can also be expanded in powers of the magnetic field as

\displaystyle P=P^{(0)}+p^{(1)a}B^{a}+p^{(2)ab}B^{a}B^{b}+\cdots,

(42)

and, assuming constant compressibility,

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{1}{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial P},

(43)

we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa P^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{(1)}}{n^{(0)}},

(44)

\displaystyle\kappa P^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n^{(1)}}{n^{(0)}}\right)^{2}+\frac{n^{(2)}}{n^{(0)}}.

(45)

If TRS holds,
P^{(1)}=0
, but if TRS is broken

\displaystyle\kappa n^{(0)}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\mathbf{B}}\right)_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{e}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{H}.

(46)

Magnetovolume effects such as these are well known in invar alloys
Takahashi 2013
, where they arise from itinerant spins and are usually studied using phenomenological models.

VIII
Example: Geometric response

There are terms in the nonlinear response which depend explicitly on the quantum metric. The origin of some of them is, intuitively, the rotation of the Bloch wave function in the complex projective plane as the electron moves in k-space. This mechanism is independent of spin or orbital angular momentum and, in this sense, it is a
geometric
response. Here we identify two such contributions. First, we collect terms which depend explicitly on the quantum metric and on
f^{\prime\prime}_{n}
. These are easier to interpret since the factor multiplying
f^{\prime\prime}_{n}
is necessarily a magnetic moment squared. These terms are the 1st term of
105
, 7th term of
110
, and last term of
111
, and their contribution for arbitrary field direction is

\displaystyle n^{(2)}_{geo}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}f^{\prime\prime}_{n}B^{c}\left(\mathfrak{m}_{n}^{(2)cc^{\prime}}+\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}^{(2)cc^{\prime}}\right)B^{c^{\prime}},

(47)

where the quadratic
geometric
moments are

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{n}^{(2)cc^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\epsilon_{cab}\epsilon_{c^{\prime}a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}g^{aa^{\prime}}_{n}v^{b}_{n}v^{b^{\prime}}_{n},

(48)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}^{(2)cc^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{cc^{\prime}}e^{2}\epsilon_{cab}g^{aa}_{n}v^{b}_{n}v^{b}_{n}.

(49)

In Sec.
VI
we showed that in a simple model one of this terms (
35
) dominates the intraband response.

IX
Conclusion

We have studied the average density of a clean 3D crystal in a static homogeneous magnetic field. We show that for insulators, in linear response, the average density agrees with the well-known Streda formula. For metals, or more generally when particle occupations in k-space have discontinuities, there is an additional contribution arising from the orbital magnetic moments at the FS. This may have implications for itinerant ferromagnets in the anomalous quantum Hall regime (assuming the expression can be extrapolated to that case).

To quadratic order, we derived a general expression for the second-order density response in terms of generic Bloch wave functions.
This expression is complex, as it accounts for the most general case of a multiband 3D crystal, with arbitrary symmetry, metallic or insulating. The expression include FS, non-FS, one-band, two-band, and three-band contributions. The relative magnitude of these contributions should be analyzed on a case-by-case basis, ideally using first-principles numerical codes. Interestingly, the quantum metric tensor induces a magnetic moment due to rotations of the Bloch wave function in the complex projective plane. In a simple two-band model, we find that the quantum metric provides the dominant contribution to the intraband (
n=m=l
) response. We also showed that density variations manifest corresponding change in volume or pressure and derived explicit expressions for these magnetovolume effects.

Finally, the transverse resistivity as a function of magnetic field typically exhibits a linear response at small magnetic fields and a nonlinear behavior at larger fields (
\sim 1
T). The carrier density is usually extracted from the slope in the linear regime using a semi-classical two-carrier model
Ziman 2001
. The quadratic corrections to the density derived here may apply to the nonlinear regime of the transverse resistivity
Gao
.

The method developed here is free of singularities, explicitly gauge invariant, treats intraband and interband processes on equal footing, can accommodate relaxation, and can be extended to analyze electronic behavior well beyond semiclassical dynamics.
