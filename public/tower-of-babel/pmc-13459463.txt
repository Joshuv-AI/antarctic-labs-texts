The creative personality or general factor of personality? Integrating the big five personality traits in relation to creativity
Authors: Peiqian Wu, Dimitri Van der Linden, Saifang Liu, Mengyun Wang, Curtis S Dunkel, Paraskevas Petrou, Marise Ph Born
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCreativity has been found to be a fundamental factor in innovation and generating new knowledge, and it is influenced by personality. The present study innovatively tested the personality-creativity relationship by resorting to the General Factor of Personality (GFP). Multiple measures were used for a comprehensive and unbiased estimation of the target relationship while controlling for the common method bias. The results showed that the GFP was consistently associated with multiple creativity indicators, with observed correlations above .60 for questionnaire-based measures and latent estimates ranging from .31 to .40 for performance-based abilities. Based on the findings, the present study indicates a stable association between the GFP and several core components of creativity. Additionally, the present study found that the GFP correlated with creative traits (.75) more strongly than creative abilities (.36), based on which we proposed a division of creative traits vs creative abilities. The reason for all found correlations can be attributed to the GFP being the most comprehensive personality structure encapsulating social characteristics, which is associated with multiple components of creativity. The progress of human civilization throughout history has been greatly influenced by the presence of remarkable geniuses. Newton, Beethoven, and Van Gogh are among the greatest geniuses in human history, known for their profound knowledge, innovation, and aesthetic masterpieces. One intriguing question is what made them extraordinary and gave them their drive to innovate. One obvious answer is creativity [71]. For innovation [88] and generating new knowledge [4], creativity is a fundamental resource that has been found to be largely influenced by personality [26, 35]. As such, studying creative personalities may help to elucidate the drivers of innovation. Although many studies have examined the creativity of geniuses, it has become clear that many of the processes underlying creativity can also be tested in general samples, as creativity is considered to be a continuum. Accordingly, in the present study, we will elucidate the creativity-personality relationship in general samples using the perspective of a General Factor of Personality [28]: A factor that comprises the shared variance of lower-order personality dimensions such as the Big Five and mostly captures the socially desirable ends of behavior. By doing so, we aim to provide a more comprehensive and parsimonious understanding of the link between personality and creativity.

Creativity and personality

Creativity is a complex psychological phenomenon, of which there are over fifty different definitions in the literature. One commonly used definition is that “Creativity is the interaction among aptitude, process, and the environment by which an individual or group produces a perceptible product that is both novel and useful as defined within a social context.” [58] p.90, original italics). To generate creativity, there are six putative resources: personality, intelligence, knowledge, intellectual style, motivation, and environment. Among those resources, personality was thought to be a crucial one, which not only underlies creative potential [36] but also contributes to the realization of creative potential [46]. Since Guilford's [32] seminal work on traits of creativity, large numbers of studies on creative personality have been published.

Many personality characteristics of creative individuals have been identified, such as high valuation of aesthetic qualities, broad interests, attraction to complexity, high energy, independence of judgment, activeness, alertness, ambition, argumentativeness, artistic expression, and assertiveness, etc. [7, 34]. However, to systematically reduce the number of creative personality characteristics, the majority of studies on creative personality have used the influential Big Five personality model [31, 49].

The Big Five model [48] assumes that there are five basic traits in the personality structure: Openness to new experiences (O), Conscientiousness (C), Extraversion (E), Agreeableness (A), and Neuroticism (N, the opposite of Emotional stability). Each of the Big Five traits has been found to relate to creativity [27, 45]. Among the five traits, O and E were found to be the two most important predictors of creativity. As reported in the meta-analysis of [59], O and E were related to general creativity with values of ρ = 0.24 and 0.14, respectively. In contrast, the other three traits showed lower correlations with general creativity, with values of ρ = 0.02 (C), 0.03 (A), and −0.04 (N). It is worth noting, however, that in their meta-analysis, Puryear and colleagues also found that, for different measures of creativity, the Big Five traits showed different correlational patterns. Therefore, [60] conducted a follow-up study and found that the correlation between the Big Five traits and creativity depends on the type of creativity measure. Different measures may assess different aspects of creativity, and as such, show differential relationships with the Big Five. Given that multiple dimensions of personality seem to be involved in various aspects of creativity, it may be useful to study creativity in relation to a construct that integrates personality traits. In line with recent literature, one way of doing so is by using the General Factor of Personality [28].

Beyond the big five: the general factor of personality

Although the Big Five traits are often considered to reflect the most basic and independent personality dimensions, empirical studies have made it clear that the traits are systematically covary [17]. [53] reported that the Big Five covariance can be explained by the existence of a single higher-order factor, often referred to as the General Factor of Personality or GFP. The GFP was found in individuals' personality structure across nations and cultures and depicts a composite pattern of the Big Five: + O, + C, + E, + A, and -N (or + ES; [80, 83]. Since the GFP integrates all the Big Five traits, it can be thought of as a comprehensive assessment of personality. Regarding the implication of the GFP, empirical research suggests that the GFP is indicative of social effectiveness or adaptiveness—the ability to deal successfully with the social aspects of life to achieve one's goals (Dunkel & [21, 74, 78]. Persons with higher GFP scores indeed seem to have social advantages, such as being popular among classmates [81], well adaptive to university life [86], obtaining leadership positions [84], or receiving good ratings on job performance [56], etc. However, the notion of a substantive GFP was also criticized. [3] considered the GFP mainly as a statistical artifact, while [6] considered the GFP to be a social desirability bias. The debate involving these different views continues and has been well-documented in the literature (e.g., [74, 76, 78]). What is important, however, is that there is now a sufficient body of empirical research confirming that the GFP has wide criterion-related validity [54] and is useful in predicting social-related outcomes [74, 77, 78]. One large meta-analysis (k = 142; N = 36,268) even identified the GFP as a synonym for the trait Emotional Intelligence, since both structures extremely overlapped (r ≈ 0.85, [79]. A recent study even verified the GFP using natural language and deep learning technics [82].

The GFP and creativity

When adopting a substantive account of the GFP, there are several reasons to expect that the GFP positively relates to creativity. First, it is assumed that the GFP, as a higher-order factor, plays a role in some of the specific attitudes and behaviors connected with its underlying dimensions [55]. Therefore, the GFP can be expected to play a role in creativity, which has been found to be related to O and E [59]. To conceptually integrate the notions of personality and creativity, [27] proposed four creativity-related psychological categories: social, cognitive, motivational, and affective. In those categories, each of the Big Five can be expected to have a position. Specifically, Extraversion and Agreeableness can be clustered into being predominantly social since they focus on one's attitudes and interactions with others. Openness would be linked to cognitive dispositions such as ideation. In carrying out creative work, motivational characteristics such as persistence, drive, and discipline are needed, which can be captured by Conscientiousness. The last category—affective—would obviously cluster with Neuroticism. Therefore, the GFP, by representing the common variances of the Big Five [53], can be considered a concept that integrates personality and creativity across Feist's categories.

Within Feist’s social category, a second reason to expect a link between GFP and creativity is the notion that being creative does not only involve generating new ideas but also has a significant social component. Conveying new ideas to others or implementing them requires the ability to convince others, form strategic alliances, and persist in the face of critique. The GFP includes characteristics that support those mentioned abilities. However, the presumed relation between the GFP and social effectiveness does not imply that individuals with high GFP scores are always friendly or cooperative. A more accurate description would be that they have the social and emotional agility to adapt their behavior to maximize the odds of achieving their personal goals [76]. The supportive evidence can be seen from the large overlaps of the GFP with not only the aforementioned emotional intelligence [65] but also ego resilience (r = 0.93, N = 15,609, [25]. Interestingly, both emotional intelligence and ego resilience [9] have been linked to creativity in previous studies. For example, [67] found that trait EI, defined as self-perceptions of emotional abilities, correlated with general creativity, and [51] found an association between ego resilience and creativity. Based on these findings, it can be expected that the GFP would also show a relationship with creativity.

In recent literature, [42] did not find a direct correlation between GFP and creative achievement, as measured by the Creative Achievement Questionnaire. However, they did discover a negative correlation between GFP and lawbreaking behavior (r =—0.36). It is important to note that lawbreaking is often considered a positive indicator of creativity [15], so the GFP-lawbreaking correlation may partly reflect the GFP-creativity correlation. More recently, [66] tested the relationship between GFP and idea evaluation—a typically creative process. They found that GFP was indeed linked to interpersonal and social creative skills. High-GFP individuals provided better evaluations of peer ideas compared to low-GFP individuals. The authors recommend that creativity scholars consider using GFP in their future work due to the parsimony of the general factor.

While the Big Five framework has significantly advanced our understanding of the creative personality, empirical studies have made it clear that these traits systematically covary [17]. Utilizing the General Factor of Personality (GFP) offers distinct theoretical and methodological advantages over isolated dimensional approaches. Theoretically, we conceptualize the GFP as reflecting social effectiveness and ego-resiliency [25, 74, 78]. Creativity is inherently dual-staged: it requires not only the cognitive capacity to generate novel ideas (often linked to Openness) but also the social and emotional resilience to express, justify, and implement these ideas within a social context [27]. The GFP parsimoniously encapsulates this precise constellation of adaptive characteristics, including the sociability required for idea promotion, the conscientiousness necessary for persistence, and the emotional stability needed to withstand critique. Methodologically, extracting the GFP accounts for the covariance among the Big Five, resolving multicollinearity and providing a more robust, parsimonious predictor of complex, real-world criteria like creative performance than any single dimension [66]. By adopting a GFP perspective, the present study tests whether a unified, higher-order personality structure can holistically capture the creative personality.

Furthermore, recent literature underscores the utility of this higher-order construct in predicting creative outcomes. Contemporary research increasingly conceptualizes the GFP as an index of general social effectiveness and ego-resiliency [25, 77], reflecting an individual's capacity to adaptively modify self-control to fit environmental demands. Within the domain of creativity, this adaptability is critical. A recent preprint meta-analytically demonstrates that the GFP correlated with creativity (ρ = 0.22; N = 56,748) and Creative Self-beliefs (ρ = 0.50; N = 19,861; [85]. Furthermore, high-resolution investigations into the creative personality, such as recent machine learning analyses of divergent thinking, indicate that creative output (e.g., fluency and originality) is driven by a complex tension among multiple personality attributes rather than a single trait like Openness [19]. By capturing the shared variance across these diverse traits, the GFP provides a parsimonious framework to encapsulate the integrated, socially adaptive characteristics required to not only generate novel ideas but also successfully implement and champion them.

Current study

The present study aimed to investigate the direct correlation between GFP and creativity. In designing the tests, we took into account that the way creativity is measured can significantly affect the correlation between creativity and personality [60]. Therefore, we used multiple measures of creativity, as suggested by [30]. To assess the various components of creativity, we used self-rated creativity as a measure of creative self-beliefs [41] and peer-reported creativity to measure observable creative performance [72]. These measures were used to capture creative traits—dispositional characteristics for creativity [40]. Additionally, we included two tests to measure creative abilities [13], namely the Remote Association Test (RAT) and the Alternative Uses Test (AUT), which are presumed to measure convergent [50] and divergent thinking [18], respectively.

While previous studies have referred to creative traits and abilities [13, 40], to our knowledge, no published article has explicitly considered both as divisions of creativity measures. However, we adopted this division in the present study to compare both types of variables in their correlations with GFP. We did so because we noted a similarity with findings in another but related field, namely trait EI measured with self-reports [57], and ability EI measured with tests [47]. The current study advances existing literature both theoretically and methodologically [40]. Theoretically, we expand the conceptualization of the "creative personality" beyond traditional cognitive traits by positing that creativity requires the social effectiveness and ego-resiliency parsimoniously captured by the GFP [25, 74, 78]. Methodologically, we utilize a multi-method approach to overcome the limitations of single-source assessments while carefully distinguishing between distinct constructs [13]. Specifically, we distinguish creative self-beliefs (self-perceptions of efficacy; [41] and peer-perceived creative traits (social-evaluative perceptions of typical creative behavior; [72] from objective creative abilities [18]. These abilities refer to the actual capacity for divergent and convergent problem-solving as demonstrated in performance-based tests, namely the Alternative Uses Task (AUT; [18] and the Remote Association Test (RAT; [50]. This refined taxonomy allows for a more rigorous and nuanced understanding of how a socially adaptive personality structure relates to different components of the creative process while strictly controlling for common method variance (CMV).

Method

Samples and procedures

The study was approved by the Academic Ethics Committee of Anhui Normal University (Approval No. AHNU-ET2024139). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study began. Participants were informed of the study's purpose, their right to withdraw at any time, and the confidentiality of their data. Before data collection, we calculated the required sample size by means of power calculations (for correlational tests) with the R package “pwr” [11] and found that to approach power 0.95, a minimum of 46 participants is needed. Nevertheless, for stable estimates, we considered the suggestion of having an n of at least 200 [69]. We recruited 258 students from six classes (an average of 43 from each class) from a college in the Shanxi province of China. Each participant was part of a pair of desk mates for at least one year. In the context of the sampled college in China, students within the same cohort are assigned to fixed classrooms and fixed seating arrangements for the duration of the academic year. Therefore, 'desk mates' are defined as two students assigned to sit immediately adjacent to each other during all daily classes. This specific peer-rating approach was selected because this fixed seating arrangement facilitates prolonged, high-frequency, and close-proximity interactions. Consequently, desk mates possess a high degree of familiarity with each other’s typical behaviors, study habits, and personality traits, enabling them to serve as highly reliable and accurate peer-raters, potentially offering greater observational validity than general classmates. All students received credits for participating in the study, which consisted of two sessions in which they had to fill in surveys and do tests.

The participants were first asked to rate their desk-mates on personality and creativity (peer-reported) and then also rated themselves on both variables (self-reported). This (first) session took about one hour.

One week later, the same participants were asked to conduct the creativity tests (RAT, AUT) and an intelligence task, namely the Raven Progressive Matrices (RPM). Finishing all tasks (second session) took about one hour.

After eliminating incomplete questionnaires, we obtained a sample of 197 of paired-participants (108 females, M = 19.94 years, SD = 1.04, ranging from 18 to 28).

Measures

Big five inventory

We used the 44-item Big Five Inventory (BFI; [39] to measure participants’ personality. The items could be answered on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from not agreeing (1) to absolutely agreeing (5). We computed the scores for the Big Five traits: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism. In the present study, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.70 (O), 0.67 (C), 0.80 (E), 0.59 (A), and 0.65 (N) for the self-report measure, and 0.63 (O), 0.69 (C), 0.79 (E), 0.68 (A), and 0.73 (N) for the peer-report measures.

Creativity questionnaire

The original version of the Creativity Questionnaire (CQ) consisted of 9 items (Cronbach alpha 0.95) and was used to test employee creativity with a 6-point Likert scale [73]. The original CQ was adapted for the student sample by implementing minor context-specific wording modifications (e.g., substituting the word “work” with “study”). This adaptation follows the well-established "studies as work" paradigm [68], which posits that students' academic activities are functionally equivalent to professional work in their psychological demands and goal-oriented structures. Such alignment ensures that the items are contextually meaningful to the respondents, enhancing ecological validity without altering the underlying psychological construct. To empirically justify this adaptation and ensure the construct validity was preserved, we conducted Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFA) testing a unidimensional structure for both rating sources. The CFA results demonstrated a strong model fit for both the self-reported version (χ2 = 56.52, df = 27, CFI = 0.967, TLI = 0.956, RMSEA = 0.074) and the peer-reported version (χ2 = 72.60, df = 27, CFI = 0.941, TLI = 0.921, RMSEA = 0.093). Furthermore, all standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001), ranging from 0.58 to 0.83 for self-reports and 0.50 to 0.82 for peer-reports. These objective results confirm that the wording modifications successfully preserved the structural validity of the original scale. The sum score of all nine items provided an assessment of participants’ general creativity. In the present study, we computed each of the sum scores for self-reported and peer-report creative traits.

Remote association test

Based on the original version of the Remote Association Test (RAT; [43, 44] created the 49-item Chinese Remote Association Test, or RAT, Chinese version. The RAT was used to measure participants’ convergent thinking. Respondents need to figure out a word that could combine with every word of the given triad to be a phrase. For example, the word “star” is the resolution to items “Falling, Actor, Dust”. Filling the correct word in an item implies receiving one point. The sum score of all 49 items is the estimate of participants’ converging thinking ability.

Alternative uses task

The AUT [33] asks participants to think of as many uses as possible for common objects: in this case, a paper, a book, and a pillow. Three trained raters judged the answers of the participants on four dimensions: (1) Originality – how unusual the answers are – evidence of ‘thinking different; (2) Fluency – the number of alternative uses you can think of; (3) Flexibility – the range of ideas, in different domains and categories; (4) Elaboration – level of detail and development of the idea. In the present study, each rater gave scores for three items on four dimensions. The sum score of those four dimensions represented the overall AUT score [60]. For three out of the four specific dimensions, the Intraclass Correlation coefficients (ICC) were low: 0.39 (originality), 0.79 (fluency), 0.40 (flexibility), and 0.25 (elaboration). Therefore, any analyses involving these dimensions should be interpreted with great care. The ICC for the overall AUT scores was acceptable (0.55), though. Given the rather low ICCS for the specific dimensions, we decided to only focus on the general AUT score in this study. The AUT and the RAT described above were supposed to measure creative abilities.

Raven’s progressive matrix

We also included a measure of general cognitive ability (or intelligence), the RPM [61]. This nonverbal group test measures abstract reasoning, which is regarded as a non-verbal estimate of fluid intelligence. The full version of this test consists of five blocks (A to E) of 12 items each (e.g., A1 through A12). The difficulty of blocks gradually amounts from A to E. Each item consists of a visual geometric design with a missing piece. Respondents are given six to eight choices to pick from and fill in the missing piece. Giving one right choice could receive one point. In the present study, we utilized a truncated version of the RPM, specifically excluding Blocks A and E. This decision was informed by methodological literature demonstrating the validity of abbreviated matrix reasoning tests when administered under strict time constraints [2, 8]. For our sample of university students, pilot testing indicated that Block A lacked discriminative power due to ceiling effects, while Block E proved excessively difficult and time-consuming, risking participant fatigue and floor effects. By focusing on Blocks B, C, and D, we effectively targeted the medium-to-high difficulty range most appropriate for this population, thereby optimizing the balance between measurement precision and testing efficiency. Participants had two minutes to finish block B, four minutes to finish block C, and six minutes to finish block D (12 min in total). The total score of the rest three blocks is the estimate of respondents’ intelligence.

Results

EFA for the GFPs

By using the self- and peer-reports, we obtained two versions of the Big Five and creative traits (measured by the Creativity Questionnaire). Based on the self-reported and peer-reported Big Five traits, we extracted two GFPs, respectively. The results of the Principal Axis Factoring supported the existence of the GFPs in both measurements. In detail, the peer-reported GFP with eigenvalue (EV) of 2.32 explained 46.37% of the variances of all OCEAN and as a comparison, the self-reported GFP explained 33.30% of the OCEAN’s variances with EV of 1.66. The peer-reported and self-reported GFPs loaded on OCEAN for 0.67, 0.63, 0.73, 0.57, and −0.79, and 0.61, 0.55, 0.49, 0.60, and −0.63, respectively.

Similar to [10], we compared the HTMM correlations to heterotrait-heteromethod (HTHM) correlations. The fact that no significant differences were found (∆r = 0.05, Z = 0.56, p = 0.58) implied that the HTMM correlations are likely to approach the true correlations among the Big Five.

Beyond the Big Five, the GFP showed even stronger MTHM correlations than the Big Five traits (r = 0.62). The GFP significantly correlated with creativity in mono- and across-measures. Considering CMVs may inflate the correlations, the cross-rating associations were particularly relevant. The cross-ratings tests revealed that the peer-reported GFP significantly related to the self-reported creativity scores with r = 0.49, respectively. In the conversed direction (self-rated GFP to peer-rated creativity), those values became r = 0.45. We calculated the average of those two values as the conservative estimate: r = 0.47 for the GFP.

SEM testing the correlation between the GFP and creative traits while controlling for common method variances

To estimate the true correlations between the GFP and creativity, we controlled for the common method variance in the SEMs. We did so, first, by extracting two measurement factors, namely the self-report factor and the peer-report factor (see Fig. 1). Although the sources of the reports were different (self vs peer), the content (the items) and the way to obtain the ratings (subjective scores) were the same. So, we allowed those two measurement factors to correlate with each other. Alternatively, we considered another type of common method variance, namely acquiescence bias, which implies the tendency to respond in the same manner on all questions (e.g., strongly agree with all items). We correspondingly extracted a single acquiescence bias factor [1] instead of two measurement factors (see Fig. 1). To ensure robustness and establish the statistical validity of the GFP, we employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in addition to commonly used EFA for its extraction while systematically controlling for CMVs. We tested the extraction of the GFP using two distinct, theoretically supported approaches. In the first approach (the one-layer or direct model), the GFP was extracted directly from the five primary Big Five traits. In the second approach (the two-layer or hierarchical model), the GFP was extracted from two mid-level meta-traits—Stability (comprising Conscientiousness, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism) and Plasticity (comprising Extraversion and Openness; [10].

The statistical validity of the extracted GFP was evaluated using a dual-criteria approach. First, we assessed the overall model fit using established indices, e.g., CFI/TFI ≥ 0.90. Second, we examined the construct validity by verifying the direction and magnitude of the factor loadings. A statistically valid GFP was confirmed by observing the theoretically expected loading pattern: the general factor loaded positively on Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and Agreeableness, and negatively on Neuroticism. By comparing these nested models, we were able to isolate the substantive variance of the GFP from method-specific noise, thereby ensuring its reliability as a predictor in our subsequent analyses.

In a series of comprehensive models, we made a few modifications: allowing correlations between a) several Big Five traits due to their intercorrelations [53]; b) allowing the unique variance of Openness and creativity to correlate [59]. Discussion

Previous research has confirmed that creativity is a vital resource for innovation [88] and for generating new knowledge [4]. It has also been established that creativity is partly influenced by personality [26, 35]. The present study adopted an innovative way to test the relationship between personality and creativity, using the GFP as an integration of various Big Five aspects [66]. To obtain a comprehensive estimation that takes method bias into account, this study used multiple creativity measures to test the relationship of interest. Specifically, the present study measured various components of creativity: creative self-beliefs (self-reported creativity), creative performance (rated by others), divergent thinking (tested by AUT), and convergent thinking skills (tested by RAT). Personality traits and the GFP were also measured while controlling for CMV. Intelligence as a possible confounding variable was added. To our knowledge, t only very limited previous research has applied such a diverse range of measures in one study to test the personality-creativity relationship.

Regarding the study’s outcome, the first finding worth noting was that there was a considerable amount of overlap between self- and peer-reported measurements, ranging from r = 0.42 to 0.72. Thus, the different raters showed substantial agreement on personality as well as creativity, suggesting that they partly tap the same underlying constructs [14]. Subsequently, we consistently found relatively strong and positive correlations between the GFP and creativity within each type of rating. We acknowledge that the within-rater correlations need to be interpreted with caution, as they likely include common method variance [23]. Nevertheless, a more convincing finding was that the GFP-creativity link remained largely intact when examining the associations across the type of ratings (i.e., self-creativity with other-rated personality and self-personality with other-rated creativity). In other words, when participants reported that they possessed a relatively broad range of socially desirable traits (i.e., high GFP), then, on average, classmates independently provided these participants with higher scores on creativity and vice versa. As the peer reports were provided by classmates who also lived together in collective dormitories during college, it can be assumed that the targets and peer raters knew each other quite well. This may have contributed to the overlap in ratings.

To test the correlations of interest and reduce bias, we applied a series of statistical models that controlled for CMVs. Previous studies have only used this approach to examine the personality hierarchy and the viability of higher-order factors [1, 10]. We adopted the same approach to test the robustness of the correlations between the GFP and creativity, as measured by questionnaire-based creativity measures. Using this approach, we found even stronger correlations between the GFP and creativity measures (from 0.75 to 0.83). This finding suggested that when measurement errors such as CMVs were taken out, individuals' creative traits, as measured by the questionnaire, largely overlap with their GFP. Note that the exceptionally high correlations between the GFP and creative traits may partially reflect a 'halo effect.' Because peer ratings were provided by desk mates with long-term familiarity, their judgments may be influenced by a global evaluative bias where high-GFP individuals are perceived more positively across all desirable domains, including creativity, regardless of distinct behavioral evidence.

A question that remains open, however, is why the GFP-trait creativity correlation was so strong. One possible reason is that because the GFP integrates all the Big Five dimensions, it also captures most of the trait aspects of creativity. [72] stated that there are six resources for creativity—personality, knowledge, motivation, environmental context, intellectual processes, and intellectual style. As the most general factor in personality, the GFP has been empirically found to be associated with almost all other resources of creativity. For example, the GFP was found to link to social knowledge [75] and motivation [54], and be strongly associated with persistence [53] and grit [20], which are often required to establish innovations. Furthermore, the GFP has been associated with a better environmental context in terms of higher socio-economic status [77] and good neighborhoods [24]. Therefore, part of the GFP-creativity association may also include environmental pathways. Regarding intelligence, several studies have linked the GFP to higher general intelligence [22, 38]. As intelligence is one of the resources for being creative or providing significant novel contributions to a field, part of the GFP-creativity relationship may be established through intelligence. Unfortunately, the present study did not support such a deduction since no significant correlation was found between the GFP and intelligence. Instead, the present study found the GFP and intelligence as two distinct resources for creativity (see Fig. 2), which is in line with [53] argumentation that the GFP integrates the most general non-cognitive dimensions of personality. Nevertheless, most findings from the present study support Feist's [27] idea that personality (the GFP in this case) interacts with the environment and influences creativity.

Furthermore, compared to creative traits (as either self-rated or other-rated), creative abilities (as measured by either AUT or RAT) displayed weaker correlations with the GFP. These findings support the division between creative traits and creative abilities, which could simplify the category of creative criteria. Specifically, creativity-related dispositional or behavioral characteristics that can be self-rated or other-rated may be categorized as creative traits, while creative abilities refer to the capacity for creatively solving problems and are supposed to be linked to intelligence [7]. In the present study, a correlation between creative abilities and intelligence was indeed found and relevant (r ≈ 0.36).

While the GFP has historically sparked debate regarding whether it represents a methodological artifact (e.g., socially desirable responding) or a substantive psychological construct [64, 76], the current study aligns firmly with the substantive interpretation. Specifically, we conceptualize the GFP as a holistic index of 'social effectiveness' and 'ego-resiliency' [25, 74, 78]. This theoretical stance provides a vital lens for interpreting our findings. Creativity is not solely a cognitive endeavor occurring in isolation; rather, it is an inherently social process requiring individuals to navigate complex interpersonal environments to express and champion novel ideas [27]. The robust relationship observed between the GFP and creative performance (e.g., fluency and originality) suggests that the creative personality is heavily reliant on this general adaptability. To successfully generate and implement original concepts, individuals must leverage the emotional stability to withstand criticism, the sociability to garner support, and the conscientiousness to persist through setbacks—a constellation of traits parsimoniously captured by the GFP.

In conclusion, the GFP as a global measure of personality addresses multiple components of creativity. Accordingly, the General Factor of Personality shows stable associations with multiple indicators of creativity. High GFP persons are those who a) have a high level of confidence in their creativity and believe they can apply their creativity; b) are rated as creative by others; have relevant c) divergent and d) convergent thinking skills.

Returning to a classic debate, creative people may question social norms and be relatively independent of conforming influences, which allows them to spend their time and energy on bold creations. However, this is only the first stage of creating (discovery/idea generation). Without the second stage – justification/carrying out ideas [62], particularly expressing/practicing creative ideas that are socially useful, those ideas may lose impact and power, and their creators would not be remembered in human history. [27] p. 301) speculated that “The most creative people are those who often prefer to be away from other people, but who master expressing their ideas in media that others can understand and appreciate. This is precisely what makes the creative act inherently social, -it must be expressed in a social context and ultimately be understood by others if it is to be creative by the definition given earlier, namely, be both novel and useful.”

Considering the above, testing a comprehensive personality structure that encapsulates social characteristics may provide a good fit for studying creative personalities. To achieve creative outcomes, people must have a sufficient level of sociability (extraversion, agreeableness), the ability to persistently and meticulously work (conscientiously), and cope with challenges and critique by others (emotionally stable/low on neuroticism). Combining all the characteristics described above points in the direction of the GFP as a part of having creative personalities.

An interesting finding of the current study is the lack of a significant correlation between the GFP and intelligence, as measured by the RPM. While some prior research has suggested a weak positive link between the GFP and general cognitive ability, other rigorous investigations have yielded very low correlations (e.g., [38]. This discrepancy can be explained by two primary boundary conditions present in our study. First, from a theoretical standpoint, [38] posited that cognitive intelligence and personality may operate as somewhat “mutually exclusive strategies” for resource acquisition and adaptation. RPM is a pure measure of non-verbal fluid intelligence, capturing abstract logical reasoning, whereas the GFP encapsulates socio-emotional adaptability and interpersonal effectiveness. It is highly plausible that the cognitive mechanisms driving abstract pattern recognition operate independently of those driving social navigation. Second, a statistical boundary condition must be considered: our sample consisted entirely of college students. This cohort has inherently undergone academic selection, resulting in a restricted range of cognitive ability. As demonstrated by [37], indirect range restriction in such selected samples mathematically attenuates correlation coefficients. In line with [38] assertion that range restriction suppresses GFP-g correlations, it is highly probable that the cognitive homogeneity of our university sample rendered any subtle, population-level associations undetectable. Therefore, we conclude that within a higher-education setting, the GFP and abstract cognitive reasoning function as largely independent resources.

It should be noted that the present findings are based on a general student sample. Therefore, the findings might not generalize to outlier creative individuals. Specifically, previous research has indicated that highly creative people, on average, may not only be non-conformist and inclined to break rules but also socially maladjusted [87] and less socially desirable [16, 52]. Thus, in such extremely creative individuals, it is possible that the GFP is not a suitable predictor of creativity [42] or may even be a negative predictor. Future studies could investigate domain-specific creative personalities (i.e., creative scientists or artists; [27] or large samples that include a sufficient number of "creative outliers" to gain more insight into any differential associations regarding the GFP.

Limitations and future research

A notable limitation of the current study concerns the ICC of the AUT subdimensions. While the overall composite score demonstrated acceptable reliability for aggregate analysis (ICC = 0.55), the individual dimensions exhibited lower consistency (ICC = 0.25 to 0.40 for elaboration, originality, and flexibility; 0.79 for fluency). As highlighted by prior methodological research, the inherently open-ended nature of divergent thinking tasks introduces substantial subjectivity into human scoring [5, 29]. Evaluating originality, flexibility, and elaboration requires raters to apply internal thresholds for novelty, conceptual category shifts, and response detail, which frequently diverge among judges. Furthermore, even fluency—typically a quantitative count—incorporates subjective judgment, as raters must filter out invalid, nonsensical, or redundant responses before establishing a valid count [70]. Due to this limited measurement stability at the dimensional level, the current study refrained from drawing conclusions based on specific AUT subdimensions, relying solely on the overall aggregate score. Future research employing divergent thinking tasks may benefit from incorporating automated semantic distance algorithms or more exhaustive rater training protocols to mitigate human scoring variance.

Additionally, the findings of the current study are limited by the use of a single student sample from a Chinese college context. This specific educational setting and demographic may not generalize to professional working populations, older adults, domain-specific creators (e.g., professional artists), or culturally distinct samples.

Practical implications

The findings of the present study offer valuable insights for both educational and organizational settings. In educational contexts, pedagogical strategies aimed at fostering student creativity often focus primarily on cognitive skills, such as brainstorming or divergent thinking exercises. However, our results suggest that educators should equally emphasize the development of socio-emotional adaptability and ego-resiliency. Because creative expression is inherently social and involves the risk of peer rejection, educators must cultivate classroom environments that build students' social effectiveness. By integrating collaborative problem-solving, teaching constructive feedback management, and fostering psychological safety, educators can equip students with the GFP-related resources necessary to confidently express and champion their novel ideas [12, 63].

Similarly, in organizational settings, these findings hold direct implications for talent acquisition, team building, and employee development. When organizations seek to drive innovation, hiring or training exclusively for cognitive creativity may prove insufficient. Human resource professionals and managers should recognize the critical role of the General Factor of Personality in translating creative potential into applied innovation, particularly in leadership and team dynamics [77, 84]. Professional development programs that enhance employees' emotional stability, sociability, and interpersonal resilience can foster a workforce that is not only capable of generating original concepts but also adept at navigating the social and bureaucratic complexities required to successfully pitch, defend, and implement those ideas within a corporate structure.

Concluding remarks

Knowing how personality traits relate to creativity has always been one of the major themes in creativity research. In the current study, we examined this relationship using the GFP as a construct that captures much of the social aspects of personality. Accordingly, the GFP was found to correlate with multiple measures of creativity. Therefore, we support the recommendations of [42] that the general factor found in personality measures should be given serious consideration when attempting to identify the characteristics of creative personalities.
