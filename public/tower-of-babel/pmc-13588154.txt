Omnidirectional bending sensor with three-fold structural symmetry enables decoupling of bending angle and direction
Authors: Yanzhen Li, Zheren Cai, Yongli He, Zhihua Liu, Cong Wang, Jiaofu Li, Wenlong Li, Nuan Chen, Dong Wu, Huajian Gao, Xiaodong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate shape sensing requires simultaneous quantification of bending angle (φ) and bending direction (θ). Yet existing bending sensors remain confined to uniaxial or discretized modes, relying on tedious, non-generalizable calibration procedures. Here, we present a snowflake-shaped omnidirectional bending sensor that achieves complete and linear decoupling of φ and θ through a three-fold rotationally symmetric arrangement of strain-sensitive resistors. By harnessing geometric symmetry as a physical prior, we establish a closed-form analytical model and signal projection framework that transform calibration from an empirical process into a simple, quantitative, universal procedure. The sensor is compatible with standard flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) fabrication on polyimide (PI) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrates, ensuring scalability and integration. We demonstrate real-time reconstruction of multidirectional surface deformation and in situ correction of ultrasound imaging artifacts during bending. This symmetry-guided approach redefines the design principles of deformation sensing, providing a general platform for dynamic shape reconstruction and opening avenues for self-adaptive and self-aware electronic systems.

INTRODUCTION

Flexible electronics have redefined sensing technologies by enabling intimate integration with complex, curvilinear surfaces while preserving mechanical compliance and functional reliability (1–3). Among these, bending and strain sensors are fundamental for capturing deformation states in flexible substrates, underpinning a broad spectrum of applications ranging from biomedical monitoring (4–6) and human–machine interfaces (7–9) to soft robotics and adaptative structures (10–12).

Despite rapid progress, most existing bending or strain sensors remain restricted to uniaxial bending detection modes, providing only partial description of complex deformation (13–19). Structural innovations such as cross-shaped geometries have been introduced to expand the sensing range, yet these configurations often suffer from symmetry-induced ambiguities, leading to coupled or indistinct responses that obscure the full two-dimensional (2D) bending state (20–24). Attempts to break symmetry using triangular geometries have improved angular discrimination but still depend on empirical calibration and non-generalizable signal fitting, lacking a unified quantitative framework (25, 26). Consequently, current approaches fall short of analytically decoupling bending deformation into independent, descriptive parameters, bending angle (φ) and bending direction (θ), which are essential for precise shape reconstruction (27–32).

Equally important, integration challenges persist, as many reported designs rely on materials, fabrication processes, or post-processing steps that are incompatible with flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) manufacturing, hindering their scalability and system-level integration (33–35). These limitations collectively constrain the development of shape-sensing platforms capable of reliable and omnidirectional performance.

Here, we present a snowflake-shaped omnidirectional bending sensor that resolves these long-standing challenges by achieving complete and linear decoupling φ and θ through three-fold structural symmetry. Guided by this geometric prior, we formulate a closed-form signal-projection model that converts empirical calibration into a simple, quantitative, and transferable process. The resulting sensor is fully compatible with standard FPCB fabrication on substrates such as polyimide (PI) or polyethylene terephthalate (PET), enabling scalable production and integration. We further demonstrate real-time surface deformation mapping and artifact correction in flexible ultrasound imaging, establishing a symmetry-driven strategy for dynamic shape reconstruction. This work introduces a generalizable framework for geometry-guided mechanosensing, advancing flexible electronics toward self-adaptive and self-aware systems.

RESULTS

Design principles of the snowflake-shaped omnidirectional bending sensor

To capture complex bending deformations, we designed an omnidirectional bending sensor that independently resolves φ and θ, as depicted in Fig. 1A, where φ denotes the magnitude of bending, and θ defines the orientation of the bending axis relative to the sensor. The sensor adopts a rotationally symmetric design, incorporating three resistors (R14, R25, R36) with a common central symmetry point distributed at 120° intervals, like a snowflake (Fig. 1B). The primary sensing element is a thin Au film fabricated by thermal evaporation (Fig. 1C). During bending deformation, the thin film undergoes small strain that induces measurable resistance changes in the three channels, which are simultaneously acquired and analyzed.

We established a theoretical model (text S1 and fig. S1) to describe the influence of the small strain induced by bending on the resistance of the thin film, linking resistance changes to both φ and θ. The decoupling mechanism, derived from the theoretical model, was validated by finite element analysis (FEA) (fig. S2). Notably, summing up the three relative resistance changes (δRx = ΔRx/R0) removes dependence on θ, revealing a linear relationship with φ. For instance, under a fixed bending angle (e.g., φ = 60°) with different bending directions (Fig. 1D), the three relative resistance changes vary with θ, but their sum remains constant (Fig. 1E), indicating dependence solely on φ. Due to the small strain induced by bending deformation, the relationship between the sum and φ is highly linear (Fig. 1F).

For θ, taking the ratio of an individual resistance change to the sum of all three can eliminate the influence of φ. Under a fixed bending direction (e.g., θ = 60°) with varying bending angles (Fig. 1G), although the individual relative resistance changes vary with φ, the ratio of any one to the total remains constant, indicating successful decoupling of φ (Fig. 1H). To uniquely determine θ, two ratios are needed since one ratio is symmetric within the direction range (Fig. 1I). Thus, the snowflake-shaped sensor enables decoupling of φ and θ, allowing accurate reconstruction of bending deformation.

The key to this design lies in its rotational symmetry, defined by the rotational symmetry order (N) (Fig. 2A). To evaluate the effect of N on the decoupling process, we introduce the decoupling efficiency (η), defined as inversely proportional to both N and Nstate (text S2). N represents the number of sensing signals and reflects system complexity and sensing redundancy, while Nstate represents the number of symmetry-induced indistinguishable bending states and therefore quantifies directional ambiguity. Consequently, η provides a quantitative measure of how efficiently a sensing architecture achieves unique directional identification while minimizing sensing redundancy.

Specifically, when N = 1, a single resistance signal is insufficient to resolve the bending parameters, yielding η = 0. For N = 2, symmetry produces two possible outcomes, giving η = 25% (fig. S3A). When N = 3, the bending direction can be uniquely determined without ambiguity (Nstate = 1), representing the minimum rotational symmetry order required for complete directional decoupling (fig. S3B). For higher symmetry orders (N ≥ 4), the bending direction remains uniquely identifiable; however, the additional sensing channels do not provide new independent directional information and instead introduce sensing redundancy (fig. S3C). Therefore, N = 3 corresponds to the minimum complete solution for directional decoupling and consequently exhibits the highest decoupling efficiency (Fig. 2B).

To investigate the role of structural symmetry, we examined configurations under N = 3, where two angles exist between the three resistors. We fixed the first angle at 60° and introduced an adjustable angle (α) to define the second angle (Fig. 2C). By varying the α, we systematically broke the rotational symmetry of the structure. This symmetry breaking caused deviations in the φ inversion process (Fig. 2D), resulting in non-uniform linear coefficients during signal analysis. In other words, the linear coefficient became dependent on θ, dramatically increasing the inversion complexity (Fig. 2E). Simulations confirmed that only the rotationally symmetric configuration (α = 60°) preserves a unique and constant linear coefficient across all θ values (Fig. 2F). These results exhibit that the rotational symmetry is essential for robust decoupling and accurate linear inversion processes of bending parameters.

To optimize electromechanical performance, the sensing units were configured in a serpentine layout defined by a rhombic contour (fig. S4). The serpentine geometry elongates the effective conductive path, thereby amplifying absolute resistance changes under bending and improving the signal-to-noise ratio (fig. S4D), while its distributed curvature helps homogenize mechanical stress, mitigating localized strain concentrations and enhancing long-term mechanical durability. The rhombic contour, aligned with the intrinsic rotational symmetry of the sensor, maximizes active area utilization and facilitates efficient transduction from bending-induced strain to electrical signals. As illustrated in fig. S4F, this geometric regularity provides a symmetry-consistent framework for the observed angular response and also enables seamless tessellation for scalable large-area integration.

Beyond these electromechanical advantages, the combined serpentine-rhombic design also helps maintain the mechanical integrity of the Au thin film under large bending deformation. Microscopic inspections reveal no observable buckling or micro-crack formation in the Au layer, indicating that the conductive pathways remain continuous during operation (fig. S5). This mechanical robustness ensures that the measured resistance changes originate from homogeneous mechanical deformation rather than damage-induced electrical artifacts.

Although the primary parameters extracted by the sensor are φ and θ, these quantities can be directly translated into curvature values—an important metric in mechanics (text S3). Since curvature (κ) is linearly proportional to φ with a constant coefficient determined by the sensor length (κ=φ/l), this mathematical equivalence allows for straightforward conversion between angular and curvature descriptions. This flexibility further broadens the sensor’s applicability in diverse engineering scenarios where curvature serves as a more intuitive or relevant description of deformation.

Decoupled and linear performance of the snowflake-shaped bending sensor

We experimentally validated the sensor response across a range of φ and θ values by characterizing the relative resistance changes in the three individual resistors (Fig. 3, A to C). Since θ and θ + 180° correspond to the same bending axis, measurements within a 180° angular span are sufficient to reconstruct full 360° behavior. Across all values of θ, the relative resistance changes exhibited strong linearity with φ, in agreement with the theoretical model. Angular sensitivity analysis further revealed that each resistor shows maximum response when the bending direction aligns with its own orientation, and minimal response when the axis is orthogonal. This results in characteristic dumbbell-shaped polar plots for each resistor (Fig. 3, D to F), which closely match the theoretical fitting curves. These complementary angular patterns collectively enable robust and comprehensive extraction of the bending parameters. To further verify that the decoupling model is not limited to symmetry-related directions, the key cos2θ relationship was examined at finer angular intervals using both experiments and simulations (fig. S6). The resistance responses of a single sensing unit follow the theoretical decoupling model with excellent agreement across various bending directions.

To reconstruct φ, we summed up the relative resistance changes from all three resistors. Experimental data confirm that the sum remains a consistent linear relationship with φ across various θ values, thereby simplifying the inversion process (Fig. 3G). To determine θ, we used two ratios of individual relative resistance change to their sum, resolving the ambiguity (Fig. 3H). Due to the rotational symmetry, the ratios are consistent across all three resistors, comparing to their own directions (Fig. 3I). Moreover, the durability of the sensor was evaluated over 500 bending cycles within a range of −60° to 60°, exhibiting negligible degradation between the 1st and 500th cycles (fig. S7). The observed stability is consistent with the relatively low strain experienced by the Au sensing layer during operation (<1% even at 180° bending, as estimated from classical bending theory; text S4), which minimizes the likelihood of deformation-induced damage. Together with the absence of observable buckling or micro-crack formation (fig. S5), these results indicate that the sensing architecture is intrinsically robust under repeated bending and capable of maintaining stable performance under practical operating conditions. In addition, Au is chemically stable and resistant to oxidation under ambient conditions, which further contributes to the long-term stability of the sensing layer.

The decoupling and linear inversion strategy substantially reduces calibration complexity. To calibrate φ, one nonzero bending condition suffices to determine the linear coefficient. For θ, two calibration points—aligned with the orientations of maximum and minimum sensitivity of a single resistor—are needed. The rotational symmetry of sensor ensures that calibration performed on a single resistor can be directly generalized to the other two. To evaluate the transferability of the calibration strategy, four independently fabricated devices were characterized under identical bending conditions (fig. S8). Although minor variations in the initial resistance values were observed, the normalized resistance responses remained highly consistent across all devices. These results demonstrate good reproducibility of the calibration parameters and further support the transferability of the proposed calibration method.

To optimize the calibration process and identify the most effective calibration point, we evaluated the linearity of the resistor response across several bending angles. The linear coefficient fitted using all calibration points served as the reference standard (fig. S9, A to C). Calibration at different angles introduced varying levels of calibration errors, with larger bending angles generally yielding lower errors (fig. S9, D to F). The minimum error occurred at −60°, which was thus selected as the optimal calibration point and applied in all subsequent experiments and demonstrations (fig. S9G).

Another challenge in φ calibration lies in the uncertainty of θ during the calibration process. To assess whether this uncertainty affects the accuracy, we evaluated the calibration error induced by different θ values. Specifically, we calibrated the sensor at a fixed φ and θ, then tested the inversion accuracy at other θ orientations. All conditions yielded comparable results (fig. S10). Frequency analysis revealed that over 80% of φ inversions exhibited errors below 5° under all conditions (fig. S11, A to F), as further summarized in fig. S11G. Although larger bending angles introduced greater errors (fig. S11H), the calibration of φ was largely independent of θ (fig. S11I).

For calibration of θ inversion, two ratios are required. In practice, three combinations of resistor pairs are possible: R14 & R25, R14 & R36, and R25 & R36. We evaluated the inversion error of θ using each combination as calibration input (fig. S12, A to F). All combinations yielded similar results, with over 80% of errors falling within 8°, consistent with the overall error distribution (fig. S12G). This confirms that the choice of resistor pair has negligible impact on θ inversion accuracy. Additionally, larger φ values produced larger strain, improving θ inversion accuracy (fig. S12H). In contrast, changing θ had negligible influence on the inversion error (fig. S12I).

Versatile sensing for real-time shape reconstruction and ultrasound imaging correction

These findings demonstrate a simple and reliable inversion strategy for accurately reconstructing bending deformation, grounded in the principles of decoupling and linearity. Our sensor serves a versatile tool across diverse domains that involve dynamic or complex deformation, such as real-time reconstruction and ultrasound imaging correction.

We first demonstrated real-time reconstruction of bending deformation using the sensor. Mechanical deformations were applied to an FPCB substrate integrated with the sensor (Fig. 4A), and the corresponding resistance changes were recorded and analyzed in real time (Fig. 4B). The derived bending parameters were then used to reconstruct the deformation in a virtual environment (Fig. 4C). Both convex and concave bending modes were tested, each with four distinct states varying in bending angles and directions (Fig. 4D, movie S1). The reconstructed virtual deformations closely matched the physical inputs, confirming the system’s high accuracy and responsiveness under diverse deformation conditions.

(D) Representative examples of real-time reconstruction of convex (φ > 0°) and concave (φ < 0°) bending modes, demonstrating accurate recovery of both φ and θ over a broad angular range and consistent reconstruction of bending states.

To further quantitatively evaluate the reconstruction accuracy, controlled bending experiments were performed using cylindrical objects with predefined radii and bending directions (fig. S13). The sensor was conformally attached to cylindrical surfaces, and the reconstructed bending angles and directions were compared with ground-truth values calculated from the object geometry. The results show that both the bending angle error and direction error remain within 5° across all tested conditions, demonstrating reliable quantitative agreement between the physical deformation and its virtual reconstruction. These results also indicate that the linear decoupling model remains accurate over the demonstrated bending range. Although the analytical treatment is based on a small-strain approximation, no noticeable deviation from the analytical prediction is observed experimentally. At sufficiently large deformations, higher-order geometric effects and non-uniform bending may introduce minor deviations; however, such effects remain negligible within the operating range investigated in this work.

Beyond this, the sensor sensitivity can be tuned by adjusting the substrate thickness (text S4 and fig. S1). Under the same bending condition, a thicker substrate induces greater strain in the sensing layer, leading to larger resistance changes and higher sensitivity (fig. S14, A to C). This tunability allows the device to be customized for different scenarios, from subtle gesture detection to high-strain structural deformation.

We also explored the sensor’s utility in ultrasound imaging correction. During bending deformation, the flexible ultrasound array undergoes geometric distortion, which alters the relative positions of individual transducer elements and introduces phase mismatches in the transmitted and received acoustic signals. These bending-induced path-length variations lead to image artifacts such as target deformation, defocusing, and lateral distortion. A linear ultrasound array was mounted on the underside of the PI substrate, while the sensor was placed on the top side (Fig. 5, A to C). To validate its capability, a spherical target (20 mm in both height and width) was visualized (Fig. 5D). Its dimensions were first confirmed using a commercial ultrasound probe (C5–2V), showing good agreement with baseline measurements from our system (Fig. 5, E and F).

During convex bending, the sphere appeared compressed along the lateral direction, with increasing distortion observed at larger bending angles (Fig. 5G, fig. S15). However, real-time compensation using the sensor signals effectively restored the sphere’s geometry (Fig. 5H). Quantitative analysis revealed that deviations in both height and width increased substantially without correction but were reduced to minimal levels when compensation was applied (Fig. 5I). A similar trend was observed during concave bending, where the sphere appeared stretched along the lateral direction (Fig. 5J, fig. S16). Applying compensation with the sensor markedly suppressed the distortion (Fig. 5K), and quantitative analysis confirmed minimal deviation in shape parameters after correction (Fig. 5L). These results demonstrate the sensor’s versatility, such as interactive control and imaging correction, emphasizing its potential as a core component in multifunctional, self-adaptive electronic systems.

DISCUSSION

We present a snowflake-shaped bending sensor that achieves quantitative, omnidirectional deformation reconstruction by exploiting a rotationally symmetric architecture with an optimized symmetry order. This symmetry-guided design enables independent determination of the bending angle (φ) and bending direction (θ) with high linearity and simplified calibration, addressing the long-standing challenges of symmetric ambiguity, non-linear response, and qualitative interpretation. Fully compatible with standard FPCB fabrication, the sensor can be readily integrated with widely used substrates such as PI and PET, enhancing versatility and system-level integration. Beyond intrinsic sensing performance, we demonstrate real-time reconstruction of multidirectional deformation and in situ correction of ultrasound imaging artifacts. These advances establish a generalizable and scalable framework for high-fidelity bending sensing, providing a robust platform for next-generation self-adaptive electronic systems that require both mechanical flexibility and precise geometric awareness.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of the snowflake-shaped bending sensor on PET/PI

The snowflake-shaped bending sensor was fabricated by physical vapor deposition with a customized metal mask. The metal mask was first designed and produced by laser cutting technique. A thin layer of chromium (5 nm) was then thermally evaporated onto the substrate to promote adhesion, followed by the deposition of a 25 nm gold layer to form the strain-sensitive resistor elements.

Bending test setup for snowflake-shaped bending sensors

A custom-built rotational bending platform was developed for controlled mechanical testing (fig. S17). The platform features 360° angular markings and was driven by a programmable electric motor. The bending angle and speed were precisely controlled via a LabVIEW interface, enabling repeatable deformations across a wide range of angles. Electrical resistance was measured with an LCR meter and recorded simultaneously through the LabVIEW interface.

Mechanical modeling and finite element analysis (FEA)

Finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to simulate strain distributions and electrical resistance behavior under bending deformation. Simulations were performed using commercial software (COMSOL), with material parameters defined as follows: Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of gold were set to 80 GPa and 0.42, respectively; for chromium, 265 GPa and 0.21 were used. Deformed configurations and electrical responses of the sensor under varying bending angles and orientations were computed to support the theoretical framework.

Fabrication of the snowflake-shaped bending sensor via the FPCB process for real-time reconstruction

For sensors designed for real-time reconstruction, an FPCB process was adopted to ensure scalable and system-level integration. Conductive traces and connection pads were co-designed to match the snowflake geometry, enabling seamless incorporation of sensing elements. Thin metal films—comprising a 5 nm chromium adhesion layer and a 25 nm gold sensing layer—were deposited via thermal evaporation onto the substrate. Resistance signals were acquired through a custom-designed circuit board and wirelessly transmitted to a processing unit via Bluetooth. Real-time data analysis was performed in MATLAB to calculate bending parameters and reconstruct the corresponding deformation in a virtual environment.

Fabrication of the ultrasound imaging system integrated with the snowflake-shaped bending sensor

A multilayer FPCB substrate was designed to accommodate both mechanical sensing elements and ultrasound imaging components. The snowflake-shaped bending sensor was patterned on one side of the substrate via thermal evaporation of a metallic thin film. To ensure mechanical robustness and environmental stability, the sensor was encapsulated with a thin layer of PDMS. A linear ultrasound transducer array was then bonded to the opposite side of the substrate, with precise alignment to the sensor for optimal integration. Electrical interconnections between the transducers and FPCB circuitry were established using conductive adhesives, and the entire assembly was subsequently encapsulated with an additional PDMS layer to provide protection, completing the fabrication of the integrated device.

Ultrasound image compensation based on bending reconstruction

Owing to the integration of the bending sensor and the ultrasound transducer array on opposite sides of the same flexible substrate, both components experience identical substrate deformation. During operation, resistance signals from the bending sensor are first recorded and used to reconstruct the bending state of the substrate, including the bending angle or curvature. Based on the reconstructed bending profile, the relative positions of individual transducers in the linear ultrasound array are calculated. Corresponding phase corrections are then applied to the excitation signals of each transducer to compensate for bending-induced phase mismatch, thereby mitigating image distortion.
