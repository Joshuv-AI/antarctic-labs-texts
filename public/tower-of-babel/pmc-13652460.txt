Laparoscopic management of a 23-cm hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocyst presenting with acute inflammatory symptoms: a case report
Authors: Amir Monshizadeh, Amirhossein Latif, Narjes Mohammadzadeh, Ali Asadollahi-Amin, Mohammad Reza Azimi Aval, Fezzeh Elyasi Nia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Adrenal pseudocysts are rare cystic adrenal lesions that may present incidentally or with symptoms related to hemorrhage or mass effect. Giant hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocysts are uncommon and can mimic malignant intra-abdominal cystic masses.Presentation of case:A 48-year-old woman presented with severe abdominal pain, fever, abdominal distention, and dyspnea. Laboratory studies showed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a 23 × 20 × 18 cm well-demarcated, thin-walled cystic mass in the right hemiabdomen and retroperitoneum, containing homogeneous low-attenuation fluid (9 Hounsfield units) without solid components or abnormal enhancement. Marked mass effect with leftward displacement of abdominal structures was observed. Plasma-free metanephrines were normal, tumor markers were within normal limits, and hydatid serology was negative. Due to persistent symptoms, laparoscopic exploration was performed. Controlled decompression yielded 4000 mL of hemorrhagic fluid without gross spillage, followed by complete laparoscopic excision. Operative time was 90 minutes with minimal blood loss. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3. Histopathology confirmed a hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocyst.Clinical discussion:Giant adrenal pseudocysts may present with acute inflammatory features and significant mass effect, closely simulating malignant disease. Careful radiologic assessment and appropriate preoperative biochemical evaluation are essential before intervention. In selected patients with benign imaging features, laparoscopic resection with controlled decompression may be feasible even for lesions exceeding 20 cm.Conclusion:Laparoscopic resection with controlled decompression may be feasible for carefully selected giant adrenal pseudocysts when imaging and biochemical evaluation do not suggest malignant or functional disease, while maintaining oncologic caution.

Introduction

Adrenal cystic lesions account for approximately 1–2% of adrenal incidentalomas[1,2]. Adrenal pseudocysts are fibrous-walled lesions without epithelial lining and often follow intralesional hemorrhage[3,4]. Large lesions may cause symptoms through hemorrhage or mass effect and can mimic cystic pheochromocytoma or necrotic adrenocortical carcinoma[5–9]. Current European Society of Endocrinology guidelines recommend radiologic characterization and appropriate biochemical assessment before intervention[10,11]. Although open surgery was historically preferred, minimally invasive treatment has increasingly been reported in selected patients[12–14]. This case is notable for its 23-cm size, acute inflammatory presentation, marked mass effect, and laparoscopic excision using controlled decompression without gross spillage or conversion. It is reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 checklist[15].

Presentation of case

History and physical examination

A 48-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with 2 days of severe generalized abdominal pain, fever (38.5°C), progressive abdominal distention, and dyspnea. She had no known comorbidities, prior abdominal surgery, regular medications, drug allergies, anticoagulant use, or abdominal trauma. Family history was negative for endocrine or malignant disease. She denied smoking, alcohol use, or recreational drug use. Heart rate was 100 bpm, and blood pressure was 110/60 mmHg. The abdomen was markedly distended and diffusely tender, without peritoneal signs. No clinical stigmata of endocrine disease or lymphadenopathy were identified.

Laboratory and imaging workup

Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (WBC 13.99 × 103/µL; reference range, 4–10 × 103/µL) and elevated C-reactive protein (54.0 mg/L; reference range, <6.0 mg/L). Blood and urine cultures were negative. Plasma-free metanephrines were within the laboratory reference range; there were no clinical features of cortisol excess or primary aldosteronism. Tumor markers and hydatid serology were negative. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a 23 × 20 × 18 cm well-demarcated, thin-walled cystic mass with homogeneous low-attenuation fluid (9 HU), without solid components, septations, abnormal enhancement, or invasive features (Figs. 1 and 2). The lesion displaced the right kidney and bowel loops to the left and compressed the liver, pancreas, and mesenteric root. It was centered in the right adrenal/retroperitoneal region, displaced rather than invaded adjacent organs, and the right adrenal gland was not separately visualized. Differential diagnoses included adrenal pseudocyst, cystic pheochromocytoma, necrotic adrenocortical carcinoma, and pancreatic pseudocyst. Operative management

Persistent symptoms and the lesion size justified surgery. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and mechanical and pharmacologic venous thromboembolism prophylaxis were administered. Under general anesthesia, with the patient in the supine position with right tilt, pneumoperitoneum was established through a 12-mm left upper quadrant optical trocar. Two 5-mm trocars were inserted into the right upper and left lower quadrants; the right upper quadrant trocar was later exchanged for a 12-mm trocar to facilitate retrieval bag insertion. The operation was performed at a tertiary referral center by a board-certified surgeon experienced in minimally invasive adrenal surgery.

A 5-mm suction was positioned from the left side, and the cyst was punctured under direct vision with a 10-mm metal suction device (Fig. 3). Approximately 4000 mL of dark brown hemorrhagic fluid was evacuated without gross intraperitoneal spillage. Aspirated cyst fluid culture was negative. The puncture site was closed with hemoclips to prevent residual leakage. The collapsed cyst wall was dissected from the liver, gallbladder, retroperitoneum, and inferior vena cava using laparoscopic energy devices and selective clips. At its adrenal origin, the cyst could not be separated from the right adrenal tissue and was excised with adherent adrenal tissue. Limited right hepatic lobe retraction was required; formal mobilization was not. The specimen was removed in fragments within an endoscopic retrieval bag. Operative time was 90 minutes, estimated blood loss was 50 mL, and no conversion or drain placement was required. Antibiotics were discontinued after negative cultures. Recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 3 with wound care and activity instructions. Histopathology showed fibrofatty tissue with acute and chronic inflammation, hemosiderin deposition, and residual adrenal cortical tissue, consistent with a hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocyst. No epithelial lining, malignant cells, necrotic tumor component, vascular invasion, or features of adrenocortical carcinoma or pheochromocytoma were identified. At the 4-week follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, with no readmission, re-intervention, or complications (Clavien–Dindo Grade 0). Discussion

Fever, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers can make giant adrenal pseudocysts mimic an adrenal abscess or infected intra-abdominal cystic disease. Negative cultures and absence of marked inflammatory fat stranding made infection less likely. Rapid expansion and/or intralesional hemorrhage were considered most plausible, although infection could not be fully excluded initially. No trauma, anticoagulant use, recent procedures, or clear precipitating stressors were identified, thus supporting spontaneous intralesional hemorrhage.

The main concern in massive adrenal-region lesions is occult malignancy or a functional tumor. In this case, a thin wall, homogeneous 9-HU content, and the absence of nodules, enhancement, invasion, or metastasis favored a benign pseudocyst over necrotic adrenocortical carcinoma. Normal plasma-free metanephrines and the absence of suspicious enhancement reduced concern for a functional pheochromocytoma. A pancreatic pseudocyst was unlikely because there was no history of pancreatitis, no imaging continuity with the pancreas, and an adrenal/retroperitoneal epicenter. There is no established evidence that pituitary or hypothalamic disorders directly cause adrenal pseudocyst formation or hemorrhagic transformation.

Open surgery remains appropriate when imaging suggests invasive disease, enhancing solid components, organ involvement, or unsafe laparoscopic control. However, selected large adrenal cysts may be managed with minimally invasive approaches when radiologic and biochemical findings do not suggest malignant or functional disease[12–14]. Controlled decompression reduces tension and improves exposure while maintaining visual control of the puncture site. The dual-suction technique minimizes gross spillage and allows complete excision. Published experience remains limited: a systematic review identified 45 patients across 39 studies, and robust morbidity or mortality estimates cannot be derived[3]. Lesions approaching 30 cm, including laparoscopically resected pseudocysts, have been reported[4,16].

Strengths

This report provides a technical description of the laparoscopic management of a 23-cm hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocyst, with controlled decompression and preoperative risk stratification.

Limitations

This single case has a short follow-up period. MRI was not performed because of the acute presentation. Follow-up was limited to 4 weeks, and no follow-up CT or MRI was obtained; therefore, late recurrence, delayed complications, residual adrenal pathology, or incidental adrenal lesions cannot be assessed.

Patient perspective

The patient reported relief following the resolution of abdominal distention and dyspnea and satisfaction with a rapid, minimally invasive recovery.

Conclusion

Giant hemorrhagic adrenal pseudocysts may mimic malignant disease. Careful radiologic evaluation and appropriate biochemical assessment are critical before intervention. When preoperative findings support a benign, nonfunctional disease, laparoscopic resection with controlled decompression may be considered in selected patients and at experienced centers.
