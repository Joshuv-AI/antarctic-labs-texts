Clinical benefit of tenecteplase despite failed recanalization in late-window ischemic stroke: a post hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial
Authors: Zhengzhao Lu, Ziran Wang, Aoming Jin, Fengyuan Che, Lihua Wang, Li Zhou, Hongguo Dai, Xintong Liu, Zhixin Cao, Liyuan Wang, Manjun Hao, Wanxing Ye, Yunyun Xiong, Yongjun Wang

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe TRACE-3 trial established the efficacy of intravenous tenecteplase for patients with acute ischemic stroke and large vessel occlusion in the late time window. It remains unclear whether intravenous tenecteplase confers clinical benefits specifically in the subpopulation of patients who did not achieve successful large vessel recanalization.MethodsThis is a post-hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial, a multicenter randomized controlled trial comparing tenecteplase with standard medical therapy in patients with large vessel occlusion between 4.5 and 24 h of symptom onset. The analysis included participants who had failed recanalization (defined as an Arterial Occlusive Lesion [AOL] score of 0) on follow-up angiography at 24 h. Primary outcome of current study was functional independence (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 0–2) at 90 days. We assessed effect modification by baseline characteristics, including stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS]) and collateral circulation status.ResultsAmong 288 patients with failed recanalization (123 in the tenecteplase group and 165 in the control group), tenecteplase was associated with a significantly higher rate of functional independence at 90 days compared with standard medical therapy (42.3% vs. 29.7%; adjusted risk ratio [RR] 1.37, 95% CI [Confidence Interval] 1.00–1.86, P = 0.048). Tenecteplase also resulted in greater reduction in NIHSS scores at 7 days (adjusted β 0.31, P = 0.028). A significant interaction was observed between treatment effect and baseline stroke severity (P for interaction = 0.02). The benefit of tenecteplase was significant in patients with moderate-to-severe stroke (NIHSS ≥ 10; RR 2.75, 95% CI 1.30–5.79) but not significant in those with minor-to-moderate stroke (NIHSS < 10). Collateral status did not significantly modify the treatment effect (P for interaction = 0.35).ConclusionsIn patients with ischemic stroke treated in the late time window, tenecteplase was associated with improved functional outcomes despite persistent large vessel occlusion. Our findings suggest that tenecteplase may confer therapeutic efficacy through mechanisms beyond proximal arterial reopening.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05141305.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04877-x.

Background

Intravenous thrombolysis remains a cornerstone of reperfusion therapy for acute ischemic stroke [1]. While tenecteplase has demonstrated non-inferiority to alteplase within the 4.5 h’ time window [2], the Tenecteplase Reperfusion Therapy in Acute Ischemic Cerebrovascular Events-3 (TRACE-3) trial further confirmed the safety and efficacy of tenecteplase administered between 4.5 to 24 h after stroke onset in patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO) who demonstrated ischemic penumbra on perfusion imaging and were ineligible for endovascular thrombectomy [3]. Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of patients—approximately half in the TRACE-3 trial—fail to achieve successful recanalization of the proximal arterial trunk following thrombolysis.

Although angiographic recanalization of the large vessel is traditionally considered the primary surrogate for therapeutic success [4], persistent proximal occlusion does not invariably preclude clinical recovery [5]. Emerging evidence suggests a dissociation between macrovascular patency and tissue-level reperfusion, a phenomenon where favorable functional outcomes occur despite failed recanalization [6–8]. While spontaneous reperfusion or robust collaterals may explain some of these cases [9], there is increasing interest in whether pharmacological agents can actively promote this dissociation. This phenomenon may be driven by the dissolution of downstream microvascular thrombosis and the preservation of microcirculatory patency [10]. Tenecteplase, characterized by high fibrin specificity and a long half-life, may theoretically penetrate distal vascular beds via collateral channels to lyse microthrombi [11], thereby restoring tissue perfusion even in the absence of proximal recanalization [5, 11]. However, this hypothesis has primarily been explored in early time windows or preclinical models. Whether this ‘microvascular benefit’ persists in the extended time window (4.5–24 h), where thrombi are more organized and collateral failure is more prevalent, remains unknown. Furthermore, it is unclear whether this potential benefit is uniform across all patients or if it is modified by baseline characteristics such as stroke severity or collateral status.

In this post hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial, we aimed to evaluate the association between tenecteplase and functional outcomes specifically in patients who did not achieve successful recanalization. We hypothesized that tenecteplase would be associated with improved 90-day functional outcomes in this population, and we further sought to explore potential effect modifiers, including baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and collateral circulation status, that might identify patients most likely to benefit from this "microvascular salvage" effect.

Methods

Patient population

This study is a post-hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05141305). The TRACE-3 trial was a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial that evaluated the efficacy and safety of intravenous tenecteplase (0.25 mg/kg; maximum dose, 25 mg) versus standard medical therapy in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to LVO within 4.5 to 24 h of symptom onset [3]. Eligible patients had evidence of ischemic penumbra on perfusion imaging and were ineligible for endovascular thrombectomy. This trial involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval Number: KY2021-129–03). Written informed consent to participate in the trial was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives prior to enrollment. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as trial protocols, have been previously reported [12].

The current analysis included participants from the TRACE-3 intention-to-treat population who had failed recanalization at the 24-h follow-up. Failed recanalization was defined as an Arterial Occlusive Lesion (AOL, a scale from 0 [no recanalization] to 3 [complete recanalization]) score of 0 on computed tomography angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) performed 24 h (± 6 h) after randomization. To isolate the effect of intravenous thrombolysis, patients who underwent rescue endovascular thrombectomy after randomization were excluded.

Clinical and radiological assessment

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics—including age, sex, NIHSS score, prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, time from symptom onset, medical history, laboratory parameters, occlusion site, and stroke etiology (Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classification [TOAST]) —were prospectively collected. Functional outcomes at 90 days were assessed via telephone interview by blinded investigators.

All participants underwent multimodal imaging, including non-contrast computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), along with vascular (CTA/MRA) and perfusion imaging, at baseline and approximately 24 h (± 6 h) after enrollment. All imaging data were transmitted to an independent central imaging core laboratory for centralized adjudication. Assessors evaluated arterial occlusion sites, recanalization status using the AOL scale [13], and the presence of intracranial hemorrhage. Assessors were blinded to clinical data and treatment allocation.

Perfusion parameters derived from computed tomography perfusion (CTP) or perfusion-weighted imaging (PWI) were quantified using iStroke software (version 3.13) [14, 15] to determine volumes of the irreversibly injured ischemic core, hypoperfused tissue, and ischemic penumbra. The ischemic core was defined as regions with cerebral blood flow (CBF) < 30% relative to the unaffected hemisphere on CTP, or an apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) < 620 × 10⁻⁶ mm2/s on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Hypoperfusion was defined as tissue with a time-to-maximum (Tmax) delay > 6 s. The penumbral volume was calculated as the difference between the total hypoperfusion volume (Tmax > 6 s) and the ischemic core volume.

Collateral circulation was assessed on baseline single-phase CTA (or the arterial phase of multiphase CTA) using the modified Tan score [16], consistent with the methodology of the Endovascular Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands for Late Arrivals (MR CLEAN-LATE) trial [17]. This scale grades collateral flow in the middle cerebral artery territory of the affected hemisphere relative to the contralateral side: Grade 0 (no collateral flow), Grade 1 (≤ 50% filling), Grade 2 (> 50% but < 100% filling), and Grade 3 (100% filling). For this analysis, good collateral circulation was defined as a modified Tan score of 2–3, and poor collateral circulation was defined as a score of 0–1.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome for this post-hoc analysis was functional independence at 90 days, defined as an mRS score of 0 to 2. This differs from the original TRACE-3 trial, which utilized an excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) as its primary outcome. Given that failed recanalization is typically associated with larger infarct volumes and a poorer prognosis [18], achieving functional independence (mRS 0–2) represents a clinically meaningful therapeutic goal in this specific high-risk population. Furthermore, the selection of this outcome aligns with established methodological precedents [6, 7], as prior studies investigating failed large vessel recanalization have consistently utilized mRS 0–2 as their primary outcome.

Secondary efficacy outcomes included: (1) excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) at 90 days; (2) major neurologic improvement at 72 h (NIHSS reduction ≥ 8 points or score ≤ 1); (3) change in NIHSS score from baseline to 7 days; and (4) volume of hypoperfusion lesion at 24 h. Safety outcomes included symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) within 36 h (defined according to the ECASS III criteria) and all-cause mortality within 90 days.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped by treatment (tenecteplase vs. control). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Baseline characteristics were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney tests for continuous variables. Associations between treatment and outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear models with a log link and binomial distribution (for binary outcomes) and linear regression models (for continuous outcomes). Adjustments were made for potential confounders selected based on a P-value < 0.1 in univariable analyses or clinical relevance, including age, sex, baseline ischemic core volume, smoking history, hemoglobin and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Major neurologic improvement at 72 h was not adjusted for multivariate analysis because of the low incidence of outcome events. Results are presented as adjusted risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) or adjusted β coefficients. To explore potential effect modifiers, subgroup analyses were performed based on age, sex, onset-to-treatment time, baseline NIHSS score (< 10 vs. ≥ 10), occlusion site, and collateral circulation status. Interaction terms between treatment and each subgroup variable were entered into the models to calculate P-values for interaction.

A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

A total of 516 patients were enrolled in the TRACE-3 trial. Among these, 291 had failed recanalization on 24-h follow-up imaging, and 3 of them received endovascular treatment after randomization. Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics were well balanced between the two groups (Table 1). The median age was 66 years (IQR 57–73) in the tenecteplase group and 67 years (IQR 59–73) in the control group. Sex distribution was comparable, with 31 (25.2%) females in the tenecteplase group versus 53 (32.1%) in the control group. The median time from symptom onset to treatment was also similar between the tenecteplase group (12.9 h; IQR 9.3–17.0) and the control group (13.3 h; IQR 9.4–18.6). Median baseline NIHSS scores were similar (9 [IQR 7–13] vs. 10 [IQR 7–14]). Regarding secondary outcomes, tenecteplase was associated with a greater reduction in NIHSS score at 7 days compared with the control group (median change − 3 [IQR − 5 to 0] vs. − 1 [IQR − 4 to 1]; adjusted β 0.31, 95% CI 0.11–0.88, P = 0.028). The rate of excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) was higher in the tenecteplase group (31.7% vs. 21.8%), though this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted RR 1.35, 95% CI 0.90–2.02, P = 0.142). Safety outcomes

The safety profile was comparable between the two groups. No sICH occurred within 36 h in either group. The 90-day mortality rate was lower in the tenecteplase group (8.1% [10/123]) compared with the control group (12.7% [21/165]), although this difference was not statistically significant (adjusted RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.28–1.36, P = 0.230).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial, tenecteplase thrombolysis within the 4.5 to 24 h’ time window demonstrated a significant association with favorable 90-day functional outcomes among patients who failed to achieve recanalization. While the original TRACE-3 trial established the overall efficacy of tenecteplase in the extended 4.5 to 24 h’ time window, our post-hoc analysis builds upon the TRACE-3 study to further explore the potential mechanisms underlying the prognostic improvement conferred by large vessel recanalization. In the TRACE-3 trial, approximately half of the patients failed to achieve successful recanalization of the proximal arterial trunk; failure to achieve recanalization is often viewed with therapeutic nihilism [19]. Our post-hoc analysis supports the concept of a dissociation between macrovascular recanalization and clinical improvement, indicating that tenecteplase may confer therapeutic benefits through mechanisms beyond large vessel recanalization. Furthermore, exploratory subgroup analyses suggest that this association may be modified by baseline stroke severity, appearing more pronounced in patients with moderate-to-severe neurological deficits (NIHSS ≥ 10), whereas no significant interaction was observed with collateral circulation status.

Notably, we selected functional independence (mRS score 0–2) as the primary outcome for this post-hoc analysis, rather than the excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) utilized in the original TRACE-3 trial. This methodological adjustment reflects the more severe natural history inherent to persistent LVO. In the absence of macrovascular recanalization, patients typically experience extensive infarct evolution, making the achievement of an excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) an excessively rigorous target [18]. For this specific high-risk cohort, shifting the clinical trajectory from severe disability or death to functional independence (mRS 0–2) represents a profound and realistic therapeutic achievement. Furthermore, this outcome selection aligns closely with contemporary stroke literature. Prior studies investigating failed recanalization in both direct endovascular thrombectomy and bridging therapy have consistently adopted mRS 0–2 as the standard metric to accurately capture clinically meaningful treatment effects [6, 7].

Interestingly, an exploratory analysis revealed a significant interaction between baseline stroke severity and the treatment effect (P for interaction = 0.02). The positive association with tenecteplase appeared more pronounced in patients with moderate-to-severe stroke (NIHSS ≥ 10), whereas no significant effect was observed in those with minor-to-moderate deficits (NIHSS < 10). If validated, this heterogeneity may be explained by a "ceiling effect" in milder strokes, where robust collateral circulation often preserves tissue viability and leads to favorable outcomes independent of thrombolysis [20, 21]. Conversely, patients with higher NIHSS scores typically harbor larger volumes of critically hypoperfused tissue [22, 23]. We hypothesize that in these patients, the possible microvascular reperfusion and neuroprotective effects of tenecteplase might be able to rescue more neurons, thereby shifting the clinical trajectory from severe disability to functional independence. However, given the limited sample size of this subgroup, these findings are strictly hypothesis-generating and must be interpreted with caution. Notably, whereas meta-analyses demonstrate that patients with minor stroke and large vessel occlusion derive significant prognostic improvement from intravenous thrombolysis in the early therapeutic window, the divergent efficacy profile observed in our cohort—specifically within the late time window and under the premise of persistent macrovascular occlusion—underscores the pathophysiological complexity of this population [24]. Larger, adequately powered studies are required to confirm these observations.

The most plausible physiological explanation for the observed "benefit without recanalization" lies in the potential preservation of microcirculatory patency. Ischemic stroke pathophysiology involves not only proximal macroscopic occlusion but also downstream microvascular thrombosis, which drives tissue hypoperfusion and infarct expansion [5]. Tenecteplase, with its high fibrin specificity, is postulated to penetrate the distal vascular bed and lyse nascent microthrombi within the capillary network, even when the organized proximal thrombus remains resistant to thrombolysis [25]. This "microvascular resolution" mechanism could theoretically decrease downstream resistance and sustain metabolic supply to the penumbra [25]. Such a hypothesis aligns with the concept of a dissociation between vessel recanalization and tissue-level reperfusion, suggesting that clinical recovery may be driven by microvascular salvage despite persistent proximal blockade. However, direct verification of this hypothesis is hindered by current imaging limitations. Conventional perfusion parameters (e.g., Tmax and CBF) primarily reflect macrovascular characteristics [26] and lack the sensitivity to measure microvascular reperfusion [27]. Consequently, the absence of widely accessible microvascular imaging in clinical practice makes directly quantifying these microcirculatory improvements challenging [28].

The role of collateral circulation in mediating this benefit remains to be fully elucidated. Theoretically, collaterals serve as conduits for delivering thrombolytic agents to the distal microvasculature [29]. In our analysis, patients with good collateral circulation showed a trend toward greater benefit from tenecteplase, but the interaction was not statistically significant (P = 0.35). This lack of significance may be attributable to limited statistical power or the limitations of single-phase CTA in accurately grading collateral flow compared with multiphase CTA or perfusion imaging [30, 31]. Consequently, while good collaterals likely facilitate drug delivery, our data do not definitively identify collateral status as a strict prerequisite for tenecteplase efficacy in this setting.

Delayed spontaneous recanalization beyond 24 h may represent an additional mechanism contributing to favorable outcomes in this subgroup. Recent studies have identified a dense outer thrombus shell composed of fibrin, von Willebrand factor (VWF) and aggregated platelets. This shell exhibits lower porosity and greater spatial compactness compared to the thrombus core. Key components of the shell-including platelets, extracellular DNA and fibrin-actively impede fibrinolysis [32, 33]. Thrombolytic agents such as alteplase appear to disrupt this shell architecture. A histopathological study comparing thrombi from bridging thrombectomy versus direct thrombectomy cohorts revealed that intravenous alteplase pretreatment significantly reduces thrombus burden [34], with treated thrombi demonstrating thinner fibrin strands and reduced structural density [35]. These findings suggest that even in cases without recanalization at 24-h imaging follow-up, thrombi in the tenecteplase-treated group undergo compositional alterations that predispose to late spontaneous recanalization. However, critical questions remain unresolved: (1) the extent of salvageable penumbral tissue persisting between 28.5–48 h post-stroke, and (2) whether reperfusion within this window translates into meaningful clinical benefits. To address these gaps, our team will initiate the Tenecteplase Reperfusion therapy in Acute ischemic Cerebrovascular Events-BEYOND (TRACE-BEYOND) trial, investigating the therapeutic potential of tenecteplase-mediated reperfusion in the 24–72-h post-onset window.

Beyond its primary role in intravascular thrombolysis, tissue plasminogen activator (tPA; e.g., alteplase) may exert direct neuroprotective effects in ischemic stroke via various receptor-mediated, non-thrombolytic pathways. For example, tPA promotes the degradation of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3), thereby increasing the bioavailability of free insulin-like growth factor (IGF1). The subsequent activation of the IGF1 receptor (IGF1R) significantly suppresses detrimental autophagy during ischemia. Notably, this mechanism has been validated in patients with acute stroke receiving intravenous thrombolysis [36]. Furthermore, tPA enhances neuronal tolerance to ischemia by activating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway or upregulating tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), ultimately improving functional outcomes [37, 38]. Because tenecteplase is a bioengineered variant of native tPA, whether it shares these direct neuroprotective properties or possesses distinct functional domains and physiological effects warrants further investigation using systems biology approaches, such as proteomics.

Our findings also delineate critical avenues for future research aimed at unraveling the "benefit without recanalization" paradox. While we hypothesize that downstream microvascular reperfusion drives the observed clinical recovery, this mechanism remains inferential based on current imaging standards. Future studies should incorporate advanced neuroimaging modalities, such as multiphase CT angiography or dynamic perfusion imaging with specific microvascular parameters (e.g., capillary transit time heterogeneity), to directly visualize and quantify microcirculatory patency in vivo [39, 40]. Additionally, the potential synergy between thrombolytics and neuroprotective agents or antithrombotic therapies warrants investigation. For instance, adjunct therapies that stabilize the endothelium or prevent microthrombosis re-accumulation could amplify the microvascular salvage effect of tenecteplase [41, 42]. Finally, randomized trials specifically designed for the "thrombectomy-ineligible" or "failed-recanalization" populations are needed to prospectively validate whether optimizing microvascular perfusion-rather than solely pursuing proximal recanalization-can serve as an independent and viable therapeutic target in the era of precision stroke medicine.

Our study has several limitations: First, the sample size of the subgroup with failed recanalization was relatively small, which limited the statistical power to detect significant interactions (such as with collateral status) and necessitated the use of wider confidence intervals. Second, collateral circulation was assessed using single-phase CTA-based Tan scores. While widely used, multiphase CTA or perfusion-based collateral grading might provide greater precision [43]. Third, the TRACE-3 trial was conducted in China, where the prevalence of intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) is higher than in Western populations. Since ICAD-related occlusions may respond differently to thrombolysis compared to cardioembolic clots, generalization to other populations should be done with caution [44, 45].

Conclusions

In this post hoc analysis of the TRACE-3 trial, tenecteplase thrombolysis within 4.5 to 24 h was significantly associated with better 90-day functional outcomes in patients who failed to achieve recanalization. Our findings suggest that tenecteplase may confer clinical efficacy through mechanisms beyond proximal arterial reopening, supporting its potential value as a therapeutic option in the late time window even when macroscopic recanalization is not achieved.
