OsteoStrong and dynamic exercise in older women: rethinking comparative effectiveness
Authors: Furkat Ruzmetov, Rizaev Jasur, Laziz Jumayev, Aminjon Amonov, Xondamir Ahmadov, Baxodir Usmanov, Farrukh Narzullayev, Tarazi Naghashidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exercise is a cornerstone of osteoporosis management, yet the comparative effectiveness of isometric machine‑based training versus dynamic multicomponent exercise remains unclear. Lindberg and colleagues conducted a randomized trial comparing OsteoStrong (OS) to dynamic multicomponent exercise (DME) in 194 older women with osteopenia or osteoporosis, finding superior improvements in gait speed, back endurance, and balance with DME. While these findings are clinically relevant, several methodological considerations warrant attention: the six‑fold difference in training volume (20 vs. 120 min/week) confounds modality comparisons; functional outcomes were interpreted as superiority evidence despite the trial’s non‑inferiority design; participants’ high baseline activity may limit generalizability; and unblinded follow‑up assessments risk detection bias. We offer a perspective on these methodological challenges and proposes directions for future research to refine exercise recommendations for this population.

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Lindberg and colleagues, which compares the effects of OsteoStrong and dynamic multicomponent exercise on physical function in older women with osteopenia or osteoporosis [1]. The trial reported that DME produced superior improvements in gait speed, back endurance, and balance. However, a closer examination of the trial’s design raises several methodological considerations that warrant careful attention before such conclusions can be accepted. Specifically, the substantial imbalance in training volume, the shift from a non-inferiority design to a superiority interpretation, the high baseline physical activity of participants, and the lack of assessor blinding at follow-up collectively temper the strength of the reported between-group differences.

The volume–dose dilemma

A critical methodological consideration in the BONEMORE trial is the marked disparity in total exercise volume between the two intervention groups. Participants in the OsteoStrong (OS) group trained once weekly for approximately 20 min per session, while the dynamic multicomponent exercise (DME) group trained twice weekly for 60 min per session, a six‑fold difference in total weekly training time [1]. This substantial imbalance confounds the interpretation of the observed between‑group differences, making it impossible to determine whether the superior outcomes in the DME group reflect the type of exercise or simply the greater volume of training. The authors suggest that high‑magnitude loading in OS may compensate for its brevity, citing evidence that high‑load resistance training can elicit meaningful adaptations even with low volume. However, this hypothesis was not directly tested. Research on exercise dose‑response in older adults demonstrates that benefits increase with dose until an optimal threshold is reached [2], and both intensity and volume independently contribute to improvements in muscle strength and physical performance in frail populations [3]. The absence of a dose‑finding design precludes any definitive attribution of the observed effects to exercise modality rather than total volume. Without such data, the conclusion that DME is superior to OS in improving gait speed, back endurance, and balance remains confounded [1]. If twice‑weekly OS sessions could achieve comparable outcomes, it might offer a more time‑efficient option for older women facing barriers to longer sessions. Until dose‑response studies are conducted, the relative efficacy of these two modalities cannot be reliably established.

A superiority interpretation

A fundamental methodological concern arises from the disconnect between the trial’s stated design and its interpretation of findings. The BONEMORE trial was explicitly designed as a non‑inferiority study for bone strength outcomes, with a pre‑specified margin of BMSi − 5.2, and successfully established non‑inferiority for the primary outcome [4]. Yet the physical function results are presented using a superiority framework, concluding that DME provides superior benefits in gait speed, back endurance, and balance. This shift from a non‑inferiority design to a superiority interpretation is methodologically problematic. As Dasgupta and colleagues have noted, a nonsignificant result obtained from a superiority test does not indicate that two treatment options are similar, and conversely, non‑inferiority trials are not designed to detect superiority [5]. The statistical approach required for non‑inferiority, testing whether the confidence interval excludes a pre‑specified margin, differs fundamentally from the superiority testing framework. To claim superiority within a non‑inferiority trial, a sequential testing procedure is recommended: first establish non‑inferiority through the pre‑specified margin, and only then proceed to a superiority test [6]. This step was not performed for the functional outcomes in the BONEMORE trial, nor were these secondary outcomes powered for superiority detection. Valid claims of superiority require a trial specifically designed with adequate statistical power for that purpose, not a secondary analysis of a non‑inferiority study. We suggest that the observed between‑group differences in gait speed, back endurance, and balance should be interpreted as exploratory findings rather than definitive evidence of DME superiority. Future studies specifically designed to test superiority of DME over OS for functional outcomes, with appropriate sample sizes and statistical frameworks, would be required to substantiate such claims.

High baseline physical activity

A notable limitation in the BONEMORE trial is the exceptionally high baseline physical activity level of the participants. Approximately 87% met the WHO recommendation of 150–300 min of weekly activity, substantially exceeding the national average of 54% for Swedish women aged 65–84 years [1]. This likely reflects a sample with above‑average fitness, raising concerns about generalizability to the broader population of older women with osteopenia or osteoporosis. Such high baseline function may create a ceiling effect, where the potential for further improvement is constrained [7]. As the authors note, mean baseline gait speed (1.77 m/s) was markedly higher than normative data for Swedish older women (1.54 m/s) [1]. When participants already perform well above population averages, the capacity to detect meaningful intervention effects diminishes. Ceiling effects can lead to underestimation of true intervention efficacy and obscure between‑group differences [7]. Studies on frail older populations demonstrate that lower baseline function yields greater relative gains from exercise interventions [8]. We suggest that future research should include participants with lower baseline activity levels to better represent the broader population.

Unblinded follow‑up assessments

A notable methodological limitation in the BONEMORE trial is the lack of assessor blinding at the nine‑month follow‑up. While the physical therapists conducting the assessments were blinded at baseline, they were not blinded at follow‑up, as the authors acknowledge was not feasible [1]. This introduces a risk of detection bias that warrants careful consideration, particularly given the performance‑based nature of many outcome measures. Evidence from a meta‑regression of 73 randomized controlled trials of progressive resistance training in older adults demonstrated that trials with unblinded outcome assessors reported significantly larger effect sizes than those with blinded assessors, with a standardized mean difference of -0.80 (95% CI, -1.35 to -0.25). This finding persisted even after adjusting for intention‑to‑treat analysis (adjusted difference − 0.65; 95% CI, -1.26 to -0.04). The authors concluded that unblinded assessors tend to exaggerate treatment effects, particularly when performance‑based measures, such as gait speed, balance, and strength tests, are used [9]. Even subtle encouragement, such as verbal prompts during physical performance tests, can significantly influence participant performance. Although muscle strength and functional tests may appear objective, they remain susceptible to detection bias when assessors are aware of treatment allocation. A systematic review of blinding in surgical trials further confirmed that lack of blinding is associated with overestimation of treatment effects, particularly for subjective or performance‑based outcomes [10]. Given that the BONEMORE trial’s most pronounced between‑group differences were observed in gait speed and balance, outcomes highly susceptible to assessor influence, the absence of blinding at follow‑up may have contributed to the observed superiority of DME over OS. We suggest that future studies should prioritize blinded outcome assessments at all time points to safeguard internal validity.

Conclusion

The BONEMORE trial demonstrates that both OsteoStrong and dynamic exercise improve physical function in older women with osteopenia or osteoporosis. However, unequal training volume, the non‑inferiority to superiority shift, high baseline activity, and unblinded assessments limit definitive conclusions about comparative effectiveness. Future research should address these methodological gaps to strengthen exercise recommendations.
