CLABP: a contrastive learning framework integrating protein language models and structural information for antibacterial peptide prediction
Authors: Xiangrun Zhou, Guixia Liu, Ji Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibacterial peptides (ABPs) represent a promising alternative strategy for combating antimicrobial resistance. Graph neural networks and their variants have shown substantial potential in ABP identification. However, most existing graph-based methods focus on node features while neglecting edge features. Edge features play an important role in characterizing peptide conformation and spatial stability, thereby directly influencing interactions with bacterial membranes and antibacterial activity. Moreover, node and edge features extracted by heterogeneous encoders reside in disparate feature spaces, making their effective integration nontrivial. These challenges highlight the need for an explicit alignment mechanism to bridge heterogeneous representations. In this study, we propose CLABP, a contrastive learning-based framework for ABP identification that integrates edge features. Specifically, backbone dihedral angles, ProtT5 embeddings, and Define Secondary Structure of Proteins-derived secondary structures were employed as node features, while inter-residue distance, motion vectors, and rotation quaternions were used to construct edge features. Node and edge representations were independently extracted using dedicated encoders and subsequently aligned into a shared latent space via contrastive learning, which minimizes the distance between paired representations derived from the same peptide. The aligned features were then fused through a dual cross-attention mechanism for downstream prediction. CLABP outperforms other state-of-the-art methods, achieving an accuracy of 92.6% and a Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.853. Ablation studies further confirm that both edge features and the alignment mechanism are critical to model performance. The datasets and code for the CLABP model are available at https://github.com/IBelieve1234/CLABP.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health concern [1, 2], which has attracted widespread attention from governments and society around the world. A systematic analysis of global AMR burden revealed that in 2019, there were 1.27 million deaths worldwide due to AMR [3]. In recent years, the research and development of new antibiotics has been slowed, and most of the antibiotics used in clinical practice were discovered in the decades following World War II [2]. Due to the continuous development and evolution of AMR, the clinical efficacy of many antibiotics is gradually weakening and even becoming ineffective [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new antimicrobial drugs to combat AMR.

Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) are promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics [5, 6], and have shown distinct advantages and broad applications (e.g., medical antimicrobial agents [5, 6], food preservatives [7], and feed additives [8]). AMPs are widely found in animals, plants, and microorganisms, and are typically 16 to 100 amino acids in length [9]. Compared with traditional antibiotics, AMPs not only exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity but are also less susceptible to AMR due to their unique mechanisms of action (i.e. targeting bacterial cell membrane) [10]. However, the development of AMPs is a challenging task. In experimental research, the identification of AMPs is not only complicated, but also inefficient [11]. In recent years, the accumulation of AMP data [12] and the development of computational technology have provided new opportunities for AMP prediction. So far, researchers have developed various computational models and dedicated tools for peptide prediction [13, 14]. These models can be divided into two groups: machine learning models and deep learning models. In traditional machine learning models, peptide features (e.g. amino acid composition, structural information, and physicochemical properties) are fed into classifiers (e.g. support vector machines and random forests) to determine whether a given peptide sequence exhibits specific functional activity [15–17]. However, the implementation of traditional machine learning methods relies on expert-level domain knowledge and feature engineering. In recent years, deep learning has gained widespread attention in both academia and industry due to its outstanding learning ability and advantages in feature extraction [18–20]. It has also demonstrated great potential in peptide prediction [21–25]. For example, Lv et al. [26] developed AMPpred-EL, an ensemble-learning-based model for AMP prediction that integrates multiple sequence features. This study showed the effectiveness of feature combination for improving AMP classification performance. Xu et al. proposed a deep learning algorithm called iAMPCN. This algorithm first encoded peptide sequences using one-hot encoder (e.g. one-hot encoding). Then, a convolutional neural network (CNN) was used to extract features from the encoded data. Finally, the extracted features were concatenated and fed into fully connected layers for AMP prediction [27]. Experimental results demonstrated that iAMPCN outperforms traditional machine learning algorithms. However, the vectors generated by one-hot encoding are high-dimensional and sparse, and their lengths are often inconsistent, which may negatively affect AMP prediction performance. Inspired by the pretrained models in natural language processing, protein language models (e.g. PESTV [28], ESM [29], and ProtT5 [30]) have been proposed. Sharma et al. used PESTV to encode peptide sequences [31]. It is shown that the performance of the model degrades if PESTV is replaced with a non-pretrained encoder (e.g. one-hot encoder). Protein language models have demonstrated excellent performance in AMP prediction. However, these methods ignore structural information of peptides, which plays a crucial role in AMP prediction [32]. To address this problem, Yan et al. [32] proposed a model named sAMPpred-GAT. They constructed a graph neural network (GNN) based on peptide structure predicted by trRosetta32 and used one-hot encoding features, positional encoding features, hidden Markov model (HMM) features, and position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) features as node features. It is shown that the introduction of structural information can further improve the performance of the model. However, HMM and PSSM features rely on multiple sequence alignments and protein sequence databases, which are both time-consuming and memory-intensive. To handle this problem, protein language models can be used for peptide sequence encoding [33, 34]. For example, Jiao et al. [35] demonstrated the promise of combining pretrained protein language models with structure-aware GNNs for peptide property prediction. Hao et al. [36] proposed a novel deep learning method named PGAT-ABPp. They first constructed a graph using the structure predicted by AlphaFold2 and used ProtT5 [30] to describe peptide characteristics. Then, a graph attention (GAT) network was used to learn global discriminative features. It has shown that PGAT-ABPp exhibits robust performance. While GAT architectures [32, 36] have demonstrated promising performance in AMP prediction, their exclusive focus on node features neglects the crucial contribution of edge features (i.e. residue-residue interactions). These interactions regulate the conformational folding and spatial stability of peptides, which in turn affect their interactions with bacterial cell membranes and ultimately their antibacterial activity [37]. Although the EGAT [38] architecture can integrate both node and edge features, it lacks an explicit mechanism to align them. Without the alignment mechanism, the model fails to capture the inherent consistency between node and edge features, thereby limiting its performance. Contrastive learning provides an effective way to integrate heterogeneous information (i.e. node and edge features) [39]. More recently, contrastive learning has shown considerable promise for peptide prediction tasks [40–42].

Previous studies have suggested that backbone dihedral angles, secondary structures, motion vectors, and rotation quaternions capture important structural factors associated with AMP activity. Backbone dihedral angles (φ/ψ) reflect residue-level conformational preferences and are closely related to α-helical and β-strand formation, while secondary structures influence AMP amphipathicity and membrane interaction [43–45]. Motion vectors provide information on dynamic flexibility related to membrane adaptability and antibacterial potency [43, 46]. Inter-residue distances and rotation quaternions encode relative 3D orientation and help characterize hydrophobic–hydrophilic spatial arrangement relevant to amphipathic membrane disruption [44, 45, 47]. Therefore, these features are used to provide the model with structural information relevant to AMP activity.

In this study, we integrate protein language model and structural information into a contrastive learning-based framework for antibacterial peptide (ABP) prediction, which we term CLABP. ABPs are an important subgroup of AMPs, and their antibacterial properties have become a major focus of current research [48]. Specifically, node features (i.e. backbone dihedral angles, secondary structures, and embedding vectors generated by ProtT5) and edge features (i.e. inter-residue distances, motion vectors, and rotation quaternions) are first encoded by their respective neural encoders. Subsequently, a contrastive learning module is used to align these nodes and edge representations in a shared feature space. To capture the mutual dependencies between aligned node and edge features, a dual cross-attention mechanism is used, followed by a feature fusion layer to combine the representations. Finally, the fused features are fed into a classification layer for ABP prediction. Experimental results demonstrate that CLABP achieves state-of-the-art performance. We also visualize the contribution of contrastive learning by analyzing feature trajectory movements. Ablation experiments further confirm the importance of contrastive learning in model performance. To investigate model interpretability, we visualize the attention weights across the peptide sequence. The visualization reveals how CLABP captures meaningful patterns at critical binding sites.

Materials and methods

Dataset

In this study, we used our recently constructed dataset (http://www.acdb.plus/ABPDB/) to train and evaluate CLABP [49]. More details are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1, respectively. We obtained 3013 ABPs from ABPDB after excluding ABPs with lengths of 101 or more and those containing non-standard amino acids. For the negative dataset, we collected peptides that did not show antibacterial activity from the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) database [50]. Specifically, peptides involving the following keywords were excluded: “membrane,” “toxic,” “secretory,” “defensive,” “antibiotic,” “anticancer,” “antiviral,” “antifungal,” and “antibacterial.” Similarly, non-ABPs longer than 100 amino acids and non-ABPs containing non-standard amino acids were excluded. To reduce sequence redundancy in the negative dataset, peptide sequences were clustered using CD-HIT. A sequence identity threshold of 0.6 and a word size of four were applied, and only one representative sequence from each cluster was retained. This step was used to reduce internal redundancy within the negative dataset and alleviate bias caused by highly similar samples during model training and evaluation. Since many peptides lack experimentally determined crystal structures, AlphaFold2 [51] was used to predict three-dimensional structures of all peptides due to its strong performance in peptide structure benchmarks [52, 53].

The architecture of CLABP

The proposed model CLABP is a contrastive learning-based architecture (Fig. 2), and it can be split into three stages: (i) representation stage, (ii) node-edge fusion stage, and (iii) prediction stage.

The framework of CLABP for ABP prediction. (A) Overall architecture. The model consists of three main stages: (i) representation learning: sequences are processed by AlphaFold2 to generate predicted structures and by ProtT5 to generate sequence embeddings. Resulting features are divided into node-level features and edge-level representations. (ii) Node-edge fusion stage: node-level and edge-level features are encoded by encoders independently and aligned using contrastive learning in a shared feature space. A dual cross-attention then integrates aligned features. (iii) Prediction: the features are fused and finally classified to predict ABP activity. (B) Edge encoder: edge features are processed by an edge encoder through a network structure inspired by U-net, consisting of convolutional blocks (conv, BN, ReLU) and deconvolutional blocks (Deconv, BN, ReLU). A pooling layer is used for subsequent dimension alignment. (C) Node encoder: ProtT5 embeddings are integrated with secondary structure annotations and backbone dihedral angles (Φ/Ψ) using linear layers, ReLU activation, and an attention mechanism to obtain node-level representations.

Representation learning

In the representation learning stage, node-level and edge-level features were derived from peptide sequences and their AlphaFold2-predicted structures. The features were then independently processed by the corresponding encoders to generate node and edge representations, which served as inputs for the subsequent node-edge fusion module.

Graph representation of peptides

The 3D structure of each peptide was represented as a residue-contact graph (A, Eq. 1). Node-level feature representation

For each residue i, the node encoder (Fig. 2C) integrates three types of features: backbone dihedral angles (Φ and Ψ), secondary structure annotations, and ProtT5-derived embedding vectors. Backbone dihedral angles were represented using their sine and cosine transformations. Secondary structures (e.g. α-helix, β-sheet) were predicted using Define Secondary Structure of Proteins (DSSP) [54] and encoded as one-hot vectors. Since one-hot representation is inherently sparse, an embedding layer was used to project it into a more compact and semantically informative low-dimensional space. In addition, ProtT5 was employed as a pretrained sequence encoder to generate 1024-dimensional residue-level embeddings, providing rich contextual information to further enhance the node representation. During the training of CLABP, the ProtT5 parameters were kept frozen. All features (i.e. backbone dihedral angle encodings, secondary structure embeddings, ProtT5 vectors) were concatenated to form the initial node features. However, these features contain overlapping structural information. Naïve concatenation may introduce redundancy. To mitigate this, an attention mechanism was used to extract key features from redundant information. Furthermore, residual connections were incorporated to stabilize training and alleviate gradient-vanishing issues in deeper neural network layers.

To integrate edge features, we employed an edge encoder (Fig. 2B) based on a three-scale 2D CNN architecture inspired by U-Net. This network adopts an encoder-decoder structure that enables effective integration of edge representations while maintaining relatively low memory and computational cost. Each convolutional (or deconvolutional) block consists of a convolution (deconvolution) layer followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation function. Skip connections were used to link convolutional and deconvolutional blocks with matching dimensions. Finally, pooling operations were used for aggregating edge features after U-Net to align with the node features. Specifically, we applied average pooling and max pooling to the edge features after U-Net. The average operation is then performed to obtain the final pooled edge features.

Double cross-attention

Double cross-attention was employed to further align and integrate node and edge representations [55, 56]. In this mechanism, node and edge features were respectively used as queries in the attention module. When the aligned node features act as queries, the aligned edge features serve as keys and values. Conversely, when the aligned edge features were used as queries, the aligned node features became the keys and values. By processing the feature types in a reciprocal manner, this mechanism enables the model to explicitly capture bidirectional interactions between node- and edge-level representations.

Implementation details

The model was implemented in PyTorch (https://pytorch.org/). Binary cross-entropy loss was used to measure the discrepancy between predicted and true class labels. Model training was performed using the Adam optimizer [57] with a learning rate of 4.6875 × 10−5. Training proceeded for 100 epochs. The temperature parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tau$\end{document} in the contrastive loss was set to 0.07, and the contrastive loss weight λ was set to 0.1. The sensitivity analysis of λ is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Batch sizes for training and evaluation were 128 and 256, respectively. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB and 32 GB RAM. The average training time was approximately 148 s per epoch, while the average inference time on the test set was 37.9 s per run, calculated over 10 repeated runs. During training, the observed GPU memory usage was approximately 16 GB, and the system memory usage was about 10–12 GB.

Results

Performance comparison

To assess the performance of CLABP, we conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis. Specifically, we evaluated CLABP on test dataset, and benchmarked it against five traditional machine learning methods (IAMP-NaiveBayes [58], IAMP-KNN [58], IAMP-RF [58], IAMP-SVM [58], IAMP-XGBOOST [58]) and five deep learning methods (Deep-ABPpred [59], AMPDLMD [60], UniDL4BioPep [61], AMPpred-MFA [62], and PGAT-ABPp [36]). Among the deep learning methods, AMPDLMD [60], UniDL4BioPep [61], and PGAT-ABPp [36] incorporated pretrained protein language models, whereas PGAT-ABPp [36] additionally integrated structural features of peptides. All models with publicly available implementations were re-run on the same benchmark dataset described in Section 2.1. CLABP outperformed the other models (Table 2) across all evaluation metrics (i.e. Acc, Pr, Sp, Fs, AUC, and MCC). To mitigate the potential bias arising from a single dataset, we also evaluated CLABP on PGAT-ABPp dataset, and the results demonstrated robustness and stability of our model (Supplementary Table S1). Compared with PGAT-ABPp, the best-performing baseline in Table 2, CLABP improves Acc from 0.873 to 0.926, Pr from 0.843 to 0.915, Sp from 0.851 to 0.924, AUC from 0.933 to 0.978, Fs from 0.868 to 0.922, and MCC from 0.749 to 0.853. Notably, the relative improvement in MCC reaches 13.89%, indicating that CLABP provides more balanced and robust predictive performance. The enhanced performance of CLABP can be attributed to its effective integration of edge information and the employment of contrastive learning to align node-level and edge-level representations. This strategy enables the model to better capture the inherent structural information of peptides, thereby yielding a more informative and discriminative representation for ABP prediction.

To further assess the contribution of contrastive learning, we conducted an alignment analysis between node and edge representations. Specifically, we randomly sampled 500 peptides from the test set and quantified the degree of alignment between their corresponding node and edge representations. The Euclidean distance was computed between paired node and edge representation vectors in the representation space. As shown in Fig. 3A, 30 peptides from this subset were then projected into a two-dimensional t-SNE space to visualize the effect of contrastive learning. Trajectory plots revealed a marked shift induced by contrastive learning: before alignment, node and edge representations of the same peptide were dispersed across distinct regions of the latent space, whereas contrastive learning brought each pair substantially closer, indicating effective integration of heterogeneous information into a shared embedding space. Quantitative results also corroborated this observation. Across the 500 sampled peptides, the average distance between node and edge representations decreased from 14.50 ± 0.75 to 7.08 ± 1.74, corresponding to a 51.2% reduction (Fig. 3B). A peptide-level comparison further confirmed that contrastive learning consistently reduced pairwise distances, with an average improvement of 7.42 ± 1.94 units and a median of 7.54 units (Fig. 3C). A one-sample t-test confirmed that this improvement was statistically significant (t = 85.404, P < .001), indicating that the observed alignment gain is unlikely to be attributable to chance. Overall, these results demonstrate that contrastive learning plays a critical role in aligning heterogeneous representations, thereby enabling the model to learn a shared embedding space for peptide information.

In addition, we also assessed the impact of different pretrained language models, including ProtT5, ProtXLNet, and ProtBERT. Among these alternatives, ProtT5 achieved the best performance (Table 6). Unlike ProtXLNet and ProtBERT, which rely on unidirectional or bidirectional self-attention architectures, ProtT5 adopts an encoder-decoder framework that enables more effective extraction of multi-scale information from peptide sequences. This architecture captures not only local motifs but also long-range interactions and global sequence-level patterns. The biophysical properties of ABPs highly depend on the combined effects of local sequence features (e.g. Lys/Arg-rich segments), long-range interactions (e.g. secondary structure) and global sequence-level patterns (e.g. amphipathicity). Therefore, the deep cross-layer interaction mechanism of ProtT5 is well suited for modeling these complex sequence semantics.

Model interpretation

In this study, we used a synthetic variant of kalata B1 (sequence: CGETCVGGTCNTPGCTCSWDKCTRNGLPV) [63] and Pin2[G] (sequence: FWGALAKGALKLIGSLFSSFSKKD) [64] as case studies to assess the interpretability of CLABP.

In the synthetic variant of kalata B1, we first visualized the attention patterns across all attention heads. As shown in Fig. 4A, the attention weight distributions of all eight attention heads revealed distinct focusing behaviors. Attention heads (Heads 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, and 8) exhibited pronounced focus patterns, indicating a strong emphasis on local dependencies, whereas others (Heads 5 and 6) exhibited more diffuse distributions that reflect long-range interactions. These complementary patterns demonstrate the ability of the multi-head attention mechanism to extract information from different representational perspectives. Subsequently, we aggregated the individual heatmaps (Fig. 4B) to reveal the overall attention pattern governing the relationships between input and output positions. This averaged heatmap provided a comprehensive view of the collective attention behavior across all eight heads. Notably, several residues received consistently high attention scores, including position 0 (C), position 1 (G), position 7 (G), position 22 (T), position 26 (L), and position 27 (P). These residues correspond to functional sites previously documented in cyclotide studies, indicating that the model successfully captures meaningful structural and functional characteristics.

Specifically, Clark et al. found that position 0 (C) participates in forming the conserved disulfide bonds in cyclotides, adopting a left-handed spiral conformation and constituting a key component of the cystine-knot core. Position 1 (G) in Loop 1 also receives high attention, consistent with evidence that Loop 1 is highly conserved in cyclotides. Position 7 (G) in Loop 2 may contribute to conformational flexibility, as the conformation of Loop 2 is influenced by variations in other loops. Position 22 (T) lies in the β-hairpin region (position 15–22), supporting the formation of this secondary structure. Finally, position 26 (L) and position 27 (P) are part of the hydrophobic patch associated with membrane interactions, suggesting that the model identifies residues relevant to biological activity.

In Pin2[G] (FWGALAKGALKLIGSLFSSFSKKD), the attention weight distributions across the eight attention heads also exhibit complementary patterns of focus (Fig. 4C). Some attention heads display more localized attention patterns (Heads 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8), while others show relatively dispersed distributions (Heads 5, 6, and 7), suggesting that the multi-head attention mechanism is capable of capturing complementary sequence relationships from different representational perspectives. Upon averaging across the individual attention heatmaps (Fig. 4D), the overall attention pattern consistently shows higher attention weights in the N-terminal to central region (positions 0–13) compared to the C-terminal region.

This pattern is broadly consistent with the structure–function relationship discussed in the original study. Rodríguez et al. noted that position 13 (Pro14) of native Pin2 forms a structural “kink” associated with membrane interaction, a residue substituted by Gly in Pin2[G]. The model’s relatively elevated attention on position 13 may therefore reflect an alignment with this key design position. Additionally, the shorter variants Pin2 [14] and Pin2 [17], derived from Pin2[G] by optimizing amphipathicity, retain sequence features predominantly from the N-terminal to central region. Their preserved inhibitory activity against Escherichia coli and Mycobacterium tuberculosis further supports the functional importance of this region, which is consistent with the model’s preferential attention toward it.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced CLABP, an ABP prediction model that integrates edge features and contrastive learning. Specifically, we first extracted node- and edge-level features from ProtT5 embeddings and predicted peptide structures. These heterogeneous representations were then aligned through contrastive learning and integrated using a cross-attention mechanism. As a result, CLABP effectively captures both local motifs and long-range interactions. Experimental results demonstrate that CLABP outperforms existing approaches across multiple evaluation metrics. Ablation studies further highlight the essential roles of edge features and alignment in ABP prediction. In addition, CLABP can highlight biologically meaningful residues, supporting the interpretability of the model. In summary, CLABP provides a powerful and interpretable framework for ABP prediction, offering valuable insights for ABP design and facilitating the discovery of novel therapeutic candidates.
