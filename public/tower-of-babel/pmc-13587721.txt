Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome associated with Rickettsia conorii infection: a rare case report
Authors: Hamidreza Naderi, Alireza Sedaghat, AbouSaleh Sobhi, Mohammadreza Seyedzadeh, Hamidreza Mirzaei, Parsa Aryanpour, Saber Esmaeili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a rare neurological disorder characterized by headaches, seizures, and visual symptoms.•Rickettsia conorii is an infectious cause of PRES.•Rickettsial infections should be considered in atypical cases of PRES.•Intravenous ciprofloxacin may be an alternative when doxycycline cannot be administered.

Introduction

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a clinical and radiological condition characterized by acute to subacute neurological disorders such as headache, seizures, altered consciousness, and visual disturbances. It is predominantly associated with posterior vasogenic edema on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The pathophysiological mechanisms of PRES involve disruption of the blood-brain barrier due to endothelial injury, autoregulation failure, and systemic inflammatory stress [1]. While this syndrome is classically linked to severe hypertension, eclampsia, and immunosuppressive therapy, it is increasingly being recognized that infectious and inflammatory triggers also play an important role [1,2].

Rickettsial infections are among the most important vector-borne diseases, causing a wide range of clinical symptoms and signs and posing a substantial public health concern. Among the various species of the Rickettsia genus, Rickettsia conorii (causative agent of Mediterranean spotted fever [MSF]) has the most extensive global distribution. MSF is a tick-borne disease that is mostly transmitted to humans by Rhipicephalus sanguineus sensu lato. It is endemic to parts of the Mediterranean Basin, the Middle East, and South Asia [3]. There has been a recent increase in the reported cases of MSF in Iran, particularly in the eastern and northeastern parts of Iran [4,5].

Patients with MSF usually present with fever, rash, and sometimes an eschar, potentially leading to multisystem involvement via widespread vasculitis [3]. Neurological complications of MSF observed in some patients include meningitis, encephalitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, and cranial neuropathy [6]. PRES following rickettsiosis is extremely rare, with only one case reported in India that was associated with spotted fever group rickettsiosis [7].

We report a young man with serologically confirmed R. conorii infection who presented with meningoencephalitis, acute kidney injury, diffuse vasculitic rash, and cortical blindness, in whom MRI findings were consistent with PRES. This case expands the clinical spectrum of rickettsial infections and provides further insight into the pathogenetic link between rickettsial endothelial injury and PRES.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old previously healthy male conscript soldier (resident of Mashhad City in northeastern Iran) was admitted to a local hospital with a 5-day history of fever, chills, and progressive weakness. On examination, he was febrile and tachycardic with a diffuse petechial and vasculitic rash involving the trunk, abdomen, and back (Figure 1). Laboratory tests showed severe thrombocytopenia and markedly elevated serum creatinine levels, and despite repeated hemodialysis sessions, renal function continued to deteriorate. He remained febrile and received empiric intravenous meropenem and vancomycin. Given the triad of fever, meningoencephalitis, and diffuse vasculitic rash with negative microbiological cultures, an infectious disease consultation was requested. Based on the MRI findings, epidemiological background, and exanthematous presentation, R. conorii infection was suspected. Blood samples were sent to the Research Centre for Emerging and Reemerging Infectious Diseases at the Pasteur Institute of Iran for molecular and serologic testing. Due to severe encephalopathy and inability to tolerate oral therapy, intravenous ciprofloxacin was initiated. Antiepileptic therapy, hemodialysis, and supportive management were continued, and dexamethasone was added to treat possible autoimmune vasculitis.

The initial indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was positive for immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies (dilution of 1:24) against R. conorii. Molecular testing was negative for Rickettsia. The patient was transferred to the infectious disease ward. Over the subsequent days, his renal function normalized, his fever subsided, and his level of consciousness returned to baseline. The seizures did not recur, and his visual acuity progressively improved. After 2 weeks, repeat IFA demonstrated a fourfold rise in the IgM titer from a dilution of 1:24 to a dilution of 1:96, confirming acute R. conorii infection. Upon discharge, the patient was afebrile and neurologically intact and experienced full recovery of both visual and renal functions. Discussion

This case represents an exceptionally rare presentation of R. conorii infection manifesting as PRES. This syndrome is characterized by vasogenic edema caused by endothelial dysfunction and impaired cerebral blood flow regulation [6]. Rickettsiae are obligate intracellular organisms with endothelial cell tropism that cause inflammation, apoptosis, and microvascular injury [6,8]. The overlap in the pathogenic mechanisms between rickettsial vasculitis and PRES supports a possible causal link.

Neurological manifestations of rickettsioses are uncommon but may include meningoencephalitis, seizures, meningitis, encephalitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, mental disturbance, myelitis, hallucinations, confusion, facial nerve palsy, cerebral infarction, and visual disturbances [6]. PRES has only been reported once in relation to spotted fever group rickettsiosis, but the species of Rickettsia was unknown, and treatment included doxycycline [7]. In our patient, R. conorii infection was serologically confirmed, with a fourfold increase in IFA IgM titers.

Doxycycline remains the treatment of choice for rickettsial infections. However, oral administration may not be feasible in patients with impaired consciousness or gastrointestinal dysfunction. In our patient, intravenous ciprofloxacin was administered because of altered mental status, which resulted in rapid clinical improvement. Although fluoroquinolones are not routinely recommended as first-line therapy, early reports demonstrated the successful use of ciprofloxacin in MSF [9]. Experimental studies have shown that ciprofloxacin exerts a deleterious effect on R. conorii-infected endothelial cells by up-regulating toxin–antitoxin modules, providing mechanistic support for its antimicrobial activity [10]. Our patient’s favorable response aligned with these observations, suggesting that fluoroquinolones may serve as effective alternatives when doxycycline is contraindicated or impractical.

This case reinforces the importance of considering rickettsial infections in patients with PRES who present with fever and vasculitis, particularly in endemic areas. Conversely, new-onset seizures, encephalopathy, or cortical blindness in rickettsial fever should prompt urgent neuroimaging to evaluate for PRES. Prompt initiation of effective empiric therapy while awaiting serologic confirmation remains essential, as delay in treatment is associated with increased morbidity.

Conclusion

Rickettsia conorii infection can rarely cause PRES via endothelial injury and systemic vasculitis. This report underscores the need for increased clinical suspicion of rickettsial etiologies in patients with atypical presentations of PRES. Intravenous ciprofloxacin may be a viable therapeutic option when standard doxycycline therapy cannot be administered.
