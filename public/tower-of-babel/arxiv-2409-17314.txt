A Mixed finite element method for the velocity-pseudostress formulation of the Oseen eigenvalue problem Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Felipe Lepe, Gonzalo Rivera, Jesus Vellojin

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce and analyze a mixed formulation for the Oseen eigenvalue problem by introducing the pseudostress tensor as a new unknown, allowing us to eliminate the fluid pressure. The well-posedness of the solution operator is established using a fixed-point argument. For the numerical analysis, we use the tensorial versions of Raviart-Thomas and Brezzi-Douglas-Marini elements to approximate the pseudostress, and piecewise polynomials for the velocity. Convergence and a priori error estimates are derived based on compact operator theory. We present a series of numerical tests in two and three dimensions to confirm the theoretical findings.

Non-symmetric eigenvalue problem, mixed formulations, Oseen equations, convergence, a priori error estimates

Introduction

Mixed formulations are an attractive technique for approximating solutions to partial differential equations, as they inherently allow for the introduction of additional variables with physical significance. A classic reference discussing the advantages of mixed methods is
[
4
]
.

Eigenvalue problems associated with partial differential equations are well-known topic and their applications are documented in
[
3
]
, which presents a comprehensive discussion on the analysis. However, the study of eigenvalue problems is continuously evolving in various contexts, including those arising in fluid mechanics.

Recently, in
[
27
]
, a finite element method for the Oseen eigenvalue problem was introduced, where the classic velocity-pressure formulation is analyzed. This paper presents, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the first effort to numerically analyze this spectral problem using inf-sup stable families of finite elements where the analysis includes a priori and a posteriori error estimates. The article also reports computed eigenvalues and eigenfunctions in different domains and for various convective velocities, allowing for comparison with results obtained by other methods and formulations.

Inspired by the work of
[

]
in the context of the Navier-Stokes equations, we continue our research program, now focusing on the Oseen eigenvalue problem. This variable, introduced in

Through certain algebraic manipulations, the pressure can be eliminated from the formulation, leading to a mixed problem that is expressed solely in terms of the velocity and the pseudostress. The introduction of the pseudostress tensor was initially motivated by its applications in source problems. this idea has been extended for elasticity problems. The introduction of the pseudostress tensor was initially motivated by its applications in source problems. Nevertheless, for eigenvalue problems, the incorporation of this unknown has been also a suitable alternative to approximate the spectrum of some differential operators as is shown on

, where classic mixed finite elements, virtual elements, and discontinuous Galerkin methods have been considered. These formulations have demonstrated accuracy in computing the respective spectra. The work of
[

]
motivates us to continue exploring pseudostress formulations, now for the non-symmetric Oseen problem.

Let us focus on the particularities of the pseudostress formulation for the Oseen eigenvalue problem. For the mathematical analysis of this formulation, particularly the well-posedness of the solution operator, we need to adopt a fixed-point strategy due to the presence of the convective term. This analysis requires certain conditions on the data, such as viscosity and convective velocity, to prove the necessary criteria for the fixed-point argument. Furthermore, the proposed solution operator is compact, leading to a spectral analysis based on the theory of
[
1
]
. Given that the Oseen eigenvalue problem is non-selfadjoint, it is essential to include the adjoint problem in the analysis, incorporating the corresponding adjoint solution operators in both continuous and discrete versions for the spectral analysis. However, one drawback of utilizing the fixed point strategy is that it is not straightforward to obtain a desirable estimate of the dependency on the data for the source problem. This limitation extends naturally to the corresponding eigenfunctions. As a result, when proving convergence in norm, it becomes difficult to establish bounds that reflect the dependence on the source data. To circumvent this issue, an additional assumption is required, namely that the solutions of both the source and eigenvalue problems exhibit continuous dependence on the data of the source problem.

Since the proposed formulation is mixed, we use classic finite element families for this type of formulation, such as the Raviart-Thomas and Brezzi-Douglas-Marini elements, for the discretization. With these families, we prove the well-posedness of the discrete source problem to define the discrete solution operator correctly. With the aid of
[
1
]
, we derive convergence and error estimates for the mixed scheme and achieve the desired spurious-free feature of the method.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive a velocity-pseudostress eigenvalue problem. The well-posedness of the source problem is studied by means of a fixed point argument. Due to the non-symmetric nature of the proposed problem, we also introduce the adjoint model along with its properties. Section
3
is devoted to state the discrete version of the problem. The discrete scheme is approximated by the Raviart-Thomas and Brezzi-Douglas-Marini elements. Some approximation properties for these spaces are presented. We also report the well-posedness of the discrete primal and adjoint formulations, together with their corresponding convergence analysis an error estimates. We conclude with Section
4
, where several numerical experiments are performed to test the accuracy and robustness of the scheme.

2
The model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\in\{2,3\}
, be an open and bounded polygonal/polyhedral domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The problem of interest in this study is based on the Oseen equations, whose main characteristic is to model incompressible viscous fluids at small Reynolds number. The corresponding strong form of the equations are given as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p&=&\lambda\boldsymbol{u},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}&=&\boldsymbol{0},&\text{in}\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement,
p
is the pressure and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a given vector field, representing a
steady flow velocity
such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is divergence free and
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity. Over this last parameter, we assume that there exists two positive numbers
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
such that
\nu^{-}<\nu<\nu^{+}
.

The standard assumptions on the coefficients are the following (see
[
20
]
)

•

\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\sim 1
if
\nu\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}
,

•

\nu\sim 1
if
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}<\nu
,

where the first point is the case more close to the real applications.

Now we introduce the pseudostress tensor

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})-p\mathbb{I}\text{ in }\Omega.

(2)

It is easy to check that
p=-\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits{(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})}\right)
in
\Omega
, which, along with (
2
), leads us to the equation

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\texttt{d}}=\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\texttt{d}}\text{ in }\Omega.

(3)

Note that given that
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
in
\Omega
, then it can be shown that
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})=(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}.
in
\Omega

On the other hand, from the first equation of (
1
) together with (
2
) we have
-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\lambda\boldsymbol{u}
in
\Omega
. Finally, from (
1
) we observe that the condition
(p,1)_{0,\Omega}=0
, is equivalent to
(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),1)_{0,\Omega}=-(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits{(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})},1)_{0,\Omega}
.

Therefore, in accordance with the above, problem (
1
) can be rewritten as follows

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\texttt{d}}&=&\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\texttt{d}},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma})&=&\lambda\boldsymbol{u},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),1)_{0,\Omega}&=&-(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits{(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})},1)_{0,\Omega},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}&=&\boldsymbol{0},&\text{in}\,\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(4)

Let us define the spaces
\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
.
We also define the norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{v})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}:=\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}
. A variational formulation for (
4
) consists of: Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\neq(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
such that
(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),1)_{0,\Omega}=-(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits{(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})},1)_{0,\Omega}
and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{u})+c(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\tau})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H},\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v})&=&-\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q},\end{array}\right.

(5)

where where the bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
,
b(\cdot,\cdot)
and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, are defined by

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\nu}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\texttt{d}}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\texttt{d}},\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H},

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\tau})\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\qquad

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{H}\times\boldsymbol{Q},

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\nu}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\texttt{d}}:\boldsymbol{\tau},\qquad

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\boldsymbol{Q}\times\mathbb{H}.

Now for our analysis it is necessary the following decomposition
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbb{I}
, where

\mathbb{H}_{0}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}\;\>\;\ \int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\tau})=0\right\}.

More precisely, each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}
can be decomposed uniquely as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{0}+c\mathbb{I},\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad c:=\dfrac{1}{n|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, if we define the tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})\mathbb{I}
, it is clear that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}
and it is not difficult to prove that Problem (
5
) can be equivalently rewritten in terms of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}
as: Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\neq(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{u})+c(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\tau})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0},\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})&=&-\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.\end{array}\right.

Consequently, to simplify the analysis, instead of addressing Problem (
5
) , we propose to analyze and discretize the following problem: Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\neq(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{u})+c(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\tau})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0},\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v})&=&-\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(6)

Remark 1
.

It is easy to check that
(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{H}_{0}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
is a solution of Problem (
5
) if and only if
(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{H}_{0}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
is a solution of Problem (
6
). Also, note that the post-processing formula for the pressure

now reduces

p=-\dfrac{1}{n}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits{(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})}-\dfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})\right).

To continue with our analysis, we invoke the following result (see
[
4
, Chapter 9, Proposition 9.1.1]
)

Lemma 2
.

There exists
c_{1}>0
, such that

c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\texttt{d}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}.

(7)

Let us define the kernel
\mathcal{K}
of

as follows
\mathcal{K}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\,:\,b(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q})=0\,\,\,\,\forall\boldsymbol{q}\in\boldsymbol{Q}\}
.
Thanks to the above lemma we have the coercivity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
in
\mathcal{K}
. i.e

\dfrac{c_{1}}{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}\leq a(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{K}.

(8)

It is easy to check that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following inf-sup condition

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{q})}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}}\geq\gamma\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{q}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.

(9)

Hence, we introduce the so-called solution operator, which we denote by
\boldsymbol{T}
and is defined as follows

\boldsymbol{T}:\boldsymbol{Q}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{Q},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},

(10)

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\mathbb{H}_{0}\times\boldsymbol{Q}
is the solution of the following source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})+c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\tau})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v})&=&-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(11)

Now our aim is to determine that
\boldsymbol{T}
is well defined. To do this task, we employ a fixed point strategy.

2.1
The fixed point argument

In this section we will define the solution operator associated to our spectral problem and analyze, through the fixed point theory, the good approach of the solution operator. Let us consider the following source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{\tau})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})&=&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\texttt{d}}:\boldsymbol{\tau}&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v})&=&-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.\end{array}\right.

(12)

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\mathcal{F}
be two functionals defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\texttt{d}}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\texttt{d}}\,\,\,\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v}):=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\,\,\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{Q}.

and are such that

\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})|}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}}\leq\frac{1}{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,\frac{|\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v})|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

|a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})|\leq\dfrac{1}{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\,|b(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v})|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Then, thanks to the above estimates, to the coercivity of

in

(13)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\left(1+\dfrac{\nu}{c_{1}}\|a\|\right)\|\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{\mathbb{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)^{\prime}}+\dfrac{\|a\|}{\gamma^{2}}\left(1+\dfrac{\nu}{c_{1}}\|a\|\right)\|\mathcal{F}\|_{0,\Omega},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{Q}\times\mathbb{H}_{0},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}):=(\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}),\mathcal{J}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}))=(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

Observe that fixing
R_{0}>0
and let us consider

Now, we define

,
C_{1}:=\dfrac{1}{2\nu\gamma}
and

.
After elementary algebraic manipulations, we can assert that the right-hand side above is smaller or

R_{0}

Lemma 3
.

There exists a positive constant
L
, depending only on data, such that

\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq L\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{0,\Omega},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in M_{R_{0}}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in M_{R_{0}}
, such that
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}):=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})
,

With the aim of proving convergence and error estimates of the non-selfadjoint problem (

The strong form of the dual equations written in terms of the velocity and pressure are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla p^{*}&=&\lambda^{*}\boldsymbol{u}^{*},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\

Hence

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{2},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle C_{J}[B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))-B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{2},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{J}[-c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega}+c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{J}c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\leq C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{2}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{2}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\left(1-C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\right)\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{2}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{2}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0

Finally, if
\displaystyle C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}<1
, we conclude the proof.

As in the continuous case, it is easy to check that
(\lambda_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))
solves problem (
27
) if and only if
(\kappa_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
.

3.2
The discrete dual problem

The dual discrete eigenvalue problem reads as follows: Find
\lambda^{*}_{h}\in\mathbb{C}
and the pair
(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})\neq(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})\in\mathbb{H}_{0,h}\times\boldsymbol{Q}_{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})-c(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{0,h},\\
b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=&-\lambda_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

(37)

and let us introduce the discrete version of (
24
) which we define by

\boldsymbol{T}^{*}_{h}:\boldsymbol{Q}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{Q}_{h},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*},

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*},\widehat{p}_{h}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
is the solution of the following adjoint discrete source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{*})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*})-c(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*})&=&0&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{0,h},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=&-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{f})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

(38)

As in the continuous case, to prove that
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}_{h}
is well defined, we can follow the same arguments used for the goodness of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
. So, we skip the details.

3.3
Convergence for the solution operators

Now, due to the compactness of
\boldsymbol{T}
, we are able to prove that
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
converge to
\boldsymbol{T}
as
h
goes to zero in norm and under some assumptions on the data. This is contained in the following result.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
be such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}
, solutions of problems (
12
) and (
29
) respectively, assume that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1+s}(\Omega)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)
with
s>0
and assume that the data is sufficiently small and that (
19
) holds. Then, if
C_{J}\dfrac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}}{\nu}<1
, there exists a positive constant
\widehat{C}_{\nu,\beta}
, independent of
h
, such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\beta}h^{s}\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}\right)\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\beta}h^{s}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Proof.

Let
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})
and
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})
be the solutions
of the continuous and discrete source problems, respectively, and
let
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
be such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{J}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

where

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}
are the best approximations of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
, respectively.
Then, from triangle inequality we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

On the other hand

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq C_{J}B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))\\
+C_{J}B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))\\
=B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))+C_{J}[-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})-[-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})]]\\
=C_{J}B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))+C_{J}c(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\\
\leq C_{J}B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))+C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
\leq C_{J}B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))+C_{J}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega})\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

From this inequality and proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
8
we have

\left(\dfrac{1}{C_{J}}-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\right)(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\leq B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))\\
+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Let us focus our attention on the right-hand side of the inequality above. According to the definition of
B(\cdot,\cdot)
(cf. (
32
)) we have

B((\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}),(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}))+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
=a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})+b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
\leq\frac{1}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
\leq\underbrace{\max\left\{\frac{1}{\nu},\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}+1\right\}}_{C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega})\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\\
+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})+\frac{C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\\
\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})+\frac{\max\{C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}},1\}}{2}(\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\\
+\frac{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}}{2}\\
\leq\left(C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\frac{\max\{C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}},1\}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

In summary, we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{I},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{I})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

where
\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\max\left\{1,\sqrt{\dfrac{\left(C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\frac{\max\{C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}},1\}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{1}{C_{J}}-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\right)}}\right\}
.
Now using the approximation properties presented in
[
24
, Section 3.2]
we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}h^{s}\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\|_{s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}\right).

We conclude the proof invoking (
19
).

For the adjoint counterparts of
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
, namely
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*}
, we can prove convergence of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*}
to
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
as
h
goes to zero. Also, this convergence result depends on some suitable choice on the data of the problem. Since the proof is essentially identical to Lemma
9
we skip the steps of the proof.

Lemma 10
.

Assume that (
26
) holds. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{h}^{*},(\boldsymbol{T}^{*}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*})\boldsymbol{f})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\beta}^{*}h^{s^{*}}\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}\|_{s^{*},\Omega}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\|_{1+s^{*},\Omega}\right)\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\beta}^{*}h^{s^{*}}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

With all these results at hand, we are in position to apply the theory of
[
21
, Chapter IV]
and
[
3
, Theorem 9.1]
to conclude that our numerical methods does not introduce spurious eigenvalues.

Theorem 11
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set containing
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})
. Then, there exists
h_{0}>0
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\subset V
for all
h<h_{0}
.

3.4
Error estimates

Now our task is to prove a priori error estimates for the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues. With this goal in mind, let us recall the definition of spectral projectors. Let
\mu
be a nonzero isolated eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}
with algebraic multiplicity
m
and let
\Gamma

be a disk of the complex plane centered in
\mu
, such that
\mu
is the only eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}
lying in
\Gamma
and
\partial\Gamma\cap\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})=\emptyset
. The spectral projections of
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{*}
, associated to
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
are defined, respectively, in the following way

a)

The spectral projector of
\boldsymbol{T}
associated to
\mu
is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T})^{-1}\,dz;

b)

The spectral projector of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
associated to
\bar{\mu}
is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{*}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}^{*})^{-1}\,dz,

where
\boldsymbol{I}
represents the identity operator and
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{*}
are the projections onto the generalized eigenspaces
R(\boldsymbol{E})
and
R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})
, respectively.

The convergence in norm stated in Lemma
9
gives as consequence the existence of
m
eigenvalues lying in
\Gamma
, which we denote by
\mu_{h}^{(1)},\ldots,\mu_{h}^{(m)}
, repeated according their respective multiplicities, that converge to
\mu
as
h
goes to zero. This motivates the definition of the following discrete spectral projection

\boldsymbol{E}_{h}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})^{-1}\,dz.

This operator is precisely a projection onto the discrete invariant subspace
R(\boldsymbol{E}_{h})
of
\boldsymbol{T}
, spanned by the generalized eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
corresponding to

\mu_{h}^{(1)},\ldots,\mu_{h}^{(m)}
.
Another necessary ingredient for the error analysis is the
gap

\widehat{\delta}(\cdot,\cdot)
between two closed
subspaces
\mathfrak{X}
and
\mathfrak{Y}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
, which is defined by

\widehat{\delta}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}):=\max\big\{\delta(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}),\delta(\mathfrak{Y},\mathfrak{X})\big\},\text{ where }\delta(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}):=\sup_{\underset{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{0,\Omega}=1}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathfrak{X}}}\left(\inf_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathfrak{Y}}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\|_{0,\Omega}\right).

Now we present the main result of this section.

Theorem 12
.

For
k\geq 0
and assume that
\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
, and
\widetilde{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are sufficiently small as in Lemma
9
and Lemma
10
. the following estimates hold

\widehat{\delta}(R(\boldsymbol{E}),R(\boldsymbol{E}_{h}))\leq\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}h^{\min\{k+1,s\}}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mu-\mu_{h}|\leq\widetilde{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}h^{\min\{2(k+1),s+s^{*}\}},

where

\widetilde{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\widehat{C}_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\left(\dfrac{1+\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}+3\right).

Proof.

The proof of the gap between the eigenspaces is a direct consequence of the convergence in norm between
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
as
h
goes to zero.
We focus on the double order of convergence for the eigenvalues. Let
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be such that
\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=\mu\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,m
. A dual basis for
R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})
is
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{m}
. This basis satisfies
A((\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{*}))=\delta_{k.l},

where
\delta_{k.l}
represents the Kronecker delta and
A((\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{*}))=a(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})+b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k})+c(\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})+b(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{*}))
.
On the other hand, the following identity holds

|\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{h}|\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|\langle(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\rangle|+\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})|_{R(\boldsymbol{E})}\|_{1,\Omega}\|(\boldsymbol{T}^{*}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*})|_{R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})}\|_{1,\Omega},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the corresponding duality pairing. For the first term on the right-hand side we note that

\langle(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\rangle=A((\boldsymbol{\sigma},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}))\\
=A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}))+A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}))\\
=A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}))\\
+A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*})))+A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}))\\
=A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*})),

where for the last equality, we have used the Galerkin orthogonality. Then

\langle(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\rangle=A((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k});(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}))\\
=a(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*})+b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*},(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k})+b(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*})\\
+c((\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*})\\
\leq\dfrac{1}{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}+\dfrac{1}{\nu}\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}.

Then, Theorem
12
follows from the above estimates and the approximation properties of discrete spaces, in addition to Lemmas
9
and
10
.

4
Numerical experiments

This section is devoted to perform several numerical experiments to analyze the behavior of the scheme in different geometries and physical configurations. We resort to the DOLFINx software
[
2
,
31
]
for the implementations of the codes, where the SLEPc eigensolver
[
17
]
and the linear solver MUMPS are used to solve the resulting general eigenvalue problem. The meshes are constructed with the help of GMSH
[
15
]
and the DOLFINx built-in generic meshes.

The rates of convergence for each eigenvalue are computed by using a least-square fitting. More precisely, if
\lambda_{h}
is a discrete complex eigenvalue, then the rate of convergence
\alpha
is calculated by extrapolation and the least square fitting

\lambda_{h}\approx\lambda_{\text{extr}}+Ch^{\alpha},

where
\lambda_{\text{extr}}
is the extrapolated eigenvalue given by the fitting.

In what follows, we denote the mesh resolution by
N
, which is connected to the mesh-size
h
through the relation
h\sim N^{-1}
. We also denote the number of degrees of freedom by
dof
. The relation between
dof
and the mesh size is given by
h\sim\texttt{dof}^{-1/n}
, with
n\in\{2,3\}
.

According to the assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we will assume
\nu=1/2
in all the experiments, unless stated otherwise. Also, for each convective velocity, we assume that it is normalized and, for the sake of uniformity, is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}/\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}
.

4.1
Results in 2D geometries

In this section we study the behavior of the scheme when choosing different geometries and convective velocities. The idea is to observe the convergence and the complex spectrum when we have different configurations of the model. To do this task, the values for the solenoidal convective velocity are considered among the following

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(x,y)=(1,0)^{\texttt{t}},\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}(x,y)=(\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),-\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y))^{\texttt{t}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{3}(x,y)=(y,-x)^{\texttt{t}},\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{4}(x,y)=\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y},-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right)^{\texttt{t}},

with
\phi(x,y)=1000(1-x^{2})^{2}(1-y^{2})^{2}
.
The geometries under study are the square and lshaped domain. Each case is described below.

4.1.1
Square domain

Let us start the experiments by considering the square domain
\Omega:=(0,1)^{2}
, which serves as a benchmark to compare the computed eigenvalues with existing references.

Table
1
describe the convergence behavior of the scheme with
\mathbb{RT}_{k}
and

4.1.2
L-shaped domain

We now focus on the L-shaped domain, defined by
\Omega:=(-1,1)^{1}\backslash(-1,0)^{2}
. Here, we have a singularity at
(0,0)
which yield to a suboptimal rate of convergence for the eigenvalues. We study the convergence and the spectrum behavior when changing the convective velocity.

Convergence results for the simplest case
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
are presented in Table
3
. It notes that, because of the singularity, the first eigenfunction is singular, hence we expect at least
\mathcal{O}(h^{2\min\{r,k+1\}})
, with
2r\geq 1.08
. The observed experimental rate for
\lambda_{h,1}
is approximately
\mathcal{O}(h^{1.7})
. The rest of eigenvalues show an optimal rate of convergence. On Table
4
we have the first fourth computed eigenvalues for different values of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
using Raviart-Thomas elements. The rates of convergence are similar to that of Table
3
. We complement the test by plotting the discrete velocity and pressure (Figure
3
).
A clear right-shift is observed, with high gradients for the pressure and pseudostress near the re-entrant corner.

We now pass to the study of the complex spectrum. The distribution of the first 50 real and complex eigenvalues are plotted in Figure
4
. We note that for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
there are only two complex conjugate eigenvalues. This is similar to the results of the square domain. In contrast, the complex spectrum for the rest of convective velocities is considerable lower. For instance, only two complex eigenvalues for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
were computed, and six for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{4}
. Because of the non-symmetric nature of the problem, the uniformity of the mesh and number of elements, we could expect different complex distribution on other cases.

4.3
Robustness with respect to
\nu

In this experiment we test the robustness of the scheme when we consider small values of
\nu
. According to
[
20
]
, small values for
\nu
are interesting for time depending problems. However, we aim to test the theoretical results in the fixed-point theory presented in Section
2.1
and
3.1
. We consider the square domain from Section
4.1.1
and the cube domain from Section
4.2.1
. The convective velocity is given by
\boldsymbol{\beta}(x,y)=\nu(1,0)^{\texttt{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}(x,y)=\nu(1,0,0)^{\texttt{t}}
for the square and the cube domain, respectively. Note that this yield to consider two case scenarios for this solenoidal vector: normalizing the field so that
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}=1
, as stated at the beginning of the numerical section, and
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}=\nu
. Finally, the values for
\nu
are taken to be
\nu=1/2^{j}
, for
j=0,...,15.
. We present here the results with
\mathbb{BDM}_{1}
. Similar results were achieved by considering
\mathbb{RT}
.

In Figure
7
we present the results for the normalized solenoidal field. Although we observe a constant decrease in the computed eigenvalues, we note that the scheme becomes unstable after the first two values for
\nu
, namely, after
\nu=1/2
. We observe that the small data requirement can be broken if the fraction
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}/\nu
grows without control. By normalizing the vector, we have that the estimator depends on
1/\nu
, which leads us to the observed instability for small values of
\nu
, where the solver even yields negative eigenvalues.

On the other hand, in Figure
8
we have the opposite case. Note that due to the constant scaling
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}/\nu=1
, the error rates remain constant and optimal, independent of the viscosity. It is worth noting that both scenarios for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
produce completely different spectrum.

4.4
A 3D benchmark

We conclude the numerical section with a benchmark designed to analyze the computational cost of the proposed method. Specifically, we focus on the cube domain from Section
4.2.1
with the convective velocity set as
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(1,0,0)^{\texttt{t}}
. The performance metrics we measure include assembly time, solve time, non-zero percentage, and floating point operations (flops). Assembly time refers to the time taken to assemble the PETSc matrix representing the left-hand side of the linear system. Solve time is measured as the time SLEPc takes to compute the four lowest eigenvalues. Non-zero percentage is calculated based on the matrix size and its sparsity pattern. Flops represent an estimate of the total double precision operations (DPOPS) executed by the MUMPS linear solver.

We compare the computations with respect to
[
27
]
using Taylor-Hood (TH) and mini element (MINI). For the present study, RT (resp. BDM) denotes the method using Raviart-Thomas (resp. Brezzi-Douglas-Marini) elements.

The results are reported in Figure
9
. We observe that the fastest assembly and solve time are measured when using RT elements, while the most expensive, up to a certain number of degrees of freedom, is the scheme that uses the TH pair. We note that solve time is almost on par with the MINI element. The non-zero percentage is smaller for the proposed scheme due to an overall higher
dof
. Finally, we note that BDM and TH are the schemes that take the most DPOPS per mesh size, while RT and MINI elements are the most efficient ones.
