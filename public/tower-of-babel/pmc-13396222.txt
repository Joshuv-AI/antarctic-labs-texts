Nurse practitioners’ competencies for occupational health services in primary care units, Thailand: a qualitative study
Authors: Napamon Pumsopa, Ann Jirapongsuwan, Surintorn Kalampakorn, Sukhontha Siri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis exploratory study examined nurses’ competencies in delivering occupational health services in Primary Care Units. A qualitative design was used, incorporating in-depth interviews and focus group discussions for data collection. Ten in-depth interviews were conducted with administrators from provincial government organizations. Three focus groups were held with 10 participants each: nurse practitioners, nurse practitioner colleagues, and agricultural workers. Data were analyzed thematically using NVivo 14, guided by the Thai Primary Care Unit Nurses’ competency framework. The analysis identified three major themes and 16 subthemes, aligned with four domains: interpersonal relationships, care management, integrated healthcare services, and professional accountability. Findings emphasize essential competencies required of Primary Care Units’ nurse practitioners to deliver effective services, particularly for agricultural workers exposed to pesticides. These competencies provide a basis for developing educational curricula and training programs to strengthen occupational health competencies among nurse practitioners. Public health administrators can use these findings to inform strategic improvement plans and better prepare community nurses to deliver comprehensive occupational health services in primary care settings. Further research should focus on designing, implementing, and evaluating interventions that enhance community nurses’ competencies and improve occupational health outcomes in Primary Care Units.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54378-3.Keywords: Nurses’ competencies, Nurse practitioners, Occupational health services, Primary healthcare, Qualitative studySubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Medical research

Introduction

Healthcare for agricultural workers exposed to pesticides should comply with standards for occupational health (OH) and environmental medicine services. Primary care units (PCUs) or health-promoting hospitals are subdistrict- or village-level health service units1. In addition to providing OH services in PCUs, both proactive and facility-based activities are implemented1. OH services delivered in PCUs for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers are considered in-house services and represent a core responsibility of nurses in PCUs1.

In Thailand, policy prioritizes primary care and requires at least one nurse in each PCU to complete a primary medical care course; however, this training does not included OH service knowledge. Evidence indicates that OH services in PCUs often fail to meet established OH and environmental medicine standards2. Consequently, many nurses encounter challenges in OH practice, leading to reduced confidence, lower service quality, and suboptimal health outcomes. Effective delivery requires nurse practitioners (NPs) to possess competencies in OH and environmental health nursing, including practice, knowledge, skills, and legal and ethical framework3. NPs in PCUs are recognized health professionals whose service delivery is skill-based3. These skills can be strengthened through practical application of acquired knowledge4. Furthermore, professional attributes are essential for translating knowledge into skills effective OH parctice5.

These attributes align with the four core competencies of PCU nurses in Thailand who care for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers: interpersonal relationships, care management, integrated healthcare services, and professional accountability6. Each competency component corresponds to OH and environmental health nursing competencies and the Iceberg Competency Model. Knowledge and skills form the visible, measurable components developed through training, whereas underlying attributes shape long-term professional behavior7. Thus, understanding nurses’ competencies is critical for fulfilling roles, clarifying responsibilities, and ensuring evidence-based care.

Strengthening competencies—professional attributes, knowledge, and skills—is essential to improve OH service delivery for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers and support nurses’ roles in PCUs8. Despite their importance, few studies have examined PCU nurses’ competencies and those of other stakeholders involved in OH service delivery in Thailand. Although OH service delivery is a key responsibility of PCU NPs, many lack specialized training and confidence, resulting in services that often fall below standards9. Although the four core competencies are established, their application in OH service delivery remains unclear6. This gap highlights the need to investigate nurses’ competencies in delivering OH services for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers to generate empirical evidence. Such evidence can guide role clarification, competency development, and capacity-building initiatives for NPs in PCUs, not only in Thailand but also in developing countries, particularly in Southeast Asia and other tropical regions with high agricultural dependence and pesticide use. Ultimately, strengthening these competencies will improve the efficiency of OH services in PCUs and enhance agricultural workers’ health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Aim

This study analyzed NPs’ competencies in delivering in-house OH services in PCUs for agricultural workers exposed to pesticides.

Design

A qualitative descriptive design was applied, using semi-structured individual interviews and focus group discussions. Semi-structured interviews enabled in-depth data collection with flexibility for probing and clarification10. This approach facilitated exploration of implementation, context, and policy directions of in-house OH services in PCUs. Qualitative methods are suitable for examining NPs’ competencies6,8, including professional attributes, knowledge, skills, and contextual influences.

Fogus group discussions were conducted online and on-site. Online Fogus groups with NPs and their colleagues examined competencies in OH service delivery, offering a practical alternative for geographically dispersed participants11. On-site FGDs with agricultural workers facilitated open discussion of experiences, perceptions, and expectations. The study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist to ensure methodological rigor and transparency12.

Setting

In Thailand, the Office of Disease Control Regions 1–12 supports PCU networks in delivering OH services, prioritizing agricultural workers13. Services for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers follow OH and environmental medicine standards1. NPs are the primary providers13, and their roles align with four core competencies: interpersonal relationships, care management, integrated healthcare services, and professional accountability6,14. The study was conducted in Chiang Rai Province and included NPs, colleagues, agricultural workers, and administrators from provincial and district public health offices, district agricultural offices, and local government organizations.

Ethical issues

Focus group research poses risks of participant identification due to shared discussions. Although data confidentiality was ensured, complete confidentiality among participants could not be fully guaranteed. Online participants were instructed to maintain confidentiality, considering risks such as unauthorized recording. On-site focus group discussions and interviews were conducted in private settings.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Public Health, Mahidol University (MUPH 2022-084), and all procedures followed relevant guidelines. Participants received detailed study information and provided informed consent. Digital consent was obtained for online focus group discussions, and written consent for on-site sessions. Participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any time without any negative consequences.

Data collection was conducted by a researcher trained in qualitative methods. Transcripts were pseudonymized, and identifiers were removed. Quotations were coded (e.g., “NP 2”) to ensure anonymity. All data were securely stored by the principal investigator.

Participants selection

Criterion-based purposive sampling was used to recruit participants with relevant roles and direct experience in OH services. Participants were selected from small (< 3,000 population), medium (3,000–8,000), and large (> 8,000) PCUs in Chiang Rai Province to capture variation in service capacity15.

Focus group discussions included three groups: (1) NPs, (2) NP colleagues (e.g., PCU directors and public health professionals), and (3) agricultural workers. Each group included 10 participants representing 10 PCUs: three from small, five from medium, and two from large PCUs.

Inclusion criteria for NPs and colleagues were current PCU employment, ≥ 1 year of experience, and sufficient digital literacy. Agricultural workers were required to have pesticide exposure, recent OH service experience within 1 year, and willingness to participate. Part-time staff and those unable to complete the study were excluded.

Ten semi-structured interviews were conducted with administrators: two from provincial public health offices, four from district public health offices, two from district agricultural offices, and two from local government organizations. This ensured diverse policy and implementation perspectives.

Data collection

Data were collected from July to September 2022 to examine current OH service delivery and identify competencies required for NPs in PCUs serving pesticide-exposed agricultural workers. Three FGDs and 10 semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted. The focus group discussions were held in two formats: online via Zoom with NPs and their colleagues to enable participation across dispersed locations, and on-site with agricultural workers to enhance accessibility, comfort, and direct interaction. Each session lasted approximately 120 min.

Semi-structured interviews with administrators captured policy-level perspectives, including implementation feasibility and strategic direction. During focus group discussions, participant interaction was encouraged using a nondirective moderation style to elicit diverse experiences and perspectives on pesticide exposure and healthcare delivery. The principal investigator moderated all sessions, while an assistant managed audio–video recording and detailed field notes to support transcription and contextual analysis.

Interview and focus group discussions guides were developed from relevant literature and policy on in-house OH services in PCUs in Chiang Rai Province. Content validity was confirmed by three experts in OH and primary care, with item–objective congruence indices ranging from 0.66 to 1.00. A pilot interview assessed clarity and flow, leading to minor revisions.

The interviewer, trained in qualitative methods and with a background in OH, conducted all sessions. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as no emergence of new themes using a 16-item saturation checklist. Analysis followed three stages: open coding, axial coding, and thematic synthesis. To ensure rigor, three researchers independently coded the dataset using a shared codebook and achieved consistent agreement16. All data were securely stored and maintained confidentially by the principal investigator.

Data analysis

All interviews and focus group discussions were transcribed verbatim and analyzed in the original language to preserve meaning and context. Selected quotations were translated into English for reporting consistency. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis, including: (1) open coding to generate initial codes through repeated transcript review, (2) axial coding to develop categories, subcategories, and code definitions, and (3) thematic synthesis to identify and refine themes and subthemes.

To enhance rigor, three researchers independently reviewed and discussed initial codes during the open and axial phases. Subthemes were grouped into overarching themes through iterative team discussions until consensus was achieved, ensuring reproducibility and strengthening credibility, dependability, and overall trustworthiness.

Code and data accessibility were supported through an open repository. Reporting followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist12. NVivo 14 was used for data management, systematic coding, text labeling, and development of thematic linkages.

Results

Demographic data

A total of 40 participants were included: 30 in FGDs and 10 key informants in semi-structured in-depth interviews. Among FGD participants, 10 were NPs, 10 were NP colleagues, and 10 were agricultural workers.

All NPs were female (n = 10), aged 25–54 years (x̅ = 37.50, S.D. = 10.44), with 3–20 years of PCU experience (x̅ = 10.30, S.D. = 5.44). Most NP colleagues were female (n = 8), with two males; ages ranged from 25 to 55 years (x̅ = 38.80, SD = 11.07), and PCU experience ranged from 2 to 32 years (x̅ = 15.70, S.D. = 11.05). The agricultural worker group included eight males and two females, aged 29–66 years (x̅ = 49.20, S.D. = 12.94), with 5–45 years of work experience (x̅ = 21.00, S.D. = 12.33).

Ten in-depth interviews were conducted with administrators: two from the provincial public health office, four from district public health offices, two from district agricultural offices, and two from local government organizations in Chiang Rai Province. This group included six males (60%) and four females (40%), aged 31–59 years (x̅ = 45.80, S.D. = 8.67), with 7–37 years of experience (x̅ = 22.60, S.D. = 9.72) (Table 1).

Theme A. Professional attributes

Competencies in professional attributes among NPs delivering OH services for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers in PCUs were consistently identified across stakeholders, including NPs, their colleagues, district public health officers, subdistrict administrative officers, and agricultural workers. These competencies comprise three components: personal characteristics, professional ethics, and interaction features.

First, personal characteristics include effective communication, service orientation, and the ability to provide appropriate advice to OH service recipients (Table 2: A, A1, A1.1–A1.3).

“Nurses must have the competence to communicate with the community” (District Public Health Officer 3).

“We treat people who have symptoms from exposure and sometimes advise agricultural workers on pesticide exposure prevention” (NP 2).

Second, professional ethics involve respecting patient rights and adhering to standard care protocols (Table 2: A, A2, A2.1–A2.2).

“If they refuse treatment, we cannot force them” (NP 4).

“We diagnose disease and provide treatment according to the criteria standards for basic disease diagnosis and treatment” (NP 5).

“Nurses already have standardized examinations and treatment” (NP Colleague 2).

“Nurses must respect the rights of the patient, the needs of the patient, and respect the patient in every way” (Subdistrict Administrative Officer 1).

Third, interaction features rate to service delivery approaches for pesticide-exposed groups. Services are often integrated into routine care, which may limit engagement with the target population. Community-based activities typically involve coordination with community leaders and village health volunteers (Table 2: A, A3, A3.1–A3.3).

“Nurses communicate with community leaders, and village health volunteers come in to help communicate with nurse practitioners” (NP 1).

“Providing services to agricultural groups exposed to pesticides is a routine service, causing no attention or action to this group” (NP 3).

“Going into the community, coordinating with community leaders, we will work together. The nurses can do a good job” (NP Colleague 2).

“Most of the nurses focus on treating symptoms according to standards, but if the symptoms are severe, they will be referred” (District Public Health Officer 3).

“Nurses must be able to coordinate with others, whether in various departments, colleagues, community leaders, and village health volunteers” (District Public Health Officer 2).

Theme B. Knowledge

Knowledge competencies of NPs delivering in-house OH services for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers in Thai PCUs encompassed seven domains:

First, fundamentals of pesticides include understanding occupational hazards and health effects of exposure (Table 2: B, B1, B1.1).

“I would like to know about the fundamentals of pesticides. If a patient is exposed to this pesticide, what should I do?” (NP 6).

“There should be increased knowledge of nurse practitioners on various types of pesticides. For example, if agricultural workers use this pesticide, what effect does it have on their health? How to protect yourself? I think it will increase the confidence of nurse practitioners in providing services” (District Public Health Officer 1).

Second, health promotion and prevention cover OH principles such as health surveillance, risk assessment, safe pesticide use, and personal protective equipment (Table 2: B, B2, B2.1–B2.2).

“We did not communicate the risks at all because we did not have deep knowledge in this area” (NP 3).

“I want updated or in-depth knowledge on the use of pesticides and all protective equipment” (NP 5).

“In the case of providing proactive occupational health services, health promotion should also focus on the development of health promotion” (NP 4).

“Risk assessment is knowledge that nurses must learn because they must assess risks, investigate diseases to control health problems from exposure to pesticides, and learn about new laws related to them” (District Public Health Officer 2).

Third, primary medical care of work-related diseases includes OH knowledge, health effects of pesticide exposure, screening, first aid, diagnosis, treatment, referral, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and patient care (Table 2: B, B3, B3.1–B3.5).

“Patients come to us, and we do a preliminary screening for all symptoms and give first aid” (NP 8).

“In-house occupational health services focus on treatment from exposure to pesticides.” (NP 8).

“Nurses must have knowledge and be able to refer knowledge to build confidence in providing services to clients” (NP 1).

“I would like to take additional short courses, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and caring for patients exposed to pesticide training. The Subdistrict Health Promotion Hospital does not have this course, and many people in our area are exposed to pesticides” (NP 1).

“An agricultural worker got chronic wounds on his foot after walking through an area where herbicide was sprayed” (NP 2).

“Most of them got rashes after exposure to pesticides” (NP 3).

“One patient got dizzy after spraying insecticide” (NP 7).

“I would like to increase knowledge about work-related diseases by exposure to pesticides and treatment guidelines” (NP 2).

“Nurses are the ones involved in treatment, referral, diagnosis, prescribing medicine, and can refer” (NP Colleague 7).

“Nurses at the Health Promoting Hospital must develop occupational health knowledge” (Provincial Public Health Office Administrator 2).

“There may be training in occupational health. If a patient has abnormal symptoms caused by exposure to pesticides and comes to the Subdistrict Health Promotion Hospital, what should they do?” (District Public Health Officer 3).

Fourth, laws and regulations involve knowledge of current policies governing pesticide use and occupational safety (Table 2: B, B4, B4.1–B4.2).

“Nurses must learn about new laws related to them” (District Public Health Officer 2).

“Government policy must be serious about the use of pesticides, passing laws to make it clear how much to use and which types of agriculture must use it” (Provincial Public Health Office Administrator 2).

Fifth, integration of OH into practice requires coordination with agencies, including Subdistrict Administrative Organizations, the National Health Insurance Fund, public health officers, and community leaders (Table 2: B, B5, B5.1).

“If we are interested in implementing a project on the prevention of pesticide hazards, the Subdistrict Administrative Organization is responsible for supporting the budget or funding sources” (NPs’ colleague 4).

“The National Health Insurance Fund can help drive policy by supporting budgets for implementing various projects in each subdistrict” (District Public Health Officer 3).

“In going into the community, nurse practitioners and public health officers go together all the time” (Subdistrict Administrative Organization Officer 3).

“Occupational health operations, we work together with village health volunteers to communicate with the population in the community” (NP 2).

“If we want to communicate with the community about the issue of pesticides. We can ask for cooperation from each village leader, and then the village leaders communicate with representatives of each village to publicize the news” (NP 6).

Sixth, recording work-related disease data addresses gaps in documenting pesticide-related illnesses, improper correcting improper International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) coding, and limited funding for prevention programs (Table 2: B, B6, B6.1).

“We do not yet record statistics on whether the disease the patient has is caused by pesticides or not” (NP 2).

“For patients who received in-house services at the Subdistrict Health Promoting Hospital, we have not yet collected in-depth calculations on whether pesticides cause skin problems” (NP Colleague 5).

“The nurse imports data according to ICD10 codes to diagnose agricultural workers exposed to pesticides from work as a general disease, not an occupational disease. As a result, the statistics of diseases caused by pesticide exposure do not reflect the actual situation. Therefore, only a small budget is received from consideration of the annual budget for preventing occupational diseases from pesticide exposure among agricultural workers” (Provincial Public Health Office Administrator 1).

Seventh, research competency in OH highlights the need for research knowledge and skills to support evidence generation (Table 2: B, B7, B7.1).

“Nurses should also receive knowledge about research to conduct research correctly” (NP 3).

“Nurses should also have knowledge and skills regarding research because we have to do it together with university professors” (NP 5).

Theme C. The skills

The skills are the perceived ability and capacity to apply knowledge and execute processes required to deliver in-house OH services. The competencies of NPs in delivering in-house OH services encompass six domains:

First, promotion and prevention of health problems encompass skills in health surveillance, risk assessment, correct pesticide use, and proper use of personal protective equipment (Table 2: C, C1, C1.1–1.3).

“In the case of providing proactive occupational health services, health promotion should also focus on the development of health promotion” (NP 4).

“Risk assessment is knowledge that nurses must learn because they must assess risks, investigate diseases to control health problems from exposure to pesticides, and learn about new laws related to them” (District public health officer 2).

“We did not communicate the risks at all because we did not have deep knowledge in this area” (NP 3).

“I want updated or in-depth knowledge on the use of pesticides and all protective equipment” (NP 5).

Second, primary medical care for work-related diseases encompasses skills in preliminary screening, first aid, CPR, examination, diagnosis, treatment, and referral (Table 2: C, C2, C2.1–2.3).

“Patients come to us, and we do a preliminary screening for all symptoms and give first aid” (NP 8).

“In-house occupational health services focus on treatment from exposure to pesticides.” (NP 8).

“Nurses are the ones who examine and diagnose diseases” (NP Colleague 3).

“Nurses are the ones involved in treatment, referral, diagnosis, prescribing medicine, and can refer” (NPs’ colleague 7).

“If there are other complications, we will refer” (NP 10).

“When a patient has a wound, we take a photo and send it to the doctor for consultation before referral” (NP 8).

“I would like to take additional short courses, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and caring for patients exposed to pesticide training” (NP 1).

“If the nurse practitioner determines that it is a work-related disease, they will be able to enter information correctly” (Provincial Public Health Office Administrator 1).

“There may be training in occupational health. If a patient has abnormal symptoms caused by exposure to pesticides and comes to the Subdistrict Health Promotion Hospital, what should they do?” (District Public Health Officer 3).

Third is OH communication and action on risk communication (Table 2: C, C3, C3.1–3.2).

“At Health Promoting Hospital, we do not communicate risks to agricultural workers because we do not understand them in depth” (NP 2).

“I want to be able to do risk communication, too” (NP 5).

Fourth, collaboration with organizations includes coordination with the District and Provincial Public Health Offices to assess healthcare facilities against basic OH service standards in health-promoting hospitals and community hospitals and engagement with the District Quality of Life Development Committee to promote OH and reduce pesticide use (Table 2: C, C4, C4.1).

“District Public Health Office together with Provincial Public Health Office evaluate occupational health operations in Subdistrict Health Promoting Hospital in accordance with occupational health service standards” (District public health officer 3).

“When a patient has a wound, we take a photo and send it to the doctor in the community hospital for consultation before referral” (NP 8).

“There is a district quality of life development committee. We work on many matters. The environment section covers making the home livable, organic vegetable consumption, occupational health, and reducing the use of pesticides” (District public health officers 1).

Fifth, work-related disease information is recorded and statistics on work-related diseases are addressed according to ICD-10 codes (Table 2: C, C5, and C5.1).

2. Barriers.

NPs and their colleagues were concerned about competency and training: reporting a lack of confidence and specialized training in OH standards.

“I would like to take additional short courses, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and caring for patients exposed to pesticide training” (NP 1).

“I would like to know about the fundamentals of pesticides. If a patient is exposed to this pesticide, what should I do?” (NP 6).

“Nurses must have knowledge and be able to refer knowledge to build confidence in providing services to clients” (NP 1).

Public Health Administrators were concerned about policy and structure: current policy favors specialized OH training for secondary care, not primary care.

“If the nurse practitioner determines that it is a work-related disease, they will be able to enter information correctly” (Provincial public health office administrators 1).

“There may be training in occupational health. If a patient has abnormal symptoms caused by exposure to pesticides and comes to the Subdistrict Health Promotion Hospital, what should they do?” (District public health officer 3).

“There should be increased knowledge of nurse practitioners on various types of pesticides. For example, if agricultural workers use this pesticide, what effect does it have on their health? How to protect yourself? I think it will increase the confidence of nurse practitioners in providing services” (District Public Health Officer 1).

3. Information Management.

NPs and their colleagues were concerned about the task level: they acknowledge gaps in recording whether a disease is caused by pesticides.

“We do not yet record statistics on whether the disease the patient has is caused by pesticides or not” (NP 2).

“For patients who received in-house services at the Subdistrict Health Promoting Hospital, we have not yet collected in-depth calculations on whether pesticides cause skin problems” (NPs’ colleague 5).

Public Health Administrators were concerned about the policy level: improper ICD-10 coding results in statistics that do not reflect reality, resulting in small annual budgets.

“The nurse imports data according to ICD10 codes to diagnose agricultural workers exposed to pesticides from work as a general disease, not an occupational disease. As a result, the statistics of diseases caused by pesticide exposure do not reflect the actual situation. Therefore, only a small budget is received from consideration of the annual budget for preventing occupational diseases from pesticide exposure among agricultural workers” (Provincial Public Health Office Administrator 1).

Discussion

Professional Attributes

Professional attributes, including openness to experience, agreeableness, and extraversion, have been shown to positively impact nursing care principles17, consistent with Erol and Turk, who reported a positive association between nurses’ professional attributes and patient care behaviors18. In the present study, NPs described professional attributes in terms of personal characteristics, professional ethics, and interactional qualities. These perspectives were similarly recognized by their colleagues and administrators, suggesting shared expectations regarding professional conduct across stakeholder groups. Such attributes correspond with the eight core competencies defined by the Thai Nursing and Midwifery Council19 and are consistent with the principles of Basic Occupational Health Services (BOHS)20.

Professional attributes function as the foundational element that facilitates effective in-house OH service delivery for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers in PCUs. Personal integrity and adherence to professional ethics foster trust, accountability, and compliance with clinical and public health guidelines, whereas interactional competencies support community engagement and intersectoral collaboration. However, agricultural workers did not explicitly recognize the involvement of village leaders or health volunteers in OH service. This discrepancy indicates that collaborative processes within PCUs operate effectively at the system level but remain insufficiently visible to service recipients. Therefore, enhancing transparency and communication regarding collaborative practices may strengthen community participation and improve the perceived effectiveness of OH services.

Knowledge

Knowledge represents the cognitive foundation that informs OH practice8. NPs identified essential knowledge domains, including pesticide hazards, health promotion and disease prevention, work-related disease management, legal and regulatory frameworks, integration of OH into routine services, documentation of work-related diseases, and OH research competence. Similar perspectives reported by colleagues and administrators show cross-stakeholder agreement regarding the knowledge required for OH service delivery.

These domains are consistent with the Thai Ministry of Public Health guidance for in-house OH services and correspond closely with the BOHS activity model20, indicating coherence between national policy expectations and frontline practice. Nevertheless, agricultural workers did not mention documentation systems or research activities, suggesting that knowledge-intensive functions embedded within health system operations are not readily perceived as direct services. This gap underscores an important distinction between professional practice and community recognition of care processes. Strengthening communication regarding surveillance activities and the use of collected data may improve community understanding of how OH services contribute to prevention and policy planning.

Notably, rehabilitation knowledge, although emphasized within BOHS standards, was not identified by any participant group. This finding indicates that certain components of OH knowledge remain insufficiently integrated into routine primary care practice or conceptualized within community-based contexts. Targeted continuing professional development and clearer operational framing of rehabilitation within OH services may therefore be required.

Skills

Skills represent the translation of professional knowledge into practical service delivery8. NPs reported competencies in health promotion and disease prevention, clinical management of work-related diseases, interorganizational collaboration, documentation of occupational health data, and engagement in OH research. Agreement among NPs, colleagues, and administrators suggests convergence in expectations regarding operational competencies required in PCUs.

The identified skill domains correspond with BOHS competencies, including risk assessment, preventive interventions, diagnosis and management of occupational diseases, emergency preparedness, general health care, and curative services20. However, agricultural workers primarily recognize visible clinical and preventive activities, whereas system-level functions such as documentation, surveillance, and research are rarely acknowledged. This discrepancy reflects differences between professional performance and stakeholder perception, indicating that some essential competencies remain embedded within institutional processes rather than experienced directly by service recipients.

The absence of rehabilitation across participant accounts warrants particular attention. This gap likely reflects systemic and contextual barriers rather than deficiencies in professional competency. OH rehabilitation may receive limited emphasis in nursing education, where rehabilitation is traditionally associated with hospital-based or disability-focused care. Furthermore, PCUs frequently operate under constraints related to workforce capacity, time allocation, and access to specialized rehabilitation resources, restraining structured rehabilitation implementation. In rural agricultural settings, activities such as follow-up monitoring, home visits, return-to-work advice, or guidance on safe pesticide practices may be performed but not explicitly recognized as rehabilitation services by providers or communities. Therefore, this finding denotes a conceptual and system-level gap rather than a complete absence of rehabilitative practice.

Although agricultural workers were not directly involved in documentation or research activities, systematic recording of work-related diseases remains important for identifying patterns of pesticide-related health risks and informing policy development and resource allocation21. Engagement in OH research also supports professional development and continuous service improvement22. Integration of rehabilitation components alongside surveillance and research activities may improve continuity of care and strengthen the long-term effectiveness of OH services for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers23.

Iceberg competency model application

When considering the Iceberg Competency Model as a conceptual lens to analyze the competencies of NPs in Thailand’s PCUs for delivering OH services, the findings are organized into three major themes—professional attributes, knowledge, and skills—shaped by the perspectives of different stakeholders and the broader policy landscape. The Iceberg Competency Model divides professional competencies into two layers: the Visible Part and the Hidden Part7.

The Visible Part comprises knowledge and skills that appear “above the waterline.” These are easily measurable and can be developed through specific training programs. The Hidden Part consists of social role, self-image, traits, and motives7. These underlying traits determine long-term behavior and are more difficult to develop. However, this part is the real driver and is reflected in professional attributes. This will contribute to increased knowledge and skills, aligning with research on the impact of competency-enhancing programs for NPs in PCUs. The research found a statistically significant increase in knowledge and skills after program participation24. This is supported by data from NPs indicating a lack of confidence in providing OH services in PCUs, such as diagnosing occupational diseases, particularly awareness gaps in recording whether a disease is caused by pesticides. Furthermore, NPs have not received specialized training in OH standards, consistent with the perspective of Public Health Administrators who believe that current Ministry of Public Health policy favors specialized OH training for secondary care, not primary care. Insufficient knowledge of disease diagnosis by NPs leads to inaccurate recording, resulting in improper ICD-10 coding and statistics that do not reflect reality, leading to small annual budgets for occupational disease prevention and promotion programs in the community. This demonstrates that knowledge and skills, components of each competency of NPs in delivering OH services in PCUs, are inadequate. However, colleagues and service recipients perceive NPs to possess sufficient competencies for service delivery. These professional attributes are typically difficult to develop. However, NPs are perceived to possess a high level of competence in this area. This may be because NPs have received a certain level of training in primary care, even if it does not cover occupational diseases2. The resulting confidence in providing services reflects the fact that focusing on self-development for NPs through short-term training that enhances their knowledge and skills would help develop their competencies for OH services in PCUs appropriately and directly. This is supported by studies on the effects of competency-enhancing programs for NPs, which found a statistically significant increase in traits that are part of professional attributes24.

Considering the use of data, this study found that NPs in PCUs should possess the following competencies, whereas the competencies currently lacking compared with the standard are Knowledge (fundamentals of pesticides, health effects of exposure, rehabilitation, and relevant laws); Skills (risk assessment, preliminary screening, first aid, rehabilitation skills, and recording work-related diseases); and Attributes (effective communication, service orientation, professional ethics [respecting patient rights], and interactional features, such as coordinating with community leaders). This information can be used to develop specialized or supplementary programs or curricula, which may be online or e-learning, where they can learn at their own pace for nurses in PCUs, particularly in developing countries, such as those in Southeast Asia, that are heavily reliant on agriculture and the extensive use of pesticides, impacting agricultural workers’ health. Therefore, the capacity of nurses or NPs in PCUs in developing countries should be enhanced to effectively prevent and care for farmers experiencing health problems.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the key competencies required of NPs in PCUs for the effective delivery of OH services. There are three concise policy recommendations for the Thai Ministry of Public Health: (1) Mandatory Specialized OH Certification: The MOPH should collaborate with the Nursing Council to integrate a mandatory “Agricultural Occupational Health” short course for PCU nurses, focusing on pesticide toxicology, ICD-10 coding for work-related diseases, and specialized first aid; (2) Institutionalization of Multisectoral Funding: Embed OH KPIs into the District Quality of Life Development Committee to mandate joint budgeting between the health and agricultural sectors for proactive farm-site risk assessments; (3) Deployment of Competency-Based Digital Tools: Upgrade primary care health information systems with automated clinical decision support that prompts nurses to record occupational histories and provides real-time guidance for managing pesticide exposure.

Limitations

The voluntary participation of NPs, their colleagues, administrators of the provincial public health offices, district public health offices, district agricultural offices, local government organizations, and agricultural workers introduces the potential for self-selection bias, possibly affecting the generalizability of the findings. Individuals who chose to participate may have had a greater interest in or awareness of OH services than nonparticipants. However, available background information indicated no substantial differences between volunteers and non-volunteers in terms of baseline OH knowledge, job responsibilities, and work settings, thereby reducing the potential impact of selection bias. Furthermore, the current policy context of the Ministry of Public Health in Thailand does not formally support specialized OH training for nurses working in primary care settings. Training opportunities are largely directed toward nurses in secondary or higher-level healthcare facilities. This structural limitation may have influenced the level of knowledge and skill showed by the participants and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Simultaneously, this context highlights the need for policy reform and the expansion of OH training opportunities tailored specifically to primary care nurses to strengthen OH service delivery at the community level. Moreover, this study examined NPs’ competencies in delivering in-house OH services to pesticide-exposed agricultural workers in PCUs. However, the scope of the investigation was limited to in-house services and did not encompass proactive or outreach-based OH services.

Suggestions

Future research should explore NPs’ competencies across both in-house and proactive OH service models for pesticide-exposed agricultural workers in PCUs. Expanding the scope to include community outreach, workplace assessments, and preventive field-based interventions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of competency requirements in real-world practice. Such research could generate robust evidence to inform the development of a comprehensive OH nursing curriculum, guide competency-based training programs, and ultimately enhance the quality, continuity, and effectiveness of OH services delivered at the primary care level.
