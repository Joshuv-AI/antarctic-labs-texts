Trauma-related moderators of associations between sex hormones and PTSD symptoms: a replication-extension
Authors: Juliette A Bichon, Robyn A Ellis, Julia B Merker, Theresa K Webber, Antonia V Seligowski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) disproportionately affects women and recent evidence suggests that unique associations between sex hormones (e.g. estradiol and progesterone) and PTSD symptoms may contribute to this difference. In addition, trauma characteristics such as age at trauma and trauma type may influence this association. We aimed to replicate and extend the findings of a prior study that identified trauma characteristics (sexual trauma and age at trauma) as moderators of associations between estradiol and PTSD symptoms.Methods: Sixty-five trauma-exposed individuals assigned female at birth were included (Mage = 31.45). Participants provided blood samples for estradiol and progesterone assays. The Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5) was used to assess trauma characteristics and PTSD symptoms.Results: There were no significant correlations between estradiol, progesterone, and PTSD symptom severity. Regression analyses revealed no moderation effects by sexual trauma or age at trauma for estradiol. However, age at trauma significantly moderated the relationship between progesterone and total PTSD severity.Discussion: Contrary to expectations, our results did not replicate prior research, as estradiol was not associated with PTSD severity in our sample. Progesterone was not directly associated with PTSD symptoms, but this relationship was moderated by age at trauma onset. Our findings suggest that trauma characteristics may influence the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD symptoms. While we didn't replicate previous associations between estradiol and PTSD symptoms, our study emphasizes the importance of considering trauma characteristics in understanding the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD.KEYWORDS: Sexual trauma, women, PTSD, estradiol, progesterone

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) disproportionately affects women and there are unique associations between sex hormones (e.g. estradiol and progesterone) and PTSD symptoms in women (e.g. Garcia et al., 2018; Seligowski et al., 2020). Estradiol appears to exert a protective influence on PTSD symptoms, such that high estradiol and phases of the menstrual cycle associated with elevated estradiol levels (e.g. late follicular and mid-luteal) correlate with enhanced fear extinction in women with and without PTSD (e.g. Glover et al., 2012; Milad et al., 2006) and less severe PTSD symptoms overall (Nillni et al., 2015). The effects of progesterone on PTSD are less well understood, although some research has found that the mid-luteal phase (characterized by high progesterone and estradiol levels) is linked to heightened Intrusion and Re-experiencing symptoms (two symptom subscales used for a PTSD diagnosis) (Garcia et al., 2018; Ravi et al., 2019), and that higher circulating progesterone levels are associated with worse extinction retention in women with PTSD (Pineles et al., 2016).

Certain trauma characteristics may be differentially affected by fluctuations in sex hormones, such as age at the time of trauma. Early trauma onset (e.g. childhood trauma) is associated with a higher likelihood of developing PTSD and more severe symptoms after its development (McCutcheon et al., 2010; Schoedl et al., 2010). Given that childhood trauma often precedes or coincides with puberty and the beginning of menstruation, early trauma onset may have lasting effects on sex hormones. Some studies have shown that exposure to childhood stressors or childhood trauma can be associated with menstrual cycle dysregulation, as well as negative impacts on some aspects of fertility later in life (Jacobs et al., 2015). Thus, considering the timing of trauma may be important as women who have experienced a traumatic event at an early age may experience different effects of trauma on both their ovarian hormone levels and PTSD symptoms (Mu et al., 2022).

The nature of the traumatic event may also influence the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD. Women who have experienced interpersonal trauma (e.g. sexual trauma) may exhibit more severe PTSD symptoms, and different menstrual cycle phases have been associated with PTSD symptoms among these women (Ehring & Quack, 2010; Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2016). A study in 40 trauma-exposed women found that sexual trauma and early age at the time of trauma were associated with higher PTSD severity, and that phases of the menstrual cycle associated with lower levels of estradiol (measured by saliva) were associated with higher PTSD symptom severity, but only among women who had experienced sexual trauma (Rieder et al., 2022). When investigating this relationship longitudinally, larger increases in estradiol were associated with greater decreases in PTSD severity across time among women with early-life trauma, but not later-life trauma. In other words, experiencing trauma at an early age could have made women more vulnerable to fluctuations in PTSD symptoms across the menstrual cycle, with a particular risk of severe symptoms during low estradiol phases. However, this relationship was not found for women who had experienced sexual trauma (vs. nonsexual trauma), indicating a differential effect of these trauma characteristics on the relationship between hormones and PTSD. Given that progesterone fluctuates concurrently with estradiol during the menstrual cycle and has also been linked to PTSD symptom severity, there is a need to characterize its role in these associations as well.

Our study sought to replicate and extend the Rieder et al. (2022) findings, including both estradiol and progesterone. We examined associations between sex hormones and PTSD severity, as well as the moderating effects of age at trauma and trauma type. Based on the Rieder et al. (2022) study, we hypothesized that estradiol would be negatively associated with total PTSD symptom severity among women with sexual trauma. Second, we hypothesized that progesterone would be positively associated with PTSD symptoms given previous literature that reported associations between progesterone, mood, and extinction retention (Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2016; Ferree & Cahill, 2009). We expected that these associations would be stronger among participants who experienced sexual trauma and/or trauma at an early age.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 65 trauma-exposed individuals assigned female at birth (Mage = 31.45, SD = 8.92). In terms of race, participants were 81.5% White, 7.7% Black or African American, 6.2% Asian or South-Asian, and 4.6% not listed; of those who self-described their race, 1.54% identified as Mixed, 1.54% identified as Latina, and 1.54% identified as Middle Eastern. In addition, 12.3% of participants were Latino, Hispanic or of Spanish Origin. Approximately half (55.4%) of the sample were naturally cycling. Of the participants who were naturally cycling, 63.9% (n = 23) were in the follicular phase of their menstrual cycle, and 36.1% (n = 13) were in the luteal phase. In addition, 72.7% of participants were single, 15.2% were married, and 10.6% were divorced. 64.6% were employed full-time or part-time, 15.2% were students, 19.7% were unemployed. On average, participants reported a household income of 35,000–49,000. In terms of diagnoses, 56.9% of participants met criteria for PTSD, 28.8% met criteria for current major depressive episode, 4.5% met criteria for mild alcohol use disorder, 6.2% met criteria for mild cannabis use disorder, 16.7% met criteria for obsessive compulsive disorder, and 10.6% met criteria for panic disorder. In terms of medications, 53% of participants were on antidepressant medication and 6.2% were on mood stabilizers.

Procedure

Participants were recruited from the greater Boston area and required to endorse Criterion A trauma exposure and be aged 18–55 years old. Exclusion criteria included a history of head injury, loss of consciousness for >5 min, major neurological illness, current moderate or severe substance use disorder, current mania or psychosis, or pregnancy. Participants provided a blood sample following informed consent and confirmation of eligibility, from which estradiol and progesterone levels were derived using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Participants then completed the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (Weathers et al., 2018), and remaining study procedures, which were approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Data analysis

Given that estradiol and progesterone data were not normally distributed, we conducted Spearman’s correlations between estradiol, progesterone, age at trauma, and total PTSD severity. A t-test was used to compare PTSD severity between participants with and without sexual trauma. Next, a series of regression models were conducted to determine the moderating effects of (1) age at trauma and (2) trauma type (sexual versus non-sexual) on the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD severity. Separate regression models were run for total PTSD severity outcome and each sex hormone (estradiol and progesterone). In each regression model, independent variables included the relevant moderator (age at trauma or trauma type), sex hormone, and the moderator-by-estradiol/progesterone interaction. Independent variables were mean centred. Simple slopes analyses were conducted for significant interactions by repeating the analysis at one standard deviation above and below the significant moderator. Regression analyses were conducted in R using the package lavaan with an alpha level of p = .05 and full-information maximum likelihood estimator.

Results

Estradiol and progesterone were correlated with each other (r = .27, p < .05), but not with any other variable. When we excluded women who were not naturally cycling (n = 37), higher progesterone levels were associated with higher age at trauma (r = .40, p < .05). There were no significant differences between participants who were naturally cycling and those using hormonal contraceptives on any demographics, PTSD status or PTSD symptom severity, or estradiol or progesterone levels. In an independent samples t-test, there were no differences in estradiol, progesterone, or PTSD severity between participants who experienced sexual or non-sexual trauma.

The main effect of estradiol and its interaction with age at trauma and trauma type on PTSD severity were nonsignificant. The main effect of progesterone and its interaction with trauma type on PTSD severity were nonsignificant. Age at trauma significantly moderated the relationship between progesterone and total PTSD severity (β = 0.46, p = .01). Simple slopes analyses indicated that progesterone was negatively associated with PTSD severity when age at trauma was low (β = −.87, p = .02), and positively associated with PTSD severity when age at trauma was high (β = .44, p = .02; Figure 1).

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD symptoms, considering the impact of trauma characteristics. Contrary to Rieder et al.’s (2022) observations, we identified no significant associations between estradiol and PTSD symptoms, nor did we observe any moderation effects by sexual trauma or age at trauma. Although there were no direct associations between progesterone levels and PTSD symptoms, our results suggested that age at trauma moderated the relationship between progesterone and PTSD severity.

Prior studies have reported associations between estradiol and PTSD. For instance, trauma-exposed women with lower levels of estradiol have been shown to exhibit higher PTSD symptoms, and among those with PTSD, menstrual phases with high estradiol and progesterone have been associated with higher re-experiencing symptoms (Garcia et al., 2018; Green & Graham, 2022; Nillni et al., 2015). Given known differences in trauma characteristics and clinical outcomes between individuals with PTSD and those with subthreshold PTSD symptoms (Pietrzak et al., 2011), our sample’s higher relative proportion of PTSD may have impacted findings such that PTSD symptoms persist above and beyond fluctuations in estradiol. In addition, while a strength of our study was our measurement of sex hormones via plasma, which is more sensitive than saliva (Arslan et al., 2023), some prior studies have used saliva and this inconsistency could partially explain our discrepant findings (Ferree & Cahill, 2009; Pineles et al., 2016; Rieder et al., 2022). Finally, our study included women who were using hormonal contraceptives and were at different phases of their menstrual cycle, which could have impacted our findings. Although our sensitivity analysis indicated there was no association between estradiol and PTSD among naturally cycling women, future studies should recruit larger samples that include women on and off hormonal contraceptives and control for menstrual cycle phase to measure associations between estradiol and PTSD.

Although there were no direct associations between progesterone and PTSD symptom severity, age at trauma was a moderator such that progesterone was negatively associated with PTSD severity when the trauma happened in childhood (<18), and positively associated with PTSD severity when the trauma happened in adulthood. Our findings resemble results from some extant literature indicating that higher progesterone levels are associated with increased depressive symptoms and mood lability in women with interpersonal trauma, as well as more salient and intrusive recollections following an aversive event among adult women (Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2016; Ferree & Cahill, 2009). Thus, fluctuations in PTSD severity across the menstrual cycle may hinge on trauma timing, with those exposed to trauma at a young age experiencing more severe symptoms when progesterone is low, and those exposed in adulthood experiencing more severe symptoms when progesterone is high.

Overall, these findings suggest that the nature of the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD symptoms may differ based on trauma characteristics. In a sample characterized by high PTSD symptom severity, progesterone and PTSD symptoms were differentially associated based on age at trauma. In a sample with lower PTSD severity (e.g. Rieder et al., 2022), estradiol and PTSD symptom severity were differentially associated based on the nature of the trauma and timing of assessment. More research is warranted to understand whether early trauma alters the nature of the relationship between sex hormones and PTSD. Our study suggests that the nature of these relationships may shift across the menstrual cycle dependent on trauma characteristics, consistent with the longitudinal results of Rieder et al., 2022. It may be important to examine these relationships among specific samples (e.g. only women who experience a traumatic event at an early age) and examine within-subject changes in hormones and symptoms to better understand how trauma characteristics influence these associations across the menstrual cycle. Future studies could longitudinally investigate whether trauma type and timing influence the evolution of PTSD symptoms in relation to sex hormones.

Some limitations must be noted. First, only 55.4% of women in our sample were naturally cycling. This factor represents both a strength and a weakness, as our sample may be representative of the general population where many individuals use hormonal birth control, however, contraceptive use affects the menstrual cycle and introduces complexities in the interpretation of results (Daniels & Abma, 2020). Notably, our correlation analyses did not change when we conducted a sensitivity analysis among the naturally cycling sample. In addition, although our sample size was sufficient for our purposes, we may have been underpowered to detect smaller effects, particularly in our simple slopes analyses. Future research should consider recruiting larger samples to strengthen generalizability. Additionally, most of our participants identified as White, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should include more diverse samples that may disproportionately experience trauma.

Conclusion

Our study findings underscore the nuanced nature of the associations between sex hormones and PTSD symptoms, which may vary based on sample characteristics or assay methodologies. Further, our findings suggest that age at trauma onset could be an important consideration in the relationship between progesterone and PTSD symptoms. Future studies should consider investigating sex hormones and PTSD in samples with specific trauma characteristics (e.g. childhood trauma), and explore how these relationships vary throughout the menstrual cycle.
