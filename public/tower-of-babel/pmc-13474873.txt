ABSTRACT
AbstractContextGenomic prediction has been established as a powerful tool in pig breeding, which typically entails the analysis of large-scale genomic datasets. Neural network models, renowned for their robust pattern recognition capabilities, have been successfully applied to extract complex genetic patterns from such high-dimensional genomic data.ObjectiveBecause growth traits are critical economic indicators in pig breeding, their accurate prediction is essential for genetic improvement. Introduction

China is the world's largest pig producer, with an annual pig output of approximately 700 million head, accounting for 54.4% of the global total. As one of the most important livestock species in China, pigs play a critical role in both economic development and social livelihood.

In the pig industry, breeding programs are of critical importance because their primary objective is to identify and select breeding pigs with superior genetic merit, which in turn directly affects the production efficiency of commercial pigs. Duroc pigs are generally used as terminal sires in China. During the breeding of Duroc pigs, traits closely related to growth rate—such as days to reach 100 kg body weight and feed conversion ratio—are particularly important [1]. Traditional pig breeding mainly relies on phenotypic records and phenotypic breeding values, involving phenotypic measurement, which generally results in a long breeding cycle and low selection efficiency [2]. Therefore, developing more efficient breeding technologies has become an urgent need.

Genomic Selection (GS), first proposed by Meuwissen et al. in 2001, uses phenotypic data and high-density genomic markers to estimate individual breeding values [3]. GS has demonstrated remarkable advantages in pig breeding [4]. Compared with traditional phenotypic selection, GS improves prediction accuracy and accelerates genetic gain, providing strong technical support for the sustainable development of the pig industry.

Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (GBLUP) is a fundamental method in genomic selection. It uses genome-wide SNP markers to construct an additive genomic relationship matrix within a linear mixed model framework for phenotype and genomic breeding value prediction [5]. However, GBLUP assumes that the weights of all SNPs are the same in the construction of the kinship matrix. However, some genetic markers are in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with quantitative trait loci (QTL) with large effects, while others are not. Therefore, the assumptions of the GBLUP models may not be appropriate for different conditions.

With the rapid development of the big data era, machine learning has emerged as an efficient data prediction method and is widely applied in genetic breeding of plants and animals [6–10]. Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models are commonly used supervised learning models in machine learning. The Random Forest model is based on decision trees and improves prediction performance by combining multiple decision trees into one model. It can handle large-scale datasets and has been applied to genomic data prediction [11, 12]. However, Random Forest models often require long training times [13], and may not be suitable for practical breeding processes. The Support Vector Machine model excels in identifying subtle patterns in small samples or complex datasets and has been applied to various biological datasets [14, 15]. Despite their success, machine learning methods still face challenges in capturing complex interactions and nonlinear relationships between SNPs, limiting their predictive power and computational efficiency.

To address these limitations, Deep Learning (DL), a subset of machine learning, has shown excellent automatic feature extraction abilities through hierarchical neural networks [16]. Compared with traditional machine learning methods, deep learning offers distinct advantages in genomic prediction, capable of uncovering complex genetic patterns [17–19]. The core advantage of neural network models lies in their exceptional ability to extract features, enabling them to uncover complex genetic patterns that traditional methods struggle to identify.

However, in practical animal and plant genomic breeding, individual genomic data often consist of millions of effective genetic loci, with small sample sizes, making it challenging for neural network models to learn all the features. Moreover, although genetic factors largely determine complex traits, only a subset of genetic variants exerts meaningful influence. The high dimensionality and intricate correlation structure of genomic data thus pose substantial obstacles to accurate phenotype prediction. Consequently, feature selection has become an indispensable step in breeding pipelines, allowing researchers to pinpoint trait-associated markers and improve the efficiency of evaluating candidate individuals [20].

Existing studies have utilized feature selection methods to extract important genetic loci. These methods are generally divided into filtering methods [21], wrapper methods [22], and embedded methods [23]. Filtering methods often rely on univariate statistics, while embedded methods depend on the performance of specific models. Filtering methods often rely on univariate statistics, whereas embedded methods depend on a model’s performance. For example, Random Forest (RF) is an embedded method used to identify SNPs relevant to genomic selection in pigs. However, its predictive performance may not always be optimal, particularly when dealing with high-dimensional data or highly correlated features [24]. XGBoost, a supervised learning model, automatically performs feature selection by constructing decision trees, prioritizing features that contribute most to prediction results. This reduces model complexity and enhances prediction performance [25]. However, despite the excellent performance of XGBoost in handling structured data, its reliance on labels and limited ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships restrict its application [26]. Piles et al. [27] noted that existing filtering and embedded methods, as well as their combinations, still have shortcomings in genomic feature selection, limiting their ability to distinguish between learning and feature selection, and failing to capture complex interactions and nonlinear relationships between genetic loci. This indicates the urgent need to develop advanced feature selection techniques to handle large-scale SNP data, enabling more precise predictions and deeper biological insights in genomic selection.

For high-dimensional genomic data, research on feature selection using intelligent optimization algorithms is still relatively scarce. Intelligent optimization algorithms, as a type of wrapper method for feature selection, are favored for their ability to explore large search spaces and obtain optimal solutions [28–30]. However, these algorithms face high computational complexity and the risk of falling into local optima. Moreover, the presence of high-dimensional features makes it difficult for optimization algorithms to find the optimal features within the search space [31]. Symmetric Uncertainty Analysis can significantly reduce redundant features, thus lowering the dimensionality of the feature search space for subsequent optimization algorithms [32]. The Kepler Optimization Algorithm (KOA) is a recently proposed algorithm with strong global search capabilities and requires minimal parameter adjustment [33]. However, like other algorithms, KOA can quickly get trapped in local optima and struggles to handle very large feature search spaces [34, 35].

To address these issues, and considering that not all genetic locis affect pig growth traits, a rational selection and analysis of genomic features is necessary to tackle the challenge posed by the vast number of genetic loci in breeding pigs. This paper improves the KOA based on the results of Symmetric Uncertainty Analysis and designs a Tent Mapping-based Kepler Algorithm (TKOA) integrated with a Neural Network Genomic Prediction (GPNN) model to predict two growth traits, FCR and C_Day_120kg, in Duroc pig offspring, followed by selection and breeding. The aim is to provide new perspectives and tools for in-depth genomic prediction and breeding analysis. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

A new prediction model framework, TKOA-GPNN, is proposed to address the limitations of existing genomic prediction methods. The framework consists of two modules: two-stage genomic feature selection and a genomic prediction neural network model, designed to improve the effectiveness of genetic selection for growth traits in Duroc pigs.

A new prediction model framework, TKOA-GPNN, is proposed to address the limitations of existing genomic prediction methods. The framework consists of two modules: two-stage genomic feature selection and a genomic prediction neural network model, designed to improve the effectiveness of genetic selection for growth traits in Duroc pigs.

Experimental results demonstrate the efficiency of the TKOA-GPNN model. The TKOA module first reduces the genomic feature search space using Symmetric Uncertainty Analysis, ensuring the computational feasibility when dealing with large-scale genomic features, which enhances the model fitting effect of the second module, enabling more accurate and faster reflection of growth traits in pigs.

Experimental results demonstrate the efficiency of the TKOA-GPNN model. The TKOA module first reduces the genomic feature search space using Symmetric Uncertainty Analysis, ensuring the computational feasibility when dealing with large-scale genomic features, which enhances the model fitting effect of the second module, enabling more accurate and faster reflection of growth traits in pigs.

The TKOA-GPNN model is applied to predict the phenotypic values of two traits in newly born offspring with only genetic chip data, facilitating the selection of superior breeding pigs in the Duroc breeding process. The results indicate that this model has significant potential for application in the selection and breeding of superior pigs.

The results indicate that this model has significant potential for application in the selection and breeding of superior pigs.

Materials and methods

Data description

This study uses Duroc pigs from a core breeding farm in Central China as experimental subjects. The data collected includes identification numbers, breed, farm, birth year, measurement year, gender, and birth weight for 468 parent pigs and 127 offspring pigs (114 genotyped with a commercial SNP array and 13 with whole-genome sequencing). The phenotype traits investigated in this study includes the feed conversion ratio (FCR) and the adjusted age at 120 kg body weight (C_Day_120kg). The genomic data for the experimental pig population are obtained using paired-end sequencing on the BGISEQ-500 sequencing platform. The sequencing coverage is 10 × , yielding the raw genomic data.

Although we focused on FCR and C_Day_120kg in this study due to data availability and their breeding importance, the proposed framework is trait-agnostic and can be directly applied to additional traits given corresponding genotype–phenotype records.

Genomic data quality control

First, strict quality control and filtering are performed on the genomic variant data using GATK and Bcftools to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the downstream analyses. For SNPs, filtering is performed using GATK with the following criteria: Quality score (QD) < 3.0, Fisher's Strand (FS) > 60.0, Mapping Quality (MQ) < 40.0, Mapping Quality Rank Sum Test (MQRankSum) < -12.5, Read Position Rank Sum Test (ReadPosRankSum) < -8.0, and Strand Odds Ratio (SOR) > 3.0. For Indels, the filtering.

criteria are: Sequencing Depth (DP) < 10, MQRankSum < -12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < -8.0. To further improve the reliability of SNPs, those located within 5 base pairs of an Indel are excluded using bcftools. This rigorous filtering process ensures that the remaining SNPs and Indel are more reliable, enhancing the credibility of the research results.

Additionally, quality control on the genotype data is performed using Plink software. The quality control thresholds are set as follows: genotype missing rate < 10%, minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5%, and sample missing rate < 10%. After this process, 9,618,104 genetic loci remained, of which 8,014,850 are SNPs and 1,603,254 are Indels.

Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) Pruning

From the field of genetic marker research, the degree of linkage disequilibrium (LD) between markers and causal variants has a significant impact on the accuracy of genomic prediction [36]. Previous studies have shown that, under limited sample sizes, using whole-genome sequencing data (WGP) has not effectively enhanced the prediction accuracy of pig traits. This phenomenon is primarily due to the randomness in the process of increasing genetic loci, leading to the incorporation of LD noise markers lacking causal variants in the WGP data [37]. Therefore, when dealing with millions of variants in whole-genome sequencing data, it is critical to exclude low-LD variants that are unlikely to be causal. To achieve this, this paper calculates the LD between genetic loci, and the LD pruning parameters are set as follows: window size = 50, step size = 5, and correlation threshold = 0.5. The corresponding Plink command is: -indep-pairwise 50 10 0.5. After applying this pruning strategy, 461,099 independent genetic loci are successfully selected for the genomic selection analysis.

Phenotype-genotype data

R software is used to combine the LD-pruned genetic loci with the phenotype data for FCR and C_Day_120kg, generating the final phenotype-genotype data.

TKOA-GPNN Model

In this section, a novel TKOA-GPNN model is designed to enhance the genomic prediction of growth traits, specifically FCR and C_Day_120kg, in Duroc pigs. The model comprises two modules: the first module involves the extraction of genomic features related to pig growth traits based on two-stage feature selection. The second module is the genomic prediction neural network (GPNN) model, which uses the genomic feature selection results from the first module for prediction and evaluation. Genomic Prediction Neural Network (GPNN) model for pig growth traits

In the genomic prediction of pig growth traits, traditional genomic prediction methods and machine learning models are often limited to analyzing simple relationships between growth traits and genetic loci, and they struggle to capture the intricate interactions between genetic loci and between loci and growth traits. Furthermore, while Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have shown excellent performance in image processing and pattern recognition fields [38, 39], they present limitations when applied to genomic data. The structure of CNNs is primarily designed for data with spatial hierarchical structures, extracting local features through convolutional layers and processing them hierarchically. However, genomic data for pig growth traits does not have a spatial structure, and the interactions between genes and their effects on growth traits are typically highly complex and abstract. To address these challenges, this study designs a Genomic Prediction Neural Network (GPNN) model with a multi-layer hidden structure to more efficiently analyze phenotype-genotype data processed by the TKOA module and improve the accuracy of growth trait predictions.

The design of the GPNN model is inspired by the superior capability of deep networks in handling high-dimensional data. By stacking multiple hidden layers, GPNN is able to deeply mine the complex feature representations in genomic data. This deep structure allows GPNN to capture the nonlinear and highly complex interactions between genetic loci and between loci and growth traits, thereby providing higher prediction accuracy for Duroc pig growth traits.

In the GPNN model, each hidden layer employs Batch Normalization and the ReLU activation function. These techniques help accelerate model convergence and introduce nonlinear transformations to enhance the model's expressive power. Additionally, the Dropout regularization strategy is employed by randomly dropping out certain neurons, effectively preventing overfitting and further enhancing the model's generalization capability.

Results and discussion

Genetic parameters for growth traits s of parent Duroc breeding pigs

Furthermore, the significant heritability estimates provide a solid genetic foundation for the subsequent implementation of genomic selection strategies, which can accelerate genetic gain for these key production traits in this Duroc population.

Comparative analysis of TKOA and competing swarm intelligence algorithms

To further evaluate the performance of the proposed TKOA, KOA, PSO, and GWO are included as benchmark algorithms. All algorithms are implemented under the same dataset, fitness function, population size, and maximum number of iterations to ensure a fair comparison. PSO [40] is implemented in its standard form with inertia weight w = 0.7, cognitive coefficient c1 = 1.5, and social coefficient c2 = 1.5. GWO [41] is implemented in its canonical form, in which the control parameter a decreases linearly from 2 to 0 during the iterative process.

In the comparative analysis of the FCR and C_Day_120kg traits, the TKOA module demonstrates clear advantages in convergence speed and, for FCR, in both fitness and accuracy (Fig. 3). In terms of fitness (Fig. 3a, c), TKOA shows larger fluctuations in the early iterations owing to the randomness introduced by tent mapping during population initialization, but it stabilizes sooner than KOA. For FCR (Fig. 3a), the fitness value of TKOA stabilizes after approximately 60 iterations at about 0.002, whereas KOA stabilizes after approximately 90 iterations at about 0.0026; thus TKOA achieves both faster convergence and a lower (better) final fitness for FCR. For C_Day_120kg (Fig. 3c), TKOA stabilizes after about 40–50 iterations with a fitness value of approximately 0.005, while KOA stabilizes after about 60–70 iterations with a slightly lower final fitness of approximately 0.0048; here TKOA converges faster but KOA attains a marginally better final fitness.

In terms of fitness (Fig. 3a, c), TKOA exhibited both a faster decrease and lower final fitness values for both growth traits. For the FCR trait (Fig. 3a), the fitness of TKOA decreased rapidly in the early iterations and became stable after approximately 40–50 iterations, finally converging to about 0.0024. In contrast, KOA decreased more slowly and stabilized after approximately 80–90 iterations at around 0.0025. Although GWO maintained a gradual downward trend throughout the iterations, its final fitness is still higher than that of TKOA and KOA, remaining around 0.0031. By comparison, PSO entered a plateau shortly after the initial stage, and its final fitness stayed at a much higher level (approximately 0.0070), indicating obvious premature convergence. For the C_Day_120kg trait (Fig. 3c), TKOA again showed the fastest decline in fitness and converged to approximately 0.0049 after about 60–70 iterations. The final fitness of KOA is slightly higher, around 0.0051. GWO achieved a final fitness of about 0.0059, which is clearly better than PSO but still inferior to TKOA and KOA. PSO showed only limited reduction in fitness and eventually remained at approximately 0.0095, representing the worst performance among the compared algorithms.

In terms of accuracy (Fig. 3b, d), TKOA also showed both faster improvement and higher final accuracy for the two growth traits. For the FCR trait (Fig. 3b), the accuracy of TKOA stabilized after about 40 iterations and finally remained around 0.9974. KOA reached a similar final accuracy, but required more iterations to become stable. GWO improved more gradually and reached a final accuracy of approximately 0.9968. In contrast, the accuracy of PSO remained almost constant at around 0.9930, with only marginal improvement. For the C_Day_120kg trait (Fig. 3d), the accuracy of TKOA increased rapidly during the early iterations and finally stabilized at approximately 0.9950. KOA achieved a slightly lower final accuracy of about 0.9949. Although GWO showed a continuous improvement trend, its final accuracy is still lower than those of TKOA and KOA, remaining around 0.9941. PSO consistently showed the lowest accuracy, at approximately 0.9905.

Taken together, the results for both growth traits indicate that TKOA exhibited superior convergence efficiency and stronger overall optimization capability in terms of both fitness and accuracy. Compared with the original KOA, TKOA reached stable solutions in fewer iterations for both growth traits while maintaining comparable or better final performance. Relative to GWO, TKOA achieved faster convergence and higher-quality final solutions. In contrast, PSO mainly updates particle positions based on the personal best and global best, which makes it more prone to rapid population aggregation and premature convergence in high-dimensional genotype feature spaces, thereby resulting in the weakest overall performance. Therefore, the comparative results further confirm the effectiveness and superiority of TKOA for growth traits genomic feature selection in Duroc pigs.

Combining the above analysis, the sensitivity of the SVM, RF, and XGBoost models to the complex interactions between features means that the genomic features selected by TKOA may not fully capture these interactions. These models generally exhibit better robustness under high-dimensional features, but TKOA may not improve their performance and could even lead to the loss of some important features. However, for the GPNN model, the application of TKOA significantly improved its prediction accuracy. As a deep learning model, GPNN can directly handle input features without relying on convolutional layers for feature extraction, thus improving computational efficiency. Additionally, GPNN is more sensitive to changes in input features. The feature set optimized by TKOA is better suited to GPNN’s learning capabilities, enhancing its performance in learning complex data patterns. Furthermore, TKOA reduced the overall training time for the GP models, improving the efficiency of FCR and C_Day_120kg breeding selection. While RF exhibited the best prediction accuracy for FCR, its excessively long training time makes it inefficient for practical applications, limiting its usability.

Application of the TKOA-GPNN model to offspring Duroc pig growth traits

To test the model's practical applicability, we first applied the TKOA-GPNN model to predict the growth traits (FCR and C_Day_120kg) of Duroc pig offspring using genotyping array-based data (n = 114), and the model exhibited satisfactory predictive performance. Subsequently, we further validated the model for the same traits in these offspring using whole-genome sequencing-based data (n = 13), and it maintained robust predictive performance, which thus demonstrates the model’s compatibility with both categories of genomic data.

To ensure consistency in genetic features, genotyping array imputation technology is employed to obtain high-density genomic data for offspring individuals. Since commercial genotyping arrays typically lack Y chromosome markers, we supplemented the genomic features with Y chromosome SNPs to ensure comprehensive genomic coverage. After rigorous quality control, genomic features are matched to the optimal feature subset identified from the training population, ensuring that offspring retained the same genetic loci used in model training. Predictions are based solely on genomic information without phenotypic data.

Conclusion and future work

Pig growth traits occupy a crucial economic position in pig production and are also the core target of genetic improvement in pigs. This study proposes a two-module TKOA-GPNN prediction model designed to assist in the selection of offspring individuals with favorable growth traits in Duroc pigs during the breeding prediction process, aiming to select pigs with rapid growth and high feed efficiency as new breeding stock.

The main conclusions of this study are as follows:

In the two-stage high-dimensional genomic feature selection algorithm, the first stage significantly reduces the feature search space for the second-stage TKOA algorithm. In the second stage, the global search capability and accuracy of the optimal solution are enhanced by initializing the initial population of KOA using the tent mapping method.

In the two-stage high-dimensional genomic feature selection algorithm, the first stage significantly reduces the feature search space for the second-stage TKOA algorithm. In the second stage, the global search capability and accuracy of the optimal solution are enhanced by initializing the initial population of KOA using the tent mapping method.

Comparative analysis of genomic feature sets optimized by KOA and TKOA with the full set of SNPs and Indel features across various GP models shows that the TKOA-GPNN model exhibits better accuracy and feasibility in genomic prediction of pig growth traits.

Comparative analysis of genomic feature sets optimized by KOA and TKOA with the full set of SNPs and Indel features across various GP models shows that the TKOA-GPNN model exhibits better accuracy and feasibility in genomic prediction of pig growth traits.

In pig breeding, the prediction of growth traits based on ultra-high-dimensional genomic features can be effectively achieved using the TKOA-GPNN model, with its training and validation phases (Sects. 4.1 and 4.2) underpinned by whole-genome data to guarantee fundamental predictive performance, while the testing phase (Sects. 4.3) further validates the model’s applicability across distinct genomic data sources via cross-validation of whole-genome data and genotyping array data. This model enables accurate predictions of FCR and C_Day_120kg values for newly born breeding piglets, facilitating the selection of superior breeding pigs for enhanced production efficiency and reduced resource consumption.

Through the TKOA-GPNN method, breeding strategies can be effectively optimized, enhancing overall breeding efficiency and providing a more scientific basis for breeding stock production decisions. This research not only contributes significantly to theoretical knowledge but also holds practical application value, promoting the development of more efficient and sustainable practices in the breeding industry.

It should be noted that the data used in this study were derived from a Duroc pig population from a single breeding farm in Central China, with a total sample size of 539 animals, including both parental and offspring populations. Based on this dataset, the predictive performance of the TKOA-GPNN model was evaluated. To further assess the potential effect of sample size on model performance, we additionally conducted small-sample analyses by randomly selecting 200 and 300 individuals from the parental Duroc population to retrain the TKOA-GPNN model, and the corresponding results are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The results showed that model performance varied across different sample sizes; in the present dataset, the predictive accuracy for FCR tended to be relatively higher in some larger sample subsets. This finding suggests that model performance may be jointly influenced by multiple factors, including sample size, sample composition, and population genetic characteristics. Therefore, the identified genomic features and the observed model performance may have been affected by sample size, pedigree relationships, genetic background, and population structure, resulting in a certain degree of population dependence. Accordingly, the current findings are primarily applicable to the specific Duroc population investigated in this study, and their robustness and generalizability still need to be further validated in larger sample sizes, additional traits, and more independent populations.
