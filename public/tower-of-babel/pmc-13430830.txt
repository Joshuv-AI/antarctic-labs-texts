Efficacy and safety of intravenous lidocaine in thyroidectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis and meta-regression
Authors: Dawood Alatefi, Omar Saeed, Malik Alkabazi, Ibrahim Gamal, Ahmed Alanzi, Mohamed Yaseen Alfeetouri, Nuran Arif Benfaid, Abdelrahman shata, Manar Ahmed AbdElfattah, Mohamed wagdy, Sumaya Emad Mohammed, Muhammed Elhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroidectomy is a common surgical procedure associated with postoperative pain and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), which may impair early recovery and quality of recovery (QoR). Intravenous (IV) lidocaine has been proposed as a component of multimodal analgesia; however, its efficacy and safety in patients undergoing thyroidectomy remain uncertain. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of perioperative IV lidocaine in this population.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to Aug 13 2025. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing IV lidocaine with placebo (normal saline) in adult patients undergoing thyroidectomy were included. The primary outcome was postoperative pain scores at 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h. Secondary outcomes included quality of recovery (QoR) scores; incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV); use of antiemetics; duration of anaesthesia; total postoperative opioid consumption; and postoperative analgesia use. Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models. The Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) method was applied when moderate or substantial heterogeneity was present (I² > 40%), whereas the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) method was used when heterogeneity was low to moderate (I² ≤ 40%).ResultsEleven RCTs involving 943 patients (121 males and 822 females; mean age 46.2 years; mean operative duration 84.4 min) were included. Overall, all outcomes were supported by moderate-certainty evidence, except for postoperative pain at 8 h, which was supported by low-certainty evidence. Postoperative pain scores were lower in the lidocaine group at time points (1, 4, 12, 24, and 48 h). At 24 h, pain outcomes favoured lidocaine (standardised mean difference [SMD] − 0.52, 95% CI − 1.01 to − 0.03; P = 0.0417; I² = 88.2%), and this benefit persisted at 48 h (SMD − 0.36, 95% CI − 0.68 to − 0.03; P = 0.0303; I² = 34.1%). Lidocaine significantly improved QoR on postoperative day 1 (MD 6.05, 95% CI 3.23 to 8.88; P < 0.0001; I² = 39.7%) and on day 2 (MD 6.52, 95% CI 4.97 to 8.06; P < 0.0001; I² = 0.0%). IV lidocaine significantly reduced the incidence of PONV compared with control (risk ratio [RR] 0.46, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.70; P = 0.0004; I² = 0.0%), while the requirement for rescue antiemetic therapy and the duration of anaesthesia did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionIV lidocaine was associated with reduced postoperative pain scores, nausea and vomiting, as well as improved QoR in patients undergoing thyroidectomy. However, the relationship between statistical significance and clinical relevance varied across pain outcomes. Accordingly, the clinical relevance of the analgesic effect should be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03965-y.

Introduction

Thyroidectomy is defined as the surgical removal of all or part of the thyroid gland, a procedure typically indicated for various thyroid pathologies, including malignancy, symptomatic goitres, and hyperthyroidism [1]. The volume of these surgeries has seen a significant upward trend over the last few decades; for instance, in the United States alone, the annual number of thyroidectomies performed increased by more than 30% between 2006 and 2011, reaching approximately 130,000 procedures per year [2, 3]. Despite its frequency and general safety, the surgery is associated with notable postoperative challenges, with pain being a primary complaint; studies indicate that patients frequently experience moderate to severe pain, particularly during swallowing (odynophagia) and neck movement, which can persist and impact recovery if not managed adequately [4]. Furthermore, the procedure carries the risk of specific postoperative complications and side effects, most notably postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), which can significantly impair the postoperative quality of recovery (QoR) and lead to physiological and psychological sequelae [5–7].

To reduce complications after thyroid surgery, especially pain, a variety of medical treatments are available. While strong pain relievers like morphine are effective, they often come with undesirable side effects such as nausea and drowsiness, which can slow a patient’s recovery. For this reason, a more modern approach involves using a combination of different pain-control methods at once (multimodal analgesia), and intravenous (IV) lidocaine has been introduced as an adjunct [8–10].

Lidocaine, one of the most widely used anaesthetic drugs, was first synthesised in 1942 as Xylocaine and approved for medical use in 1948. It is a highly versatile medication that can be administered through various routes, including topically, via injection, and intravenously. Its applications are extensive, ranging from local and regional anaesthesia in nerve blocks to the treatment of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Furthermore, lidocaine is utilised in the management of chronic and neuropathic pain, and more recently, it has been adopted for postoperative pain control through IV infusion. The broad therapeutic benefits of lidocaine in diverse surgical settings are attributed to its inherent analgesic, anti-hyperalgesic, and potent anti-inflammatory properties [11–14].

Recent clinical investigations evaluating the efficacy and safety of perioperative IV lidocaine in patients undergoing thyroidectomy have demonstrated beneficial effects compared with placebo or standard-of-care analgesic regimens. The available evidence suggests that systemic lidocaine infusion is associated with lower postoperative pain scores and a reduced requirement for supplemental opioids. In addition, this intervention has been linked to a reduced incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), thereby diminishing the need for rescue antiemetic therapy. Collectively, these effects may contribute to improved overall quality of recovery (QoR) for patients [15–18].

A previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Khan et al. [19] addressed a similar research question. They found no statistically significant benefit of IV lidocaine on postoperative VAS pain scores or the incidence of PONV. However, this review was restricted to randomised controlled trials published between 2016 and 2023 and included a limited number of studies. While some trials overlap with the present review, differences exist in study selection and the scope of outcomes. Importantly, the present study incorporates evidence published beyond this period and provides a more comprehensive synthesis of randomised controlled trials evaluating IV lidocaine in thyroidectomy. Also, our study was methodologically more robust as we assessed the certainty of evidence of outcomes using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) framework.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to investigate the efficacy and safety of intraoperative IV lidocaine administration in patients undergoing thyroidectomy.

Materials and methods

Study design and protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PRISMA) 2020 statement [20] and adhered to the Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2) criteria [21] (S Tables 1 and 2). The review’s protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on Apr 19 2025 (CRD420251032926).

Literature search and study selection

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases, from their inception until Aug 13 2025. The full search strategy is detailed in (S Table 3). Additionally, two independent reviewers manually screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews to identify any additional publications not captured by the initial database search.

Two independent reviewers conducted the entire study selection process. Initially, these two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify potentially eligible trials. Subsequently, the full texts of these selected articles were obtained and meticulously assessed for final inclusion. Any disagreements that arose at either the screening or full-text assessment stage were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision.

Eligibility criteria and PICOS framework

Studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis if they met predefined eligibility criteria based on the PICOS (Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework. Eligible studies enrolled (P) adult patients aged 18 years or older undergoing elective total thyroidectomy. (I) The intervention of interest was the administration of IV lidocaine during the perioperative period. (C) Studies were required to include a comparison group that did not receive IV lidocaine, such as a placebo, standard anaesthetic, or analgesic care. (O) Outcomes of interest included efficacy measures, such as postoperative pain scores and recovery-related outcomes, as well as safety outcomes, including postoperative adverse events. (S) Only RCTs were considered eligible for inclusion.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted using a standardised, pilot-tested data extraction form. Two reviewers (D.A. and M.A.) independently and blinded to each other extracted data from the included studies, including study characteristics, patient demographics, details of the IV lidocaine intervention and comparator groups, and outcome measures. The extracted data were subsequently cross-checked, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus; when necessary, a third reviewer (E.G.) was consulted. The primary outcome of interest was postoperative pain scores measured at 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively. Pain was evaluated at predefined postoperative time points using validated pain assessment tools, including the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), which measures pain on a 10-cm horizontal line ranging from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst imaginable pain”), the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and Verbal Numeric Rating Scale (VNRS) which quantifies pain severity on an ordinal scale from 0 to 10.

Secondary outcomes included QoR, PONV, the incidence of rescue antiemetics, the duration of anaesthesia, total postoperative opioid consumption, and postoperative analgesia use. Quality of recovery was assessed using the 40-item Quality of Recovery questionnaire (QoR-40), a validated multidimensional instrument encompassing physical comfort, emotional state, physical independence, psychological support, and pain following anaesthesia and surgery. Different types of opioids were all converted to standard oral morphine equivalent (OME) [22]. For studies reporting opioid consumption at separate timepoints postoperatively (PACU and ward), the total sum of both was calculated.

Methodological assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias for each included RCT were independently evaluated by two reviewers for each outcome. This quality assessment was rigorously conducted using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2) [23]. The appraisal systematically evaluated potential biases across five distinct domains: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of the reported result. For each domain, the risk of bias was judged as either “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.” Any conflicts in judgment between the two reviewers were settled through discussion until a consensus was achieved.

Data analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using the ‘meta’ package in R (version 4.4.3; RStudio, Vienna, Austria). For quantitative synthesis, the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) random-effects model was applied when initial analysis indicated moderate or substantial heterogeneity (I² > 40%) to provide more reliable inference in the presence of between-study heterogeneity and a limited number of included trials. Compared with the conventional DerSimonian–Laird approach, the HKSJ method yields more robust standard errors and more appropriate confidence interval coverage, particularly when heterogeneity is substantial or the number of studies is small. This approach was therefore selected a priori to account for anticipated clinical and methodological variability across studies.

The choice of effect measure depended on the outcome type: risk ratios (RRs) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes, whereas mean differences (MDs) or standardised mean differences (SMDs) were used for continuous outcomes measured on the same or different scales, respectively. All pooled estimates are reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also conducted MD sensitivity analyses for pain score outcomes to assess the robustness of our findings and to provide more interpretable estimates of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID).

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and quantified with the I² statistic. Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant when the Q-test p-value was < 0.10 or when I² exceeded 40%. To evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for each meta-analysis, sequentially excluding each study.

In the study by Xu et al. [24], four parallel groups (A, B, C, and D; 60 patients per group) were included, with groups A and B serving as controls and groups C and D receiving IV lidocaine. To avoid unit-of-analysis errors, these data were treated as two independent comparisons: group C versus group A (denoted Xu-A, 2012) and group D versus group B (denoted Xu-B, 2012), and were entered separately into the meta-analysis. Potential sources of heterogeneity were further explored using meta-regression when at least five studies were available for a given outcome. Owing to insufficient data, subgroup analyses were not feasible. For meta-regression, the proportion of female participants in each study was selected as the covariate because it was consistently reported across the included trials and was considered clinically relevant in a predominantly female thyroidectomy population. Other potentially relevant study-level variables, including lidocaine dose, infusion rate, infusion duration, surgical technique, and type of anaesthesia, were considered; however, these variables were not reported with sufficient consistency or detail across the included studies to support reliable meta-regression analyses.

Results are reported as regression coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Trial sequential analysis

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) [25] was conducted using TSA software (version 0.9.5.10 beta) to evaluate whether the accumulated evidence was sufficient and to assess the potential need for further trials. The required information size (RIS) was estimated to account for between-study heterogeneity and random errors, assuming a two-sided type I error of 5% (α = 0.05) and a type II error of 20% (β = 0.20). If the cumulative Z-curve crossed the trial sequential monitoring boundary (TSMB) before reaching the required information size, the evidence was considered conclusive; otherwise, the results were interpreted as inconclusive, indicating that additional adequately powered studies may be necessary. TSAs that could not be reliably performed for certain outcomes were dismissed because the accumulated information size was substantially below the RIS, precluding meaningful construction and interpretation of TSMB.

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of the body of evidence for each outcome was evaluated independently by two reviewers using the (GRADE) approach [26]. Starting with a baseline rating of “high certainty”, the evidence was potentially downgraded based on assessments across five specific domains. RoB was evaluated by considering the methodological limitations of the included studies as determined by the RoB 2 tool. Inconsistency was assessed by examining the heterogeneity of results, specifically looking at the I2 statistic. Indirectness was judged by determining if the evidence directly answered the review’s research question regarding population, intervention, and outcomes. Imprecision was evaluated based on the width of the CI and whether the sample size met the optimal information size. Finally, publication bias was not formally assessed because none of the outcome analyses included 10 or more studies, which is the minimum recommended threshold for reliably evaluating publication bias. The two reviewers met to compare their assessments and resolved any disagreements through discussion, resulting in a final consensus rating.

Results

The initial search yielded 479 records. After duplicates were removed and titles and abstracts were screened, 455 records were excluded. Only 24 full-text reports were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 11 RCTs [10, 15–17, 27–32] were deemed eligible and included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Characteristics of included studies

The 11 included studies comprised a total of 943 patients undergoing thyroidectomy (121 males and 822 females), with a mean age of 46.2 years and a mean operative duration of 84.4 min. The IV lidocaine intervention protocols varied across the trials. Bolus doses ranged from 1.5 mg/kg to 2 mg/kg, and maintenance infusion rates varied from 1.5 mg/kg/h to 3 mg/kg/h. The infusion duration also varied, with some studies continuing until the end of surgery and others, such as Akgul et al. [15], extending it for 1 h postoperatively. Control groups primarily received an equivalent volume of normal saline. A summary of the included studies is presented in Table 1.

Meta-analyses

Primary outcome

Postoperative pain scores at 1 h

The meta-analysis of pain score at 1 h postoperatively included five studies (252 patients in the lidocaine group and 253 in the control group). It demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in pain scores in favour of lidocaine (SMD = -0.77, 95% CI: -0.98 to -0.57, P = 0.0001). This finding was supported by a low heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 16.5%) (Fig. 3A). Meta-regression analysis showed no significant association between the proportion of female participants and the treatment effect (β = 0.0007, 95% CI -0.0221 to 0.0236, P = 0.9334) (S Fig. 1). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 2). The sensitivity analysis using the MD is shown in (S Fig. 3).

Postoperative pain scores at 4 h

At the 4 h time point, a meta-analysis of 5 studies (162 patients in the lidocaine group, 161 in the control group) also revealed a statistically significant decrease in pain scores in the lidocaine group (SMD = -1.46, 95% CI: -2.73 to -0.19, P = 0.0331). However, this analysis was characterised by high heterogeneity (I² = 88.7%, p < 0.0001) (S Fig. 4). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 5). The MD sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 6).

Postoperative pain scores at 8 h

The third analysis of postoperative pain score at 8 h, involving four studies with 119 lidocaine and 118 control patients, found no significant reduction in pain with lidocaine (SMD = -1.00, 95% CI: -2.41 to 0.42, P = 0.1108). High degree of heterogeneity was also observed in this analysis (I² = 87.7%, P < 0.0001) (S Fig. 7). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 8). The MD sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 9).

Postoperative pain scores at 12 h

At the fourth postoperative time point (12 h), data from six studies comprising 401 patients (200 in the lidocaine group and 201 in the control group) were pooled. The meta-analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in postoperative pain, favouring IV lidocaine (SMD − 0.62, 95% CI − 1.20 to − 0.04; P = 0.0400). Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 73.4%, P = 0.0021) (S Fig. 10). Meta-regression analysis showed no significant association between the proportion of female participants and the treatment effect (β = −0.0633, 95% CI − 0.1909 to 0.0644, P = 0.2408) (S Fig. 11). The results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis are presented in (S Fig. 12). The MD sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 13).

Postoperative pain scores at 24 h

The analysis of postoperative pain score at 24 h, which included eight studies (361 patients in the lidocaine group and 364 in the control group), continued to show a statistically significant, albeit smaller, reduction in pain scores (SMD = -0.52, 95% CI: -1.01 to -0.03, P = 0.0417). High heterogeneity remained a prominent feature (I² = 88.2%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3B). Meta-regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between the proportion of female participants and the treatment effect, with higher female proportions associated with greater pain reduction (β = −0.0376, 95% CI − 0.0670 to − 0.0082, P = 0.0193), explaining 56% of the between-study heterogeneity (S Fig. 14). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 15). The MD sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 16).

Postoperative pain scores at 48 h

At the final postoperative time point (48 h), data from three studies comprising 218 patients (107 in the lidocaine group and 111 in the control group) were pooled. The meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in postoperative pain, favoring IV lidocaine (SMD − 0.36, 95% CI − 0.68 to − 0.03; P = 0.0303). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate and not statistically significant (I² = 34.1%, P = 0.2191) (Fig. 3C). The results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis are presented in S Fig. 17, and the mean difference sensitivity analysis is shown in S Fig. 18.

Secondary outcomes

QoR-40 score (day 1)

Five studies were included in the meta-analysis of QoR-40 scores at postoperative day 1, comprising 398 patients (199 in the lidocaine group and 199 in the control group). The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in postoperative quality of recovery, favoring IV lidocaine (MD 6.05, 95% CI 3.23 to 8.88; P < 0.0001). Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed but did not reach statistical significance (I² = 39.7%, P = 0.1565) (Fig. 4A). The results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis are presented in (S Fig. 19).

QoR-40 score (day 2)

At postoperative day 2, the meta-analysis of three studies (121 patients in the lidocaine group, 120 in the control group) demonstrated a continued and robust improvement in the QoR for patients who received lidocaine. The pooled MD was 6.52 (95% CI: 4.97 to 8.06), which was statistically significant (P < 0.0001). Importantly, there was no significant heterogeneity observed among the studies (I² = 0%) (Fig. 4B). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 20).

PONV

Patients receiving IV lidocaine had a significantly lower risk of PONV. The pooled analysis of five studies, including 343 patients (170 in the lidocaine group and 173 in the control group), demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in PONV in favor of IV lidocaine (risk ratio 0.46, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.70; P = 0.0004) (S Fig. 21). This analysis showed no significant heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 22).

Duration of anesthesia

The meta-analysis of eight studies, involving 282 patients in the lidocaine group and 282 in the control group, found no significant difference in the duration of anaesthesia between the lidocaine and control groups (MD: -2.37 min, 95% CI: -6.64 to 1.90; P = 0.2759), with low heterogeneity observed (I²=16.7%) (S Fig. 23). Meta-regression analysis showed no significant association between the proportion of female participants and the treatment effect (β = 0.0794, 95% CI − 0.4538 to 0.6127, P = 0.7280) (S Fig. 24). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 25).

Use of antiemetics

Regarding the need for postoperative antiemetics, the meta-analysis of three studies (109 patients in the lidocaine group and 108 in the control group) found no statistically significant difference between the two groups. The pooled RR was 0.35 (95% CI: 0.10 to 1.28), with the CI crossing the line of no effect (P = 0.1128). This indicates that while the point estimate favoured the lidocaine group, the evidence is not sufficient to conclude a definite benefit of lidocaine in reducing the requirement for antiemetic medication. The analysis showed moderate, but not statistically significant, heterogeneity (I² = 39.6%, p = 0.1911) (S Fig. 26). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 27).

Total postoperative opioid consumption

The meta-analysis of three studies, involving 109 patients in the lidocaine group and 111 in the control group, demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in opioid consumption favouring IV lidocaine (MD: −3.00 OME, 95% CI: −3.73 to − 2.28; P < 0.0001), with moderate heterogeneity observed (I²=37.8%) (S Fig. 28). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 29).

Postoperative analgesia use

The meta-analysis of postoperative analgesia included six studies (207 patients in the lidocaine group and 205 in the control group) and demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in analgesic requirements, favouring IV lidocaine (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.70; P = 0.0002). This analysis showed no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (S Fig. 30). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is shown in (S Fig. 31).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses identified Geun Choi et al. [27] as the primary contributor to heterogeneity in the analyses of postoperative pain at 4, 8, 12, and 48 h. Efremov et al. [28] was the main source of heterogeneity in the analysis of quality of recovery on postoperative day 1 (QoR-40 POD1) and in the analysis of duration of anaesthesia.

Trial sequential analysis

The TSA for postoperative pain at 1 h showed that the cumulative Z-curve crossed both the conventional boundary for statistical significance and the trial sequential monitoring boundary (TSMB) before reaching the required information size (RIS = 352), with an accrued sample size of approximately 341 participants, indicating firm and conclusive evidence for the effectiveness of IV lidocaine compared with control (S Fig. 32). For postoperative pain at 24 h, the cumulative Z-curve crossed the conventional boundary for statistical significance but did not cross the TSMB and remained well below the required information size (725 of 1924 participants), indicating that the observed effect of IV lidocaine on 24-h postoperative pain is inconclusive and may be attributable to random error (S Fig. 33).

For QoR at the first postoperative day, TSA based on a required information size of 616 patients showed that the cumulative Z-curve crossed both the conventional boundary for statistical significance and the TSMB for benefit after approximately 398 patients, indicating firm and conclusive evidence in favour of IV lidocaine (S Fig. 34).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that intraoperative IV lidocaine provides clinically meaningful benefits for adult patients undergoing thyroidectomy. Across 11 RCTs, IV lidocaine was associated with significant reductions in early postoperative pain scores (1, 4, 12, 24, and 48 h), improved quality of recovery (QoR) on postoperative days 1 and 2, and a lower incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), supporting its role as an effective adjunct within multimodal analgesia strategies for thyroidectomy. Although a reduction in PONV was observed, this did not translate into a statistically significant decrease in the requirement for rescue antiemetic therapy.

The analgesic effect of lidocaine infusion demonstrated a clear temporal decline. Although a statistically significant reduction in pain scores persisted at 48 h postoperatively (SMD − 0.36, 95% CI − 0.68 to − 0.03; P = 0.0303), this difference was not considered clinically meaningful. This pattern suggests that while the pharmacological activity of lidocaine may generate a measurable effect beyond 24 h, its practical benefit for patient comfort likely diminishes substantially after the first postoperative day.

This trajectory is supported by findings from a Romanian retrospective observational study by Popa (2025) [33] involving patients undergoing robotic prostatectomy. That study reported reductions in postoperative pain at 0, 4, 12, and 24 h, with statistical significance observed only at 4 h. This pattern is broadly consistent with the present findings, which demonstrated postoperative pain reduction with IV lidocaine at all assessed time points except 8 h, with the most pronounced and statistically significant effect observed at 1 h postoperatively (SMD − 0.77, 95% CI − 0.98 to − 0.57; P < 0.0001). Collectively, these findings might support the concept that lidocaine’s peak analgesic efficacy occurs in the early postoperative period, potentially reflecting its pharmacokinetic profile and higher plasma concentrations shortly after surgery.

Further corroboration is provided by the randomised controlled trial conducted by Guo (2025) [34] in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. In that study, intraoperative IV lidocaine significantly reduced visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores at all assessed postoperative time points (1, 3, and 5 days). These findings align with the broader pattern observed in the present analysis, namely, a progressive decline in postoperative pain over time in patients receiving lidocaine. The results of Guo’s study extend the observed benefit to a longer timeframe and a different surgical context, suggesting that lidocaine’s analgesic utility may not be limited to specific procedure types. However, the magnitude of effect appears to vary over time. It was challenging to interpret the standardised mean difference (SMD) results for pain outcomes in relation to the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Therefore, we performed sensitivity analyses using mean differences (MDs) to better align with an MCID threshold of 0.99 [35]. These analyses demonstrated that, although postoperative pain scores at (1 and 24 h) time points showed statistically significant mean differences, the magnitude of these differences did not reach the MCID of 0.99, indicating that the observed effects were unlikely to be clinically meaningful.

Our findings regarding opioid consumption and rescue analgesia provide crucial context for the pain score data. Even when pain score differences narrowed, patients in the lidocaine group required significantly less rescue analgesia (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.70; P = 0.0002) and consumed lower total volumes of opioids (MD -3.00 OME, 95% CI -3.73 to -2.28; P < 0.0001). This suggests that lidocaine may not only lower reported pain intensity but also effectively blunt the nociceptive drive that necessitates opioid intervention. This opioid-sparing effect is particularly valuable in thyroidectomy, where minimising sedation and nausea facilitates early mobilisation and discharge.

Our analysis resolves a discrepancy found in previous literature regarding PONV. While earlier reviews and individual trials often lacked the power to detect significant differences, our pooled data indicate a robust reduction in PONV risk (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.70; P = 0.0004) for patients receiving lidocaine. This protective effect likely stems from lidocaine’s ability to suppress the gag reflex and modulate sympathetic tone. However, this reduction in symptoms did not translate into a statistically significant decrease in rescue antiemetic use (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.10 to 1.28; P = 0.1128). This may reflect variations in institutional protocols, where prophylaxis is administered routinely regardless of symptoms, or simply a lack of statistical power for this specific secondary endpoint.

These findings provide a necessary update to the recent meta-analysis by Khan (2025) [19], which reported no significant benefits for pain or PONV. The divergence in our results is attributable to the inclusion of recent trials (N = 943 vs. N = 375 in Khan et al.), which substantially increased statistical power. Furthermore, where the previous review was limited by heterogeneity that rendered outcomes inconclusive, our use of sensitivity analyses provides a clearer signal. Specifically, we identified that methodological differences, such as the timing of outcome assessment, were primary drivers of heterogeneity.

IV lidocaine has been increasingly incorporated into Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols because of its analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and opioid-sparing properties [36, 37]. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated its efficacy in reducing postoperative pain and opioid consumption in abdominal and laparoscopic surgeries [38, 39]. Moreover, its application in oncological surgery has expanded, with recent meta-analyses reporting reduced postoperative pain and improved QoR in patients undergoing colorectal surgery for colon cancer and breast cancer surgery [38, 39]. The present meta-analysis suggests that these benefits extend to endocrine surgery, supporting the role of IV lidocaine as an effective analgesic adjunct for patients undergoing thyroidectomy.

The TSA results of our study support a stratified interpretation of the evidence: IV lidocaine’s efficacy for early pain relief is well substantiated, but its role in 24-hour pain management remains unproven and warrants further investigation. On the other hand, the benefit for QoR on the first postoperative day is statistically significant but requires more data for precise estimation. Future research efforts should therefore be directed toward adequately powered RCTs focusing on 24–48 h of pain and QoR to reach information size and solidify these findings.

Clinical implications and future research

The findings of this meta-analysis have important implications for clinical practice. The available evidence suggests that incorporating intraoperative IV lidocaine into the anaesthetic management of thyroidectomy may represent a valuable strategy for improving postoperative outcomes. Its use may help reduce immediate postoperative pain and enhance early recovery, contributing to a smoother and more comfortable postoperative course and potentially facilitating earlier discharge.

Future research should aim to address the limitations identified in this analysis. Large-scale, high-quality randomised controlled trials with standardised lidocaine dosing regimens and control protocols are needed to provide more definitive evidence and reduce heterogeneity. Additionally, future studies should include longer follow-up periods to evaluate the impact of IV lidocaine on outcomes such as chronic postsurgical pain. A more comprehensive assessment of safety outcomes and other clinically relevant endpoints is also necessary to fully characterise the risk–benefit profile of IV lidocaine in patients undergoing thyroidectomy.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several important strengths. It addresses a clinically relevant and timely question regarding the role of IV lidocaine in thyroidectomy, with potential implications for multimodal analgesia and enhanced recovery pathways in endocrine surgery. In addition, the review included only randomised controlled trials, which strengthens the internal validity of the pooled findings, and incorporated a broader and more up-to-date body of evidence than previous reviews on this topic. The analysis was further strengthened by the use of complementary approaches, including meta-regression, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, and trial sequential analysis, which allowed a more detailed assessment of robustness, heterogeneity, and conclusiveness of the available evidence.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the small number of eligible studies and the relatively limited sample sizes may restrict the generalizability of the results. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several pain outcomes, particularly at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h.

Although meta-regression and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed, additional unexplored sources of heterogeneity likely contributed to the variability in pooled estimates. These include differences in lidocaine bolus dose and infusion rate, variation in infusion duration (including whether infusion was continued into the postoperative period), differences in surgical technique such as open and robotic thyroidectomy, variation in anaesthetic and postoperative analgesic protocols, and the use of different pain assessment instruments such as VAS, NRS, and VNRS. These methodological and clinical differences may influence both the magnitude and timing of the observed analgesic effect.

Finally, this review was unable to establish the long-term benefits of IV lidocaine, and reporting of adverse effects was limited across the included trials, precluding definitive conclusions regarding long-term safety. Notably, of the eleven included studies, only six assessed lidocaine-related toxicity, and no cases of toxicity were reported in these six studies. However, given the limited number of studies and the lack of standardised, systematic safety reporting, firm conclusions regarding the short- or long-term safety profile of IV lidocaine cannot be drawn.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the evidence from this systematic review and meta-analysis, IV lidocaine was associated with reduced postoperative pain, nausea, and vomiting, as well as improved QoR, in patients undergoing thyroidectomy. However, the relationship between statistical significance and clinical importance was inconsistent across pain outcomes: some analyses showed statistically significant reductions that did not reach the MCID threshold, whereas others showed mean differences that reached the MCID threshold without achieving statistical significance. Accordingly, the clinical relevance of the analgesic effect should be interpreted with caution. Further high-quality studies are needed to define optimal dosing strategies, long-term outcomes, and the safety profile of IV lidocaine in this setting.
