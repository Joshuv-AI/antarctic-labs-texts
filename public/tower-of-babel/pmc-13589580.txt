Engineered Exosomal miRNAs for Post-Stroke Neural Repair: Mechanisms, Delivery Strategies, and Translational Challenges
Authors: Shuangfeng Xu, Daman Tian, Zhifeng Wang, Jiao Yang, Ting Shi, Junfeng Lan, Hongyan Wu, Jiayi Wu, Manyan Zhao, Xinran Duan, Liwei Xing, Jian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional neurological recovery after ischemic stroke remains a formidable challenge in neuroscience. Exosomes serve as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication, and the microRNAs (miRNAs) they carry, in particular, offer a promising “cell-free therapy” strategy for promoting neurological repair. This review systematically summarizes the multi-target mechanisms by which exosomal miRNAs regulate the neurovascular unit (NVU) and outlines technical strategies to enhance exosomal therapeutic efficacy through targeted modification and engineered cargo loading, with the aim of constructing a smart drug delivery system capable of precise navigation. Following the trajectory of “natural exosomes–engineered exosomes–clinical translation”, this review addresses the core scientific question of how to transform exosomal miRNAs from natural messenger molecules with basic reparative activity into an intelligent therapeutic system for precise targeted delivery. It further explores precision exosome treatment models based on the pathological staging of stroke progression, and integrates single-cell, spatial transcriptomic, and multi-omics technologies to decode the “miRNA–cell source–target cell–signaling pathway” regulatory axis, thereby identifying candidate miRNA combinations with translational potential. Finally, this review provides an in-depth analysis of the core challenges in translating such nanotherapeutic strategies from the bench to the bedside, including manufacturing standardization, quality control, and potency assessment, with the aim of providing a theoretical basis for advancing exosomal miRNA therapies from mechanistic studies to precision stroke treatment.

Introduction

Stroke, also known as “zhongfeng” (wind stroke) in Chinese, is a cerebrovascular disease with a highly complex pathogenesis that severely affects human health, and it is one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide.1 The American Heart Association (AHA) recently reported that the incidence of stroke continues to rise, driven by an aging population and increasing risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. Ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and transient ischemic attack (TIA) are the three main types of stroke, each with distinct underlying causes. Among these, ischemic stroke is the most common type, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases.2 The high disability and mortality rates associated with ischemic stroke are primarily attributed to the limited capacity of the body to repair neurological deficits.3 Although revascularization therapies, represented by intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy, have significantly improved acute-phase prognosis, the majority of survivors still suffer from varying degrees of neurological dysfunction.4 Post-stroke neurological deficits refer to central nervous system damage caused by stroke, leading to sudden focal or diffuse neurological dysfunction, which manifests as multidimensional impairments in motor, sensory, cognitive and mental, visual, swallowing, and balance functions.5 The core pathologies include neuronal death, neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier disruption, and insufficient cerebral blood perfusion.6 Beyond revascularization therapies, rehabilitation training—including physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech-language therapy—constitutes the core management approach for functional improvement in survivors. The AHA’s Guidelines for Adult Stroke Rehabilitation and Recovery explicitly states that the adult central nervous system is plastic and capable of reorganizing to restore impaired functions, and that progressive, skilled motor practice is essential for functional gains at any stage after stroke.7

In recent years, stem cell therapy has garnered significant attention due to its regenerative potential. A growing body of evidence indicates that stem cells exert their therapeutic effects primarily through paracrine mechanisms, with exosomes serving as the key effector vehicles.8 Exosomes are membrane vesicles with a diameter of 30–150 nm that are actively secreted by cells. Composed of a lipid bilayer, they naturally carry bioactive molecules such as cytokines, proteins, RNA, microRNAs (miRNAs), and lipids. Exosomes are widely present in various bodily fluids, including serum, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, and act as “messengers” mediating remote intercellular communication.9,10 Compared with stem cells, exosomes—as a “cell-free therapy” agent—offer distinct advantages, including low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and ease of storage and standardization. This approach circumvents the potential risks of immune rejection and tumorigenicity associated with cell therapy, and avoids specific ethical controversies related to embryonic ethics at the source level in traditional cell therapy.11,12

With the continuous advancement of research, studies on stroke treatment centered on exosomes and their carried miRNAs have made considerable progress, and related review articles have become increasingly abundant. A narrative review published in Neuroscience in 2025 systematically summarized the biogenesis of exosomes, their cargo repertoire, and their translational potential in ischemic stroke, with a focus on elucidating how miRNAs such as miR-124 and miR-21, as well as stress proteins like HSP70, drive mechanisms of neurological repair.13 A review published in Frontiers in Immunology in the same year focused on the regulatory network of exosomes and non-coding RNAs in neuroinflammation after ischemic stroke, particularly emphasizing the ability of exosomes, as natural nanocarriers, to target and modulate the immune-inflammatory cascade.14 Additionally, a review published online in Cellular and Molecular Neurobiology in December 2024 comprehensively reviewed the therapeutic effects of exosomal miRNAs in various central nervous system injury diseases, including ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury, covering dimensions such as pathophysiological mechanisms, animal models, miRNA transfection, and administration routes.15 However, existing reviews generally have the following limitations. First, most treat the mechanisms of exosomal miRNAs as a whole, lacking in-depth analysis of how specific miRNAs precisely regulate key links in post-stroke neural circuit reconstruction, such as synaptic plasticity, axon guidance, and network reconnection. Second, the discussion on design strategies of engineered exosomes and how they achieve targeted delivery and controlled release of miRNAs remains insufficient. Third, most reviews focus on summarizing preclinical evidence and fail to clearly outline the complete path from “laboratory discovery” to “clinical translation.”

Building on this, the present review aims to fill these gaps by focusing on a central scientific question in stroke neurological repair: how can exosomal miRNAs be transformed from natural messenger molecules with basic reparative activity into an intelligent therapeutic system for precise targeted delivery through bioengineering strategies? Adopting the framework of “Mechanism Decoding – Engineering Modification – Clinical Translation,” and as illustrated in (Figure 1), this review analyzes the topic from three perspectives: First, how do exosomal miRNAs act as “molecular switches” to specifically regulate neural circuit reconstruction after stroke? Second, how can engineered modification strategies overcome the bottlenecks of natural exosomes in brain targeting and controlled release? Third, what bottlenecks hinder the clinical translation process? The aim is to provide a new theoretical framework for advancing exosome therapy from mechanism decoding to precision clinical application. Core Mechanisms of Exosomal miRNAs in Post-Stroke Neurological Repair

Biological Characteristics of Exosomal miRNAs

Exosomal miRNAs refer to a class of miRNAs that are selectively packaged into exosomes and transported between cells via exosomes. They are endogenous, non-coding, single-stranded small RNA molecules approximately 18–25 nucleotides in length. They mainly bind to the 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR) of their target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) through complete or partial complementary base pairing, mediating target mRNA degradation or translational repression, thereby negatively regulating target gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.16 The biogenesis of miRNAs follows a cascade of nuclear processing, nuclear-cytoplasmic transport, and cytoplasmic maturation: primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs) are cleaved by Drosha in the nucleus to generate precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs), which are then exported from the nucleus; upon entering the cytoplasm, pre-miRNAs are cleaved by Dicer to form miRNA duplexes, which are further assembled onto Argonaute (AGO) proteins to ultimately generate mature miRNA-induced silencing complexes (miRISCs) with gene-silencing activity. A single miRNA can regulate hundreds of target genes and is involved in almost all biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, inflammation, and metabolism, playing a crucial role in post-stroke injury and repair.17 Initially, exosomal miRNAs attracted attention as diagnostic biomarkers for diseases. In recent years, with the deepening of research, the role of exosomal miRNAs has expanded from biomarkers to a dual role as both “therapeutic vectors” and “regulatory targets.” Preclinical studies suggest that exosomal miRNAs may act through a complex regulatory network, synergistically targeting neurons, microglia, astrocytes, and vascular endothelial cells, to exert anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and pro-synaptogenic effects.

Exosomal miRNAs Regulate the Neuroinflammatory Microenvironment

Neuroinflammation is a key driver of secondary brain injury after stroke, and uncontrolled inflammatory responses exacerbate neuronal death and suppress endogenous repair. After ischemic stroke, multiple cell types within the neurovascular unit (NVU)—including neurons, microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, and neural stem cells—mediate complex bidirectional communication via exosomal miRNAs.18 Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), require a balanced activation state; exosomal miRNAs exert immunomodulatory effects by inducing the phenotypic shift of microglia from the pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype to the anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype.

MiR-146a-5p produced by microglia themselves plays a key negative feedback regulatory role in this process. In vivo animal studies showed that the core function of miR-146a-5p is to suppress the excessive activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which is achieved by directly targeting the mRNAs of two key adaptor proteins in this pathway—interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1) and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6).19 IRAK is an important kinase linking the receptor to downstream signaling molecules in the TLR4 signaling pathway, and its activation further activates TRAF6. TRAF6, acting as a ubiquitin ligase, catalyzes the ubiquitination and degradation of the NF-κB inhibitor (IκB), releasing NF-κB into the nucleus to initiate the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. Additional cell-based experiments revealed that miR-146a-5p binds complementarily to the 3′-UTR of IRAK1 and TRAF6 mRNAs, promoting mRNA degradation or inhibiting their translation, which directly reduces the expression levels of these two proteins. When miR-146a-5p expression is elevated, the protein levels of IRAK1 and TRAF6 are significantly reduced, severing the signaling bridge between TLR4 and the downstream NF-κB activation complex. Consequently, even if TLR4 continues to be stimulated, the signal cannot be effectively transmitted downstream, thereby attenuating the sustained activation of NF-κB and significantly reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.20

MiR-124 is a highly enriched miRNA in the CNS, accounting for approximately 20–30% of the total miRNAs in the brain, and it plays a crucial role in neuronal differentiation, synaptic plasticity, and maintenance of immune homeostasis.21 Animal experiments suggest that miR-124 regulates post-stroke neuroinflammation through two pathways. On the one hand, miR-124 can directly bind to the 3′-UTR of TLR4 mRNA, or target the mRNAs of the downstream TLR4 adaptor proteins myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88), interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4), and TRAF6, promoting their degradation or translational repression. This severs the coupling between TLR4 and NF-κB activation, indirectly blocking the phosphorylation and degradation of the NF-κB inhibitor alpha (IκBα) by IκB kinase (IKK), thereby retaining NF-κB in the cytoplasm and preventing it from entering the nucleus to initiate pro-inflammatory gene transcription.22 Simultaneously, it can also activate the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT repair pathway. On the other hand, studies based on a middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) mouse model have shown that exogenous supplementation with miR-124 significantly increases neuronal survival at 48 hours after stroke, promotes M2-like polarization of microglia/macrophages, and markedly reduces the infarct core bordered by reactive astrocytes over time, with improvements in neurological deficits observed within the first week after injury.23 Additionally, other animal experiments have shown that miR-124 carried by exosomes secreted from M2 microglia can induce the differentiation of neural stem cells into neurons through the AP2-associated protein kinase 1 (AAK1)/Notch signaling pathway, thereby facilitating neurological recovery after stroke.24 Notably, direct evidence for miR-124 promoting M2 polarization of microglia mainly comes from MCAO mouse models and cell experiments. Correlations between changes in miR-124 levels and prognosis in human stroke samples have been reported; for example, the serum exosomal miR-124 level in patients with ischemic stroke is positively correlated with NIHSS scores and infarct volume.25 However, direct regulation of microglial phenotype switching by miR-124 still lacks histological verification in humans. Combined evidence from cell and animal experiments suggests that miR-124 derived from mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-exos) can target and inhibit phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) mRNA, relieving its negative regulation on PI3K and activating downstream AKT signaling. Activated AKT phosphorylates transcription factors such as glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) and forkhead box O1 (FoxO1), suppressing the expression of M1 marker genes including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and CD86, while simultaneously activating M2 key transcription factors such as signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), upregulating anti-inflammatory factors including arginase-1 (Arg-1), CD206, chitinase-like protein 3 (Ym1), and interleukin-10 (IL-10), thereby driving microglial polarization toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype. This pathway has been validated in a traumatic brain injury (TBI) model,26 but its applicability in a rat MCAO model remains to be further confirmed.

Exosomes secreted by M2 microglia also exhibit remarkable neurorepair activity. Researchers from Xiangya Hospital of Central South University revealed that M2 microglia-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) deliver miR-25-3p and miR-93-5p to target and inhibit three key molecules—transforming growth factor-beta receptor (TGFBR), PTEN, and forkhead box O3 (FOXO3)—based on a rat MCAO model.27 This blocks the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) pro-scarring pathway, activates the PI3K/AKT proliferation-promoting pathway, and releases FOXO3-mediated cell cycle arrest, ultimately activating NSC proliferation and directional neuronal differentiation, inhibiting glial scar formation, and significantly promoting neurological recovery after ischemic stroke. Additionally, miR-124 and miR-137 carried by M2 microglial exosomes can target ubiquitin-specific protease 14 (USP14) and Notch1, respectively, thereby inhibiting neuronal apoptosis and alleviating ischemic injury.28,29 Concurrently, caution is warranted because miR-124, as a key molecule for maintaining the microglial quiescent phenotype, may induce excessive quiescence upon exogenous intervention, thereby weakening the brain’s innate immune surveillance capacity and posing potential therapeutic risks.

The biological effects of exosomal miRNAs vary significantly depending on their cellular origin. Astrocyte-derived exosomal miR-190b inhibits autophagy and attenuates neuronal apoptosis by targeting autophagy-related 7 (Atg7);30 however, reactive astrocytes can also promote glial scar formation and impede axonal regeneration.18 Oligodendrocyte-derived exosomes enhance neuronal survival by delivering antioxidant enzymes and activating the Akt/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway,31 while neural stem cell-derived exosomal miR-128-3p promotes myelin regeneration by targeting activin A receptor type 1 (ACVR1)/bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), establishing a functional coupling of “neuronal protection–myelin repair”32 miR-21 serves as a classic example of a single miRNA exhibiting cell-type-dependent effects. In neurons, it exerts anti-apoptotic protection by targeting Fas ligand (FASLG). In astrocytes and endothelial cells, it increases BBB leakage via the ERK/matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) pathway. In macrophages, it exerts anti-inflammatory effects by targeting programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4) to activate NF-κB, yet its targeting of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) also suppresses M2 polarization. Furthermore, the net effect of miR-21 is highly dependent on the disease phase—the acute Phase Is dominated by anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory actions, whereas the detrimental effects on BBB integrity and the suppression of M2 polarization may gradually emerge during the subacute phase.33

In summary, the design of exosomal miRNA therapeutic strategies should not be based solely on pathway activation or inhibition effects as the sole criterion, but should also comprehensively evaluate multiple factors—including target cell type, disease stage, and the local microenvironment of the lesion—thereby avoiding the off-target risk of adversely affecting immune and stromal cells while simultaneously protecting neurons.

Exosomal miRNAs Directly Protect Neurons and Promote Neurogenesis

Neurogenesis refers to the entire process by which neural stem cells (NSCs) or neural progenitor cells (NPCs), under the regulation of a specific microenvironment, undergo proliferation, directional differentiation, migration, and ultimately mature into functional neurons and integrate into existing neural circuits. Therefore, promoting post-stroke neurogenesis involves not only increasing the number of newborn neurons but, more importantly, enhancing their survival rate, dendritic spine formation, and functional circuit integration capacity. Exosomal miRNAs may directly protect the structural integrity of neural circuits by inhibiting neuronal apoptosis, alleviating oxidative stress, and promoting the activation of endogenous NSCs.

Selenium is a key component of antioxidant enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR). Selenization can increase the selenoprotein content in exosomes, thereby directly enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) clearance. Animal studies demonstrated that selenized neural stem cell-derived exosomes (NSC-exos) delivered miRNAs to inhibit Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)-mediated nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) degradation, activate the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling pathway, and enhance the nuclear stability and translocation of Nrf2.34 This, in turn, upregulates the expression of downstream antioxidant genes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), thereby improving the cell’s capacity to clear ROS, ultimately alleviating oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage and promoting neurological recovery after traumatic central nervous system injury. Additionally, human neural progenitor cell-derived exosomes (hNPC-exos) have emerged as an ideal delivery vector for brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) in the ischemic brain region, as they carry neurodevelopment-related miRNAs, neurotrophic factors, and homing molecules that target the BBB. In cell experiments, hNPC-exos were efficiently loaded with miR-100-5p via electroporation. After delivery to oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R)-treated BMECs, miR-100-5p significantly downregulated NOX4 expression (reducing mRNA by 60% and protein by 55%), blocked NADPH oxidase-mediated ROS generation, restored OGD/R-induced ROS levels to near normal, decreased the apoptosis rate by 50%, and recovered cell viability by 80%.35 Zou et al (2025) confirmed, using a rat spinal cord injury (SCI) model and an H2O2-induced PC12 cell injury model, that NSC-exos deliver miR-218a-5p to target Bmi1, promote the ubiquitination and degradation of Mettl3, reduce the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation modification of Alox12 mRNA, inhibit Alox12 protein expression, and ultimately block the ferroptosis cascade, thereby improving motor function in SCI rats.36 It should be specifically noted that the mechanistic evidence for miR-218a-5p inhibiting ferroptosis currently originates from SCI models rather than stroke models. Because SCI and ischemic stroke share pathological mechanisms such as ischemia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascades, the potential regulatory role of this pathway in neuronal ferroptosis after stroke requires further validation in stroke models such as MCAO. It is not appropriate to directly assert its neuroprotective effect in stroke at this time.

In addition to the roles of miR-100-5p and miR-218a-5p described above, miR-210 is a highly conserved hypoxia-regulated miRNA. Animal studies in a focal cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) mouse model showed that miR-210 overexpression increased the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells and microvessel density in the peri-infarct area, while also elevating the number of neural progenitor cells in the subventricular zone (SVZ) of the lateral ventricle.37 The underlying mechanism may partly involve the suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1)–STAT3–vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF-C) pathway. In a mouse model of vascular dementia (VD) induced by chronic hypoperfusion, hypoxia-induced NSC-derived extracellular vesicles delivered miR-210 to target Sprouty-related EVH1 domain-containing protein 1 (SPRED1), activating the Ras/Raf-1 signaling pathway. This promoted the survival and neurite outgrowth of OGD-treated neurons, and enhanced cognitive function, neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and dendritic spine density in VD mice.38 Based on current evidence, direct evidence for miR-210 promoting neurogenesis and angiogenesis comes primarily from cerebral ischemia/hypoxia animal models, and its causal contribution in human stroke samples remains to be clinically validated.

Promoting Angiogenesis and Neurovascular Coupling

The core pathology of ischemic stroke is the ischemic cascade injury triggered by cerebral blood flow interruption, which includes neuronal death, BBB disruption, and neurological deficits. Angiogenesis is a critical step in repairing microcirculation in the ischemic area, restoring blood perfusion, and promoting nerve regeneration. It is characterized by the proliferation and migration of microvascular endothelial cells and the formation of new capillaries. Previous studies have shown that exosomes can effectively promote angiogenesis in the ischemic region after stroke. Animal experiments demonstrated that neural stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (NSC-EVs) loaded into hyaluronic acid adhesive hydrogels and injected into the ischemic area of MCAO mice via a controlled release system delivered VEGF, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and miR-126. This activated endothelial cell function, significantly increased the expression of microvascular markers von Willebrand factor (vWF) and cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31), promoted angiogenesis in the ischemic area, restored cerebral blood flow, and improved neurological function at the animal level.39 Similarly, a study based on a TBI animal model in the same year showed that exosomes loaded into hyaluronic acid hydrogels, through the bioadhesion, controlled release function, and pro-angiogenic microenvironment of the hydrogel, synergistically delivered pro-angiogenic factors and miRNAs. This activated the PI3K/AKT and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathways in endothelial cells, upregulated the expression of microvascular markers such as vWF and CD31, promoted angiogenesis after TBI, restored cerebral blood flow to 65%, and ultimately synergistically enhanced neurogenesis and improved neurological function.40 Preclinical evidence suggests that miR-126 from endothelial progenitor cell (EPC)-derived exosomes can alter axonal growth capacity, and enhanced neurogenesis has been observed in vivo, exerting a protective effect on neural cells.41 In human samples, serum miR-126 levels in patients with ischemic stroke were significantly reduced, while miR-21 levels were significantly elevated, suggesting that miR-126 may be associated with the pathological process of stroke.42 However, evidence for its efficacy as a therapeutic exosomal cargo in humans is still insufficient.

Neurovascular coupling (NVC), also known as functional hyperemia, refers to the core physiological mechanism by which the activities of neurons and glial cells precisely regulate the diameter and blood flow of adjacent microvessels through local signaling cascades, dynamically matching the delivery of oxygen and glucose with local metabolic demands on a timescale of seconds. In brain tissue, NVC is primarily coordinated by the NVU: upon neuronal activation, astrocytic endfeet release diffusible factors such as epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), nitric oxide (NO), and prostaglandins through Ca2+ signaling,43 inducing relaxation of smooth muscle in penetrating arterioles and pericyte relaxation, thereby transiently increasing local cerebral blood flow (CBF); conversely, vascular tone is restored when metabolic demand decreases.44 After ischemic stroke, structural damage to the NVU and imbalance of inflammatory mediators can lead to “neurovascular uncoupling,” characterized by loss of vascular reactivity, no-reflow phenomenon, or hyperperfusion after the recovery of neuronal electrical activity, which constitutes an important pathological basis for limited neurological recovery and delayed cognitive impairment after stroke. Exosome-mediated miRNA transfer plays a central communicative role in multicellular interactions within the NVU, providing microenvironmental support for NVU homeostasis reconstruction and the recovery of neurovascular coupling.18 At the mechanistic level, the roles of exosomal miRNAs can be summarized into four interconnected steps. First, intercellular communication maintains cerebrovascular integrity. Neurons can deliver miR-132 to brain microvascular endothelial cells by secreting exosomes containing miR-132, targeting eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase (eEF2K) to upregulate the expression of vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin), thereby maintaining cerebrovascular integrity.45,46 Second, multi-cellular components exhibit coordinated regulation. Exosomal miRNAs from different cell types exhibit dependent regulation within the NVU: miR-19a, miR-21, and miR-146a in endothelial cell-derived exosomes can inhibit the expression of pro-thrombotic and BBB leakage-related proteins such as TLR4, intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), thereby increasing CBF and reducing BBB disruption; miR-92b-3p released by astrocytes under ischemic preconditioning or repair conditions can alleviate neuronal apoptosis induced by OGD; and miR-124 carried by microglial exosomes can inhibit neuronal inflammation and promote neurite outgrowth.47,48 Third, exosomal miRNAs consolidate the anatomical basis of coupling by promoting angiogenesis and vascular stability. Fourth, exosomal miRNAs maintain the selective permeability barrier of coupling by regulating BBB integrity. It should be noted that direct functional evidence for exosomal miRNAs in neurovascular coupling remains relatively limited at present, and most studies use surrogate endpoints such as CBF improvement, angiogenesis, BBB integrity, and NVU reconstruction for evaluation.

Overall, serving as key messengers in post-stroke intercellular communication, exosomal miRNAs exert their neurological repair value not through a linear action on a single target, but through the systemic remodeling of the microenvironment across the multi-tiered pathological processes within the NVU. Comparison of Exosomes with Other Neurological Repair Strategies

Neurological repair following ischemic stroke is a complex process involving pathological events across multiple spatiotemporal stages. A single intervention often struggles to address the full spectrum of needs, ranging from salvage in the acute phase to remodeling during the repair phase. Currently, strategies such as cell transplantation, biomaterial scaffolds, neuromodulation, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), and novel neuroprotective agents are continuously evolving. Moreover, the cross-integration of multimodal strategies is emerging as a new trend in the field. These approaches differ significantly in their biological essence and translational bottlenecks. Cell transplantation relies on cell replacement and paracrine effects; however, it is hindered by immune rejection, the risk of tumorigenicity, and poor penetration of the BBB. Biomaterial scaffolds can provide physical support and a sustained-release microenvironment, but they often require invasive implantation. Consequently, their long-term biocompatibility and the pace of clinical implementation tend to lag behind. Neuromodulation and BCIs primarily regulate neural network excitability and induce functional compensation via electrical or magnetic signals. However, they do not directly mediate structural tissue regeneration, and they are often limited by high inter-individual response heterogeneity and a ceiling effect in efficacy. Although conventional neuroprotective drugs target the acute ischemic cascade, they are constrained by an extremely narrow therapeutic time window and poor BBB penetration. As a result, the positive conversion rate in past Phase III clinical trials has been relatively low.

In contrast, exosomes, as endogenous cell-free nano-messengers, possess multiple distinct biological advantages and demonstrate outstanding translational potential in the process of post-stroke neurological repair. To date, a limited number of extracellular vesicle-based products have advanced to early-phase clinical investigation. Therefore, understanding the boundary of strengths and limitations of each strategy and their spatiotemporal complementarity is of great significance for optimizing clinical translation pathways and resource allocation. As summarized in (Table 1), the present review integrates systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, preclinical studies, and registered data from ClinicalTrials.gov published in high-impact journals from 2025 to 2026, providing a multi-dimensional comparison between exosomes and the aforementioned strategies. Human clinical evidence is prioritized as the evaluation dimension, supplemented by high-quality preclinical studies when clinical data are lacking; safety and translational stage are graded and assessed based on the latest progress of registered trials. It should be specifically emphasized that the efficacy evidence for exosome therapy in ischemic stroke still primarily comes from preclinical animal models. Existing early-phase trials mainly focus on safety validation and exploratory assessment of preliminary efficacy signals, which is insufficient to establish its independent superior efficacy position beyond standard acute-phase treatments such as reperfusion therapy. Application Prospects of Multi-Omics Integration in Target Cell Identification and Neurological Functional Remodeling

In recent years, advances in single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics (ST), and multi-omics integration technologies have provided unprecedented technical means for dissecting exosomal miRNA-mediated intercellular communication after stroke. However, their practical efficacy in identifying target cells and mechanisms of action still requires careful evaluation.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) resolves cell-type-specific responses. Bormann et al (2024) employed snRNA-seq and discovered that OPCs, mature oligodendrocytes, and reactive astrocytes are the glial populations exhibiting the most pronounced transcriptional perturbations after stroke. Notably, this study also revealed immune–glia interactions between OPN+ myeloid cells and CD44+ OPCs as well as reactive astrocytes in the peri-infarct area. However, snRNA-seq primarily captures nuclear transcripts, presenting an inherent limitation in reflecting cytoplasmic components such as exosomal miRNAs. Moreover, most current studies are still confined to rodent models, and their translational value to human pathological conditions remains unclear.60

ST enables the localization of cellular interaction axes. Han et al (2024) integrated ST with single-cell RNA sequencing and performed transcriptomic sequencing on 19,777 spatial spots in the ischemic mouse hemisphere. They identified that the LGALS9–CD44 axis derived from microglia/macrophages can promote oligodendrocyte differentiation and remyelination. This study not only established the LGALS9–CD44 axis as a trans-cellular molecular pathway, but further demonstrated through functional validation that EV-mediated delivery targeting this axis significantly promotes remyelination and long-term motor function recovery, providing direct causal evidence for exosomal miRNA-mediated neurological repair.61 In another study, Zucha et al (2024) constructed a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of glial cell responses after acute cerebral ischemia.62 However, the spatial resolution of current ST technologies is still insufficient to directly locate exosomes derived from individual cells and their target cells, and functional validation remains inadequate.

In addition, He et al (2024) reported the SEVtras algorithm in Nature Methods. Leveraging droplet-based single-cell RNA sequencing data, this algorithm identifies sEV-containing droplets at droplet resolution and estimates single-cell sEV secretion activity (ESAI) without prior sEV isolation.63 This method provides a new avenue for mining extracellular vesicle secretion information from existing single-cell transcriptomic datasets. It should be noted that SEVtras primarily relies on the transcriptomic features of sEV gene sets to indirectly infer the presence and secretion activity of sEVs; it cannot yet directly resolve the specific miRNA cargo composition within sEVs or their cell-origin specificity. Meanwhile, the original validation was mainly based on 15 normal tissue types and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from four tumors, including colorectal and pancreatic cancers. ESAI has been shown to serve as a potential indicator in the early stages of tumor progression. Therefore, the applicability and accuracy of this method in complex neuropathological samples such as stroke still remain to be independently validated. Nevertheless, SEVtras has achieved, for the first time, an unbiased estimation of single-cell sEV secretion activity at the droplet level, greatly expanding the potential to mine exosomal functional clues from existing single-cell datasets, and laying the foundation for subsequent integration with techniques such as miRNA in situ hybridization to lock in specific cargos.

In theory, integrating snRNA-seq, exosomal miRNA sequencing, ST, and Argonaute 2 cross-linking immunoprecipitation (Ago2-CLIP)/ribosome footprinting analysis enables the systematic inference of the “miRNA–cell of origin–target cell–pathway axis.” Work by Boudreau et al (2018) has demonstrated that miRNA networks regulate the transcriptome in a cell-type-specific manner during development.64 However, the practical application of multi-omics integration in stroke exosome research is still in its early exploratory stage. Heterogeneity across different omics data layers, batch effects, and the resolution mismatch between ST and single-cell data all pose technical bottlenecks. In addition, miRNA–target cell relationships inferred from integrative analysis still require validation through functional experiments one by one, which is limited in throughput.

In summary, combining single-cell resolution transcriptomics, ST, and exosomal multi-omics has the potential to provide a more refined cellular and spatial context for interpreting the bidirectional effects of the same miRNA in different cells, thereby offering methodological support for precisely anchoring stroke-specific dominant miRNA–target cell pairs and avoiding off-target damage. However, it must be soberly recognized that a significant gap still exists between correlative inference at the transcriptomic level and causal confirmation at the functional level. The precise design of exosomal miRNA therapies ultimately still relies on rigorous in vitro and in vivo functional experiments for validation and calibration.

Stroke-Specific Exosomal miRNA Profiles

In recent years, accumulating preclinical evidence has demonstrated that exosomal miRNAs derived from different cell types exert multi-temporal and multi-target repair effects after ischemic stroke—ranging from inhibiting neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in the acute phase to promoting neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and even axonal remodeling during the repair phase, ultimately improving neurological outcomes.65 Based on current studies, MSCs are currently the most extensively studied cell source in the field of exosomal miRNA therapy for stroke. Their secreted exosomes are enriched with bioactive molecules such as miR-126, miR-210, miR-21, and miR-214-3p, mediating most of the reported therapeutic benefits, and have distinct advantages in safety compared with traditional living cell transplantation. Furthermore, exosomal miRNAs derived from NSCs, endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), and reactive astrocytes have also demonstrated significant repair potential in different stroke models.

Depending on the repair target, miRNAs derived from exosomes of various stem cells and brain-resident cells exhibit a diversified profile: miR‑126 and miR‑210 are enriched in promoting angiogenesis and neurogenesis; miR‑133b and miR‑27a focus on axonal/dendritic remodeling; while miR‑124 and miR‑146a‑5p primarily target the negative regulation of inflammation. As summarized in (Table 2), the key exosomal miRNAs with therapeutic potential in stroke repair and their functions are presented. It should be emphasized that the same miRNA can exhibit entirely different functional orientations depending on the cell source, delivery vector, and pathological stage of stroke. This suggests that high caution is required in the therapeutic design and targeting selection of exosomal miRNAs, to avoid “single‑target linear extrapolation” detached from the specific microenvironment. Synergistic and Antagonistic Interaction Networks of Exosomal miRNAs

The biological effects of exosomal miRNAs are not a linear superposition of single molecules, but rather the result of multiple miRNAs synergistically or antagonistically regulating the same or parallel pathways within the same exosomal cargo. This characteristic profoundly influences their therapeutic efficacy and clinical translation potential.

In terms of synergy, exosomal miRNAs from different cell sources can jointly promote neurological repair. Exosomes derived from M2 microglia regulate NSC fate by targeting the TGFBR/PTEN/FOXO3 pathway via the miR-25-3p/miR-93-5p axis.27 Meanwhile, miR-125a-5p in NSC-derived extracellular vesicles acts as an interactive signal to coordinate microglial polarization, neuronal repair, and inflammatory microenvironment remodeling.80 Studies have shown that miR-223-3p in mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes inhibits microglial M1 polarization by downregulating cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 2 (CysLT2R); whereas miR-146a-5p in human umbilical cord MSC (hucMSC)-derived exosomes alleviates microglia-mediated neuroinflammation by inhibiting the IRAK1/TRAF6 pathway. Building on this, Cun et al81 further elucidated in a systematic review that exosomes are key mediators of the bidirectional interaction between inflammation and angiogenesis after stroke. The aforementioned regulatory effects targeting microglia represent the core link in exosome-mediated anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic crosstalk, providing a more systematic mechanistic framework for the application potential of exosomes in stroke treatment. These two pathways act in parallel on the regulation of microglial polarization, suggesting that combined targeting may yield synergistic benefits; however, their synergistic effects still require further experimental validation.

Based on a comprehensive evaluation across four dimensions—strength of mechanistic evidence, multi-pathway synergistic gain, target accessibility and safety, and clinical-grade/trial relevance—the synergistic combination of miR-126 and miR-210 currently demonstrates the relatively most definitive translational potential. The two have the most robust and complementary mechanistic evidence, covering “vascular stabilization” and “hypoxia response/neurogenesis,” respectively, and their synergistic gain has been experimentally validated in a MCAO model. Xu et al (2023) published a study in Experimental Neurology that systematically compared the effects of endothelial progenitor cell-derived exosomes (EPC-EXs) and neural progenitor cell-derived exosomes (NPC-EXs), alone or in combination, on OGD/R-injured neurons and permanent MCAO (pMCAO) mice. The results showed that the combination of EPC-EXs miR-126 and NPC-EXs miR-210 produced a stepwise synergistic gain in OGD/R neurons and pMCAO mouse models—its protective effect was not only superior to that of single exosomes but also significantly better than that of conventional NPC-EXs + EPC-EXs combined intervention. Mechanistically, miR-126 inhibits ROS-mediated oxidative damage by downregulating NADPH oxidase 2 (Nox2), while miR-210 promotes neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity by upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and activating tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB). The two pathways act in parallel, and this synergistic gain can be partially reversed by the TrkB inhibitor k-252a. This experimental evidence provides direct support for the synergistic gain of these two miRNAs, but it should be noted that the observation time window of this study was 24 hours, and long-term effects remain to be validated.82 Furthermore, EPC-exos have entered the early clinical exploration stage, and both are endogenous miRNAs delivered via exosomes with controllable safety. Attention should be paid to the temporal characteristics of the early decline of miR-210 in the peripheral blood of stroke patients, and the treatment window should be positioned in the subacute phase. In addition, the miR-17-92 cluster and miR-133b have shown promising prospects in the direction of “neural plasticity reconstruction.” Chen and Chopp pointed out that exosomes can directly transfer their miRNA cargo to recipient neurons to regulate neural remodeling. Among these, modified MSC exosomes overexpressing the miR-17-92 cluster promote recipient neuronal axonal growth by inhibiting PTEN and activating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway; whereas MSC exosomes overexpressing miR-133b can significantly enhance post-ischemic brain plasticity and neurological recovery. Further studies have shown that miR-17-92 cluster-enriched exosomes can also simultaneously promote oligodendrogenesis, neurogenesis, and neurite remodeling through the PTEN/Akt/mTOR/glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) axis, demonstrating therapeutic prospects in neural plasticity reconstruction.83 However, these pieces of evidence still mainly rely on preclinical rodent models, and causal validation in humans is insufficient. Therefore, the miR-17-92 cluster and miR-133b are more suitable as extended cargo candidates for “neural plasticity reconstruction,” rather than the preferred combination with the highest current translational certainty.

Meanwhile, functional antagonism can also exist between miRNAs. Different targets within the same miRNA family or the same pathway may exhibit opposite outputs depending on the cellular state, ischemic phase, and target gene context. The most typical example comes from neuron-derived exosomes—ischemic stress triggers neurons to release sEVs enriched with miR-100-5p. These vesicles, on the one hand, aberrantly activate adjacent neurons and expand the scope of neuronal damage, and on the other hand, drive microglial activation and exacerbate neuroinflammation. Mechanistically, miR-100-5p binds to and activates Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) through its U18U19G20 motif, thereby activating the NF-κB pathway. Knocking down miR-100-5p expression using an antagomiR can inhibit neuroinflammation, reduce infarct volume, and improve post-stroke outcomes in mice.84 This finding reveals that the same exosomal miRNA can simultaneously exert damage-amplifying effects between neurons and immune cells, constituting a typical positive feedback loop in the pathological cascade. Furthermore, the context-dependency of miRNA function further complicates the intricacy of network regulation. Wei et al noted in a review that miR-15a/16-1 is significantly upregulated after stroke and correlates with the severity of neuronal damage. Its effects exhibit an age-sex interaction—young female mice show the strongest response to antagonistic therapy, while the effect is attenuated in aged or male animals. Circulating exosomal miR-30c-2-3p is specifically upregulated in the plasma of patients with acute ischemic stroke, establishing a pro-inflammatory network by targeting SMAD2, a core anti-inflammatory component of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) pathway; in the large artery atherosclerotic subtype, exosome-derived miR-Novel-3 simultaneously drives neuroinflammation and ferroptosis via the striatin (STRN)–PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis.14 These findings suggest that the net effect of the exosomal miRNA interaction network is not determined by the simple “activation or inhibition” of a single miRNA, but is jointly shaped by multiple factors, including target cell identity, disease phase, age, sex, and comorbidities. Therefore, the cargo design of engineered exosomes should not simply pursue “miRNA stacking,” but should instead rationally combine miRNAs based on the principles of synergistic gain in target networks and the minimization of off-target/antagonistic risks.

In summary, the synergistic and antagonistic interaction network of exosomal miRNAs is one of the core characteristics of stroke pathological regulation. Understanding the dynamic balance of this network, rather than pursuing the simple activation or inhibition of a single miRNA, should be the fundamental starting point for the design of future therapeutic strategies. However, current understanding of the true topological structure of this network in the human body and its dynamic evolution patterns remains very limited. There is still a long road of validation ahead from network description to network intervention.

Clinically Investigated Exosome Therapies for Stroke

The clinical translation of exosome therapy in the field of stroke is still in its early exploratory stage, but in recent years, multiple clinical trials have been launched or completed, marking that this field is gradually moving from preclinical research to clinical validation. To date, multiple Phase I/II clinical trials have been launched worldwide. Representative products include NouvSoma001 (NCT06612710), an induced neural stem cell-derived exosome, and an adipose stem cell exosome nasal spray (NCT07398612). Routes of administration include intravenous injection, intranasal spray, or instillation; cell sources involve different lineages such as induced neural stem cells (iNSCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), MSCs, and adipose-derived stem cells. These early trials primarily validate safety and preliminary efficacy signals. To date, no exosome therapy has been formally approved for marketing by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or equivalent regulatory agencies, and clinical efficacy evidence is still in the accumulation stage. The advancement of these clinical trials provides important safety data and feasibility evidence for the translation of exosome therapy from the laboratory to the clinic, and also lays the foundation for the clinical translation of subsequent precision therapies using engineered exosomes. A detailed summary of clinical trial progress and bottlenecks in clinical-grade translation will be systematically discussed in Chapter 4.

In summary, the preceding sections systematically reviewed the mechanisms of exosomal miRNAs in anti-inflammation, anti-apoptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and neurogenesis, and based on this, selected cargo combinations with synergistic gain potential, such as miR-126 and miR-210. However, “controllability” at the mechanistic level is not equivalent to “deliverability” at the therapeutic level. Mechanistic studies answer “what to deliver,” while engineering modifications answer “how to deliver efficiently, specifically, and controllably.” Therefore, the following text will focus on engineered exosomes.

Engineered Exosomes: from Natural Messengers to Intelligent Therapeutic Systems

The BBB, a physiological protective barrier of the CNS, is formed by BMECs through tight junctions, which strictly restrict the entry of macromolecules from the bloodstream into the brain parenchyma. The core advantage of natural exosomes lies in their “natural” attributes—derived from the secretion of autologous or allogeneic cells, they require no chemical conjugation or genetic manipulation. Their biological background is relatively well-defined between batches, exhibiting low immunogenicity, abundant long-term safety data, and relatively straightforward manufacturing and quality control processes. However, natural exosomes have three inherent limitations: first, statistics indicate that the efficiency of unmodified exosomes in crossing the BBB is less than 5%, indicating a lack of active brain-targeting capability; second, the copy number of therapeutic miRNAs within a single exosome is extremely low, making it difficult to deliver nucleic acid cargo at pharmacologically effective doses; third, natural exosomes suffer from low yield and high heterogeneity, and their cargo composition is significantly affected by the donor, passage number, and culture conditions, making it challenging to achieve inter-batch functional consistency. Engineered modifications are designed to compensate for these shortcomings. Through membrane-targeted modification, cargo reconfiguration, delivery system improvement, and the development of novel sources, brain targeting efficiency, drug-loading capacity, local retention time, and source scalability can be enhanced either individually or synergistically. As illustrated in (Figure 3), natural exosomes can be transformed into intelligent therapeutic delivery systems tailored for central nervous system diseases, enabling efficient trans-BBB targeted drug delivery and facilitating clinical translation. However, it must be soberly recognized that each type of engineering strategy, while “addressing these shortcomings,” also introduces new risk variables. For instance, targeted modifications may compromise membrane integrity, and implanted materials such as hydrogels carry the risk of neurotoxicity from degradation products. Targeted Modification

Currently, exosome functionalization strategies primarily focus on two approaches: targeted modification and functional molecule loading. Targeted modification mainly leverages the modifiable properties of the exosome membrane surface. By conjugating specific ligands such as rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptide, inflammatory targeting peptides, or neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM), exosomes are endowed with the ability to cross the BBB, recognize ischemic lesions, and bind to specific cell types. Targeted modifications based on stroke pathology are mainly classified into three categories: homing peptide–mediated brain targeting (eg, via RVG peptide), inflammatory lesion targeting centered on the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis (C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4/C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 axis), and biomimetic targeting via neutrophil membrane coating.

The RVG peptide is a 33-amino-acid fragment derived from the rabies virus glycoprotein, with the sequence YTIWM-PENPRPGTPCDIFTNSRGKRASNG. It specifically binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) on the surface of BMECs, thereby avoiding non-specific uptake by other organs and crossing the BBB via receptor-mediated transcytosis. Although the RVG peptide is derived from a viral protein, it is non-infectious; combined with the naturally low immunogenicity of exosomes, this strategy exhibits a high safety profile. Studies have confirmed that the RVG peptide specifically binds to nAChRs on BMECs, triggering endocytosis. The exosomes are then encapsulated in clathrin- or caveolin-mediated vesicles and transported across BMECs via transcytosis, significantly enhancing their BBB-crossing capability. Confocal microscopy tracking showed that RVG-exosomes crossed the BMEC monolayer in 60% less time and with a 5-fold increase in efficiency compared to unmodified exosomes. This enables the efficient delivery of siRNA to the brain parenchyma, ultimately improving neurological deficits caused by cerebral ischemia.85 To address the challenge of targeted exosome delivery to the brain, Alvarez-Erviti et al used genetic engineering to fuse the RVG peptide with lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2B (Lamp2b) and express the RVG-Lamp2b protein in dendritic cells. After exosomes secreted by these cells were loaded with BACE1-targeting siRNA via electroporation, intravenous injection efficiently and specifically delivered the siRNA to various neural cells in the mouse brain, achieving effective knockdown of the BACE1 gene and pioneering the use of engineered exosomes as a platform for RNA drug delivery to the brain.86 Building on this pioneering work, and to overcome bottlenecks in yield, homogeneity, and scalability associated with primary cell-based exosome production, subsequent technologies adopted more advanced strategies. They used CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to knock the RVG-Lamp2b expression cassette into the AAVS1 safe harbor locus of human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), thereby establishing an engineered cell line capable of stably, renewably, and massively producing targeted exosomes.87 This key technological iteration has vigorously pushed the platform toward clinical application.

The latest strategies are based on the “in vivo factory” concept in synthetic biology, designing a modular plasmid‑driven “in vivo self‑assembly” system that transforms the liver into a living reactor for producing brain‑targeted sEVs. This plasmid contains three major functional units: First, a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter‑driven module to ensure efficient expression in hepatocytes. Second, an RVG‑Lamp2a navigation tag, where the RVG peptide targets neuronal receptors and Lamp2a anchors to the sEV membrane. Third, a pre‑miRNA‑structured siRNA expression cassette that is processed into mature therapeutic siRNA by the endogenous Dicer enzyme after transcription. After intravenous injection, the plasmid is taken up and expressed by hepatocytes. Through the endogenous sEV biogenesis pathway, the RVG‑Lamp2a fusion protein spontaneously integrates into the vesicle membrane, mature siRNA is selectively loaded into the lumen, and targeted sEVs are automatically assembled. Once secreted into the bloodstream, these sEVs cross the BBB via RVG‑mediated active targeting, deliver therapeutic nucleic acids to the CNS, and achieve non‑invasive, precise gene silencing. Notably, an upgraded version of this strategy employs an adeno‑associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vector to package the same modules; a single injection drives long‑term expression in hepatocytes, extending therapeutic efficacy from months to years, and provides a new dual‑mode therapeutic paradigm of “immediate intervention and long‑term maintenance” for intractable neurological disorders such as TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) proteinopathy.88

Although the aforementioned pioneering studies demonstrate that RVG‑engineered sEVs possess excellent the BBB penetration capability and brain nucleic acid delivery potential, multiple subsequent independent controlled trials, leading reviews, and mechanistic investigations have yielded contradictory conclusions, inherent limitations, and safety concerns. These findings indicate that this delivery system still faces multiple obstacles before achieving stable and safe clinical translation. Recent studies indicate that the mechanism by which RVG facilitates EVs transcytosis across the BBB remains indirect and poorly defined. Although some studies have confirmed the expression of nAChRs and the p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) on brain endothelial cells, their specific roles in the transcytosis of RVG‑modified vesicles were previously merely unsubstantiated hypotheses.85 This ambiguity in mechanistic understanding poses a fundamental challenge to the optimization and reliability assessment of RVG‑modification strategies. Other studies have pointed out that although natural exosomes possess immune‑privileged properties, engineered modifications may trigger complement activation or unpredictable organ accumulation toxicity.89 Notably, active drug‑loading processes can potentially damage the targeting features and natural structure of exosomes.90 This implies that loading processes designed to enhance therapeutic function may instead compromise the most critical targeting ability of exosomes, creating a “therapeutic paradox.” Furthermore, if exosomes are derived from tumor cells, they may carry procarcinogenic factors such as SERPINE1, which can exacerbate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by regulating macrophage polarization.91 This highlights the critical importance of cell‑source selection in safety evaluation. Beyond RVG‑specific issues, the overall clinical translation of exosomal miRNA therapy for stroke faces broader bottlenecks, including miRNA loading heterogeneity, off‑target risks, limited biodistribution data, dose uncertainty, regulatory hurdles, and a lack of stroke‑specific clinical trials.92 These challenges constitute common barriers that must be overcome for RVG‑modified exosomes to transition from “proof‑of‑concept” to “clinical tools.”

In summary, RVG peptide‑mediated brain targeting modification is currently one of the most mature engineering strategies. Its advantage lies in its ability to confer active brain‑targeting capability without altering the intrinsic biological activity of exosomes. Furthermore, the establishment of hiPSC genetically engineered cell lines provides a feasible path for scalable production. However, its limitations are equally prominent. Therefore, at the current stage, RVG modification should be regarded as a “transitional technology platform” toward an ultimate solution. Its clinical translation requires breakthroughs in three aspects: mechanistic clarification, standardization of coupling efficiency, and validation of long‑term safety.

Cargo Engineering

Exosomal cargo engineering refers to the core technology of purposefully modifying the molecular composition carried within exosomes through biotechnological or physicochemical means—either by enhancing the expression and enrichment of endogenous active components, or by actively loading exogenous therapeutic molecules such as small-molecule drugs and nucleic acids—thereby enabling exosomes to acquire specific therapeutic functions or significantly enhance their inherent biological activities. Its essence lies in transforming exosomes from natural intercellular messengers into precisely programmable “intelligent biological therapeutic platforms,” overcoming the limitations of natural exosomes, which are functionally singular and have limited efficacy regulation capabilities.

Pueraria lobata‑derived exosomes (PE), serving as natural neuroprotective delivery vehicles, exhibit low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, and intrinsic brain tropism. Their lumen is naturally loaded with endogenous functional miRNAs, which already possess fundamental neuroprotective activity. To address the core pathologies of neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease (PD), Xu Y et al (2026) developed an engineered biomimetic nanorobot platform, designated hPH‑RNPEC, using PE as the core carrier. Through a loading strategy of “carrier‑as‑drug + exogenous enhancement,” they co‑loaded the exogenous small‑molecule neuroprotective drug curcumin with PE’s endogenous miRNAs to construct a dual‑cargo system of “natural activity + artificial enhancement.” To achieve brain‑targeted delivery, the platform employs techniques such as fusion with neutrophil‑like cell membranes, surface modification with a four‑block long chain (DhPH), and RVG peptide targeting to accomplish cascade delivery involving “nasal mucosa penetration, inflammatory lesion chemotaxis, and neural cell targeting.” Specifically, after intranasal administration and brain entry, curcumin is delivered to activated microglia to inhibit neuroinflammation, while PE’s endogenous miRNAs are delivered to neurons to repair mitochondrial function. This ultimately reshapes the neuro‑glial microenvironment, promotes myelin repair, reconstructs neural circuits, and achieves multi‑target neuroprotection in PD.93 Notably, stroke and PD share a high degree of pathological commonality, suggesting that the nose‑to‑brain cascade targeting and dual‑cargo synergistic intervention strategy represented by hPH‑RNPEC could theoretically be translatable to the neurorepair of ischemic stroke. However, the actual therapeutic efficacy of this nanoplatform in ischemic injury and its in vivo spatiotemporal distribution kinetics still require independent validation using classic stroke animal models such as the MCAO model.

Synthetic gene circuits are artificial networks constructed by orderly assembling gene regulatory modules. Their essence is to embed a “biological computer” within cells—through preset genetic logic such as switches, feedback loops, and logic gates, cells are endowed with the ability to sense, compute, and respond to internal and external signals. These circuits can execute programmed responses upon stimulation by signals such as light, chemical molecules, specific biomarkers, and disease markers. They not only possess high precision and tunability but also can adjust output in real time within dynamically changing disease environments, thereby precisely controlling therapeutic effects. A review published in Cell Systems by the team of Professor Haifeng Ye at East China Normal University prospectively proposed that a future engineering direction could integrate synthetic gene circuits into exosome-producing cells to construct an intelligent feedback system of “sense-compute-respond.” Specifically, a sensor module within the circuit recognizes specific disease signals, such as the local inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β); a computation module integrates signal intensity and logic; and an execution module regulates exosome secretion or cargo release. This ultimately achieves “on-demand therapy,” meaning therapeutic cargo is released only when the lesion requires it.94 The significance of this direction lies in its potential to solve the core problems of imprecise dose control and off-target release with ineffective accumulation in non-lesion areas associated with traditional exosome therapy. Moreover, through the spatiotemporal specificity of disease-signal response, it can greatly enhance the safety and efficacy of treatment. It provides a new paradigm of dynamic, closed-loop, and personalized therapy for complex diseases such as cancer, autoimmune diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases, and is expected to propel exosome therapy from the era of “passive infusion” into a new era of “active intelligence.”

Although cutting-edge technologies such as exosomal cargo engineering, plant-derived exosomes, and synthetic gene circuits have shown initial therapeutic potential, this research field still faces numerous practical limitations. As research deepens, the efficacy heterogeneity of some preclinical studies, technical standardization bottlenecks, and long-term safety issues have gradually attracted attention, suggesting that a prudent scientific attitude is required when advancing these technologies toward clinical application. Plant-derived exosomes are widely praised for their “low immunogenicity and high biocompatibility.” However, a growing body of research has raised serious questions about this assumption. An important review published in Toxicology Letters (2026) clearly stated that the origin from edible plants should not be equated with safety, especially at high doses, upon repeated administration, or via non-oral routes.95 Furthermore, although a large body of literature claims that plant exosomes have low immunogenicity, the scientific community has not reached a consensus on their immunological characteristics in humans—the risk of immunogenicity triggered by heterologous plant proteins still requires further investigation.96 Preclinical evidence has shown that intravenous administration of specific plant‑derived exosome‑like nanoparticles (PELNs) can trigger elevated complement C3,97 demonstrating that “low immunogenicity” is not an absolute attribute but a variable modulated by plant source, administration route, dose, and individual variability. The immaturity of production and characterization methods further increases uncertainty. Methods for directly producing exosomes from plants and identification criteria for plant exosomes have not yet been established. The most suitable methods for isolation, concentration, and purification remain to be determined, and efficient methods adapted to specific plants need to be developed. Large-scale production, purity assurance, long-term safety, and clinical translation remain major obstacles for plant-derived exosomes.

In summary, although exosomal cargo engineering, plant-derived exosomes, and synthetic gene circuit integration strategies theoretically represent a transformative direction from “passive infusion” to “active intelligence,” their practical implementation still confronts multiple obstacles, and their limitations should be viewed with caution. Only by confronting existing negative evidence and technical bottlenecks head‑on can exosome therapy be robustly propelled from “proof‑of‑concept” to “clinical application.”

Delivery System Optimization

Optimization of exosome delivery systems refers to the systematic resolution of key bottlenecks encountered by natural exosomes as therapeutic carriers in clinical applications—such as weak targeting, low drug-loading efficiency, poor controlled release, and insufficient intelligence—through engineering modifications, vector integration, and strategic innovations. It aims to enhance delivery efficiency, therapeutic precision, and controllability. The core objective is to construct a next-generation “smart, efficient, and controllable” exosome therapeutic platform. Its essence lies in using “engineering” approaches to compensate for the “shortboards” (deficiencies) and amplify the “longboards” (strengths) of exosomes as natural nanocarriers. The significance of this optimization goes far beyond merely improving a single delivery tool; rather, it provides a brand-new technical paradigm and solution of “intelligent cell-derived nanomachines” for the entire biomedical field, especially for the treatment of CNS diseases and complex chronic diseases.

To address the bottlenecks of short local retention and low bioavailability of neural stem cell-derived exosomes (NSCs-Exo), a study designed a photocurable catechol-grafted hyaluronic acid adhesive hydrogel (HAD) as a controlled-release carrier. The HAD-NSCs-Exo composite was constructed and implanted into the ischemic brain region via local stereotaxic injection, achieving targeted accumulation and long-term controlled release of exosomes at the lesion site—approximately 10–15% released per day for 2–3 weeks. The multi-modal repair mechanisms of this strategy are as follows: First, the HAD hydrogel forms a “sustained-release reservoir” in the lesion area, preventing the rapid metabolism of exosomes and maintaining an effective local concentration. Second, pro-angiogenic miRNAs in NSCs-Exo are delivered to endothelial cells, where they stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) by inhibiting its negative regulators, thereby upregulating the expression of VEGF and angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1), and stimulating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation. Third, miR-124 and miR-132 inhibit pro-inflammatory factors and upregulate anti-inflammatory factors, while neurotrophic factors carried by the exosomes promote neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. Fourth, the sustained-release exosomes and the three-dimensional scaffold of HAD jointly create a reparative microenvironment that recruits endogenous neural stem cells to migrate to the ischemic area and differentiate into neurons. This “material-exosome” synergistic strategy, through the combination of “controlled release + targeting + multi-mechanism,” significantly promoted angiogenesis, reduced inflammation, and improved neurological function in the ischemic brain region, providing an efficient and clinically operable new scheme for the reparative treatment of ischemic stroke.39 Furthermore, in a similar strategy, another research team constructed Exo-HPMC nanohydrogels based on NSCs exosomes and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC). This successfully achieved long-term controlled release of NSCs exosomes in the ischemic brain region and significantly enhanced post-stroke neurological recovery by promoting angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and inhibiting apoptosis.98 This strategy effectively overcomes the problems of short in vivo half-life, rapid clearance, and limited retention time of exosomes in the lesion area.

However, the safety of hydrogels themselves constitutes a primary challenge when these exogenous biomaterials are implanted into brain tissue. Hydrogel preparation often relies on chemical crosslinkers or reactive precursors, and studies have shown that residual crosslinkers pose cytotoxicity risks. For instance, glutaraldehyde can cause necrosis and inflammatory responses; N,N’-methylenebis(acrylamide) (MBAA) exhibits cumulative neurotoxic effects; and urea, a byproduct of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) crosslinking, can induce oxidative stress and DNA damage at high concentrations. This implies that the biocompatibility of hydrogels depends not only on the polymer bulk but also, more critically, on the crosslinking chemistry and residue control processes.99 A review by Carballo-Pedrares et al (2026) published in Drug Delivery and Translational Research pointed out that despite positive preclinical results, the clinical translation of injectable hydrogels remains limited, and ensuring long-term biocompatibility is one of its major translational barriers.100 This challenge is particularly prominent in the context of intracranial hydrogels—existing reviews clearly state that the short- and long-term adverse effects of many hydrogel materials after implantation into the brain remain unknown. Acidic degradation products from synthetic polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) can induce inflammation and exacerbate brain injury, while the role of hydrogel degradation byproducts in neuroimmune regulation is still understudied. Currently, most studies focus on relatively short-term efficacy evaluations. There is still a lack of systematic assessment of the degradation kinetics of hydrogels in the brain over months or even years, and their long-term effects on surrounding brain tissue.101

In summary, the core advantage of the hydrogel‑controlled release system lies in its ability to ameliorate the inherent defects of exosomes—namely, limited local retention time and insufficient bioavailability—thereby exerting a long‑term sustained‑release effect. However, this strategy still has certain limitations, including potential cytotoxicity and the risk of inducing local inflammatory responses after implantation. Therefore, the hydrogel‑exosome composite strategy holds application potential for local tissue repair during the subacute and chronic phases of stroke; nevertheless, its clinical translation still requires residue‑free crosslinker processes and long‑term safety data on intracranial degradation as prerequisites.

Exploration of Novel Sources

PELNVs are nanoscale (typically 30–150 nm; some literature defines them as 30–300 nm) lipid bilayer vesicles naturally secreted from edible or medicinal plant tissues such as fruits, vegetables, and medicinal plants, and are rich in source-plant-specific proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and secondary metabolites.102 Compared with synthetic nanocarriers and animal-derived exosomes, PELNVs have significant advantages in production cost and scalable preparation. Their advantages of high safety, low immunogenicity, inherent bioactivity, high oral bioavailability, and the ability to cross the blood-brain barrier provide a richer and more scalable source option for exosome therapy.

The core mechanisms of PELNVs in treating CNS diseases can be summarized into three parallel pathways:102,103 First, direct neuroprotection. PELNVs can intervene in pathological processes through multi‑target approaches such as inhibiting neuroinflammation, clearing abnormal protein aggregation, and exerting antioxidant and anti‑apoptotic effects, thereby exerting direct protective effects on neurons, glial cells, and the NVU. Second, indirect regulation of the gut‑brain axis. Orally administered PELNVs maintain structural stability during gastrointestinal transit. Upon reaching the distal small intestine and colon, they interact with the intestinal epithelium and resident immune cells, modulating gut microbiota composition and the production of metabolites such as short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and reducing systemic pro‑inflammatory cytokine levels. The latter then cross the BBB to inhibit microglial activation, thereby indirectly improving CNS function. Third, intelligent delivery. After surface targeting modification or drug‑loading engineering, PELNVs can encapsulate small molecules, nucleic acids, proteins, and even gene editing components, achieving precise delivery to brain lesions. Their characteristics of “natural brain targeting, carrier‑as‑drug, and oral stability” render them uniquely irreplaceable in oral delivery scenarios such as gut‑brain axis regulation.

However, it is essential to recognize the non‑negligible bottlenecks in its clinical translation. First, the notion that “plant origin equates to safety” lacks sufficient evidence‑based support, and the risk of immunogenicity triggered by heterologous plant proteins still requires systematic investigation. Second, there is currently a lack of a unified and standardized isolation and purification protocol for PELNVs; efficient methods tailored to specific plants need to be developed. Large‑scale production, purity assurance, and long‑term safety remain major obstacles. Furthermore, more in vivo distribution and pharmacodynamic data are needed to support the specific molecular mechanisms by which PELNVs cross the BBB and the stability differences among various delivery strategies. In summary, PELNVs demonstrate considerable application prospects in oral‑mediated neuroinflammation regulation and gut‑brain axis intervention; however, if they are to be used for systemic brain‑targeted delivery, sufficient evidence‑based evidence still needs to be provided.

Toward the Clinic: Translational Challenges and Future Pathways

Although a large body of preclinical studies has fully confirmed the considerable therapeutic potential of exosomal miRNA therapy for post-stroke neuroprotection and functional repair, the clinical translation of this technology still faces severe challenges in delivery efficiency, scalable manufacturing, and long-term safety. As of August 2026, no exosome therapeutic product has been granted marketing approval via a Biologics License Application (BLA) by the US FDA worldwide. The FDA has clarified in multiple regulatory actions that exosome products intended for therapeutic use in human diseases are defined as “drugs” under Section 201 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) and are classified as biological products under Section 351 of the Public Health Service Act (PHS Act); they can only be legally used for human therapy after completing an Investigational New Drug (IND) application, passing full Phase I–III clinical trials, and obtaining approval through BLA review.104,105 Currently, stroke exosome interventions, including NouvSoma001 and ADSC-Exo nasal spray, are still in the early stages of registered clinical research. This fully demonstrates that exosomal miRNA therapy remains in a critical stage of transitioning from basic mechanisms to routine clinical application.

Progress in Clinical Trials of Exosome Therapy for Stroke

In recent years, multiple clinical trials of exosome therapy for stroke have been successively registered and initiated worldwide, marking that this field has transitioned from preclinical research to the early clinical validation stage. Based on publicly available information from registered clinical trials, mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) have emerged as a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy for acute ischemic stroke, owing to their unique advantages—including the ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and avoid the risks associated with cellular therapy.106

NCT03384433 is currently the only MSC-derived exosome clinical trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov specifically for acute ischemic stroke. Sponsored by Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Iran, the trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov in December 2017 and actually commenced in April 2019. As a Phase I/II randomized, single-blind, placebo-controlled trial, it aims to evaluate the efficacy of miR-124-enriched allogenic MSC-derived exosomes in improving disability in patients with acute ischemic stroke. The study plans to enroll 5 participants and administer miR-124-rich allogenic MSC-derived exosomes. The primary endpoint is the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events, including deteriorating stroke, stroke recurrences, brain edema, seizures, and hemorrhagic transformation; the secondary endpoint is the degree of disability in stroke patients. The registration of this trial is of milestone significance, representing the first systematic evaluation of the safety and feasibility of MSC-derived exosome therapy for stroke in humans. According to information updated in May 2024, the trial status was “Recruiting.” Although the sample size is extremely small (n=5), its exploratory results provide important safety references for subsequent larger-scale exosome clinical trials.107

Intranasal administration represents an important innovative route for exosome delivery. A Phase I/II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-/multiple-ascending-dose clinical trial (NCT07398612) is currently evaluating the safety and efficacy of a human adipose-derived stem cell exosome (ADSC-exo, STX11102) nasal spray for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). The study is dually registered under ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT07398612 and the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) number ChiCTR2600119088. It is the first—and currently the only—registered human trial on ClinicalTrials.gov to employ intranasal rather than intravenous ADSC-exo administration for AIS. The trial comprises two sequential phases: a Single-Ascending Dose (SAD) phase and a Multiple-Ascending Dose (MAD) phase. The SAD phase enrolls 12 patients with mild stroke (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score 1–4), divided into three dose cohorts of 4 patients each, who receive a single nasal spray dose alongside standard care and undergo 14-day safety monitoring. The MAD phase enrolls 48 patients with moderate stroke (NIHSS 5–12), randomized into low-dose and high-dose groups at a 2:1 ratio of active treatment to placebo; they self-administer the nasal spray daily for 14 days and are followed up to Day 90. Preclinical data demonstrate that intranasally administered ADSC-exo can cross the blood-brain barrier, modulate neuroinflammation, reduce infarct volume, and promote functional recovery. The primary endpoint of the study is safety assessment; secondary endpoints include neurological function scales (NIHSS, modified Rankin Scale [mRS], Barthel Index [BI]) and changes in infarct volume on MRI. The Chinese registry record (ChiCTR2600119088) indicates a target enrollment of 16 participants, with the study status listed as “Not yet recruiting.”

At the translational frontier, the Phase I clinical trial (NCT06612710) of NouvSoma001 injection—extracellular vesicles derived from human induced neural stem cells—has completed registration and entered the implementation phase. Its current status is “Recruiting.” This is a single-center, randomized, open-label, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation trial with a planned total enrollment of 69 participants with acute ischemic stroke. The study consists of two parts: a dose-escalation cohort (n=9) and a dose-expansion cohort (n=60), using intravenous administration once daily for 7 consecutive days. The primary endpoint is the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) within 6 months after treatment initiation, serving to evaluate the safety and tolerability of the formulation; NIHSS, mRS, biomarkers, and imaging parameters are observed as secondary exploratory endpoints.

Beyond the aforementioned trials, multiple clinical studies of exosome therapy for stroke are currently at different stages of development. For example, an exploratory study (NCT07232563) is evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of intranasal administration of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (hUC-MSC-sEV-001) for ischemic stroke. In addition, investigations into intranasal administration of small extracellular vesicles derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC-sEVs) for improving neurological function and cognitive impairment in patients with acute ischemic stroke have also been registered. These trials further enrich the cell-source diversity and administration-route options for exosome therapy. However, despite the encouraging progress of the aforementioned clinical trials, it must be soberly recognized that the clinical efficacy evidence for exosome therapy in the stroke field is still in the process of accumulation, and substantial distance remains from regulatory approval and routine clinical application.

According to the latest registry information from ClinicalTrials.gov (as of August 2026), exosome/sEV therapies for stroke that have entered the clinical stage globally remain in the early validation phase, predominantly Phase I and Phase I/II trials. Manufacturing and Quality Control of Clinical-Grade Exosomes

The clinical translation of exosome therapy is first confronted with a series of fundamental challenges at the manufacturing and quality control (QC) level. The production of clinical-grade exosomes must strictly adhere to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) guidelines, which requires the integration of a complete process chain—including cell bank establishment, serum-free medium preparation, closed-system bioreactor expansion, scaled purification via tangential flow filtration (TFF), and aseptic filling.

In terms of large-scale production, mammalian cells secrete exosomes at extremely low levels under conventional culture conditions, making it difficult to meet the massive doses required for clinical therapy. Currently, there is a distinct medium selection paradox in the industry: using serum-containing medium can maintain high cell viability to achieve high yields, but it introduces a large amount of heterologous protein impurities; whereas switching to serum-free medium reduces the impurity background, but often inhibits cell growth, causing exosome yield to plummet dramatically.108 Furthermore, scaled culture inevitably introduces large amounts of host cell proteins (HCPs), residual nucleic acids, and medium components. These impurities not only interfere with drug efficacy but also harbor the risk of triggering severe immunogenic reactions. Second, downstream isolation and purification also face enormous challenges. Ultracentrifugation is the most widely used method for exosome isolation in research, offering simple operation and no need for special reagents, making it suitable for processing large-volume samples. However, this method is extremely time-consuming and requires expensive equipment, with low recovery rates and a high tendency to cause structural damage to exosomes. In recent years, new technologies such as the combination of TFF and size exclusion chromatography (SEC), membrane affinity capture, and microfluidic chips are gradually becoming mainstream alternatives. However, when scaling up to 1000-liter bioreactors, they are highly prone to issues such as membrane fouling, resin fracture, or exosome structural rupture caused by excessive shear force.109 Therefore, how to achieve low-cost, high-purity, and large-scale industrial purification without compromising exosome bioactivity remains the top priority in current process development.

In terms of QC, it is essential to establish comprehensive characterization standards covering exosome size distribution, expression of marker proteins, purity, key miRNA content, and biological activity. Moreover, the entire production process must be conducted under GMP conditions. Exosomes are not a single compound but rather a highly heterogeneous population of nanovesicles. This intrinsic property poses a significant barrier to establishing unified QC standards. Even exosomes secreted by the same cell line under different passages or culture conditions can vary substantially. Exosomes isolated by different laboratories may differ drastically in size distribution, surface markers, and even the internal miRNA profiles.110 Although the MISEV guidelines issued by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) provide a basic framework—recommending the use of transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blot to establish a basic “identity fingerprint”—this still falls far short of the stringent release criteria for clinical‑grade drugs.111 In clinical applications, to endow exosomes with targeting capabilities or load therapeutic agents, they often undergo genetic engineering modifications or physical loading. However, due to the complexity of cellular secretion mechanisms, it is difficult to precisely control the cargo copy number and modification efficiency of each individual exosome. Facing the limitations of traditional QC methods, the industry is actively exploring more advanced quality control frameworks. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI)‑driven real‑time quality decision systems has been proposed as a direction to enhance inter‑batch consistency, and multi‑omics analysis has also shown potential in the quality evaluation of engineered exosomes. Olson et al employed dual‑omics analysis of transcriptomics and proteomics to comprehensively assess the quality of genetically engineered exosomes. Using liquid chromatography‑mass spectrometry (LC‑MS), they identified over 1,330 endogenous exosome‑associated proteins and a large number of bovine‑derived contaminants, thereby establishing a feasible analytical framework for the quality control of engineered exosomes.112

Potency Testing: The Core Challenge in Exosome Drug Approval

Potency is defined as the specific biological activity or capacity of a product as measured by its intended clinical function. Its assessment is arguably the most central and unresolved challenge in the exosome drug approval and QC system. Current exosome-related studies predominantly use particle concentration (particles/mL) or total protein concentration as the primary characterization parameters. However, a consensus has been reached among regulatory agencies and academia: the aforementioned physicochemical indicators merely reflect the quantitative level of exosome particles; they cannot reflect the proportion of functionally active particles, nor can they capture the variability in functional cargo and therapeutic activity across different batches. As pointed out in a recent regulatory review published in the Journal of Extracellular Vesicles: “defining and standardizing potency remains one of the most critical challenges in the clinical translation of EV-based products.” The fundamental bottleneck lies in the lack of standardized, methodologically validated potency assays across different laboratories.113

The root cause of this challenge lies in the inherent heterogeneity of exosomes. The molecular composition of exosomes is highly dependent on the cell type of origin, donor variability, passage number, culture conditions, isolation and purification processes, and even storage and handling conditions. Even for the same batch of cells from the same donor, the characteristics of the secreted exosomes can vary significantly under different culture conditions. The direct consequence of this is that two exosome batches with identical particle counts may exhibit significant differences in therapeutic efficacy in vivo, influenced by the composition of the miRNA cargo, the surface targeting peptide coupling efficiency, and the abundance of bioactive molecules. As stated in a foundational review in the field of exosome therapy: exosomes obtained from two separate donors may be normalized via a given quantitative method; however, the ‘bioactive’ load may differ, subsequently the potency and degree of efficacy will not be the same114

Thus, it is evident that relying solely on total protein or particle counts for dose normalization can only achieve uniformity at the physical quantity level of the preparation, but cannot reflect the true abundance of functionally active components within the vesicles. More critically, conventional QC parameters have limited ability to predict potency. A 2026 study published in Frontiers in Bioengineering provided empirical evidence that, in a wound healing dose-response model, traditional QC parameters such as protein concentration had weak predictive power for potency loss, whereas functional indicators like membrane lipid order (cGP) provided stronger predictive performance. These results experimentally confirm that relying solely on particle counts and total protein content for batch release—even if physical measurement indicators are consistent—the biological activity of the preparation can still change significantly, thereby introducing regulatory and clinical risks of “same number, different potency.” “Same number, different potency” refers to a situation where two batches of EV preparations have similar or even identical physicochemical quantitative results (eg, NTA particle counts, total protein concentration), but due to factors such as donor differences, cell culture conditions, isolation and purification processes, and storage/freeze-thaw damage, the internal active cargo and membrane structural integrity of the vesicles are altered, ultimately leading to significant differences in biological potency. In other words, “equal quantity” does not equate to “equal bioactivity.”

To address the aforementioned technical dilemmas, the future direction for exosome potency testing is to establish a multidimensional quality control system encompassing physicochemical characterization, cargo fingerprinting, functional potency assays, and internal reference standards, and to promote standardized activity metrics such as the Exosome Potency Unit (EPU), thereby shifting the batch release criteria for preparations from the traditional particle-counting paradigm to a bioactivity-oriented evaluation model.

Pharmacokinetics and Safety Assessment

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of exosomes aims to elucidate the dynamic patterns of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) following their entry into a biological organism. Accurately determining the time-dependent changes in exosome concentration in vivo is the foundation of PK research. However, traditional imaging or detection methods often struggle to distinguish endogenous exosomes from exogenously administered ones. Therefore, developing new biodistribution tracking methods with high sensitivity and specificity remains an urgent need in this field.115 At the quantitative analysis level, traditional methods mostly rely on exogenous labeling, a process that may interfere with the native structure and function of exosomes. Although label-free quantitative techniques such as liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) provide new solutions for in vivo sample analysis, the field still urgently requires the establishment of a recognized standardized quantification system.116 Regarding biodistribution characteristics, studies have shown that intravenously injected exosomes are rapidly and extensively taken up by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) and quickly cleared, leading to a shortened half-life and insufficient accumulation at target sites.117 Furthermore, Pavlova’s research confirmed that the physiological and pathological states of the organism can significantly alter vascular permeability, thereby unpredictably affecting the biodistribution and targeting efficiency of exosomes. Some studies have also pointed out that the cellular origin, membrane protein composition, and inherent heterogeneity of exosomes are key factors leading to highly variable PK characteristics.50 Therefore, establishing comprehensive pharmacokinetic research standards is crucial for the clinical success of exosome therapy.

In terms of safety assessment, although natural exosomes exhibit relatively low immunogenicity, engineered modifications may pose potential immunogenic risks and cytotoxic reactions. Consequently, systematic preclinical safety evaluation is indispensable. However, there is currently a lack of unified regulatory frameworks and quality control standards worldwide, with clinical data on long-term safety being particularly scarce. In areas such as inter-batch consistency, standardization of isolation and purification processes, and viral safety testing, no authoritative guidelines are yet available to follow, which further exacerbates the ambiguity and uncertainty in safety assessment.

Clinical Development Strategies and Regulatory Framework

Exosome therapy faces a central paradox in clinical development: the contradiction between the complexity of preclinical research and the simplification and stability required for clinical translation. On the one hand, engineering modifications can endow exosomes with multiple functions; on the other hand, overly complex modifications pose significant challenges to large-scale production and quality control. Exosomes are naturally occurring nanoscale vesicles with highly heterogeneous compositions, influenced by various factors such as cell source, culture conditions, and isolation methods. This inherent complexity makes fine-grained engineering at the laboratory level possible, but it also leads to inter-batch consistency issues. Successful clinical translation demands that products possess reproducible manufacturing processes, well-defined quality attributes, stable storage and transportation protocols, and clear potency assessment methods.118 Regulatory agencies emphasize the need to ensure controllable quality throughout the entire process—from raw materials to finished products—including source quality control, standard production, physicochemical testing, batch monitoring, and traceability tracking. As pointed out in the literature, although engineering modifications can enhance targeting ability or drug-loading capacity, they may compromise exosome membrane integrity or natural functions, thereby increasing the complexity and uncertainty of the manufacturing process.119 Meanwhile, to meet the demands of large-scale clinical trials, a shift from “laboratory customization” to “industrial-scale production” is imperative. This requires the development of scalable bioreactor systems and standardized isolation and purification processes. By expanding cell culture scale, applying regulatory stimuli using intelligent bioreactors, developing cheap alternative sources such as bacteria and plants, and fabricating artificial synthetic vesicles—supplemented by efficient separation technologies like microfluidics—the large-scale production of EVs can be comprehensively achieved.110

The regulatory classification of exosome products exhibits a fragmented landscape globally. Major regulatory agencies worldwide—such as the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA)—are still in the exploratory and refinement phase regarding the classification and review of exosome products, which constitutes a critical external factor constraining their clinical translation. To date, exosome products reside in a “gray zone” straddling biologics, advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), gene therapies, and medical devices. The FDA classifies exosome products into three categories by function—drugs (21 CFR 312), medical devices (21 CFR 812), and biologics (21 CFR 600)—with exosome products intended for therapeutic use regulated as biologics. The EMA adopts the ATMP framework. In China, in April 2026, the National Health Commission (NHC) issued the Guidelines for Clinical Research Filing of Novel Therapeutic Techniques Involving Cellular Components and Derivatives (Version 1). Through the “cellular components and derivatives” framework, extracellular vesicles (including exosomes) and other non-intact live-cell therapies were incorporated into systematic regulation for the first time. For non-registration clinical research, the guidelines require that preparation follow relevant GMP principles and that process development and quality control be conducted based on risk thinking, reflecting the quality-by-design philosophy. Japan, via the Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act (PMD Act), allows conditional and time-limited approval for regenerative medicine products. This lack of harmonized classification poses challenges for transnational development.

Facing the aforementioned challenges, a rational clinical development strategy proposed in recent literature aims to reduce risks and accumulate evidence through scientific trial design. For example, to mitigate the potential risks of systemic exposure, early-phase clinical trials tend to favor local or regional administration. In TBI research, the induced neural stem cell-derived exosome (iNSC-Exo) therapeutic platform employs local injection combined with hydrogel-based delivery, which further enhances exosome retention and sustained release at the injury site, thereby increasing local therapeutic concentration and bioavailability.120 In terms of indication selection, there is a preference for disease subtypes or post-acute stages where existing therapies have limited efficacy and the pathological mechanisms are relatively well-defined. Although conventional imaging is important, it is insufficient to comprehensively reflect the potential roles of exosomes in promoting neural repair, modulating inflammation, and improving function. Therefore, clinical trial designs should place greater emphasis on refined neurological function scores, patient-reported quality of life metrics, activities of daily living (ADL) assessments, and long-term follow-up data. These endpoints can better capture the impact of treatment on functional recovery and quality of life, aligning with the evaluation logic for regenerative medicine products.

Commercial Translation and Multidisciplinary Ecosystem Building

The clinical translation of exosome therapy is a systematic project. Its success relies not only on scientific breakthroughs, but more importantly on the effective integration of capital, industry, clinical practice, and regulatory forces to build a highly efficient and collaborative innovation ecosystem. Current research has shifted from broad mechanistic exploration to targeted development aimed at specific “clinical gaps.” In the field of stroke, exosomes are regarded as a highly promising solution due to their unique neuroprotective and reparative properties, particularly for chronic neurological deficits that persist after acute thrombolysis or thrombectomy. This requires close collaboration between basic researchers and frontline clinicians from the very beginning of project design, to clearly define the treatment scenario, target population, and core clinical endpoints, ensuring that the research direction directly addresses unmet medical needs.

Furthermore, the development pipeline of exosome products spans multiple disciplines, including biology, engineering, clinical medicine, and regulatory science. Expertise in any single field is insufficient to address the end-to-end challenges of this pipeline. Therefore, cultivating and establishing such multidisciplinary teams, or bridging talent gaps through inter-institutional collaboration, has become a consensus in the industry. Establishing an efficient “clinician-engineer-researcher-enterprise” collaboration model, which transforms clinicians from end-users of research outcomes into active participants in translation, is key to accelerating this process. In this model, clinicians should not merely be the ultimate end-users of the product; rather, they should serve as deeply involved participants in the translation process, engaging early to define critical quality attributes and clinical development pathways. Simultaneously, engineering teams are responsible for translating laboratory processes into stable manufacturing processes that comply with GMP guidelines, while enterprises provide project management, capital support, and regulatory-compliant quality systems. This deeply integrated collaboration ensures that product design, process development, and clinical validation consistently revolve around the same goal, substantially reducing the risk of late-stage failure.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

Based on current research advances in the field of post-stroke neural repair, this review argues that the core value of exosomal miRNA therapy does not lie in serving as a conventional alternative to stem cell therapy, but rather in its potential to act as a “programmable molecular switch for neural circuits”—that is, using a single or a few key non-coding RNAs to endogenously reprogram the inflammatory microenvironment and synaptic plasticity programs. Although exosomal miRNA therapy, by regulating endogenous neurorepair mechanisms, offers a new paradigm for post-stroke functional reconstruction—shifting from “replacement therapy” to “mechanism-based regulation”—current research is still in a critical stage of transitioning from basic mechanisms to routine clinical application. Building upon the existing macro-translational framework, future research should focus on the construction of standardized systems, systematic safety assessment, quantitative potency detection technologies, and specialized clinical trial design, so as to address the shortcomings in the field’s translation and thereby drive exosomal miRNA stroke therapy from basic research to standardized clinical application through engineering modifications.

Based on the above comprehensive review, the author proposes the following independent insights to clarify the future path: First, target selection should shift from the generalized narrative of “broad‑spectrum anti‑inflammation/pro‑angiogenesis” to the precise targeting of “stroke‑specific dominant miRNAs.” Existing studies often enumerate numerous different types of differential miRNAs, yet rarely distinguish between causation and epiphenomena. Future research should prioritize molecules such as miR‑124 and miR‑210, which possess both BBB penetrability and well‑defined synaptic regulatory functions, and establish their dynamic plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exosomal cargo levels as companion diagnostic biomarkers for efficacy prediction, rather than relying solely on the NIHSS score for retrospective evaluation, thereby constructing a closed‑loop evidence chain of “therapeutic target – pharmacodynamic biomarker.”

Second, engineering modifications should abandon the mindset of “stacking functions” and establish a quantitative assembly logic similar to that of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). Although RVG peptide modification demonstrates favorable brain-targeting efficacy in animal models, the precise molecular mechanism of its BBB crossing remains controversial; while local hydrogel-based sustained-release strategies can prolong exosome retention, the long-term degradation kinetics of intracerebrally implanted materials and the potential neurotoxicity of degradation products have not been fully evaluated. These uncertainties suggest that, at the current stage, both strategies should be regarded as transitional technological platforms toward an ultimate solution, rather than mature clinical translation regimens. The significance of the recently reported chimeric adaptor protein (CAP) platform lies not in the surface chemistry details, but in the fact that it has achieved, for the first time, the linear quantification of the number of functional molecules conjugated per single vesicle.121 This serves as a warning that next-generation stroke exosome products must define a “potency unit—for example, the number of functional miRNA copies per unit dose or the target gene silencing rate—” and balance targeting efficiency and membrane integrity through modular surface engineering, while exploring “inflammation-responsive on-demand release” endowed by synthetic gene circuits to reduce the risk of off-target systemic accumulation. Meanwhile, caution should be exercised regarding emerging sources such as plant-derived exosomes; priority should be given to supplementing evidence-based data on heterologous immunogenicity and scalable purification, so as to avoid falling into the cognitive trap of “natural equals safe.”

Furthermore, at the technical methodology level, future exosomal miRNA research should proactively embrace snRNA‑seq, single‑cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq), spatial transcriptomics, and multi‑omics integration. By utilizing snRNA‑seq/scRNA‑seq to define the transcriptomic landscape of each cell type within the NVU, spatial transcriptomics to map the spatial proximity of cell‑cell interactions, and SEVtras‑like tools to infer cell‑type‑specific sEVs secretion activity, and by integrating exosomal miRNA sequencing with Argonaute 2 cross‑linking immunoprecipitation (Ago2‑CLIP) target gene identification, researchers can systematically construct a comprehensive “post‑stroke exosomal miRNA – secreting cell – target cell – target gene” atlas. This will propel therapies from “pathway description” to “cell‑type‑specific precise regulation.”

Finally, clinical translation necessitates the reconstruction of evaluation systems and development strategies. Currently, most exosome clinical trials follow the early development framework of cell therapy, with safety verification as the primary endpoint. Although some trials have incorporated dose-escalation designs to explore the dose-effect relationship, the lack of unified dose units and standardized potency detection methods makes it difficult to systematically evaluate dose-response relationships and conduct cross-trial comparisons. Dose-response has been identified by multiple systematic reviews as a critical but underappreciated gap in current exosome clinical research, urgently requiring the establishment of a unified dose-anchoring system and dose-effect evaluation criteria. In terms of indication layout, compared with acute-phase neuroprotection—which is highly competitive and has an extremely narrow time window—the subacute and chronic neurological deficits remaining after mechanical thrombectomy may be a more rational clinical entry point; administration routes can prioritize intranasal delivery or stereotactic implantation to accumulate human brain PK data while reducing the risk of systemic exposure.

In summary, transforming exosomal miRNAs from natural repair factors into an intelligent therapeutic system for precise targeted delivery, and ultimately bringing this system to the bedsides of stroke patients, is not only a scientific endeavor but also a systematic project. Only by maintaining a clear awareness of bottlenecks amid progress and building a four‑wheel‑drive translational ecosystem of “mechanism elucidation – engineering empowerment – standard establishment – regulatory support” in the course of advancement, can this field truly achieve a complete transition from “benchside discovery” to “bedside benefit.”
