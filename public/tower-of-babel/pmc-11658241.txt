Surgical treatment of spinal tuberculosis: an updated review
Authors: Jinjing Gan, Chuanzhi Zhang, Dagang Tang, Xing Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) is a worldwide disease which seriously affects the global public health. Spinal TB is the most common extra-pulmonary TB and may cause vertebral bone destruction, collapse, kyphosis and even paralysis. Anti-TB chemotherapy is considered the cornerstone treatment of spinal TB and surgery is often required for patients with severe kyphosis, impaired neurological function or spinal instability. Debridement of TB lesions, bone grafting and internal fixation are the key procedures of spinal TB surgery. However, the selection of surgical approach, the extent of TB lesion debridement, the choice of bone graft materials, and the method and extent of internal fixation are all remain controversy. The aim of this updated review is to evaluate current literature for advances in management of spinal TB, with particular focus on surgical techniques.Keywords: Spine tuberculosis, Surgery, Debridement, Bone graft, Internal fixation

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the major diseases that seriously affects the global public health [1]. In recent years, with the emphasis on TB prevention and the improvement and standardization of TB diagnosis and treatment level, the incidence of TB continues to decline [2]. However, the trend of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) mixed TB infection has been increasing [3]. Osteoarticular TB accounts for 1% to 3% of all TB cases, and spinal TB is the most common osteoarticular TB which represents approximately 50% [4]. Spinal TB mostly occurs in thoracolumbar segments and the TB lesions often involve the vertebral body and intervertebral space, spinal TB rarely affects only the adnexa [5]. The onset of spinal TB is insidious, the disease progress is slow, the clinical symptoms of patients are not specific, and the positive detection rate of TB bacillus culture test is low, so the missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis rate of spinal TB is high [6]. Moreover, spinal TB patients are usually complicated with malnutrition and low immunity, and anti-TB drug chemotherapy is not always standardized, these factors along with lead to the increasing incidence of multiple DR-TB (MDR-TB) and make the treatment time window of spinal TB be delayed and the treatment difficulty increase [7–9].

Since the clinical presentation of spinal TB is atypical and there is a lack of specificity, the diagnosis and treatment of spinal TB are mainly based on the clinical experiences of the surgeon [10]. Although 82% to 95% of spinal TB patients can achieve satisfactory clinical efficacy after anti-TB chemotherapy [10, 11], the risk of progressive neurological impairment is up to 10% to 43% [11]. Because conservative treatment for spinal TB, especially thoracic TB, is reported with a high disability rate and mortality rate, surgical intervention is one of the important methods to treat spinal TB [12]. When spinal TB leads to severe low back pain that cannot be alleviated after regular conservative treatment, impairment of the spinal cord or nerve function, giant cold abscess formation, persistent or recurrent TB infection, kyphosis deformity, or spinal segmental instability, surgical treatment should be considered [13]. The objectives of spinal TB surgery are to radically debride TB lesions, relieve spinal cord or nerve compression, correct kyphosis deformity, and reconstruct spinal stability [14].

With the update of spinal TB treatment concept and the improvement of internal fixation instrumentation, how to reduce surgical trauma and optimize individualized comprehensive treatment plan on the basis of ensuring the surgical efficacy has become a hot topic of spinal TB research.

Surgical approach

Anterior surgery

According to the “three-column theory” of the spine, the maintenance of spinal stability depends on the integrity of the anterior and middle column structure [15]. Spinal TB lesions often destroy the anterior or middle column and thus result in spinal instability. Anterior surgery has the following advantages [16–18]: (1) the anterior and middle column can be fully exposed to facilitate the radical debridement of TB lesions, and thus improve cure rate and reduce recurrence risk; (2) the dural sac can be fully exposed during the operation, it is benefit for reducing the risk of spinal cord or nerve injury; (3) in anterior surgery, surgeon can perform structural support bone graft and rigid internal fixation for the anterior and middle columns which can effectively correct the kyphosis, reconstruct the spinal stability; (4) debridement, bone graft and internal fixation can be completed in only one incision, which can significantly shorten the operation and anesthesia time and reduce blood loss.

However, since the ribcage and mediastinal organs limit the exposure of surgical area, anterior surgery often cannot achieve satisfactory deformity correction effect, and has high risk of complications (such as thoracic organ or vessel injury) in treating cervicothoracic junction area TB, especially in patients with kyphosis, which brings great challenges to spinal surgeons [19]. It was reported that anterior surgery was superior to posterior and combined approach surgery in terms of operative time, blood loss and length of hospital stay in the treatment of cervicothoracic junction area TB, but was less effective in maintaining long-term spinal stability and had more complications, such as laryngeal nerve palsy, hemothorax, pneumothorax and hypertrophic scar formation [20, 21]. Lumbosacral TB lesions are generally extensive and often associated with sacral or psoas abscess, and anterior surgery can create a clear surgical field which is benefited for radical debridement of TB lesions and sufficient structural bone graft [22]. Compared with the anterior and posterior combined approach surgery for the treatment of lumbosacral TB, the anterior surgery has shorter operation time, less blood loss, less fusion segments and less surgical trauma, and can also avoid damage to the posterior column structure (such as the posterior ligament complex), which is beneficial to maintain the spinal stability [23]. However, it was also reported that the incidence of pseudarthrosis formation after anterior surgery for spinal TB is high, the correction and maintenance effect of spinal deformity is poor [24], moreover, there are also high risks of screw or titanium plate displacement or fracture especially in cases with long segmental screw and plate internal fixation [25].

Based on previous experiences, we suggest that: (1) when the vertebrae destruction is > 3 spinal motion segments, the correction and maintenance effect of spinal deformity of anterior surgery is poor, and the risk of postoperative kyphosis aggravation, neurological impairment and internal fixation related complications are also high; (2) unilateral anterior instrumentation of the TB lesion segments in anterior surgery may lead to asymmetric support forces on both sides of the spine, resulting in potential biomechanical instability; (3) when the vertebral body of the affected segment is too damaged to implant two screws, it is often necessary to extend the fixed segment at the expense of normal spinal motion; (4) the risk of injury to thoracoabdominal organs, blood vessels, and nerves is higher in anterior surgery.

In our opinion, the indications for anterior surgery for spinal TB are: (1) the range of TB lesion is relatively limited (< 3 spinal motion segments), and the TB lesions are mainly confined to the anterior and middle columns of the spine; (2) the residual height of the vertebrae after debridement can satisfy the implantation of screws via anterior approach (residual height > 1/3 of the normal vertebrae height); (3) mild or moderate kyphosis (kyphosis angle < 40°); (4) lumbosacral TB patients complicated with presacral abscess or psoas muscle abscess.

Posterior surgery

Spinal TB lesions are mainly located in the anterior and middle columns of the spine, and paravertebral abscesses or efflux abscesses are also mostly located in the front of the vertebral body. Therefore, anterior surgery is considered as the gold standard for treating spinal TB in previous studies, however, in recent years, posterior surgery has also gradually become popular [26]. Posterior surgery, such as costal transverse process approach and pedicle approach, has been proven to achieve satisfactory clinical efficacy in the treatment of spinal TB [27, 28]. Posterior surgery can debride TB lesions and decompress the spinal cord or nerves adequately, meanwhile, posterior internal fixation system effectively correct kyphosis and maintain long-term stability of the spine, reducing surgical and instrument-related complications [29, 30].

It has been reported that posterior surgery has similar efficacy with anterior surgery in promoting neurological recovery and maintaining symptomatic improvement for spinal TB [31, 32]. Posterior surgery has the advantages of small surgical trauma, strong ability of deformity correction and spinal stability maintenance. However, some scholars also have the following concerns about the posterior surgery for spinal TB [33–36]: (1) the spinal TB lesions are mainly located in the anterior and middle columns, posterior surgery may bring TB bacilli into the relatively healthy posterior columns and thus lead to TB infection spread; (2) the surgical vision of posterior surgery is small, so there is a high risk of intraoperative nerve and dural sac injury, meanwhile, it is difficult to fully expose and debride TB lesions, especially for multi-segmental TB and lumbosacral TB, and the residual TB lesions is easy to lead to TB recurrence; (3) posterior surgery is not conducive to structural bone graft of the anterior column; (4) posterior surgery damages the stability of the posterior column and thus requires long segmental fixation at the expense of normal spinal motion segments; (5) the posterior approach resulted in iatrogenic injury of the paraspinal muscles, and the patients may suffer with long-term low back pain after surgery. According to our previous experiences, posterior surgery has the following advantages [37, 38]: (1) all the surgical procedures, such as TB lesion debridement, spinal cord or nerve decompression, bone graft fusion, deformity correction and rigid internal fixation, can be performed in only a one incision; (2) all patients obtained bone graft fusion, satisfactory neurological recovery and deformity correction and maintenance in postoperative follow-up; (3) posterior surgery can reduce the risk of injury to thoracic and abdominal organs and large blood vessels, and the incidence of complications such as failure of internal fixation. Spinal TB at the cervicothoracic or lumbosacral junction can also be treated by posterior surgery which has been proven with satisfactory results [21, 39].

We believe that the indications of posterior surgery for spinal TB are as follows: (1) spinal TB located in the adnexa, with or without posterior cold abscess; (2) the range of TB lesions range < 3 spinal motion segments, the bone destruction degree of vertebral body was light, the TB lesion was limited to one side of vertebral body without obvious psoas abscess formation; (3) lumbosacral TB with iliac vessel bifurcation located in front of the TB lesion; (4) cervicothoracic junction area TB; (5) patients with multiple anterior surgical history and anatomical structure cannot be accurately identified; (6) elderly patients complicated with multiple underlying diseases and poor operation tolerance.

Combined anterior and posterior surgery

The combined anterior and posterior surgery has the advantages of both anterior and posterior approaches: (1) sufficient decompression of the spinal cord or nerves; (2) performing the correction of deformity and internal fixation via the posterior approach; (3) performing TB lesion radical debridement, structural bone graft and selective anterior internal fixation via the anterior approach. The combined anterior and posterior surgery can effectively remove TB lesions, correct kyphosis, and maintain spinal stability [40]. However, it also has some concerns, such as large trauma, long operation time, more blood loss, complicated surgical procedures, and high risk of injury to thoracic and abdominal organs or blood vessels [41].

The combined anterior and posterior surgery has achieved satisfactory efficacy in the treatment of cervicothoracic junction area TB, but at the expense of the range of motion of the cervical spine [19, 42]. Some scholars believe that lumbosacral TB is especially suitable for the combined anterior and posterior surgery, which has the advantages of clear surgical vision, radical debridement of TB lesions, good correction effect of deformity and high rate of bone graft fusion [23, 43]. Anterior structural bone graft combined with posterior rigid internal fixation can not only reduce the axial compressive stress in the bone graft area, increase the stability of the spine, but also reduce the risk of complications associated with bone graft and implants [44, 45]. Minimally invasive surgery

Previous studies have suggested that spinal TB should be treated with open surgery to achieve radical debridement of TB lesions, bone graft fusion, and strong internal fixation. However, with the development of minimally invasive spine surgery and the update of the treatment concept of spinal TB, some surgeons have tried to use CT-guided percutaneous catheter drainage for the treatment of vertebral TB and paravertebral or iliopsoas abscess, and achieved satisfactory clinical efficacy, which accumulated preliminary experiences for the minimally invasive treatment of spinal TB [46, 47]. But it has also been questioned that this surgical method may lead to the failure of local anti-TB drug concentrations to reach an effective bactericidal dose, resulting in increased TB drug resistance [48]. Zhang et al. treated lumbar TB and paravertebral abscess by CT-guided puncture and catheter drainage combined with local high concentration of anti-TB drugs chemotherapy, and achieved satisfactory clinical efficacy [49]. Yang et al. treated thoracolumbar TB by posterior minimally invasive laminectomy and decompression combined with local anti-TB drugs chemotherapy, and achieved satisfactory clinical efficacy, but some patients suffered mild kyphosis aggravation during postoperative follow-up [50]. Therefore, only performing the TB abscess drainage without radical debridement of TB lesions and sufficient decompression of spinal cord or nerve may lead to poor long-term therapeutic effect and a high risk of complications, such as neurological impairment, kyphosis aggravation, and TB recurrence [51, 52]. In addition, some surgeons tried to use fenestration decompression or minimally invasive transforaminal approach for TB lesions debridement, bone graft fusion, and internal fixation to treat thoracic and lumbar TB, and achieved satisfactory clinical outcomes, but showed complications such as dural tears, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and poor placement of pedicle screws [53, 54]. In recent years, with the development of endoscopic technology, some scholars have reported that thoracoscopic-assisted TB debridement, bone graft fusion, and internal fixation can achieve satisfactory results in the treatment of thoracic and lumbar TB [55, 56].

We believe that minimally invasive CT/B-ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage combined with local anti-TB chemotherapy can achieve good clinical results in the treatment of spinal TB with mild vertebral destruction, especially in patients with simple paraspinal or iliopsoas cold abscess [57]. In our opinion, the indications of minimally invasive percutaneous catheter drainage combined with local anti-TB chemotherapy for spinal TB are as follows: (1) short-segment spinal TB with mild vertebral destruction, mild spinal cord compression, and mild kyphosis deformity; (2) simple paravertebral, iliopsoas or superficial cold abscess on the back. However, open surgery is still required in patients with severe spinal cord or nerve compression, severe kyphosis, and severe vertebral destruction which leads to spinal instability.

Selection of internal fixation segment

Intraoperative internal fixation of spinal TB can effectively correct spinal deformity, maintain the correction effect, reconstruct the stability of the surgical segments, and promote the fusion of bone graft. At present, there are three main choices of internal fixation segment in spinal TB surgery, including long segment fixation, short segment fixation and diseased intervertebral fixation [58]. However, there is no unified principle for the selection of internal fixation segments in spinal TB surgery.

With high strength and good stability, long segment fixation can effectively share the axial load of the anterior column and promote bone graft fusion while maintaining spinal long-term stability, however, the operation time is long, the intraoperative bleeding is much, the operation procedures are complex, and the cost is high, and there are risks of postoperative pseudarthrosis formation and internal fixation failure [59]. Moreover, long segment fixation will sacrifice the range of motion of the normal motor units of the spine and increase the risk of adjacent vertebral disease and junction kyphosis [25]. Compared with long segment fixation, short segment fixation reduces fixed segments, retains more normal motor units of the spine, has short operation time, less intraoperative bleeding, and relatively low cost, and reduces the incidence of adjacent vertebral degeneration and failure of internal fixation [60]. In previous studies, both long segment fixation and short segment fixation have been widely used and reported, but a new view of diseased intervertebral fixation has been proposed in recent years and reported with satisfactory results [61, 62]. It is concerned that diseased intervertebral fixation cannot provide effective fixation strength and meet the requirements of spinal stability reconstruction [63]. However, biomechanical experiments and clinical studies have confirmed that single-segment diseased intervertebral fixation can not only meet the loading force requirements of spine stability maintenance and bone graft fusion, but also reduce the surgical trauma and the risk of complications compared with long segment fixation [64].

Regardless of the internal fixation methods, it should be emphasized that the selection of fixed segments is determined by the residual height of the vertebral body after TB lesions debridement [65, 66]: (1) anterior or posterior intervertebral fixation is feasible when the height of residual vertebral body is greater than 2/3 of the normal vertebral body height; (2) when the height of the residual vertebral body is between 1/3 to 2/3 of the normal vertebral body height, the anterior screw–plate system usually cannot be implanted, thus anterior short segment fixation or posterior diseased intervertebral fixation is feasible; (3) posterior diseased intervertebral fixation with a short pedicle screw (25 to 35 mm) or posterior short segment fixation can be performed when the residual vertebral body height was less than 1/3 of the normal vertebral body height. However, the diseased intervertebral fixation is contraindicated for spinal TB patients with incomplete upper or lower endplates of affected vertebrae, severe pedicle destruction, severe osteoporosis, and severe kyphosis who are unable to undergo effective spinal stability reconstruction [66, 67].

According to the previous experience, we think that: (1) long segment fixation is suitable for static or clinically cured spinal TB, especially for those with severe kyphotic deformity which require osteotomy and correction or those with severe osteoporosis; (2) for spinal TB in active period, the choice of internal fixed segment should be according to the size of the residual vertebral body after TB lesions debridement, on the basis of ensuring effective spinal stability reconstruction, it is recommended to use short segment fixation or single-segment disease intervertebral fixation, to retain the normal motor unit of the spine as far as possible, and to reduce surgical trauma and the risk of postoperative complications, in order to achieve the best clinical efficacy.

Debridement of TB lesion

Debridement of the TB lesions is an important procedure of spinal TB surgery, and also an important factor determining the therapeutic effect. At present, most studies conclude that spinal TB lesion should be radically debrided to reduce the risk of TB recurrence and drug resistance [68]. It was reported that the risk factors of spinal TB recurrence after surgery were incomplete TB lesion debridement, increasing DR-TB cases, non-standard anti-TB chemotherapy, poor nutritional status of patients, and insufficient internal fixation strength [69]. Therefore, radical debridement of TB lesions, structural bone graft, rigid internal fixation, standard and individualized ant-TB chemotherapy, and postoperative local lavage and drainage are recommended as the main keys to the success of the spinal TB surgery [69, 70].

The traditional view is that radical TB lesion debridement should include cold abscess, caseous necrotic tissue, residual intervertebral disc, sinus tract, sequestrum, and tuberculous granulation tissue [68]. In addition, some scholars believe that on the basis of the traditional TB lesion debridement range, it should also include the sclerosis bone, TB cavity, bone bridge and the sub-health bone tissue outside the central TB lesion (that is, the sub-health bone tissues within 4 mm around the lesion should be all radically removed) [71].

However, with the deep understanding of spinal TB, some scholars think that under the premise of standardized anti-TB chemotherapy, limited debridement of TB lesions can reduce iatrogenic injury, maintain the stability of spine, create a stable local mechanical environment for bone graft fusion, which is conducive to promoting the recovery of TB [72]. Ma et al. thought that radical TB lesion debridement was a relative concept, and debridement of traditional so-called TB lesions and preservation of the healthy and sub-healthy bone tissue could be also considered as radical debridement [73]. According to Gao et al., only the central TB lesions need to be debrided, for the tissues outside the central lesion, if it cannot be cured by anti-TB chemotherapy, it needs to be completely removed; but if the lesion is small and can be cured by anti-TB chemotherapy, it does not need to be removed [74].

With the development of minimally invasive spine surgery techniques, scholars have tried to use CT/B-ultrasound guided puncture tube drainage combined with anti-TB drug chemotherapy, minimally invasive posterior approach fenestration or transforaminal approach to remove TB lesions, or even the simple internal fixation without debridement to treat spinal TB [46, 47, 50, 52, 53, 75, 76]. These methods all have achieved satisfactory clinical efficacy although none of them performed radical debridement. However, it is worth noting that these treatment methods have their strict indications, including mild vertebral bone destruction, non-serious kyphosis, no obvious spinal cord or nerve compression [47, 52, 76].

We agree with most scholars, that spinal TB lesions should be radically removed to improve the cure rate and reduce the occurrence of TB resistance. The debridement range of the TB lesions should not only include the traditional TB lesions, but also include the hardened wall, bone bridge, large TB cavities and the sub-healthy bone tissue outside the central lesion. If there are large TB lesions under the sclerosing bone, they should be radically removed, however, if the number of TB lesions under sclerosing bone is small and the volume is not large, they are not need to be removed. We believe that radical debridement of TB lesion, standardized anti-TB chemotherapy, structural autograft bone graft, and rigid internal fixation are the keys to the surgical treatment of spinal TB.

Bone grafting

Bone graft method

In order to achieve permanent stable reconstruction of the spine after the radical debridement of spinal TB lesions, surgeons usually need to perform spinal fusion. The methods of spinal fusion mainly include intervertebral fusion and posterolateral fusion [77]. Transverse interprocess bone graft fusion is the most common method of posterolateral fusion [78]. Phillips et al. thought that the simple transverse interprocess bone graft is an effective bone graft and fusion method [79], but it was reported that the strength intertransverse process fusion is low, and it is rarely used alone at present [80, 81]. Intervertebral bone graft, including autologous bone graft and fusion device bone graft, has been reported to be more in line with the biomechanical requirements of spine, a high fusion rate and satisfactory clinical efficacy in clinical application, and is the most commonly used spinal fusion method [82–84]. There are a variety of bone graft methods for spinal TB surgery. Most scholars agree with the idea of intervertebral bone graft between the affected vertebrae after the debridement of TB lesions [85]. However, whether to perform posterolateral bone graft fusion at the same time and the selection of the segment of bone graft fusion are still controversial.

At present, many scholars recommend intervertebral fusion plus posterior or posterolateral transverse process fusion in spinal TB surgery to form a 360° circular fusion, so as to improve the high fusion rate and stability [86]. In the posterolateral fusion, the grafted bone particles are located in the normal bone tissue area, so the fusion speed of bone graft is faster [87]. Posterolateral fusion is an enhancement of the interbody fusion between the diseased vertebrae, it can not only promote anterior intervertebral fusion, but also reduce the force stress of the posterior internal fixation device and the risk of internal fixation failure, kyphosis deformity aggravation, and nerve function damage, especially when the infusion or delayed union of intervertebral vertebrae causes the bone graft to sink, slip, or even fracture [88]. Yin et al. tried to perform the posterolateral fusion at all the posterior fixed segments in spinal TB surgery; although the strong fixation effect was achieved after surgery, the normal spinal motor units were sacrificed, and the risk of stiffness of the surgical area and degeneration of adjacent segments was thus increased [89].

In our opinion, the choice of bone graft method in spinal TB surgery should be based on the size of vertebral bone destruction [90]. Spinal TB is mainly characterized by bone destruction of the anterior and middle columns, and therefore, intervertebral bone graft can reduce the load and stress of the internal fixation device, which is more in line with the requirements of biomechanics [91]. Therefore, in the anterior surgery of spinal TB, we recommend intervertebral fusion and structural support bone graft, especially when bone destruction is severe. Intervertebral fusion is also preferred in posterior surgery of spinal TB, and it is often combined with posterolateral bone graft fusion, in order to achieve the optimal spinal stability reconstruction.

Bone graft material

At present, the most common used bone graft materials in spinal TB surgery include autogenous bone and allogeneic bone [92].

Autogenous bone graft mainly include autogenous ilium or rib and titanium cage filled with autogenous cancellous bone. It was reported that autogenous bone graft materials all have good ability of bone formation, bone conduction and bone induction, and the clinical follow-up showed that the fusion rate of these bone graft methods were all satisfactory [93–95]. However, the biomechanical strength of the autogenous ilium and ribs is poor, so the grafted bone is prone to displacement, fracture, or absorption, and there are also donor site complications [96, 97]. The problem of titanium mesh subsidence is also getting more and more attention from spine surgeons, especially in patients with osteoporosis [98, 99].

Clinically used allograft bone is mainly derived from the donor bone tissue. With the improvement of the modern bone bank technology, the immunogenicity of the allograft bone has been greatly reduced, and the rejection reaction is reduced, and the safety has been greatly improved [100]. Allogeneic bone has strong bone conductivity, but its osteoinductive activity is weak and almost has no osteogenic activity [101]. The greatest advantage of allogeneic bone graft is that it is not limited by the size of bone defects and can be used for the repair and reconstruction of larger bone defects without the concern of bone source [102]. Allogeneic bone was used for bone defect reconstruction after cervical TB lesions debridement, and reported with satisfactory clinical efficacy, but its bone graft fusion rate was lower than that of autologous bone [103, 104].

Our previous study showed that both titanium cage graft and iliac crest graft can achieve good clinical efficacy for single-segment thoracolumbar TB [37]. We also found that for single-segment thoracic/lumbar TB with residual vertebral height was > 1/2 of the normal vertebral height after debridement, autogenous granular bone grafts had similar efficacy as titanium mesh and iliac crest grafts, with the advantages of less surgical trauma and shorter bone graft fusion time [105, 106].

Therefore, we recommend that: (1) when vertebral bone defect < 1 motor unit of the spine, autogenous iliac bone or local bone fragment graft can be used; (2) when vertebral bone defect > 1 motor unit of the spine, titanium cage bone graft can be used; (3) titanium cage is not suitable for patients with severe osteoporosis since the risk of complications such as titanium cage subsidence and displacement is high, and thus the application of titanium cage should be supplemented by effective internal fixation.

Emerging technologies in spinal TB surgery

With the development of digital orthopedic technology, the reconstruction of the huge space after the resection of the diseased vertebrae can be realized through the precise individual design of the 3D printed vertebrae [107, 108]. Zhang et al. reported the first clinical application of 3-dimensional printing-assisted cervical anterior bilateral pedicle screw fixation of an artificial vertebral body for cervical tuberculosis and achieved satisfactory efficacy [109]. In order to reduce the adhesion of mycobacterium TB to 3D printed artificial vertebra and further improve the efficacy of local killing of mycobacterium TB, scholars combined 3D printing technology with drug slow release to propose a new drug loading 3D printed vertebral body. Dong et al. put forward two feasible schemes [110]: one is to attach anti-TB drugs to the surface of internal plants through low temperature drying technology to achieve antibacterial effect, the other is to combine anti-TB drugs with artificial vertebrae materials in the printing process through 3D printing technology. The short-term efficacy of 3D printed artificial vertebrae in the reconstruction of spinal TB is worthy of recognition, but more cases and data are needed to support it.

In recent years, the rapid development of orthopedic surgery robot-assisted technology can reduce surgical trauma, fluoroscopy times and operation time, improve the accuracy of pinning and surgical safety [108]. Pan et al. treated 15 cases of thoracolumbar TB with robot-assisted and minimally invasive access via transforaminal expansion approach for lesion removal, bone graft, and internal fixation, and found that compared with traditional posterior open operation, the use of robot-assisted minimally invasive access could reduce the operation time and intraoperative bleeding, minimizes surgical trauma, and obtain definite effectiveness [111].

With the progress of artificial intelligence technology, in recent years, some scholars have applied it to the diagnosis and treatment of spinal TB, and achieved good results. By constructing nomogram and machine learning algorithms, Dong et al. developed a novel predictive model and web calculator to evaluate blood transfusion risk after spinal fusion for spinal TB [112]. Yasin et al. established an interpretable machine learning model that could predict prolonged hospital stays after surgery for TB spondylitis patients, which can provide valuable insights for treatments and a web-based application was implemented [113]. Moreover, Feng et al. developed a prediction model for spinal cord injury in spinal tuberculosis patients using multiple machine learning algorithms [114].

Drug-resistant spinal TB

According to the 2016 WHO Global TB Report, there were about 10.4 million new cases of TB worldwide in 2015, including about 480,000 cases of MDR-TB, accounting for about 20% of the global number of TB patients [115]. The lack of normative understanding of anti-TB drug therapy, unreasonable chemotherapy regimen, emphasis on surgery instead of chemotherapy, and inadequate health education and supervision of patients by doctors make drug-resistant spinal TB more and more common [116].

The key to the treatment of drug-resistant spinal TB lies in early diagnosis. The current diagnostic methods mainly include [117, 118]: (1) laboratory testing and pathological examination: Mycobacterium TB culture (bacterial type detection), molecular biology detection, pathological molecular biology detection and pathological examination; (2) clinical judgment without objective laboratory evidence: comprehensive judgment is made based on the patient’s history, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, diagnostic anti-TB drug therapy and other results. Among them, molecular biology is the most advanced and rapid diagnostic method at present, which can determine the drug-resistance of Mycobacterium TB according to the gene mutation at the detection site, mainly including GeneXpert MTB/RIF, linear probe hybridization technology, etc., which has broad application prospects.

Standardized anti-TB chemotherapy is the key to the treatment of drug-resistant spinal TB. For surgical patients, a full course of anti-TB chemotherapy should be carried out before the operation, and the lesion should be completely removed during the operation to maintain the stability of the spine, and anti-TB chemotherapy should be insisted after the operation [119]. In addition, the risk of reoperation for drug-resistant spinal TB is high, which should be given sufficient attention [120].

Conclusion

The treatment of spinal TB should follow a comprehensive and individualized treatment strategy. When performing spinal TB surgery, it must be considered comprehensively according to the location of the TB lesion, the extent of vertebral destruction, the degree of spinal cord compression, and the condition of the patient's systemic condition. In conclusion, on the basis of standardized anti-TB chemotherapy, the number of the fixed and fused segments should be reduced as much as possible to reduce the surgical trauma and maintain the stability of the spine, thus achieve the optimal efficacy of surgery.

The future research of spinal TB mainly focuses on the following aspects: (1) gene diagnosis and drug resistance research: in recent years, with the development of gene technology, gene diagnosis and drug resistance of spinal TB have become a hot spot. Research priorities include the use of genetic testing technology to more accurately diagnose spinal TB, as well as exploring the resistance mechanisms of Mycobacterium TB to develop more effective drug resistance treatment strategies. (2) Minimally invasive surgical technique: the application of minimally invasive surgical techniques in the treatment of spinal TB is gradually increasing. This technique is able to reduce surgical trauma, speed recovery, and help reduce complications. In the future, with the further development of technology, minimally invasive surgery is expected to play a more important role in the treatment of spinal TB. (3) Immunotherapy and individualized therapy: immunotherapy and individualized therapy are important directions of spinal TB treatment in the future. By studying the immune system’s response to Mycobacterium TB and developing a personalized treatment regimen based on a patient’s specific circumstances, it is expected to improve treatment effectiveness and reduce side effects. (4) Complications prevention and rehabilitation research: patients with spinal TB often face serious complications such as paraplegia and kyphosis. Future research will focus more on the development of prevention and rehabilitation measures to reduce the occurrence of complications and improve the quality of life of patients. (5) Interdisciplinary cooperation: the treatment of spinal TB requires the cooperation of orthopedics, neurology, radiology and other disciplines. Future research will emphasize the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration to provide more comprehensive and effective treatment for patients with spinal TB by integrating expertise and technology from different fields.
