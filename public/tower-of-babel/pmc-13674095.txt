Mind the gap: Pulmonary artery autograft patch for repair of a long-gap interrupted aortic arch type A
Authors: Sameh M Said, Ahmed Wahba, Yasin Essa
Published: 2026

Pulmonary artery autograft patch used to bridge long-gap interrupted aortic arch type A.

Central Message

Pulmonary artery autograft patch enables tension-free, single-stage repair of long-gap interrupted aortic arch type A in neonates, offering growth potential and excellent early clinical outcomes.

Interrupted aortic arch (IAA) is a rare congenital anomaly, accounting for approximately 1.5% of all congenital heart diseases, with mortality exceeding 80% during the first month of life without intervention.1 Advances in neonatal cardiopulmonary bypass and surgical techniques have permitted routine single-stage biventricular repair; however, the optimal method of arch reconstruction remains debated, particularly when a long gap precludes tension-free direct anastomosis.2,3

The severity of IAA, including the type A subtype, is significantly influenced by the length of the gap between the aortic segments, which presents surgical challenges for repair; however, the definition of a “long gap” in IAA is not uniform and although it may seem that anatomical measurements, such as the distance between the interrupted segments of the aorta, and the clinical context, including patient age and associated cardiac anomalies, should be used to determine this, we find it not realistic and not entirely accurate, and a major part of this is attributed to the surgeon’s experience and intraoperative judgment. To us, “long gap IAA” is an interruption that could be associated with anastomotic tension or compression of nearby anatomic structures such as the left bronchus or left pulmonary artery if it is repaired primarily and therefore a great deal of judgment and experience play a role in this.

Multiple techniques have been described, including direct anastomosis, which remains the preferred approach for us and many others, especially when the gap length allows tension-free repair. However, in cases in which the gap is prohibitive, carotid artery turndown, subclavian flap interposition, and patch aortoplasty with various materials may be considered to bridge the discontinuity and reduce tension at the repair site.1,2 Some surgeons rarely resort to interposition grafts on first-time repair, favoring techniques that avoid prosthetic material where possible.

No technique has proven its superiority. Extensive mobilization of the descending aorta allows the performance of a direct anastomosis in most cases, but the tension on the suture line and the presence of residual ductal tissue can result in a considerable rate of recurrence. Direct anastomosis carries a recurrence rate of 5% to 25% and risks bronchial compression from excessive tension.3,4 This includes all patients with IAA and it is quite challenging to specify the rate of these complications for each subtype. Prosthetic patches are associated with aneurysm formation and lack growth potential, whereas homograft and pericardial patches have demonstrated considerable recurrence rates.3,4 Pulmonary autograft patch aortoplasty, introduced by Roussin and colleagues,3 offers viable autologous tissue with growth potential and has demonstrated 100% freedom from recurrent arch obstruction at midterm follow-up. However, its application in type A IAA, the least-common subtype, characterized by interruption distal to the left subclavian artery, with long-segment discontinuity, has been scarcely reported. In this report, we present a neonate with type A IAA and a large posterior malalignment ventricular septal defect (VSD) in whom the long gap between the left subclavian artery and proximal descending aorta precluded direct anastomosis. We describe the successful single-stage repair using a pulmonary autograft patch to bridge the aortic arch gap, emphasizing the technical nuances and key procedural steps.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was not required because of the case report nature of the current manuscript; however, parental consent was obtained.

Patient's Presentation

A neonate was diagnosed prenatally with IAA and large posterior malalignment VSD. Echocardiography demonstrated good biventricular function with a mildly hypoplastic left ventricle and left ventricular outflow tract, but this did not seem prohibitive to proceed with biventricular repair. Computed tomography confirmed type A IAA with a long gap between the left subclavian artery and the proximal descending aorta. After stabilization and prostaglandin initiation, the patient underwent single-stage repair.

Surgical Technique

The procedure was performed via median sternotomy, and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated via dual arterial (indirect cannulation of the innominate artery, ie, using a 3.5-mm polytetrafluoroethylene graft) and the main pulmonary artery (MPA) and bicaval cannulation (Video 1). Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion was used at 28 °C. A free autograft was harvested from the anterior wall of the MPA and was used to bridge the gap present between the left subclavian artery and proximal descending aorta. The anterior wall of this connection was augmented with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch (Figure 1).

Illustration showing repair of type A interrupted aortic arch where a pulmonary autograft patch (dark red) is harvested from the anterior wall of the dilated main pulmonary artery, forming the posterior wall of the neoaortic arch to bridge the long gap between the left subclavian artery and the proximal descending aorta. The anterior wall of the arch is completed with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch (light brown), achieving a tension-free, arc-shaped reconstruction. The main pulmonary artery harvest site is reconstructed with a second decellularized homograft patch.

The VSD was then closed through the right atrium with a bovine pericardial patch. The MPA harvest site was reconstructed with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch.

Outcome

The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed a widely patent left ventricular outflow tract, no residual VSD, excellent biventricular function, and widely patent aortic arch. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 8. He continued to do well during his 6-month follow-up with no gradient across the aortic arch.

Discussion

Management of IAA with long-segment discontinuity remains a significant surgical challenge. In such cases, tension on the anastomosis even after extensive mobilization of the aortic segments might contribute to the outcome. Excessive tension at the anastomotic site increases the risk of postoperative complications, including anastomotic disruption, bronchial or pulmonary artery compression, and recurrent arch obstruction.2,3

In this context, several techniques have been proposed to avoid the aforementioned complications. These include carotid artery turndown, subclavian flap interposition, and patch aortoplasty with various materials or the use of an interposition grafts however prosthetic grafts use mandates future reoperations. An additional technique that has been proposed by Urencio and colleagues4 is the ascending aortic slide, which is a modification of Norwood arch reconstruction. Outcomes after primary nonpatch repairs vary by IAA subtype and gap length. Although complications such as bronchial compression and recurrence of obstruction have been reported, they are not universal and depend heavily on individual anatomy and surgical technique.

When it comes to the use of patches, pulmonary autograft patch aortoplasty offers a valuable solution by bridging the long gap using viable, autologous arterial tissue that is capable of growth and remodeling over time. This approach effectively eliminates anastomotic tension while preserving native tissue characteristics, which can be especially advantageous in the dynamic environment of the growing neonate's cardiovascular system.3

The choice of patch material is crucial in determining both early- and long-term outcomes. Roussin and colleagues3 reported 100% freedom from recurrent arch obstruction at a mean follow-up of 29 months in patients receiving pulmonary autograft patches, a result significantly superior to outcomes observed with direct anastomosis (81%) and homograft or pericardial patches (28%). Similarly, Lee and colleagues5 confirmed the durability of autologous vascular patch augmentation, noting no recoarctation events at a median follow-up of 24.8 months. The pulmonary artery wall, particularly in cases with ductus-dependent systemic circulation, is naturally dilated and possesses native arterial structure, making it ideal for use as a patch. This tissue's inherent growth potential supports long-term aortic arch patency and reduces the likelihood of future interventions.

Alternative techniques carry distinct limitations. The carotid artery turndown carries a 36.2% reintervention rate with 57.4% freedom from reintervention at 15 years.1 Lee and colleagues5 noted this technique was predominantly applied to type B IAA (98%), with 67% 15-year survival comparing unfavorably with single-stage end-to-side repair (97% at 18 years). The subclavian flap technique creates an arc-shaped aorta but demonstrated a 30% reintervention rate and requires subclavian artery sacrifice.2

In summary, pulmonary autograft patch aortoplasty represents an effective and reliable technique for the single-stage repair of type A IAA with long-segment discontinuity. It allows for tension-free reconstruction of the aortic arch using biological tissue capable of growth, thereby minimizing risks of recurrence and vascular complications such as bronchial and left pulmonary artery compression. Given these advantages, this approach should be considered a valuable option in the surgical management of complex IAA cases, especially those involving extensive arch defects not amenable to direct repair.
