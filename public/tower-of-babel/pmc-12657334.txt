Truth, power, and the crisis of forensic independence
Authors: Aaron Olson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractForensic laboratories operating within law enforcement hierarchies face an inherent structural conflict between scientific independence and institutional loyalty. When scientists challenge prosecutorial narratives or expose systemic problems, they frequently experience professional retaliation, forced resignations, or career marginalization. This editorial examines documented cases of such retaliation and argues that these patterns reflect deeper cultural mechanisms that protect institutional authority by expelling dissenters who threaten the myth of forensic objectivity. Drawing on historical parallels to religious and state orthodoxy and René Girard's theory of scapegoating, the analysis demonstrates that these conflicts are not isolated incidents but symptoms of a fundamental governance failure. Genuine scientific independence, mandatory full disclosure, external peer review, and whistleblower protections are essential to restore public trust and ensure that forensic science is guided by truth rather than institutional interests.Keywords: Forensic science, Scientific independence, Institutional bias, Laboratory oversight, Retaliation in science, Forensic governance, Whistleblowing

The structure of authority in forensic laboratories

Forensic science faces an institutional crisis: laboratories claim scientific independence while operating under the administrative and financial control of law enforcement hierarchies. This tension between truth and power is ancient, appearing whenever those who generate knowledge must answer to those who wield authority. This editorial explores the influence of institutional authority on the determination of knowledge within both laboratory and courtroom contexts. It encourages forensic scientists to reflect not only on the methods used in evidence analysis, but also on the specific truths sanctioned by those in positions of power.

The credibility of forensic science depends on the public's trust that laboratories are objective, transparent, and independent of the institutions whose cases they serve. Yet, when laboratories operate under the administrative or financial control of law enforcement agencies, scientists face subtle but powerful pressures to conform to prosecutorial expectations. Those who reinforce official narratives are rewarded with deference and institutional protection, while those who question or contradict them often face heightened scrutiny, marginalization, or retaliation.

From divine truth to state doctrine

Historically, religion provided a unifying moral framework for society. In the modern world, that role has been largely assumed by the state. Like the Church in earlier eras, it defines acceptable belief, provides for those in need, and enforces orthodoxy. In ancient texts, those who dared to defy the divine order were condemned. The Biblical story of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego recounts how three men who refused to bow before King Nebuchadnezzar's golden idol were cast into a furnace for their disobedience [1]. Their ordeal symbolized the cost of independence in the face of absolute power. Today, the mechanisms of punishment are more subtle. The flames are reputational and professional, but the purpose is the same: to preserve the legitimacy of the governing order by consuming the dissenter.

The scientist who questions the state's conclusions stands in the same lineage as those ancient resisters. Galileo Galilei, whose telescopic observations contradicted Church doctrine, was condemned not because his science was wrong but because it threatened the authority of the institution that claimed a monopoly on truth. His forced recantation was an act of political preservation, not intellectual correction. In a similar fashion, modern forensic scientists who challenge institutional orthodoxy risk professional exile, even when their claims are empirically sound. The machinery of authority has simply changed its symbols. Where the Church once guarded the heavens, the state now guards the laboratory.

Differential scrutiny: state affiliation vs. independent expertise

Scientists employed by the state are often shielded from serious scrutiny; their findings are presumed credible simply because they align with institutional orthodoxy. The system grants them a kind of immunity, treating their reports and testimony as self-validating. Yet when an independent expert presents data that contradicts the state's position, that same system reverses course. Suddenly, every calculation, credential, and word is examined under a magnifying glass, and every nuance is interpreted as bias or betrayal. As De Roo and colleagues have shown, reports from prestigious, state-affiliated laboratories are subjected to far less critical examination than those from unfamiliar or independent sources, even when the latter are equally or more accurate [2]. This imbalance does not merely distort perception; it shapes outcomes. When credibility is conferred by institutional affiliation rather than by evidence, truth becomes conditional on authority.

Transparency and retaliation in toxicology

These dynamics are not theoretical. A recent publication, Errors in Toxicology Testing and the Need for Full Discovery, documents this recurring pattern within forensic science [3]. Gregory Ohlson, a senior toxicologist with the Arizona Department of Public Safety, testified that laboratories should disclose full batch data to ensure analytical transparency. His agency ordered him to conform his testimony to its preferred narrative. When he refused, he was forced into early retirement and later denied relief despite federal acknowledgment that his speech was constitutionally protected. In Vermont, Darcy Richardson reported that state laboratory technicians were artificially spiking control samples to achieve “passing” results. Her warnings were ignored, and she ultimately left public service. Courts eventually dismissed cases on the very grounds she had identified.

Retaliation and reassignment: the Wilmington Crime Lab case

The same retaliatory structure appears in the case of Bethany Pridgen, former director of the Wilmington, North Carolina, Crime Lab. Pridgen testified candidly about missing drug evidence and laboratory mismanagement. These were disclosures that senior officials feared would jeopardize prosecutions [4]. According to her complaint and subsequent reporting, rather than address the substance of her concerns, authorities transferred the entire lab out of city control to eliminate her position. They later attempted to discredit her as an expert witness.

Cognitive bias and institutional resistance: the Dror case

The experience of cognitive psychologist Itiel Dror offers another example of this pattern [5,6]. His paper, Cognitive bias in forensic pathology, showed that forensic experts, including pathologists, can be unconsciously influenced by irrelevant case information, such as the subject's race or information about the subject's caregiver [7]. Although the study was methodologically sound and later cleared of any misconduct by his university and the publishing journal, it provoked intense backlash from some in the forensic community. Professional associations demanded retraction, colleagues questioned his integrity, and formal complaints were filed against him. The controversy did not expose flaws in the research but rather highlighted discomfort with its implications, suggesting that human bias can infiltrate even the most credentialed scientific judgments. Dror was not accused of error so much as punished for revealing one.

Preserving the myth of objectivity

The experience of Dror, Ohlson, Richardson, and Pridgen illustrates how institutions protect their authority not by confronting error, but by attempting to expel those who reveal it. Together, these examples reveal a deeper cultural instinct to defend authority against those who question it.

The defensive reactions suggest that the problem is not simply administrative or procedural, but cultural. It touches a deeper human tendency to protect shared myths of objectivity. René Girard's theory of scapegoating provides a fitting lens for understanding this process [8]. For Girard, communities preserve unity by expelling the individual who exposes the internal contradictions of their shared myth. In the forensic sciences, the myth is that state laboratories are impartial guardians of truth. When an outsider undermines that belief, the institution responds not with reflection but with expulsion. The dissenter becomes the scapegoat through whom the collective restores harmony. The punishment of Galileo, the isolation of modern whistleblowers, and the marginalization of independent forensic experts all stem from the same anthropological mechanism: the defense of order against the contaminating influence of truth.

Systemic lessons for forensic reform

The recurring nature of these conflicts underscores that they are not isolated incidents, but symptoms of a structural problem long recognized by national scientific bodies. The 2009 National Academy of Sciences report, Strengthening Forensic Science in the United States: A Path Forward, recognized this structural problem with remarkable clarity [9]. It warned that forensic laboratories cannot claim objectivity while operating under the administrative control of law enforcement. The report called for independence, transparency, and rigorous external oversight. Yet more than a decade and a half later, the institutional framework remains largely unchanged. The lessons have not been absorbed. Forensic science too often serves to legitimize authority rather than to question it. Without independent oversight and consistent feedback, forensic systems tend to repeat and reinforce their own biases rather than correct them [10].

Upholding justice through scientific independence

Science must remain self-correcting and free from the gravitational pull of power. Scientists must be aware of the structural and cultural forces that impact everyday forensic work. They shape how case data are interpreted and how dissenting views are handled within laboratories.

Forensic science cannot be truly objective when laboratories operate under the administrative and financial control of law enforcement. Forensic laboratories should be governed by scientists, not by law enforcement hierarchies. Full disclosure, external peer review, and public accountability must become the norm. We must also establish protected avenues for those who challenge entrenched narratives without fear of reprisal. When science can question authority without penalty, it fulfills its role as an instrument of truth. The integrity of forensic practice depends upon preserving that freedom.

This editorial is not intended as a criticism of individual scientists, most of whom strive to do their best work with integrity, but as a critique of the broader structural, sociological, anthropological, and historical context in which forensic science operates. It is meant to stimulate thought, and to encourage a commitment to independence, transparency, and critical scrutiny within the field.
