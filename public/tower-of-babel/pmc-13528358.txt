Safety and efficacy of allogeneic CD19-directed CAR-T therapy CTX110 in relapsed/refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma∗
Authors: Joseph P McGuirk, Armin Ghobadi, Carlos R Bachier, Paul J Shaughnessy, Farrukh Awan, Joseph E Maakaron, Michael R Bishop, P Joy Ho, Constantine S Tam, Michael Dickinson, Justin M Darrah, Edmund K Waller, Hemant S Murthy, Ainsley Ross, Leah M Williams, Chenyi Pan, William N Stevens, Laura Serwer, Thao Nguyen, Jonathan Terrett, Demetrios Kalaitzidis, Anna Ma, Huansheng Xu, Mark Benton, Annie Weaver, Sarah Beaussant Cohen, Richard T Maziarz, Chan Cheah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•CTX110 showed acceptable safety and promising preliminary efficacy at higher DLs in patients with R/R B-cell NHL.•Allogeneic, off-the-shelf CTX110 is a more consistent product with decreased time to treatment compared with marketed CAR T-cell treatments.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises a heterogeneous group of malignancies originating from B cells, T cells, or natural killer cells, and is the 10th most common malignancy worldwide, with >60 different subcategories defined by the World Health Organization.1,2 The most common subtype is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), which accounts for 30% to 40% of cases.3 The neoplastic cells typically express pan–B-cell markers such as CD20, CD22, and CD19. CD19 is a type 1 transmembrane protein that plays important roles in B-cell activation and signaling, and is maintained among indolent and aggressive NHL subtypes.4 It has been the target of several approved and investigational therapeutics, including immunotherapies such as tafasitamab and highly efficacious CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells. Of note, the only approved CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapies are autologous products, which come with inherent challenges: (1) the need for leukapheresis and additional therapies to bridge the patient during manufacturing; (2) manufacturing cost and risk of failures; and (3) treatment accessibility, because most patients do not have access.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 In contrast, the immediate availability of allogeneic CAR T-cell products allows for prompt initiation of lymphodepleting (LD) chemotherapy, avoiding the need for bridging therapy and its associated safety concerns (eg, prolonged cytopenias). Allogeneic CAR T cells are manufactured using healthy donor T cells in a standardized process, resulting in consistent product quality and reduced cost. Additionally, the use of healthy donor T cells eliminates the possibility of infusing drug product that may be contaminated with malignant cells collected from patients. Also, healthy donor T cells do not have chemotherapy-induced exhausted phenotypes or dysfunction due to previous chemotherapy or exposure to the tumor microenvironment.10 Lastly, allogeneic CAR T-cell products allow for redosing to potentially deepen or prolong responses. These advantages make allogeneic CAR T-cell products attractive and potentially applicable to a large proportion of patients.11

CTX110 is a CD19-directed T-cell immunotherapy comprised of allogeneic healthy donor T cells that are genetically modified ex vivo using CRISPR-Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats–CRISPR-associated protein 9) gene editing components (guide RNA and Cas9 nuclease). The modifications include disruption of the T-cell receptor alpha constant (TRAC) and beta-2 microglobulin (B2M) loci, and simultaneous insertion of an anti-CD19 CAR transgene into the TRAC locus, which allows for specific placement and diminishes risk of insertional mutagenesis. The CAR is comprised of a CD19-specific murine single-chain variable fragment, followed by a CD8 hinge and transmembrane region fused to intracellular signaling domains for CD28 and CD3ζ (supplemental Figure 1). TRAC and B2M knockouts are intended to reduce the probability of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and increase persistence of the allogeneic cells, respectively, and insertion of the CAR construct redirects the modified T cells toward CD19-expressing tumor cells.

In this study we evaluated the safety and efficacy of the CD19-targeted CAR T-cell immunotherapy CTX110 in adult patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) NHL. Primary end points in dose escalation and cohort expansion were incidence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), and objective response rate (ORR), respectively.

Methods

Study design

This first-in-human, open-label, multicenter, phase 1/2 study evaluated the safety and efficacy of CTX110 in patients with R/R B-cell NHL. Eligible patients included adults with select NHL subtypes who were refractory to previous treatment or had relapsed (Table 1). Phase 1 comprised dose escalation followed by cohort expansion, whereas phase 2 further explored efficacy in cohorts evaluated in phase 1. The data presented here are based on patients with NHL from both phases 1 and 2. All patients provided written informed consent. Institutional review boards at each participating institution approved the study, which was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial (NCT04035434; a safety and efficacy study evaluating CTX110 in subjects with relapsed or refractory B-cell malignancies ([CARBON]) was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov.

Study procedures

Patients were recruited into the CRSP-ONC-001 trial between 2019 and 2024, and enrolled into 4 cohorts evaluating different regimens of IV CTX110 infusion on day 1 at escalating dose levels (DL; DL1: 3 × 107; DL2: 1 × 108; DL3: 3 × 108; DL3.5: 4.5 × 108; and DL4: 6 × 108 CAR T cells) following a standard 3 + 3 design in combination with LD agents (fludarabine 30 mg/m2 and cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m2 IV daily for 3 days, followed by a 2-7 day washout period; supplemental Figure 2). A course of treatment lasted 2 to 5 weeks, with additional infusions of CTX110 available for most patients meeting criteria for response and safety. In phase 2, the 2-dose regimen consisted of 1 CTX110 infusion each on days 1 and 35, administered as a single course of treatment, with each infusion preceded by LD chemotherapy. Patients were monitored for DLTs for 28 days after CTX110 infusion. Response assessment was performed using Lugano Response Criteria for Malignant Lymphoma12 for NHL. After completion of an end-of-study visit, all patients were asked to enter long-term follow-up for up to 15 years, with annual safety visits. Adverse events (AEs) were collected and graded according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0, except for cytokine release syndrome (CRS), which was graded per American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria13; neurotoxicity, graded per immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) criteria13 and CTCAE; and GVHD, graded per Mount Sinai Acute GVHD International Consortium criteria.14

Statistical analysis

The primary end points were incidence of AEs defined as DLTs for each cohort, and ORR (complete response [CR] + partial response [PR]). Descriptive statistics for demographic and baseline parameters are summarized. Efficacy was analyzed and summarized by CTX110 DL and regimen for each cohort using the full analysis set. For patients who received DL3+ (DL3, DL3.5, DL4) and DL4∗ (2 CTX110 infusions at DL4 on days 1 and 35), response was further evaluated using a swimmer plot of response assessments after CTX110 infusion for individual patients. A Kaplan-Meier plot for duration of response (DOR) is provided for patients who received DL3+ vs DL4∗. AEs were categorized using CTCAE, except for CRS, ICANS, and GVHD.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Samples were collected per the protocol schedule of assessments. DNA was extracted from whole blood samples using QIAGEN Blood DNA Midi Kit (catalog no. 816-0551; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA samples were digested with BamHI (catalog no. 852-0449; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and KpnI (catalog no. 852-0450; New England Biolabs). Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) was prepared with 2 × ddPCR Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA), FAM dye/MGB probe targeting the CAR insert (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), VIC dye/QSY probe targeting reference gene ABHD4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), deoxynucleoside triphosphates, and MgCl2. Droplets were generated automatically with QX200 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Reactions were placed in a thermal cycler and underwent the following: enzyme activation at 95°C for 10 minutes; 45 cycles of 94°C for 30 seconds, 64°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 2 minutes; 98°C for 10 minutes; and 4°C for 2 hours. Droplet acquisition and analysis were performed with QX200 Droplet Reader and QuantaSoft Software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Assays were validated according to US Food and Drug Administration and International Council for Harmonisation guidance on bioanalytical method validation.

The number of CAR construct copies (normalized per microgram total DNA) was plotted against visit days, and the peak number of CAR construct copies (normalized per microgram total DNA) was plotted using GraphPad Prism (Dotmatics, Boston, MA).

All data were analyzed by the study sponsor, CRISPR Therapeutics, and all authors had access to primary clinical trial data.

Results

Study population

A total of 66 patients were enrolled in the study (Figure 1), including 3 who did not receive CTX110 due to rapid disease progression (n = 2) and intercurrent COVID-19 infection (n = 1), and 63 who received CTX110, with a median time of 7 days (range, 5-28 days) from enrollment to first infusion of CTX110. The median follow-up from first CTX110 infusion to death or last-known-alive date was 8.5 months (range, 1.1-53). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics for the 63 patients who received CTX110, most (54%) of whom had DLBCL not otherwise specified. In total, 51 patients (81%) had stage III or IV disease, and all received ≥2 previous therapy lines, including 27 (43%) patients who received ≥3 previous therapy lines. There were 37 (59%) patients whose disease was categorized as primary refractory (lack of CR to first-line treatment or relapsing <3 months since start of first-line therapy) or as high-risk relapse (relapse within 12 months of start of first-line therapy).

CONSORT diagram of patient disposition. As of the data cutoff date of 26 November 2024, 66 patients with R/R NHL were enrolled in the study. Among the enrolled patients, 3 did not receive CTX110 infusion due to intercurrent COVID-19 infection (n = 1) or rapid disease progression (n = 2), and 63 received CTX110 infusion, with median time from enrollment to CTX110 infusion of 7 days.

Treatment

Dose escalation began with a single CTX110 infusion at DL1 and escalated per protocol to DL4. After the single infusion of DL4 was deemed safe, the DL4∗ regimen was introduced to determine if a second prespecified infusion could improve DOR, with infusions on days 1 and 35 for patients with no evidence of progressive disease (PD) at day 28. In total, 35 patients were assigned to receive the DL4∗ regimen, including 4 patients in phase 1 dose escalation and all 31 cohort expansion patients treated in phases 1 and 2. Of the 35 patients, 24 received the second (day 35) infusion. Three of these 24 patients experienced a delay in the day 35 infusion due to grade 3 thrombocytopenia and grade 4 neutropenia (2 patients) and grade 3 COVID-19 infection (1 patient). In the 11 patients who did not receive the second infusion, 10 experienced rapid disease progression before day 35 and 1 patient chose to initiate alternative anticancer therapy. One patient receiving DL4∗ did not receive LD chemotherapy before their second infusion due to pre-existing treatment-related cytopenias, including grade 4 neutropenia, grade 3 thrombocytopenia, grade 3 lymphopenia, and grade 2 anemia.

Patients could receive an additional infusion of CTX110 as a second course of treatment if the patient had a clinical response to the first course of treatment (1 CTX110 infusion) and subsequently progressed. A second course of treatment was administered to 9 patients after progression, including 1 patient at DL2, 1 patient at DL3, 3 patients at DL3.5, and 4 patients at DL4. The second courses of treatment were at the same DL as the first course, except for 1 patient at DL2 whose second course was at DL3, and 1 patient at DL3.5 whose second course was at DL4. All second course treatments were administered at the highest DL cleared at the time of disease progression.

Safety

All 63 patients who received CTX110 infusion were included in the safety analysis set, and the safety data presented here are inclusive of first and second CTX110 infusions (Table 2). CRS was reported in 34 (54%) patients, with 2 patients in the DL4∗ group experiencing grade 3 CRS that resolved. The median time to incidence of CRS was 2 days (range, 1-17) after CTX110 infusion, and 21 of 34 (62%) patients with CRS required a median of 1 tocilizumab dose. Median times from onset to resolution of any and grade ≥3 CRS were 4 and 5.5 days, respectively. ICANS was reported in 8 (13%) patients, with 2 cases of grade ≥3 ICANS at DL4, including 1 patient with grade 4 ICANS that resolved within 8 days, and 1 patient with ICANS and concurrent human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) encephalitis that resulted in death. The median time to incidence of ICANS was 5 days (range, 2-28) after CTX110 infusion, and 3 of 8 (37.5%) patients with ICANS required a median of 8 anakinra doses. Median times from onset to resolution of any and grade ≥3 ICANS were 7.5 and 16.5 days, respectively. A positive correlation was found between baseline tumor burden and incidence of grade ≥2 ICANS, with significantly higher tumor burden as measured in sum of product of perpendicular diameters (SPD) in patients who experienced grade ≥2 ICANS (n = 4, mean ± standard error of SPD: 89.8 ± 35.0 cm2) compared with the other patients (n = 59, mean ± standard error of SPD: 33.3 ± 6.0 cm2). No GVHD was reported. The most common grade ≥3 treatment-emergent AEs were neutropenia (37 [59%]), and anemia and thrombocytopenia (22 [35%] each). Grade ≥3 cytopenias (excluding lymphopenia) occurred in 40 of 63 (63%) patients ≥30 days after CTX110 infusion, and most resolved by month 3. Grade ≥3 infections occurred in 7 of 63 (11.1%) patients, including 3 events of grade 3 pneumonia (1 of which was possibly related to CTX110). One event of grade 3 pneumonia (unrelated to CTX110) did not resolve and was ongoing at the time of death on day 52. Other grade ≥3 infections included 1 event each of bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, enterococcal infection, Escherichia sepsis, infection, periorbital cellulitis, and Pseudomonas sepsis, none of which was related to CTX110. Treatment-emergent grade 2 hypogammaglobulinemia occurred in 1 patient on day 2 following a second infusion of CTX110 at DL4, which resolved on day 19 without immunoglobulin G replacement, and was assessed as possibly related to CTX110. There were 2 patients with grade 1 and 2 hypogammaglobulinemia before CTX110 infusion; the grade 2 event was ongoing at time of death, and the grade 1 event resolved within 14 days.

Serious AEs occurred in 20 (32%) patients. The most frequently reported serious AEs were CRS (9 [14%]), ICANS (5 [8%]), and febrile neutropenia (4 [6%]). Three (5%) of all dosed patients (N = 63) developed a new malignancy after receiving CTX110 (all myelodysplastic syndrome [MDS]). Two of these 3 patients had received ≥8 systemic anticancer therapies, and all 3 were at increased risk of MDS.

Efficacy

All 3 patients treated with CTX110 at DL1 experienced PD within 28 days after infusion (Figure 2). Objective response was achieved after CTX110 infusion starting at DL2, with ORR increasing in a dose-dependent manner from DL2 to DL4 (Figure 2). For patients with first CTX110 infusion at DL ≥3, ORR and complete response rate were 65% (37/57) and 39% (22/57), respectively. Post-treatment response to CTX110 and follow-up for individual patients with first infusion at DL ≥3 (n = 57) was assessed (Figure 3). Of these 57 patients, 13 (23%) maintained CRs ≥6 months after infusion, including 5 patients with ongoing durable CRs as of data cutoff (15-50 [median 34] months follow-up). Four of these 5 patients were assayed for minimal residual disease (MRD), and 3 were MRD negative at day 28. Although the single-infusion DL3+ group had comparable ORR and complete response rate to the DL4∗ group, the latter was associated with extended DOR compared with single infusion, with median DOR of 4.2 and 1.7 months for DL4∗ and DL3+, respectively (hazard ratio, 0.65; Figure 4). The median time between the first and second infusions for DL4∗ was 42.5 days (range, 35-92). Among the 9 patients who received a second course of treatment after progression, 6 (67%) had restored clinical response, including 4 CRs and 2 PRs, with a median (minimum, maximum) duration of 4.8 (0.03, 9.2) months for restored response.

CD19 expression at baseline

Understanding baseline expression of CD19 and its association with efficacy has been a relevant question as the use of CD19-directed therapies has increased. We analyzed 31 patients’ baseline CD19 expression data using immunohistochemistry across DLs and assessed correlation to day 28 response. Consistent with published findings,15,16 expression for most patients was high (average percentage of CD19 expression on lymphoma cells: ∼80%), with no correlation between expression and response (supplemental Figure 3).

Pharmacokinetic results

In DL1 and DL2, CTX110 pharmacokinetics (PK) were quantified in peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples. In DL1, no expansion of CTX110 was observed, and cells were detected 20 minutes after infusion, whereas in DL2 expansion of CTX110 after infusion was observed in the 3 patients treated with CTX110 (data not shown). We therefore focused our PK analyses on DL ≥3 (n = 56 with PK data available following initial CTX110 infusion), in which CTX110 was detected readily in whole blood via a validated ddPCR assay. CTX110 concentration data reflected the typical triphasic CAR T-cell kinetics in which an initial period of redistribution is followed by expansion to peak, then clearance to levels below limit of detection (Figure 5). Maximum expansion was generally observed between days 5 and 8 after infusion, followed by a rapid decline in CTX110 levels in the blood. In most patients, CTX110 was no longer detected by day 28. Although the time course of expansion was similar across patients, peak levels of expansion varied considerably between individuals. Generally, dose-dependent increases in median CTX110 maximum expansion were observed (supplemental Figure 4). PK after the day 35 CTX110 infusion were similar to PK after initial CTX110 infusion, as well as in patients who received a second course of treatment (supplemental Figure 5).

Immunogenicity

Immunogenicity to the CAR (antidrug antibodies [ADAs]) as well as alloimmune responses mediated by anti-HLA antibodies specific to the donor (donor-specific antibodies [DSAs]) have been highlighted as major concerns that could potentially lead to treatment failure in an allogeneic setting.17,18 To address these concerns, CTX110 CAR T cells were edited to disrupt the B2M subunit of the class I major histocompatibility complex, and ADAs and DSAs were assessed before and after infusion. Only 11.1% of patients were positive for DSAs at baseline (supplemental Table 1). Two patients were negative before infusion and became positive for DSAs at day 28, with no clear effect on response or expansion. Results for a second infusion after day 28 were similar, although data were limited (supplemental Table 1). A similar assessment was conducted on screening and day 28 serum samples from 31 patients to evaluate the presence of ADAs. Only 1 patient had ADAs, observed before infusion and at day 28. We also investigated HLA match between donor CAR T cells and patients by performing HLA subtyping. The gold standard is high-resolution typing at the HLA-A, -B, -C, -DR beta 1 (DRB1), and -DQ beta 1 (DQB1) loci (10/10 match) for hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT).19 From our data set, only very low grades of HLA allele match were identified between donors and recipients (ie, for a 10/10 match, mean = 1.4 alleles, minimum = 0, maximum = 5) that would not qualify as a match for HSCT (supplemental Table 2). Given the low HLA matches in the donor and recipients, low incidence of pre-existing and induced ADAs and DSAs, and lack of correlation with these data and CAR T-cell expansion, patient safety, or clinical response, the edits appear to have sufficiently reduced immunogenicity in this cohort.

Discussion

This multicenter evaluation assessed the safety and preliminary efficacy of the anti-CD19 allogeneic CAR T-cell therapy CTX110 in 63 patients with R/R NHL enrolled in the CRSP-ONC-001 phase 1/2 trial.

The safety profile in the 63 patients treated across all DLs is comparable to autologous CAR T-cell therapies, with CRS and ICANS generally low-grade and resolved with appropriate therapy. The lowest dose (DL1) did not produce any clinical responses; however, DL2 and above showed increasing response rates (Figure 2).

CTX110 treatment was associated with CR/PR in most (60%) patients. CRs were observed in patients with different disease subtypes, including DLBCL not otherwise specified and transformed follicular lymphoma, as well as in patients with disease that was primary refractory or relapsed after previous autologous HSCT, indicating potential clinical benefit in higher-risk disease groups. Among the 22 patients with CRs dosed at DL ≥3 for first infusion, there were 5 ongoing CRs as of data cutoff.

Whereas the DL4∗ regimen had a response rate comparable to single infusion at DL ≥3 (Figure 2), it apparently prolonged DOR compared with the single-infusion regimen, as indicated by the DOR curve for DL4∗ shifted to the right of the curve for DL3+ before they converged at ∼11 months after infusion (Figure 4). The second infusion did not appear to increase the risk of cytopenia, as there was a comparable frequency of each examined cytopenia (lymphopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, or leukopenia) of grade 3 or 4 at day 28, month 2, or month 3 following the second vs first infusion. Given these data, the DL4∗ regimen (DL4 [6 × 108 CAR T cells] infused on days 1 and 35 for patients with stable disease or better at the day 28 assessment) was selected for further evaluation in cohort expansion in phases 1 and 2.

In general, higher doses of CTX110 resulted in increased response rates. Similarly, re-infusion of CTX110 was associated with deepened and prolonged response in some patients, as evidenced by 6 of 9 (67%) patients experiencing restored response after receiving a second course of treatment after progression, and a decreased hazard rate for PD or death with the 2-infusion regimen compared with a single infusion (Figure 4; DL4∗ vs DL3+, respectively).

Next-generation sequencing-based MRD detection is an emerging tool to stratify risk and assess treatment responses for patients with large B-cell lymphoma and other hematological malignancies.20 Several studies have shown MRD monitoring can identify patients at risk of relapse before clinical evidence of disease. Of the patients that were in durable CR and were assayed, 3 of 4 (75%) were MRD negative at day 28, indicating that CTX110 treatment can result in MRD-negative CRs.

Similar to the observed dose-dependent response rate, PK analysis showed that CTX110 readily expands in peripheral blood at higher doses and can expand to a similar degree as the initial infusion after a second round of LD and CTX110 infusion. No apparent correlation between expansion and response was observed in this data set.

Allogeneic CAR T-cell products can circumvent the most pressing issues surrounding autologous CAR T cells, such as time, cost, and accessibility outside of centers able to perform leukapheresis. For this study, CTX110 was readily available, as evidenced by a median of 7 days (range, 5-28 days) from enrollment to first infusion of CTX110, compared with a median of 26 days (range, 16-52 days) from leukapheresis to infusion for approved autologous CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy axicabtagene ciloleucel (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03391466), eliminating the need for bridging therapies and minimizing risk of PD before CTX110 treatment.21 This is of particular importance among a heavily treated and/or R/R NHL population, 43% of whom had received ≥3 previous lines of therapy and 59% of whom were primary refractory.

CTX110 showed an acceptable safety profile in this heavily pretreated population. The most common CAR T-cell–related treatment-emergent AE was CRS, which occurred in 34 (54%) treated patients, with most cases grade 1 or 2. These rates were consistent with those of approved CD19-targeting CAR T-cell therapies (ie, tisagenlecleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel, brexucabtagene autoleucel). Reported events of CRS and ICANS were responsive to interventions with standard of care except for 1 event of grade 2 ICANS that was ongoing at the time of the patient’s death from PD. Observed AEs of special interest were consistent with those reported for approved autologous CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapies in patients with NHL. Grade ≥3 hematopoietic cytopenias were commonly reported, including lymphopenia, neutropenia, leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, which typically resolved or were grade <3 by month 3 after CTX110 infusion. One DLT of grade 5 ICANS occurred concurrently with HHV-6 encephalitis, and the death was attributed to HHV-6 encephalitis. No GVHD was reported among patients with NHL in the study.

Overall, 3 (5%) of all dosed patients (N = 63) developed a new malignancy after receiving CTX110 (all MDS), with a median onset time of day 282 after first CTX110 infusion. Before receiving CAR T-cell therapy, 2 of these 3 patients had received ≥8 systemic anticancer treatments, and all 3 patients had received cyclophosphamide as part of previous anticancer therapy; thus the new malignancies are likely best explained by previous exposure to cytotoxic therapies or other patient factors, including pre-existing risks for malignancy (eg, comorbid conditions, environmental risks, radiation therapies). There were no safety data to indicate new malignancies resulting from mutated allogeneic CAR T cells.

The standard practice for HSCT is high-resolution typing at the HLA-A, -B, -C, -DRB1, and -DQB1 loci (10/10 match). Single differences for HLA-A, -B, -C, or -DRB1 are associated with increased risk of post-transplant complications but are more permissive in patients with advanced disease and in patients undergoing T-cell–depleted allografting. HLA-DQB1 mismatches seem to be better tolerated, and some HLA-C, -DRB1, and -DP beta 1 disparities are potentially less immunogenic. When there are mismatches at the HLA-DP beta 1 loci, some mismatches are more immunogenic in inducing GVHD, classified as nonpermissive (vs permissive) mismatches.19 From our data set, very low frequencies of HLA allele match were identified between donors and recipients, which did not represent clinically relevant levels of HLA-matching to the donor. These data, along with the single instance of ADAs, low incidence of anti-DSAs, any correlation with response, and overall lack of any GVHD events, provide further proof of concept of the safety and tolerability of allogeneic CAR T-cell therapies.

Limitations of the study include the study’s small size, exploratory nature, and restricted patient population. Response rates observed with CTX110 at higher DLs appeared comparable to those of already approved autologous CAR T-cell therapies22, 23, 24, 25; however, the DOR with CTX110 indicated that additional edits were needed to increase CTX110 potency.

Overall, the data presented indicate the potential for deep and durable responses following a single infusion of CTX110 preceded by standard LD chemotherapy, as well as the potential to achieve a second response after additional infusions in patients who experience PD, further extending the period of clinical benefit. Additionally, the time to infusion, overall safety profile, and precision of CRISPR-Cas9 editing collectively provide evidence for the promising potential of genetically edited, off-the-shelf CAR T-cell treatments. This study of a first-generation allogeneic CAR T-cell therapy with a positive benefit-risk profile provides the foundation for next-generation allogeneic CAR T-cell therapy, zugocabtagene geleucel (formerly CTX112), which has additional edits to enhance CAR T-cell proliferation and potency,26 and is being evaluated in an ongoing clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05643742) in R/R lymphoma.27
