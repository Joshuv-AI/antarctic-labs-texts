Establishment of disk diffusion national epidemiological cut-off values for Pasteurella multocida and Mannheimia haemolytica isolated from bovine respiratory diseases and characteristics of antibiograms in Japan
Authors: Tomoko KOSHIISHI, Akiko KATO, Mio KUMAKAWA, Yuta HOSOI, Michiko KAWANISHI, Shunsuke MORI, Tomomi KURUMISAWA, Junko SAITO, Mie KISHIKAWA, Kazuhiro KAWAI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance is a global public health threat, and accurate antimicrobial susceptibility testing is crucial for guiding appropriate antimicrobial use. Although the disk diffusion method is widely used in livestock, standardized breakpoints for veterinary pathogens are lacking. Bovine respiratory disease, a major cause of economic loss in calves, is commonly associated with Pasteurella multocida and Mannheimia haemolytica. This study aimed to establish inhibition zone diameter cut-off values for disk diffusion and to create an antibiogram for these pathogens. The cut-off values were calculated based on minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) reference values: the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute clinical breakpoints for penicillin and tetracycline; the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing epidemiological cut-off values for kanamycin and tilmicosin (P. multocida); and MIC cut-off values calculated from this study for cefazolin, marbofloxacin, thiamphenicol, and tylosin. The cut-off values for penicillin and tetracycline provide useful guidance for clinical drug selection, whereas caution is required when interpreting the results for the other agents evaluated because their cut-off values were based on epidemiological cut-offs rather than clinical breakpoints. Re-evaluation of previously reported cut-off values for kanamycin, cefazolin, and tetracycline using a larger number of isolates provided more robust thresholds that were less susceptible to strain-specific variation. The antibiogram revealed low susceptibility rates for some agents. Because antimicrobial use is associated with the development of resistance, these findings indicate the need for prudent antimicrobial stewardship and continued surveillance of resistance in livestock pathogens.Keywords: antibiogram, bovine respiratory disease, disk diffusion method, Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida

INTRODUCTION

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) threatens the effectiveness of infectious disease treatment and has become a global public health threat [32]. In Japan, the Action Plan for Antimicrobial Resistance (2023–2027) was developed to promote AMR countermeasures, with targets in six areas: (1) dissemination and education, (2) trends and surveillance, (3) infection prevention and control, (4) appropriate use of antimicrobial agents, (5) research and development and drug discovery, and (6) international cooperation [27]. Antibiograms are practical tools for antimicrobial resistance surveys in individual medical facilities or testing centers [17]. They provide periodic summaries of the antimicrobial susceptibility of clinical isolates and are used to guide empirical antimicrobial therapy against expected pathogens, monitor changes in resistance rates over time, and promote the appropriate use of antimicrobial agents [17, 23, 25]. The microdilution method for antimicrobial susceptibility testing can determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of antimicrobial agents, allowing evaluation of the degree of susceptibility or resistance to these agents [10]. However, although MIC results are simple and easy to interpret, MIC-measuring plates are expensive. In contrast, the disk diffusion method is a qualitative test that classifies bacteria as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant. It offers the advantages of simplicity, low cost, and ease of result interpretation [3], and is therefore widely implemented in livestock production settings. Reliable interpretive criteria are essential for classifying pathogenic microorganisms as susceptible or resistant to antimicrobial agents in antimicrobial resistance surveys and susceptibility testing. The cut-off values established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) have been widely adopted internationally. However, established cut-off values are unavailable for many animal-derived pathogenic microorganisms, making susceptibility test results difficult to interpret in the veterinary field.

Bovine respiratory disease (BRD) has a high morbidity rate in calves and causes significant economic losses to calf producers due to reduced growth rates and increased treatment costs [24]. The causative factors of BRD are complex, involving viruses, bacteria, and stress. However, BRD typically begins with a primary viral infection and is exacerbated by secondary bacterial infection [11], and antimicrobial agents are therefore used for treatment [4]. The primary causative bacteria are Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni, and Mycoplasma bovis. However, established cut-off values for interpreting antimicrobial susceptibility testing results against these causative bacteria remain insufficient. Tentative inhibition zone diameter cut-off values have been reported using the disk diffusion method for ampicillin (ABPC), cefazolin (CEZ), dihydrostreptomycin (DSM), kanamycin (KM), and tetracycline (TC) against M. haemolytica and P. multocida isolated from BRD [20]. However, these findings do not comprehensively cover the antibiotics used in clinical practice. Furthermore, because a range of inhibition zone diameter cut-off values showed equivalent sensitivity and specificity for several antimicrobial agents, likely due to the absence of isolates with intermediate susceptibility levels, re-evaluation using additional strains was considered necessary [20]. We therefore added the strains used in the present study to those reported by Kumakawa et al. [20]. The objective of this study was to establish epidemiological cut-off values in Japan for determining susceptibility and resistance to penicillin (PCG), marbofloxacin (MBFX), thiamphenicol (TP), tylosin (TS), and tilmicosin (TMS: P. multocida only) using the disk diffusion method. For KM, CEZ, and TC, strains from this study were added to those for which provisional cut-off values had previously been reported, and they were re-evaluated [20]. Furthermore, because few recent reports have evaluated the susceptibility rates of P. multocida and M. haemolytica derived from BRD in Japan, this study also aimed to establish antibiograms for both bacterial species.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Animal Experiment Committee of Azabu University (Sagamihara, Japan).

BRD sample collection and identification of causative bacteria

Veterinarians in the field treated naturally occurring cases of BRD and collected samples for microbiological testing, which we routinely perform on request. In this study, the isolated and preserved strains obtained after such inspections were used for testing. The number of preserved strains was limited to no more than two samples per farm, each derived from a different individual. For P. multocida, samples were collected from February 1, 2021, to January 12, 2023, in 13 prefectures in Japan: Hokkaido, Aomori, Ibaraki, Iwate, Okayama, Kagoshima, Saitama, Chiba, Tochigi, Hyogo, Miyagi, Miyazaki, and Yamagata. For M. haemolytica, samples were collected from January 14, 2020, to January 9, 2024, in 14 prefectures: Hokkaido, Aomori, Ibaraki, Iwate, Ehime, Kagoshima, Saitama, Chiba, Tochigi, Nagasaki, Hyogo, Miyagi, Miyazaki, and Yamagata). Nasal swabs, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or lung tissue were inoculated onto 5% horse blood agar medium and cultured at 37°C for 24 hr in the presence of 5% CO2. Species identification of the isolated strains was performed by 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis. The primer sequences were the 27F sense primer (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and the 1492R antisense primer (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), as reported by Turner et al. [29]. PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, annealing at 58°C for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 2 min, repeated for 32 cycles followed by a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. The amplified DNA was subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis, and the PCR products were treated with exonuclease I (20 U/μL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and shrimp alkaline phosphatase (1 U/μL) (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Exonuclease I and shrimp alkaline phosphatase were mixed at a 1:2 ratio, and 1 μL of the mixture was added to 5 μL of PCR product. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min and then inactivated by heating at 85°C for 15 min. The resulting products were used as templates for sequencing reactions using a Big Dye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing analysis was outsourced to FASMAC Co., Ltd. (Atsugi, Japan). Species identification was performed by confirming the sequence similarity between the obtained DNA sequences and known sequences using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) [1].

Drug susceptibility testing

Microdilution method: The antimicrobial agents used were as follows: PCG, KM, florfenicol (FFC), TP, MBFX, enrofloxacin (ERFX), TS, TMS (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), CEZ, and TC (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The MIC of the antimicrobial agents was measured by the microdilution method in accordance with the antimicrobial susceptibility testing methods established by the CLSI [6]. Test microplates were prepared by serially diluting the antimicrobial agents in a 12-step, two-fold dilution series. Bacterial suspensions were inoculated to achieve approximately 5 × 104 CFU/well. After incubation at 35°C in ambient air for 18 hr, bacterial growth was assessed visually. All antimicrobial agents were tested at concentrations ranging from 0.125 to 256 μg/mL. Cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (Becton Dickinson Co., Ltd., Sparks, MD, USA) was used as the medium. For each test, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 was used as the quality control strain, and testing was conducted in accordance with CLSI guidelines [6].

Disk diffusion method: The disk diffusion method was conducted according to the antimicrobial susceptibility test method established by the CLSI [6]. The following antimicrobial drug disks were used: PCG (10 μg), KM (30 μg), TC (30 μg), CEZ (30 μg) (Becton Dickinson Japan Co., Ltd.), FFC (30 μg), TP (30 μg), TS (60 μg), TMS (15 μg), ERFX (5 μg) (Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and MBFX (5 μg) (Vetoquinol, Lure, France). A bacterial suspension adjusted to McFarland 0.5 was spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar (Becton, Dickinson Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood. Disks were placed on the agar plates and incubated at 37°C in ambient air for 18 hr, after which inhibition zone diameters were measured. When the disk lot was changed, the S. aureus ATCC 25923 strain was used as the quality control strain in accordance with CLSI guidelines [6]. For the TS (60 μg) disk, the acceptable range criteria established by the Japanese Society of Antimicrobials for Animals were used [16].

Determination of MIC and inhibition zone diameter cut-off values

The MIC cut-off values were used to calculate the inhibition zone diameter cut-off values. The following MIC criteria for PCG, KM, CEZ, TC, TP, TS, TMS (excluding M. haemolytica), and MBFX were applied as previously reported [20]: 1) if a clinical breakpoint (CBP) had been established by CLSI [5], that value was adopted; 2) if no CBP had been established by CLSI [5] but epidemiological cut-off values (ECOFFs) had been set by EUCAST [7], the ECOFF was adopted; and 3) if neither a CBP nor an ECOFF was specified, then the MIC cut-off values were calculated using the SOP for ECOFF determination published by EUCAST [9]. The inhibition zone diameter cut-off values were calculated based on the MIC cut-off values using the formula: (1-sensitivity§1)2 + (1-specificity§2)2, with the minimum value selected as the cut-off [20]. If one or more inhibition zone diameters met this requirement, the largest value was adopted. For the calculation of MIC and inhibition zone diameter cut-off values for KM, CEZ, and TC, strains reported by Kumakawa et al. (P. multocida, 77 strains; M. haemolytica, 158 strains) [20] were added, resulting in a total of 201 P. multocida strains and 258 M. haemolytica strains.

Antibiogram analysis

The inhibition zone diameters of P. multocida and M. haemolytica strains were classified as susceptible, intermediate if criteria were available, or resistant. Classifications for PCG, KM, TC, CEZ, TP, TS, TMS (P. multocida), and MBFX were determined based on newly established disk diffusion cut-off values, whereas those for FFC, ERFX, and TMS (M. haemolytica) were determined based on CLSI CBPs. Antibiograms were created according to the Antibiogram Creation Guidelines of the Infectious Diseases Education Consortium [14]. The proportion of susceptible isolates was shown in the antibiograms. Susceptibility rates were calculated for each region: Hokkaido, eastern Japan, and western Japan. Eastern Japan included Aomori, Ibaraki, Yamagata, Iwate, Miyagi, Saitama, Chiba, and Tochigi prefectures, whereas western Japan included Hyogo, Okayama, Ehime, Miyazaki, Nagasaki, and Kagoshima prefectures. Values based on sample sizes of fewer than 30 were interpreted with caution [14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using EZR [18] (ver. 1.68, Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). In the antibiogram, Bonferroni multiple comparisons were performed for susceptibility rates between regions, and P<0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference.

RESULTS

Bacterial isolation

During the study period, 6,988 samples consisting of nasal swabs, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or lung tissue were received for testing. Of the 2,555 P. multocida isolates and 1,290 M. haemolytica isolates, 124 and 100, respectively, were cryopreserved and included in the study.

Antibiogram analysis

Table 4 shows the antibiograms. The susceptibility rates of FFC, ERFX, and TMS (M. haemolytica) shown in the antibiograms of this study were calculated using the CLSI breakpoint values for inhibition zone diameters. The susceptibility rate of FFC against M. haemolytica was high overall, but the rate in Hokkaido (84.4%) was significantly lower than that in eastern Japan (100%) (P<0.05). The susceptibility rate of TMS against M. haemolytica was low in all surveyed regions combined (70.0%). The susceptibility rates of PCG and TC were calculated based on the inhibition zone diameter cut-off values derived from the MIC CBPs. Low susceptibility rates were observed for TC against P. multocida (all surveyed regions combined: 66.1%) and for PCG and TC against M. haemolytica (all surveyed regions combined: 61.0% and 54.0%, respectively). The susceptibility rates of KM and TMS against P. multocida were calculated based on the inhibition zone diameter cut-off values derived from the ECOFFs. For P. multocida, the susceptibility rate of TMS in Hokkaido (58.6%) was significantly lower than those in other regions (eastern Japan: 91.7%, western Japan: 100%) (P<0.01). The susceptibility rate of KM against M. haemolytica was low (all surveyed regions combined: 69.0%). The susceptibility rates for CEZ, TP, MBFX, and TS were calculated based on the inhibition zone cut-off values determined by this study. The susceptibility rate of MBFX against P. multocida in Hokkaido (24.3%) was significantly lower than that in western Japan (53.3%), and the susceptibility rate of TS against P. multocida in Hokkaido (61.4%) was significantly lower than those in other regions (eastern Japan: 95.8%, western Japan: 100%).

DISCUSSION

In this study, epidemiological cut-off values for the disk diffusion method were established for P. multocida and M. haemolytica, the primary pathogenic bacteria associated with BRD, and antibiograms were created for the surveyed regions. The inhibition zone diameter cut-off values for PCG, KM, TC, TS, TP, TMS, and MBFX against P. multocida and those for PCG, KM, CEZ, TC, TS, TP, and MBFX against M. haemolytica showed sensitivity and specificity of ≥0.8. The antimicrobial agents selected in this study are expected to be used in Japan for the treatment of BRD caused by P. multocida and M. haemolytica [15]. These cut-off values may facilitate antimicrobial susceptibility testing in livestock production settings and support the prudent use of antimicrobials. In this study, the inhibition zone diameter cut-off values for PCG and TC were established based on the MIC CBPs. The TC cut-off value established in a previous report [20] was re-evaluated and revised by adding the strains used in the present study. If the strains used for analysis are limited, the results are likely to be strongly influenced by strains exhibiting specific susceptibility profiles, potentially introducing bias into the calculated cut-off values. By integrating the strains reported by Kumakawa et al. [20] with those used in the present study and accumulating data from two facilities, we calculated cut-off values that are less susceptible to strain-specific variability. The TC inhibition zone diameter cut-off values exhibited high sensitivity and specificity; furthermore, the TC susceptibility rate in all surveyed regions combined was similar to the MIC resistance rate reported in a previous study [30]. These findings suggest that the inhibition zone diameter cut-off value for TC against M. haemolytica established in this study provides useful guidance for clinical drug selection, and similar utility may be expected for PCG and TC against P. multocida and PCG against M. haemolytica. The inhibition zone diameter cut-off values for KM and TMS against P. multocida and for KM against M. haemolytica were calculated based on the MIC ECOFFs. The cut-off value for KM was calculated by combining the strains used by Kumakawa et al. [20] with those used in this study. In previous studies, the cut-off value for KM against M. haemolytica was set at 18 mm because no strains with inhibition zone diameters of 7–17 mm were found among those at or below the ECOFF (MIC ≤16 μg/mL) [20]. In this study, a strain exhibiting an MIC of 16 μg/mL and a zone diameter of 15 mm was identified, leading to a revision of the cut-off value to 15 mm (Fig. 1B). Although further evaluation of strains with zone diameters of 7–14 mm remains necessary, this revision may reduce false-resistant classifications (i.e., major discrepancies) in the 15–17 mm range. Since an ECOFF distinguishes wild-type strains from non-wild-type strains with resistance mechanisms, ECOFFs and clinical cut-off values often coincide [8]. However, low-level resistant strains may remain clinically susceptible, and strains classified as wild-type are not necessarily clinically susceptible [8]. The inhibition zone diameter cut-off values established in this study for KM and TMS against P. multocida should therefore be used with consideration of the characteristics of these ECOFFs. For CEZ, TP, MBFX, and TS, MIC cut-off values were derived according to the SOP, and the inhibition zone diameter cut-off values were calculated based on these values. For CEZ, the values were calculated by combining the strains used by Kumakawa et al. [20] with those used in this study. The CEZ cut-off value for P. multocida showed low sensitivity and specificity. Specifically, visual inspection revealed that the data points for MIC and inhibition zone diameter were widely scattered. The MIC distribution was clustered in the range 0.5–2.0 μg/mL (Fig. 1A), which falls within the three-dilution range considered to be the variability range of the microdilution method [6]; the poor correlation and the low sensitivity and specificity of the cut-off values were likely due to the clustering of strains within this narrow MIC range. No quality control acceptable range has been established for TP in CLSI guidelines. A report indicated that the MIC of TP for S. aureus ATCC 29213 was 32 μg/mL [31], which was similar to the results of this study. To the best of our knowledge, no reports regarding quality control data for TP using the disk diffusion method have been published to date. When using the cut-off values for CEZ, TP, MBFX, and TS established in this study, it should be considered that the MIC cut-off values calculated according to the SOP were derived solely from the strains used in this study and that these strains were isolated from clinically affected BRD cases. Furthermore, drug susceptibility breakpoints must consider therapeutic efficacy, pharmacokinetic (PK)/ pharmacodynamic (PD) data, and drug susceptibility test data for each species [26]. In the present study, the epidemiological cut-off values for the inhibition zone diameters of PCG and TC in Japan were established based on CLSI MIC CBPs; however, those for KM, CEZ, TS, TP, MBFX, and TMS (P. multocida) were based on EUCAST ECOFFs or MIC cut-off values calculated according to the SOP. Therefore, PK/PD data and therapeutic efficacy were not considered, which is a limitation of this study. Future studies should review these cut-off values with consideration of these factors.

The susceptibility rate of FFC against M. haemolytica, as shown in the antibiograms, was similar to that reported previously based on MIC values [30]. In this study, the susceptibility rate of FFC in Hokkaido was significantly lower than that in eastern Japan, indicating regional variation. In contrast, the susceptibility rate of ERFX against M. haemolytica was lower than that reported previously [30]. Although this study confirmed farm-level routine therapeutic use of fluoroquinolones, detailed histories of antimicrobial administration were not investigated. However, since isolates were collected from calves exhibiting clinical signs of BRD on farms with a high disease incidence, it is likely that these animals were routinely exposed to antimicrobials. Conversely, a previous report noted that the health status of the cattle could not be investigated [30], which suggests the possibility that isolates from healthy cattle or those with subclinical infections were included in the analysis. Since susceptibility can differ between isolates from healthy and diseased animals [28], the difference in susceptibility rates between the two studies was inferred to be a result of these factors. For agents other than FFC and ERFX, the susceptibility rates in the antibiograms were calculated using MIC cut-off values derived from CBPs (PCG and TC), ECOFFs (KM and TMS for P. multocida), or this study (CEZ, TP, MBFX, and TS). Although the susceptibility rate of MBFX against P. multocida differed from that of ERFX, direct comparison was not considered possible because the cut-off value setting procedures differed. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics show high tissue penetration, distributing to skin, lungs, bones, prostate, neutrophils, and macrophages at concentrations exceeding serum levels [22]. Since this study was limited to in vitro evaluation, the efficacy of MBFX may have been underestimated. Similarly, the susceptibility rates for CEZ, TP, and TS could not be directly compared with those of drugs for which the cut-off value setting procedures differed; therefore, the evaluation of susceptibility rates for these drugs is considered useful primarily for comparisons of the same drugs among regions. However, low susceptibility rates were suggested for MBFX and TS against P. multocida and for TP and TS against M. haemolytica. Because of the small number of strains investigated, data for P. multocida in eastern Japan (24 strains) and M. haemolytica in western Japan (16 strains) should be interpreted with caution [14]. The low susceptibility rates observed in the antibiograms may be related to the high use of these antibiotics. In Japan, all of the antimicrobials investigated in this study are approved for the treatment of BRD [15]. In fact, the use and sales of antimicrobial drugs for dairy and beef cattle in Japan were reported to be the highest for tetracyclines, followed by penicillins, sulfonamides, aminoglycosides, macrolides, thiamphenicols, and cephalosporins [13, 21]. Given that the emergence of resistant bacteria and resistance rates are strongly affected by antimicrobial use [2, 12], antimicrobial usage patterns may be one factor contributing to the low susceptibility rates observed in this study. It is important to promote the prudent use of antimicrobials through the prevention of BRD by vaccine administration and improved hygiene management. In human medicine, empirical antimicrobial treatment based on antibiograms recommends selecting agents with a susceptibility rate of 90% or higher [19]. Therefore, the low susceptibility rates observed in the present study are a cause for concern. Continued monitoring of susceptibility trends is therefore warranted. When selecting antimicrobial therapy, factors such as the type and severity of the infection, the causative pathogen, treatment history, and previous antimicrobial use must be considered. It is not possible to select the optimal empirical antimicrobial therapy for individual animals based on a local antibiogram alone [17]. If the MIC remains below the resistance cut-off value or the inhibition zone diameter remains above the resistance cut-off value, emerging resistance may not be detected [17]. Furthermore, antibiograms only summarize the percentage of isolates that are susceptible to a given microorganism–antimicrobial agent combination, and they are not suitable for understanding patterns of co-resistance with other antibiotics [17]. These limitations should be considered when using antibiograms.

The findings of this study suggest that antimicrobial-resistant P. multocida and M. haemolytica isolated from BRD cases are present in the surveyed regions. In clinical practice, using the cut-off values established by the disk diffusion method in the present study and actively performing antimicrobial susceptibility testing may enable more appropriate antimicrobial therapy compared to current levels. When susceptibility testing is not feasible, antibiograms could potentially be used to select agents with high susceptibility rates. These approaches are expected to contribute to the prudent use of antimicrobials. Further investigation of the spread of resistant strains according to serotype and genotype, together with continued monitoring of antimicrobial resistance using antibiograms, will improve our understanding of resistance trends in BRD-causing bacteria and contribute to the control of antimicrobial resistance.
