Psychological and physical distress in patients with advanced cancer after the COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yirui Liu, Xiao Xiao, Jun Wang, Fengming Ran, Yuan Wu, Shan Liu, Yu Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract The pandemic has exacerbated psychological and physical distress in vulnerable populations, including patients with advanced cancer. However, the full scope and nature of this distress in the post-pandemic era remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the psychological distress and physical symptom burden between two independent cohorts of patients with advanced cancer undergoing active treatment, assessed during versus after the COVID-19 pandemic. In a comparative cross-sectional design, two independent cohorts were evaluated: during-pandemic (n = 129; March-April 2020) and post-pandemic (n = 102; September 2023-February 2024) using HADS and ESAS. The prevalence of clinically significant anxiety was significantly lower in the post-pandemic cohort (35, 34%; 95% CI: 26.7–40.3%) compared to the during-pandemic cohort (54, 53%;95% CI: 46.7–62.9%; p = 0.021). In contrast, the prevalence of depression remained persistently high (43, 42% vs. 51, 50%; p = 0.57). The ESAS scores indicated that the symptoms were mild (with a range of 0 to 3) for both groups, and there were no significant differences in any of the items, including anxiety and depression. Although anxiety decreased after the pandemic, depression persisted. This underscores the need for sustained psychological monitoring in cancer survivorship care.Keywords: COVID-19, Advanced cancer, Anxiety, DepressionSubject terms: Cancer, Psychology

Introduction

Public health emergencies of international concern exert disproportionate psychological consequences on medically vulnerable populations1. Pre-pandemic data showed 20% of cancer patients had clinically significant anxiety or depression2. Although, risk factors for depression and anxiety in cancer are poorly characterised. It is believed that the anxiety experienced by cancer patients is primarily linked to a personal history of anxiety or trauma, as well as metastatic disease. Their depression, on the other hand, is associated with factors such as younger age, social deprivation, and a tendency to blame oneself. The severity of these distresses could be influenced by functional limitations and poor social support.

While further elevations in these symptoms were confirmed by studies conducted during the pandemic3,4. For cancer patients, the COVID-19 pandemic has uniquely exacerbated pre-existing psychological vulnerabilities through dual mechanisms: (1) acute pandemic-related stressors (e.g., treatment delays, infection risks), and (2) chronic cancer-specific distress (e.g., body image disturbance, fear of cancer recurrence)5–8.

However, nearly all prior work focused on short-term impacts during the pandemic; no studies have compared symptom trajectories between the pandemic period and the post-pandemic era (after WHO declared COVID-19 no longer a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in May 2023). To investigate differences in physical and psychological distress among cancer patients during and after the pandemic, we conducted a cross-sectional study of cancer patients from the same institute. We collected their data and compared it with our earlier findings from before the pandemic9.

Methods

Study design and participants

This comparative cross-sectional study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Hubei Cancer Hospital (Approval No. LLHBCH2024YN-026). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Two cohorts were analyzed: (a) the during-pandemic cohort (n = 129), assessed between March and April 2020 as previously published9; and (b) the post-pandemic cohort, newly recruited for this study.

Adult cancer patients who were receiving active cancer treatment, such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, were recruited from the Hubei Cancer Hospital from September 2023 to February 2024. To ensure comparability between the two cohorts, identical inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied for both time periods. Inclusion criteria comprised: (a) age ≥ 18 years; (b) histologically confirmed advanced (Stage III/IV) solid tumor; (c) currently receiving active anti-cancer treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or a combination); (d) ability to understand and complete the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria were: (a) severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders preventing reliable communication; (b) refusal to provide informed consent. Eligible patients were identified through clinical databases. The research staff contacted eligible patients during their hospital stay or via telephone to explain the study. Those who provided informed consent were then sent a structured questionnaire link through WeChat.

Measures

The questionnaire collected demographic and clinical characteristics. Psychological and physical symptoms were assessed using two validated tools.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS): A 14-item self-report questionnaire with two 7-item subscales for Anxiety (HADS-A) and Depression (HADS-D). Each item is scored from 0 to 3. A subscale score ≥ 8 indicates clinically significant symptoms10.

Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS): A validated tool assessing the severity of 12 common symptoms in cancer patients (pain, fatigue, nausea, depression, anxiety, drowsiness, appetite, well-being, shortness of breath, sleep, financial distress, spiritual pain). Patients rate each symptom from 0 (none) to 10 (worst possible) based on their experience over the past 24 h11. Scores are typically interpreted as: 0 (none), 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), 7–10 (severe)12.

HADS focuses on emotional distress (anxiety and depression), while ESAS provides a comprehensive assessment of physical and psychological symptoms, making them complementary in capturing the multifaceted burden of advanced cancer. Validate Chinese version of ESAS was applied in our survey13.

Data analysis

Data were summarized using standard descriptive statistics such as mean, median, and range for continuous variables and frequency and proportion for categorical variables. Association between categorical variables was examined using a χ2 test or Fisher exact test, when appropriate. A Wilcoxon rank sum test or Kruskal-Wallis test was used to examine the difference in continuous variables between or among patients’ characteristic groups. All tests were 2-sided, and P ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All computations were carried out using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

A prospective sample size calculation was performed to determine the minimum number of participants required for this cross-sectional survey. The primary outcome for this calculation was the score on the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS), which ranges from 0 to 10. The calculation indicated a minimum required sample size of 102 participants. Given the study’s exploratory nature of cross-sectional study (focused on symptom comparison rather than testing pre-specified independent hypotheses) and the clinical correlation between assessed symptoms (e.g., HADS-Anxiety and HADS-Depression, ESAS-Fatigue and ESAS-Sleep)10,12, formal statistical adjustment for multiple comparisons was not applied. Instead, results were interpreted by integrating three dimensions: statistical significance, effect size (absolute changes in prevalence/median scores), and clinical relevance (per ESAS/HADS validation criteria)10,12—a approach to balance rigor and avoidance of Type II error (missing clinically meaningful trends). In order to control for Type I error in analyses with multiple outcomes, we adjust p value as ≤ 0.025.

Results

A total of 191 patients were invited for the post-pandemic survey, and 102 completed it, yielding a response rate of 53.5%. The baseline characteristics of the post-pandemic (n = 102) and during-pandemic (n = 129) cohorts are presented in Table 1. The post-pandemic cohort comprised 102 patients, predominantly elderly (87.2% aged > 50 years), with a balanced gender distribution (n = 44, 43.1% female). Head and neck cancer (n = 27, 26.5%) and lung cancer (n = 53, 52%) were the most common diagnoses. The majority had a college and above education (n = 55, 53.8%), and 30.4% (n = 31) reported a history of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular (n = 15, 14.7%) and chronic lung diseases (n = 4, 3.9%). Caregiver support was available for 86.3% (n = 88), while 6.8% (n = 7) required mental health assistance. The HADS results showed that 35 (34%) patients and 43 (42%) patients had anxiety and depression, respectively (Table 2). The incidence of anxiety is significantly lower than during the pandemic, as indicated by the HADS.

The HADS results (Table 2) showed that the prevalence of clinically significant anxiety was significantly lower in the post-pandemic cohort compared to the during-pandemic cohort (n = 35, 34% vs. n = 54, 53%, p = 0.021). The prevalence of depression did not differ significantly between the two cohorts (n = 43, 42% vs. n = 51, 50%, p = 0.57).

ESAS assessments revealed that the median scores for all symptoms, including depression and anxiety, were in the mild range (0–3) in both cohorts. There were no significant differences in the median scores for ESAS-depression (p = 0.32) or ESAS-anxiety (p = 0.33) between the two time points. However, statistically significant but clinically minor differences were observed for several symptoms: fatigue, nausea, and dyspnea scores were slightly higher post-pandemic, while financial distress and spiritual pain scores were lower (all p < 0.05).

Discussion

This comparative study reveals the divergent long-term trajectories of anxiety and depression in patients with advanced cancer following the COVID-19 pandemic. While anxiety prevalence decreased significantly one year after the pandemic was declared over, depression rates remained persistently high, underscoring the distinct nature of these psychological burdens.

The reduction in anxiety symptoms aligns with the hypothesis that anxiety, often fueled by acute, external threats like a pandemic, diminishes as the immediate crisis resolves and healthcare systems normalize. This finding highlights the context-dependent nature of anxiety in this population. Conversely, the persistence of depression suggests it is more deeply tethered to the chronic and multifaceted burden of advanced cancer itself, including ongoing treatment toxicities, existential concerns, functional limitations, and body image disturbances8,14, which remain unchanged by the end of the public health emergency.

However, the data show no significant difference between the two cohorts by ESAS. As we know, the ESAS, which is commonly used to assess symptoms in cancer patients, was initially designed for symptom assessment during routine care practices. The influence of the pandemic does not seem strong enough to impact the symptoms of cancer patients compared to the impact of cancer itself.

According to our results of the HADS scale, the incidence of anxiety was significantly reduced after the pandemic compared to the during-pandemic period. This suggests a potential recovery phase for these patients as they adapt to the post-pandemic environment, possibly aided by the normalization of routine care practices. However, the HADS and ESAS scales showed no significant difference in the incidence of depression between the two periods. The discrepancy between the HADS and ESAS in detecting anxiety—where HADS showed a significant change but ESAS did not—can be attributed to the design of these tools. The HADS is a specific psychometric instrument designed to screen for clinical levels of anxiety and depression10. The ESAS-anxiety item, however, is a single question capturing the momentary perception of anxiety symptom severity within a broader spectrum of physical symptoms11. This suggests that while the specific clinical construct of anxiety improved, the general sensation of feeling anxious or worried, perhaps related to the illness itself, may persist at a mild, stable level. For cancer patients, different assessment scales can be selected according to different needs, or the two scales can be used together.

The emergence of novel cancer therapeutics has transformed advanced cancer into a more chronic condition, inadvertently introducing new psychological challenges15. Prolonged survival often occurs within a state of chronic uncertainty—“liminality”—where patients live with the constant fear of progression despite treatment16. This state, coupled with the long-term toxicities of treatments like immunotherapy and targeted therapy (e.g., fatigue, neurocognitive changes)17 and sustained socioeconomic strain (“financial toxicity”)18, creates a fertile ground for persistent depression that is not easily alleviated.

Our findings highlight the necessity of integrating long-term, systematic psychological monitoring (e.g., using HADS) into oncology survivorship care. This calls for continuous screening protocols tailored to the distinct trajectories of anxiety and depression. Intervention strategies must be similarly personalized: while system-level support sufficed for pandemic-induced anxiety, addressing chronic depression requires sustained, multifaceted individual-level support. This includes embedding psycho-oncology services, promoting access to cognitive-behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based interventions19, and leveraging eHealth and AI-driven platforms20 to enhance accessibility and personalization of care. Just as there are different results of anxiety indicated by two assessment tools (ESAS and HADS), AI tools should be developed cautiously, taking professionals’ attitudes and viewpoints into account. Future multi-center longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these trends and develop effective interventions specifically targeting the persistent depression observed in cancer survivors.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design (Hubei Cancer Hospital, Wuhan) and modest sample size (n = 129 during pandemic, n = 102 post-pandemic) limit generalizability. The post-pandemic cohort was predominantly elderly (> 50 years: 87.2%), with lung cancer (52%) and head and neck cancer (26.5%) as the most common diagnoses—results may not apply to younger patients, those with other cancer types (e.g., breast, gastrointestinal cancer), or patients in regions with different pandemic mitigation strategies (e.g., areas with less severe COVID-19 impacts). Future multi-center studies with diverse cancer populations are needed to validate these trajectories. Second, the comparison of independent cohorts (not longitudinal follow-up of the same individuals) introduces potential selection bias (e.g., survivor bias, as post-pandemic patients may represent a subset of more resilient individuals). Although, baseline characteristics (gender, education, caregiver support) were balanced between cohorts (Table 2), mitigating this bias to some extent.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the psychological impact of a public health emergency on advanced cancer patients is not monolithic. The acute anxiety triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic subsided after its resolution, but the shadow of depression, intrinsically linked to the chronic cancer experience, remained. This divergence calls for a nuanced and sustained approach to psychological care in oncology, ensuring that as we manage the disease, we also prioritize the mental well-being of those living with it long-term.
