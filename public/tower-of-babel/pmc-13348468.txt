Genetic evidence of population admixture with European pigs and Southern Chinese pigs in Chinese indigenous Diqing Tibetan breed
Authors: Suyun Fang, Yuzhan Wang, Yuedong Zhang, Yiqiang Zhao, Xiaoxiang Hu, Ruifei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Chinese native Diqing Tibetan pig has a long history of domestication and is distributed in the southernmost region of China compared to other Tibetan breeds. In this study, we performed whole-genome sequencing of the Diqing Tibetan pigs. By integrating previously published data, we explored the genetic structure, population diversity, and genetic introgression from commercial European pigs and Southern Chinese domestic pigs into Diqing Tibetan pigs. We further examined the potential genetic influences of European pig introgression and inferred the historical events of admixture with Southern Chinese breeds.ResultsOur analysis revealed various instances of population admixture between Diqing Tibetan pigs and European pigs, as well as redundant introgression from South Chinese pigs when compared to other Tibetan pig populations. The introgression from European pigs significantly increased the population diversity in Diqing Tibetan pigs, which may influence fertility. Additionally, the introgression from European pigs affected the proportion of introgression from Southern Chinese pigs, with the most pronounced effect near the PSCK2 gene, which is associated with body size. Regarding ancestral genetic components from Southern Chinese pigs, we discovered a significant level of redundant proportion in Diqing Tibetan pigs compared to other Tibetan breeds. This admixture may have occurred at multiple time points from different breeds, with the Diannan small ear breed in Yunnan Province of China potentially serving as a recent primary contributor to the introgression into Diqing Tibetan breed.ConclusionOur study reveals that the Diqing Tibetan breed has been shaped by admixture from European and multiple Southern Chinese sources, where European introgression increased diversity and reshaped Southern ancestry, while Southern introgression itself derives from distinct Southern Chinese pig breeds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12951-0.Keywords: Genome resequencing, Genetic introgression, Population admixture, Population diversity, Pig

Background

The Tibetan pig is one of the indigenous breeds of China, renowned for its remarkable stress tolerance to extreme cold and plateau hypoxia [1]. Evidence from ancient bone samples collected from archeological sites have indicated that the domestication of Tibetan pigs commenced approximately 5000 years ago [2]. Over long-term processes of domestication and human migration, the population structure may have evolved and diversified, leading to the formation of multiple domestication centers of Tibetan pigs [2–5]. Currently, there are four primary populations of Tibetan pigs, located in the provinces of Sichuan (specifically in the Aba and Ganzi Autonomous Prefectures), Tibet (including Linzhi, Shannan, and Chamdo within the Tibet Autonomous Prefecture), Gansu (in the Gannan Autonomous Prefecture), and Yunnan (in the Diqing Autonomous Prefecture) in China [1]. Research has demonstrated that these four populations exhibit distinct evolutionary trajectories and significant genetic divergence, with each displaying notable adaptations to the plateau environment [3, 4, 6–8]. However, for an extended period, local residents raised Tibetan pigs in a traditional free-range manner, resulting in less human selection pressure compared to commercial breeds [1, 4].

The Chinese province of Yunnan is one of the primary centers for the domestication of Tibetan pigs [2]. The population found in Yunnan, specifically the Diqing Tibetan pig, inhabits areas at altitudes exceeding 3000 m around the city of Shangri-La, which represents the southernmost distribution of Tibetan pigs [1]. Research has demonstrated that Chinese pigs possess distinct ancestral genetic components, primarily categorized into southern and northern lineages [9]. Consequently, the Diqing Tibetan breed has likely undergone admixture with certain Southern Chinese pig breeds, resulting in a unique population structure when compared to other Tibetan pig breeds.

In recent decades, improvements in infrastructure and the completion of highways connecting Tibetan areas to external regions have facilitated the importation of Western commercial pigs into local areas [1]. These pigs exhibit significantly better growth performance and yield higher economic benefits compared to local indigenous breeds, resulting in a decline in the population of local pigs. Additionally, the extensive importation of commercial European pigs (EP) may have led to increased hybridization events with local breeds, potentially resulting in phenotypic changes among indigenous populations. Previous studies have provided genetic evidence of gene flow between Chinese and European pigs [10–12]. For the EP introgression into Chinese population, there have been three waves of EP introgression into Chinese pigs in recent centuries [13], which may have potentially cause phenotypic alterations such as changes in coat color [14], body size [15], and reproductive traits [16]. Unlike adaptive introgression or human-mediated introgression associated with ancient human migrations [9, 17], the EP introgression events are relatively recent and may contribute to increased population diversity and divergence from the original ecology [18]. The Diqing Tibetan pig is one such local indigenous breed that may have undergone potential European introgression, which could lead to significant changes in genetic variation and population structure. In this study, we generated genome resequencing data for 21 Diqing Tibetan pigs and combined it with previously published data, resulting in genomic data from a total of 778 pigs. We conducted genetic analyses to investigate the genetic relationships among these pigs. We performed population admixture analysis and compared the ancestral genetic components from other populations across four types of Tibetan pigs, with a particular focus on the comparison of EP and SCDP introgression. For EP introgression, we analyzed its source and examined the potential genetic influences in Diqing Tibetan breed. In the case of SCDP introgression, we compared different Tibetan populations and inferred the transmission history of SCDP introgression in Diqing Tibetan pig population. Our study provides genetic evidence of EP and SCDP introgression in Diqing Tibetan breed, highlighting their significant genetic influences and multiple breed sources.

Methods

Sample preparation and genome resequencing

Ear tissue samples were collected from 21 live Diqing Tibetan pigs at the age of six months, and the pigs were not slaughtered or euthanized. All individuals were raised and managed under consistent temperature and feeding regimes at a breeding farm located near Shangri-La City in the Diqing Autonomous Prefecture of China, where the altitude exceeds 3300 m.

Genomic DNA was extracted using a standard phenol–chloroform extraction method. For genome resequencing, a minimum of 0.5 µg of genomic DNA from each sample was utilized to construct a library with an insert size of 150 bp. Paired-end sequencing libraries were then constructed and sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequence alignment and variant calling

We used the fastp software (v0.23.4) (https://github.com/OpenGENE/FASTP) to filter the original data [19], applying the following quality control parameters: -w 20, -q 20, -u 30, and -l 50. Following quality control, we obtained an average of 32.57× high-quality clean data, with a total data volume reaching 1775.86 G (Table S1). For genome alignment and variant calling, we used the GTX platform from Genetalks, which is based on a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) hardware accelerator. The alignment process was accelerated by FPGA implementation of a parallel seed-and-extend approach based on the Smith–Waterman algorithm, while the variant calling process was accelerated by FPGA implementation of GATK HaplotypeCaller (PairHMM) [20]. The reference genome, Sscrofa11.1, was retrieved from the Ensembl database (GenBank accession number: GCA_000003025.6). Resequencing data for 757 pigs from the public NCBI database were downloaded and subjected to the same procedures of quality control, genome alignment, and variant calling (Table S2). These pigs were classified into different populations according to their geographical distribution (http://iswine.iomics.pro/pig-iqgs/iqgs/index) [21], including 282 pigs from four commercial European breeds (Duroc: DUR; Large White: LWT; Landrace: LR; Pietrain: PT) and European wild boar (EWB); 49 domestic pigs from four regions in Southwest China (i.e., Southwestern Chinese domestic pigs (SWCDP): Baoshan: BS; Neijiang: NJ; Wujin: WJ; Rongchang: RC; Yanan: YN); 91 Tibetan pigs from four provinces of China (Gansu: GSTP, in Gansu Province; Sichuan: SCTP, in Sichuan Province; Tibet: TTPA (all set), in the Tibet Autonomous Region; Yunnan: DQTPA (all set), in the Diqing Autonomous Region, Yunnan Province); 90 domestic pigs primarily distributed in Zhejiang or Jiangsu Province of Eastern China (i.e., Eastern Chinese domestic pigs (ECDP): Meishan: MS; Erhualian: EHL; Jinhua: JH; Jiquhai: JQH; Jiaxinghei: JXH); 44 pigs from Northern Chinese domestic breeds (Northern Chinese domestic pigs (NCDP): Laiwu: LWH; Min: MZ; Hetao: HT; Dingyuan: DY); 38 pigs from Mid China (Mid Chinese domestic pigs (MCDP): Daweizi: DWZ; Ningxiang: NX; Shazilin: SZL; Tongcheng: TC); 126 pigs from Southern Chinese domestic breeds (Southern Chinese domestic pigs (SCDP): Bamaxiang: BMX; Wuzhishan: WZS; Luchuan: LC; Dahuabai: DHB; Lantang: LT; Diannan small ear: DNSM; Guangdong small ear: GDSM; Debao: DB); 14 Chinese wild boars (CWB); 5 Sus barbatus pigs (SBB); 4 Sus verrucosus pigs (SVCS); and 14 Phacochoerus africanus pigs (PA).

After obtaining the VCF files described above, we retained autosomal SNPs and utilized VCFtools (v0.1.17) to filter low-quality SNPs using the following parameters: --maf 0.05, --min-alleles 2, --max-alleles 2, --remove-indels, --hwe 1e-4, and --max-missing 0.95 [22]. Subsequently, we employed Beagle (v5.4) to impute missing SNP genotypes from published data [23]. Upon completion, we used this data as a reference panel to impute SNPs for our generated data, which we then combined using bcftools (v1.8) [24]. In total, 778 pigs were retained, resulting in 11,196,256 SNPs, with a marker density of one SNP per 202 bp.

Phylogenetics and population structure

Matrices of identity-by-state (IBS) between individuals were calculated using PLINK (v1.9) with the command (--distance square 1-ibs) [25]. These matrices were subsequently used to construct unrooted neighbour-joining (NJ) trees with FastME (v2.1.6.1) [26]. Tree visualization was performed using the ‘ggtree’ package in R [27]. Additionally, we used PLINK to prune the SNP data based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) with the parameter ‘--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2’ [25]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the LD-pruned data using genome-wide complex trait analysis (GCTA) software (v1.26.0) [27]. Population admixture analysis was executed with ADMIXTURE software (v1.3.0) [28], varying K from 2 to 15, and cross-validation errors were obtained from each run. The results of the admixture analysis were visualized using the R package ‘Pophelper’ [29]. LD decay analysis was conducted using PopLDdecay software with the parameters ‘-MaxDist 100000 -MAF 0.05’, and the script ‘Plot_MultiPop.pl’ from the PopLDdecay toolkit was used to plot the LD decay of Tibetan pig populations [30].

Global ancestry and local ancestry inference

To confirm the population admixture between the EP or SCDP and Tibetan pigs, we applied D and f4 statistics to identify global introgression signals using Dsuite software (v0.5 r52) [31]. We partitioned Tibetan pigs from the Diqing and Tibet regions into DQTP and DQTP_EP, and TTP and TTP_EP based on the results of the population admixture analysis. If the proportion of ancestral components from EP (LWT, DUR, LR, and PT) exceeded 10%, the pig was classified as either DQTP_EP or TTP_EP. For EP introgression detection, we designated EP as the P3 population, while closely related populations to Tibetan pigs served as the P1 population (sister population, including breeds classified as SWCDP and Tibetan pigs). Tibetan pigs were designated as the P2 population, and the outgroup included SBB, SVCS, and PA, according to a previous study. For SCDP introgression detection, we designated LC, BMX, DB, WZS, DNSM, DHB, LT and GDSM as the P3 population, while P1, P2 and the outgroup remained consistent with the aforementioned designations. The significance level for global introgression was set at Z > 3 and p-value < 0.05.

Based on the significant results obtained from Dsuite analysis, we conducted local ancestry inference using RFMix (v2.03) software to quantify the introgressive hybridization of genomic segments from EP and SCDP into Tibetan pigs [32]. We selected breeds identified with high purity, characterized by the highest specific ancestral components (K = 5), to serve as our reference population. In our study, we represented the ECDP, SWCDP, and SCDP populations with MS, NJ, and LC, respectively. For EP, we used LWT, LR, and DUR as representatives, as these breeds are primarily utilized in the modern pig industry (three-way cross). Subsequently, we employed MS, NJ, LC, LR, LWT, and DUR as the reference population. The target population included six Tibetan pig populations: DQTP_EP, DQTP (EP component proportion < 10%), TTP_EP, TTP (EP component proportion < 10%), SCTP, and GSTP, which were analyzed to detect local introgression signals across the genome. The regulation of encoding introgressed haplotype followed a previous study [33]: the genome was divided into non-overlapping blocks of 5 SNPs, if the probability of a genetic segment from a specific population exceeded 0.5, we classified it as a haplotype block from that population; otherwise, it was classified as a segment from other populations. Consequently, for EP or SCDP introgression, if the sum of the probabilities of a genetic segment from DUR, LR, and LWT (for EP) or LC (for SCDP) exceeded 0.5, we encoded the haplotype from EP or SCDP as ‘1’; otherwise, it was encoded as ‘0’. For specific breeds in EP (DUR, LR, or LWT), the encoding process followed the same criteria as described above. All subsequent analyses were based on SNP and the encoded haplotype data. We then computed the frequency of introgression from EP or SCDP across genome-wide bins and Z-transformed these frequencies. A bin with a Z-score greater than 2 was considered evidence of significant introgression from EP or SCDP. Adjacent bins (with a distance of less than 100 Kb) that were also identified as significant introgression were combined into a block.

The inference of introgression time from SCDP into DQTP was conducted using the ALDER tool (V1.03) [34], with LC and DNSM serving as representatives of the SCDP for the P1 population, while NJ represented the P2 population. The results were according to the output of the 1-ref inference, where one generation corresponds to five years [14, 35].

Comparison of local genetic introgression

For EP introgression, comparative analyses were primarily conducted on DQTP versus DQTP_EP and TTP versus TTP_EP. The N50, N60, N70, N80, and N90 block sizes of EP introgression segments were calculated and compared among these groups. Subsequently, using the encoded haplotype data, we computed the frequency of EP-derived haplotypes with VCFtools (--freq) [22]. We then compared the distribution of the overall EP-derived haplotype frequency across the DQTP, DQTP_EP, TTP, and TTP_EP groups. The thickness, representing the overall EP-introgressed variation, was assessed throughout the entire genome, and coefficients of variation were compared among these groups. Furthermore, we partitioned the EP introgression into DUR, LWT, and LR introgressions within the DQTP_EP and TTP_EP groups. Based on the haplotype frequency across the genome and its correlation with total EP introgression, the Pearson coefficient (R) was compared between the DQTP_EP and TTP_EP groups.

Results

Population structure, PCA, and population admixture of Diqing Tibetan breed with other pigs

PCA analysis based on whole-genome resequencing data from 755 geographically classified pigs (excluding outgroup) (Tables S1–S2) revealed their clear patterns of population structure. The results indicated that the 1st Principal component (PC1) effectively distinguished the European pigs (EP and EWB) from Chinese indigenous breeds, while PC2 and PC3 further separated the Chinese indigenous populations into regional groups, consistent with their geographic distributions (Fig. 1a). Among them, all Tibetan pig breeds clustered with Southwestern Chinese pigs, suggesting their genetic similarity and proximity in Southwest China. Furthermore, the NJ tree illustrated that Tibetan pigs from distinct regions formed separate branches (Fig. 1b). Among these, Diqing Tibetan pigs (DQTPA) clustered with Southwestern Chinese pigs (BS breed), indicating a relatively similar genetic relationship and recent population admixture among some individuals within these populations. We further conducted a population admixture analysis for the number of ancestors, specifically for K values ranging from 2 to 15 (Fig. 1c). At K = 2, we observed a significant presence of ancestral genetic components from European pigs in several Chinese pig populations. Notably, the North Chinese pigs (NCDP) exhibited the highest proportion of European genetic components compared to the other populations. Tibetan pigs from different regions of China demonstrated variable levels of European genetic components (Fig. 1c). Among these, DQTPA and TTPA displayed higher levels of genetic contributions from European pigs than GSTP and SCTP. Based on the results for K = 5, which indicated a notable gradient in the decrease of cross-validation error (ΔCV = 1.58%) (Table S3), the DQTPA and TTPA populations exhibited various ancestral components from European pigs, primarily from Duroc and Large White pigs (DQTPA: 0.00-30.78% from DUR and 0.00-11.66% from LWT; TTPA: 1.31–17.38% and 0.00-36.55%) (Fig. 1c and Table S4). Additionally, the genetic ancestral components from South Chinese pigs (SCDP) also varied among Tibetan pigs, with proportions of 0.00-1.96% in GSTP, 1.40-10.91% in SCTP, 0.00-12.49% in TTPA, and 5.97–11.94% in DQTPA. Consequently, we concluded that DQTPA, along with other Tibetan pigs, clustered with SWCDP, while showing varying degrees of population admixture with other breeds.

Genetic diversity in Diqing Tibetan breed compared to other Tibetan pig group

The NJ tree and population admixture results indicated distinct genetic variations among different Tibetan populations (Fig. 1b and c). We further examined the genetic diversity of Tibetan pigs. Given the presence of various EP ancestral genetic components in DQTPA and TTPA (Fig. 1c and Table S4), we categorized these populations into two groups: DQTP and TTP with EP component proportions less than 10%, and DQTP_EP and TTP_EP with EP component proportions of 10% or greater. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, DQTP and GSTP exhibited lower He, site-pi, and PIC values compared to SCTP and TTP. Notably, populations with significant EP components (DQTP_EP and TTP_EP) demonstrated the highest genetic diversity when compared to DQTP, TTP, GSTP, and SCTP, suggesting that substantial EP introgression may contribute to increased genetic diversity in Tibetan pigs. Furthermore, we assessed LD decay across these six populations, revealing that the DQTP population exhibited the smoothest decay rate, while TTP_EP showed the steepest (Fig. 2b). DQTP_EP exhibited a higher rate of LD decay than DQTP, as well as TTP_EP compared to TTP, suggesting a greater frequency of recombination events in these populations, which in turn reflects the higher genetic diversity associated with redundant EP introgression. Furthermore, we calculated the proportion of |iHS| scores greater than 2 within the top 1% window bins, revealing that DQTP_EP and TTP_EP displayed higher values than the other four populations (Fig. 2c). However, since the |iHS| score does not indicate the direction of selection, we utilized the NJ pig (a breed in Southwestern Chinese pig population with highest purity in population admixture result) as a comparative population to compute the XPEHH score for each group. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, DQTP_EP and TTP_EP exhibited the lowest number of bins with XPEHH > 2 compared to the other groups, suggesting there may be very recent gene flow between these populations and European pigs, diluting the divergence of selection signals. The increased genetic diversity may have contributed to the positive selection signals observed in the comparative NJ breed, as evidenced by more XPEHH < -2 signals in TTP_EP compared to TTP, and in DQTP_EP compared to DQTP (Fig. S1).

Admixture between DQTP_EP and European pigs

As demonstrated in the preceding analyses, we identified a redundant EP ancestral component in DQTP_EP and TTP_EP (Fig. 1c). To validate the global introgression signals, we conducted a comparison of global introgression (D-statistic and f4 ratio) among Tibetan pigs. Consequently, we detected EP introgression signals in the DQTP_EP, TTP_EP, GSTP, and TTP populations, with DQTP_EP and TTP_EP exhibiting significantly higher signals than GSTP and TTP. This finding aligns with the results of the population admixture analysis (Figs. 1c and 3a, and Table S5). We subsequently concentrated on the DQTP_EP and TTP_EP groups, as both displayed markedly higher EP introgression signals compared to the other groups, designating TTP_EP as a comparative group against DQTP_EP.

We conducted local introgression detection (see Methods) and used the haplotype bins defined as EP introgression to compare the lengths of EP-introgressed haplotype blocks. The results indicated that the length of the EP-introgressed block in DQTP_EP was shorter than that in TTP_EP (Fig. 3b). This difference may be attributed to a more recent EP introgression event occurring in TTP_EP compared to DQTP_EP, rather than strong selection on the introgressed segments. This is supported by the higher genetic diversity and weaker selection sweep signals observed in the Tibetan population with redundant EP introgression compared to other Tibetan pig groups (Fig. 2b-d). Additionally, we distributed the EP haplotype frequency across the entire genome. As shown in Fig. 3c, both populations exhibited a nearly bell-shaped distribution of EP introgression. However, DQTP_EP displayed a flatter distribution than TTP_EP, with the coefficient of variation (CV) for DQTP_EP (50.49%) being higher than that for TTP_EP (38.81%). This indicated that the genome-wide EP introgression in DQTP_EP was more uneven compared to TTP_EP, suggesting that the ancestral sources from EP may differ between these two groups. Therefore, we partitioned the EP introgression into three sources: DUR, LWT, and LR, as these three populations represent the most prevalent lines in the modern pig industry. The frequency of haplotypes from DUR, LWT, and LR in the DQTP_EP and TTP_EP groups exhibited significant differences, with a notably higher redundant DUR introgression (p-value < 2.2e-16, T-test) compared to the less significant introgressions from LWT (p-value < 2.2e-16, T-test) and LR (p-value < 2.2e-16, T-test) in DQTP_EP than in TTP_EP (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, the total EP introgression frequency showed a stronger correlation with DUR introgression in DQTP_EP than in TTP_EP (0.65 in DQTP_EP and 0.30 in TTP_EP), while the correlations with LWT (0.53 in DQTP_EP and 0.65 in TTP_EP) and LR (0.42 in DQTP_EP and 0.46 in TTP_EP) were comparatively weaker (Fig. 3e). Notably, the correlation coefficient for DUR introgression was more than twice as high in DQTP_EP compared to TTP_EP. These results suggest that Duroc breed contributed more significantly to EP introgression than the other European commercial lines in DQTP_EP, reflecting a distinct hybridization mode compared to TTP_EP.

To investigate the genetic impact of the EP introgression, we overlapped the SNP sites in the region of introgressed bins with the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) (p-value < 1e-5) obtained from the pigGTEX platform and subsequently performed enrichment analysis (see Methods). In our comparisons, the sites located within the introgression bins exhibited significantly greater enrichment (> 2-fold) for eQTLs associated with embryo, ovary, and lymph node tissues in DQTP_EP compared to TTP_EP. Conversely, these sites showed considerably less enrichment (< 0.5-fold) for kidney, frontal cortex, and duodenum tissues (Fig. 3f). This suggests that the EP introgression may introduce greater molecular phenotypic variations (gene expression levels) and result in a more distinct expression profile in certain tissues. We further computed the enrichment score based on different EP lines (DUR, LWT, and LR). For the DUR introgression, we observed a higher number of tissues with more than two-fold enrichment score in DQTP_EP (8 tissues) compared to TTP_EP (2 tissues) (Fig. 3g). This indicates that the more redundant DUR introgression in DQTP_EP compared to TTP_EP leads to greater molecular expression changes. Among the 8 tissues, we found that more than half were associated with embryonic development (blastocyst, fetal thymus and morula) and reproduction (ovary and oocyte). This may imply that the redundant DUR introgression contributes to increased genetic variation related to fertility in DQTP_EP compared to TTP_EP. Additionally, we compared the enrichment scores for LWT and LR introgression (Fig. S2). The comparison of enrichment scores between DQTP_EP and TTP_EP did not show a greater differences than when DUR was designated as the comparative group, as the differences in LR and LWT introgression frequencies between DQTP_EP and TTP_EP were less pronounced than those observed for DUR (LWT: 4 in DQTP_EP vs. 3 in TTP_EP; LR: 2 in DQTP_EP vs. 3 in TTP_EP) (Fig. 3d and e).

Admixture between DQTP and Southern Chinese pigs

In addition to various ancestral components from EP in DQTPA, we identified a significantly higher level of ancestral components from SCDP in DQTP compared to other groups (p-value < 0.01, Duncan test) (Fig. 1c; Table S4 and Fig. S3). To validate the introgression from SCDP into the DQTP population, we conducted global introgression signal detection. Consequently, we detected significant SCDP introgression signals in DQTP, TTP, SCTP, and GSTP groups (Fig. 4a and Table S5). Furthermore, we based our analysis on local introgression detection signals using the Luchuan (LC) breed as a representative SCDP reference group (see Methods) and subsequently calculated the frequency of SCDP-derived haplotypes in six Tibetan pig groups. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, DQTP exhibited the highest haplotype frequency, averaging 11.32% for each 5-SNP window bin, whereas GSTP displayed the lowest frequency, averaging 3.76% per bin. This finding aligns with the population admixture results, indicating the highest level of SCDP ancestral genetic component in DQTP and the lowest in GSTP (Fig. 1c, Table S4 and Fig. S3). Next, we compared the distribution of genome-wide SCDP-derived haplotype frequencies. Unlike the EP-derived haplotypes, the frequency of SCDP-derived haplotypes exhibited a right-skewed distribution in each population, characterized by a high density of low-frequency haplotypes and a gradual decline as frequency increased (Fig. 4c). Among the four groups, the magnitude of density varied, indicating differences in haplotype diversity and population admixture. The GSTP exhibited the highest density at low SCDP-derived haplotype frequencies. In contrast, the DQTP displayed the flattest distribution compared to the other groups, with haplotypes more evenly distributed across the genome. The wider frequency range of haplotypes suggested a more balanced contribution of both rare and moderately common haplotypes, indicating that DQTP may have experienced different selective pressures or demographic events compared to GSTP. The length of SCDP-derived haplotype blocks was longest in DQTP and shortest in GSTP across the four comparative groups (Fig. 4d), further highlighting the differences in SCDP-derived introgression within Tibetan pigs. We calculated genetic diversity using SNPs located in the significant SCDP introgression regions (Z > 2) across the four groups. The results indicated that the genetic diversity within SCDP introgressed blocks was comparable to the genome-wide level, with DQTP and GSTP exhibiting lower genetic diversity compared to TTP and SCTP (Fig. 4e). Notably, the SCDP-derived haplotype blocks demonstrated higher genetic differentiation (Fst index) when GSTP was used as a comparison, and this differentiation was also greater than the genome-wide level in three comparative groups (Fig. 4f). Among these, the genetic differentiation in SCDP-derived introgressed regions between DQTP and GSTP displayed the highest Fst index compared to other groups, suggesting that although both populations exhibited the lowest genetic diversity in the SCDP-derived introgression blocks, the selective pressures may differ for SCDP introgression in DQTP and GSTP.

As shown in Fig. 1c, we observed a significantly higher proportion of the ancestral genetic component from SCDP in DQTP compared to DQTP_EP, with an average of 10.97% SCDP component in DQTP versus 7.40% in DQTP_EP (p-value < 0.01, Duncan test) (Fig. S3). This difference may be attributed to the EP introgression, which likely contributes to the decrease of the SCDP component in DQTP. Consequently, we compared the genome-wide SCDP and EP introgression between the DQTP and DQTP_EP groups. The results indicated that the haplotype frequency of EP introgression for each bin in DQTP_EP was 19.20% higher than in DQTP, while the haplotype frequency of SCDP introgression for each bin in DQTP was 2.84% greater than that in DQTP_EP, exhibiting a thicker right tail compared to the left for SCDP introgression (Fig. S4). The most significant differentiation was detected near the region of the PCSK2 gene on Chromosome 17 (17:25,395,847 − 25,490,580 bp), where the delta haplotype frequency (DAF) reached 0.633 for SCDP introgression (DQTP compared to DQTP_EP) and 0.380 for EP introgression (DQTP_EP compared to DQTP). Based on SNP sites within this region, we correlated the allele frequency among Tibetan breeds (DQTP_EP, DQTP, TTP, SCTP, and GSTP) and the LC group. The results demonstrated that the LC breed was most closely correlated with DQTP compared to the other groups (R² = 0.753) (Fig. 4h). Therefore, DQTP exhibited a high level of introgression with SCDP in this region; however, the redundant EP introgression could lead to genetic changes affecting this introgression in DQTP_EP.

The SCDP introgression in DQTP may not be directly transmitted from SCDP breeds located in southern China. Instead, the introgression event in DQTP appears to have occurred gradually from other breeds. As we observed a more significant global introgression signal from SCDP when using Diannan small ear pigs (DNSM) as the reference group (P3) compared to other SCDP breeds (Table S4). Next, we focused on the DNSM breed, which also habited in Yunnan Province, China, but further south with a subtropical climate. Based on the genome regions identified as SCDP introgression using the LC breed as the reference group, we reanalyzed these regions using both LC and DNSM breeds as reference groups. As a result, 85.37% of these haplotypes were identified as derived from DNSM, while only 14.63% were from the LC breed (Fig. 4i). To validate the genetic source of these bins, we performed genetic differentiation analysis using the Fst index, focusing on SNPs located within the representative regions that exhibited high introgression haplotype frequency from either LC or DNSM (see Methods). As shown in Fig. 4j, the average Fst based on SNPs in the bin regions derived from DNSM was significantly lower than that in LC-derived bins when comparing DQTP and DNSM (p-value < 0.01, T-test), but conversely based on the SNPs in the bin regions derived from LC (p-value < 0.001, T-test). Furthermore, the genetic diversity of the introgression regions from DNSM was considerably higher than that from LC (Fig. S5), and even higher than the whole genome level (Fig. 2a and Fig. S5), suggesting the possibility of the more than one admixture events with SCDP populations in DQTP.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the population genetic structure and diversity of Diqing Tibetan breed and compared it to other Tibetan pigs. We found that Diqing Tibetan pigs, along with other Tibetan groups, were classified as part of the SWCDP population and exhibited admixture with various pig populations (Fig. 1c). The Diqing Tibetan pig population primarily showed admixture from European and Southern Chinese pigs.

Gene flow between Diqing Tibetan breed with European pigs

The admixture event between Tibetan pigs and the European pigs may have occurred in recent decades [1, 18]. Traditionally, Tibetan pigs have been raised through free-range farming and typically kept in higher altitudes. This free farming practice facilitates mating with other pigs, such as wild boars or breeds located in the surrounding regions [1]. Our previous study has demonstrated that the Lijiang pig could serve as a potential mediator for the transmission of EP introgression into the Diqing Tibetan breed [18]. Additionally, the completion of infrastructure and highways in Yunnan Province of China in recent decades has facilitated the transportation of various pig breeds within the local region. Historical records indicate that Tibetan pigs, particularly those located near the river valleys, have hybridized with commercial European breeds [1]. Our study has confirmed admixture between Diqing Tibetan breed and commercial European pigs (Fig. 3a). Admixture with EP has resulted in significant genetic alterations, leading to varying levels of EP introgression in DQTPA and increased population diversity in DQTP_EP (Figs. 1c and 2a). DQTP with a lower proportion of EP ancestral components (< 10%) exhibited the lowest genetic diversity and linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay compared to other breeds. This may indicate that the population size of this highly pure breed has diminished in recent decades, as an increasing number of pigs have begun to hybridize with European pigs. Although we detected redundant EP introgression in the DQTP_EP population, we infer that this introgression has not undergone strong selection, as very few bins have been identified under positive selection, as illustrated in Fig. 2d. Furthermore, we compared the EP composition in DQTP_EP with that of TTP_EP, revealing notable differences between these two groups. For DQTP_EP, we discovered the EP introgression primarily originated from the Duroc breed (Fig. 3d and e), which is generally used as a terminal sire in the modern pig industry [41].

The redundant EP introgression may have a genetic impact on phenotypic changes in Tibetan pigs. To investigate this potential influence, we collected significant eQTL SNP sites from pigGTEX database (p-value < 1e-5) and conducted an enrichment analysis. Many of these sites exhibited low differences between DQTP_EP and TTP_EP, such as in muscle and adipose tissues, which reflected the overall impact of EP introgression on both DQTP_EP and TTP_EP (Fig. 3f). Several genes related to muscle growth and adipose deposition have been identified in both DQTP_EP and TTP_EP. For instance, the muscle expression of NDUFB6 is associated with the degree of DNA methylation, which correlates with insulin sensitivity [42]; COl24A1 has been identified as one of the target gene for regulating myoblast differentiation [43]; RIGI deficiency has been shown to promote obesity and insulin resistance induced by a high-fat diet [44]; ACO1 activity is required for the normal adipogenic capacity of adipose tissue by connecting iron, energy metabolism, and adipogenesis [45]. In addition to the general enrichment observed with fewer differences between TTP_EP and DQTP_EP, we identified several specific enrichments with significant differences (more than 2-fold difference in enrichment) (Fig. 3f). Many of these enrichments originated from DUR introgression and were predominantly found in embryonic and reproductive tissues in DQTP_EP. We discovered 760 genes overlapped with the introgressed regions enriched in blastocysts, fetal thymus, oocytes, ovaries, and morulae. We then performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. The terms related to cellular component organization and metabolic processes were the most significantly enriched, including lipid biosynthetic process (p-value = 0.007), cellular organohalogen metabolic process (p-value = 0.001), and glycerol transport (p-value = 0.001) (Table S6). These results suggest that EP introgression may influence cellular activities associated with reproduction and embryonic development, potentially leading to variations in fertility traits in DQTP_EP. The genetic impact of molecular expression changes and phenotypic variation in DQTP_EP requires further investigation. Additionally, the originality of the population structure could be significantly altered by EP introgression. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the proportions of SCDP ancestral genetic components in DQTP were substantially higher than those in the DQTP_EP group. Notably, the most significant representation was observed near the gene region of PCSK2 (17:25,395,847 − 25,490,580 bp), where the frequency of the SCDP-derived haplotype was markedly reduced when comparing DQTP_EP to DQTP, due to the redundant EP introgression in the DQTP_EP group (Fig. 4g and h). Previous studies have shown that PCSK2 expression is correlated with body weight and adiposity in mice [46–48], suggesting that EP introgression may lead to greater variations in body size within the DQTP_EP population. However, within the Diqing Tibetan breed, the genetic influences of EP introgression variation, as well as the corresponding molecular mechanisms, require further exploration in larger populations and through experimental validation.

Population admixture between Diqing Tibetan pigs and Southern Chinese breeds

In the context of SCDP admixture in DQTP, we propose that the admixture event may have occurred more than one time. Unlike DQTP, the DNSM pig used in this study is a local indigenous breed from the Southern Yunnan province of China, characterized by a subtropical climate that is relatively mild and wet at lower altitudes [1]. The migration of pig populations from South to Southwestern China appears to have been gradual, as we observed that the DNSM breed exhibits a close genetic relationship with SCDP and a high proportion of genetic components derived from both SWCDP and SCDP groups (Fig. 1). Consequently, the DNSM may represent a transitional breed between other SCDP groups and Tibetan breeds. Furthermore, we noted that the global introgression signals were stronger in DNSM than in any other SCDP breeds (Table S5). The introgression of SCDP in DQTP was therefore more likely attributed to the DNSM breed, which exhibits a higher genetic similarity compared to LC (Fig. 4i and j). However, some genetic compartments from LC were still identified. The DNSM introgression exhibited greater genetic diversity than that of the LC introgression (Fig. S5), suggesting that the hybridization event between DQTP and DNSM may be more recent than that between DQTP and LC. We further used ALDER software to infer the admixture time, and the results showed the estimated times of introgression for DNSM and LC were approximately 1000 (226.12 ± 48.87 generations) and 2000 (415.44 ± 40.50 generations) years ago, respectively. It is important to note that ALDER may underestimate the timing of ancient admixture events, particularly concerning the LC introgression event. Nevertheless, the relative comparison remains valid, allowing us to reasonably infer that the introgression from LC predates that from DNSM. This migration likely facilitated the introduction of pigs adapted to subtropical regions into the plateau area, leading to hybridization with local indigenous pigs and resulting in the significant SCDP introgression observed in the modern Diqing Tibetan pig population. Notably, the DNSM breed is among the representative breeds classified within the SCDP group; therefore, it is plausible that other DNSM-like populations in East Asia contributed to the transmission of SCDP introgression into DQTP. Regarding the introgression from LC into DQTP, the population diversity within introgressed region from LC were lower than that from DNSM (Fig. S5), leading us to infer that these introgression events may have occurred significantly earlier in the history of the hybrid than the DNSM introgression. Consequently, we hypothesize that this may represent a more ancient introgression event, potentially involving other pig breeds from Southeast Asia at an earlier time [49], either before or after the breed divergence with DQTP. Future research should focus on the origin and domestication of more diverse pig breeds in Yunnan Province of China, as well as ancient breeds in Southeast Asia, to enhance our understanding of these gradual genetic introgressions.

Conclusions

The results of this study lead to several conclusions: (1) we demonstrate that the Diqing Tibetan breed shares a close genetic relationship with pigs found in Southwest China; (2) we provide genetic evidence of introgression from European pigs into the Diqing Tibetan breed, with the primary contribution originating from the Duroc breed, which may be related to fertility; (3) we reveal that there is more redundant admixture from Southern Chinese pigs in Diqing Tibetan pig population compared to other Tibetan breeds, characterized by a gradual introgression mode. Collectively, our study offers genetic evidence for various instances of European introgression that significantly contribute to population diversity, alongside a more pronounced introgression from Southern Chinese pigs with potentially different sources in the Diqing Tibetan breeds.
