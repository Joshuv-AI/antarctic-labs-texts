Causal effects of ferroptosis-related traits on ovarian dysfunction: insights from integrating genome-wide Mendelian randomization, DNA methylation, gene expression, and proteome
Authors: Qun Zhou, Bangli Song, Huifang Li, Yibo He, Hualiang Zhang, Jing Guo, Minjuan Wu, Wenjun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis has been implicated in the development of ovarian dysfunction; however, the underlying causal genetic mechanisms remain largely unclear. In this study, a multi-omics Mendelian randomization (MR) approach was applied to systematically investigate the causal effects of ferroptosis-related molecular traits on ovarian dysfunction.MethodsSummary-level data for blood-based methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs), expression QTLs (eQTLs), and protein QTLs (pQTLs) were obtained from large-scale consortia. Genetic associations with ovarian dysfunction were sourced from the FinnGen consortium (discovery) and the GCST90079756 dataset (replication). A two-sample Summary-data-based MR (SMR) analysis, incorporating the HEIDI test (P-HEIDI > 0.01) to filter for pleiotropy, was employed to assess causal relationships. Colocalization analysis (PP.H4 > 0.50) was performed to identify shared causal variants. Key findings were validated using tissue-specific eQTLs from GTEx_Ovary. Subsequently, all significant associations were used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and explore enriched biological pathways.ResultsA total of 1175 ferroptosis-related genes were analyzed. 75 methylation sites, 3 gene expressions, and 4 proteins were identified with ovarian dysfunction through SMR and colocalization analyses. Notably, BMP4 was identified in the mQTL and eQTL analyses. Specifically, the methylation at CpG site cg05923197 within BMP4 was associated with higher BMP4 expression (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.21,1.39); this aligns with the finding that cg05923197 methylation increases the risk of ovarian dysfunction (OR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.03,1.50). Additionally, tissue-specific validation indicated a positive correlation between HSD17B13 expression in ovarian tissue and ovarian dysfunction risk (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.04-1.38). Integration of all candidate genes into a protein-protein interaction network further underscored the central roles of BMP4 and HSD17B13 and revealed enrichment in key biological pathways, including HIF-1 signaling and apoptosis.ConclusionThis multi-omics MR study provides evidence for potential causal associations between ferroptosis-related genes and ovarian dysfunction, with BMP4 and HSD17B13 highlighted as key candidates. These findings improve understanding of its pathogenesis and may guide future therapeutic research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01875-0.Keywords: Ferroptosis, Ovarian dysfunction, Multi-omics, Mendelian Randomization analysis, Co-localization

Introduction

Ovarian dysfunction is a condition that significantly impacts women's reproductive health, presenting with a range of clinical features such as infertility, menstrual irregularities, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), ovarian fibrosis, and even neurocognitive issues [1]. It poses not only a risk to reproductive capabilities but is also linked to several long-term health concerns, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and osteoporosis [2]. Moreover, these disorders can lead to psychosocial challenges that profoundly affect patients' quality of life. [3]. Therefore, it is essential to gain a comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of ovarian dysfunction and to develop effective treatment strategies to enhance women's health.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and plasma membrane rupture [4]. Iron is an essential mineral that influences various physiological functions in women and is involved in the development of ovarian tumors, presenting both risks and therapeutic possibilities in ovarian diseases [5]. Studies have shown that ferroptosis in ovarian granulosa cells (GCs) plays a role in ovarian dysfunction and the advancement of PCOS [6]. Notably, the pharmacological modulation of ferroptosis shows considerable promise for treating benign ovarian conditions [7]. Further studies highlight that ferroptosis is crucial to the onset of PCOS, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [8]. The application of ferroptosis inhibitors, such as Fer-1, has demonstrated the ability to alleviate the pathological features of PCOS, leading to enhancements in the morphology of polycystic ovaries and resolving ovulatory issues [9, 10]. Additionally, NF-κB is linked to PCOS, and its inhibition can trigger ferroptosis in GCs [11]. Furthermore, research utilizing both animal and cellular models of PCOS has revealed that silencing METTL3 can reduce ovarian fibrosis by increasing the m6A modification of GPX4, thereby suppressing genes related to ferroptosis in the context of PCOS and ultimately reducing ovarian fibrosis [12].

Establishing causal links between molecular traits of ferroptosis-related genes and ovarian dysfunction is complicated by the confounding factors and reverse causation that often arise in observational studies. To address these challenges, Mendelian Randomization (MR) employs genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causality, effectively reducing these limitations [13]. For this study, Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) integrates summary-level data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of ovarian dysfunction with data from molecular quantitative trait loci (QTL) studies (e.g., eQTLs, mQTLs, pQTLs) of ferroptosis-related genes[14]. This approach enables us to investigate whether a genetic variant affects ovarian dysfunction by influencing specific molecular traits, including gene expression, DNA methylation, or protein levels [15]. Consequently, SMR offers a robust framework for identifying specific molecular pathways related to ferroptosis that are causally linked to ovarian dysfunction, providing valuable mechanistic insights.

In this study, we apply SMR to integrate multi-omics datasets, encompassing gene expression, DNA methylation, and protein expression, with the goal of identifying genetic variants linked to ovarian dysfunction. Our objective is to elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms of ferroptosis-related genes in the context of ovarian dysfunction, aiming to uncover new molecular pathways. By accurately identifying these genetic markers, we hope to facilitate advancements in disease prediction, prevention, and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a multi-omics MR approach. We first curated a list of ferroptosis-related genes. Then, we obtained summary-level data for blood methylation QTL (mQTLs), expression QTLs (eQTLs), and protein QTLs (pQTLs) from large-scale consortia, and genetic associations with ovarian dysfunction from the FinnGen consortium (discovery) and the GCST90079756 dataset (replication). A two-sample SMR analysis, colocalization analysis, and integration of mQTL-eQTL data were performed. The overall design is depicted in Fig. 1. All studies included received approval from their respective institutional review boards. This study adheres to the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines and employs MR analysis to elucidate potential causal relationships between genes and diseases.

Data resources

Exposure sources

Blood eQTL data for ferroptosis-related genes were sourced from eQTLGen, including genetic expression information from 31,684 individuals [16]. Blood mQTL summary data were derived from a meta-analysis of two European cohorts, namely the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study (n = 614) and the Lothian Birth Cohorts (n = 1,366) [17]. Blood pQTL summary data were obtained from Sun et al., encompassing 54,219 UK Biobank participants [18]. Subsequently, we assessed tissue-specific expression of target genes using tissue-specific eQTL data retrieved from the GTEx database, exploring the potential causal impact of ferroptosis genes on ovarian dysfunction. The GTEx v8 dataset includes data from 838 donors and 17,382 samples across 52 tissues and two cell lines. For the analysis of ovarian dysfunction, we utilized eQTL data from the GTEx Ovary dataset.

Outcome sources

We extracted ferroptosis-related genes from the FerrDb V2 database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/), which includes 564 genes associated with ferroptosis. Since iron metabolism regulates ferroptosis, we supplemented this list with genes related to iron metabolism. Using the keyword “iron metabolism”, we downloaded iron metabolism-related genes from the GeneCards database. We identified 712 “Protein Coding” genes related to iron metabolism. Combining the ferroptosis and iron metabolism genes from these two databases, we defined a set of ferroptosis-related genes, totaling 1,175 genes (Table S1). The genome-wide summary statistics for ovarian dysfunction were obtained from the FINNGEN R10 database, which includes data from 942 cases and 118,228 controls, covering a comprehensive assessment of 16,379,685 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [19]. Validation was performed using the GCST90079756 study dataset, which includes 500 cases and 210,556 controls, with a total of 52,375,285 SNPs [20]. Ovarian dysfunction in this study is defined according to ICD-10 code E28, encompassing E28.0 (estrogen excess), E28.1 (androgen excess), E28.2 (polycystic ovarian syndrome), E28.3 (primary ovarian failure), E28.31 (premature menopause), E28.310 (symptomatic premature menopause), E28.319 (asymptomatic premature menopause), E28.39 (other primary ovarian failure), E28.8 (other ovarian dysfunction) and E28.9 (ovarian dysfunction, unspecified). The detailed information for all datasets used in this study was summarized in Table S2.

Statistical analyses

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis

SMR and HEIDI tests were performed using the SMR software tool (SMR v1.3.1). We utilized SMR to evaluate the association between the methylation, expression, and protein levels of ferroptosis-related genes and ovarian dysfunction. In scenarios where both exposure and outcome data are sourced from two sizable, independent population cohorts, the application of SMR leveraging the most significantly associated cis-QTLs yields enhanced statistical power relative to conventional Mendelian randomization approaches. For selecting top-associated cis-QTLs, SNPs within a ± 1000 kb window centered around the corresponding gene and with a P-value of 5.0 × 10−8 were analyzed [21]. SNPs with allele frequency differences greater than 0.2 between any two datasets (including LD reference samples, QTL summary data, and outcome summary data) were excluded. The maximum allowed proportion of SNPs with allele frequency differences was set to the default value of 0.05 (i.e., corresponding parameter –diff-freq-prop 0.05).

Building on the exploration of causal relationships between QTLs (mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL) and ovarian dysfunction, we used mQTLs as the exposure and eQTLs as the outcome to further investigate the causal associations between mQTLs and eQTLs. Key findings from the associations of mQTLs and eQTLs with ovarian dysfunction were considered significant signals between mQTLs and eQTLs. Similarly, we used eQTLs as the exposure and pQTLs as the outcome to explore causal relationships between eQTLs and pQTLs. Based on SMR analysis, we developed an advanced multi-SNP SMR analysis method (SMR-multi) that accounts for multiple SNPs at QTL loci [22]. This study focuses on the results obtained using this method. Subsequently, HEIDI testing was applied, with P-HEIDI > 0.01 used to filter out results with potential pleiotropy [23].

Co-localization analysis

Co-localization analysis was performed using the R package ‘coloc’ to identify shared causal variants between ferroptosis-related cis-QTLs (including eQTLs, mQTLs, and pQTLs) and ovarian dysfunction. Specifically, when GWAS signals and QTLs were detected as colocalized, it was inferred that the loci on the GWAS signal might affect the phenotype by altering gene-related biological processes. The co-localization analysis reported five different posterior probabilities corresponding to five mutually exclusive hypotheses: H0, No trait is genetically associated with the SNP in the region; H1, Only trait 1 is genetically associated with the SNP; H2, Only trait 2 is genetically associated with the SNP; H3, Both traits are associated with the SNP but use different causal variants; H4, Both traits are associated with the SNP and share a common causal variant.

For the co-localization analysis of mQTL-GWAS, eQTL-GWAS, and pQTL-GWAS, a co-localization region window of ± 1000 kb was used. To allow weaker p-value QTLs to colocalize with the signal, co-localization success was defined by a PP.H4 > 0.5 under the condition of p12 = 5 × 10−5 [24]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.0). The R package ‘ggplot’ was used for Manhattan plots, and ‘forestplot’ was used for forest plots. ‘SMRLocusPlot’ and ‘SMREffectPlot’ code was sourced from Zhu et al. [22].

PPI network and enrichment analysis

The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was employed to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network to elucidate the potential interactions among targets [25]. More specifically, the signals associated with ovary dysfunction were retrieved and the interactions among these targets were explored, with confidence interaction score over 0.7. Subsequently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was utilized to investigate the pathway in which these genes were involved. The package "Clusterprofiler" was used for KEGG analysis and visualization.

Results

SMR analysis of Cis-eQTL and ovarian dysfunction

The causal relationship outcomes between the expression of ferroptosis-related genes and ovarian dysfunction are summarized in Table S3, whereas the whole analytical contents derived from the SMR approach are delineated in Table S4. A total of 15 genes were found to be associated with ovarian dysfunction (employing criteria of p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_SMR < 0.05 in conjunction with P-HEIDI > 0.01) (Fig. 2A). Notably, elevated expression levels of seven genes—PAQR3, BMP4, SRSF9, UQCR10, SLC1A4, AHR, and HSD17B13—were positively correlated with an increased risk of ovarian dysfunction. Conversely, heightened expression of the remaining genes was inversely related to the risk of ovarian dysfunction. The co-localization analysis revealed that within the co-localization windows of SNPs associated with three genes, there are loci associated with ovarian dysfunction (PPH4 > 0.50).

SMR analysis of Cis-mQTL and ovarian dysfunction

Applying the criteria of p_SMR_multi < 0.05 in conjunction with p_SMR < 0.05, and P-HEIDI > 0.01, we identified 142 CpG sites (corresponding to 77 genes). Among these identified signals, 75 CpG sites (associated with 43 genes) were supported by strong co-localization evidence (PPH4 > 0.50), which is illustrated in Figure S1 for the CpG sites with robust co-localization support. Notably, among these mQTL-identified genes, BMP4 and CNR2 also demonstrated significant associations at the gene expression level (via independent eQTL analysis) with ovarian dysfunction, suggesting a multi-omics convergence for their potential roles. Specifically, at the mQTL level, methylation at BMP4 sites cg12128696 and cg05923197, and at CNR2 site cg10978503, was positively correlated with an increased risk of ovarian dysfunction (Fig. 2B). The full contents of the analyses derived from the SMR approach are provided in the supplementary Table S7.

The SMR analysis yielded findings for 13 CpG sites corresponding to nine genes—PARK7, NR5A2, PDIA6, SURF1, PTHLH, BMP4, CPEB1, MIR130B, and TP53—which were validated in the GCST90079756 cohort (using criteria p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_SMR < 0.05 in conjunction with P-HEIDI > 0.01) (Table S8). The entirety of the analysis content obtained from the SMR analysis in the GCST90079756 cohort is provided in Table S9.

MR analysis of Cis-pQTL and ovarian dysfunction

The causal relationship outcomes between ferroptosis-related proteins and ovarian dysfunction are detailed in Table S10, with the complete analytical contents from the SMR approach provided in the supplementary Table S11. Applying the screening criteria of p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_SMR < 0.05 alongside P-HEIDI > 0.01, a total of six genes corresponding to proteins were found to be associated with ovarian dysfunction (Fig. 2C). Among these, four proteins exhibited strong co-localization evidence (PPH4 > 0.50).

The results obtained from the SMR analysis did not reach validation in the GCST90079756 cohort when using the criteria of p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_SMR < 0.05 in conjunction with P-HEIDI > 0.01. The comprehensive analytical contents from the SMR analysis conducted in the GCST90079756 cohort are supplied in the supplementary Table S12. The lack of replication for pQTL findings could be attributed to several factors, including potentially lower statistical power of pQTLs as genetic instruments compared to eQTLs or mQTLs, differences in cohort characteristics between the discovery and replication pQTL datasets, or the inherent complexity and post-transcriptional regulation influencing protein abundance.

Integration of multi-omics evidence

Critical findings based on mQTL and eQTL associated with ovarian dysfunction and ferroptosis revealed two intersecting genes (CNR2 and BMP4), corresponding to three methylation sites. Through the integration of mQTL and eQTL data using SMR analysis (p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_SMR < 0.05 along with P-HEIDI > 0.01), we further investigated the potential regulation of key ferroptosis gene expression in ovarian dysfunction by blood methylation. The possible regulatory relationships between mQTLs and eQTLs associated with ovarian dysfunction are outlined in Table 1. The results of the analysis identified that one methylation site (cg05923197) associated with one gene (BMP4) emerged as potentially critical in ovarian dysfunction. The full analytical contents are provided in the Table S13. However, no significant associations were found when integrating eQTLs and pQTLs data. In the mQTL level, the site cg05923197, located within the functional region of BMP4, showed a positive correlation with the risk of ovarian dysfunction (OR = 1.242, 95% CI = 1.03,1.50; PSMR = 0.02, PSMR-multi = 4.81 × 10−2). The Manhattan Plot for cg05923197 within the context of mQTL analysis is presented in Fig. 3A. Figure S2A illustrates the SMR Locus Plot for the eQTL of BMP4 in relation to the GWAS for ovarian dysfunction. Similarly, in eQTL data, the expression of the BMP4 gene was positively associated with the risk of ovarian dysfunction (OR = 2.31, 95% CI = 1.12–4.75; PSMR = 2.32 × 10−2, PSMR-multi = 1.17 × 10−2). The Manhattan Plot for BMP4 within the eQTL analysis is depicted in Fig. 3B. Figure S2B presents the SMR Effect Plot for the eQTL of BMP4 in association with the GWAS for ovarian dysfunction. In the integrating mQTL with eQTL data, the methylation status at site cg05923197 was positively correlated with the expression of the BMP4 gene (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.21,1.39; PSMR = 1.98 × 10−12, PSMR-multi = 9.96 × 10−13). Consequently, a hypothesized mechanism may exist whereby elevated methylation levels at cg05923197 facilitate the upregulation of BMP4 gene expression, thereby contributing to an increased risk of ovarian dysfunction. It is noteworthy that while methylation in promoter regions is often associated with gene silencing, the observed positive correlation between cg05923197 methylation and BMP4 expression suggests this CpG site might be located within a gene body, enhancer, or other regulatory element where methylation can have activating effects, or that the association is indirect. Further genomic annotation of cg05923197 and functional studies would be needed to elucidate this specific regulatory mechanism. Figure S3A illustrates the genetic concordance between ovarian dysfunction GWAS and mQTL near cg05923197 through a trajectory zoom plot. Figure S3B presents the SMR Effect Plot depicting the relationship between the mQTL of cg05923197 and ovarian dysfunction GWAS.

Tissue-specific validation

We further investigated the causal relationship between the expression of the aforementioned pivotal genes in tissues and the risk of ovarian dysfunction. Utilizing GTEx ovary tissue ferroptosis-related eQTLs in conjunction with GWAS data for ovarian dysfunction, we performed an SMR analysis to corroborate the findings from the integrated blood cis-QTL analysis. Results were filtered using the criteria of p SMR < 0.05 and p SMR multi < 0.05 and p HEIDI > 0.01.

It was discovered that the positive correlation between the expression of HSD17B13 and the risk of ovarian dysfunction was substantiated in GTEx_Ovary tissue (OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 1.01,1.29; PSMR = 3.72 × 10−2, PSMR-multi = 3.72 × 10−2, Table S14). The entirety of the results from the SMR analysis conducted on the GTEx_Ovary tissue type is provided in Table S15.

Molecular network construction

As mentioned above, a total of 96 genes associated with ovary dysfunction were identified based on the m/e/pQTLs results (p_SMR < 0.05 and p_SMR_multi < 0.05 and p_HEIDI > 0.01). After merging and removing duplicates, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.10.0), focusing on the top 15 significantly enriched pathways with corrected p-values < 0.05 that are not disease pathways for subsequent network construction. The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database, suggesting physical or functional associations within the context of disease, with a total of 82 genes located within this interaction network (Fig. 4). Discussion

By integrating multi-omics data, including eQTL, mQTL, and pQTL, we identified 75 methylation sites, 3 gene expression, and 4 proteins associated with ovarian dysfunction. Additionally, our analysis revealed that BMP4 is a crucial gene linked to ovarian dysfunction. Tissue validation further confirmed HSD17B13 as another significant gene involved in this condition, and this was also supported by our eQTL analysis. In conclusion, our findings suggest that both BMP4 and HSD17B13 may play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of ovarian dysfunction.

BMP4 showed a positive association with ovarian dysfunction, both at the level of DNA methylation and gene expression. This suggests that the increased methylation at the cg05923197 locus (located within the BMP4 gene body) may lead to upregulation of BMP4, thereby contributing to ovarian dysfunction. Although methylation in the promoter region usually suppresses gene expression, studies have shown that methylation in the gene body or enhancer regions can sometimes promote transcription [26]. Nonetheless, the specific regulatory mechanism of cg05923197 on BMP4 still needs further experimental verification. In addition, we demonstrated that the elevated expression levels of HSD17B13 were positively associated with the risk of ovarian dysfunction. Collectively, these results suggest the distinct roles of these genes in the pathogenesis of ovarian dysfunction and provide insights for identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Previous studies have highlighted the diverse roles of BMP4 in ovarian physiology and pathology, particularly concerning granulosa cell function and steroidogenesis [27]. The BMP family is believed to play a crucial role in regulating follicular development, indicating that disruptions in BMP signaling pathways could lead to the onset of POI. Specifically, researchers have explored the potential involvement of the BMP-15 gene in POI [28], and BMP4 has been shown to promote the transition of primordial follicles to primary follicles in mouse ovary cultures [29]. Additionally, BMP4 and BMP7 have been found to inhibit both basal and luteinizing hormone-induced androgen production in primary cultures of bovine theca interna cells, suggesting a possible autocrine or paracrine mechanism within the follicle that modulates theca cell function [30]. Furthermore, the BMP system, including BMP4, has been shown to be overexpressed in granulosa cells from women with PCOS, where BMP4 inhibits FSH-induced estradiol production [31]. In granulosa cells, BMP4 has been shown to down-regulate connexin43 expression and decrease gap junction intercellular communication activity, which impacts follicle and oocyte development [32]. More recently, BMP4 has been implicated in regulating glucose metabolism and autophagy in granulosa cells under hyperandrogenic conditions, playing a protective role in ensuring subsequent energy utilization and mitochondrial function in PCOS [33]. In addition to these established roles, emerging evidence suggest a potential link between BMP4 signaling and ferroptosis [34]. More specifically, while direct evidence for BMP4 regulation of GPX4 in ovarian tissue is currently lacking, a study in a nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) model has demonstrated that BMP4 interacts with GPX4 and upregulates its expression, thereby inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis [34]. Given the ubiquity of ferroptosis across various tissues and disease states, it is plausible to hypothesize that a comparable regulatory network may also exist in ovarian tissues. Therefore BMP4 may influence ovarian cell ferroptosis sensitivity through the modulation of GPX4 expression, thereby contributing to the pathophysiology of ovarian dysfunction. However, this hypothesis warrants direct validation through further functional experiments. Additionally although direct studies on BMP4 and ferroptosis in ovarian cells remain limited, its involvement in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress regulation implies that it may influence ferroptotic pathways. Dysregulation of BMP4 could therefore contribute to ovarian dysfunction via modulating redox homeostasis and ferroptosis. These findings suggest that dysregulation of BMP4 signaling may contribute to ovarian disorders such as PCOS. Our results further support this notion, as they demonstrate a positive correlation between BMP4 expression and the risk of ovarian dysfunction. This suggests that BMP4 may be significant in both ovarian function and dysfunction.

Although there is limited literature directly linking HSD17B13 to ovarian dysfunction, previous studies have highlighted its significant role in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and indicated that the expression level of HSD17B13 was associated with an increased risk of ovarian dysfunction [35]. This suggests that HSD17B13 may have a potential role in the occurrence and development of ovarian dysfunction. Given the central role of lipid peroxidation in ferroptosis and the established involvement of HSD17B13 in lipid metabolic pathways [36], it is plausible that aberrant expression of HSD17B13 could modulate the susceptibility of ovarian cells to ferroptosis by altering the intracellular lipid environment. In this study, we found a positive association between the expression of HSD17B13 and the risk of ovarian dysfunction. These results suggest that HSD17B13 may play an important role in the pathogenesis of ovarian dysfunction. In addition, an analysis of ferroptosis-associated eQTLs in GTEx ovarian tissue reinforced the positive association between heightened HSD17B13 expression and the risk of ovarian dysfunction. Based on these findings, future studies could explore how HSD17B13 specifically affects ferroptosis in ovarian tissue and further investigate its exact role in the development of ovarian dysfunction. In conclusion, while the exact mechanisms linking HSD17B13 and ovarian dysfunction are yet to be fully understood, this study offers preliminary evidence supporting its potential involvement and outlines avenues for further investigation.

The present study employed a SMR approach to elucidate the causal relationship between ferroptosis-related genes and ovarian dysfunction. By integrating multi-omics data, including eQTL, mQTL, and pQTL, our analysis offers a thorough understanding of the genetic factors contributing to ovarian dysfunction. The SMR method presents several benefits compared to traditional Mendelian randomization techniques. Notably, it utilizes the most significantly associated cis-QTLs from large, independent population cohorts, which enhances statistical power and minimizes the risk of false positives.. Furthermore, the multi-SNP SMR analysis method accounts for multiple SNPs at QTL loci, allowing for more robust causal inference. This integrated approach not only strengthens the evidence for the causal relationships but also provides a framework for understanding the complex interplay between genetic variation and ovarian dysfunction.

The correlation between our study and the existing literature, particularly the work by Ni et al. [4], highlights both similarities and unique contributions to the field of ovarian dysfunction research. Similarly, our study employs a multi-omics Mendelian randomization approach to explore causal relationships between molecular traits and ovarian dysfunction. However, our study diverges from previous work by identifying novel candidate genes such as BMP4 and HSD17B13, which were not previously highlighted in the context of ovarian dysfunction. The comprehensive integration of DNA methylation, gene expression, and protein abundance data in our study provides a more holistic view of the molecular mechanisms at play. This multi-faceted approach not only validates the effectiveness of using omics data to uncover disease-related genes but also offers novel insights that could pave the way for new therapeutic strategies. Thus, while our study aligns well with the existing literature, it also significantly contributes to the field by proposing new candidate genes and a more integrative methodological framework.

Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. First, one key limitation is the reliance on summary-level data, which restricts detailed individual-level analyses and may obscure potential confounding factors or interactions. Second, the validation of HSD17B13 in ovarian tissue supports our findings were derived from blood samples due to data availability. Moreover, our findings did not reveal the causal associations between several well-established core ferroptosis-related genes, such as GPX4 and SLC7A11, and PCOS. This may be primarily attributed to tissue-specific differences. Blood-based QTLs may not always accurately reflect the molecular events in the ovarian tissue, and this remains a consideration for interpreting findings not directly validated in ovarian tissue. Thirdly, the use of a single cohort (FINNGEN R10) for both exposure and outcome data may introduce biases due to shared environmental or genetic factors within the European population, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should aim to replicate these findings in diverse populations and utilize additional cohorts to enhance external validity. Lastly, the interpretation of the biological mechanisms inferred from the SMR analysis is limited by the current understanding of the functional significance of the identified genetic variants. Experimental validation in cellular and animal models will be necessary to confirm the mechanistic hypotheses.

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical importance of ferroptosis-related genes, such as BMP4 and HSD17B13, in the development of ovarian dysfunction. The findings provide a basis for future functional studies aimed at clarifying how these genes contribute to this condition. It is also essential to recognize the influence of genetic variability on the outcomes of the research. Ongoing investigations are vital for understanding the intricate biological processes associated with ovarian dysfunction and for bringing up effective prevention and treatment strategies.
