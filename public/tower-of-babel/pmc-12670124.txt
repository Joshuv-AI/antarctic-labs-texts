The NITAG debate in Italy: an opportunity for prevention
Authors: Massimo Agosti
Published: 2025

We read with interest the Comment by Casigliani and colleagues on the appointment and subsequent revocation of members of the National Immunisation Technical Advisory Group (NITAG).1 The Authors highlight the risk that recent events could erode decades of progress in vaccine confidence in Italy. We would like to expand this discussion by addressing additional aspects that may further strengthen this crucial scientific point.

The initial appointment by the Ministry of Health of two vaccine sceptics to the NITAG raised profound concern within the scientific community. Physicians, scientific societies, and institutions—including the national medical council—warned that such “anti-scientific” positions could undermine public trust in vaccination strategies. The subsequent revocation was therefore welcome, but it will only be meaningful if followed by a transparent nomination process involving all relevant stakeholders.

In this context, it is essential to build a new NITAG that also includes clinical professionals who work daily with patients and families on preventive strategies, including vaccination. The Italian Society of Neonatology has pointed out a major gap: the absence of representatives from specialties crucial to prevention, namely neonatologists and obstetricians. This omission is critical, as parents’ decisions regarding vaccination are often made during pregnancy and in the very first days of life. Today, several maternal–fetal vaccines are available, and neonatal immunisation against respiratory syncytial virus has recently entered clinical practice, with further vaccines expected to follow.2

Neonatologists are the first physicians to interact with newborns and families during the sensitive window of the first 1000 days of life. Beyond providing early care, they play a pivotal role in prevention: performing neonatal screening, fostering parental trust, and promoting long-term health. Their contribution is therefore indispensable to any comprehensive national immunisation strategy.

Technical-scientific committees such as the NITAG are essential to translate policy into effective action. To succeed, they must be independent from ideological or political influence and include all key professional figures. Notably, the French NITAG includes a neonatologist and an obstetrician, the Spanish NITAG a neonatologist.3,4 Perinatology is thus more strongly represented in other European countries than in Italy.

This imbalance reflects a deeper structural issue: the absence of full professorships in neonatology in Italian universities, recently highlighted in The Lancet Regional Health—Europe.5 Italian neonatologists call for a stronger strategic alliance in preventive medicine, particularly vaccination. Newborns are the citizens of tomorrow; they embody the future of a country facing a severe demographic winter and a record low birth rate.

We would like to believe that the NITAG controversy could be regarded as an opportunity to reaffirm the primacy of science in public health decision-making and to ensure that neonatologists, together with other key clinicians, are appropriately represented in shaping national immunisation policies.
