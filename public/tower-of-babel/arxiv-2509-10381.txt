Most incompatible measurements and sum-of-squares optimisation
Sébastien Designolle

Abstract
Measurement incompatibility, or joint measurability, is a cornerstone of quantum theory and a useful resource. For finite-dimensional systems, quantifying this resource and establishing universal bounds valid for all measurements is a long-standing problem. In this work, we exhibit analytical universal parent measurements giving access to bounds that beat the state of the art. In particular, we can show that, for relevant robustnesses, sets of anticommuting observables give rise to the most incompatible dichotomic measurements. We also formalise the construction of such universal parent measurements in the framework of sum-of-squares optimisation and obtain preliminary numerical results demonstrating the power of the method by improving on our own analytical values. All results find direct application for demonstrating genuine high-dimensional steering, that is, certifying the dimensionality of a quantum system in a one-sided device-independent manner.



I
Introduction

A defining feature of quantum theory is the existence of phenomena with no classical counterpart.
Among these, measurement incompatibility has emerged as a central concept for understanding nonclassical behaviour
GHK+23
.
Incompatibility captures the impossibility of jointly realising certain quantum measurements, and as such it underlies many foundational quantum effects, including Bell nonlocality
BCP+14
and Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen steering
UCNG20
.
The connection to quantum steering is particularly strong: it is not merely qualitative but quantitative.
Indeed, the degree of incompatibility directly determines the robustness of steering experiments against noise
QVB14
;
UBGP15
;
CS16
.
This insight motivates the systematic search for maximally incompatible sets of measurements
BQG+17
.

Important progress has been made in the case of pairs of measurements.
In
DFK19
, the authors characterised the most incompatible pairs of measurement in a fixed dimension.
Through the relation to steering, these results yield a one-sided device-independent dimension witness, which has even been demonstrated experimentally
DSU+21
.
In a complementary direction,
TFR+21
considered the scenario where the number of outcomes is fixed instead of the dimension, and identified the most incompatible pairs in that setting.
Together, these works provide a rather complete picture for measurement incompatibility when restricted to pairs, albeit from different but complementary perspectives.

Going beyond pairs is a natural and desirable goal.
Larger sets of incompatible measurements can reveal stronger forms of nonclassicality and lead to protocols that are more robust against noise and losses
QWZ+22
;
GCH+23
;
MRR25
.
Yet, results in this direction remain scarce.
Recent work has shown that random measurements are asymptotically maximally incompatible
BLN25
, but such constructions are not experimentally viable.
Other approaches to the finite-dimensional case rely on combining pairwise incompatibility information, which only partially captures the richer structure of larger sets
Des22
.
From a technical perspective, the main obstacle is the construction of a universal parent measurement valid for arbitrary sets, which hinges on proving semidefinite positivity
DW25
.

In this work, we address this challenge by developing a hierarchy based on sum-of-squares certificates that allows for the systematic construction of universal parent measurements.
This framework both unifies and extends the analytical methods of
DSFB19
;
DFK19
;
TFR+21
, while at the same time opening the door to detailed numerical investigations.
Among our analytical results, we prove that sets of dichotomic measurements arising from anticommuting observables are maximally incompatible with respect to many incompatibility measures where this question is well defined.
On the numerical side, our results on a restricted hierarchy serve as a proof of principle for the power of the method, and we expect further research to substantially improve on them.
Overall, our work establishes a pathway to certifying incompatibility in scenarios involving larger collections of measurements, thereby shedding new light on the structure of measurement incompatibility beyond the two-measurement regime.

The structure of this article reflects the way our hierarchy was developed.
We begin with purely analytical constructions of universal parent measurements, expressed as positive polynomials in certain sums of the measurements involved.
These results already improve on the state of the art, and are even tight in the case of anticommuting observables.
The general hierarchy is introduced subsequently, when we observe that positivity can be certified through more general polynomials, which we then investigate numerically.

II
Preliminaries

We start by fixing some notations used in this work:
k
will always be the number of measurements,
d
the dimension of the underlying Hilbert space, and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
the numbers of outcomes.
As for the indices,
x\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
will label the measurements and
a\in\{1,\ldots,n_{c}\}
the outcome, so that a set of measurements will be denoted by
\{\{A_{a|x}\}_{a}\}_{x}
, often abbreviated
\{A_{a|x}\}
.
Denoting
\delta
the Kronecker delta and
{\vec{\jmath}}=j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
, we can state the definition of compatibility, also called joint measurability.
A set
\{A_{a|x}\}
of measurements is jointly measurable if it admits a parent measurement, that is, a measurement
\{G_{\vec{\jmath}}\}_{\vec{\jmath}}
such that, for all
a
and
x
,

\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}G_{\vec{\jmath}}=A_{a|x}.

(1)

From this dichotomic definition, one can further define incompatibility measures by quantifying the robustness of the incompatibility of
\{A_{a|x}\}
when adding various types of noise
DFK19
.
There also exists a distance-based quantifier
TKKB23
for which some methods developed in this work may apply, although we do not address this formally.
The incompatibility measures we consider can be cast as semidefinite programming (SDP) instances
(
DFK19
, Appendix E.1)
; here we recall some of these definitions.

\displaystyle\text{Depolarising incompatibility robustness:}\quad\eta^{\mathrm{d}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\eta,\{G_{\vec{\jmath}}\}_{\vec{\jmath}}}&\eta\\
\text{s.t.}&\eta\leqslant 1,\qquad G_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant 0,\quad\forall{\vec{\jmath}}\vphantom{\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}},\\
&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}G_{\vec{\jmath}}=\eta A_{a|x}+(1-\eta)\Tr A_{a|x}\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\quad\forall a,x.\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\text{Random incompatibility robustness:}\quad\eta^{\mathrm{r}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\eta,\{G_{\vec{\jmath}}\}_{\vec{\jmath}}}&\eta\\
\text{s.t.}&\eta\leqslant 1,\qquad G_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant 0,\quad\forall{\vec{\jmath}}\vphantom{\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}},\\
&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}G_{\vec{\jmath}}=\eta A_{a|x}+(1-\eta)\frac{\mathds{1}}{n_{x}}\quad\forall a,x.\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\text{Generalised incompatibility robustness:}\quad\eta^{\mathrm{g}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\eta,\{G_{\vec{\jmath}}\}_{\vec{\jmath}}}&\eta\\
\text{s.t.}&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}G_{\vec{\jmath}}=\mathds{1},\qquad G_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant 0\quad\forall{\vec{\jmath}},\\
&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}G_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant\eta A_{a|x}\quad\forall a,x.\\
\end{array}\right.

We also recall some inequalities between these measures
(
DFK19
, Appendix B)
:

\eta^{\mathrm{d}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}+\frac{1-\eta^{\mathrm{d}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}}{d}\leqslant\eta^{\mathrm{g}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{\mathrm{r}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}+\frac{1-\eta^{\mathrm{r}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}}{\max_{x}n_{x}}\leqslant\eta^{\mathrm{g}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}.

(11)

They simply follow from the computation of the objective function for
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
using the optimal solution of
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
or
\eta^{\mathrm{r}}
.

With these definitions, the question of the most incompatible sets of measurements with respect to one of these measures (denoted * below) can be formally stated
(
DFK19
, Section II.D)
.
Given the dimension and numbers of outcomes, this question amounts to solving the optimisation

\chi^{\ast}(d;n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\min_{\{A_{a|x}\}}\eta^{\ast}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}

(12)

over corresponding sets
\{A_{a|x}\}
of measurements.
We also define quantities that only fix either the dimension or the number of outcomes:

H^{\ast}_{k}(d)=\inf_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\chi^{\ast}(d;n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\chi^{\ast}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\inf_{d}\chi^{\ast}(d;n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}).

(13)

Note that this is a slight change of notation compared to
Des22
: the quantity
H^{\mathrm{g}}_{k,d}
defined there is denoted
H^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(d)
here.
Finally, for convenience, we write
\chi^{\ast}_{k}(n)=\chi^{\ast}(n,\ldots,n)
when all
k
numbers of outcomes are identical.

III
Degree two

In this section, we illustrate the main method used in this work: exhibiting universal parent measurements that give lower bounds on some incompatibility robustness that are valid for all sets of measurements, hence for the worst case as well.
This technique was introduced in
DFK19
and further exploited in
TFR+21
;
Des22
.
Note that, even though the universal parent measurements shown here are
only
of degree two, the results already improve on the state of the art and are even sufficient to obtain tight bounds in some cases.

III.1
Random incompatibility robustness

Exhibiting a universal parent measurement as described above is a difficult task, and a first step has to be taken to somehow restrict the class of measurements considered without loss of generality.
For
\eta^{\mathrm{r}}
(as well as
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
), we can exploit the
pre-processing monotonicity
of the incompatibility measure to study only
projective
measurements, up to a Naimark dilation that would not improve the robustness
DFK19
.
The results obtained this way are naturally agnostic of the dimension of the system.

Hence, let us consider
k
projective measurements with
n
outcomes, denoted
\{P_{j_{1}|1}\}_{j_{1}=1}^{n},\ldots,\{P_{j_{k}|k}\}_{j_{k}=1}^{n}
.
We define

S_{\vec{\jmath}}=\sum_{x=1}^{k}P_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{k,n}=\frac{k}{n}\left(1-\frac{2(n-1)}{n-2+\sqrt{n^{2}+4(k-1)(n-1)}}\right)\quad\text{to construct}\quad G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}\propto\left(S_{\vec{\jmath}}-\alpha_{k,n}\mathds{1}\right)^{2},

(14)

where the proportionality factor in the definition of
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}
is fixed by normalisation, i.e.,
\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}=\mathds{1}
.
Note that the positivity of
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}
is trivial as the polynomial
(X-\alpha_{k,n})^{2}
is a square.
Our goal is to find the noise associated with this
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}
, that is, the value of
\eta
such that we have a feasible point in
Section

II
.
This requires finding the marginals of
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}
and its normalisation factor, a task that boils down to computing those of the successive powers of
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
as
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}\propto S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}-2\alpha_{k,n}S_{\vec{\jmath}}+\alpha_{k,n}^{2}\mathds{1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=n^{k}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=n^{k-1}\mathds{1},

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}

\displaystyle=kn^{k-1}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}

\displaystyle=n^{k-1}P_{a|x}+(k-1)n^{k-2}\mathds{1},

(16)

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}

\displaystyle=k(n+k-1)n^{k-2}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(n+2(k-1)\big)n^{k-2}P_{a|x}+(k-1)(n+k-2)n^{k-3}\mathds{1}.

(17)

With these marginals, we can compute the desired value of
\eta
and conclude:

\eta=\frac{n-2+\sqrt{n^{2}+4(k-1)(n-1)}}{2k(n-1)}\leqslant\chi^{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(n).

(18)

Note that the value of
\alpha_{k,n}
chosen initially is precisely picked to maximise the resulting value of
\eta
.
For
k=2
, this result surpasses the best known bound for
\eta^{\mathrm{r}}
, namely,
\frac{1}{2}[1+1/(n+1)]

(
DFK19
, Eq. (35))
(setting
n_{A}=n_{B}=n
).
One could in principle use the same technique to make this bound dependent on different numbers of outcomes.

III.2
Application to anticommuting observables

Here we identify the most incompatible sets of dichotomic measurements with respect to
\eta^{\mathrm{r}}
and
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
by showing that

\chi^{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\quad\text{and}\quad\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(2)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right).

(19)

Note that the case
k=2
was already known, see
(
TFR+21
, Appendix B.4)
.
To the best of our knowledge, such results are new, although they were to be expected as similar observations have been obtained in the context of entropic uncertainty relations
WW08
.
They may give rise to a semi-device independent certification of the number of dichotomic measurements as studied in
VPLU24
.

On the one hand,
\chi^{\mathrm{r}}_{k}(2)\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
directly comes from
Eq.

18
when setting
n=2
, and this can be extended to
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
using
Eq.

11
.
On the other hand, anticommuting observables allow to reach these bounds; such observables being known to exist from dimension
2^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}
on
Sha00
.
This tightness is a direct application of the upper bounds from
(
DFK19
, Eq. (E6))
, which read:

\eta^{\mathrm{r}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}\leqslant\frac{\lambda-\sum_{x}\frac{1}{n_{x}}}{f-\sum_{x}\frac{1}{n_{x}}},\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{\mathrm{g}}_{\{A_{a|x}\}}\leqslant\frac{\lambda}{f},\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda=\max_{\vec{\jmath}}\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{k}A_{j_{x}|x}\right\|_{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad f=\sum_{a,x}\frac{\Tr A_{a|x}^{2}}{d}.

(20)

For projective measurements coming from
k
anticommuting observables, we indeed have that
f=k
and that the minimum polynomial of the operator whose spectrum defines
\lambda
is, by anticommutation,
X^{2}-kX+\frac{1}{4}k(k-1)
, so that,
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(k+\sqrt{k})
, which concludes the proof.

As a side note, we observe that this proof extends to other incompatibility measures defined in
DFK19
, namely,
\eta^{\mathrm{p}}
and
\eta^{\mathrm{jm}}
, showing that
\chi^{\mathrm{p}}_{k}(2)=1/\sqrt{k}
and
\chi^{\mathrm{jm}}_{k}(2)=2/(\sqrt{k}+1)
.
As for
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
, the question of the most incompatible sets of dichotomic measurements is ill-posed since this measure is not monotonic under pre-processing.

III.3
Depolarising incompatibility robustness

For
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
(as well as
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
), we can exploit the
post-processing monotonicity
of the incompatibility measure to study only
rank-one
measurements, which are extremal
DFK19
.
The results obtained this way are naturally agnostic of the number of outcomes.
This section follows closely
Section

III.1
, trading the number of outcomes for the dimension; this parallel will be generalised in
Section

V.2
below.

Hence, let us consider
k
rank-one measurements in dimension
d
, denoted
\{A_{j_{1}|1}\}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\{A_{j_{k}|k}\}_{j_{k}}
.
We further write
A_{j_{x}|x}=(\Tr A_{j_{x}|x})Q_{j_{x}|x}
with
Q_{j_{x}|x}
rank-one projectors.
With this, we define

t_{\vec{\jmath}}=\prod_{x=1}^{k}\Tr A_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{\vec{\jmath}}=\sum_{x=1}^{k}Q_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{to construct}\quad H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}\propto t_{\vec{\jmath}}\left(T_{\vec{\jmath}}-\alpha_{k,d}\mathds{1}\right)^{2},

(21)

where
\alpha_{k,d}
is defined in
Eq.

14
and the proportionality factor is again fixed by normalisation.
Our goal is to find the noise associated with this
H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}
, that is, the value of
\eta
such that we have a feasible point in
Section

II
.
This requires finding the marginals and the normalisation factor of
H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(2)}\propto t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}-2\alpha_{k,d}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}+\alpha_{k,d}^{2}t_{\vec{\jmath}}\mathds{1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}t_{\vec{\jmath}}\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=d^{k}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}t_{\vec{\jmath}}\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=d^{k-1}\mathds{1},

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}

\displaystyle=kd^{k-1}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}

\displaystyle=d^{k-1}P_{a|x}+(k-1)d^{k-2}\mathds{1},

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}

\displaystyle=k(d+k-1)d^{k-2}\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(d+2(k-1)\big)d^{k-2}P_{a|x}+(k-1)(d+k-2)d^{k-3}\mathds{1}.

(24)

With these marginals, we can compute the desired value of
\eta
and conclude:

\eta=\frac{d-2+\sqrt{d^{2}+4(k-1)(d-1)}}{2k(d-1)}\leqslant H^{\mathrm{d}}_{k}(d).

(25)

For
k=2
, this result reproduces the value from
(
DFK19
, Eq. (28))
.

Interestingly, for
k=3
and
d=2
, projections onto the eigenbases of
X,Y,Z
saturate this bound so that we have, using
Eq.

11
to extend it to
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
,

H^{\mathrm{d}}_{3}(2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad H^{\mathrm{g}}_{3}(2)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right).

(26)

This was already proven in
(
DFK19
, Appendix E.4)
, albeit with a more obscure proof of positivity for the parent measurement exhibited therein.
Moreover, this parent measurement involved terms of degree three as the Jordan product was being used; this is in contrast with the degree two of our parent measurement.

IV
Degree three

In this section, we keep the form of the universal parent measurement (as a polynomial in
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
) but climb one step in the degree ladder.
For a fixed number of outcomes, we extend
Section

III.1
and obtain tighter bounds on
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
.
For a fixed dimension, we extend
Section

III.3
and give consequences of the obtained bounds on
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
for the experimental observation of genuine high-dimensional steering.

IV.1
Fixed number of outcomes

Like in
Section

III.1
, we consider
k
projective measurements with
n
outcomes
\{P_{j_{1}|1}\}_{j_{1}=1}^{n},\ldots,\{P_{j_{k}|k}\}_{j_{k}=1}^{n}
, extending the final result to all measurements with the pre-processing monotonicity of
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
.
With

S_{\vec{\jmath}}=\sum_{x=1}^{k}P_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{k,n}=\frac{k+n-2-\sqrt{(k-1)(n-1)+1}}{n},\quad\text{we construct}\quad G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}\propto S_{\vec{\jmath}}\left(S_{\vec{\jmath}}-\beta_{k,n}\mathds{1}\right)^{2},

(27)

where the proportionality factor in the definition of
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
is fixed by normalisation, i.e.,
\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}=\mathds{1}
.
Note that
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
is semidefinite positive since the polynomial
X(X-\beta_{k,n})^{2}
takes nonnegative values on the interval containing the eigenvalues of
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
.
Our goal is to find the noise associated with this
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
, that is, the value of
\eta
such that we have a feasible point in
Section

II
.
This requires finding a lower bound on the marginals of
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
and its normalisation factor, a task that boils down to computing those of the successive powers of
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
as
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}\propto S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}-2\beta_{k,n}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}+\beta_{k,n}^{2}S_{\vec{\jmath}}
.

The linear and quadratic terms have already been tackled in
Eqs.

16
and
17
, and we can use
\mathds{1}\geqslant P_{a|x}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant(n+k-1)n^{k-2}P_{a|x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}\geqslant\left(n^{2}+3(k-1)n+(k-1)(k-2)\right)P_{a|x}.

(28)

The cubic term, however, requires more care.
On the one hand, its normalisation is trivial, though somewhat tedious:

\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}=k\left(n^{2}+3(k-1)n+(k-1)(k-2)\right)n^{k-3}\mathds{1}.

(29)

On the other hand, its marginals involve pinched terms like
P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1}
which cannot be simply summed over
j_{1}
.
This is where the choice of the generalised incompatibility robustness
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
is justified: since the definition of this measure only requires a lower bound on the marginals, we can handle such terms by using the inequality

\sum_{j_{1}}P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}P_{j_{2}|2},

(30)

for which a proof can be found, for instance, in
(
TFR+21
, Eq. (B86))
.
This allows us to derive

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(n^{2}+5(k-1)n+(k-1)(3k-5)\right)n^{k-3}P_{a|x}+(k-1)\left(n^{2}+3(k-2)n+(k-2)(k-3)\right)n^{k-4}\mathds{1}

(31)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(n^{3}+6(k-1)n^{2}+(k-1)(6k-11)n+(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)\right)n^{k-4}P_{a|x},

(32)

from which we can deduce the noise
\eta
associated with
G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
:

\eta=\frac{k+n-2+\sqrt{(k-1)(n-1)+1}}{kn}\leqslant\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n).

(33)

Note that the value of
\beta_{k,n}
chosen initially is precisely picked to maximise the resulting value of
\eta
.
For
n=2
, we obtain again the value studied in
Section

III.2
, which is no surprise as this value, being already optimal, could not be improved on.
For
k=2
, this result reproduces
(
TFR+21
, Proposition B.12)
(setting
n_{A}=n_{B}=n
).

IV.2
Fixed dimension

Like in
Section

III.3
, we consider
k
rank-one measurements in dimension
d
, denoted
\{A_{j_{1}|1}\}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\{A_{j_{k}|k}\}_{j_{k}}

A_{j_{x}|x}=(\Tr A_{j_{x}|x})Q_{j_{x}|x}

With this, we recall that

t_{\vec{\jmath}}=\prod_{x=1}^{k}\Tr A_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{\vec{\jmath}}=\sum_{x=1}^{k}Q_{j_{x}|x}\quad\text{to construct}\quad H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}\propto t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}\left(T_{\vec{\jmath}}-\beta_{k,d}\mathds{1}\right)^{2},

(34)

where
\beta_{k,d}
is defined in
Eq.

27
and the proportionality factor is fixed by normalisation.
Note that
H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
is semidefinite positive since the polynomial
X(X-\beta_{k,n})^{2}
takes nonnegative values on the interval
[0,k]
containing the eigenvalues of
T_{\vec{\jmath}}
.
Like in
Section

IV.1
, our goal is to find the noise associated with this
H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}
.
This requires finding a lower bound on the marginals of
H_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(3)}\propto t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}-2\beta_{k,d}t_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}+\beta_{k,d}^{2}t_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}
.

The linear and quadratic terms were treated in
Eqs.

23
and
24
, and we can use
(\Tr A_{a|x})\mathds{1}\geqslant A_{a|x}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}\geqslant(d+k-1)d^{k-2}A_{a|x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}t_{\vec{\jmath}}T_{\vec{\jmath}}^{2}\geqslant\left(d^{2}+3(k-1)d+(k-1)(k-2)\right)A_{a|x}.

The normalisation of the cubic term remains straightforward

\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}=k\left(d^{2}+3(k-1)d+(k-1)(k-2)\right)d^{k-3}\mathds{1},

V
Higher orders: unveiling a hierarchy

For higher powers of
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
, the normalisation is not ensured anymore in general.
In this section, we take a detour by considering the special case of mutually unbiased bases (MUBs) before coming back to general measurements where we drop the specific form of the universal parent measurement considered, namely, as a polynomial in
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
.

We first investigate parent measurements of MUBs using the fourth power of
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
, improving on some lower bounds on
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
obtained in
DSFB19
.
We then realise that the bounds obtained therein involve terms of higher degree combined in a way that positivity can still be easily proven, although not following the same procedure as above (polynomials in
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
).
This allows us to relax this assumption and give a general sum-of-squares hierarchy that encompasses the previous constructions.
Finally, we give a numerical proof of principle for this hierarchy by deriving universal lower bounds on
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
which improve on the results of the previous sections, as shown in
Table

1
.

V.1
Degree four and mutually unbiased bases

We consider rank-one projective measurements onto
k

, where

We refer to

Here we only need their defining property:
P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}P_{j_{x_{2}}|x_{2}}P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}=\frac{1}{d}P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}
for
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
.
This property allows us to rewrite
P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}P_{j_{x_{2}}|x_{2}}P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}P_{j_{x_{2}}|x_{2}}=\frac{1}{d}P_{j_{x_{1}}|x_{1}}P_{j_{x_{2}}|x_{2}}
, so that
S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{4}
can be normalised.

Equipped with this now valid higher power, we can consider parent measurements of the form

G_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(4)}\propto\left(S_{\vec{\jmath}}-\gamma^{(1)}_{k,d}\mathds{1}\right)^{2}\left(S_{\vec{\jmath}}-\gamma^{(2)}_{k,d}\mathds{1}\right)^{2}

(42)

to derive bounds on
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
valid only for MUBs.
Up to degree two, everything remains unchanged with respect to
Section

III.1
.
For degree three, the normalisation is still given in
Eq.

29
but for the marginals, instead of the lower bound proposed in
Eq.

31
, the mutual unbiasedness yields

\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{3}=\left(d^{2}+5(k-1)d+3(k-1)(k-2)\right)d^{k-3}P_{a|x}+(k-1)\left(d^{2}+(3k-5)d+(k-2)(k-3)\right)d^{k-4}\mathds{1}.

(43)

For degree four, we can derive the normalisation

\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{4}=k\left(d^{3}+6(k-1)d^{2}+(k-1)(6k-11)d+(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)\right)d^{k-4}\mathds{1}

(44)

and the marginals

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}S_{\vec{\jmath}}^{4}

\displaystyle=\left(d^{3}+9(k-1)d^{2}+2(k-1)(7k-13)d+4(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)\right)d^{k-4}P_{a|x}

(45)

\displaystyle+(k-1)\left(d^{3}+3(2k-3)d^{2}+(k-2)(6k-13)d+(k-2)(k-3)(k-4)\right)d^{k-5}\mathds{1}.

(46)

V.2
Normalisable polynomials and semidefinite programming

In
DSFB19
, the parent measurement giving the lower bound on
\eta^{\mathrm{d}}
for MUBs is written as a sum of projectors, guaranteeing its positivity.
In this section, we understand that a sum-of-squares approach is possible to obtain proofs of positivity that go beyond both the polynomials in
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
studied above and the decomposition shown in
DSFB19
for MUBs.

Let us start by writing the first term of this decomposition
(
DSFB19
, Eq. (13))
as a polynomial of the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
:

\mathcal{G}_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(1)}\propto X_{\vec{\jmath}}^{\dagger}X_{\vec{\jmath}}\quad\text{where}\quad X_{\vec{\jmath}}=P_{j_{1}|1}\left[\prod_{x=2}^{k}\left(\mathds{1}+\alpha_{x}\sqrt{d}P_{j_{x}|x}\right)\right],

(47)

where the product is expanded from left to right.
Interestingly,
\mathcal{G}_{\vec{\jmath}}^{(1)}
contains monomials of degree up to
2k-1
, which soon grows past the limit of degree three that was explored so far for general measurements.
The difference lies in the form chosen:
Eq.

47
is not anymore a (positive) polynomial in
S_{\vec{\jmath}}
but simply a (positive) combination of
normalisable
monomials in the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
.

We call a monomial in the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
normalisable when the sum over
{\vec{\jmath}}
can be reduced down to a multiple of the identity only using the projector property.
In other words, there exists an order of the summations over
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
leading to a full reduction of the monomial.
For instance,
P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}
mentioned above is not normalisable while
P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{3}|3}P_{j_{2}|2}
is, since summing over
j_{1}
and
j_{3}
yields
P_{j_{2}|2}^{2}=P_{j_{2}|2}
which can be finally summed over
j_{2}
.
A normalisable polynomial only has normalisable monomials.

It is straightforward that all universal parent measurements presented so far in this work only involve normalisable polynomials.
In light of the more general proof of positivity of
Eq.

47
, we can now generalise the process of finding universal lower bound on
\eta^{\mathrm{g}}
to an SOS optimisation problem
TPBA24
.
More precisely, for
t\geqslant 1
, denoting
\mathrm{SOS}_{2t}
the set of sum-of-squares polynomials of degree up to
2t
in the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
, we can write

\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n)\geqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\eta,G}&\eta\\
\text{s.t.}&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})=1,\quad G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})\in\mathrm{SOS}_{2t}\vphantom{\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}},\\
&\sum\limits_{\vec{\jmath}}\delta_{j_{x},a}G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})-\eta P_{a|x}\in\mathrm{SOS}_{2t}\quad\forall a,x.\\
\end{array}\right.

(48)

Eq.

48
is simply obtained by rewriting
Section

II
when fixing the form of the parent measurement as a normalisable polynomial in the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
.
Importantly, the polynomial
G
has to be normalisable, which can be enforced by choosing a basis of normalisable monomials.
In the last constraint, without loss of generality, we can set
a=1
.

Note that the similarity between
Sections

III.1
and
III.3
(also
Sections

IV.1
and
IV.2
) generalises, so that bounds on
\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n)
(for
n
-outcome measurements) can be transposed to
H^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n)
(for
n
-dimensional measurements) by considering rank-one measurements instead of projective ones and adding a factor
t_{\vec{\jmath}}
in the parent measurement.

A crucial open question that numerical investigation should clarify is whether the hierarchy converges to the actual value of
\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n)
or
H^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(d)
, that is, whether
Eq.

48
becomes tight for some
t
(or even asymptotically).
As shown in the literature and in this work, the answer is positive in some simple cases:

•

for
k=2
(see
(
DFK19
, Section III.G)
or our remark at the end of
Section

IV.2
),

•

for
d=2
and
k=3
(see
(
DFK19
, Appendix E.4)
or our simplified proof in
Section

III.3
),

•

for
n=2
(see
Section

III.1
).

Showing such a tightness in more general cases would demonstrate the power of this hierarchy and potentially give access to a proof that projective measurements onto complete sets of MUBs in dimension
d
are among the most incompatible sets of
d+1
measurements in dimension
d
.
Moreover, this could provide an interesting duality between the incompatibility properties of
d
-dimensional and
d
-outcome measurements.

Computationally, however, the hierarchy quickly becomes challenging, and we do not explore it extensively in this work, only giving some preliminary results in the next subsection.
The symmetrisation technique developed in
GP24
could turn useful to investigate this question in the smallest open case, namely,
k=3
and
n=3
.

V.3
Pinched-marginalisable polynomials and preliminary numerical results

As mentioned above, solving
Eq.

48
is computationally demanding.
Here we add some restrictions on the form of
G
to define a less general hierarchy for which we can derive numerical results, hence demonstrating the potential of
Eq.

48
.
The main idea is to select only certain types of monomials for which we can easily deal with the marginals.

Consider a normalisable polynomial in the projectors
P_{j_{x}|x}
and denote by
\Sigma
the operation that formally transforms this polynomial into its normalisation.
For example,
\Sigma(P_{j_{1}|1})=n^{k-1}
or
\Sigma(P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1})=n^{k-2}
.
This operation amounts to rewriting rules, erasing one by one the factors as we sum over all indices
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
(not necessarily in this order), applying the projector identity along the way.

The restriction of this section aims at using such rewriting rules for the marginals as well, making the last constraint from
Eq.

48
much simpler as it will only depend on
k
variable, and not all
n^{k}
of them.
More precisely, we define
pinched-marginalisable
monomials to be such that, for all
x
, their marginals along
x
(when summing over all indices except for
j_{x}
) can be rewritten into a multiple of
P_{j_{x}|x}
using the projector rules together with
Eq.

30
, which we recall for convenience:
\sum_{j_{1}}P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}P_{j_{2}|2}
(note that using
Eq.

30
repeatedly is permitted since
B\geqslant 0
implies
ABA\geqslant 0
for
A
Hermitian).
We denote
\Sigma_{x}
the associated transformation.
A pinched-marginalisable polynomial only has pinched-marginalisable monomials and
\Sigma_{x}
extends to polynomials by linearity.
For instance,
\Sigma_{1}(P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1})=n^{k-2}
and
\Sigma_{2}(P_{j_{1}|1}P_{j_{2}|2}P_{j_{1}|1})=n^{k-3}
.
Here,
\Sigma_{1}
does not use
Eq.

30
whereas
\Sigma_{2}
does; since
Eq.

30
is an inequality, special care has to be taken to ensure the positivity of the coefficient in the polynomial.
We do not explicitly state these conditions in the following but they should not be dropped in practice.

Now that there are only
k
variables involved, we can state, for
t\geqslant 1
, the restricted hierarchy:

\chi^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}(n)\geqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\eta,G}&\eta\\
\text{s.t.}&\Sigma\big(G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})\big)=1,\quad G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})\in\mathrm{SOS}_{2t},\\
&\Sigma_{x}\big(G(P_{j_{1}|1},\ldots,P_{j_{k}|k})\big)\geqslant\eta\quad\forall x.\\
\end{array}\right.

(49)

For
d
-dimensional measurements, the procedure mentioned above works as well, the only difference being that
Eq.

37
is now used instead of
Eq.

30
.

Finding the most incompatible sets of measurements can be addressed by exhibiting universal parent measurements, which we summarise in

On the one hand, this work has exhausted the direction where the parent measurement comes from a positive polynomial in certain sums of the measurements.
The bounds obtained this way reproduce known results for pairs of measurements and extend them nontrivially for larger sets.
Importantly, we have shown that anticommuting observables are maximally incompatible with respect to measures where this question is relevant.
On the other hand, we have outperformed this approach through an SDP hierarchy that we defined, where the positivity of the universal parent measurements and the marginal constraint are proven via sum-of-squares certificates.
This gives more freedom in the choice of the universal parent measurement and hence unlocks tighter bounds, which we numerically prove with a restricted hierarchy that involves smaller SDP instances.

The central question opened by this work is simple: can the general hierarchy be tight?
It is already true in many edge cases identified by previous works and this one, namely, when
k=2
(pairs),
n=2
(dichotomic), or
d=2
(qubit).
The most natural candidates for testing this in general would be complete sets of MUBs, starting in dimension
d=3
, which could be numerically tractable.
Whether the answer is positive or negative, improving on the bounds that we set in this work will certainly contribute to making the contrast between systems of increasing dimensions more easily observable in applications where incompatibility plays a role.
