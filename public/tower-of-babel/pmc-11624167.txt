Omnigene-Guttm ensures fecal microbiome stability in the pediatric population
Authors: Raoull Hoogendijk, Thijs J. M. van den Broek, Hyunju Lee, Sabine Mueller, Cassie Kline, John Bianco, Janetta Top, Marcel R. de Zoete, Lennart Kester, Friso Calkoen, Jasper van der Lugt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Increasing evidence exists that the gut microbiome influences toxicity as well as outcomes in a variety of cancers. To investigate the role of the gut microbiome in pediatric neuro-oncology, microbiome analysis has been included in multiple prospective pediatric neuro-oncology clinical trials (NCT05009992, NCT04732065, NCT04775485). In these trials, the OMNIgene-GUTtm preservation tubes are used for the collection of the feces. OMNIgene-GUTtm has demonstrated reliability in preserving the composition of the gut microbiome in adults; however, its validation for use in the pediatric population remains limited. Therefore, we compared the quality of the DNA by 16S rRNA gene sequencing after various methods of stabilizing fecal samples in pediatric populations, from the direct freeze method at − 80 °C to preserving samples with OMNIgene-GUTtm at room temperature for various durations. Our results showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the alpha-diversity, and beta-diversity. However, pairwise differential abundance analyses demonstrated that OMNIgene-GUT™ is superior in maintaining microbial community structure compared to storing samples without any preservation method. With the OMNIgene-GUTtm’s stabilization of the fecal samples being superior and its ease-of-use benefits, it proves to be a valid and ideal method of stabilizing fecal samples for current and future pediatric clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-024-01798-x.

Introduction

It is increasingly recognized that the microbiome plays a vital role in the anti-tumor response of systemic therapies (Liang et al. 2022). The microbiome has been shown to alter therapeutic effects through interaction with the immune system and the related tumor microenvironment (Qiu et al. 2020; Dono et al. 2022). This is of special interest for the field of neuro-oncology where studies investigating immunotherapy have resulted in variable outcomes (Ratnam et al. 2021; Gallego Perez-Larraya et al. 2022; Wen et al. 2022). To better understand the underlying response mechanism it is therefore vital to gain insight into the microbial composition of trial participants. For this reason, microbiome analyses have recently been incorporated in multiple pediatric neuro-oncology trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05009992, NCT04732065, NCT04775485).

To make a valid statement on the microbial composition and enable comparisons between different cohorts it is important to stabilize the fecal samples to maintain bacterial diversity and community structure. For the stabilization of fecal samples, the gold standard is immediate freezing at − 20/− 80 °C. However, direct freezing and storage at low temperatures are challenging when collecting fecal samples in a clinical trial setting, as samples are most commonly collected at home and stored at room temperature (RT) before analyses. Additionally, for cases where immediate freezing at home would be possible, costs for transportation can increase rapidly.

To overcome these issues, multiple RT sample stabilization methods have been developed (Choo et al. 2015; Anderson et al. 2016; Hill et al. 2016; Abrahamson et al. 2017; Ilett et al. 2019). A widely used collection method is the OMNIgene-GUTtm (DNA Genotek Inc., CA). The OMNIgene-GUTtm is a chelating agent-based kit that has previously been reported to have acceptable technical reproducibility and compositional stability (Choo et al. 2015; Anderson et al. 2016; Hill et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2018; Pribyl et al. 2021). However, sample sizes were limited and mainly focused on adults. Multiple studies have indicated that the healthy pediatric gut microbiome differs in composition from the healthy adult population (Yatsunenko et al. 2012; Hollister et al. 2015; Gacesa et al. 2022). Additionally, previous research has indicated that the gut microbiome is highly variable in children under the age of four (Palmer et al. 2007; Yatsunenko et al. 2012). Hence it is important to validate the performance of the OMNIgene-GUTtm for the pediatric population and differentiate between younger and older children. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated the technical reproducibility and compositional stability of fecal samples collected with OMNIgene·GUTtm and stored at RT for different periods compared to the direct freeze method (− 80 °C) for children by using 16s rRNA sequencing analysis.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and storage

To test the OMNIgene-GUTtm for the ability to accurately preserve microbial communities, we gathered one fecal sample from 24 healthy individuals below the age of 18 years. This sample was split for 5 different storage conditions: two OMNIgene-GUTtm tubes for storage at RT for 48 hours and 7 days followed by storage at − 80 °C, three tubes for storage without preservatives, including direct storage at − 80 °C, and RT for 48 hours, and 7 days followed by storage at − 80 °C, resulting in a total of 120 samples. This project was submitted to MREC Utrecht for approval but as the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) does not apply to this study, a waiver was provided (reference number 22-907/DB).

DNA extraction

Upon the start of DNA extraction, all tubes were thawed and samples were divided into aliquots of 0.2 mL. One aliquot was used for DNA extraction and the remaining aliquots were stored at– 80 °C. DNA extraction was performed as described previously (Stege et al. 2023).

16S rRNA sequencing

All samples were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing to determine gut microbiome composition. The 16S rRNA gene hypervariable regions V3 and V4 were amplified (~430 bp) and sequenced with an Illumina MiSeq reagent kit v3 (600-cycles) on a MiSeq system (Illumina, Eindhoven, the Netherlands), as was described previously (Fadrosh et al. 2014; Paganelli et al. 2019). Sequences were taxonomically assigned using QIIME 2 (version 2018.8), DADA2 (package version 1.16), and the SILVA database 138, applying a cutoff of 5000 reads (Quast et al. 2013; Callahan et al. 2016; Bolyen et al. 2019).

All samples had more than 10.000 reads per sample except for one sample with 6.392 reads. The average number of reads for all samples combined was 31.369 (range 6.392–80.232).

Quality control

Negative controls and mock communities (ZymoBIOMICS microbial community standard (D6300) and ZymoBIOMICS microbial community DNA standard (D6305), ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard II (Log Distribution) (D6310), Zymo Research, USA) were used from the beginning of DNA isolation up to the data analysis stage and matched with the distribution expected mock compositions (Table S1).

Data analyses

Characteristics of the study population were described using standard descriptive statistics. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 together with the functions of the packages phyloseq v1.42.0 and ggplot2 v3.4.0 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013; R Core Team 2013; Wickham 2016). The abundance of the top 10 gut microbiome taxa and ARGs were plotted using aggregate top taxa and plotting functions of the microbiome package v1.20.0. Chao1, Shannon, and inversed Simpson index of diversity were calculated using the alpha-diversity functions of the microbiome package and plotting functions of the microbiome utilities package v1.00.17 (Lahti and Shetty 2018; Shetty et al. 2018). The Bray-Curtis dissimilarity/distance matrix was calculated using the vegdist() function from the vegan package. Beta diversity was compared between groups by PERMANOVA using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2015). Additionally, using the direct freeze method as the reference, we performed a patient-matched comparison of Bray-Curtis dissimilarities for different storage conditions using the pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Lastly, differential abundance analyses were performed with the analysis of compositions of microbiomes with bias correction (ANCOM-BC) package (Lin and Peddada 2020). and DeSeq2 in combination with EnhancedVolcano v1.23.0 for visualization (Love et al. 2014; Blighe et al. 2024). Results were analyzed for the complete study population and stratified to the age groups 0–3 years and 4–17 years for relative abundance, alpha-diversity, and beta-diversity.

Results

Participants had a median age of 5 (IQR 2–7.25). Ten (42%) participants were in the 0–3 year age group and 14 (58%) in the 4–17 year age group. Of the 24 subjects, 4 (17%) were on a vegetarian diet, and 2 subjects (8%) were using medication or supplements during the time of sampling. Medication consisted of contraception, topical fucidin, and miconazole. Supplementation was limited to iron supplements. Alpha- and beta-diversity

Alpha-diversity was assessed using three metrics: richness (Chao1 diversity index), and diversity (Shannon and Simpson diversity indices). The results for alpha-diversity under various storage conditions are graphically depicted in Fig. 2A-C. When comparing the direct freezing method with alternative storage conditions, the Chao1 diversity index exhibited the lowest p-value (p = 0.4) when using the OMNIgene-GUTtm method stored at 48 hours. For the Shannon diversity index, the lowest p-value (p = 0.25) was observed for the difference with 7 days at RT without preservatives. The Simpson index yielded results consistent with the Chao1 diversity index, with the lowest p-value (p = 0.35) recorded for the OMNIgene-GUTtm method at 48 hours. When comparing the storage at 48-hour with storage at 7-day for tubes without preservatives, no significant differences were observed for any of the diversity indices (Chao-1 index, p = 0.65; Simpson's index, p = 0.55; Shannon index, p = 0.62). Similarly, for the OMNIgene-GUTtm method, the outcomes were comparable for the storage at 48-hour with storage at 7-day (Chao-1 index, p = 0.56; Simpson's index, p = 0.88; Shannon index, p = 0.93). Differential abundance analysis

Utilizing ANCOM-BC, we observed no significant differences in microbial differential abundance across all storage conditions for the 0–17 age group. Due to the limited sample size, we conducted differential abundance analyses on the entire study population without stratifying by age. Since ANCOM-BC did not support pairwise comparisons, we implemented DESeq2 to account for multiple samples from the same patient. We compared the direct freeze samples with those stored for 48 hours without preservatives (Fig. 5A), 7 days without preservatives (Fig. 5B), 48 hours with OMNIgene-GUTtm (Fig. 5C), and 7 days with OMNIgene-GUTtm (Fig. 5D). We found that all conditions exhibited differences compared to the direct freeze samples and in both conditions (without preservatives, Fig. 5A-B and with OMNIgene-GUTtm, Fig. 5C-D), it is clear that the duration of storage impacts the microbial community, with an increase in the number and magnitude of significant changes in microbial genera between 48 hours to 7 days. However, with the OMNIgene-GUTtm, there are fewer significant changes in taxa, and the log fold changes (LFCs) are generally smaller, suggesting that OMNIgene-GUTtm is effective in minimizing microbial fluctuations during storage. Additionally, in the samples without preservatives, significant LFCs of Escherichia-Shigella were observed at both timepoints, which in combination with their presence among the top 10 relative abundances at the genus level suggested outgrowth in the non-preservative method.Fig. 5Impact of storage duration and preservative method on microbial community stability using the direct freeze method as the reference: Volcano plots of differential abundance at A 48 h without preservatives, B 7 days without preservatives, C 48 h with OMNIgene-GUTtm, and D 7 days with OMNIgene-GUTtm

Discussion

In this study, we showed that fecal sample storage with OMNIgene-GUTtm for the pediatric population has comparable alpha- and beta-diversity to the direct freeze method when using 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis. In addition, when comparing all storage conditions no differences were found in differential abundance analyses with ANCOM-BC, but analysis with DeSeq2 showed that OMNIgene-GUTtm helps maintain microbial community stability during storage, with the most pronounced effect observed after 7 days of storage. The comparability with the direct freeze method and superiority in maintaining microbial stability up to 7 days makes OMNIgene-GUTtm an ideal method for implementation in pediatric clinical trials, especially as others have previously reported stability for longer periods in other populations (Hill et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2023).

A previous study indicated that the lower diversity in the infant's gut (≤2 years of age) microbiota led to an increased vulnerability to general alterations in the gut microbiota profile following storage of the OMNIgene-GUTtm (Hill et al. 2016). Although older children up to 17 years old were also included, the median age of our cohort was 5 years and 25% of our cohort was ≤2 years of age. As the gut microbiome composition is highly variable in young children, we further investigated the impact of the contribution of the relatively young children in our cohort, by stratifying a subset of our analyses to the age groups 0–3 years and 4–17 years. These sub-analyses have not pointed out notable differences in the relative abundance across storage conditions although the top 10 relative abundance on a genus level was dependent on age. The species identified in the current study largely overlap with the keystone species identified in the Dutch Microbiome Project, which are reported to be highly consistent across western and non-western populations (Gacesa et al. 2022).

The results of this study are in line with previous reports in the adult population where no significant differences were found in alpha-diversity indices when comparing the direct freeze method with storing OMNIgene-GUTtm at RT for different periods (Chao-1 index (Choo et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2018), Simpson’s index (Choo et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2018), Shannon index (Choo et al. 2015; Szopinska et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2020). Additionally, Park et al. found comparable outcomes for the alpha-diversity indices when storing OMNIgene GUTtm samples at RT for 2, 15, 30, 45, and 65 days (Park et al. 2020). However, Chen et al. found differences in Shannon index, but not in Chao-1 index, when comparing different storage conditions and clustering different storage methods (Chen et al. 2020). Regarding beta-diversity we found no significant differences. These results are in line with several other studies investigating OMNIgene GUTtm (Hill et al. 2016; Park et al. 2020).

OMNIgene GUTtm has been commercialized for preserving DNA integrity for up to 60 days at room temperature. Our study has limited the storage conditions to 7 days, and when performing differential abundance analyses with DeSeq2, we found that OMNIgene-GUTtm can better maintain the microbial community stability during storage at RT. Based on these results we can therefore conclude that OMNIgene GUTtm is superior to storing feces without any preservative method for the pediatric population. Of note, is that samples were stored in a temperature-controlled lab environment within 48 hours after collection, limiting temperature shifts that might occur when collecting samples at home. The controlled environment possibly limits compositional changes over time, impacting our results.

There are more preservative methods available that were not included in this paper. However, multiple authors have previously addressed the superiority of the OMNIgene GUTtm compared to other methods. For example, using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to estimate stability, concordance, and reliability, Guan et al. compared OMNIgene-GUTtm, fecal occult blood test (FOBT) cards, RNAlater, Microlution and ethanol, and concluded that OMNIgene-GUTtm had the highest stability (most ICCs 0.75, p< 0.05) (Guan et al. 2021). Moreover, Choo et al. reported that the OMNIgene-GUTtm had the least alterations and Pribyl et al. confirmed that OMNIgene-GUTtm had a high technical reproducibility (Choo et al. 2015; Pribyl et al. 2021). Additionally, by using whole genome sequencing Anderson et al. assessed the efficacy of the OMNIgene-GUTtm for the preservation of stool samples, comparing them to fresh stool samples as a baseline and direct freezing (Anderson et al. 2016). The results indicated that stabilized samples performed at least as well as frozen samples and added additional advantages. Specifically, stabilized samples provided higher quantities of both DNA and RNA, leading to a reduction in the quantity of stool required for subsequent analysis. Lastly, although we have not investigated patient preferences on collection methods, a previous study investigating the OMNIgene-GUTtm has reported that participants responded positively to the OMNIgene-GUTtm collection kit, and the mailing process used in this study for sample transfer was successful (Abrahamson et al. 2017). These results further support our conclusion that the OMNIgene-Guttm is a reliable, patient-friendly, and effective way of collecting and storing feces at RT for children.

Other potential methods in collecting samples from patients that this study did not look at include the digital rectal exam (DRE) rectal swabs and the glove tip method. These methods have been shown to be consistent in recording microbiota diversity and taxonomic abundance when compared to OMNIgene-Guttm (Short et al. 2021). While patient preference amongst these various methods and cost-analysis has not been studied, the DRE rectal swab and glove tip method requires a doctor to administer the exam, making it hard to implement for longitudinal sample collection (Short et al. 2021). Thus, the OMNIgene-Guttm continues to be advantageous in its ability to increase the viability of at-home collection of stool samples.

Within pediatric oncology, studies have primarily analyzed fresh stool samples or stabilized stool samples by directly freezing them (Moser et al. 2011; Gu et al. 2015; Dantes et al. 2016; Nycz et al. 2018; El-Badry et al. 2019; Kelly et al. 2019; Sahoo et al. 2022; Elgarten et al. 2022). Many of these studies have focused on the microbiome in relation to infections or in the context of hematopoietic stem cell transplantations (Gu et al. 2015; Dantes et al. 2016; Nycz et al. 2018; Kelly et al. 2019; Sahoo et al. 2022; Elgarten et al. 2022). The OMNIgene-Guttm can expand the potential avenues for understanding the mechanisms between the microbiome and pediatric tumors beyond what is currently seen in the published literature as it increases the flexibility for collecting stool samples. Especially in a multi-center clinical trial where consistent and low threshold methods for collections are of great importance.

The pediatric cohort in this study introduces a layer of intricacy due to the dynamic and evolving nature of the gut microbiome in young children (Ronan et al. 2021). To address this inherent variability of the young population, we employed a strategy involving the repeated collection of samples from individual subjects and stratifying participants on their age. This approach enhanced our capacity to accurately discern the impact of sample storage, thus strengthening the methodological rigor of our study. A notable limitation of this investigation is the constraint imposed by the relatively modest sample size. Nevertheless, our findings are in line with previously reported results in the adult population and are to our knowledge the largest study investigating the OMNIgene-Guttm method in the pediatric population.

In conclusion, fecal sample storage with OMNIgene-GUTtm at RT is comparable to the direct freeze method, and based on differential abundance analyses a superior method compared to storing samples without preservatives. OMNIgene-GUTtm can therefore be considered a reliable and valid way for fecal collection in the pediatric population. These results hold particular significance for pediatric neuro-oncology, as longitudinal fecal collection using OMNIgene-GUTtm was implemented in multiple pediatric neuro-oncology trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05009992, NCT04732065, NCT04775485).
