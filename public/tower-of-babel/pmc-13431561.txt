Real‐world risk factors and clinical implications of early estimated glomerular filtration rate decline after sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor therapy
Authors: Jangwook Lee, Dayoung Kang, Kyoung‐Ah Kim, Han Seok Choi, Soomin Jo, Joeun Kim, Jae Yoon Park, Seung Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo assess the prevalence, risk factors, estimated glomerular filtration rate trajectory, and clinical implications of the early estimated glomerular filtration rate dip after sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors in a real‐world population.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 1,550 patients who received sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor treatment at a single teaching hospital between 2014 and 2024. Patients were stratified into three groups based on early estimated glomerular filtration rate decline within the first 3 months of treatment: non‐, moderate, and severe dippers (<10%, 10%–30%, and ≥30%, respectively).ResultsAn early estimated glomerular filtration rate dip occurred in 21% of the patients; 17.6% were moderate dippers, and 3.5% were severe dippers. The risk factors for dipper classification included older age, atrial fibrillation, loop diuretic use, and high estimated glomerular filtration rate variability. Although the estimated glomerular filtration rate slopes declined progressively across dipper groups, the mean annual decline did not exceed age‐expected changes even in the severe dipper group. Patients who discontinued sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor therapy experienced a steeper decline than those who continued therapy. Three patients in the severe dipper group on concomitant loop diuretics experienced extreme estimated glomerular filtration rate reductions, which persisted during follow‐up.ConclusionsEarly dips and continued sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor use are not generally associated with unfavorable outcomes. However, caution is warranted in frail or volume‐sensitive patients, particularly with concomitant diuretics.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 850 million individuals worldwide 1 , and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the leading cause of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD). Among patients with type 2 diabetes, approximately 27% have comorbid CKD 2 . The economic burden associated with diabetic ESKD is substantial, with a prior report estimating the average monthly expenditure for patients undergoing long‐term dialysis to be $12,299 3 . Moreover, DKD is associated with an increased risk of systemic complications, including cardiovascular disease 4 , dementia 5 , and fractures 6 , underscoring the imperative need for its prevention and management.

Sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), originally developed as glucose‐lowering agents, have demonstrated significant benefits in delaying the onset and progression of CKD 7 . A series of randomized controlled trials have consistently shown that SGLT2i reduce CKD progression 8 , 9 not only in patients with diabetes but also in those without diabetes 10 , 11 . Consequently, several international guidelines and expert consensus statements now recommend the use of SGLT2i to slow CKD progression, regardless of diabetes status 12 , 13 .

Despite the long‐term renoprotective effects of SGLT2i, these agents are associated with an early decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) after initiation, which is mediated by afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction 14 . Although this transient decline in eGFR has raised concerns, multiple randomized trials have shown no association between an early eGFR decrease and adverse clinical outcomes 15 , 16 . Furthermore, some studies suggest that an early eGFR dip may even predict slower long‐term decline in kidney function 17 , 18 .

However, findings from randomized trials may not fully translate into real‐world settings. In clinical practice, patients may be intermittently exposed to nephrotoxic agents, such as nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or may receive diuretics or experience volume depletion, predisposing them to pre‐renal acute kidney injury (AKI).

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors associated with an early eGFR dip in a real‐world population and to examine the longitudinal eGFR trajectory and clinical impact according to the presence or absence of an early dip.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This study included patients who were newly prescribed SGLT2i at Dongguk University Ilsan Hospital between January 1, 2014, and May 31, 2024 (n = 1,550). The SGLT2i used in this study were empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and ipragliflozin. Patients were excluded if they had no eGFR measurements within 180 days of the first prescription, missing covariates, or a follow‐up duration of less than 3 months. We collected data, including age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, drug information, and laboratory results, from the Clinical Data Warehouse (CDW).

Definitions

Comorbidities, including stroke, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation (A‐fib), hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, were defined as present if at least one relevant ICD‐10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition) diagnostic code was recorded in the CDW. For hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, the presence of the condition was established based on the use of the corresponding medications.

The eGFR was estimated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation 19 . An eGFR dip was defined as the lowest eGFR value within 110 days after the initiation of SGLT2i therapy, reflecting the conventional 3‐month follow‐up interval for diabetes management with an additional 20‐day window period. Patients were categorized into the following groups based on the initial decline in eGFR after SGLT2i initiation: (i) Non‐dipper (eGFR dip <10%), (ii) moderate dipper (10% ≤ eGFR dip <30%), and (iii) severe dipper (eGFR dip ≥30%) based on previous studies 20 , 21 . NSAID use was defined as NSAID exposure for ≥14 days during a given period.

To plot the eGFR trajectories, we defined prespecified time points. The eGFR value closest to each pre‐specified time point was used for representation: 12, 6, 3, and 1 month before the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors, and 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months after initiation. For example, if the eGFR measurement was available 92 days before SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, it was classified as an eGFR value 3 months before initiation. The exception was time zero, which was representative of the estimated intercept of the pre‐index slopes, in accordance with a previous study 22 . The eGFR trajectory plot was visually displayed and stratified by the dipper group. Additionally, the trajectory was displayed based on whether the medication was continued or discontinued.

For each patient, the standard deviation (SD) of the eGFR was computed based on all eGFR values recorded within the 2‐year period preceding the date of drug prescription to assess eGFR variability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal‐Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. Differences in eGFR trajectories across the dipper groups were analyzed using a linear mixed model in R, assessing the interaction between dipper group and time. Similarly, differences in the eGFR trajectory trends were further analyzed based on whether the medication was continued or discontinued. To estimate the annual rate of eGFR decline (slope), a linear mixed‐effects model was used, incorporating an interaction term between the time and dipper groups, along with subject‐specific random effects. This model, which included both fixed and random effects, was used to assess the differences in longitudinal changes in eGFR among the dipper groups. The analysis focused on 3–30 months after the initiation of SGLT2i therapy. The model was constructed using the lmer function from the lme4 package in R. The estimated slopes for each group represented the annual mean change in eGFR, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors associated with the dipper group. All P‐values were two‐sided, and P‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) and R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Among the 1,550 patients, 1,224 (79.0%), 272 (17.6%), and 54 (3.5%) were classified as non‐dippers, moderate dippers, and severe dippers, respectively. Table 1 shows the baseline demographic characteristics of the study population. As dipping progressed from the non‐dipper group to the severe dipper group, the prevalence of comorbidities increased, including cardiovascular disease, heart failure, A‐fib, and hypertension. Additionally, the mean duration of diabetes mellitus was significantly longer in the severe dipper group than in the non‐dipper group. Similarly, the frequency of medication use, including calcium channel blockers, beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and loop diuretics, increased from the non‐dipper group to the severe dipper group. Regarding kidney function, the baseline eGFR significantly decreased, whereas the SD of the eGFR and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) significantly increased with increasing dipping severity.

Following the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy, a decrease in eGFR was observed within the first 1–3 months in the moderate and severe dipper groups (Figure 1). After 3 months, a gradual decline in eGFR was observed across all groups, including non‐dippers, moderate dippers, and severe dippers. The mean eGFR slope from 3 to 30 months was −1.57 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −1.82 to −1.32) in the non‐dipper group, −0.78 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −1.41 to −0.15) in the moderate dipper group, and − 2.97 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −4.27 to −1.68) in the severe dipper group. Compared with the severe dipper group, both the non‐dipper and moderate dipper groups exhibited significantly smaller declines in eGFR slope (P = 0.034 for non‐dippers; P = 0.002 for moderate dippers).

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify patients predisposed to an initial decrease in eGFR. Using the non‐dipper group as a reference, we assessed the risk factors associated with classification as either moderate or severe dippers (Figure 2a). Older age (odds ratio [OR]: 1.02, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.04), a history of A‐fib (OR: 2.37, 95% CI: 1.25–4.50), and a higher SD of eGFR over the 2 years prior to the index date (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.14–1.22) were all associated with an increased likelihood of dipper classification. Conversely, higher baseline eGFR was associated with a lower likelihood of being a dipper; specifically, each interquartile range (IQR) increase in eGFR (25.25 mL/min/1.73 m2) was associated with an OR of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.55–0.88).

When the non‐dipper and moderate dipper groups were used as the reference to assess the risk factors for severe dipper classification, use of loop diuretics (OR: 4.96, 95% CI: 1.98–12.38) and greater eGFR variability (expressed as SD) were significantly associated with increased risk (OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.22) (Figure 2b). Again, higher baseline eGFR showed a protective association; each IQR increase (27.85 mL/min/1.73 m2) corresponded to an OR of 0.46 (95% CI: 0.28–0.76). In sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with at least 3 pre‐index eGFR measurements, greater eGFR variability remained independently associated with an increased risk of both being a dipper (Figure S1A: OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.14–1.23) and a severe dipper (Figure S1B: OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07–1.22).

Among the 1,550 patients, 317 (20.5%) discontinued medication during follow‐up (Table 2). When stratified by group, the proportion of patients who discontinued therapy was 19.6% (240/1,224) in the non‐dipper group, 20.2% (55/272) in the moderate dipper group, and 40.7% (22/54) in the severe dipper group, indicating a significant difference in discontinuation rates across the groups.

To investigate whether treatment persistence was associated with differences in kidney function trajectory, we plotted eGFR trends according to the treatment continuation status (Figure 3). In the non‐dipper group, the mean eGFR slope was −1.31 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −1.78 to −0.84) among those who maintained therapy, and −2.37 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −2.91 to −1.83) among those who discontinued therapy, with a significant interaction between treatment persistence and time (P < 0.001) (Figure 3a). Similarly, in the moderate dipper group, the mean eGFR slope was 0.11 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −1.50 to 1.72) among those who maintained therapy and − 3.46 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −4.86 to −2.06) among those who discontinued therapy (Figure 3b). In the severe dipper group, the mean eGFR slope was −0.24 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −4.08 to 3.61) among those who maintained therapy and −5.77 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year (95% CI: −8.52 to −3.02) among those who discontinued therapy (Figure 3c). A significant interaction between treatment persistence and time was also observed in the moderate dipper (P < 0.001) and severe dipper (P = 0.005) groups, suggesting a more attenuated decline in eGFR among patients who remained on therapy.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that approximately 20% of patients treated with SGLT2i experienced an early decrease in eGFR. Risk factors associated with this dip include older age, A‐fib, concomitant use of loop diuretics, lower baseline eGFR, and high eGFR variability. Although the rate of eGFR decline became progressively steeper from the non‐dipper to the moderate and severe dipper groups, the eGFR slope in the severe dipper group did not exceed the expected range for natural age‐related decline. Furthermore, the steeper slope of the eGFR observed in patients who discontinued SGLT2i after an early dip indicates that the decline in kidney function is unlikely to be attributed to SGLT2i.

Previous studies have investigated early eGFR dips following the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors, and the reported prevalence of significant dips has varied among studies. In a post hoc analysis of the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial, 28.3% of empagliflozin‐treated patients experienced an initial eGFR decline >10% 15 , and a post hoc analysis of the CREDENCE trial 16 reported that 45% of patients in the canagliflozin group experienced an acute drop in eGFR >10%. In both studies, an eGFR decrease >30% was rare, occurring in only 1.4% and 4.0% of the participants, respectively. The difference in the frequency of eGFR dips between the two studies may be attributed to the baseline CKD population (EMPA‐REG vs. CREDENCE, eGFR 74.0 vs. 56.2 mL/min/1.73 m2, macroalbuminuria 11% vs. 100%, and proportions of patients using loop diuretics 8.5% vs. 21.7%). In our study, which mainly included patients treated with dapagliflozin and empagliflozin and had a mean baseline eGFR of 87.5 mL/min/1.73 m2, approximately 20% of patients experienced an early eGFR dip greater than 10%, among whom 3.5% showed a decline exceeding 30%.

The mean eGFR slopes of non‐dippers, moderate dippers, and severe dippers were −1.57, −0.78, and −2.97 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year, respectively. The rate of eGFR decline varied widely among individuals. Typically, rapid eGFR decliners are defined as those with an annual decrease in eGFR exceeding 3–5 mL/min/1.73 m2 23 , 24 , 25 . Reported risk factors associated with rapid decline include poor glycemic control, hypertension, older age, anemia, diabetic retinopathy, and proteinuria 23 , 26 . In our study, patients classified as severe dippers were significantly older, had higher UACR levels, and lower hemoglobin concentrations than those in the other groups. Moreover, 98.2% of patients had concomitant hypertension, suggesting a high likelihood of being rapid decliners. Notably, even severe dippers showed the mean eGFR decline was −2.97 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year, reinforcing the notion that an early dip does not necessarily predict poor prognosis.

In our study, patients who discontinued SGLT2i had worse kidney outcomes than those who did not, suggesting that the decline in kidney function is unlikely to be attributed to SGLT2i. However, the likelihood of discontinuation increases proportionally with the degree of dip, reflecting safety concerns in real‐world clinical practice. Moreover, although the decline in kidney function may not be directly attributable to SGLT2i use, the concomitant use of diuretics in real‐world settings may promote dehydration and increase the risk of AKI, particularly in vulnerable patients. A manual chart review of the severe dipper group identified three patients with marked kidney function decline within 3 months of SGLT2i initiation (−39.2 [44.8%], −32.6 [34.2%] and −28.6 mL/min/1.73 m2 [33.4%]), which persisted throughout the follow‐up period. Notably, all three patients received concomitant loop diuretic therapy. Similarly, a previous case report documented SGLT2i‐related AKI, mainly due to volume depletion 27 , suggesting the importance of exercising caution in frail patients or those with volume‐sensitive conditions.

The association between A‐fib and dipper classification could be explained by hemodynamic and neurohormonal mechanisms. A‐fib reduces effective cardiac output and renal perfusion pressure through impaired atrial contraction and irregular ventricular rate 28 , and chronic A‐fib sustains renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system activation, further reducing renal hemodynamic reserve 29 . Against this vulnerable background, the intraglomerular pressure reduction induced by SGLT2i may be amplified, resulting in a more pronounced early eGFR decline.

We also focused on the clinical implications of eGFR variability. Varying degrees of eGFR variability were commonly observed 30 . Several studies have documented eGFR variability and its association with clinical outcomes. Greater variability has been linked to increased risks of cardiovascular events, mortality, and kidney disease progression 31 , 32 , 33 . In our analysis, greater eGFR variability was independently associated with an early decrease in eGFR. Future research is warranted to define the clinically relevant parameters of eGFR variability and their utility in patient risk stratification.

This study has some limitations. First, the overall sample size was relatively small, particularly in the severely dipped subgroup. Second, this was a retrospective observational study using data extracted from the CDW, and as such, some data were not measured; for example, not all patients had consistent kidney function testing at 1, 3, and 6 months following SGLT2i initiation. Missing data further reduces the number of analyzable cases at specific time points. Third, owing to the observational nature of this study, causality could not be established. For instance, although a greater decline in eGFR was observed in patients who discontinued SGLT2i, we cannot guarantee that the observed decline was directly attributable to the discontinuation of SGLT2i. Nevertheless, this study is among the first to assess the clinical significance of an early eGFR drop in a real‐world setting. To date, most studies on eGFR dip have been conducted as post‐hoc analyses of randomized controlled trials, with only a single study evaluating this phenomenon in a real‐world clinical context 20 . While this previous study analyzed Veterans Affairs electronic healthcare databases and concluded that an early eGFR dip was not harmful, it did not involve a meticulous case‐level review. We employed comprehensive statistical analyses and manual chart reviews to address these limitations.

In summary, an early eGFR decrease following SGLT2i initiation occurred in approximately 20% of patients. Risk factors include advanced age, A‐fib, high baseline eGFR variability, and loop diuretic use, whereas a higher baseline eGFR may be protective. Although an early dip does not generally portend poor kidney outcomes, clinicians should be cautious when managing patients with frailty or volume‐depletion risk, as adverse outcomes may occur in these subpopulations.
