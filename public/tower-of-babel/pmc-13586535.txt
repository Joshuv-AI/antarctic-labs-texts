A Zur-dependent regulatory RNA involved in maintaining zinc homeostasis in Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Mathilde Charbonnier, Hugo Racine, Samuel Probst-Lotze, Jana N Radin, Gustavo Rios-Delgado, Liangyu Lyu, Hannah M Laster, Maximilian P Kohl, Rafał Mazgaj, Marion Blum, Virginie Marchand, Johana Chicher, Pascale Romby, Stefano Marzi, Isabelle Caldelari, Jai J Tree, Kevin J Waldron, Jeffrey M Boyd, Julien Y Dutheil, Thomas E Kehl-Fie, David Lalaouna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) are key drivers of bacterial adaptation to environmental fluctuations, including iron and manganese restriction imposed by the host. This study explored the repertoire of sRNAs produced by the human pathogen Staphylococcus aureus in response to metal limitation. Two sRNAs were identified, S1077 and ZinS, that were regulated by zinc (Zn) availability. Further investigations revealed that, similar to the cnt operon from which it derives, S1077 synthesis is controlled by the transcription factors Zur and Fur. In contrast, zinS transcription is solely repressed by Zur. RNA affinity purification identified several ZinS-associated mRNAs encoding Zn-dependent enzymes, including the alcohol dehydrogenase Adh, whose synthesis is negatively regulated by ZinS. Loss of ZinS does not alter staphylococcal metal accumulation, suggesting a role in a Zn-sparing response. Remarkably, zinS also encodes a small peptide, ZinP. Genomic analysis shows that the regulatory portion of zinS emerged from the 3′ untranslated region of zinP and is conserved in S. aureus and closely related species, whereas zinP is also found in phylogenetically distant organisms. Our findings demonstrate that sRNAs facilitate bacterial adaptation to Zn limitation, and that genetic exchange and subsequent neofunctionalization have enabled S. aureus to adapt to metal-restricted environments.

Introduction

In the environment and during infection, bacteria must cope with the scarcity of essential nutrients, including transition metals [1]. During infection, Staphylococcus aureus and other invaders experience acute iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and zinc (Zn) starvation due to the active restriction of metal availability by the host, a defense known as nutritional immunity. Survival within the host and other metal-restricted environments requires adaptive changes that are mediated by a combination of proteinaceous and RNA-based regulatory mechanisms [2, 3]. While proteinaceous regulators are known to contribute to the ability of S. aureus and other microbes to adapt to changes in Zn availability, it is unknown if RNA-based regulatory mechanisms are used to respond to Zn limitation.

Zn is an essential trace element playing a pivotal role in numerous biological processes, such as enzymatic catalysis and protein structural stability [4]. The host leverages this essentiality to control infection through the action of metal-sequestering proteins such as calprotectin (CP) [5, 6]. This S100 protein can be found at sites of infection at concentrations exceeding 1 mg/ml [7]. CP can sequester a variety of transition metals, including Zn, which it binds with picomolar affinity [8–11]. This tight binding allows CP to severely restrict Zn availability in culture and during infection [9, 12]. Staphylococcus aureus and other microbes, including all ESKAPE pathogens, regulate their response to Zn limitation via Zur, a member of the ferric uptake regulator family [13]. Zur is a zinc-dependent transcriptional repressor with a femtomolar sensitivity [14]. Under zinc-replete conditions, Zn-bound Zur recognizes specific DNA sequences known as Zur boxes in the promoter region of target genes, thereby inhibiting their transcription. In the absence of Zn, Zur dissociates from DNA, leading to the expression of Zn transporters to enhance Zn acquisition and adaptations that enable the preservation of essential processes [15]. In S. aureus, the Zur regulon encompasses genes involved in the synthesis of the Zn acquisition system (acdA, acdBC-zur, and the cnt operon), Zn-independent paralogs of the ribosomal proteins S14 (rpsN2) and L33 (rpmG2), and the Zn-binding GTPase ZagA (COG0523 subfamily of putative Zn chaperones) [16–19]. Although the exact Zur regulon varies between species, a similar set of processes is controlled by this regulator [13].

While bacteria must cope with metal limitation, they must also contend with Zn intoxication within the host and the environment [20]. In excess, Zn can inhibit the uptake of other metals and disrupt bacterial metabolism by displacing essential metal ions in enzymes [21, 22], a phenomenon known as mismetallation. The dual nature of zinc necessitates mechanisms for the strict maintenance of its homeostasis in bacterial cells, leading bacteria, including S. aureus, to possess regulators and systems intended to prevent overaccumulation. Elevated levels of Zn are sensed by CzrA, a member of the ArsR family. Unlike Zur, the apo form of CzrA has higher DNA binding affinity than the Zn-bound form. Consequently, apo-CzrA acts as a repressor of its regulon, including the Zn efflux pump CzrB under Zn-limiting conditions [23, 24]. Conversely, in the presence of Zn, Zn-bound CzrA becomes inactive, inducing the expression of its regulon and of CzrB [25]. Another mechanism preventing damage caused by Zn is cytosolic buffering via Zn-binding proteins, which reduces the concentration of free Zn ions [13, 26]. In S. aureus, inactivation of the Mg transporter MgtE enhances Zn tolerance, likely because MgtE can transport Zn [27].

Numerous studies have shown the crucial role of regulatory small RNAs (sRNAs) in coordinating the bacterial response to Fe and Mn limitation [2, 3]. However, despite the critical importance of Zn, there are no sRNAs known to facilitate the bacterial response to Zn starvation. In S. aureus, IsrR and RsaC are involved in the Fe- and Mn-sparing response, respectively [28–31]. The expression of both sRNAs is directly controlled by the respective metal-sensing transcription factors, Fur (Fe) and MntR (Mn) [26, 32]. This leads to the hypothesis that if sRNAs that modulate Zn utilization exist, they should be regulated by Zur. Leveraging this idea, this study found that metal starvation highly induces the expression of two Zur-dependent regulatory sRNAs, S1077 and ZinS (formerly RsaX20 [33]). Subsequent investigation revealed that the synthesis of S1077 sRNA is induced by Zn and Fe depletion, whereas ZinS responds specifically to Zn starvation. ZinS controls the synthesis of several Zn-dependent enzymes, including the alcohol dehydrogenase Adh, likely reducing Zn usage in response to Zn starvation. ZinS is also a dual-function sRNA, coding for a small peptide called ZinP. Finally, we will illustrate how genome comparison reveals an evolutionary trajectory of zinS enabling S. aureus to adapt to metal restriction during infection.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

Bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1A. For plasmid construction, PCR fragments amplified by oligonucleotides indicated in Supplementary Table S1B were digested with respective restriction enzymes and ligated into a similarly digested vector. To create the transcriptional reporter fusion, the zinS promoter and the first 19 nucleotides of zinS were PCR amplified using the indicated primers (Supplementary Table S1B) and cloned into pAH5 [34]. To generate the translational reporter, the 5′ UTR and the first 2 codons of ZinP were PCR amplified using the indicated primers (Supplementary Table S1B) and cloned into pCN52 [35] under control of the pHELP promoter [36]. Plasmids isolated from E. coli TOP10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were then transferred into indicated backgrounds. Escherichia coli strain IM08B [37] was used as recipient strain for plasmid amplification and to efficiently transform S. aureus cells.

Staphylococcus aureus strains were isolated on Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) or blood agar plates. Overnight cultures were diluted 50-fold in NRPMI medium to induce metal starvation. To prepare NRPMI medium, 1% casamino acids (MP Biomedicals) and 5% Chelex-100 resin (Sigma–Aldrich) were added to Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI; Sigma–Aldrich), then mixed for 6 h at room temperature. The resulting medium was sterile-filtered and supplemented with metal ions at the following concentrations: 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µM CaCl2, 25 µM ZnCl2, 25 µM MnCl2, and 10 µM FeSO4 (Sigma–Aldrich). The final concentrations of metal ions in NRPMI supplemented with MgCl2 and CaCl2 were measured by ICP: Zn 0.08 µM; Mn 0.01 µM; Fe 0.245 µM; Cu 0.0062 µM; Mg 1.1 mM; Ca 113.58 µM. Cells were then grown with shaking at 37°C (180 rpm, 5:1 flask-to-medium ratio). Antibiotics were added as needed: 100 μg/ml of ampicillin, 10 μg/ml of chloramphenicol, and 10 μg/ml of erythromycin.

Gene deletion

The deletion of the zinS (ΔzinS), zur (Δzur), fur (Δfur), and mntR (ΔmntR) genes in S. aureus HG001 strain was performed using the pMAD suicide vector [38]. Upstream and downstream regions of the respective gene (≈600 nts) were amplified by PCR (Supplementary Table S1B) and cloned into pMAD plasmid. The obtained plasmids were then transferred to the IM08B recipient strain and finally electroporated into S. aureus HG001. Mutant strains were selected as previously described [38]. Cells were grown at restrictive temperature (44°C) and incubated at 28°C to favor a double crossover. The gene deletion was verified by PCR and sequencing. The same strategy was used to insert the MS2 sequence at the 5′ end of zinS gene.

Phylogenomic and molecular evolution analyses

Staphylococcal species from Barwinska-Sendra et al. [39] were selected for complete genome alignment. The reference genomes of each species were retrieved using the NCBI command line interface (CLI) tools. The genome of Mamaliicoccus vitulinus (formerly S. vitulinus) has 223 contigs and was discarded. All sequences annotated as plasmids were ignored. A pairwise distance matrix was generated using the alignment-free phylogeny reconstruction method Andi [40]. A phylogenetic tree was subsequently generated with FastME [41] using default parameters. The distance-based phylogenetic tree was used as a guide tree for the cactus aligner [42] to produce a multiple genome alignment. A genome alignment in MAF format was then created after projection on the HG001 strain and further processed using MafFilter [43]. The genome alignment was refined in blocks of maximum 10 kb using the MAFFT aligner [44]. Synteny blocks containing all selected species and no duplicated sequences were retained. Site-filtering was applied by sliding a window of 10 nt by 1 nt. Any window containing more than two gaps in at least two species was discarded, and the corresponding synteny block was split. Blocks in synteny distant by <100 nt were then merged, and missing positions were replaced by N, resulting in masking of ambiguously aligned regions. Local phylogenetic trees were then inferred by removing blocks <500 nt long and splitting the remaining blocks into windows with an average size of 5 kb. A tree was built with the PhyML method [45], using the HKY85 [46] nucleotide substitution model and a four-class gamma distribution of substitution rates, using the best tree from nearest-neighbor interchange (NNI) and subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) topology search algorithms. The 748 resulting local trees were given as input to the ASTRAL-IV [47] method from the ASTER package (https://github.com/chaoszhang/ASTER) in order to get a phylogeny, which was subsequently rooted using the MAD method [48]. The resulting phylogeny slightly differed from the alignment-free guide tree and was consistent with that in Barwinska-Sendra et al. [39]. It was used as a new guide tree to the cactus aligner to generate a new genome alignment. This final alignment was used to extract the zinS locus sequence.

A blast nucleotide database was created with all complete genomes to look at possible paralogs of the zinS locus. The ZinP amino acid sequence was used as a query for the tblastn program. All matching sequences with an E-value lower than 1 × 10⁻⁶ were extracted and aligned with the ClustalO aligner [49]. The alignment was converted to PAML input file format using the Bio++ Program Suite [50]; the HG001 sequence was discarded for this analysis, as it is identical to the reference sequence for S. aureus. The codeml program from the PAML 4.10.7 package was used to fit models of codon sequence evolution [51]. Several models allowing for different selective pressure along the tree were serially tested to select the best model fit.

Small RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from cells grown in NRPMI supplemented with 1 mM MgCl2 and 100 µM CaCl2 or RPMI + 1% CA (control) and harvested at OD600nm = 1.5. RNA was treated with DNase I (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. RNA was selected using the PippinPrep instrument (80–300 nts; Sage Science). Note that the unexpected detection of large regulatory RNAs like RsaC is due to the presence of shorter fragments. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent). The cDNA library was obtained using the NEBNext® Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina® and sequenced with the NextSeq 2000 system (Illumina). The ligation of adaptors to unfragmented RNA was performed after treatment with RppH (NEB). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the READemption pipeline [52]. Reads were mapped to sRNA features only. Small RNA boundaries were defined using the S. aureus strain RN1 annotation from the Staphylococcus regulatory RNA database [53]. The RN1 sRNA annotation was transferred to the HG001 genome (NZ_CP018205.1) [54] using RATT [55] and the Strain.global setting. Data were visualized using VolcaNoseR [56]. Results are representative of three independent experiments.

Gel retardation assays

Transcription start sites were determined according to Koch et al. [57], with the exception of zinS. An arbitrary value of −200 nt was used when no clear +1 was observed. PCR fragments containing T7-5′adh (from nt −276 to +564, nt −276 to +168), T7-3′adh (from nt +540 to +1115), T7-isaB (full-length; from nt −39 to +589), T7-tdh (from nt −67 to +662), T7-hslO (from nt −200 to +676), T7-mntR (full-length; from nt −20 to +713), T7-sepF (from nt −200 to +568), and BamHI-digested pUT7 plasmids containing 5′aur (from nt −40 to +745) or 3′aur (from nt +718 to 1660) were used as DNA templates for in vitro transcription with homemade T7 RNA polymerase. T7-zinS was cloned into pJET1.2/blunt plasmid (Thermo Fisher). The ZinS mutants (mut1, mut2, del1) were obtained using the QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis method (Agilent Technologies) following manufacturer’s instructions. Oligonucleotides containing the desired mutations are listed in Supplementary Table S1B. The plasmid pJET-T7-zinS was used as template to generate the mutated plasmids. RNAs were finally purified and radiolabeled when required [28]. 5′-radiolabeled ZinS (20 000 cpm/sample) and the aforementioned cold RNAs were separately denatured at 90°C in GR-buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 60 mM KCl, 40 mM NH4Cl, 3 mM DTT), immediately cooled on ice for 1 min, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the presence of 10 mM MgCl2. The renatured RNAs were then mixed and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. The specificity of the interaction was tested by competition with cold ZinS and yeast tRNA when indicated. Finally, the samples were loaded onto a 6% polyacrylamide gel under non-denaturing conditions. Gels were exposed to Fuji X-ray films and developed in an Optimax X-ray film processor. The results are representative of two independent experiments.

Northern blot analysis

Samples were collected at specified OD600nm. After centrifugation, the bacterial pellets were resuspended in 1 ml of RNA Pro Solution from the FastRNA Pro Blue kit (MP Biomedicals). Bacteria were transferred into a 2-ml Lysis Matrix B tube (MP Biomedicals) and then mechanically disrupted for 40 s at 6 m/s using the FastPrep-24 homogenizer system (MP Biomedicals). The tubes were centrifuged at 16 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove beads and cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5-ml tube and extracted with an equal volume of cold chloroform and chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (19:1). RNA was precipitated by adding one-tenth volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and 3 volumes of cold absolute ethanol at −20°C overnight. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 16 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, washed with 0.5 ml of cold 80% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in milli-Q water. Total RNA (5 µg) was loaded onto a 6% polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed at 120 V. RNA was then transferred to a Hybond N + nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Membranes were hybridized with specific digoxygenin (DIG)-labeled probes, which were prepared using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche). Luminescent detection was performed using Anti-Digoxigenin-AP, Fab fragments, and CDP-Star (Roche). The RiboRuler Low Range RNA Ladder (Thermo Scientific) was used to determine the length of S1077-related bands. Results represent at least two independent experiments.

Calprotectin growth assays with transcriptional and translational fusions

CP growth assays were performed as described previously [58]. Briefly, overnight cultures (grown 16–18 h in 5 ml of NRPMI supplemented with 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µM CaCl2 and 10 µM FeSO4 in 15 ml conical tubes at 37°C on a roller drum) were used directly and diluted 1:100 into 96-well round-bottom plates containing 100 µl of growth medium [38% TSB and 62% calprotectin buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2)] supplemented with 1 µM MnCl2 and 1 µM ZnSO4 in the presence of varying concentrations of CP. Throughout CP C→S variants that do not alter metal binding or antimicrobial activity were used [59]. In the TSB-CP buffer growth media, the concentrations of divalent ions were as previously described [9]: Fe 0.7 µM, Zn 0.6 µM, Mn 40.6 nM, Cu 8.9 µM, Mg 46 µM, and Co 10.8 µM. For all assays, the bacteria were incubated with orbital shaking (180 rpm) at 37°C and growth was measured by assessing optical density (OD600nm) every 2 h. Prior to measuring optical density, the 96-well plates were vortexed. Expression of zinS was assessed by measuring yellow fluorescence (excitation and emission wavelengths of 505 nm and 535 nm, respectively), normalized to OD600nm, as previously described [12]. ZinP translation was assessed by measuring green fluorescence (excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 528, respectively), normalized to OD600nm. Data correspond to the mean of three independent experiments ± SD.

Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) activity assay

Overnight cultures were diluted to an OD600nm of 0.05 in 3 ml of fresh TSB medium and cells were cultured with shaking (250 rpm) at 37°C for 16 h. One milliliter of cell suspension was pelleted by centrifugation and washed with 0.05 M phosphate buffer pH 8. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µl of phosphate buffer containing 20 µg each of DNase I (ITW Reagents) and lysostaphin (AMBI). After 1 h of incubation at 37°C, cell debris was separated from lysates by centrifugation. The reaction mixture (1 ml) contained potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.5 (83.35 mM), 0.666 mM NAD+, 150 mM ethanol, and 40 µl of cell-free lysate. The formation of NADH (ε340 = 6220 M−1 cm−1) was followed spectrophotometrically at A340nm [60] for 3 min using a Cary 60 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Agilent). Protein concentrations of the cell-free lysates were determined by Bradford protein colorimetric assay (Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate) modified for a 96-well plate. Data correspond to the mean of three independent experiments ± SD.

Primer extension assays

Primer extension assays were conducted using 30 µg of DNase I (Roche)-treated total RNA, incubated with a 5′-radiolabeled oligonucleotide (Supplementary Table S1B), 2.5 mM dNTPs, and 4 units of AMV reverse transcriptase (NEB). The RNA template was removed by adding 3 µl of 3 M KOH and 20 µl of Destroy Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 0.5% SDS, 7.5 mM EDTA) and incubating for 3 min at 90°C, followed by 1 h at 37°C. The samples were then precipitated and run on a denaturing 10% polyacrylamide gel alongside a sequencing ladder. The sequencing ladder was produced using a DNA template with ddNTPs added to terminate the reaction catalyzed by 2 units of Vent (exo−) DNA polymerase (NEB). PCR was performed with the following conditions: 1 min at 95°C, 1 min at 52°C, and 1 min at 72°C for 25 cycles. The results are representative of two independent experiments. Note that the presence of additional bands can be attributed to premature termination of reverse transcription (e.g. strong secondary structure or presence of RNA modifications).

Toeprinting assays

Toeprinting assays were performed as described by Fechter et al. [61]. One pmol of in vitro transcribed ZinS sRNA and 4 pmol of radiolabeled primer were mixed in a total volume of 9 µl, denatured at 90°C, re-folded on ice for 1 min, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min before the addition of 1 µl of TP10X+ buffer (100 mM MgCl2, 200 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 600 mM NH4Cl and 10 mM DTT). In parallel, purified S. aureus 30S ribosomes were adjusted to 2 µM in TP1X+ buffer and pre-incubated at 37°C for 15 min. Next, 2 µl of mRNA/primer, 2 µl of 30S, 1 µl of 50 mM MgCl2 and 1 µl of TP10X− buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 600 mM NH4Cl and 10 mM DTT) were mixed in a total volume of 10 µl. Increasing concentrations of wild-type (WT) and mutant ZinS (50−400 nM) were added when indicated. Samples were incubated for 10 min at 37°C before the addition of 1 µl of 20 μM initiator tRNAfMet. After 5 min at 37°C, 2 µl of RT mix (0.2 mM dNTPs and 1 U/µl AMV RT in AMV RT buffer 1×; Promega) were added. After 30 min at 37°C, cDNA was extracted using PCI (Roth) and precipitated by addition of 2.5 volumes of absolute ethanol and 0.1 volume of 3 M NaOAc pH 5.2. Pellets were washed, air-dried, and resuspended in urea blue loading buffer. Radioactivity (cpm) was measured and equal amounts resolved on a 10% denaturing polyacrylamide gel. The results are representative of two independent experiments.

3′RACE

Total RNA was treated with DNase I (Roche). A ligation step was performed to add a 3′-blocked adaptor to the 3′ end of RNA [62]. Four micrograms of total RNA were incubated with 10 µM adaptor at 90°C for 2 min, followed by cooling on ice. Then, 1× T4 RNA ligase buffer (NEB), 1 mM ATP, 100 U RNasin Plus (Promega) and 20 U T4 RNA ligase 1 ssRNA (NEB) were added, and the ligation reaction was performed overnight at 16°C. RT-PCR was performed using ZinS- and adaptor-specific oligonucleotides and the One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Qiagen). Nested PCR was used to increase specificity and yield. The PCR products were then cloned into the pJET 1.2 vector. Finally, the resulting plasmids were sequenced (Eurofins GATC).

ICP-OES analysis

Samples were collected at OD600nm = 1. After centrifugation, the pellets were washed twice with 0.1 mM EDTA DPBS and twice with DPBS. Dry cell pellets were digested with concentrated nitric acid (65%, Merck) and heated at 70°C for 16 h. Samples were then diluted 10-fold into a 1% nitric acid solution containing 50 µg/l iridium (internal standard). Calibration curves were generated using matrix-matched standard solutions containing Mn, Fe, Zn, Cu, Mg, Ca, and S. All standards and samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) on a Thermo iCAP PRO instrument (RF power 1250 W, with nebulizer gas flow 0.5 l/min, auxiliary gas flow 0.5 l/min, and cool gas flow 13.5 l/min argon). The concentration of each element was determined by comparing to the standard curve using Qtegra ISDS Software (Thermo) and normalized to the intracellular level of sulfur (ppb). Data correspond to the mean of four independent experiments ± SD.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using a two-way ANOVA followed by a Sidak’s multiple comparisons test or a Mann–Whitney test (Prism software). All statistical details of the experiments can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Identification of sRNAs induced by metal limitation

Knowing that regulatory RNAs are involved in the iron- and manganese-sparing responses in S. aureus [28–31], we hypothesized that sRNAs must also exist for other metals, including zinc. Therefore, S. aureus was grown in Chelex-treated RPMI + 1% casamino acids supplemented with 1 mM MgCl2 and 100 µM CaCl2 (NRPMI; metal-depleted condition) and RPMI + 1% casamino acids (control condition). Size-selected RNAs (80–300 nts) were extracted, and adaptors were ligated to the unfragmented, RppH-treated RNAs. The RNAs were then subjected to differential RNA sequencing analysis (Fig. 1). As expected, the synthesis of IsrR and RsaC was triggered in response to metal starvation. The modest increase of RsaC (1116 nts) level compared to IsrR (174 nts) is likely explained by the size selection carried out prior to sequencing. In total, 42 RNA transcripts were up-regulated (e.g. S1077, ZinS, RsaOW2) and 42 were down-regulated (e.g. RsaI, RsaH, SprA2, SprC) in response to metal depletion (log2FC>|1|; −log10(padj) > 1.3). The whole list is presented in Supplementary Table S2. S1077 and ZinS are remarkably responsive to metal starvation (log2FC = 7.2), and both are located near genes that facilitate staphylococcal resistance to host-imposed Zn and Mn starvation. S1077 is present in the 5′ UTR of the cnt operon, responsible for the biosynthesis and transport of the metallophore staphylopine [12, 16]. The presence of zinS, immediately adjacent to the gene encoding Mn-independent phosphoglycerate mutase GpmA [63], suggests it could be involved in coordinating the staphylococcal response to Mn availability. However, we also noticed the presence of a putative Zur-box upstream of ZinS, suggesting a role in Zn homeostasis.

Data were visualized using VolcaNoseR [56] and are representative of three independent experiments. The following thresholds were applied: −log10(padj) > 1.3; log2FC>|1|.

S1077 is a 5′UTR-derived sRNA induced in response to Zn and Fe limitation

Given its integration in the 5′UTR of the cnt operon, we first determined S1077 boundaries using primer extension assays (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S1A) and rapid amplification of cDNA ends (3′RACE) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The obtained results fit with the 5′ and 3′ ends identified by our sRNA-seq analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1C), suggesting that S1077 is a 220-nt-long sRNA (Fig. 2B). We then showed that the 5′ end of S1077 is triphosphorylated, thereby protecting S1077 from degradation by the Terminator 5′-Phosphate-Dependent Exonuclease (Supplementary Fig. S1D). This indicates that S1077 and the cnt operon share the same promoter. A longer transcript of ~450 nt was also detected, likely containing two repeat motifs (Fig. 2B). We hypothesize that S1077 arises from a processing event or premature transcription termination. While we cannot rule out the possibility that S1077 is a riboswitch, we have not detected alternative pairings that are a hallmark of such cis-acting regulatory element. Further experiments are required to identify the origin of the 3′ end of S1077. The secondary structure of S1077 was predicted based on the experimentally determined 5′ and 3′ ends (Fig. 2C).

To check whether S1077 sRNA is regulated similarly to the cnt operon, levels of S1077 were assessed using Northern blots. Total RNA was extracted from HG001 WT strain grown in NRPMI medium supplemented with Fe, Zn, or Mn (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S1E). Using a S1077-specific probe, we observed two clear bands. The lower, main band corresponds to the expected length (220 nts), while the upper band (≈500 nts) potentially contains two repeated sequences, previously identified between S1077 and cntK [16] (Fig. 2B). Although an increase of their levels was observed in the absence of Zn, the maximum was reached when depleting Zn and Fe. In agreement with this observation, we noticed an increase in S1077 levels in Δzur and Δfur mutant strains grown in RPMI + 1% casamino acids (Fig. 2E).

To assess the potential regulatory functions of S1077, we used the CopraRNA algorithm to predict conserved S1077-RNA base pairing interactions [66]. Among the best candidates, we identified three classes of mRNAs related to metal homeostasis (Supplementary Table S3). The first class includes mRNAs encoding metal ABC transporters for staphyloferrins A (htsAB) and B (sirC), the metallophore staphylopine (cntD), and manganese (mntA). The second class encodes proteins involved in iron homeostasis, including the Fe-S cluster synthesis system SufAB, the ferrochelatase HemH, and FepA for heme iron utilization. The third class encodes Zn-containing proteins, including murT (lipi II isoglutaminyl synthase), phoB (alkaline phosphatase), glpQ (glycerophosphodiester phosphodiesterase), pepT (a peptidase), and rpsZ (ribosomal protein S14), suggesting a potential role in Zn-sparing responses.

All in all, our data support the involvement of the Zur and Fur transcription factors in the control of S1077 synthesis, similar to the cnt operon [12, 16], and highlight potential crosstalk between Fe, Zn, and Mn homeostasis.

ZinS and the encoded peptide ZinP are induced in response to Zn limitation

ZinS (previously annotated as RsaX20) was identified in a high-throughput screen of intergenic regions with predicted RNA structure [33], but little is known about its boundaries, regulation, or function. We initially determined the boundaries of this putative regulatory RNA using primer extension assays and 3′RACE. The identified and characterized transcription start site (5′PPP) of ZinS (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S2A) was consistent with both the sRNA-seq analysis and previous dRNA-seq analysis [67] (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Despite the substantial variability observed at the 3′ end (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S2C), the longest observed form of ZinS ends with a well-defined Rho-independent terminator containing a polyU stretch (Fig. 3B and C) and was confirmed by a previous Term-seq analysis [67] (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Remarkably, it corresponds to a bidirectional transcription terminator (predicted using the TransTermHP algorithm [68]), suggesting that the 3′ ends of zinS and gpmA may overlap. Using 3′RACE assays, we confirmed that both share 50 nts (Supplementary Fig. S2C and D). In total, this indicated that ZinS is a 274 nt long sRNA.

Given the uncertainty regarding the inducing metal, the transcription of ZinS was first evaluated in the presence of calprotectin (CP), observing induction at 120 µg/ml in both HG001 and Newman backgrounds (Fig. 3D). Because CP has broad metal specificity, we next determined which metal ZinS is responding to. We monitored the level of ZinS via Northern blot following HG001 WT strain growth in NRPMI medium supplemented with Zn, Mn, or Fe. A ZinS-specific signal was only observed in media lacking Zn (Fig. 3E). The same result was obtained using a promoter fusion (Supplementary Fig. S3A). We then deleted the genes encoding the two Zn import systems to block Zn acquisition and drastically reduce intracellular Zn concentrations. Loss of the Adc and staphylopine-Cnt Zn import systems, but not the MntABC and MntH Mn importers, led to a significant increase in transcription of the sRNA following growth in the presence of CP in Newman strain (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C). Therefore, zinS expression is strongly dependent on the intracellular concentration of Zn. The presence of a predicted Zur box suggests that ZinS responds directly to Zn availability via Zur. Consistent with this model, the loss of Zur led to constitutive expression of ZinS in metal-replete medium (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S3D). Considering the Zn-specific regulation, we renamed this sRNA ZinS, for Zinc-responsive small RNA.

A short open reading frame encoding a small peptide of 34-amino acids (initially referred to as sRNA34; HG001_02453 or SAOUHSC_02702 in NCTC8325 background) was detected within ZinS using ribosome profiling [69]. This peptide was renamed ZinP (Figs 3B and 4A). Toeprinting assays confirmed binding of the 30S ribosomal subunit to the SD sequence upstream of the start codon (Fig. 4B), consistent with our previous findings using Retapamulin-enhanced ribosome profiling (Supplementary Fig. S4) [69]. To assess the translational regulation of zinP in vivo, we constructed a ZinP + 6-GFP translational fusion under the control of zinP promoter. Upon exposure to calprotectin, we observed a strong increase in fluorescence in both HG001 and Newman backgrounds (Fig. 4C), indicating that zinP translation was efficient under zinc-limiting conditions. Intriguingly, zinP homologues are frequently located in the vicinity of genes encoding the proteins ZnuA/ZinT or domains known to be part of Zn transporter system, potentially linking their function to Zn homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. S5). Although the role of ZinP remains to be elucidated, its predicted structure (Fig. 4A) shows notable similarity to the MntS peptide in E. coli, which inhibits Mn efflux by obstructing the channel of the MntP exporter [70]. However, we did not find reproducible growth defects when ZinP was overexpressed from a strong and constitutive promoter in the presence of toxic zinc levels (results not shown). The ZinP sequence reveals that the peptide is highly basic and amphipathic. These two properties suggest potential interactions with the phosphate backbone of nucleic acids and/or bacterial membrane.

Altogether, these results indicate that ZinS and the small peptide ZinP that it encodes are transcribed specifically in response to zinc starvation in a Zur-dependent manner.

ZinS alters the S. aureus proteome in response to Zn limitation

Small RNAs typically function by base-pairing with target mRNAs, affecting their stability and/or translation, thus providing an additional layer of post-transcriptional regulation. To gain a better understanding of how ZinS shapes the staphylococcal response to Zn limitation, we performed a mass spectrometry analysis of cytoplasmic proteins in the presence and absence of Zn. We compared the cellular content between the WT strain, the Δzur, ΔzinS, and Δzur ΔzinS deletion mutants grown in NRPMI medium supplemented with 25 µM MnCl2, 10 µM FeSO4 ± 25 µM ZnCl2. The whole proteomic analysis is presented in Supplementary Table S4.

We first examined the effect of Zn bioavailability by comparing the WT strain grown in the absence or presence of ZnCl2 (WT− versus WT+; Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table S4). 69 and 38 proteins were significantly positively and negatively regulated, respectively, in the absence of Zn. As expected, the two Zn acquisition systems (AdcABC and Cnt), the non-zinc-containing paralog of the ribosomal protein S14 (RpsN2), and the putative Zn chaperone ZagA were more abundant in the absence of Zn. Concomitantly the levels of several metal-bound proteins were reduced, such as the Zn-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase Adh, the copper-sensitive operon repressor CsoR, and the copper-exporting P-type ATPase A (CopA). These two examples highlight links between Zn and Cu homeostasis [72].

For the comparison between WT and Δzur in the presence of Zn, the abundance of 250 proteins changed upon the loss of Zur: 149 were reduced and 101 were enhanced (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table S4). Besides most of the previously known (e.g. ZagA, AdcA, Cnt) and in silico predicted targets (e.g. HG001_00792, HG001_02621–24; RegPrecise [73]) (Supplementary Table S5), Zur appears to negatively regulate the zinc-responsive transcription factor CzrA and the two Fe-staphyloferrin importer systems (SbnB, SfaD). However, no clear Zur-boxes [74] were identified upstream of the related genes, suggesting an indirect regulation. In contrast, Zur seems to promote the synthesis of several metalloproteins, most probably in an indirect manner. This includes Zn-dependent (e.g. Adh, the 30S ribosomal protein RpsZ), Cu-dependent (e.g. CsoR, the copper chaperone CsoZ), and Mn-dependent (MntC, the substrate-binding component of the Mn ABC transporter MntABC) proteins.

We then compared the cytoplasmic protein content in the WT and ΔzinS backgrounds in the absence of Zn (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Table S4). The levels of 93 proteins were significantly impacted by the presence of ZinS (WT); among them, 63 were decreased and 30 were enhanced. Interestingly, ZinS represses the synthesis of Zn-dependent (Adh, CzrA), but also Cu-dependent (CsoR), and Fe-dependent proteins [e.g. staphyloferrin A (Sfa) and B (Sbn), iron-regulated surface determinant (Isd), and uroporphyrinogen-III methyltransferase (UroM)], either directly or indirectly. Conversely, ZinS notably induced the production of the two secreted (phospho)lipases GehA and GehB, which bind Ca and Zn near their catalytic sites [75]. These two proteins contribute to infection by enhancing biofilm formation through the release of extracellular DNA and host cell invasion [76].

Although Zur regulates several essential genes, deletion of zur and/or zinS had no significant impact on growth, irrespective of the presence or absence of Zn (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B). In several pathogenic bacteria, deletion of the zur gene attenuates virulence, but this effect is not always the result of Zn deprivation or toxicity (for a review, [13]). Conversely, deletion of zur in S. aureus demonstrated no attenuation of virulence in a mouse skin-lesion model [18]. Regarding ZinS, possible functional redundancy may exist with S1077, which is produced under Zn-limiting conditions, and may be involved in the Zn-sparing response, as it is predicted to interact with mRNAs encoding Zn-containing enzymes (Supplementary Table S3).

Overall, our data suggest that intricate connections between metallosystems and ZinS function complement the regulon of Zur.

adh mRNA is a main target of ZinS sRNA

To identify the targets of ZinS sRNA, we performed MS2-affinity purification coupled with RNA sequencing (MAPS) [77, 78]. To avoid artefactual expression using a plasmid and preserve native regulation, the MS2 sequence was chromosomally fused to the 5′ end of the zinS gene. Crude extracts from MS2-zinS and WT (control) strains were collected at an OD600nm of 1 in NRPMI medium. Northern blots confirmed that the addition of the MS2 sequence did not alter ZinS levels, while control experiments confirmed that MS2-ZinS, but not ZinS, binds to an MS2 column (Supplementary Fig. S7A). After sequencing and data analysis, we obtained a list of 116 putative targets (FoldChange > 2, P-value <0.05; Supplementary Table S6). This includes mRNAs coding for Zn-dependent enzymes such as the alcohol dehydrogenase Adh, the metalloprotease Aureolysin (Aur), the L-threonine dehydrogenase (Tdh), and the molecular chaperone HslO.

To refine our analysis, we compared the MAPS results with those obtained with the CopraRNA algorithm (Supplementary Table S7) [66]. MAPS and CopraRNA analyses shared 24 mRNA targets (Supplementary Fig. S7B). We decided to validate the interaction between ZinS and seven putative targets based on their dependence on Zn (adh, aur, tdh, and hslO) and/or the overlap between MAPS and CopraRNA data (adh, mntR, isaB, and sepF; in the TOP20). We monitored the interaction between ZinS and putative targets using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA). We observed that ZinS sRNA forms stable complexes with adh (5′ part), aur (3′ part), hslO, and mntR mRNAs in vitro (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S7). Even at high concentrations, no significant binding was observed between ZinS and isaB, sepF, and tdh mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S7). These mRNAs might be false positives due to indirect co-purification with RNA-binding proteins or other RNAs. We also cannot exclude the possibility that protein co-factors may be required to facilitate pairing. Interestingly, using the CopraRNA algorithm (Supplementary Table S7), we noticed that most of the predicted pairing sites on ZinS are located from nts 190 to 230, a region that is mainly single-stranded and far from the zinP coding region (Fig. 3C).

Of the potential cytoplasmic targets, only Adh was identified by proteomics, MS2 pulldowns, and CopraRNA (Supplementary Fig. S7B and Supplementary Table S4). Therefore, we focused on further elucidating how ZinS impacts the expression and activity of Adh. The specificity of the ZinS:adh interaction was validated by competition with cold ZinS and yeast tRNA (Fig. 6A). One of the predicted interaction sites of ZinS on adh mRNA covers a large part of the ribosome binding site, including the coding sequence, suggesting that ZinS sequesters the SD sequence and interferes with ribosome binding and translation initiation (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S8C). Therefore, we used toeprinting assays to assess the impact of ZinS binding on adh translation initiation (Fig. 6C). We observed that ZinS efficiently inhibits the formation of the ribosomal initiation complex with adh mRNA, as evidenced by the significant decrease in the +16 toe-print signal in the presence of ZinS. We also noticed a second reverse transcription stop at nucleotide −9 in the presence of ZinS, which suggests that ZinS binds to the SD sequence as predicted in Fig. 6B. Since ZinS prevents the recruitment of ribosomes on adh mRNA in vitro, we monitored the impact of ZinS on Adh activity in vivo, by following the formation of NADH spectrophotometrically (Fig. 6D). In the presence of ZinS, upon deletion of zur, we observed a significant decrease in Adh activity, similar to that observed in an adh mutant. The return to WT levels in a zur zinS double mutant further demonstrates that ZinS negatively regulates Adh synthesis and activity. Because sRNA-dependent regulation often affects mRNA levels, we analyzed the steady-state levels of adh mRNA in WT and ∆zinS mutant strains (Supplementary Fig. S8C). The data showed that the expression of ZinS causes a significant decrease in adh mRNA.

To better analyze the molecular mechanism of repression, we introduced point mutations in ZinS (mut1, UU205 to AA and CC208 to GG; mut 2, CUC251 to GAG) to weaken the interaction with the ribosome binding site (Supplementary Fig. S8E); however, both ZinS mutants were able to bind the adh mRNA in vitro (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S8A). Surprisingly, prediction of the ZinS-adh pairings revealed that the same region of ZinS is able to form stable interactions with two distant regions of the mRNA (nt −23 to +60 and nt +433 to +500). This second binding site is supported by the presence of a ZinS-specific RT pause at position +500 (Supplementary Fig. S8D). However, a shorter version of 5′adh (−276 to +168) also had no effect on WT ZinS or ZinS mut2 binding. Only the deletion of the interhelical region (nt 196 to 228) of ZinS (del1) significantly impaired adh mRNA binding (Fig. 6B). Using toeprinting experiments, addition of either ZinS mut2 or ZinS del1 did not significantly inhibit the ribosome binding, and concomitantly the RT pause at position −9 was no longer observed (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S8B). This suggests that both the hairpin loop of the terminator of ZinS and the large unpaired interhelical region are both necessary to occlude ribosome binding. Finally, the Adh activity was analyzed in the ∆zinS mutant transformed with either an empty plasmid or a plasmid expressing WT ZinS or its mutant derivatives (Fig. 6D). Expression of ZinS del1 partially alleviated the inhibition, while ZinS mut1 reduced Adh activity similar to WT. Interestingly, mutations at the SD (mutSD, AGGAGG-9 to AGGAAA-9) of zinP, which abolishes the toe-print signal (Supplementary Fig. S4B), had no effect on Adh activity, supporting that the coding and trans-acting activities of ZinS are independent.

Altogether, our data suggest that at least two molecules of ZinS bind to adh mRNA. Both the hairpin loop of ZinS terminator and the large unpaired interhelical region are necessary to prevent the formation of the initiation ribosomal complex. In addition, the yield of adh mRNA strongly decreased when ZinS was produced, suggesting that the stability of the repressed mRNA was altered. It is tempting to propose that ZinS-dependent inhibition of translation may facilitate access of ZinS to the second binding site in the coding sequence of adh mRNA to induce its degradation (Supplementary Fig. S8G).

ZinS does not alter Zn acquisition

The observation that ZinS suppresses the expression of a Zn-utilizing enzyme in response to Zn limitation is reminiscent of IsrR and RsaC, which promote survival in metal-limited environments without altering the uptake of Fe or Mn [30, 31]. Therefore, the impact of ZinS on cellular metal levels was evaluated using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) following growth in NRPMI supplemented with Fe and Mn ± Zn (Fig. 7). The WT and the zinS mutant strains accumulated comparable levels of all metals tested, including Zn, Fe, and Mn. When combined with the suppression of a non-essential Zn-containing enzyme (Fig. 6), these results support a role for ZinS mediating a Zn-sparing response in S. aureus.

ZinS and ZinP peptide emerged via horizontal gene transfer and neofunctionalization

An initial analysis suggested that the prevalence of ZinS is restricted to a subgroup of the staphylococci. Although ZinS is only highly conserved in S. aureus and closely related strains such as S. schweitzeri (96% sequence identity) and S. argenteus (97%), the 5′ part corresponding to the ZinP protein-coding region is conserved in other staphylococci species (Fig. 8A). Therefore, using a multiple-genome alignment of staphylococcal species, the underlying species phylogeny was reconstructed, and orthologous regions of the zinP locus were identified. This analysis places zinP origin in the common ancestor of S. aureus and S. simulans (Fig. 8B). A tblastn search also revealed the occurrence of a ZinP protein at a distinct location in Mammaliicoccus lentus, albeit without a start codon. A global analysis showed that ZinP is also conserved in several organisms such as Enterococcus spp., Tetragenococcus spp., Granulicatella spp., and Streptococcus spp. (Fig. 8B), hinting that ZinP may have been acquired by horizontal gene transfer from phylogenetically distant organisms. We also do not exclude that these distant organisms acquired zinP from Staphylococcus species.

Evolution of the zinP/S gene. (A) Sequence alignment of the region orthologous to the zinS locus in S. aureus. The upper brace indicates the ZinP coding region. (B) Phylogeny of staphylococci species inferred from complete genome alignment, showing the similarity searches of the ZinP coding region. The tree branches are colored according to the inferred ratio of nonsynonymous over synonymous substitutions (dN/dS), according to the model that best fit the data. See also Supplementary Fig. S9.

The regulatory part of ZinS (nt 154–274) is contained within the 3′UTR of zinP. The alignment reveals that this region is highly conserved in closely related Staphylococcus species, including S. aureus, S. argenteus, S. schweitzeri, and S. simiae. In the sister clade comprising S. capitis, S. epidermidis, and S. warneri, the 3′UTR is highly divergent, while in the other species, the region was not detected (Fig. 8A). These observations suggest that the 3′UTR was present in the common ancestor of S. simiae and related species or that multiple independent loss or degeneration events have occurred. We favor the first hypothesis for a neofunctionalization, which most probably occurred in two steps: first in the common ancestor of the S. warneri group and second in the common ancestor of S. simiae group (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S9).

The analysis of the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) allows the inference of the selective constraints under which a protein has been evolving. In the absence of selection, the two types of nucleotide substitutions occur at the same rate. When functionally constrained by a coding sequence; however, non-synonymous mutations are selected against, resulting in a lower non-synonymous substitution rate than the synonymous rate, yielding a ratio below 1. We fitted a series of increasingly complex models of codon sequence evolution using maximum likelihood, allowing distinct parts of the phylogeny to evolve under distinct constraint levels and have specific dN/dS ratios. All ratios were <0.11, and even <0.01 in S. aureus, a strong signature of protein functionality (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

In this study, we discovered and characterized two novel sRNAs induced by zinc starvation: S1077 and ZinS. S1077 is a 220-nt-long sRNA originating from the 5′UTR of the cnt operon. Its synthesis is controlled by both Zur and Fur, like the staphylopine-Cnt system, whereas the 274-nt-long sRNA ZinS is under the exclusive control of Zur. Besides S1077 and ZinS, 40 predicted sRNAs were activated in response to metal starvation (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2). This includes the effector of the agr quorum-sensing system RNAIII [79], confirming recent findings showing its enhanced expression in the presence of calprotectin [19]. Conversely, 42 sRNAs were repressed, such as RsaI and SprC. The catabolite control protein A (CcpA)‐repressed sRNA RsaI is a key actor in the metabolic switch occurring during glycolysis. It primarily regulates glucose uptake, catabolism, and biofilm formation, potentially providing a link between metal homeostasis and carbon metabolism [80]. The importance of sRNAs in fine-tuning metabolism in response to metal availability is evidenced by ZinS suppression of Adh, which contributes to fermentative metabolism. Interestingly, SprC sRNA reduces the synthesis of the Zn efflux pump CzrB [81]. SprC was previously described as involved in resistance to oxidative stress, immune escape, and attenuation of S. aureus virulence [82]. However, its role in Zn homeostasis remains unclear, as the SprC-mediated regulation of CzrB is detrimental to S. aureus in the presence of zinc toxicity [81] and is only known to be negatively controlled by SarA, which does not respond to zinc [83].

The role of sRNAs was previously reported for Fe homeostasis in a number of other microbes, where the tandem pair of the Fur protein and RyhB-like sRNAs critically modulate the response to Fe limitation [3]. In S. aureus, Fe, Mn, and Zn homeostasis are all controlled by a tandem of transcription factor and sRNA: Fur-IsrR [29, 30], MntR-RsaC [28], and Zur-ZinS. A common characteristic of the Fe and Mn regulatory circuits is that transcription factors control transport systems, while sRNAs contribute to metal-sparing by suppressing the synthesis of metal-utilizing proteins. For example, RyhB-like sRNAs (e.g. RyhB in E. coli, PrrF in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, FsrA in Bacillus subtilis, and IsrR in S. aureus) inhibit the synthesis of many Fe-containing enzymes, notably involved in the TCA cycle, such as aconitase and succinate dehydrogenase [30, 84–88]. RyhB-like and RsaC sRNAs also share a similar strategy to spare their cognate metal: both interfere with the synthesis of metal-utilizing superoxide dismutases [28, 85, 86]. Interestingly, the two sRNAs are both required for infection, showing that metal-sparing responses are important for S. aureus infection [29, 31, 89]. Upon Zn starvation, ZinS negatively regulates Zn-containing proteins, including alcohol dehydrogenase (Adh). This suggests that this sRNA also activates a metal-sparing response. We also do not exclude that functional redundancy existed between S1077, IsrR, and ZinS, as S1077 is predicted to interact with mRNAs encoding Fe- or Zn-containing proteins (Supplementary Table S3). Furthermore, ZinS could have a broader role in metal homeostasis and virulence via the regulation of the Mn-dependent transcription factor MntR, as it represses the synthesis of MntABC transporter, which confers the ability of S. aureus to compete with the host for Mn during infection. MntABC is also required not only for nutritional immunity but also for other stresses imposed by the host’s defense during infection [90]. Additionally, ZinS regulates the synthesis of several virulence factors, such as the metalloprotease Aureolysin, involved in immune evasion [91], and by enhancing the levels of two Zn/Ca-binding lipases, GehA/GehB, which promote biofilm formation through DNA release [76]. Zinc is also important for biofilm formation, as it activates the adhesive properties of the Zn-binding protein SasG [92]. Therefore, we speculate that Zn-sparing response mediated by ZinS may be important for preserving zinc for biofilm formation. However, experimental validation will be required to demonstrate whether ZinS directly or indirectly affects lipase synthesis and whether ZinS has an impact on biofilm formation. Although ZinS is the first Zur-regulated sRNA described in bacteria, it seems likely that other species utilize sRNAs to adapt to Zn limitation and activate a Zn-sparing response, given the conservation of this regulatory logic for other metals and the universal essentiality of Zn.

In this study, we also expanded the regulon of Zur. In addition to previously known targets (Supplementary Table S5), we identified Zur boxes upstream of (i) HG001_02621–02624 operon, coding for a putative Zn-binding GTPase (COG0523 subfamily), a putative iron transporter and a putative oxidoreductase, and (ii) HG001_00792, coding for a putative pyridine nucleotide-disulfide oxidoreductase. Their synthesis is induced by CP treatment [93, 94], confirming our proteomic analyses (Fig. 6B). Remarkably, we found that any perturbation of Zn homeostasis (Fig. 6) also affects key proteins involved in other metal-related adaptive responses, such as metal importers (Mn: MntABC; Fe: Sbn, Sfa, Isd), metal exporters (Cu: CopA), and transcription factors (Cu: CsoR; Zn: CzrA). Intricate connections between metallosystems have already been described in bacteria. As mentioned above, the metallophore staphylopine is produced via Zur and Fur dual regulation in S. aureus [12, 16]. Interestingly, the Cnt system also drives copper uptake, triggering the copper stress response [95]. The responses to Zn and Cu are intimately linked in bacteria, likely due to the co-localization of these metals in phagolysosomes and their antibacterial activity [72]. In Enterococcus faecalis, an interplay between Cu and Zn homeostasis is mediated by Zur [96]. In Pseudomonas aeruginosa [97] and Streptococcus agalactiae [98], cross-regulation occurs between Cu and Zn homeostasis via transcriptional regulators. We propose that the Zur/ZinS-mediated regulation of CsoR is another illustration of such crosstalk in S. aureus. Additionally, several studies highlighted close interactions between Fe and Mn homeostasis [99, 100, 101]. Overall, these interconnected regulatory networks allow bacterial cells to optimally respond to encountered stresses by integrating multiple environmental signals.

ZinS is a dual-function sRNA, which codes for a peptide renamed ZinP. This is not an isolated example in bacteria, as other regulatory RNAs share this feature (e.g. RNAIII encodes hld in S. aureus and RybA encodes mntS in E. coli) [102, 103]. Although the function of ZinP remains unknown, its conserved sequence across phylogenetically distant bacteria, expression pattern, and localization near metal transporters (Supplementary Fig. S5) suggest that ZinP also plays a role in metal homeostasis. When adapting to metal-restricted environments, bacteria induce the expression of metal uptake systems, but they also reduce the expression of efflux pumps to prevent secretion of a now limited resource. While transcriptional and translational regulation prevent the production of new efflux pumps, they do not inactivate previously produced proteins. During the transition from high to low Mn, the Mn efflux pump MntP and the sRNA-encoded peptide MntS are simultaneously present in E. coli cells [70]. Although MntP synthesis is tightly repressed at the transcriptional level, existing MntP pumps are still active at the membrane. Therefore, as part of a Mn-sparing response, MntS inhibits Mn efflux by interacting directly with MntP. As the ZinP structure is reminiscent of the MntS structure and is amphipathic (Fig. 4A), it is tempting to speculate that ZinP has a similar role in S. aureus, potentially by inhibiting Zn efflux via CzrB at the membrane. Alternatively, ZinP peptide sequence is highly basic, suggesting it may recognize nucleic acids. In B. subtilis, three Fur-regulated small basic peptides act together with FsrA sRNA to regulate a subset of FrsA-dependent mRNA targets upon iron starvation [104]. Although mutation of the SD of ZinP had no major effect on ZinS-dependent repression of adh, we cannot exclude the possibility that ZinP may act as an RNA chaperone to regulate specific events at the translational or post-transcriptional levels. Finally, the translation of zinP might also influence the folding of ZinS to promote its regulatory activities. Further experiments are underway to better decipher the functions of this highly conserved peptide.

Gene duplication and gain-of-function mutations are key mechanisms of bacterial adaptive evolution. For example, the amplification of genes encoding iron transporters confers a selective advantage to overcome nutritional immunity [105]. In S. aureus, the superoxide dismutase SodM was obtained by duplication of sodA and then neofunctionalized to utilize either Mn or Fe, a key advantage for resisting manganese starvation. This duplication was accompanied by the acquisition of the 3′UTR-derived sRNA RsaC, released from the mntABC polycistronic transcript, encoding the main Mn importer in S. aureus [28]. RsaC specifically inhibits the synthesis of the Mn-dependent SodA and indirectly promotes the switch to the cambialistic SodM [106, 107]. Remarkably, we showed that the regulatory domain of ZinS was acquired in several steps from zinP after horizontal gene transfer (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S9). The fact that both ZinS and RsaC were exclusively acquired by S. aureus and closely related strains indicates that these organisms are under strong selective pressure to acquire additional mechanisms to survive metal limitation. [28]. Hence, ZinS, RNAIII, and RsaC are striking illustrations that 3′UTRs serve as an important reservoir of unrestricted RNA sequence for the evolution of regulatory elements. In line with our study, recent observations have shown that large 3′ UTRs evolved rapidly to confer species specificity and, in turn, contribute to bacterial diversity [108]. We previously hypothesized that the presence of 5′→3′ exoribonucleases RNases J1/J2 might present a significant obstacle to the evolution of 3′UTR sRNAs in Gram-positive bacteria [109]. However, this barrier can be bypassed by using various strategies to protect the 5′ end from RNase attack. Our studies showed that the 3′UTR of ZinP is not released as an independent 3′UTR sRNA and consequently retains the protective 5′PPP. The presence of a 5′ hairpin structure and ribosome binding provides stabilizing elements that prevent exoribonuclease activity in Gram-positive bacteria (e.g. [110, 111]). This is likely the case of ZinS, which is characterized by a very short 5′UTR, a strong SD sequence, and a large 5′ hairpin structure. Staphylococcus aureus RNAIII belongs to this class of dual-function, stable sRNA, with its major regulatory domain located within the 3′UTR of hld mRNA (e.g. [112]). For effective sRNA-mediated regulation, cellular abundance must not be limited, and this is often achieved through strong promoters or RNA stabilization [113]. While numerous 3′UTR-encoded sRNAs have been described in Gram-negative bacteria [114, 115], it now becomes clear that this evolutionary strategy is also widely used by Gram-positive bacteria [28, 116, 117, 118].

In conclusion, this study highlights novel tandem regulatory systems involving a transcription factor and sRNAs responding to zinc homeostasis in S. aureus; whether this occurs in other bacteria remains an open question. It also illustrates how pathogenic bacteria acquire new regulatory elements and integrate them into new regulatory circuits in response to strong selective pressure, such as the metal restriction imposed by the host immune system.
