Air Pollution-Associated Intracellular Metals, Immune Gene Expression, and Erythrocyte Indices in COPD with Anemia
Authors: Chi-Thien Dinh, Jer-Hwa Chang, Tzu-Tao Chen, Ching-Huang Lai, Kuan-Yuan Chen, Po-Hao Feng, Kang-Yun Lee, Chien-Hua Tseng, Huan Minh Tran, Tzu-Hsuen Yuan, Shu-Chuan Ho, Sheng-Ming Wu, Hsiao-Chi Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia is common in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and may be influenced by air pollution-related metal accumulation. However, the biological links among air pollution, intracellular metals, immune gene expression, and erythrocyte alterations remain unclear.MethodsWe examined associations between air pollutants, intracellular metals, gene expression, and erythrocyte indices in sixty-one COPD patients and ten healthy controls. Annual exposures to particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤10 µm (PM10), ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) were estimated using land use regression. Metals were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and single-cell ICP-MS. Gene expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was profiled by RNA sequencing.ResultsIn COPD, PM2.5 was associated with higher red blood cell (RBC) count (β = 0.0314×106 cells/µL; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.0066 ~ 0.0561) and ln(red cell distribution width) (β = 0.0051; 95% CI: 0.0003 ~ 0.0098). PM2.5 was also associated with higher ln(chromium (Cr)) in PBMCs (β = 0.0632; 95% CI: 0.0124 ~ 0.1139), ln(zinc (Zn)) in RBCs (β = 0.1338; 95% CI: 0.0388 ~ 0.2288), and ln(cadmium (Cd)) in RBCs (β = 0.1297; 95% CI: 0.0125 ~ 0.2469). RBC Cr, Cd, lead, and vanadium were positively associated with hemoglobin, hematocrit, and mean corpuscular volume. In PBMCs, Cr, Cd, Zn, and single-cell iron were associated with ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3 expression. Anemic COPD patients showed upregulation of IFI27 and TRAV38-1 and downregulation of DEFA1, PI3, CXCR1, and ALPL.ConclusionIntracellular metals, particularly Cr and Cd, were associated with immune-related gene expression profiles and anemia-related erythrocyte indices in COPD. These findings suggest potential biological associations among air pollution exposure, intracellular metal burden, immune dysregulation, and hematologic alterations in COPD.

Introduction

Anemia is a disorder identified by reduced red blood cells (RBCs) count or hemoglobin (HGB) concentration, impairing oxygen transport. It may arise due to chronic diseases, deficiencies in nutrition, or genetic disorders.1 Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a prevalent issue defined by persistent airflow limitation and systemic inflammation.2 Approximately 7.5 to 34% of COPD patients are affected by anemia, associated with systemic inflammation, iron deficiency, and worse dyspnea, fatigue, and survival.3–5 Anemia in COPD is multifactorial and may include anemia of chronic disease, anemia of inflammation, and iron deficiency anemia. Anemia of chronic disease and anemia of inflammation are commonly related to systemic inflammation, cytokine activation, hepcidin-mediated iron restriction, and impaired iron mobilization, whereas iron deficiency anemia reflects reduced iron availability due to nutritional deficiency, blood loss, or other clinical factors.3–5

Emerging evidence suggests air pollution may contribute to anemia. In a US elderly cohort, interquartile increases in particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) were associated with a 33–43% higher anemia prevalence and a 0.81 g/dL reduction in HGB.6 Consistent findings were reported in older Chinese and Korean populations exposed to particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤10 µm (PM10), PM2.5, and NO2,7,8 suggesting air pollution may impair erythropoiesis through oxidative stress and iron dysregulation. Consistent with these findings, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 36 studies reported that each 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 and NO2 was associated with higher anemia risk, while PM10 exposure was associated with lower HGB levels, supporting a broader association between air pollution exposure and hematologic impairment.9

Particulate matter (PM), especially PM2.5 and ultrafine particles, can penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, transporting metals such as chromium (Cr), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), zinc (Zn), and iron (Fe).10 These PM-bound metals accumulate in blood cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and RBCs,11 supporting the hypothesis that air pollution may elevate intracellular metals, contributing to immune dysregulation and impaired erythropoiesis.

Metals including Pb, arsenic (As), and Cd contribute to anemia by disrupting erythropoiesis and iron metabolism. Pb inhibits heme synthesis and disrupts iron utilization, As affects RBC membrane stability,12 and Cd induces oxidative stress and bone marrow damage.13 IFI27 is upregulated in inflammatory hematologic conditions with anemia, implying a possible role of interferon signaling in erythropoiesis dysregulation.14 However, few studies have examined how metal accumulation may influence anemia-related gene expression, particularly in chronic inflammatory conditions like COPD.

Despite known associations between COPD, anemia, and metals, the role of air pollution-associated intracellular metal retention in modulating immune gene expression and erythrocyte indices remains unclear. This study aimed to examine associations among air pollution exposure, intracellular metal accumulation, PBMC immune-related gene expression, and erythrocyte indices within a clinically characterized COPD cohort. We further compared COPD patients with and without anemia to explore anemia-related transcriptomic signatures in the context of COPD. This association-based design was intended to generate biologically plausible evidence on potential associations between environmental exposure, intracellular metal burden, immune dysregulation, and anemia-related erythrocyte changes in COPD.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The study included seventy-one subjects recruited between December 2021 and January 2024, consisting of sixty-one COPD patients from Shuang Ho Hospital, Taipei Medical University, New Taipei City, Taiwan, and ten healthy controls. COPD was confirmed based on spirometry with a post-bronchodilator ratio of forced expiratory volume during the first second to forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) <70%, aged 40–90 years. The prevalence of anemia among COPD patients in our study is consistent with the reported range of 7.5–34% in the general COPD population.4 Among the 61 COPD patients, 14.8% (9 patients) were identified as having anemia. The non-anemic COPD patients served as the within-COPD comparison group for anemia-related analyses. Healthy controls were included as a biological reference group for selected measurements; however, they were not intended to represent a factorial comparator group for separating the independent effects of COPD and anemia. Controls had no pulmonary disease history. Exclusion criteria included recent exacerbations (past three months), asthma, bronchiectasis, malignancies, or other inflammatory conditions unrelated to COPD. Based on available clinical and medication records, none of the participants were receiving oral or intravenous iron supplementation or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents at the time of recruitment. Blood samples were collected at recruitment. Information regarding age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status, was gathered. The study followed ethical approval from the Taipei Medical University-Joint Institutional Review Board (Taipei, Taiwan; N202003075). Prior to participation, both written and oral explanations were given to all subjects, and informed consent was obtained. The study design is summarized in Figure 1. Exposure Assessment

Annual mean concentrations of air pollutants were estimated using land use regression (LUR) models based on published methods.15,16 Ambient air data from monitoring stations operated by the Taiwan Ministry of Environment (MOENV; formerly the Environmental Protection Administration, EPA) were combined with geographic predictors using a hybrid kriging/LUR approach. Subjects’ addresses were geocoded using the Taiwan Geospatial One Stop (TGOS) system (Taiwan Datum 1997), and analyzed in ArcMap 10.8 (ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA). Circular buffers were used to extract land use data for estimating annual PM10, PM2.5, NO2, and nitrogen oxides (NOx) exposure, reflecting long-term spatial pollutant contrasts. Although the subjects were recruited between December 2021 and January 2024, the LUR models, based on stable predictors (eg, road density, land use, natural surface cover), remain reliable for estimating exposures over time, as supported by previous studies.17–19 These estimates represent long-term residential outdoor ambient exposure and did not incorporate indoor exposure, occupational exposure, or individual time-activity patterns.

Erythrocyte Indices

Erythrocyte indices were assessed in COPD patients using the automated hematology analyzer Sysmex XE-5000 (Sysmex; Kobe, Japan). These indices included RBC count, hematocrit (Hct), HGB, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and red cell distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV).

Plasma, RBCs, and PBMCs

Whole blood samples were processed to isolate plasma, RBCs, and PBMCs using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Plasma was kept at −80°C. PBMCs were preserved in TRIzol® or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde depending on downstream application. RBCs were fixed and stored at 4°C for metal analysis. Further processing details are described in section S1 of SI.

RNA-Seq Analysis in PBMCs

RNA-seq analysis followed a previous report.20 The purity and integrity of RNA was evaluated before library construction and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Clean reads were mapped against the Homo sapiens reference genome using the BioTools RNA-seq workflow, which integrates standard RNA-seq tools for quality control, read alignment, gene quantification, and differential expression analysis. Gene expression was quantified as TPM-normalized values for downstream association analyses. Differential expression between anemic and non-anemic COPD groups was analyzed using a DESeq2-based workflow. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing anemic and non-anemic COPD groups using predefined exploratory criteria of log2 fold change ≥1 and p <0.05 as cutoffs, which are commonly used in transcriptomic studies of COPD to identify biologically relevant DEGs.21 Because this was an exploratory study with a limited number of anemic COPD patients, FDR correction was not applied as the primary filtering criterion for DEG selection. Therefore, the DEG findings were interpreted as exploratory candidate signals rather than confirmatory transcriptomic findings. PBMCs were selected for RNA-seq because they reflect systemic immune and inflammatory responses relevant to COPD. Although PBMCs do not directly represent erythroid progenitor cells or bone marrow erythropoiesis, they provide information on immune-related transcriptomic signatures that may be associated with anemia-related erythrocyte alterations in chronic inflammatory conditions.3,22,23 Therefore, PBMC RNA-seq in this study was used to explore immune-hematopoietic signaling associated with COPD-related anemia, rather than to directly assess erythroid differentiation. Further information on RNA-seq is found in section S2 of SI.

Bulk Metal Concentrations in RBCs and PBMCs

The concentrations of twelve metals: vanadium (V), Cr, manganese (Mn), Fe, nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), Zn, As, selenium (Se), Cd, Pb in RBCs and PBMCs were measured using the iCAP RQ ICP-MS. Samples were diluted and digested using microwave digestion. Quality control used blanks and certified standards. For quality assurance, metal concentration data were checked against raw ICP-MS output, calibration and quality-control records, blank correction, dilution factors, unit conversion, sample identifiers, and the final analytical dataset. Details are in section S3 of SI.

Single-Cell Iron Concentrations in RBCs and PBMCs

Single-cell iron (sc-Fe) was measured in RBCs and PBMCs by scICP-MS, which atomizes cells individually to yield discrete ion signals reflecting intracellular Fe content. More information is in section S4 of SI.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and skewed variables were log-transformed using the natural logarithm (ln). Winsorization reduced outlier effects. Multiple linear regression examined associations between predictors and outcomes, adjusted for demographic variables and COPD severity. Additional exploratory analyses were performed to examine associations between candidate PBMC gene expression and erythrocyte indices among COPD patients. Each candidate gene was modeled separately, with adjustment for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and COPD severity. Anemia was classified per World Health Organization criteria.1 Detailed anemia-subtyping markers, including serum iron, ferritin, transferrin saturation, C-reactive protein (CRP), vitamin B12, and folate, were not completely available for all participants; therefore, anemia subtypes were not assigned. R software 4.3.2 was used with significance set at p <0.05. No a priori power calculation was performed because this was an exploratory study based on an available clinically characterized COPD cohort with complete exposure, intracellular metal, erythrocyte index, and transcriptomic data. Given the exploratory nature of this study, p values from transcriptomic and multiple association analyses were interpreted as nominal statistical evidence. The findings were considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger independent cohorts. Additional statistical details are in section S5 of SI.

Associations Between Air Pollution and Anemia-Related Indices

Table 2 presents the associations between annual mean concentrations of air pollutants and erythrocyte indices in COPD. A 1 µg/m3 increase in annual PM2.5 exposure was associated with a 0.0314×106 cells/µL increase in RBC count (95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.0066 ~ 0.0561), and a 0.0051-unit increase in ln(RDW-CV) (95% CI = 0.0003 ~ 0.0098). Furthermore, Table 3 presents the associations between annual mean concentrations of PM and intracellular metal concentrations in PBMCs and RBCs in COPD. Specifically, a 1 µg/m3 increase in annual PM10 exposure was associated with a 0.0981-unit increase in ln(Zn) in PBMCs (95% CI = 0.0108 ~ 0.1854) and a 0.2183-unit increase in ln(Zn) in RBCs (95% CI = 0.0748 ~ 0.3618). Associations Between Metals and Gene Expression

Figure 2 presents the associations between metal concentrations in PBMCs and gene expression in COPD. The top ten candidate DEGs were selected based on the largest differential expression between anemic and non-anemic COPD groups using the predefined exploratory criteria. These genes were interpreted as exploratory transcriptomic candidates rather than FDR-confirmed DEGs. Key upregulated genes included IFI27 and TRAV38-1, while downregulated genes included MTND2P28, DEFA1, MTCO3P12, PI3, SLC12A1, CXCR1, ALPL, and ENSG00000285534. A 1-unit increase in ln(V) was associated with a 0.3806-unit decrease in ln(SLC12A1) (95% CI = −0.7021 ~ −0.0590). ln(Cr) was associated with a 0.4153-unit decrease in ln(MTND2P28) (95% CI = −0.7874 ~ −0.0432), a 0.4946-unit increase in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = 0.0821 ~ 0.907), a 0.3193-unit increase in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = 0.0194 ~ 0.6192), and a 1.1779-unit increase in ln(PI3) (95% CI = 0.4588 ~ 1.8969). ln(Cu) was associated with a 0.2518-unit decrease in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = −0.5009 ~ −0.0027) and a 0.1813-unit decrease in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = −0.3606 ~ −0.002), while it was associated with a 0.2875-unit increase in ln(ENSG00000285534) (95% CI = 0.0169 ~ 0.5582). ln(Zn) was associated with a 0.5845-unit increase in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = 0.2046 ~ 0.9644), a 0.3736-unit increase in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = 0.0952 ~ 0.652), and a 0.9765-unit increase in ln(PI3) (95% CI = 0.2785 ~ 1.6744). ln(Se) was associated with a 0.2671-unit decrease in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = −0.4981 ~ −0.0361) and a 0.2267-unit decrease in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = −0.3898 ~ −0.0636), while it was associated with a 0.3031-unit increase in ln(ENSG00000285534) (95% CI = 0.0522 ~ 0.554). A 1-unit increase in ln(Cd) was associated with a 0.5179-unit increase in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = 0.2451 ~ 0.7906), a 0.3684-unit increase in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = 0.1715 ~ 0.5653), a 0.3308-unit decrease in ln(ENSG00000285534) (95% CI = −0.6529 ~ −0.0087), and a 0.9103-unit increase in ln(PI3) (95% CI = 0.4117 ~ 1.4089). ln(Pb) was associated with a 0.3341-unit decrease in ln(ENSG00000285534) (95% CI = −0.6063 ~ −0.0619). Lastly, ln(sc-Fe) was associated with a 0.5749-unit increase in ln(ALPL) (95% CI = 0.2501 ~ 0.8997), a 0.4102-unit increase in ln(CXCR1) (95% CI = 0.1759 ~ 0.6444), and a 0.6493-unit increase in ln(PI3) (95% CI = 0.0204 ~ 1.2783). Complete statistical results are available in Table S1 of SI. Figure S2 shows DEGs distinguishing COPD patients with and without anemia. Figure S3 further shows the biological pathways affected by these DEGs, including cell chemotaxis and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, which are involved in the pathophysiology of anemia in COPD. Associations Between Metals and Erythrocyte Indices

Figure 3 illustrates the associations between metal concentrations in RBCs and erythrocyte indices in COPD. A 1-unit increase in ln(V) was associated with a 0.1661 g/dL increase in HGB (95% CI = 0.0114 ~ 0.3208) and a 0.4698% increase in Hct (95% CI = 0.0557 ~ 0.884). A 1-unit increase in ln(Cr) was associated with a 0.1809 g/dL increase in HGB (95% CI = 0.0053 ~ 0.3566) and a 0.6708% increase in Hct (95% CI = 0.2153 ~ 1.1263). A 1-unit increase in ln(As) was associated with a 0.0157-unit increase in ln(MCV) (95% CI = 0.0017 ~ 0.0297). Associations Between Candidate Gene Expression and Erythrocyte Indices

Additional exploratory analyses examined associations between candidate PBMC gene expression and erythrocyte indices in COPD patients (Table S3). In the fully adjusted models, IFI27 expression was inversely associated with RBC count (β = −0.1224; 95% CI = −0.2200 ~ −0.0248), HGB (β = −0.3254; 95% CI = −0.5463 ~ −0.1046), and Hct (β = −0.9569; 95% CI = −1.5561 ~ −0.3576). ALPL expression was inversely associated with ln(MCV) (β = −0.0132; 95% CI = −0.0258 ~ −0.0006), ln(MCH) (β = −0.0177; 95% CI = −0.0331 ~ −0.0023), and MCHC (β = −0.1493; 95% CI = −0.2983 ~ −0.0003). CXCR1 expression was inversely associated with ln(MCH) (β = −0.0220; 95% CI = −0.0433 ~ −0.0007), while MTCO3P12 expression was inversely associated with ln(RDW-CV) (β = −0.0264; 95% CI = −0.0455 ~ −0.0074). Discussion

Our study examined associations among air pollution exposure, intracellular metal accumulation, immune-related gene expression, and erythrocyte indices in COPD. In COPD patients, annual PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased RBC count and RDW-CV. PM10 and PM2.5 exposures were also associated with selected intracellular metals, including Cr, Cd, and Zn. In parallel, intracellular metals were associated with immune-related genes, particularly ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, and with erythrocyte indices, including HGB, Hct, and MCV. In anemic COPD patients, IFI27 and TRAV38-1 were upregulated, whereas DEFA1, PI3, CXCR1, and ALPL were downregulated. Together, these findings provide an integrated view of potential biological associations among environmental exposure, intracellular metal burden, immune dysregulation, and anemia-related erythrocyte alterations in COPD.

We observed associations between PM exposure and both erythrocyte indices and intracellular metal concentrations in COPD patients. Annual PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased RBC count and RDW-CV, while both PM10 and PM2.5 were associated with elevated intracellular Cr, Cd, and Zn in PBMCs and RBCs, suggesting PM may function as a carrier of metals. In the adjusted models, PM2.5 was not significantly associated with HGB, Hct, or MCV, suggesting that ambient PM2.5 exposure may be more closely related to erythrocyte heterogeneity and intracellular metal accumulation than to direct hemoglobinization changes. Therefore, the association between PM2.5 and RDW-CV may represent an early or indirect hematologic alteration in COPD, whereas HGB, Hct, and MCV may be influenced by broader clinical, nutritional, inflammatory, and compensatory mechanisms.22,23 RDW-CV reflects variability in erythrocyte size and may capture inflammation, oxidative stress, impaired iron mobilization, and altered erythrocyte turnover; a recent meta-analysis further showed that elevated RDW was associated with increased short- and long-term mortality in elderly hip fracture patients.24 A study in industrial and traffic-heavy regions found a significant correlation between PM2.5-bound metals and elevated blood metal concentrations of Pb, As, Cr, and Cd in children, confirming the role of PM2.5 in metal accumulation.25,26 These findings support that metals in PM2.5 may enter the bloodstream through inhalation. While several studies reported lower HGB levels and anemia risk,6–8 others showed higher RBC count and HGB, such as in a Danish cohort where PM2.5 was positively associated with both parameters in women (p <0.01),27 and in populations with higher odds of erythrocytosis per 1 μg/m3 PM2.5 (OR = 1.049).28 These may reflect compensatory erythropoietic activity under systemic stress or hypoxia in COPD.29 A recent meta-analysis supports an overall association between air pollution and increased anemia risk or lower HGB, although substantial heterogeneity across populations and study designs suggests that responses may vary by susceptibility, exposure context, and underlying disease status.9

We found significant associations between metals in RBCs and erythrocyte indices in COPD patients. Among these, V was positively associated with HGB and Hct, potentially reflecting its role in enhancing iron metabolism and reducing hepcidin levels.30 Cr was also positively associated with HGB and Hct, supporting its capacity to improve hematological indices,31 despite inconsistent findings.32 Cd showed positive associations with HGB, Hct, and MCV, while Pb was positively associated with HGB and Hct. These positive associations should not be interpreted as beneficial hematologic effects. Previous studies have shown that Cd and Pb exposures are associated with altered erythrocyte parameters, including RDW and other hematologic indices, suggesting complex effects on erythrocyte production, turnover, or morphology.33,34 Moreover, the direction of metal-erythrocyte associations may vary by exposure level, exposure duration, population susceptibility, and underlying disease status. Therefore, the positive associations observed in this COPD cohort may reflect exposure-context-dependent or compensatory hematologic responses rather than direct enhancement of erythropoiesis. Chronic exposure to Cd and Pb remains well recognized for hematotoxic potential.35,36 The absence of significant associations between PM2.5 and HGB or Hct does not necessarily contradict the observed associations between intracellular metals and erythrocyte indices. Ambient PM2.5 reflects external residential exposure, whereas intracellular metals in RBCs reflect retained biological burden from multiple exposure sources and host biological processes. Thus, intracellular metals may provide a more proximal biological indicator of metal-related hematologic alterations than ambient PM2.5 exposure alone.

Beyond their associations with erythrocyte indices, intracellular metals may represent a potential biological association between PM exposure, immune-related gene-expression changes, and anemia-related erythrocyte alterations. We found significant associations between metals in PBMCs and gene expression in COPD patients. Cr was negatively associated with MTND2P28 and positively with ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, implicating its role in mitochondrial stress and inflammatory signaling.37 Zn and Cd were positively associated with ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, while Cd showed an inverse association with ENSG00000285534, suggesting a dual role in oxidative stress and immune modulation.38,39 Cu and Se were negatively associated with ALPL and CXCR1, but positively with ENSG00000285534, reflecting their complex involvement in immune and epigenetic regulation.40,41 In addition, sc-Fe was positively associated with ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, underscoring the role of iron dysregulation in chronic immune activation and impaired erythropoiesis.42 Importantly, Cr and Cd, which were both associated with PM2.5 exposure, showed consistent associations with increased expression of ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, and decreased expression of MTND2P28 and ENSG00000285534. These genes are functionally involved in immune signaling, oxidative stress, and hematopoietic regulation. Our findings suggest that PM2.5-related metals may be associated with immune transcriptomic changes and erythrocyte alterations relevant to COPD-related anemia.

The PBMC RNA-seq findings should be interpreted in the context of systemic immune regulation rather than direct erythroid differentiation. Among the candidate genes, ALPL, PI3, and CXCR1 may provide biologically relevant immune-inflammatory signals in COPD-related anemia. ALPL encodes tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase and is involved in mineral metabolism; reduced alkaline phosphatase activity has been associated with hypophosphatasia and may be accompanied by hematologic abnormalities, including anemia.43 PI3 encodes peptidase inhibitor 3, also known as elafin, an endogenous inhibitor of neutrophil elastase with roles in epithelial defense and airway inflammation. In COPD, altered protease-antiprotease balance and neutrophil-driven inflammation are central biological features, suggesting that PI3 changes may reflect airway and systemic inflammatory activation rather than direct erythroid differentiation.44 CXCR1 is a chemokine receptor involved in CXCL8/IL-8-mediated neutrophil recruitment and inflammatory signaling, which are highly relevant to COPD pathobiology.45 Because anemia of inflammation is driven by cytokine activation, hepcidin-mediated iron restriction, and impaired erythropoiesis, altered expression of these immune-related genes may provide a plausible transcriptomic context for anemia-related erythrocyte alterations in COPD.46 PBMCs are not erythroid progenitor cells and do not directly represent bone marrow erythropoiesis. However, COPD is characterized by systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, and anemia in COPD is commonly linked to chronic inflammation and altered iron homeostasis. Therefore, PBMC transcriptomic profiles may provide insight into immune-related signatures associated with anemia-related erythrocyte alterations in COPD.3,47,48 Exploratory transcriptomic analysis identified candidate differences in PBMC gene expression profiles between anemic and non-anemic COPD patients, suggesting possible immune-hematopoietic signaling differences associated with anemia within COPD. Upregulation of IFI27, a type I interferon-stimulated gene, may reflect heightened inflammatory signaling in anemic patients; IF27 promotes the production of chemokine including CCL18, CXCL5, and CXCL9 by alveolar macrophages, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation.49 TRAV38-1, also upregulated, may contribute to T-cell activation and immune dysregulation in COPD-related anemia.50 In contrast, downregulation of DEFA1 suggests impaired apoptotic cells clearance and persistent inflammation,51 while CXCR1 downregulation reflects reduced neutrophil chemotaxis and compromised innate immune response.52 Downregulation of ALPL and PI3 further implicates disrupted erythropoiesis. ALPL is involved in bone mineral metabolism, and reduced activity has been associated with anemia in hypophosphatasemia.53 PI3 is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factors and glycolysis, both essential for erythropoiesis under stress.54 Together, these findings suggest that COPD-associated anemia may involve interferon-mediated inflammation, impaired chemotaxis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered erythropoietic signaling. Because the candidate genes were selected from the comparison between anemic and non-anemic COPD patients, they represent anemia-related transcriptomic signatures within COPD. To further examine their relationship with erythrocyte alterations, additional exploratory analyses were performed between candidate PBMC gene expression and erythrocyte indices. These analyses showed that selected candidate genes were associated with anemia-related erythrocyte indices, including the association between MTCO3P12 and RDW-CV and the associations of IFI27, ALPL, and CXCR1 with selected erythrocyte parameters. These findings provide additional association-based support linking immune-related transcriptomic signatures with erythrocyte alterations in COPD. Notably, several of these dysregulated genes, including ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, were also associated with intracellular concentrations of Cr and Cd, which were in turn associated with PM2.5 exposure. This pattern suggests that PM2.5-associated metal accumulation is related to immune gene-expression differences observed between anemic and non-anemic COPD patients.

This study has several limitations. First, although the COPD cohort included both anemic and non-anemic patients, an anemia-only control group was not available; therefore, anemia-specific effects could not be fully separated from COPD-related anemia. Second, the small number of COPD patients with anemia may reduce statistical power, particularly for the exploratory differential gene-expression analysis, and may limit the generalizability of the transcriptomic findings. Third, healthy controls differed demographically from COPD patients; thus, between-group comparisons were interpreted descriptively, whereas the primary association analyses were focused on the COPD cohort. In addition, complete data on recent COVID-19 infection, prior blood transfusion, and anemia-subtyping markers, including serum iron, ferritin, transferrin saturation, total iron-binding capacity, CRP, vitamin B12, folate, and hepcidin, were not available for all participants. Indoor exposure, occupational exposure, and individual time-activity patterns were also unavailable, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. Finally, the RNA-seq findings were not validated by qPCR or functional experiments, and FDR-adjusted q-values were not used as the primary criterion for transcriptomic selection. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future studies with larger COPD cohorts, more detailed anemia characterization, and longitudinal designs are warranted.

Conclusions

This study identified associations among air pollution exposure, intracellular metal accumulation, immune-related gene expression, and erythrocyte indices in COPD. PM2.5 exposure was associated with selected intracellular metals, particularly Cr and Cd, which were also related to immune-related genes, including ALPL, CXCR1, and PI3, and anemia-related erythrocyte indices, including HGB, Hct, and MCV. These findings suggest potential biological associations between environmental exposure, intracellular metal burden, immune dysregulation, and hematologic alterations in COPD. Overall, this study provides hypothesis-generating evidence supporting the relevance of intracellular metal accumulation and immune transcriptomic changes in understanding anemia-related alterations in COPD.
