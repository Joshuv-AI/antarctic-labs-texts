Left subclavian access for pentaspline pulsed field ablation system: A feasible option in bilateral femoral vein thrombosis
Authors: Vincenzo Schillaci, Assunta Di Costanzo, Armando Salito, Francesco Solimene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStandard femoral venous access may not be feasible in patients with inferior vena cava (IVC) obstruction or bilateral deep vein thrombosis (DVT), necessitating alternative venous routes.Case summaryWe report the case of a 73-year-old man with paroxysmal AF, antithrombin III deficiency, and chronic bilateral femoral and iliac vein thrombosis. After a failed prior attempt at PVI due to inaccessible femoral veins, a left subclavian approach was selected based on favorable ultrasound imaging. Under deep sedation, subclavian venous access was obtained and a Pentaspline PFA system (FaraWave™) was used via a steerable sheath. Transseptal puncture was performed using the VersaCross™ RF pigtail system under fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiographic guidance. Ablation was successfully delivered to all pulmonary veins, including a left common trunk, using standard electroporation protocols. Total procedure time was 117 minutes. No complications occurred, and the patient was discharged the following day in stable condition.ConclusionThis case demonstrates the technical feasibility and safety of using the Pentaspline PFA system via left subclavian venous access in a patient with extensive lower extremity venous thrombosis. The approach enabled successful PVI in a complex clinical scenario where traditional femoral access was precluded. While alternative access routes for PFA are rarely reported, this case expands the potential use of superior venous access and may serve as a reference for similar high-risk patients. Such procedures should be reserved for experienced centers with advanced imaging and transseptal expertise.

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as an established strategy for the pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) [1]. The femoral venous access is standard approach to access the left atrium, allowing catheter advancement through the inferior vena cava and transseptal puncture [[2], [3], [4]].

However, in rare but clinically relevant conditions — such as anatomical variants (e.g., congenital interruption of the inferior vena cava), venous occlusions (e.g., deep vein thrombosis involving the femoral, iliac, or caval veins), or the presence of intravascular devices (e.g., vena cava filters) — the femoral approach may not be feasible [5,6]. In such cases, alternative access via the superior venous system — jugular, axillary, or subclavian — may be employed, though with increased technical and logistical complexity [7].

The Pentaspline PFA system (FaraWave™, Boston Scientific, Boston, MA) is designed for femoral access. Other cases in the literature have previously reported successful use of the Pentaspline PFA system through superior venous access [8,9].

In this report, we describe the successful use of the left subclavian venous access for performing PVI with the Pentaspline PFA system in a 73-year-old patient with paroxysmal AF and a complex medical history of thrombophilia and bilateral deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Case report

A 73-year-old male with paroxysmal AF was referred to our center following a failed prior attempt at PVI due to the unavailability of suitable femoral access. His medical history included antithrombin III (AT III) deficiency-related thrombophilia, prior DVT complicated by pulmonary embolism (PE), and spleno-portal thrombosis treated with splenectomy and partial gastric resection. Computed Tomography (CT) angiography revealed bilateral femoral vein thrombosis and partial thrombosis of the right external iliac vein.

Given these findings, alternative venous access was planned. Ultrasound evaluation confirmed the patency of the left subclavian vein with a diameter of 10 mm at the mid-clavicular level. The patient was on chronic anticoagulation with edoxaban, which was withheld for one dose prior to the procedure. The procedure was performed under deep sedation with invasive blood pressure monitoring via radial artery line. Following left subclavian venous access, a fixed curve diagnostic catheter (Viking ™, Boston Scientific, Boston, MA) was placed in the coronary sinus.

A Pentaspline PFA system (FaraWave™, Boston Scientific, Boston, MA) and a 16.6 F steerable sheath (FARADRIVE™, Boston Scientific, Boston, MA) were used to perform the PVI. Transseptal puncture was performed using the VersaCross™ system (Boston Scientific, Boston, MA) and an 8.5F steerable sheath (AGILIS™, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA) was used to optimize directional control. For enhanced safety, a transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) probe was placed in esophagus for image guidance.

The Versacross RF Pigtail guidewire was advanced into the right atrium (RA). Correct positioning was confirmed fluoroscopically and by TEE. After tenting the fossa ovalis, RF energy was delivered to create an iatrogenic atrial septal defect (ASD), allowing the guidewire to enter the left superior pulmonary vein (LSPV). The entire system, dilator and sheath, was then advanced into the left atrium (LA) via the central portion of the fossa ovalis. Intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) > 300 seconds. Atropine 0.5 mg was administered to prevent vagally mediated bradycardia during energy delivery. After dilation of the iatrogenic ASD, the pigtail wire was exchanged for a 0.035″ x 260 cm InQwire Guide Wire (3 mm J-Tip Fixed Core, PTFE Coated wire, Merit Medical). The guidewire was positioned in the left superior pulmonary vein.

The steerable sheath was advanced in the LA, and the PFA catheter was positioned over the guidewire into each pulmonary vein (PV). Catheter–tissue contact was assessed indirectly by pacing from the PFA catheter and requiring consistent local myocardial capture on the intracardiac electrograms before delivery of each application. In addition, under fluoroscopic guidance we confirmed adequate deformation and stable apposition of the pentaspline array at the pulmonary vein ostia throughout the respiratory and cardiac cycle, and pulses were delivered only when these criteria were fulfilled. Fluoroscopic mapping of the LA and PV revealed a common trunk for the LSPV and the left inferior pulmonary vein (LIPV). In particular, accurate placement of the ablation catheter in the left common trunk was achieved under echocardiographic guidance and confirmed fluoroscopically. Circumferential electroporation was delivered to the PV ostia until electrical disconnection was confirmed. A standard ablation protocol was used for PVI: for each pulmonary vein, four applications were delivered in basket configuration and four in flower configuration; in each configuration, after the first two applications the catheter was rotated by approximately one-tenth of a turn for the subsequent two applications, in order to achieve adequate circumferential overlap of the lesions (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Fluoroscopy views of the transseptal access and (1–3) and pulsed field ablation procedure (4–6). 1: Transseptal access was obtained with the VersaCross™ system (Boston Scientific, Boston, MA). 2: A radiofrequency septostomy was performed with the Versacross RF pigtail guidewire, subsequently advanced into the LSPV. 3: The entire system, dilator and sheath, was advanced into the LA to enlarge the access site. 4: PFA was delivered at the right superior pulmonary vein (RSPV). 5: PFA was delivered at the right inferior pulmonary vein (RIPV). 6: PFA was delivered at the common trunk of the LSPV and LIPV.Fig. 1

Following PVI, high-rate atrial pacing induced self-limited AF, with rapid restoration of sinus rhythm. Hemostasis of the subclavian access site was achieved via manual compression. Total procedural duration was 117 minutes. No complications occurred, and the patient was discharged the next day in stable clinical condition.

The patient underwent serial clinical follow-up. At 1 month, he attended an outpatient visit focused on symptoms assessment, 12-lead ECG, and evaluation of any access-site complications. At 3 months, a 24-h Holter ECG was performed, during which the patient remained asymptomatic and no atrial fibrillation recurrences were documented. At 9 months, he was still asymptomatic, with no evidence of arrhythmic recurrence and no late procedure-related complications.

Discussion

The inability to access the LA via the femoral route represents a significant technical challenge in transcatheter ablation procedures. Although alternative access have been documented for both radiofrequency and cryoballoon ablation, clinical reports describing the use of PFA via superior venous access remain limited [10] [11]. To date, a few previous cases describe use of the Pentaspline PFA system through a superior venous access [8,9].

In the present case, left subclavian access was selected following detailed ultrasound assessment confirming patency and adequate vessel diameter. The use of the VersaCross™ system facilitated transseptal puncture, despite the less favorable angulation compared to the femoral approach. Integration of TEE allowed precise localization of the fossa ovalis and ensured optimal system positioning [12]. A limitation of our report is that intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) was not used; however, continuous TEE guidance provided adequate imaging support for transseptal puncture and left atrial navigation in this complex venous setting.

Although contact-force was not directly measured, catheter–tissue contact and stability were assessed using indirect criteria (pacing with consistent local capture and fluoroscopic evaluation of spline deformation), and the absence of arrhythmic recurrences and complications during 9-month follow-up supports the effectiveness and safety of lesion creation with this strategy. During the 9-month follow-up, including serial outpatient visits and 24-h Holter monitoring, the patient remained free of symptoms and documented atrial arrhythmias, with no access-site or procedure-related complications. Despite the unconventional approach, use of the Pentaspline PFA system enabled successful access to all PV, including a left common trunk.

This case supports the feasibility and safety of PFA-guided PVI via subclavian access, expanding the procedural toolkit in patients with complex venous pathology. However, this approach should be reserved for centers with advanced expertise in imaging, transseptal techniques, ablation strategy and vascular complication management.
