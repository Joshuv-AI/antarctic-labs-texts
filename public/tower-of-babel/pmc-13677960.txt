All-natural sodium alginate films reinforced with Rana chensinensis ovum protein isolate/xanthan gum-stabilized Pickering emulsions for active preservation and freshness indication
Authors: Manman Zhang, Mengyuan Li, Hang Li, Kexin Zhang, Zixuan Jiao, Kexin Xu, Zhihan Wang, Yongsheng Wang, Shihan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address food spoilage and real-time monitoring, an all-natural multifunctional composite film was developed by incorporating Rana chensinensis ovum protein isolate/xanthan gum–lemon essential oil (RCOPI/XG-LEO) Pickering emulsion and anthocyanin, which achieved active preservation and visual sensing. The Pickering emulsion with notable stability enhanced the film hydrophobicity and thickness, while reducing water content (WC) and moisture absorption (MA). The film achieved optimal performance at an emulsion addition of 2 mL. The film degradation rate reached 60.89% within four weeks, with strong antioxidant effects (DPPH 61.84%, ABTS 81.58%), antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus, and low UV transmittance (8.56%) in the 200–400 nm range. The incorporation of anthocyanin imparted pH-responsive properties. In banana and pork preservation experiments, the film effectively delayed spoilage, reduced weight loss, and provided a visual freshness indication. SA-based films reinforced with RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsions demonstrate considerable potential for multifunctional, biodegradable intelligent food packaging.Keywords: Pickering emulsion, pH indicator, Food preservation, Active packaging

Introduction

Food safety is seriously threatened by air exposure during storage, which can rapidly induce food deterioration and spoilage, and result in substantial economic losses and the production of harmful metabolites (Oliveira et al., 2020). To address this challenge, advanced food packaging is required that not only provides a barrier against oxygen and microbial contamination but also offers multifunctional properties such as antioxidant activity and real-time quality monitoring. Conventional petroleum-based food packaging is widely used but recalcitrant to degradation, which causes persistent environmental pollution (Sun et al., 2022). Now, developing multifunctional, edible active packaging from natural biomass has become more and more attractive in green preservation.

One of the main difficulties in active packaging is to incorporate bioactive substances efficiently. In this case, Pickering emulsions stabilized by natural solid particles provide a unique vehicle, which can give superior interfacial stability and safety without chemical surfactants (Cheng et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024). However, the construction of Pickering emulsions with high performance is critically dependent upon the selection of appropriate solid stabilizers. While plant proteins and polysaccharides are widely used (Hei et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), finding novel, cost-effective, and multifunctional biopolymers remains essential to expand their applications. Rana chensinensis ovum protein isolate (RCOPI) presents a high-value candidate. Extracted from Rana chensinensis ovum and an underutilized by-product of Oviductus Ranae processing, RCOPI is cost-effective, readily available, and sustainably sourced. Currently, full exploitation of this by-product has not been achieved yet. Not only is the waste problem addressed by the utilization of RCOPI (Zhang et al., 2026), but also the stabilization of Pickering emulsions is enabled by its highly amphiphilic protein structure (Li et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Different from common plant proteins, RCOPI possesses a unique amino acid structure, antioxidant activity, and favorable functional properties, which confer dual advantages of structural stability and bioactivity (Wang et al., 2022). Nevertheless, single-component RCOPI often exhibits limited emulsifying capability and poor physical resistance against environmental fluctuations (Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025). To overcome these structural limitations, the incorporation of polysaccharides such as xanthan gum (XG) offers a viable strategy (Dong et al., 2025). XG is an anionic polysaccharide renowned for its fantastic cold-water solubility, thermal stability, and stable viscosity across broad pH and salt conditions (Li et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). It has been demonstrated, when complexed with proteins, XG can enhance the interfacial steric hindrance and viscoelasticity, effectively preventing droplet coalescence during the matrix drying (Wang et al., 2026).

Beyond mechanical stability, the incorporation of natural antimicrobials such as essential oil is also crucial for active preservation (Abdalla et al., 2025). Plant essential oils, such as Lemon essential oil (LEO) rich in limonene, citral, and β-pinene, exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy against foodborne pathogens (Gonçalves et al., 2025). However, direct addition of LEO to hydrophilic matrices leads to high volatility, poor water solubility, active component leakage and impaired material structure, severely restricting its practical applications (Liu et al., 2022). If LEO is encapsulated in Pickering emulsions stabilized with RCOPI–XG complexes, the compact particulate interfacial layer hinders essential oil diffusion and volatilization, and then realize long-term controlled release of active components.

Herein, LEO-loaded Pickering emulsions stabilized by RCOPI–XG complexes were constructed via one-pot homogenization and subsequently blended with anthocyanin into a sodium alginate (SA) matrix to fabricate multifunctional, pH-responsive active films. Characterization was performed on the surface structure, water vapor permeability (WVP), thermogravimetric analysis–differential scanning calorimetry (TGA–DSC), and moisture absorption (MA) of these films. Further application evaluation was carried out, including degradability, pH responsiveness, antimicrobial activity, antioxidant capacity, and food preservation performance, by which their potential for food packaging was assessed.

Materials and methods

Materials

RCOPI is laboratory-made. Sodium alginate (CP, viscosity 200 ± 20 mPa·s) and anhydrous calcium chloride (94% purity) were purchased from Energy Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Xanthan gum (food grade) was obtained from Jianlong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China). Lemon essential oil was supplied by Fengzhishi Essential Oil Customization. Anthocyanin (25% purity) was purchased from Hanzhong Hansuyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Glycerol was sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. ABTS and DPPH were acquired from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pork and banana were procured from a local supermarket (Changchun, China). The soil degradation experiment was conducted using standard natural topsoil collected from the campus garden (Changchun, China). All other reagents used were of analytical grade unless otherwise stated.

Preparation of the Pickering emulsion RCOPI/XG-LEO

The emulsion preparation was conducted in accordance with the method detailed in (Dong et al., 2025). The optimal ratio of RCOPI to XG (1:1, w/w) and the LEO loading level were determined based on our preliminary optimization experiments. At this ratio, the protein–polysaccharide complex achieved the lowest droplet size and maximum physical stability. XG (0.12 g) was accurately weighed and dissolved in deionized water (3 mL), the solution was stirred at 35 °C on a magnetic stirrer until complete dissolution to obtain a 4% (w/v) XG solution. An equal volume of pre-prepared 4% (w/v) RCOPI solution was added dropwise under constant stirring to yield a homogeneous RCOPI/XG composite solution, and the pH was adjusted to 4.0 ± 0.01 with HCl (2 M). Combine 4.5 mL of the RCOPI/XG composite solution with 0.5 mL LEO in a glass vial and pre-mix by gentle swirling. Immediately homogenize the mixture at 15,000 rpm for 4 min to obtain a 10% (v/v) LEO emulsion (Fig. 1A). The emulsion was designated as RCOPI/XG-LEO.

Preparation of antibacterial film

SA (1 g) was dissolved in deionized water (100 mL) with continuous stirring at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, glycerol (2 mL, 15%, w/v) and anthocyanin solution (1 mL, 0.3%, w/v) were added and mixed thoroughly to obtain the SA film-forming solution. The anthocyanin loading was 0.23% (w/w) of the total dry film mass.

The emulsion prepared in Section 2.2 was added to the SA film-forming solution at volumes of 0.1, 0.5, 1, and 2 mL to achieve final Pickering emulsion volume fractions of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2%, respectively. The mixtures were then stirred for 2 h. A control solution containing an equal volume of SA film-forming solution without the emulsion was prepared. Each 100 mL solution was poured equally into two 98 mm diameter Petri dishes and dried for 24 h in an oven at 40 °C. The resulting films were peeled off and designated as SA, RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-0.1, RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-0.5, RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-1, and RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2. Fig. 2A presents a schematic illustration of the film preparation process. Meanwhile, a direct-mixing control group was established by directly incorporating RCOPI, XG, and LEO at identical proportions into the SA film-forming solution, so as to validate the necessity of pre-emulsification.

Type of emulsion

The type of emulsion was determined by the dilution method. A small quantity of the RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion was added dropwise to separate containers holding water and oil, respectively. After shaking each container for one minute, the dispersion behavior was observed.

Particle size and potential

The RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion was observed by optical microscopy (Phenix, China). From different fields of view, 200 droplets were randomly selected, and their sizes were measured using ImageJ software. The sample was diluted 10-fold with deionized water. The zeta potential was then measured using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Microstructure observation

The oil phase and aqueous phase of the emulsion were stained with Nile Red and Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), respectively. A drop of the stained emulsion was placed on a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, LSM 900, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence images were acquired with excitation at 488 nm for FITC and 561 nm for Nile Red, respectively.

Rheological behavior of film-forming solutions

The rheological properties of the film-forming solutions were characterized using a rheometer (Kinexus Lab+, 40 mm parallel-plate geometry). Apparent viscosity was recorded as a function of shear rate over the range of 10–100 s−1 at 25 °C. Additionally, frequency sweep measurements were performed to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) (Zhang et al., 2022).

Degradation experiments of the film

Film samples (3 cm × 3 cm) of different concentrations were buried in soil at a depth of 5 cm, which was characterized by a pH of 6.77 ± 0.15, moisture content of 25.12% ± 0.37%, and microbial activity of 0.01 mg CO₂·g−1·h−1. Water contact angle (WCA) of the film

The WCA of the film surface was measured using the sessile drop method with a contact angle goniometer (DSAHT17C, KRÜSS GmbH, Germany). Mechanical properties of the film

Tensile tests were performed on film specimens (0.8 cm × 5 cm) using an INSTRON universal testing machine in accordance with GB/T 1040.1—2006. The initial gauge length was 10 mm, and the crosshead speed was 5 mm/min.

Antibacterial activity of the film

The antibacterial activity of SA-based films against E. coli and S. aureus was evaluated using the plate count method. Overnight cultures of E. coli and S. aureus were prepared separately in LB broth (37 °C, 180 rpm). Each bacterial suspension was adjusted to 105 CFU/mL using sterile physiological saline. Film samples (1 cm × 1 cm) were placed in 10 mL of sterile broth, inoculated with the adjusted bacterial suspensions, and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, 50 μL aliquots were spread onto agar plates. After 24 h of incubation, colony formation was examined.

Structural characterization of the films

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurement of the film

The microstructure of the films was characterized using AFM (Sun et al., 2026). The film surface was imaged in contact mode using an AFM, acquiring both 2D and 3D images at a scanning speed of 1 Hz. Surface topography was analyzed with Bruker Nanoscope Analysis 3.0 software, and surface roughness was quantified using Rq and Ra.

Attenuated total reflection -Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) of the film

The films were analyzed by ATR-FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Scientific, USA). Spectra were acquired over the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 and processed using OMNIC 9.2 software (Gao et al., 2026).

UV transmittance analysis of the film

The light transmittance of SA-based films was measured over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-2700, Shimadzu, Japan). Before measurement, film samples were placed in a cuvette, and air was used as the blank reference.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the film

The crystalline structure of film samples was analyzed by XRD. Samples were cut into pieces (1 cm × 1 cm) and mounted on a glass slide for measurement. Diffraction patterns were collected over the 2θ range of 5°–50° at a scanning rate of 5°/min.

Results and discussion

Basic characteristics of Pickering emulsion

Types of Pickering emulsion

The type of emulsion can be identified by its dispersion behavior in different solvents. As shown in Fig. 1(B—C), when the RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion was gently agitated, it dispersed uniformly in the aqueous phase while remaining aggregated in the oil phase. This indicates that the emulsion is of the oil-in-water (O/W) type.

Microstructure and zeta potential of Pickering emulsion

Particle size analysis revealed a relatively uniform droplet size distribution for the RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion, with droplet sizes predominantly in the range of 2–5 μm (Fig. 1D and F). This narrow distribution results from the Pickering stabilization mechanism, whereby solid particles form a dense interfacial layer that confers considerable physical stability on the emulsion.

Fig.1 (G–I) presents the CLSM images of the Pickering emulsion. The oil phase exhibited red fluorescence, while the aqueous phase displayed green fluorescence, which clearly revealed that oil droplets were uniformly dispersed within the continuous aqueous phase and confirmed the emulsion as an O/W type. No significant coalescence or flocculation was observed between droplets, and the oil-water interface remained intact and well-defined, which reflected the dispersion stability and structural homogeneity of the system(Wang et al., 2024).

A zeta potential with an absolute value exceeding 25 mV is generally considered sufficient for stabilizing emulsion systems via electrostatic repulsion (Jiang et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1E, the zeta potentials of three replicate samples ranged from −27 to −28 mV, whose absolute values exceeded this common threshold. These values indicate that the emulsion droplets possess a high absolute surface charge, which generates strong electrostatic repulsion that effectively prevents droplet coalescence. This electrostatic stabilization is one of the key factors contributing to the physical stability of the RCOPI/XG-LEOPickering emulsion.

Rheological behavior of film-forming solutions

The rheological behavior of film-forming solutions is regarded as a critical parameter for evaluating their performance, as it effectively reflects the intrinsic correlation between the mechanical properties and molecular structure of biopolymer solutions. Within the investigated shear rate range (10–100 s−1), typical non-Newtonian behavior was exhibited by the solutions, characterized by viscosity reduction with increasing shear rate (Supplementary Fig. S1A). This shear-thinning phenomenon was attributed to the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonds under shear force(Jiang et al., 2020; Punia et al., 2019). The dynamic frequency sweep results indicated that both storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) increased with frequency (Hz), and viscous behavior was exhibited with G″ predominating (G″>G′) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Compared with the other groups, a shift of the G″-G′ crossover point toward lower frequencies was exhibited by RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 with increasing Pickering emulsion addition, which indicated a transition toward enhanced elastic characteristics. This change could be attributed to the modulation of the microstructure by emulsion particle addition, which altered system crosslinking (Gagliardi et al., 2020). The favorable rheological properties of the film-forming solutions are conducive to the subsequent preparation of films with uniform surfaces and intact structures through casting or coating processes.

The appearance of the film

As shown in Fig. 2B, increasing RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion content resulted in progressively darker film coloration and reduced transparency. These optical changes arose from the combined effects of the emulsion and anthocyanin colorant. Furthermore, both surface roughness parameters (Ra and Rq) increased with higher emulsion loading, and indicated rougher surface morphology (Fig. 2C–D). This was likely attributable to the successful incorporation of Pickering emulsion into the sodium alginate matrix, which promoted the formation of a microporous surface structure. Increasing RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion content resulted in a gradual increase in film thickness from 61.8 to 74.4 μm, attributable to the incorporation of composite particles into the SA matrix and their space-occupying effect (Zhang et al., 2024). As a control, equivalent amounts of RCOPI, XG, and LEO were directly incorporated into the SA-based film-forming solution. LEO was observed to float on the solution surface, and as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, abundant oil droplets were evident on the film surface along with obvious pores. This phenomenon was attributed to LEO volatilization and consequent phase separation during drying, which severely compromised the film's surface appearance.

As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the SA film exhibited high WC and MA values of 17.43% and 23.94%, respectively. The addition of Pickering emulsion significantly reduced these parameters, and further increases in emulsion content did not result in significant changes in WC or MA. This indicates that the incorporation of hydrophobic components decreased film moisture content and hindered water molecule adsorption and penetration (Dong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023).

WVP is an important indicator for evaluating the moisture barrier performance of films. The pure SA film exhibited a WVP of 6.36 × 10−11 g·m−1·s−1·Pa−1. As Pickering emulsion content increased, the WVP of the composite films increased accordingly, with the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film reaching 8.13 × 10−11 g·m−1·s−1·Pa−1 (Ran et al., 2025). The elevated WVP documented herein was attributable to the incorporation of Pickering emulsion, which disrupts the integrity of the sodium alginate continuous phase. By establishing low-resistance diffusion channels for water vapor molecules, such structural discontinuity underlies the observed increase in WVP (Zhu et al., 2025). Jackson Andson Medeiros et al. also observed that the incorporation of a Pickering emulsion formulated with palm oil, BCNC, and NaCl into the sodium alginate matrix increased the WVP of the resulting films (Medeiros et al., 2022). Homogenization of the emulsion yielded uniform and densely distributed submicron droplets. This highly dispersed state generated a substantial interfacial area, exerting dual effects. On the one hand, the numerous droplets served as densely interspersed structural defect sites within the film matrix, which significantly compromised effective hydrogen bonding between polymer chains and increased the fractional free volume. On the other hand, the homogeneous distribution disrupted the integrity of the continuous phase, and provided long-range diffusion pathways with reduced tortuosity for water molecules, which consequently increased WVP.

The TGA-DSC of the film

Thermal stability is a critical property for packaging materials, as it directly determines the performance consistency of films during both processing and storage. As depicted in Fig. 3(A–E), the TGA curves of the five film types are similar, all characterized by three distinct stages of weight loss. The first stage of weight loss (30–218 °C) corresponds primarily to the evaporation of moisture and volatile components from the film. As the Pickering emulsion content increased, the weight loss percentage decreased gradually at this stage. Compared with the SA film, the composite film RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 showed a lower weight loss of 10.89%. This reduction can be attributed to the enhanced barrier properties of the Pickering emulsion, which improve the film's ability to retain water and reduce its moisture content (Yang et al., 2024). The second stage of weight loss, occurring between approximately 220 and 300 °C, is associated with the rapid thermal decomposition of the film, which primarily involves the degradation of its SA and protein components. In the third degradation stage, the residual mass of the pure SA film was 33.59%, whereas that of the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film was 31.09%. A higher Pickering emulsion content led to a corresponding decrease in the final residual mass of the films. This reduction was attributed to the increased proportion of thermally labile components from the emulsion and the concomitant decrease in sodium alginate content, both of which lower the overall char yield at high temperatures (Ning et al., 2025).

Optical inspection and analysis of the film

As shown in Fig. 3F, the SA film and RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-0.1 film exhibited moderate UV shielding performance below 250 nm, with transmittance of approximately 50%. Above 400 nm, however, their shielding efficiency dropped below 20%, demonstrating that SA itself had limited absorption in this wavelength range. After incorporating the Pickering emulsion, the film's UV transmittance significantly decreased, especially for the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film. Consequently, the UV shielding performance across the entire spectrum (200–400 nm) was substantially improved, with shielding efficiency exceeding 80%. Notably, within the 200–300 nm range, the film achieved nearly complete UV blocking. These effects were attributed to the multi-scale scattering structure formed by solid particles within the film, which increased the optical path length of UV light through multiple scattering events, and enhances overall shielding efficiency (Ni et al., 2025). Additionally, the incorporated anthocyanin contributed to reduced light transmittance (Bi et al., 2019). These UV-blocking properties significantly reduce the damaging effects of ultraviolet radiation on packaged foods, inhibiting nutrient degradation, color fading, flavor deterioration, and photo-induced chemical reactions, and this extends shelf life and maintains product quality.

As shown in Fig. 3G, the SA film exhibited a broad absorption band in the 3000–3500 cm−1 region, a feature typical of to O—H stretching vibrations. Characteristic peaks were also observed at 1595 and 1408 cm−1, which were assigned to asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the COO− group, respectively. Compared with the pure SA film, the incorporation of RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion, even at higher loadings, did not significantly alter the FTIR absorption peaks of composite films or introduce any new peaks. This indicates that the emulsion formed no new chemical bonds and did not engage in strong interactions with sodium alginate molecules, but was rather physically entrapped within the alginate network (Gan et al., 2026).

XRD analysis is a rapid, non-destructive technique widely used for the qualitative characterization of crystalline structure of in film materials. It provides a critical means of correlating microstructural features with macroscopic properties. XRD analysis revealed broad, diffuse peaks in the 2θ range of 20°–22°, which corresponded to the characteristic diffraction pattern of amorphous SA (Fig. 3H). This confirmed that the SA matrix was predominantly amorphous. A comparison of the XRD patterns of RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA films with varying compositions revealed no significant changes in the position and width. This consistency indicates good compatibility between the RCOPI/XG-LEO Pickering emulsion and the SA matrix (Zhao et al., 2022).

The pH-responsive properties of the film

As shown in Fig. 4(A–C), after fumigation with ammonia water and hydrochloric acid, RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA films with different concentrations exhibited contrasting color changes, and turned yellow and red, respectively, which preliminarily confirmed their pH-responsive nature. Further color development tests in solutions of different pH (Fig. 4 D–E) showed that the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film underwent continuous and distinguishable color transitions across a pH range of 2 to 11. The color shifted from red under acidic conditions (pH 2) to pale red (pH 5), pale blue (pH 7), pale yellow (pH 9), and finally yellow (pH 11) as the pH increased. This color behavior is directly linked to the reversible structural transformation of anthocyanin molecules in response to pH variation. In acidic environments, anthocyanins exist predominantly as protonated flavylium cations. Their specific electronic structure results in strong absorption in the green–blue region of the visible spectrum, which produces a red hue. As the pH rises toward neutrality, deprotonation yields a quinonoidal base structure, which leads to a red-shifted absorption spectrum and a blue-violet appearance. Under alkaline conditions, anthocyanins further transform through an alcohol-type pseudobase intermediate to ultimately form a chalcone structure. This transition shifts the absorption peak further toward shorter wavelengths, and its residual absorption of violet light accounts for in a yellow color (Sendri & Bhandari, 2024). These results demonstrate that the composite film exhibits sensitive, stable, and reproducible color responses across a wide pH range. The color transition is clearly discernible and consistent with known optical manifestations of anthocyanin structural transformations, this indicates considerable potential for real-time visual monitoring of food freshness.

The WCA and the mechanical properties of the film

The WCA is a key indicator for characterizing the wettability of film surfaces. A WCA of 90° is typically used as the threshold to distinguish hydrophilic from hydrophobic surfaces (Li et al., 2022) As shown in Fig. 4F, the pure SA film showed a WCA of approximately 49.95°, which indicated its hydrophilic nature due to the abundance of hydrophilic groups in its molecular chains. After the incorporation of the Pickering emulsion, the WCA increased significantly. The RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-0.5 and RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 films exhibited angles of 70.25° and 81.4°, respectively. The observed increase is primarily attributed to the incorporation of Pickering emulsion, whereby emulsion droplets and their stabilizing particles undergo preferential migration toward the film surface during film formation, which significantly enhances surface roughness, macroscopically, this increased roughness manifests as a marked increase in the WCA (Ran et al., 2023). This is consistent with the WC and MA results discussed previously. Meanwhile, although the results revealed an increasing trend in WVP with higher emulsion loading, these observations are not inherently contradictory. The barrier performance of the films is not solely dictated by surface chemistry but is rather more profoundly governed by alterations in bulk-phase microstructure. As previously discussed, the introduction of excess emulsion disrupts the original dense hydrogen-bonding network among sodium alginate molecular chains. Emulsion droplets, acting as a discrete phase, occupy a finite volume within the continuous phase, and generate penetrating micropores or crack-like defects. These structural imperfections shorten the diffusion pathways for water vapor molecules and increase the fractional free volume, which offsets the barrier enhancement conferred by the increased surface hydrophobicit. Collectively, these results indicate that the moisture barrier performance of the films represents an integrated manifestation of surface wettability and bulk structural integrity.

The mechanical properties of SA-based films prepared with different SA concentrations are shown in Fig. 4G. With increasing Pickering emulsion content, both the tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) of the films first increased and then decreased. The pure SA film exhibited a TS of 70.93 MPa and an EAB of 5.09%. Incorporation of 0.1% and 0.5% Pickering emulsion enhanced both TS and EAB. However, at 2% emulsion loading, TS and EAB decreased to 53.52 MPa and 3.44%, respectively. The underlying reason lies in the dynamic evolution of the emulsion structure during the drying process. At low loading levels, uniformly dispersed emulsion droplets remain intact by virtue of interfacial film stability and steric stabilization, acting as physical crosslinking sites that effectively transfer and dissipate stress. Concurrently, hydrophobic particles fill micropores within the matrix and prolong water molecule diffusion pathways, that significantly enhances tensile strength and elongation at break. Relative to neat polymer films without emulsion addition, mechanical strength is markedly superior. However, as emulsion loading increases, excessive droplet proximity triggers interfacial film destabilization, disrupts the continuity of the film matrix, and generates interfacial defects, ultimately compromising film performance, leading to localized stress concentration and ultimately reducing both TS and EAB (Shi et al., 2016). As shown in Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, mechanical characterization further demonstrated that films prepared via direct addition underwent complete fracture under a 500 g load, while Pickering emulsion-containing films maintained structural integrity and sustained the load. Given the marked contrast in load-bearing capacity, alongside the differences in appearance, all subsequent formulations employed pre-emulsified additives delivered as Pickering emulsions within the SA matrix.

The biodegradability of the film

As shown in Fig. 5(A–B), SA-based films exhibited rapid weight loss and morphological shrinkage over time, which is attributable to the hydrophilicity of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. After four weeks, the SA film showed a degradation rate exceeding 80%, this indicated substantial biodegradability. The incorporation of the Pickering emulsion effectively prevented microbial penetration by enhancing the film hydrophobicity and leveraging the antimicrobial properties of the essential oil. Additionally, the emulsion filled the internal structure of the film and formed a barrier layer that further reduced the degradation rate. Consequently, under identical conditions, the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film achieved a final degradation rate of 60.89%. Overall, the addition of the Pickering emulsion enhanced film stability and degradation resistance while preserving favorable biodegradation potential.

The antibacterial activity and antioxidant properties of the film

The antibacterial activity of composite films was assessed against E. coli and S. aureus using the agar plate coating method. As shown in Fig. 5D, the SA film exhibited antibacterial properties, which are presumably attributed to its anthocyanin content. The composite films exhibited significant antibacterial activity against both bacterial strains tested. This inhibitory effect became stronger with increasing concentrations of Pickering emulsion. The suppression of S. aureus was particularly pronounced in the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-1 group, in which only a few colonies grew at the edges of the medium, whereas the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 group showed nearly complete inhibition of bacterial growth. This difference is likely because the antibacterial components in the essential oils can more easily penetrate the cell membrane of Gram-positive bacteria. Gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli, possess an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharides, which forms a specialized barrier that impedes the penetration of hydrophobic compounds (Nikaido & Vaara, 1985).

As shown in Fig. 5C, the pure SA film exhibited low DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging rates of 3.91% and 2.16%, respectively. However, the incorporation of the Pickering emulsion significantly enhanced the radical scavenging capacity of the films, with scavenging rates increasing in a concentration-dependent manner. The RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film achieved notably higher scavenging rates of 61.84% and 81.58% for the DPPH and ABTS radicals, respectively. This enhanced antioxidant performance is likely attributable to synergistic effects among the system components. Anthocyanins, which are rich in phenolic hydroxyl groups, can directly scavenge free radicals via hydrogen atom or electron transfer. Similarly, active components in the essential oil, such as terpenoids and phenolics, also contribute to antioxidant activity (Akachat et al., 2025). Furthermore, the interfacial structure of the Pickering emulsion facilitates the efficient loading and dispersion of these active components while providing a physical barrier that may enhance their stability.

The practical applications of film

Application of films in Banana slice preservation

To evaluate practical applicability, fruit preservation experiments were conducted using bananas as a model system. Bananas undergo a typical climacteric respiratory burst and are prone to rapid quality deterioration due to enzymatic browning and microbial infection (Brat et al., 2020). Supplementary Fig. S5A shows the visual appearance of banana slices from different treatment groups during storage. Slices in the control group developed pronounced surface browning and moisture loss, with significant darkening of core color observed by day 3. In contrast, banana slices wrapped with RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 film maintained superior visual quality throughout storage, exhibiting only minimal surface browning (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Furthermore, the film displayed distinct color changes during preservation, functioning as an effective visual freshness indicator (Supplementary Fig. S5D).

Weight loss analysis revealed that the control group exhibited the greatest weight loss and reached 15.66% by day 5 of storage, which reflected substantial moisture loss and consequent quality deterioration (Supplementary Fig. S5B). In contrast, samples wrapped with composite films showed significantly lower weight loss, averaging approximately 8.15%, with no significant differences among film-treated groups. This difference is attributed to the water vapor barrier properties of the films. Compared with the control, all film treatments effectively inhibited water evaporation, which helped retard quality degradation. The pH of control samples fluctuated considerably during storage, whereas that of RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2-treated samples remained relatively stable (Supplementary Fig. S5C). These results demonstrate that the composite film effectively preserved the quality of banana slices.

Application of films in pork

As pork is rich in water and protein, it is highly susceptible to rapid spoilage during storage due to the combined effects of microbial proliferation and lipid peroxidation. Fig. 6A and B show the changes in appearance of different treatment groups during storage and a schematic illustration of the preservation mechanism, respectively. The pork samples in the control group exhibited marked darkening and surface juice loss as early as the second day of storage. By the tenth day, the weight loss rate had reached 18.39% ± 2.00%. In contrast, all film treatments significantly delayed quality deterioration. Among them, the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 treated group exhibited a weight loss rate of 7.36% ± 0.27%, and demonstrated the best preservation effect (Fig. 6C).

The pH is regarded as a fundamental indicator of post-slaughter maturation and spoilage in pork. The dynamic changes in the pH of pork from the various treatment groups during storage are shown in Fig. 6D. Overall, the pH exhibited a fluctuating trend, with a slight initial decrease followed by a continuous rise. In the early stages, anaerobic glycolysis occurs in the muscle tissue, which produces lactic acid and causes a brief drop in pH. However, as storage time increased, the breakdown of proteins and amino acids was promoted by microbial proliferation and enhanced endogenous enzyme activity, and basic nitrogenous compounds such as amines were generated, which drove a gradual rise in pH. Significant differences in the rate of pH increase were observed among the different treatment groups. In the control group, the pH exceeded the spoilage threshold of 6.7 on approximately the fifth day of storage, whereas all film-treated groups effectively slowed the rise in pH. Particularly good performance was demonstrated by the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 group, whose pH did not reach the threshold until around the eighth day, and the spoilage process of the pork was significantly delayed.

TVB-N is regarded as a core indicator for assessing meat freshness. It refers to the alkaline volatile nitrogenous compounds, such as ammonia and biogenic amines, that are generated from protein breakdown by enzymatic and bacterial action during the spoilage of animal-derived foods. A higher level is indicative of lower meat freshness (Liu et al., 2025). In accordance with the hygiene standards for fresh pork, the maximum permissible limit for TVB-N is set at 15 mg/100 g (Sun et al., 2025). As storage time increased, a continuous upward trend was observed in TVB-N levels in all treatment groups, with the control group exhibiting the highest accumulation rate (Fig. 6E), and exceeding the limit as early as day 3, which indicates a higher spoilage risk for pork without preservation treatment. In contrast, the limit value was reached in the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 treatment around day 8, and the formation and accumulation of TVB-N were effectively delayed.

TBARS is regarded as an important indicator for assessing the extent of fat oxidation in pork. During storage, the TBARS values in all treatment groups exhibited a continuous rise as storage time increased, which indicated a progressive increase in the extent of fat oxidation (Fig. 6F). Among these, the control group exhibited the most rapid rise, from an initial 0.22 ± 0.01 mg MDA/kg to 1.96 ± 0.04 mg MDA/kg at the end of storage. In contrast, lower TBARS values were maintained by all preservation treatment groups than those in the control group throughout the storage period, and the most pronounced inhibitory effect was exhibited by the film treatment group, which recorded a TBARS value of 0.83 ± 0.01 mg MDA/kg at the end of storage. The results indicate that the oxidation process of pork fat could be effectively slowed down by preservation treatments, the extent of lipid deterioration could be reduced, and the quality stability of chilled pork could be maintained.

TVC is regarded as a key indicator for assessing the extent of microbial contamination and freshness deterioration in pork. The bacterial growth in the pork from each treatment group on the 10th day of storage was illustrated in Fig. 6G and H. The control group exhibited densely distributed colonies, as revealed by observation of the agar plates, which indicated significant microbial proliferation. In contrast, a markedly reduced and sparsely distributed number of colonies was observed on the plates of the various preservation treatment groups, by which effective inhibition of microbial growth was indicated. The quantitative results were consistent with the plate observations, the TVC of the control group had risen to 8.01 ± 0.15 lg CFU/g by the 10th day of storage, by which the safety limit of 6 lg CFU/g stipulated by the national standard was exceeded. In contrast, significantly lower TVC values were exhibited by all preservation treatment groups than those of the control group; notably, the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 group recorded a TVC of only 5.69 ± 0.27 lg CFU/g, which demonstrated a particularly pronounced inhibitory effect. These findings indicated that the growth and proliferation of microorganisms in pork could be effectively inhibited by the preservation treatments, and that the spoilage process could be delayed.

As the storage time increased, the color of RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA-2 first turned blue-purple and then gradually shifted to blue (Supplementary Fig. S6). Quantitative analysis of the L⁎a⁎b⁎ values showed that the film brightness index L⁎ of the film decreased from 75.67 to 59.67, while the a⁎ and b⁎ values increased and decreased continuously, respectively, which corresponded to shifts toward red and blue. The total color difference ΔE increased continuously and reached 17.70 by day 10. Meanwhile, ΔE was significantly correlated with TVB-N (Fig. 6 I—K). As shown inSupplementary Fig. S7, the R2 of the quadratic polynomial fitting curve was 0.9288. In protein-rich meat products, the TVB-N generated during spoilage and the accompanying pH changes can trigger color changes in anthocyanin. These results preliminarily indicate that the film can reflect the spoilage process in a real food matrix. However, anthocyanin are limited by their inherent sensitivity to light, heat, and oxidation, as well as by batch-to-batch variations in raw material composition, and interference from components such as proteins in real food matrices. Further efforts are still needed to improve stability and response accuracy, and expand their application potential (He et al., 2026).

Furthermore, with regard to the development of intelligent active packaging materials, issues such as raw material supply, scale-up potential, batch consistency, and overall economic feasibility still warrant further discussion. The RCOPI employed in this study is derived from agricultural by-products generated during the processing of Oviductus Ranae, and its high-value utilization does not require additional consumption of wild or farmed Rana chensinensis resources. At present, a relatively mature extraction process for RCOPI has been established, which enables stable scale-up preparation under laboratory conditions with good batch-to-batch quality consistency. Meanwhile, compared with other protein-based materials, RCOPI also shows potential advantages in terms of cost. RCOPI not only shows good economic feasibility and promising prospects for high-value utilization, but also aligns with the principles of sustainable resource utilization. Subsequent studies will further facilitate the translation of this laboratory-scale process to production scale and systematically evaluate its long-term storage stability and techno-economic feasibility, which provides a more solid basis for its practical application in intelligent active packaging.

Conclusion

This study reported an all-natural, multifunctional composite film by integrating Rana chensinensis ovum protein isolate/xanthan gum-stabilized Pickering emulsions and anthocyanin into a SA matrix. The incorporation of the Pickering emulsion significantly optimized the microstructure of the SA films, leading to enhanced water resistance and thermal stability due to the uniform dispersion of oil droplets and the synergistic effects at the protein-polysaccharide interface. In addition, the composite film demonstrated robust antioxidant and antibacterial activities, effectively delayed the physiological deterioration of banana slices and pork, and reduced weight loss during storage. Notably, the integration of anthocyanin endowed the film with highly sensitive pH-responsive properties, allowing for the real-time, visual monitoring of food freshness through distinct color transitions. In conclusion, the RCOPI/XG-LEO-SA film represents a sustainable and high-performance strategy for active and smart food packaging, serving as a reference for preserving perishable fruits and pork.
