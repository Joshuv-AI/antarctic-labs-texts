Single-site oxidation-directed topological evolution of hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks for efficient photocatalysis
Authors: Yaoyao Peng, Lewang Yuan, Kang-Kai Liu, Dalong Zhang, Jiang-Pei Yuan, Banglin Chen, Yu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photocatalytic hydrogen peroxide synthesis offers a green alternative to the energy-intensive anthraquinone process. However, hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks remain underexplored in this field due to their limited stability and lack of photoactive units. In this work, we modulate the oxidation state of photosensitive structural motifs to precisely direct topological evolution of hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks at a single site. Stepwise oxidation of the sulfur center enhances framework stability and optimizes hydrogen-bonding networks and charge transport pathways. PTH-SO2-HOF exhibits high charge separation efficiency, delivering a competitive hydrogen peroxide production rate of 2480 μmol g-1 h-1 in pure water, which reaches 5329 μmol g-1 h-1 with a sacrificial agent. Mechanistic studies reveal synergistic interplay between the higher oxidation state and hydrogen-bonding network in promoting the oxygen reduction pathway. This work provides a new strategy for designing robust and efficient hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks for photocatalysis.

Introduction

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is an important green chemical and an emerging energy carrier due to its high oxidation efficiency, environmental benignity, and high energy density1–3. However, the current anthraquinone process, which accounts for over 90% of global production, is not only energy-intensive but also generates substantial toxic byproducts and organic waste. This underscores the need for efficient and eco-friendly synthetic alternatives such as photocatalysis or electrocatalysis4–6. As a representative application of diverse framework materials, visible-light-driven photocatalysis is emerging as a sustainable alternative to conventional H2O2 production methods7–10. The overall catalytic process involves the photocatalytic water oxidation reaction (WOR) and oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), requiring photocatalysts to simultaneously possess strong light absorption, efficient photogenerated charge separation, and distinct catalytic sites for both WOR and ORR11. Furthermore, spatial proximity between the oxidation and reduction catalytic centers is essential for facilitating charge carrier separation and utilization12. In this context, various porous materials, including graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4)13,14, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)15,16, covalent organic frameworks (COFs)17–19, porous coordination cages (PCCs)20, and conjugated organic polymers21,22, have been extensively explored as promising photocatalysts for H2O2 production, achieving considerable efficiencies. Despite these advances, the development of new material platforms with distinct advantages and the continuous optimization of their structures remain highly desirable.

Among these materials, hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) represent an emerging class of porous crystalline materials that offer distinct advantages, including mild synthesis conditions, solution processability, and high crystallinity that enables precise structure-property correlations23–25. Within HOFs, five representative hydrogen-bonding motifs—O-H···O, O-H···N, N-H···O, N-H···N, and C-H···O—exhibit a gradient of bond energies (15-30 kJ/mol for O-H···O, 10-20 kJ/mol for O-H···N, and 5–15 kJ/mol for N-H···O/N-H···N/C-H···O) alongside distinct differences in directionality and dynamic reversibility26. The near-linear geometry (≈180°) of the O-H···O bond facilitates the formation of highly ordered porous architectures, while the O-H···N interaction combines the strong polarity of O-H donors with the electron-accepting capacity of nitrogen, enabling precise electronic modulation within the framework. Notably, the weaker N-H···O, N-H···N, and C-H···O bonds (8–15 kJ/mol, 5–15 kJ/mol and 5–10 kJ/mol, respectively)—though less robust than O-H···O—surpass most van der Waals interactions, striking a balance between structural stability and the formation of extended, adaptive hydrogen-bonding networks27,28. Leveraging their atomically precise pore environments and stable π-π stacking arrangements, HOFs constructed from diverse photoactive building blocks have shown promising performance in photocatalytic hydrogen evolution24,29, CO2 reduction30,31, and organic pollutant degradation32,33. However, the exploration of HOFs for photocatalytic H2O2 production remains limited, and designing systems that simultaneously achieve high catalytic activity and photostability poses a significant challenge. Recent advances highlight the potential of this field: in 2023, Hua34 et al. reported a perylene-based HOF (PTBA) with a H2O2 production rate of 2699 μmol g−1 h−1, while the Lu35 and co-workers developed a donor-acceptor HOF (TTF-Bpy-HOF) achieving 681.2 μmol g−1 h−1 under visible light. Despite these advances, mechanistic understanding and design strategies for HOF-based photocatalytic H2O2 production remain fragmented. There is a clear need for approaches to engineer photoactive HOFs with tunable charge-transfer pathways and long-term operational stability, thereby unlocking their full potential for sustainable chemical synthesis.

In this work, we introduce a synthetic protocol to obtain HOFs by integrating phenothiazine (PTH) as the photosensitive core building unit and N-H···N, N-H···O, and C-H···O bonds as the assembly interactions (Fig. 1). PTH has proven to be effective photosensitive moieties in MOFs, COFs and PCCs, amenable to post-synthetic modification36–41. The benzimidazole moiety provides primary hydrogen bonds through N-H···N interactions with adjacent building units, facilitating tunable framework topology (Fig. 1). More importantly, stepwise oxidation of the central sulfur atom in PTH enables gradient control over electronegativity and introduces secondary hydrogen bonds between photosensitive cores, thereby promoting charge transfer properties in the resulting photoactive HOFs (Fig. 1). When applied to photocatalytic H2O2 production, three heterotopic structures based on phenothiazine with different oxidation states exhibit orders-of-magnitude differences in catalytic activity, confirming the synergistic regulatory effect of framework oxidation state and hydrogen bond participation degree on photoreactivity. This work establishes a pathway for designing photoactive crystalline materials through hydrogen bond-mediated dynamic assembly strategies.Fig. 1Diff erent types of the hydrogen bond and their functionalities.Schematic illustration for constructing photoactive HOFs using phenothiazine (PTH) with benzimidazole-directed hydrogen bonding and stepwise sulfur oxidation for charge-transfer modulation.

Results

Preparation and structural characterization of PTH-HOFs

Building on prior research showing PTH, an N/S-heterocyclic aromatic donor, can modulate photocatalytic performance, this study applies this molecular design to HOFs36–38. We analyzed the electronic structures of three PTH-Bim monomers via electrostatic potential (ESP) calculations to investigate how sulfur oxidation affects the hydrogen bonding (Supplementary Fig. 1). ESP visualization revealed that sulfur oxidation reconstructs hydrogen bond acceptor distribution: in PTH-S-Bim, the N atom serves as the strongest acceptor; in PTH-SO-Bim, the O atom becomes the primary acceptor with enhanced ESP; in PTH-SO2-Bim, the ESP of the O atom decreases slightly but remains higher than in PTH-S-Bim. Meanwhile, ESP positive regions identified hydrogen bond donors, revealing differential donor-acceptor synergy during oxidation. Specifically, PTH-SO-Bim introduces an additional strong acceptor site while maintaining donor strength, whereas PTH-SO2-Bim enhanced both acceptor and donor strength. This ESP reconstruction directly reflects the electron-withdrawing effect of the sulfoxide (-SO-) and sulfone (-SO2-) groups. These groups withdraw electron density from the PTH core, creating regions of negative potential around the oxygen atoms. This electronic modulation is a factor in the subsequent topological evolution and photocatalytic performance, working in concert with the formal increase in oxidation state.

Based on this design, three aldehyde-based building blocks were employed: 4’-[3,7-bis(4-formylphenyl)−10H-phenothiazin-10-phenyl][1,1’-biphenyl]−4-carboxaldehyde (PTH-S-CHO), 4’-[3,7-bis(4-formylphenyl)−10H-phenothiazin-10-phenyl-5-oxide][1,1’-biphenyl]−4-carboxaldehyde (PTH-SO-CHO), and 4’-[3,7-bis(4-formylphenyl)−10H-phenothiazin-10-phenyl-5,5-oxide][1,1’-biphenyl]−4-carboxaldehyde (PTH-SO2-CHO). We first attempted a stepwise approach, which involved synthesizing the PTH-S-Bim monomer separately using o-phenylenediamine (OPD) as the linker. Crystals suitable for measurement, designated PTH-S-OH, were obtained via solvothermal synthesis in DMF/H₂O (2:1 v/v) (Supplementary Table 2). However, single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) analysis revealed that PTH-S-OH crystallizes in the monoclinic C2/c space group, with each asymmetric unit containing one PTH-S-Bim monomer and one water molecule (Supplementary Fig. 2). The incorporated water molecules participate in hydrogen bonding, giving rise to one-dimensional channels with an approximate width of 4.6 Å (Supplementary Figs. 2–4). This structure renders PTH-S-OH susceptible to framework collapse upon solvent removal or drying, as well as in aqueous environments, limiting its applicability in aqueous-phase photocatalysis.

Given the consistency between the solvent systems used for monomer synthesis in stepwise assembly and framework crystallization, we attempted to couple monomer synthesis with framework assembly for direct HOF construction. Specifically, the PTH-CHO building block and OPD precursor were mixed directly with KI as a catalyst in DMF/H2O mixed solvent. A Schiff base reaction enabled one-pot in situ assembly. This convergent strategy successfully and efficiently yielded three crystalline, benzimidazole-based heterotopic HOFs: PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF (Supplementary Figs. 5–7, and Supplementary Tables 1-2). This approach overcomes limitations of stepwise synthesis by achieving concurrent monomer and framework crystallization within a single reaction system, producing crystalline heterotopic structures.

SC-XRD analyses revealed the assembly patterns and topological evolution of the three PTH-HOFs. As shown in Supplementary Tables 1–2, PTH-S-HOF crystallizes in the monoclinic C2/c space group with one PTH-S-Bim monomer in its asymmetric unit. The structure features three intermolecular N-H···N hydrogen bonds (N···H distances: 2.02–2.17 Å) and one C-H···C interaction (2.72 Å), forming a (3,3)-connected layered rhombic network (Fig. 2a). In contrast, PTH-SO-HOF with sulfoxide (SO)-modified sulfur maintains the C2/c space group but exhibits a reconfigured hydrogen-bonding network: Each PTH-SO-Bim unit connects to six neighbors via one N-H···N bond (2.01 Å), four N-H···O bonds (2.10-2.16 Å), and two C-H···C interactions (2.65-2.66 Å), generating an expanded (6,3)-connected rhombic grid (Fig. 2b). The composition and structure of PTH-HOFs were characterized using nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (1H NMR), ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-vis), Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) (Supplementary Figs. 5–30). In the 1H NMR spectra of PTH-Bim monomers, two characteristic singlets at ~13 ppm (integral ratio 1:2), attributable to the imidazole N-H protons, confirm their formation (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). UV-vis spectra reveal broad visible-light absorption for PTH-Bims, similar to their ligand precursors, with blue shifts observed with increasing sulfur oxidation state (Supplementary Figs. 8–11). FT-IR spectra of PTH-HOFs show the disappearance of the aldehyde peak at 1691 cm−1 and the emergence of a band at 1663 cm−1, corresponding to the C = N stretching vibration of imidazole (Supplementary Figs. 12–14). Compared to PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF exhibits an additional peak at 1056 cm−1 attributed to S = O stretching vibrations42, while PTH-SO2-HOF displays a peak at 1143 cm−1 from O = S = O stretching vibrations (Supplementary Figs. 12–14)42. XPS S 2p spectra further confirm the progressive oxidation of sulfur sites (Supplementary Figs. 15–26). SEM images show uniform rectangular morphologies for all PTH-HOFs (Supplementary Fig. 27). PXRD analysis confirmed their crystallinity, with experimental patterns showing agreement with the simulated patterns derived from single-crystal data (Supplementary Figs. 28-30). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) demonstrates thermal stability, with all PTH-HOFs remaining stable without significant mass loss up to ~260 °C (Supplementary Fig. 31). Chemical stability tests reveal retained crystallinity and structural integrity after three days of immersion in H2O, MeOH, and H2O/MeOH (9:1, v/v), as evidenced by unchanged PXRD patterns (Supplementary Figs. 32–34). These characterization data collectively support the structural assignment of the heterotopological HOFs.

It is worth noting that while the experimental PXRD patterns of the three PTH-HOFs show agreement with the simulated patterns in terms of peak positions and overall diffraction profiles, minor peak shifts are observable in Supplementary Figs. 28–30. Such discrepancies between experimental and simulated patterns can be attributed to several factors. First, residual solvent molecules trapped within the frameworks may induce slight lattice expansions or contractions, leading to minor peak shifts. Second, the removal of guest molecules during activation can result in subtle rearrangements of framework packing. Third, variations in measurement conditions, such as temperature and humidity, may also contribute to these observations. Additionally, the possible presence of minor amorphous impurities or small amounts of unreacted starting materials cannot be ruled out, as is common in framework materials synthesized via solvothermal methods. Despite these minor peak shifts and the potential presence of impurities, the characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the key crystallographic planes remain identifiable. The combined characterization data, including XPS, FT-IR, and TGA (Supplementary Figs. 12–26 and 31), are consistent with the overall structural assignment.

The porosity characteristics of PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF were evaluated through gas sorption measurements. Initially, ethanol washing and supercritical CO2 activation were used to remove guest molecules. Subsequently, N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K. However, experimental results revealed limited adsorption capacity of the PTH-HOFs toward N2, a phenomenon observed in HOF-related studies43,44. This may be attributed to interactions between N2 molecules and channel apertures, which impede their diffusion within HOF frameworks. Therefore, CO2 adsorption measurements were conducted at 195 K to reassess the porosity features of these materials. The isotherms of all three PTH-HOFs exhibited Type I behavior, indicating a microporous nature (Supplementary Figs. 35–40). The apparent Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas calculated from the CO2 adsorption data are 197 m2 g−1, 269 m2 g−1, and 263 m2 g−1 for PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF, respectively. The pore size distributions were 0.40 nm, 0.41 nm, and 0.40 nm, respectively, consistent with the pore dimensions observed in their single-crystal structures (Supplementary Figs. 35–40). While the small pore apertures (~4 Å) and limited N2 uptake at 77 K may raise considerations about mass transfer, several lines of evidence suggest efficient molecular transport in this system. First, the kinetic diameters of the key reactants, O2 (3.46 Å) and H2O (2.65 Å), are smaller than the measured pore size. Second, the CO2 uptake (kinetic diameter 3.3 Å) at 195 K demonstrates accessible microporosity under conditions where diffusion is less hindered, which is relevant to room-temperature photocatalytic operations. The confirmed permanent porosity, combined with the stability, supports the effectiveness of our synthetic strategy in creating accessible porous frameworks.

Photoelectric characterizations of PTH-HOFs

After confirming the structures of PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF, their optoelectronic properties were further investigated. UV-vis spectroscopy was used to characterize the light absorption ranges of the PTH-HOFs. As shown in Fig. 3a, a blue shift in the absorption spectra is observed for PTH-SO-HOF and PTH-SO2-HOF compared to PTH-S-HOF. This blue shift is attributed to the electron-withdrawing nature of the S = O and O = S = O units introduced upon oxidation. By withdrawing electron density from the PTH core, these groups effectively increase the HOMO-LUMO gap, which not only widens the optical band gap but also thermodynamically adjusts the redox potentials45. The calculated optical band gaps of the PTH-HOFs were determined to be 2.41 eV, 2.77 eV, and 2.89 eV, respectively, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 41. A positive slope in the Mott-Schottky plots (Supplementary Figs. 42–44) confirms that all PTH-HOFs are n-type semiconductors18, with flat-band potentials of −1.00 V, −0.89 V, and −0.79 V (vs. Ag/AgCl, pH = 7.0). From these values, the corresponding conduction band potentials of PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF were determined to be −0.90 V, −0.79 V, and −0.69 V (vs. NHE), as illustrated in Fig. 3b. A detailed analysis of the band structures of the PTH-HOFs reveals that, thermodynamically, all three are capable of catalyzing both the 2e- oxygen reduction reaction (ORR, EO2/·O2- = −0.33 V) and the 4e- oxygen evolution reaction (OER, EH2O/O2 = 0.82 V), thereby enabling a catalytic cycle46. We next probed the photogenerated charge carrier behavior within the frameworks. To evaluate the photogenerated electron generation capabilities of PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF, solid-state electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed. As shown in Fig. 3c, PTH-SO2-HOF exhibited the greatest EPR signal intensity under both dark and light conditions. This result indicates that among the PTH-HOFs, PTH-SO2-HOF possesses the highest ability to generate photogenerated electrons, which is beneficial for its photocatalytic activity47–49. To assess the photogenerated charge separation capabilities of the PTH-HOFs, photoluminescence (PL) fluorescence spectroscopy and time-resolved fluorescence decay measurements were conducted. Although PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF share the same excitation wavelength maximum at 400 nm (Supplementary Fig. 45), differences in their charge separation abilities result in distinct fluorescence intensities. Specifically, PTH-SO2-HOF exhibited the lowest fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3d), indicating a lower degree of photogenerated charge recombination16,50. Under optimal excitation, PTH-SO2-HOF demonstrated the longest fluorescence lifetime (2.72 ns) compared to PTH-S-HOF (1.68 ns) and PTH-SO-HOF (1.75 ns) (Fig. 3e). The prolonged fluorescence lifetime further indicates that PTH-SO2-HOF suppresses photogenerated charge recombination, reflecting its photophysical properties20,51. Complementary insights into charge separation and transport were obtained from photoelectrochemical measurements. The transient photocurrent results (Fig. 3f) reveal that PTH-SO2-HOF exhibits the highest photocurrent density among the PTH-HOFs. Furthermore, in the EIS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 46), PTH-SO2-HOF demonstrates the lowest impedance compared to the other PTH-HOFs. These findings suggest that PTH-SO2-HOF has favorable photogenerated charge separation capability, facilitating efficient separation and transport of photogenerated carriers, and thereby contributing to photocatalytic activity52–55.

To directly probe the ultrafast charge carrier dynamics, we employed femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy56. As shown in Fig. 3g–i, upon 350 nm excitation, all three PTH-HOFs exhibit negative ground-state bleaching (GSB) signals across a broad spectral range, with negligible positive excited-state absorption (ESA). This spectral profile indicates efficient population of the excited state and mirrors the steady-state absorption characteristics of the materials. Kinetic analysis at the GSB maximum (Fig. 3j-l) reveals a biexponential decay, from which two characteristic time constants, τ1 and τ2, were extracted. The faster component (τ1) is attributed to the initial trapping or localized relaxation of hot carriers, while the slower component (τ2) is assigned to the interfacial charge transfer and recombination kinetics57. A systematic elongation of both lifetime components is observed with increasing sulfur oxidation state. The extracted time constants are: τ1 = 3.6 ps, τ2 = 0.34 ns for PTH-S-HOF; τ1 = 5.8 ps, τ2 = 0.58 ns for PTH-SO-HOF; and τ1 = 24.8 ps, τ2 = 1.29 ns for PTH-SO2-HOF. This represents approximately a 7-fold and 3.8-fold increase in τ1 and τ2, respectively, from PTH-S-HOF to PTH-SO2-HOF. The prolonged charge carrier lifetimes in PTH-SO2-HOF provide kinetic evidence for suppressed charge recombination and enhanced charge separation efficiency. This is consistent with the charge-transfer-mediated sequential energy transfer model, where the introduction of electron-withdrawing groups and the associated hydrogen-bonding network may create pathways for electron-hole separation and migration58. These fs-TA results, together with the PL and photoelectrochemical data, indicate that the hydrogen-bond-facilitated framework influences charge flow, thereby contributing to photocatalytic performance.

Photo-irradiated water oxidation reaction

Building on the photophysical properties of the PTH-HOFs, we evaluated their photocatalytic activities through H2O2 synthesis to validate the effectiveness of our synthesis strategy. The concentration of photogenerated H2O2 was quantified using iodometric titration, with the corresponding standard curve provided in Supplementary Figs. 47–489. First, we determined the optimal catalyst concentration by measuring H2O2 production rates under varying catalyst loadings. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 49, the highest activity was achieved at a concentration of 0.2 mg mL−1. Subsequently, the photocatalytic H2O2 production rates of the PTH-HOFs were assessed under Ar, Air, and O2 atmospheres at this optimal concentration (Fig. 4a). Among them, PTH-SO2-HOF exhibited the highest activity across all conditions. Under an O2 atmosphere without any sacrificial agent, it achieved a production rate of 2480 μmol g−1 h−1. In contrast, PTH-S-HOF showed a rate of 868 μmol g−1 h−1. This corresponds to an ~285% enhancement for PTH-SO2-HOF over PTH-S-HOF (Fig. 4c). The performance was reproducible, with a standard deviation of less than 2.3% across three independent measurements (Fig. 4c). The activity of PTH-SO2-HOF is consistent with its photophysical properties and suggests a synergistic effect between molecular electronic structure and framework topology. While stepwise oxidation of the sulfur atom enhances the electron-withdrawing capability of the PTH core, it also reshapes the framework architecture. As detailed in Fig. 2d-f, oxidation alters the hydrogen-bonding motifs, leading to a more interconnected framework. In PTH-SO2-HOF, the enhanced N-H···O and C-H···O interactions within its topology may facilitate charge carrier migration and create a favorable nanoenvironment for substrate enrichment and activation. To elucidate the reaction mechanism underpinning the photocatalytic performance, we performed a comprehensive investigation through controlled experiments. As shown in Fig. 4b, negligible H2O2 production was detected under dark conditions for all PTH-HOFs, confirming the essential role of photoexcitation. The introduction of specific quenchers provided critical mechanistic insights. The addition of an electron scavenger (KBrO3) or a superoxide radical (·O2-) scavenger (p-benzoquinone, p-BQ) led to a drastic, near-complete suppression of H2O2 generation (Fig. 4b). This strong inhibition points to a superoxide radical-mediated pathway, consistent with a 2e- ORR as inferred from the band structure analysis. We further investigated the potential involvement of singlet oxygen (1O2). While electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) confirmed 1O2 generation under illumination, the signal intensities were comparable across the catalysts and showed no correlation with their activities (Supplementary Fig. 50). More conclusively, adding β-carotene, a specific 1O2 quencher, did not suppress H2O2 production (Fig. 4b), excluding 1O2 as a key intermediate in the dominant reaction pathway.

Additionally, comparative experiments under different gas atmospheres further corroborated this conclusion, demonstrating that the introduction of O2 influence on the photocatalytic H2O2 production rates of the PTH-HOFs (Fig. 4a). Introducing methanol (10%) as a hole scavenger increased the H2O2 production rates for all catalysts. PTH-SO2-HOF achieved a rate of 5329 μmol g−1 h−1, representing an ~510% enhancement over PTH-S-HOF (1040 μmol g−1 h−1) under the same condition (Fig. 4c). The comparison of its performance with other classes of materials (such as MOF, COF, and HOF) indicates that PTH-SO2-HOF can serve as a catalyst for photocatalytic H2O2 production (Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, adding isopropanol (IPA), a hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenger, had a negligible effect on the reaction rate (Fig. 4b). The distinct roles of methanol and IPA offer mechanistic differentiation: methanol enhances yield by consuming photogenerated holes and suppressing charge recombination, whereas the inactivity of IPA confirms that free ·OH radicals are not primary reactive intermediates.

The above evidence collectively suggests that the WOR proceeds via a direct 4e- pathway to O2 without generating free ·OH, while H2O2 is produced predominantly through a superoxide radical-mediated 2e- ORR pathway. To obtain direct electrochemical validation, rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) measurements were conducted on PTH-HOFs59. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves confirmed the electrocatalytic activity of PTH-SO2-HOF, as evidenced by its lower onset potential and higher current density (Supplementary Fig. 51). Quantitative analysis yielded an average electron transfer number (n) of ~2.5 in the potential range of −1.0 to −0.4 V vs. Ag/AgCl (Supplementary Fig. 52), indicating that the ORR predominantly follows the desired 2e- pathway toward H2O2.

The importance of assembling molecular monomers into a well-defined framework is underscored by control experiments. The photocatalytic activities of the molecular components (PTH-S-Bim and PTH-SO2-Bim) are lower than those of their corresponding PTH-HOFs (Fig. 4d). This enhancement highlights the advantages conferred by the crystalline framework, which may mitigate charge recombination and decomposition prevalent in molecular catalysts. Although PTH-S-OH forms a framework, its activity remains low, comparable to its monomer, due to limited stability arising from hydrogen-bonding connections with water molecules (Fig. 4d). These results demonstrate the necessity of constructing stable framework materials to achieve photocatalytic performance.

The operational stability of the PTH-HOFs was evaluated through recycling tests. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 53–56, PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF maintained catalytic activity and crystallinity over five consecutive cycles. To assess long-term durability under realistic conditions, we further conducted an extended 10-cycle photocatalytic test for PTH-SO2-HOF in an aqueous medium (Supplementary Fig. 57). Furthermore, photocatalytic activity was reproducible across multiple independently synthesized batches, with standard deviations below 2.3% for all three materials (Fig. 4c). This reproducibility, combined with the retention of crystallinity and chemical structure after photocatalysis (Supplementary Figs. 58–63), indicates that the minor PXRD peak shifts observed in pristine materials (Supplementary Figs. 28–30) do not compromise catalytic activity. Comprehensive post-reaction characterizations, revealed no evidence of framework degradation, ligand leaching, or chemical transformation of catalytic centers during photocatalysis.

The cycling stability of PTH-SO2-HOF can be attributed to the multiple synergistic interactions within its framework. Structural analysis reveals that π-π stacking between rigid aromatic blocks provides mechanical robustness, while the hydrogen-bonding network ensures reversible topology formation. The synergy between these interactions contributes to the stability of the framework under photocatalytic conditions. In summary, the PTH-HOFs constructed via this synthesis strategy exhibit tunable photophysical and photocatalytic properties. PTH-SO2-HOF, achieves H2O2 production predominantly via a 2e- ORR pathway (O2 → ·O2- → ·OOH → H2O2), a conclusion supported by scavenger experiments, electrochemical verification, and control studies contrasting molecular and framework structures.

Isotopic labeling experiments were performed to trace the oxygen source11,37. When the reaction was conducted under an 18O2 atmosphere, mass spectrometry analysis of the produced O2 (from decomposed H2O2) showed a dominant signal at m/z = 36 (18O2), alongside minor signals for 16O18O (m/z = 34) and 16O2 (m/z = 32) (Fig. 5d). Conversely, when H218O was used as the reactant under an Ar atmosphere, 18O2 was detected both in the residual gas and in the product H2O2 (Fig. 5e, f). These results confirm a dual-pathway mechanism: H2O2 is produced predominantly via the 2e- ORR of adsorbed O2 (O2 → ·O2- → ·OOH → H2O2), while a parallel 4e- WOR occurs (H2O → O2), generating O2 that can be subsequently fed back into the ORR cycle, creating an internal oxygen loop.

In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) provided evidence for key adsorbed intermediates (Fig. 5g–i). Prior to irradiation (0 min), a peak at ~962 cm−1, assigned to adsorbed O2 (O2*, where * denotes adsorbed species), was observed11,64–66. Upon illumination (0-60 min), new peaks sequentially emerged at ~1166 cm−1 (·O2-*), 1201 cm−1 (·OOH*), and 1374 cm−1 (HOOH*), mapping the ORR pathway from O2 to H2O211,64–66. The intensities of these intermediate peaks, particularly for O2* and HOOH*, were most pronounced for PTH-SO2-HOF, consistent with its activity. Notably, no characteristic peak for adsorbed ·OH (typically at 1362 cm−1)11,64–66 was detected, ruling out its involvement as a major intermediate and aligning with scavenger experiments.

XPS analysis of the pristine PTH-HOFs was conducted to probe their initial surface chemical states (Supplementary Figs. 64–66). The O 1 s spectrum of PTH-S-HOF (containing no intrinsic oxygen) showed only a signal from ambiently adsorbed oxygen species. In contrast, the spectra of PTH-SO-HOF and PTH-SO2-HOF exhibited more complex profiles. Deconvolution confirmed contributions from both the intrinsic oxygen in their S = O/SO2 moieties and a distinct component from surface-adsorbed oxygen. Notably, the adsorbed oxygen signal was most pronounced in pristine PTH-SO2-HOF, indicating its initial surface coverage of such species prior to any reaction. This characteristic may be attributed to the electronic structure of PTH-SO2-HOF. The electron-withdrawing nature of the sulfone groups renders the PTH core electron-deficient, which may foster an affinity for adsorbing oxygen-containing molecules from the environment. This oxygen-enriched surface state is thus a consequence of its molecular design and may contribute to the subsequent photocatalytic oxygen reduction cycle.

Theoretical calculations and proposed reaction mechanism

To gain insights into the charge transfer and reaction mechanisms, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations67. Analysis of the ESP and charge distribution suggests that photogenerated electrons preferentially accumulate on the PTH core, a trend that appears enhanced by sulfur oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 1)67. Quantitative calculations indicate that the PTH core in PTH-SO₂-HOF accommodates up to 76.40% of photogenerated electrons, compared to 10.71% in PTH-S-HOF and 10.66% in PTH-SO-HOF (Fig. 6a, and Supplementary Figs. 67–69). This electron accumulation is consistent with the electron-withdrawing effect of the sulfone groups68,69. Further analysis from HOMO-LUMO orbital calculations (Fig. 6b) suggests spatial charge separation70. The LUMO is predicted to be predominantly localized on the PTH core, while the HOMO resides mainly on the imidazole linkers, with this separation being most pronounced in PTH-SO2-HOF. To investigate possible active sites, control experiments with N-HOF (lacking the PTH core) were performed, which showed a lower activity of 332 μmol g−1 h−1, suggesting that the phenothiazine-derived S/O sites contribute to the catalytic performance (Supplementary Figs. 70, 71, and Table 3). DFT calculations of the 2e- ORR pathway (O2 → ·O2- → ·OOH → H2O2) suggest the formation of the ·OOH* intermediate as the rate-determining step (RDS) (Fig. 6c). The calculated energy change for this step varies from -5.62 eV for PTH-S-HOF to -0.68 eV for PTH-SO2-HOF, which is consistent with the in-situ DRIFTS observations of ·OOH* kinetics. DFT calculations were also employed to investigate the complementary WOR pathway on the imidazole linkers (Supplementary Fig. 72). The calculations suggest that WOR may proceed via a 4e- pathway (2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e-), with the free energy change for the potential-determining step (*OH formation) calculated to be −11.23 eV, −11.13 eV, and -9.54 eV for PTH-S-HOF, PTH-SO-HOF, and PTH-SO2-HOF, respectively. These computational results provide insights that are consistent with the proposed reaction pathways. It should be noted that these computational models represent simplified systems, and the actual catalytic environment, including solvation effects, dynamic framework flexibility, and interactions with reactants, may influence the energetics and reaction pathways. As such, the calculated values are intended to provide qualitative trends rather than quantitative predictions.

Integrating the experimental evidence with theoretical calculations, we propose that the photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis over PTH-HOFs operates via an ORR-dominated multi-pathway cooperative mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 6d. Under photoexcitation, PTH-HOFs generate photogenerated electrons and holes. Specifically, the photogenerated electrons flow toward the PTH core, while the holes migrate to the imidazole units. Subsequently, the photogenerated electrons and holes react with O2 and H2O, respectively, generating intermediates such as ·O2- and ·OOH, which ultimately lead to the formation of H2O2 (ORR) and O2 (WOR). Notably, the O2 generated in the WOR pathway can further participate in the ORR pathway, providing substrate support for the photochemical synthesis of H2O2. The differences in electrostatic potential distribution and energy barriers may contribute to the photophysical properties and photocatalytic activities observed among the three PTH-HOFs. These findings suggest that the synthesis strategy can modulate the photocatalytic performance of PTH-HOFs.

Discussion

By tuning the photosensitive structural motifs within the molecular building blocks, we successfully prepared three PTH-HOFs via a one-pot strategy. This approach mitigated interference during the assembly process, thereby enhancing the chemical stability of the resulting frameworks. The presence of a single-site on the ligand plays a role in dictating the topological variations of the PTH-HOFs. These topological differences, in turn, influence the charge separation and transfer efficiency within the materials. PTH-SO2-HOF exhibits a H2O2 production rate of up to 5329 μmol g−1 h−1 with the addition of methanol as a sacrificial agent. Combined control experiments and theoretical calculations suggest that the higher oxidation state of the phenothiazine core and the associated evolution in framework topology are contributing factors. These factors may synergistically contribute to charge separation and transfer and to the reaction pathways. Mechanistic studies reveal an ORR-dominated multi-pathway cooperative mechanism, where the water oxidation reaction serves as a synergistic cycle. The stability of the framework originates from a supramolecular network formed through hydrogen-bonding and π–π stacking interactions. This work establishes a proof-of-concept, illustrating the relationship between molecular oxidation-state engineering, framework stability, and photocatalytic efficacy. While industrial-scale translation will require addressing engineering challenges such as scalable synthesis and process integration, the design strategy demonstrated here—modulating both electronic structure and supramolecular topology at a single site—provides a blueprint for navigating these future hurdles. By establishing structure-property relationships and demonstrating the simultaneous enhancement of stability and charge transport, this study contributes to the development of robust photocatalysts for the practical application of organic porous materials.

Photocatalytic H2O2 production

Photocatalytic reaction: In a typical experiment, 1 mg of photocatalyst was dispersed in 5 mL of deionized water in a 30 mL quartz reaction tube. The mixture was sonicated in the dark for 15 min and then purged with oxygen for 30 min. The reaction was then irradiated using a 300 W Xe lamp (PLS-SXE 300 + ) equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter; the light intensity was calibrated to 300 mW cm−2 using a radiometer. After 1 h of illumination, the catalyst was removed by filtration, and the H2O2 concentration in the solution was quantified. All photocatalytic reactions were performed in triplicate. Reported production rates represent mean values from three independent experiments, and error bars in the figures indicate standard deviation (SD).

H2O2 quantification: H2O2 was quantified by iodometry. After 1 h of illumination, an aliquot of the solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and diluted with deionized water. Then, 1 mL of the diluted solution was mixed with 1 mL of 0.1 M (25 mL) potassium hydrogen phthalate (C8H5KO4) and 1 mL of 0.4 M (25 mL) potassium iodide (KI). The absorbance at 350 nm was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and the H2O2 concentration was determined from a standard calibration curve.

Detection of ·O2- by NBT: Photocatalyst (1 mg) was mixed with 8 mL of NBT solution (0.05 mM) (25 mL) in a quartz tube. The mixture was irradiated with a 300 W Xe lamp (λ > 420 nm) for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the UV-Vis spectrum was recorded.

18O2 isotope labeling experiments: Under an 18O2 atmosphere, a reaction mixture containing H216O (2 mL) and photocatalyst (5.0 mg) was irradiated with a 300 W Xe lamp (λ > 420 nm) for 10 h. The supernatant was then decomposed with MnO2, and the gas phase was analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS, Agilent 7890B).

H218O isotope labeling experiments: Under an Ar atmosphere, a reaction mixture containing H218O (2 mL) and photocatalyst (5.0 mg) was irradiated with a 300 W Xe lamp (λ > 420 nm) for 10 h. The gas phase was first analyzed by GC-MS. The suspension was then decomposed with MnO2, and the released gas phase was also analyzed by GC-MS.
