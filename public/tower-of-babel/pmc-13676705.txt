Welfare aspects of dromedary camels (Camelus dromedarius) and their impact on meat quality: Review
Authors: Isam T. Kadim, Issa S. Al-Amri, Abdulaziz AlKindi, Osman Mahgoub, Samira Farhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Consumer concern about animal welfare is increasing, and inadequate pre-slaughter handling of dromedary camels can negatively affect both animal welfare and meat quality. Road transport is a main stressor, with its intensity influenced by factors such as transport distance, vehicle type, load density, ambient temperature, humidity, topography, and the nature of the journey. Multiple factors including transportation, loading, lairage conditions, and slaughter methods, can decrease muscle glycogen levels and increase meat pH, thereby deteriorating meat quality. In developing countries, camel slaughter practices often fail to meet animal welfare standards due to inadequate transport, handling, and restraint methods. These issues are further worsened by the absence of legislation and guidelines specifically addressing camel welfare. Camels show different physiological responses to cope with pre-slaughter stress, which can disrupt bodily functions and oxidative stability. This results in poor processing, functional, and sensory qualities of the meat, increasing the risk of consumer rejection due to dark meat colour or other quality parameters. Therefore, improving camel welfare requires considerable improvements in slaughterhouse infrastructure, management practices, and effective supervision, along with the adoption of humane technologies. Stunning and slaughter methods also play a critical role in animal welfare and meat quality. To ensure product safety and ethical treatment, camel meat production must operate within a transparent and welfare-focused supply chain. This review focuses on major pre-slaughter factors affecting camel welfare and their direct impact on meat quality.

Introduction

Dromedary camels (Camelus dromedarius) play a fundamental socio-economic role in many countries in Asia and Africa. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (Food & Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2025), global camel meat production is estimated at over 604 thousand tons, with a global camel population exceeding 41 million. Camel production represents a vital animal sector in many arid and semi-arid regions, providing transportation, milk, meat, and socioeconomic advantages. Recently, camel meat production has received growing attention because of its nutritional value and the expanding demand for alternative red meat sources (Kadim et al., 2024). Despite their importance, the handling of camels during loading, transportation, unloading, lairage, and slaughter can be highly stressful, adversely affecting both animal welfare and meat quality in dromedary camels (Kadim et al., 2024). General meat science principles indicate that pre-slaughter stress can negatively affect meat quality and animal welfare (Lawrie & Ledward, 2006), while studies in cattle have shown that pre-slaughter stress adversely affects both meat quality and animal welfare (Roudbari et al., 2025). Warriss (1996) and Sullivan et al. (2022) identified the key attributes of meat quality as appearance, technological characteristics, palatability, wholesomeness, and ethical quality (Warriss, 1996; Sullivan et al., 2022). In dromedary camels, pre-slaughter handling can affect lean meat quality through alterations in muscle glycogen stores (Kadim et al., 2024). A similar relationship between pre-slaughter stress, muscle glycogen depletion, and meat quality has also been reported in lambs (Xin et al., 2018). General livestock handling guidelines proposed by Smith and Grandin (1998)) indicate that appropriate pre-slaughter handling practices can substantially improve animal welfare and meat quality parameters (Smith & Grandin, 1998). Although animal welfare and its influence on meat quality traits have received substantial consideration in developed countries (Kadim et al., 2024; Terlouw & Bourguet, 2022), dromedary camel welfare research remains relatively limited (Kadim et al., 2024), particularly in developing countries where most camels are raised and where awareness, training, infrastructure, and funding are often inadequate.

The World Organization for Animal Health (World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), 2018) describes animal welfare as "the mental and physical condition of an animal in relation to the environments in which it lives and dies. Animal welfare is influenced by multiple factors, including emotional, physiological, biochemical, and behavioral responses, as well as reactions to chemical, physical, and social stimuli. Fear, pain, and stress are interconnected components that can compromise welfare. In addition to reducing negative experiences such as pain, fear, and distress, current animal welfare frameworks emphasize the idea of a 'Life Worth Living,' whereby animals should be provided with chances to experience positive welfare states, including comfort, pleasure, interest, confidence, and the expression of species-specific behaviors. This approach recognizes that good welfare involves not only preventing suffering but also promoting positive experiences that contribute to an overall quality of life. Growing public awareness of animal welfare issues has increased consumer expectations regarding the ethical treatment of animals and strengthened the adoption of welfare-oriented management practices through the meat production system. Accordingly, animal welfare has become an essential component of sustainable animal production, affecting animal health, productivity, meat quality, and consumer acceptance. Stress is the body’s behavioral and physiological response to perceived threats to homeostasis (Tilbrook & Clarke, 2006), while suffering occurs when an animal's coping capacity is exceeded (Njisane & Muchenje, 2016). Stressors may be physical, physiological, or psychological and can triggerthese responses (Cappellozza & Marques, 2021). In dromedary camels, common pre-slaughter stressors include deprivation of feed and water, fatigue, injuries, exposure to disease, mixing with unfamiliar animals, vibrations, noise, poor road conditions, high stocking density, poor vehicle design, and harsh environmental conditions, all of which can adversely affect both camel welfare and meat quality (Kadim et al., 2024). Similar adverse effects of pre-slaughter stress have also been recognized in cattle, where transport conditions, handling practices and lairage management affect animal welfare and meat quality (Hultgren et al., 2022; Sullivan et al., 2022). Most slaughter facilities are not designed to accommodate the size or the specific behavioral characteristics of camels. Moreover, camel meat production and consumption remain relatively low compared with those of other red meat species, which has led to limited investment in camel-specific infrastructure (Kadim et al., 2013).

In many countries, camel welfare is further compromised by out-of-date slaughterhouse infrastructure, lack of education and training for camel handlers, and inadequate pre-slaughter stunning and slaughter practices. For instance, dromedary camels are often denied access to clean water during the lairage time, leading to dehydration. Loading and unloading facilities are usually poor, increasing the risk of injuries during manual handling (Kadim et al., 2024). Camels react to stressful environments with complex physiological, biochemical, and behavioral responses such as adrenaline, corticosteroids, glucagon, prolactin, and vasopressin, along with elevated levels of creatine kinase activity and lactate concentrations in muscle tissue (Lemrhamed et al., 2019). These changes increase metabolic activity and elicit behavioral responses that help camels cope with stress. (Lemrhamed et al., 2019). Camel pre-slaughter stress can substantially deplete muscle glycogen reserves, which in turn impairs meat quality traits such as tenderness, color, water-holding capacity, cooking loss, shelf life, and sensory properties (Hamad et al., 2023). Insufficient glycogen reserve in muscles leads to high ultimate pH due to reduced lactic acid accumulation during postmortem anaerobic glycolysis (Xin et al., 2018; Matarneh et al., 2023). The physiological responses of camels to pre-slaughter stress are also similar to those reported in cattle (Hultgren et al., 2022; Sullivan et al., 2022; Wibowo et al., 2022). Therefore, maintaining proper camel welfare during pre-slaughter handling is essential, not only from an ethical standpoint but also to improve meat quality and protect slaughterhouse workers. Welfare assessment should consider not only productivity and physical condition but also the animal’s ability to experience comfort.

Compared with cattle and small ruminants, scientific literature addressing pre-slaughter welfare and meat quality interactions in dromedary camels is extremely limited. This knowledge gap is particularly concerning given the rapid rise in camel meat production and the unique physiological stress responses of camels. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing species-specific guidelines that ensure ethical slaughter practices and improve carcass quality traits such as pH, colour, tenderness, and water-holding capacity.

Despite the growing importance of camel meat production, scientific knowledge regarding the relationship between pre-slaughter welfare and meat quality in dromedary camels remains limited compared with that available for cattle and small ruminants. Most available information is fragmented and focuses on specific aspects of transportation, handling, lairage, or slaughter, resulting in an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms linking welfare status to meat quality characteristics. Given the unique physiological adaptations of camels and the increasing demand for camel meat in many countries, there is a need to integrate present knowledge and identify research gaps that may hinder the development of species-specific welfare guidelines. Therefore, this review synthesizes current evidence on main pre-slaughter stressors, associated physiological and behavioral responses, and their consequences for meat quality, while highlighting priorities for future research, legislation, and welfare improvement strategies.

Methodology and literature search strategy

This review was structured as a narrative review combining the authors' long-standing expertise in meat science, animal welfare and camel husbandry with an organized search of the scientific literature. Relevant publications were identified through searches of major electronic databases, including ScienceDirect, Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar. Searches were carried out using combinations of the following keywords: animal welfare, camel welfare, dromedary camel, Camelus dromedarius, transportation stress, cortisol, stress biomarkers, pre-slaughter handling, lairage, stunning, slaughter methods, meat quality, and welfare legislation.

Priority was given to publications related to dromedary camels published between 2000 and 2025. Earlier landmark publications were included when they provided important information on animal welfare, stress physiology, slaughter practices, or meat quality. Since research specifically addressing dromedary camels remains limited, selected studies involving cattle, sheep, goats, alpacas, and other livestock species were also included when they provided relevant comparative information and helped interpret camel responses. Furthermore, international guidelines and related documents from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the World Organization for Animal Health (World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH)), the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), and documents from other recognized authorities were also consulted.

The published literature was assessed for significance to the objectives of this review, with particular emphasis on studies examining the association between pre-slaughter handling, animal welfare indicators, physiological stress responses, and meat quality characteristics in dromedary camels. The objective was not to achieve a systematic review or meta-analysis but rather to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current scientific knowledge and identify gaps requiring further investigation.

Preslaughter handling treatments

In dromedary camels, pre-slaughter stress begins as early as the farm, breeding site, or market and continues through several stages, including loading, transportation, unloading, lairage, and slaughter (Kadim et al., 2024). Transportation is a major stressor, triggering physiological, hematological, biochemical, and endocrine responses that compromise animal welfare and, consequently, meat quality. During transportation and pre-slaughter handling, dromedary camels exhibit raised concentrations of cortisol, thyroid hormones, glucose, and oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde and catalase activity (El Khasmi et al., 2015). These stress indicators are positively correlated with travel distance and lairage conditions, consistent with findings in pigs where stress biomarkers are closely associated with meat and carcass quality outcomes (Dokmanovic et al., 2015). In dromedary camels, additional responses include hemolysis, increased leukocyte counts, and a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, all of which reflect acute stress (Bargaâ et al., 2016; Lemrhamed et al., 2019).

Slaughter-related stress is affected by several factors including the duration of camel restraint, water and feed deprivation, and lairage time. Studies in cattle and other livestock species have demonstrated that these factors markedly affect animal welfare and physiological stress responses (Bourguet et al., 2011; Terlouw et al., 2011). In dromedary camels, Lemrhamed et al. (2019), reported that the halal slaughter process may reactivate stress responses that had previously declined during lairage, as indicated by renewed increases in cortisol, glucose, and other biomarkers. Evidence from pigs indicates that pre-slaughter stress activates neuroendocrine stress responses involving the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and associated physiological biomarkers (Oliván et al., 2018). In dromedary camels, comparable pre-slaughter stress responses, including changes in cortisol and glucose concentrations, have also been reported (Lemrhamed et al., 2019).

Stress is intensified when camels witness the slaughter of other animals (Guya & Neme, 2015). Best practices for the pre-slaughter handling camels include: 1. the use of trained experienced handlers at all stages from loading to slaughter; 2. calm and gentle loading, unloading and movement of camels without shouting, beating, or excessive force; 3. avoidance of overcrowding during transport and excessive lairage periods; 4. minimizing exposure of camels to noise and unfamiliar animals; 5. designing passageways and entry points that facilitate voluntary forward movement and reduce fear responses, 6. transporting camels in appropriately designed vehicles equipped with non-slip flooring, adequate headroom, effective ventilation, appropriate stocking density, and secure partitions to minimize slipping, falling, and injuries ; and 7. avoiding the slaughter of camels within sight of other animals (Kadim et al., 2024).

These practical measures are consistent with established welfare-oriented livestock handling principles and provide a basis for developing camel-specific handling protocols (Kadim et al., 2024; Grandin & Committee, 2017).

Recent camel-specific evidence further indicates that positive-reinforcement-based training can facilitate voluntary self-loading and unloading and may help mitigate transport-related stress and reduce handling-related risks for both camels and handlers (Masebo et al., 2026).

Transportation stress

Dromedary camels are often raised in remote rural areas, far from slaughterhouses, which necessitates transportation either on foot or by vehicle (Kadim et al., 2013). However, camel transportation remains poorly regulated in many camel-rearing regions, with limited standardized practices regarding loading and unloading, vehicle design and size, journey duration, loading density, and space allowance (Padalino et al., 2026; Kadim et al., 2024). Recently, a camel-specific welfare assessment protocol has been developed to evaluate dromedary camels before and after loading, during the journey, upon arrival, and after arrival, together with indicators related to the loading and unloading process, vehicle conditions, and journey characteristics (Padalino et al., 2026). Most animal handlers lack formal training in camel welfare, which further exacerbates stress during transport (Kadim et al., 2024).

Sources of stress during transport

During transportation, dromedary camels are exposed to multiple emotional and physical stressors, including inappropriate handling during loading and unloading, often using cranes or unsuitable ramps (Fig. 1: putting camels in cargo nets and hoisting with a crane should never be used), poor vehicle design and overcrowding, unpaved, bumpy roads and extreme temperatures, noise, vibration, high speed, and prolonged journey without rest. Loading ramps and vehicle floors should be fitted with non-slip surfaces, as recommended in General livestock handling guidelines (Grandin & Committee, 2017). In dromedary camels, these stressors activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in alterations in cardiovascular, immune, and endocrine functions (El Khasmi et al., 2010; Lemrhamed et al., 2019). Studies in other livestock species have also shown that emotional stressors, such as loud truck noise, intensify physiological disruption (Iulietto et al., 2018). 4.1.1 Loading: loading is one of the most significant phases of transportation, during which dromedary camels may experience considerable emotional and physical stress. Stress can result from inappropriate handling practices, including the use of cranes or unsuitable loading equipment (Fig. 1). The practice of placing camels in cargo nets and hoisting them with a crane should never be used due to the risk of injury and severe distress. To reduce stress, camels should be handled calmly and quietly during loading (Kadim et al., 2024). Loading facilities should incorporate well-designed ramps with suitable elevations, sufficient safety features, and structurally sound construction to ensure safe and practical camel movement. General livestock guidelines recommend that loading and unloading ramps should ideally have a slope not exceeding 20° (approximately 36% gradient) for animals, with gentler slopes preferred whenever possible to facilitate animal movement and reduce the risk of slipping, falling, and handling-related stress. Moreover, ramps and loading areas should be equipped with non-slip flooring to decrease the risk of slipping and injury (Grandin & Committee, 2017). Because camel-specific research describing an optimal ramp angle remains limited, the approximately 36% gradient recommended in general livestock guidelines may be considered for camels in the absence of species-specific evidence. The principal features of a welfare-oriented vehicle for dromedary camel transportation, together with recommended and unacceptable handling practices during loading and unloading, are illustrated in Fig. 1.

4.1.2 In-Transit: During transit, dromedary camels are usually subjected to a range of environmental and management-related stressors that can adversely affect their welfare and meat quality parameters. These include poor vehicle design, overcrowding, unpaved and bumpy roads, excessive vehicle speed, noise, vibration, extreme temperatures, and prolonged journeys without adequate rest. Studies in other livestock species have shown that loud truck noise can further aggravate stress responses and increase physiological disturbances (Iulietto et al., 2018). In dromedary camels, exposure to these conditions activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, resulting in alterations to cardiovascular, immune, and endocrine functions (El Khasmi et al., 2010; Lemrhamed et al., 2019).

4.1.3 Off-Loading: The off-loading procedure represents another potential source of stress for dromedary camels upon arrival at the slaughterhouse or destination. General livestock guidelines indicate that poorly designed or excessively steep ramps can increase the risk of fear, injury, and distress (Grandin & Committee, 2017). Therefore, unloading facilities should provide safe, suitable, and non-slip surfaces that facilitate smooth camel movement. Calm handling practices during off-loading are essential to reduce stress and support animal welfare before entry into lairage and subsequent slaughter procedures.

Physiological and biochemical effects

Transportation induces considerable physiological changes in dromedary camels (Table 2). These changes include elevated cortisol and plasma glucose levels , increased heart and respiratory rates, increased rectal temperature, a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, alterations in thyroid hormone levels, and reduced muscle glycogen (El Khasmi et al., 2015; Lemrhamed et al., 2018). In goat kids, transport- and stress-related physiological responses have been associated with alterations in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (Sowińska et al., 2020). In cattle, this response is largely driven by stress hormones such as adrenaline and corticosteroids, which increase neutrophil counts and reduce lymphocytes, particularly helper T cells, thereby suppressing cell-mediated immunity (Earley et al., 2017). In dromedary camels, the neutrophil-to-lymphocytes ratio is positively correlated with cortisol, glucose, and lactate levels under transport and preslaughter stress (Lemrhamed et al., 2018; Lemrhamed et al., 2019), and may reflect oxidative stress and immunosuppression, increasing susceptibility to infections and inflammatory diseases. Impact of transportation on meat quality

In dromedary camels, pre-slaughter stress reduces muscle glycogen reserves and hampers normal postmortem acidification. As a result, meat often exhibits undesirable characteristics, including an elevated ultimate pH, dark and firm texture, dry surface, diminished tenderness and flavor, and increased drip loss, cooking loss, and malondialdehyde, a key marker of oxidative damage. Although El Khasmi et al. (2010) found no significant change in ultimate pH after 2 h of dromedary camel transport, most studies show that transportation generally causes marked declines in overall meat quality (El Khasmi et al., 2010; Kaoutar et al., 2016). Comparable effects have been reported in cattle (Hultgren et al., 2022) and sheep (Ruiz-De-La-Torre et al., 2001), indicating that transport stress adversely influences meat quality across livestock species, although the magnitude of the response may vary among species.

Mitigation strategies

Reducing transportation stress is essential to protect dromedary camel welfare and preserve meat quality. Key strategies include using well-designed vehicles with adequate space and ventilation, training handlers in low-stress management techniques, ensuring calm and gentle loading and unloading in quiet surroundings, and providing sufficient lairage time after transport to allow emotional and physiological recovery. Based on studies of dromedary camels , Kadim et al. (2024, Fig. 2) identified road transport as one of the most stressful pre-slaughter stages and emphasized the urgent need for species-specific guidelines and improved infrastructure. Even though transportation characterizes one of the most significant pre-slaughter stressors, the succeeding lairage period may either assist recovery or add additional stress depending on management practices. Lairaging and mixing of camels with other species

Lairage, the period during which camels rest at the slaughterhouse before slaughter, is a critical stage for reducing transport and handling-related stress and improving meat quality parameters. Appropriate management of animal lairage duration and environmental circumstances supports emotional and physiological recovery and mitigates pre-slaughter stress, affecting meat quality outcomes (Kadim et al., 2024, Code of Federal Regulations). Evidence from camels suggests that both excessively short (1–8 hs) and excessively long (48–96 hs) lairage periods can negatively affect meat quality, indicating a U-shaped relationship between lairage duration and physiological recovery (Kadim et al., 2024). This pattern differs from cattle, where longer lairage often improves muscle glycogen repletion.

Furthermore, adequate lairage environments are important for allowing camels to recover from transport stress before slaughter. Lairage temperature should be maintained within a range that minimizes heat stress (below 35 °C). Elevated temperatures combined with insufficient ventilation can intensify dehydration, fatigue, and physiological stress (Kadim et al., 2024). Access to shade, sufficient airflow, and at libitum access to clean drinking water are valuable components of lairage management.

Stocking density should give sufficient space for camels to stand, turn around, and rest comfortably without excessive competition. Overcrowding with different animal species can increase the risk of injury, obstruct resting behavior, and increase stress levels, whereas very low stocking densities may compromise efficient facility utilization.

Lairage duration should be adequate to allow recovery from transportation while avoiding prolonged fasting. Studies in animals generally suggest that a resting period of approximately 6–12 h may enable recovery from transport stress, although the appropriate duration may vary depending on journey length, environmental conditions, and animal condition. In general, short lairage periods may not allow sufficient recovery, whereas prolonged lairage may increase fasting duration, live weight loss, dehydration, and stress. So, lairage management should be designed to balance animal welfare considerations with operational requirements to optimize both welfare outcomes and meat quality characteristics.

Upon arrival at the slaughterhouse, camels are exposed to an unfamiliar environment that may trigger fear and stress responses. Unfamiliar environments, including slaughterhouse design, equipment, noise, human presence, and interactions with other animals, can further exacerbate stress (Kadim et al., 2024). Dromedary camels are particularly sensitive to dark, narrow, and slippery entryways, which they perceive as unusual and distressing. So, suitable lairage design and handling practices are vital to facilitate camel movement, minimize stress, and promote recovery from transportation before slaughter.

Lairage management and its effects on camel welfare and meat quality

Lairage is a critical resting periods in pre-slaughter management, camels have an opportunity to recover from the physical and psychological stress associated with transport before slaughter. Suitable lairage practice can enhance camel welfare, decrease physiological and emotional stress responses, and contribute to improved meat quality characteristics. However, the efficiency of lairage periods depends on many factors, such as environmental conditions, duration, facility design, stocking density, and the provision of water and resting opportunities. Usually, upon arrival at the slaughterhouse, camels are exposed to an unfamiliar environment that may itself be a source of stress. Slaughterhouse design, equipment, noise, human activity, and interactions with unfamiliar animals can aggravate fear and distress (Kadim et al., 2024). Dromedary camels are particularly sensitive to dark, narrow, and slippery passageways, which may impede movement and escalate stress impact. Therefore, lairage facilities should be designed to facilitate calm animal movement and minimize fear-inducing stimuli.

Camels held in lairage should have continuous access to clean drinking water to restore hydration and support normal physiological functions, particularly after long transport journeys. Adequate space should be provided to allow camels to stand, lie down, and move easily without excessive competition or aggression. In addition, lairage areas should include shade, adequate ventilation, and non-slip flooring to protect animals from adverse environmental conditions and reduce the risk of injury. Mixing unfamiliar animals should be avoided whenever possible, as it may lead to social stress, aggressive interactions, and increased energy expenditure.

The duration of lairage is an important factor influencing both camel welfare and meat quality parameters. Although the optimal lairage period may vary according to journey period, camel condition, and environmental temperatures, a resting period of approximately 6–12 h is generally considered sufficient to allow recovery from transport stress while minimizing the adverse effects of prolonged fasting. Longer lairage periods may be required following extended transport; however, excessive lairage duration should be avoided because it can increase dehydration, live-weight loss, and physiological stress. Pre-slaughter stress, whether physiological or physical, can markedly alter muscle metabolism by accelerating glycolysis, increasing lactic acid accumulation, and disrupting pH balance, all of which compromise meat quality (Ekiz et al., 2012). Camel muscle glycogen storage prior to slaughter is particularly important because it determines the extent of postmortem lactic acid formation and the decline in muscle pH (Kadim et al., 2024). Consequently, ultimate muscle pH is widely recognized as a key indicator of meat quality.

Extended fasting and prolonged lairage periods can deplete muscle glycogen reserves before slaughter, thereby limiting postmortem lactic acid accumulation and resulting in high ultimate pH values (Kadim et al., 2024; Terlouw & Rybarczyk, 2008). Water and feed deprivation may further accelerate glycogen depletion and compromise normal muscle acidification after slaughter, making glycogen availability a critical determinant of postmortem biochemical processes. As noted by Del Campo et al. (2021), animals deprived of feed rely predominantly on existing muscle glycogen for postmortem lactic acid production (Del Campo Gigena et al., 2021). The influence of lairage duration on glycogen status depends on the balance between recovery from transport stress and continued energy expenditure. A moderate lairage period under calm conditions, with ad libitum access to water, allows animals to rest, reduces physical activity and stress, and may help preserve the remaining muscle glycogen reserves before slaughter. However, because muscle glycogen is unlikely to be substantially replenished in fasted animals, prolonged lairage without feed, particularly when accompanied by overcrowding, mixing with unfamiliar animals, or poor environmental conditions, may result in continued glycogen depletion. Consequently, less glycogen is available for postmortem glycolysis, leading to reduced lactic acid accumulation, higher ultimate pH, and an increased risk of dark, firm, and dry (DFD) meat. Interestingly, not all studies agree on lairage time effects. In cattle, Teke et al. (2014) found that lairage duration did not significantly affect water-holding capacity or tenderness, suggesting that species differences, management practices, and prior stress levels can modify these outcomes (Teke et al., 2014). In beef cattle, Mounier et al. (2006) reported that inadequate lairage conditions and prolonged preslaughter stress of beef bulls are associated with increased pH, darker meat color, reduced tenderness, and lower water-holding capacity (Mounier et al., 2006). The same authors concluded that these changes in final meat products may negatively affect consumer acceptance and the palatability of meat products. The relationship between lairage duration and meat quality is not always consistent. While several studies have reported benefits of adequate resting periods before slaughter, evidence from cattle has also shown limited effects of lairage duration on water-holding capacity and tenderness (Teke et al., 2014). These discrepancies may reflect differences among species, transportation conditions, previous handling experiences, and management practices. Consequently, optimal lairage duration should be determined according to production conditions and animal requirements.

Overall, appropriate lairage management is critical for safeguarding camel welfare and optimizing meat quality characteristics. Providing appropriate environmental conditions, ad libitum access to clean water, acceptable space allowance, and an adequate recovery time before slaughter can help reduce stress, preserve muscle glycogen contents, and enhance postmortem meat quality characteristics. Further camel-specific research is needed to establish evidence-based recommendations for optimal lairage duration and management practices under different production systems.

Meat quality parameters and stress indicators

Hamad et al. (2023) reported that dromedary camels slaughtered immediately after transport had significantly higher thawing loss than those rested in lairage, likely because of acute stress (Hamad et al., 2023). Similar results were reported by Tabite et al. (2019), linking high pre-slaughter stress to decreased water-holding capacity (Tabite et al., 2019). In alpacas, Biffin et al. (2020) reported that an extended lairage period (7 days vs. 24 h) led to decreased water-holding capacity due to stress and energy depletion (Biffin et al., 2020). Excessive lactic acid accumulated under intense pre-slaughter stress has also been related to protein denaturation and decreased water-holding capacity in pigs (Vermeulen et al., 2015). These stress-induced alterations affect other meat quality traits as well, such as cooking loss, dry matter, and myoglobin content. Behavioral stress and handling

Stress physiology, coping styles, and behavioral responses to handling have been investigated in several species, including pigs (Bolhuis et al., 2005), lambs (Çakmakçı et al., 2021), guanacos (Correa et al., 2019), and dromedary camels (Pastrana et al., 2024). However, compared with conventional livestock species, dromedary camels remain relatively underexplored in terms of coping strategies across different management and preslaughter environments. In contrast, camels are well recognized as models for studying adaptation to different rearing conditions (Gebreyohanes & Assen, 2017). Dromedary camels are often kept in open, unshaded pens, where they are exposed to loud noise, unfamiliar surroundings, and regrouping with other livestock. When combined with feed and water deprivation, these factors can cause starvation, dehydration, and clear behavioral signs of stress, including vocalization, resistance to handling, and avoidance behavior (Kadim et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2023). In dromedary camels, insufficient lairage time may accelerate the onset of rigor mortis, while inadequate rest can raise muscle temperature and increase electrical conductivity (Hamad et al., 2023). Tabite et al. (2019), also working with dromedary camels, demonstrated a positive correlation between cortisol levels and electrical conductivity, reinforcing the link between acute stress and cellular dysfunction (Tabite et al., 2019).

Mixing camels with other species

Mixing dromedary camels with cattle during lairage heightens the risk of injury, particularly in overcrowded pens or when cattle have large horns. This practice also raises the likelihood of carcass contamination because lairage environments can harbor pathogens (Kadim et al., 2024). The stress response during lairage depends on multiple factors such as facility design as reported in lambs (Miranda-de La Lama et al., 2010), environmental conditions in cattle (Njisane & Muchenje, 2016), stocking density in dromedary camels (Lemrhamed et al., 2018), handling practices in cattle (Terlouw et al., 2011). In dromedary camels, loud noises and inappropriate handling procedures, such as electric prods, can intensify this stress (Kadim et al., 2024). While lairage is intended to promote recovery and improve meat quality traits, inappropriate management can become another source of stress. Studies in cattle have demonstrated that muscle glycogen depletion and lactic acid accumulation play a key role in reducing meat palatability and quality parameters (Bourguet et al., 2011; García-Torres et al., 2021). Therefore, lairage facilities must be designed to minimize stress, maintain strict hygiene, and facilitate full physiological recovery, essential steps for both animal welfare and the production of high-quality camel meat.

Minimizing stress in dromedary camels prior to slaughter

To maintain high meat quality and safeguard dromedary camels’ welfare, animals should be handled carefully both before and after arrival at the slaughterhouse. Upon arrival, camels should be calmly guided into the lairage for an adequate rest period prior to slaughter, with access to clean water but no feed (Kadim et al., 2013). Resting in lairage enables camels to recover from transport stress, restore energy reserves, and improve both welfare and meat quality (Kadim et al., 2024). Handling should be performed by experienced personnel trained in camel-specific welfare practices and familiar with relevant regulations. Adequate ventilation is essential to keep animals cool, and overcrowding or mixing with unfamiliar livestock should be avoided to limit stress and aggression. Dromedary camels can kick in all directions and are capable of biting; therefore, they are considered potentially dangerous. Strict safety measures are therefore necessary to protect both animals and handlers (Kadim et al., 2024). Pre-slaughter stunning

Welfare implications of stunning

In certain religions including Islam, exsanguination of dromedary camels must be performed without pre-slaughter stunning (Koei, 2009). In Contrast, in developed countries, pre-slaughter stunning is regarded as a standard practice, because it aligns with high animal welfare standards (Kadim et al., 2024). These countries typically operate modern, well-regulated slaughter facilities that support humane handling and consistent meat quality characteristics. Stunning is widely recognized as the most humane method, as it reduces an animal’s sensitivity to stress and pain and thereby helps preserve meat quality (Kadim et al., 2024). The primary aim of stunning is to induce immediate loss of consciousness by disrupting normal brain function, usually through mechanical or electrical means. As in cattle, dromedary camels can be stunned with a captive bolt pistol applied at the intersection of the medial eye corner and the upper ear attachment (Herrmann & Fisher, 2004). Although rarely practiced in camel-meat–consuming countries, stunning, especially electrical stunning, is inexpensive, effective, and remains the most common pre-slaughter method worldwide.

Effects of stunning on meat quality

Although some religious practices permit slaughter without stunning, studies show that properly stunning animals before slaughter significantly reduces pain and distress. While the Halal procedure can be humane when carefully executed, inconsistent practice makes it difficult to gauge the animal’s actual level of discomfort (Kadim et al., 2024). Therefore, improvements in slaughter methods and standard operating procedures are critical to meeting basic welfare expectations. The effectiveness of stunning depends on both the equipment used and the competence of personnel. Proper training and routine maintenance of stunning devices is essential to prevent unnecessary animal suffering. Educating stakeholders about the mechanisms and benefits of stunning can encourage its adoption in regions where it is not yet common. Public concern about pre-slaughter handling continues to increase. Studies in cattle and other livestock species have demonstrated growing consumer concern regarding methods used to render animals unconscious before slaughter (Chulayo et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2023). Studies have shown that consumers increasingly demand ethical treatment of animals throughout the meat production process (Verma et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2023). Minimizing stress, pain, and fear before slaughter is therefore both an ethical obligation and an important prerequisite for maintaining high meat quality. Therefore, implementing sensitive and precise assessments of pre-slaughter stress remains essential to ensure both animal welfare and product quality.

Welfare implications of restraint and bleeding

In livestock slaughtered without stunning, unconsciousness is not immediate but develops progressively following severance of the carotid arteries as cerebral blood flow declines (Health & Welfare, 2013). The time to unconsciousness and death varies considerably between animals. Slaughter of dromedary camels is commonly performed using halal methods without stunning, in accordance with religious requirements. As shown in Fig. 2, dromedary camels are typically restrained in a squatting position by tying their front legs with a rope at the knees (Kadim et al., 2024). The head is then turned toward the tail to restrict movement and expose the neck after which a swift incision is made at the base of the neck, between the cervical region and the thorax, severing the jugular veins, carotid arteries, trachea, and esophagus, while preserving the spinal cord (Kadim et al., 2024). Then dromedary then falls to the ground, as the first sign of impending unconsciousness after the neck cut (Gregory et al., 2010), indicating loss of cortical control over posture. Rapid exsanguination is essential to minimize bacterial growth. Typically, 40–60% of total blood volume is lost within the first five minutes post-slaughter, promoting early brain death and reducing bacterial proliferation (Kadim et al., 2013). For the slaughter of livestock without stunning, a set of indicators and outcomes is recommended to (a) assess unconsciousness before animals are released from restraint, and (b) confirm death prior to the start of carcass dressing (Health & Welfare, 2013); these indicators should also be applied to camels. Despite its religious and cultural significance, the halal slaughter method, can impose considerable stress on dromedary camels. WOAH (2018) prohibits forcing animals to sit or lie down by having one or more handlers jump on or lie across their backs (World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), 2018). The procedure is physically and emotionally challenging for both the operator and the animal, particularly due to the camel’s large size and potential aggressiveness. Improper restraint may cause the animal to attempt to rise, resulting in injuries to itself or slaughter staff and complicating effective bleeding (Kadim et al., 2013; Kadim et al., 2024). Studies in cattle have found that collapse following neck cutting is one of the first noticeable indicators of imminent unconsciousness (Gregory et al., 2010). In dromedary camels, animals similarly collapse after the neck incision during halal slaughter (Kadim et al., 2024).

Effects on physiological stress and meat quality

Camels are often handled roughly at slaughter and may be forced into a crouched position, restrained caudally, and exposed to the slaughter of conspecifics, all of which can intensify stress. Comparable distressing stimuli at slaughter, including exposure to noise, rapid human movements, and unfamiliar abattoir conditions, have been described in cattle (Bourguet et al., 2011) and pigs (Terlouw & Rybarczyk, 2008). Studies confirm that camels slaughtered without stunning exhibit significantly higher stress indicators than those slaughtered post-stunning (Kadim et al., 2024). For instance, Lemrhamed et al. (2019) reported that camels slaughtered without stunning had higher concentrations of thyroid hormone, cortisol, glucose, malondialdehyde, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, catalase activity, and evidence of hemolysis than those slaughtered with post-stunning (Lemrhamed et al., 2019). Indicators oxidative stress, including elevated malondialdehyde and reduced catalase and superoxide dismutase activities, have been observed in both camel blood and muscle, suggesting that stress significantly compromises meat quality traits (Kaoutar et al., 2016; Lemrhamed et al., 2019). Elevated ultimate muscle pH in stressed camels is linked to greater drip loss, cooking loss, and electrical conductivity during storage. This may result from increased cortisol levels, which impair glycogen metabolism and water-holding capacity. In buffalo, Naveena et al. (2011) reported that a high ultimate muscle pH increases the number of hydrophilic sites available to bind water through hydrogen and ionic bonds (Naveena et al., 2011).

A similar mechanism is considered to contribute to water-holding capacity in dromedary camel meat, although species-specific differences may exist. In certain abattoirs, dromedary camels are subjected to inhumane practices, including the severing of the Achilles tendon to immobilize them or forcing fully conscious animals into a squatting position before slaughter (Guya & Neme, 2015). Such methods are painful, unethical and detrimental to meat quality. To improving camel welfare, the development of specialized restraint devices is essential. Restraint systems should be safe for both camels and operators, preventing unnecessary movement without causing pain or fear. Grandin (1995) outlines key principles of low-stress restraint such as solid barriers to reduce visual distractions, non-slip flooring, sufficient but not excessive pressure to secure animals, and minimization of noise and sudden movements (Grandin, 1995). Adequate lighting can also guide the animals forward by taking advantage of their natural tendency to move from darker to lighter areas (Lambooij, 2014).

Although, most slaughtermen possess the technical skills for knife use, many lack formal training in animal welfare and food safety, which contribute to suboptimal practices during slaughter (Koei, 2009). Improvements can be achieved through proper training, enhanced handling protocols, and modification of equipment, such as adapting cattle V-restrainers for camels. Practical recommendations include avoiding slaughter in view of other animals, refraining from sharpening knives in their presence, and maintaining hygiene in the bleeding area to minimize stress and discomfort (APHIS, 2016; Grandin & Committee, 2017). Prioritizing animal welfare during slaughter can reduce physiological stress responses and mitigate their negative effects on meat quality, ensuring compliance with ethical standards.

Animal welfare legislation

In countries where dromedary camels are slaughtered, there is an urgent need to establish species-specific animal welfare legislation. Unlike cattle, sheep, or poultry, camels are often excluded from formal welfare frameworks. Most countries lack standardized regulations or dedicated welfare units addressing camel management during transportation, sale, lairage, and slaughter. Consequently, there are limited efforts to monitor or control camel welfare during preslaughter handling (Lemrhamed et al., 2019). Currently, no official protocols are available from veterinary services or animal welfare organizations to ensure appropriate camel handling at any stage of the preslaughter process. Moreover, training programs for personnel directly involved in camel slaughter are virtually absent. To safeguard camel welfare, it is essential to establish clear and measurable welfare criteria, particularly in slaughterhouses. These criteria should emphasize validity reliability, repeatability, and practical feasibility (Velarde & Dalmau, 2012). Most existing monitoring systems focus on input-based measures (e.g., resources provided to animals), but there is increasing recognition of the importance of outcome-based assessments, such as tracking the proportion of animals slipping, falling, turning around, or showing resistance during handling. Camel-specific welfare assessments have also incorporated animal-based indicators related to feeding, housing, health, and behavior, with welfare outcomes influenced by management factors such as caretaker experience, space allowance, shade availability, bedding conditions, and access to feeding and watering resources (Menchetti et al., 2021). Poor preslaughter management directly correlates with impaired meat quality traits and increased carcass losses, both in quantity and quality (Lemrhamed et al., 2019; Xin et al., 2018). Implementing fundamental training programs on humane camel handling, combined with improved facility design, is therefore crucial. Such programs can be evaluated by monitoring camel behavior, physiological responses, and welfare outcomes. Evidence from other livestock industries shows that training initiatives can significantly reduce carcass downgrading caused by bruising and physical damage (da Costa et al., 2014).

International approaches to animal welfare legislation

Although camel welfare regulations are absent in most countries, several nations have developed comprehensive livestock welfare frameworks that can serve as useful models:

European Union (EU): Animal welfare protection, sanitary inspections, and slaughter procedures are uniform across EU member states under regulations CE 853/2004, 854/2004, and 1099/2009 (European Food Safety, 2004; Koei, 2009).

Canada: The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) oversees humane livestock handling and slaughter, with shared responsibility among industry stakeholders, transporters, and slaughterhouse operators. Regulations provide detailed guidance for unloading, holding, animal movement, and the exclusion of sick or injured animals.

United Kingdom (UK): Livestock slaughter is regulated by both national and EU laws. The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (DEFRA) ensures compliance with welfare codes of practice specific to slaughterhouses.

United States: The United States Department of Agriculture (APHIS, 2016) enforces humane slaughter regulations that prohibit procedures causing “undue excitement and discomfort” to livestock (Koei, 2009). Moreover, both U.S. regulations and AVMA (2024) guidelines state that conscious animals must not be dragged, and the USDA further prohibits dragging disabled or otherwise immobile animals while they are conscious (Meyer et al., 2024).

New Zealand: The Ministry for Primary Industries, through the National Animal Welfare Advisory Committee, sets up welfare standards. The “Code of Welfare Commercial Slaughter” (2018) developed under the Animal Welfare Act 1999, provides comprehensive guidance for the humane treatment of animals during slaughter (Ministry for Primary Industries, 2018).

Although these regulatory systems were developed primarily for conventional livestock rather than specifically for dromedary camels, they provide practical examples of welfare-oriented handling that can be adapted to camel production systems. Key features include trained and competent handlers, calm animal movement, appropriate loading and unloading facilities, non-slip flooring, prevention of overcrowding, humane handling of injured animals, and avoidance of unnecessary force, fear, and distress. These international models demonstrate the feasibility of structured welfare systems and provide a practical foundation for developing camel-specific handling standards in major camel-producing regions.

Summary

Pre-slaughter handling has a profound impact on both animal welfare and meat quality characteristics in dromedary camels. Mishandling during transport, lairage, and slaughter not only raises ethical concerns but also causes substantial economic losses by compromising meat quality parameters. Stressful practices typically result in unfavorable traits such as darker meat color, high ultimate pH, reduced tenderness, poor water-holding capacity, and greater cooking losses. Physiological stress responses, including elevated glucose, cortisol, thyroxine, malondialdehyde, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, alongside reduced glycogen reserves and antioxidant activity, clearly demonstrate the negative impact of poor handling. Major stressors include rough handling, overcrowding, prolonged transport, inadequate facilities, and inappropriate restraint methods. Witnessing the slaughter of conspecifics further exacerbates stress, undermining both welfare and meat quality, while also increasing risks for slaughterhouse personnel. Research shows that even short transport durations can induce significant hormonal and metabolic changes. However, careful management practices can mitigate these effects. Adequate lairage is essential for recovery, though excessive lairage may itself cause stress and energy depletion. Thus, welfare improvements must strike a balance between recovery opportunities and avoiding prolonged fasting or environmental challenges. Overall, reducing pre-slaughter stress in camels requires evidence-based handling protocols, skilled staff, species-specific infrastructure, and enforceable welfare legislation. Humane lairage and slaughter procedures are essential not only to safeguard camel welfare but also to ensure high-quality meat production that meets both ethical and consumer standards. The existing evidence indicates that minimizing pre-slaughter stress through suitable transportation, lairage, handling, and slaughter practices is important for improving both camel welfare and meat quality.

Future research

Future research should focus on quantifying threshold levels of transport duration, stocking density, lairage time, and restraint methods that optimize both welfare and meat quality.
