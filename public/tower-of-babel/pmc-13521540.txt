Targeted detection and antimicrobial susceptibility of common pathogens of failed root canal treatments: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Chatura Mohan, Vaishnavi Vishram Naik, Tushar Shaw, Varsha Manikandan, Swaroop Hegde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pathogens persisting in the root canals lead to endodontic treatment failure. Understanding their prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility is essential for improving disinfection and treatment outcomes.Objective:To estimate the prevalence of five specific pathogens in failed root canal treatment cases using both culture-based and molecular methods and to evaluate the association between pathogen detection and clinical/radiographic findings and assess the antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates.Methodology:One hundred samples were collected using the absorbent paper point method and analyzed using culture and multiplex quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) for the detection of Enterococcus faecalis, Candida albicans, Streptococcus mutans, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Porphyromonas gingivalis. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on culture-positive isolates (n = 34). Statistical analysis included the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and logistic regression. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:Detection rates were 29% by culture and 82% by qPCR, with E. faecalis frequently identified. No statistically significant association was observed between pathogen detection and clinical/radiographic findings. Isolates were relatively more sensitive to combined antibiotic formulations than individual antibiotics and resistant to calcium hydroxide under test conditions.Conclusion:Failed root canal treatments demonstrated a high prevalence of persistent pathogens, which are better detected by molecular methods. Observed antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly to conventional intracanal medicaments, highlights the growing concern of AMR in endodontic infections and the need for better disinfection strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Successful endodontic treatment primarily depends on the effective elimination of microorganisms from the root canal system. However, microorganisms can survive the disinfection protocols or are reintroduced into the canal through coronal or apical leakage, leading to treatment failure.[1] These infections are polymicrobial in nature, involving a combination of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria as well as facultative and obligate anaerobes.[2]

Among the microorganisms associated with failed root canal treatments, Enterococcus faecalis has been widely reported due to its ability to survive harsh environmental conditions and persist within the root canal system.[3] Similarly, Candida albicans, an opportunistic dentinophilic fungus, is said to be highly resistant to canal disinfection procedures.[4] Streptococcus mutans, another Gram-positive bacterium, survives in the root canals because of its collagen-binding ability.[5] Gram-negative anaerobes such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Porphyromonas gingivalis have also been detected in secondary endodontic infections and may contribute to disease progression through interspecies communication and cross-feeding.[6]

Both culture and molecular techniques have been widely employed for the detection and identification of endodontic pathogens. Culture methods enable phenotypic characterization and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.[7] In contrast, molecular approaches such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) provide higher sensitivity and detect noncultivable organisms. Therefore, the combined use of both methods offers complementary advantages for studying endodontic microbiota. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) studies have revealed that the endodontic microbiome is highly diverse and complex,[8] but targeted investigations focusing on clinically relevant pathogens remain important for translational and diagnostic applications. Data correlating the presence of specific pathogens with clinical and radiographic findings and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of pathogens isolated from failed cases are limited.

The present study focused on the detection of five selected organisms – E. faecalis, C. albicans, S. mutans, F. nucleatum, and P. gingivalis – based on their documented association with persistent infections, resistance to intracanal medicaments, and frequent reporting in endodontic literature. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to estimate the prevalence of these pathogens in failed root canal treatments using both culture-based and quantitative PCR (qPCR) methods. The secondary objective is to evaluate their association with clinical and radiographic findings and assess the antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of culture-positive isolates.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional study was conducted following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref. No: EC-23/122-F-FDS–31/10/2023). Root canal samples were collected from patients reporting to the outpatient department. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population

Based on the reported prevalence of E. faecalis in failed endodontic cases,[7] a sample size of 82 was estimated at 95% confidence level with 9% precision. To account for potential data loss, 100 samples with clinical and radiographic evidence of failed endodontic treatment were included. Patients aged 25–60 years who had undergone root canal treatment at least 2 years prior and presented with pain, swelling, sinus tract, tenderness on percussion, or radiographic apical periodontitis with intact coronal restoration were included. Exclusion criteria comprised recent antibiotic use (within 3 months), systemic illness, periodontal pockets >3 mm, root fractures, and inability to achieve adequate isolation.

Clinical data collection

Clinical and demographic data, including age, sex, tooth number, clinical symptoms, duration since primary treatment, presence of sinus tract, tenderness on percussion, exudate, and quality of obturation on the radiograph, were recorded. A periapical diagnosis was made for each case based on the guidelines given by the American Association of Endodontists and the European Society of Endodontology in 2025.

Sampling procedure

Root canal sampling was performed following aseptic protocols as described previously.[7] After removal of coronal restorations and caries, teeth were isolated with a rubber dam, and the operative field was disinfected using 6% hydrogen peroxide and 3% sodium hypochlorite, followed by neutralization with 5% sodium thiosulfate.

Root filling material was removed using sterile Gates Glidden drills, Peeso reamers, and H-files without solvents. Working length was established, and canals were irrigated with saline. Gentle instrumentation was done with a sterile file introduced 1 mm short of the working length. Sterile paper points were placed to working length to absorb canal contents and immediately transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing enriched thioglycolate broth (HIMEDIA, Mumbai, Maharashtra, India).

In multirooted teeth, the largest canal or the one with the most pronounced radiographic signs of infection was sampled. Samples were transported to the laboratory, centrifuged at 7830 rpm for 5 min, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h in a shaking incubator. Each sample was then divided into two aliquots for culture-based identification and molecular analysis.

Culture-based identification of pathogens

Under aseptic conditions, samples were inoculated onto selective and differential media (HIMEDIA, Mumbai, Maharashtra, India) that support the growth of target pathogens. Brain Heart Infusion Agar, Sabouraud Dextrose Agar with Chloramphenicol, and Mitis Salivarius Agar with bacitracin were used to culture E. faecalis, C. albicans, and S. mutans, respectively, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Blood agar supplemented with hemin, Vitamin K1, and 5% defibrinated sheep blood was used for F. nucleatum and P. gingivalis and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 5–7 days. Preliminary identification was based on colony morphology and Gram’s staining, followed by qPCR confirmation of culture-positive isolates.

DNA extraction and molecular identification

Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions and stored at −20°C. Multiplex real-time PCR was performed using two primer–probe sets: one targeting E. faecalis, C. albicans, and S. mutans and the other targeting F. nucleatum and P. gingivalis. qPCR was carried out on the Rotor-Gene Q 5plex HRM system (QIAGEN). Each 25 μL reaction contained 3 μL template DNA, Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix, primers, and probes. The cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 95°C for 60 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 56°C for 30 s. Reference strains (E. faecalis ATCC 29212, C. albicans ATCC 10231, S. mutans ATCC 25175, F. nucleatum ATCC 25586, and P. gingivalis ATCC 33277) served as positive controls.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

All culture-positive isolates were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the broth microdilution method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined for commonly prescribed antibiotics – Ciprofloxacin, Metronidazole, Clindamycin, Amoxicillin and Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid as well as for intracanal medicaments – calcium hydroxide and modified triple antibiotic paste (mTAP). mTAP, comprising Ciprofloxacin, Metronidazole and Clindamycin, was freshly prepared in ratios of 1:1:1 and 1:3:3 with sterile distilled water as vehicle. In the absence of standardized interpretive breakpoints for intracanal medicaments, MIC values were reported descriptively without categorical classification.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 SPSS version 25.0 (IBM CORPORATION, Armonk, New York, Westchester). Associations between pathogen detection and clinical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariate analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of pathogen carriage.

RESULTS

One hundred samples of failed endodontic treatments were analyzed. The mean age group of the study population was 38–40 years with nearly equal sex distribution. Pain and tenderness on percussion were the most common complaints. Molars accounted for 63% of the cases, indicating a higher susceptibility of multirooted teeth to treatment failure. Most cases developed symptoms within 1–5 years of initial treatment. Radiographic evaluation showed that most teeth had inadequate obturation, whereas a small proportion (8%) exhibited periapical pathology despite an apparently adequate coronal and apical seal. Localized symptomatic apical periodontitis was the most common periapical diagnosis [Table 1].

In addition to the predefined target organisms, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was detected in 15% of samples by culture and confirmed by qPCR. As this organism was not part of the predefined detection panel, it is reported as an incidental finding.

Relatively lower MIC values were observed for mTAP formulations and Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid compared to individual antibiotics and calcium hydroxide. E. faecalis was susceptible to all antimicrobials except Metronidazole. C. albicans seemed least susceptible of all pathogens to all tested antimicrobials. S. mutans recorded higher MIC values than E. faecalis, indicating higher resistance. In contrast, F. nucleatum showed better susceptibility to all drugs, including Metronidazole. Calcium hydroxide demonstrated comparatively high MIC values under test conditions.

DISCUSSION

Unsatisfactory obturation, coronal microleakage, procedural errors, and missed canals are some of the causes of primary treatment failure.[9] In this study, 8% of the samples had periapical lesions despite being well sealed both coronally and apically. This could be due to missed canals, insufficient canal disinfection, or the presence of extraradicular pathogens.[10]

With both methods of pathogen detection, E. faecalis was frequently found. This is similar to previous studies that have demonstrated dominance of E. faecalis in secondary endodontic infections.[11] This is attributed to its high tolerance to environmental stresses,[12] its biofilm formation which provides physical protection against phagocytosis.[13] In addition, it has key virulence factors such as cytolysin which facilitates evasion of host defence and suppression of competing microorganisms,[14] gelatinase which facilitates the pathogen’s adhesion to dentine[15] and lipoteichoic acid in its cell wall which promotes increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and lesion progression.[16] However, studies that employed NGS indicate that this bacterium may not be the most dominant one in endodontic failures.[171819] Therefore, its predominance in the present study should be interpreted cautiously, given the targeted nature of species-specific PCR. Although PCR displayed a higher detection rate, it is important to recognize that it may detect DNA from nonviable cells, leading to overestimation of microbial presence.[20]

Polymicrobial infections were identified in a subset of cases, supporting the concept that endodontic infections are driven by complex microbial communities rather than individual species. While many pathogenic species exhibit low virulence individually, their coexistence within a community can result in enhanced virulence due to synergism.[20] In this study, simultaneous detection of multiple organisms in the same samples suggests potential microbial interactions contributing to persistence. Clinically, this reinforces the importance of effective chemomechanical preparation, thorough irrigation, and appropriate use of intracanal medicaments.

However, 18% of the samples in this study gave a negative result for both culture and PCR. The probable reasons are that they harbor pathogens that were not targeted by our highly species-specific PCR, or it may be a failure of the paper point sampling method,[21] or the microorganisms were cleared by immune cells.[6] Cryogenic grinding or pulverization of roots is an alternative to paper point sampling to obtain true representative samples of endodontic microbiota.[22]

No statistically significant association was observed between pathogen detection and clinical or radiographic findings. Although pathogen detection was more frequent in symptomatic cases, the absence of a significant correlation suggests that disease manifestation is multifactorial. Host immune response, pathogen virulence, and interspecies interactions play an important role in determining clinical outcomes more than the mere presence of specific microorganisms.[23]

An incidental but alarming finding of this study was the detection of P. aeruginosa, a Gram-negative aerobic rod and a well-documented pathogen in dental unit waterlines. Its presence in failed cases points toward a lack of adequate disinfection during primary treatment or contamination of canals by the use of water from three-way syringe for irrigation.[24] It highlights the need for regular disinfection of waterlines in dental clinics.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing demonstrated high MIC values for individual antibiotics, implying reduced susceptibility of the isolates. Similar findings were observed in another study.[2] Lack of blood supply in nonvital teeth restricts antibiotic access into the root canal, rendering systemic antibiotics useless.[25] This not only allows the pathogens to survive and but also contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). mTAP medicament and Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid were effective against the isolates of this study. However, the use of multiantibiotic formulations as intracanal medicament, irrational use of antibiotics, and overexposure to antiseptics and disinfectants facilitate the emergence and horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARG) within the oral cavity.[26] Resistance of E. faecalis to Metronidazole was demonstrated in another study.[15] Poor susceptibility of C. albicans to all the drugs indicates the need for agents with better antifungal action. Calcium hydroxide demonstrated comparatively high MIC values under the test conditions, implying poor efficacy toward the isolates. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as in vitro MIC values do not directly reflect clinical effectiveness, particularly for intracanal medicaments. Factors such as diffusion rates, contact time, buffering action of the dentinal fluid, and the vehicle used can significantly influence their clinical performance.[27] From a clinical standpoint, the use of antibiotics should be reserved for cases with systemic involvement, while primary emphasis should remain on adequate mechanical debridement and intracanal disinfection.

Limitations

Targeted analysis of five selected organisms limits comprehensive characterization of the endodontic microbiome. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was limited to culture-positive isolates, which may affect generalizability. Furthermore, the absence of multivariate analysis limits the assessment of potential confounding factors.

CONCLUSION

Understanding endodontic microbiota is becoming increasingly important now than ever before due to the rising incidence of AMR. Overall, these findings emphasize that persistent endodontic infections represent a reservoir of antimicrobial-resistant microorganisms. Effective management should prioritize mechanical debridement, strict aseptic protocols, and judicious use of antibiotics to limit further dissemination of AMR. Future studies employing comprehensive microbiome approaches and periodic surveillance of antimicrobial susceptibilities are needed to better understand the complexity of persistent endodontic infections and to guide evidence-based clinical practice.
