Technical Tips for Surgical Treatment of Takayasu Arteritis
Authors: Jayeon Ahn, Seung-Kee Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a rare form of vasculitis that is more common in young Asian females. TAK is characterized as idiopathic, inflammatory, granulomatous, and large-vessel panarteritis. This review provides a brief summary of the etiology and clinical phase of TAK, its epidemiology in Korea, challenges in diagnosis and treatment, and the outcomes of open versus endovascular therapy. In addition, it outlines the general principles of surgical treatment and provides technical tips for TAK surgery in detail.

INTRODUCTION

Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a rare type of vasculitis which is more common in young Asian females including Korea, Japan, India and Turkey. It has also been historically referred to as pulseless disease, occlusive thromboaortopathy, aortic arch syndrome, aortoarteritis, and obstructive productive arteritis.

TAK was first reported in 1908 by Mikito Takayasu, a professor of ophthalmology at Kanazawa University, Japan [1]. He reported a coronary-like anastomosis around the optic disc, which was an arteriovenous connection thought to result from retinal ischemia, in a young female who presented with vision loss, vertigo, syncope, and conjunctival injection.

TAK can be defined as an idiopathic, inflammatory, granulomatous, large-vessel panarteritis [2,3]. “Idiopathic” indicates that the cause of the disease is not known yet. “Inflammatory” indicates that the main pathophysiology is driven by vascular inflammation of undetermined origin. “Granulomatous” refers to the presence of granulomatous inflammation, similar to those seen in tuberculosis, suggesting a possible infectious etiology. TAK preferentially involves large vessels, including the aorta, its major branch arteries, and pulmonary arteries. “Panarteritis” denotes involvement of the entire arterial layers. However, it does not involve veins, which makes the term “angiitis” inappropriate.

This review provides a concise overview of TAK, with a focus on technical tips for surgical treatment.

ETIOLOGY AND CLINICAL PHASES

The etiology of TAK is not fully understood; however, it is probably multifactorial. The most widely accepted hypotheses include autoimmune mechanisms, infections, genetic susceptibility, and hormonal influences [4]. TAK is associated with a progressive inflammatory process, possibly caused by an autoimmune disorder. However, studies attempting to identify the specific antigens that trigger autoimmunity in TAK have failed [5]. Although no causative microorganism has been confirmed, infectious agent—particularly viruses or less virulent bacteria—may initiate or exacerbate the autoimmune process through a molecular mimicry mechanism. TAK is not considered a hereditary disorder, but genetic susceptibility has been associated with the HLA-B*52 allele, which is more common in the Japanese population than in the Western populations [6].

Histologically, TAK is defined as a panarteritis involving all layers of the arterial wall. The characteristic findings include intimal fibrous thickening, destruction of the medial smooth muscle and elastic layers, inflammaroty cell infiltration and collagenous fibrosis within the media, and adventitial thickening with perivascular inflammation around the vasa vasorum [4-6].

Vascular Inflammation can cause either steno-occlusive lesions or aneurysm formation. TAK has two distinct clinical phases: the initial acute phase (nonspecific constitutional symptoms of fever, malaise, and weight loss) and the chronic stenotic phase with symptoms of ischemia due to arterial occlusion (pulselessness, bruits, claudication, and hypertension) [7].

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF TAK IN KOREA

Epidemiological studies investigating the incidence and mortality rates of TAK in South Korea are limited. Park et al. [8] conducted a nationwide, population-based study using the Rare Intractable Disease program registry and the National Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service database from 2008 to 2012. There were 612 new patients with TAK over 5 years, and the annual incidence was 0.24 per 100,000 population, which was similar to that in Japan (0.23) and higher than that reported in Caucasians. The prevalence was 2.82 per 100,000 population, and the number of registered patients was 1,438 as of 2012. The average age was 46 years (range, 30-60 years), with a marked female predominance (4.3:1). The mortality rate was 0.02 per 100,000 population, which was 3.1 times higher than that of the general population. The most common cause of death was cardiovascular disease (45%; 34% cardiac and 11% cerebrovascular), followed by malignancy (14%).

Jang et al. [9] analyzed 2,731 newly diagnosed patients with TAK from 2006 to 2017 and reported an overall 10-year survival rate of 85%. The survival rate in male was significantly lower than that in female (79.7% vs. 86.3%; P<0.001). Cardiovascular disease was the most common cause of death, including cardiac disease (54.8%) and stroke (33.6%). Interestingly, second and third leading causes of death in male were malignancy and external causes of injury or poisoning, whereas those in female were musculoskeletal and connective diseases and malignancy.

CHALLENGES IN THE DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT

Despite many previous studies, significant gaps remain in the diagnosis and treatment of TAK in clinical practice. Kim and Beckman [2] and Direskeneli [10] have published excellent comprehensive reviews on this topic.

1) Diagnostic challenges

The diagnostic and classification criteria for TAK are continuously evolving, and no single laboratory test or imaging modality serves as a gold standard with sufficient sensitivity and specificity. The clinical manifestations are highly variable, and nonspecific constitutional symptoms often delay early diagnosis. Diagnostic biomarkers of TAK have not yet been identified, and autoimmune antibodies associated with other vasculitides are not useful in diagnosing TAK. Furthermore, biochemical indicators of disease activity remain inadequate: elevations in acute-phase reactants, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) or C-reactive protein, do not consistently correlate with disease activity. Validated composite measures for assessing disease activity are also lacking.

2) Management challenges

In medical treatment, large prospective clinical studies are lacking, and most available studies are retrospective, single-center series. Conventional treatment relies on corticosteroids and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, such as methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide. Biological agents, including anti-tumor necrosis factor alpha and anti-interleukin 6 receptor therapies, have been reported to be effective, but only in small, open-label studies.

Active biologic inflammation has been identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative complications (odds ratio [OR] 7.48, P=0.04) [11]. Patency after revascularization depends on the disease activity. Therefore, revascularization is usually delayed until the disease becomes inactive.

To move beyond expert opinion and small observational studies, a collaborative work is needed to collect a large enough sample size to provide sufficient power for drawing meaningful conclusions. Therefore, a well-designed multicenter collaborative approach is crucial for improving the understanding of this rare vasculitis, including a standardized definition of disease activity, blinded assessment of relapse, and a protocol-driven imaging schedule [2].

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF TAK

1) Open vs. endovascular therapy

In the era of endovascular therapy, “which is the better treatment option for TAK: open vs. endovascular therapy?” is an important question that needs to be addressed. Similar to atherosclerotic occlusive disease, endovascular therapy is generally preferred for short-segment stenoses, whereas open bypass is favored for long-segment stenosis or total occlusion. However, no randomized controlled trials directly comparing bypass and endovascular intervention have been performed because of the rarity of the disease, heterogenous lesion locations, and variable disease activity.

Acute inflammation of the involved vessel wall or diffuse, heavy arterial calcification often complicates revascularization. In particular, severe calcification may preclude the use of endovascular treatment. Jung et al. [12] performed the first meta-analysis on this question in 770 patients with TAK. The restenosis rate was significantly higher after endovascular intervention than after open surgery (OR 5.18, P<0.001), especially in the coronary, supra-aortic, and renal arteries. However, the stroke risk was lower after endovascular intervention (OR 0.33, P=0.003).

Kim and Beckman [2] reported that percutaneous transluminal angioplasty for short-segment stenosis achieved a high technical success rate (81%-100%) but poor long-term durability, with a restenosis rate of 71% over 1.3 years. For long-segment stenosis or occlusion, open bypass is superior to endovascular therapy, with an occlusion or restenosis rate of 8%-31% at 3-6 years. However, the outcomes in TAK remain inferior to those in atherosclerotic disease. This can be explained by the longer lesion length, more fibrotic vessel changes, and persistent subclinical inflammation even in the absence of clinically active disease.

In our institutional series at Seoul National University Hospital, we analyzed 73 cases and observed similar findings [13]. Primary patency was better with open surgery than with endovascular intervention (10-year patency: 75.7% vs. 31.7%; 20-year patency: 59% vs. 17%). Restenosis and reintervention rates were significantly higher in the endovascular group than in the surgery group (55% vs. 12.1%, P<0.001). Most restenoses developed within the first 3 years after intervention, underscoring the need for frequent serial monitoring during this period.

Kim et al. [14] reported the outcomes of open vs. endovascular treatment in a subgroup of 21 Korean patients with supra-aortic arterial occlusive disease. Endovascular interventions were performed for short (<5 cm) stenotic lesions, whereas bypass surgery was used for longer occlusive lesions. Over a 16-year study period, 9.6% of patients with TAK required revascularization of supra-aortic occlusive lesions by intervention or bypass due to cerebral ischemic symptoms. During a mean follow-up of 39 months, the restenosis rate was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the bypass group (53% vs. 12.5%, P=0.01). However, serious complications, including cardiac tamponade (n=1) and intracerebral hemorrhage (n=2) caused by cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome, developed in the bypass group. To mitigate the risk of hyperperfusion syndrome, the authors recommended treating renovascular hypertension before supra-aortic revascularization and performing unilateral rather than bilateral carotid reconstruction.

2) General principles of surgical treatment of TAK

The European League Against Rheumatism has published recommendations for the management of large-vessel vasculitis, including TAK. The key principles include: (1) thorough clinical and imaging assessment of the arterial tree, (2) early initiation of high-dose glucocorticoid therapy for the induction of remission, (3) use of immunosuppressive agents as adjunctive therapy, (4) regular clinical monitoring supported by inflammatory marker measurements, and (5) performing reconstructive surgery for TAK in the quiescent phase at expert centers [15,16].

① Multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach

Treatment must be individualized, and an MDT approach is crucial for optimal outcomes. The MDT, including vascular surgeons, cardiac surgeons, rheumatologists, interventional radiologists, and interventional cardiologists, can provide the highest level of care for these rare and complex patients [17]. As TAK is a systemic disease, rheumatologists play a central role in confirming the diagnosis, assessing disease activity, and controlling active inflammation before surgery. The MDT determines whether to operate or not, when to operate, and which modality of surgical or interventional treatment should be attempted. Before deciding on a treatment strategy, disease progression, long-term durability (particularly in younger patients), comorbidities, and treatment-related risks should be carefully considered. The MDT should also ensure structured, long-term follow-up.

Although most patients with TAK are young and otherwise healthy, perioperative risk should not be underestimated. These patients may have significant cardiac problems, including a low but definite incidence of coronary artery involvement, and cardiovascular disease remains the most common cause of death [8,9]. Therefore, surgeons should not neglect possible complications from systemic medical problems, even though they are uncommon.

② Elective surgery after control of active disease

Most TAK patients do not require urgent or emergency surgery and can undergo elective procedures, allowing time for comprehensive evaluation and adequate control of the disease activity. The main emergency indications include impending aneurysm rupture and stroke. Whenever possible, surgical procedures should be delayed until the active phase of the disease has resolved. Patients should be considered for surgery only after completing steroid and immunosuppressive therapy.

Fields et al. [18] demonstrated that operative outcomes correlate strongly with disease activity. The 5-year freedom from graft revision after surgery was 100% in patients with quiescent disease not requiring steroids, 95% in those with quiescent disease on steroids, 57% in those with active disease on steroids, and 33% in those with active disease not receiving long-term steroid therapy (P<0.001).

However, a major challenge is the lack of reliable inflammatory biomarker that accurately reflect active vascular inflammation. Currently, disease activity is assessed using the National Institute of Health criteria, defined by the presence of at least two new or worsening abnormalities within the previous three months: (1) systemic features, including fever, myalgia, arthralgia, and malaise; (2) elevated ESR greater than 20 mm/h; 3) features of vascular involvement, including claudication, diminished or absent pulses, bruits, carotidynia, and asymmetric blood pressure; (4) angiographic evidence of new or worsening lesions confirmed by computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography, or conventional angiography [19].

3) Technical tips for TAK surgery

Because the etiology, pathophysiology, and natural history of TAK differ substantially from those of common atherosclerotic diseases, the surgical techniques used for TAK should also be adapted accordingly.

① Avoid diseased segment for anastomosis

Suture lines should never be placed in diseased arterial segments in order to avoid false aneurysm formation and anastomotic stenosis. Anastomoses must be constructed in disease-free vessel segments because of the high incidence of pseudoaneurysm formation, particularly in aneurysmal or fibrotic segements. Miyata et al. [20] reported an anastomotic aneurysm rate of 13.8%, and recommended regular imaging follow-up because anastomotic aneurysms may occur at any time after surgery. To reinforce the aortic anastomotic suture line, polytetrafluoroethylene patches can be applied [21,22].

Anastomosis on a diseased segment may also cause iatrogenic dissection (Fig. 1, 2). For example, a patient presented with bilateral leg claudication and hypertension. CTA revealed suprarenal aortic, celiac, superior mesenteric, and right renal artery stenoses. Both iliac arteries were small, but appeared uninvolved with the disease. Therefore, a bypass from the supraceliac aorta to the aortic bifurcation was planned. After aortotomy, the distal aorta showed chronic fibrotic changes due to the disease; however, there was no apparent active inflammation, and distal anastomosis was performed uneventfully. However, early postoperatively, bilateral iliac artery dissections were detected, with flow limitation more pronounced on the right side. Therefore, both iliac stents were subsequently deployed to resolve this issue. This case illustrates that the extent of inflammation may exceed what is apparent on CT images, and vascular surgeons should always be prepared with secondary options.

② Avoid vessels with high incidence of TAK vasculitis as the inflow source

Bypass inflow should not originate from arteries that have a high incidence of vasculitis involvement, even if the disease is not apparent on arteriography. For supra-aortic reconstructions, an aorto-subclavian bypass is therefore preferred over a carotid-subclavian bypass, as the carotid arteries are more frequently affected in TAK.

③ Bypass is preferred than endarterectomy

Bypass grafting or interposition grafting is the preferred surgical approach for obstructive lesions. Endarterectomy with patch angioplasty is not recommended because of the transmural involvement characteristics of the TAK. Even if endarterectomy is possible, suturing arteries affected by the disease may later cause anastomotic and other local complications.

④ Visceral artery reconstruction

In renal artery reconstruction, extra-anatomic bypasses, such as hepato-renal or spleno-renal bypass, are generally discouraged because celiac artery stenosis is present in approximately 15%-20% of TAK patients. However, in selected cases, such extra-anatomic bypass may be a valid option (Fig. 3) [23].

Aorto-renal bypass grafts are preferred in adolescents requiring renal revascularization, and the internal iliac artery is the conduit of choice. A great saphenous vein graft can be used as an alternative conduit [24].

⑤ Inflow from the descending thoracic aorta

Common vasculitis involvement of the proximal abdominal aorta (30%-40%) mandates the use of alternative inflow sites for graft construction. The distal descending thoracic aorta often serves as an excellent inflow site (Fig. 4).

⑥ Inflow from the ascending aorta

In cases of diffuse calcification from the descending thoracic aorta to the abdominal aorta, the ascending aorta can be a good inflow site because it is usually spared in TAK (Fig. 5, 6). When performing a bypass from the ascending aorta to the abdominal aorta, the course of the prosthetic graft should be positioned carefully to avoid direct bowel contact, which could result in enteric erosion or graft infection [25,26].

Kim et al. [27] reported excellent outcomes in eight TAK patients with mid-aortic syndrome treated with extra-anatomic ascending aorta-to-abdominal aorta bypass. Operative strategies included proximal anastomosis using a side-biting clamp without cardiopulmonary bypass, and a graft course without bowel contact down to the infrarenal aorta. The distal anastomosis site was wrapped with the omentum for protection.

Recently, Ku et al. [28] reported a mini-access ascending aorto-bifemoral bypass for aortic steno-occlusive disease. They reported favorable early outcomes in seven patients with mid-aortic syndrome treated with videoscope-assisted mini-thoracotomy, laparoscopy, and limited skin incisions on the right chest, umbilicus, and both groins. However, concerns remain regarding the long-term patency of such long prosthetic graft and the potential for graft infection due to enteric erosion. Further studies are required to establish the long-term safety and efficacy of this novel technique.

PROGNOSIS

Another key feature in the natural history of TAK is its chronic, relapsing, and progressive course. The clinical course is often unpredictable, mirroring the uncertainty of its etiology. The prognosis is largely determined by the presence of complications and the progressive course of the disease. Previously, we reported a case of relapse 20 years after an aorto-renal bypass, which presented as an aortic pseudoaneurysm [29]. The patient was treated with a prosthetic bypass graft from the descending thoracic aorta to the aortic bifurcation, a left renal artery bypass with the saphenous vein, and exclusion of the suprarenal aortic pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 7).

Ham et al. [30] evaluated 40 patients with TAK who had undergone initial surgical treatment and found that 40% developed new or recurrent symptomatic disease progression during a mean follow-up of 6.4 years. They concluded that lifelong vascular surveillance is essential for all patients undergoing surgical intervention for TAK and that the beneficial effects of steroids and immunosuppressive agents on surgical outcomes are questionable.

CONCLUSION

Vascular surgeons should be aware of the inflammatory and systemic nature of TAK and adhere to the recommended multidisciplinary treatment strategies for this rare disease. Surgical procedures should be avoided during the active phase of the disease. Bypass surgery remains the preferred surgical option for obstructive lesions; endarterectomy and patch angioplasty are generally not feasible. Anastomosis should be performed on disease-free vessel segments, and the ascending or descending thoracic aorta serves as the most reliable inflow site. It is also important to avoid vessels that are frequently affected by vasculitis; therefore, aorto-subclavian bypass is preferred over carotid-subclavian bypass when reconstructing supra-aortic lesions.
