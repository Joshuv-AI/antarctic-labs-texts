Secreted frizzled-related proteins in angiogenesis: molecular mechanisms and clinical implications
Authors: Wei Wu, Yukun Zhang, Weihua Song, David Virshup, Xiaomeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature, is essential for tissue homeostasis, development, and repair. Dysregulation of this tightly regulated process contributes to a wide range of diseases, including cancer, ischemic disorders, and chronic inflammatory conditions. This review focuses on the secreted frizzled-related protein (SFRP) family, a group of pivotal yet underappreciated regulators of neovascularization. We discuss the tissue-specific expression patterns, regulatory mechanisms, and functional roles of SFRPs in both physiological and pathological vascular remodeling. Particular attention is given to their interactions with key signaling pathways, including Wnt, highlighting their context-dependent effects on angiogenesis. Drawing on extensive preclinical evidence, we position SFRPs as novel regulators of vascular remodeling and explore their potential as promising targets for therapeutic intervention. This comprehensive analysis underscores the importance of further mechanistic and clinical studies to unlock the therapeutic potential of SFRPs in vascular pathologies.

Introduction

Overview

Angiogenesis, the formation of a complex vascular network from existing blood vessels, represents a double-edged sword [1]. While indispensable for physiological processes such as reproduction, development, growth, and wound healing, dysregulated angiogenesis under pathological conditions contributes to cancer progression, cardiovascular diseases, diabetic vasculopathy, and ischemic disorders [2, 3]. The delicate balance between its physiological and pathological roles makes the molecular regulation of angiogenesis a compelling frontier in contemporary biomedical research.

The angiogenic cascade is tightly controlled [4] and initiated by signals such as hypoxia, hemodynamic changes, and growth factors [5]. In response, endothelial cells (ECs) undergo a coordinated sequence of events: disruption of the cell–cell junctions, degradation of the basement membrane (BM), and phenotypic specialization into migratory tip cells and proliferative stalk cells. Tip cells lead the sprouting front by extending dynamic filopodia to sense angiogenic cues, while stalk cells elongate the sprout and form the vessel lumen [6]. Subsequent events include basement membrane re-deposition, vessel anastomosis, and recruitment of mural cells, which contribute to vessel stabilization and maturation [7]. Finally, pruning removes redundant or non-functional branches, optimizing the vascular network for efficient perfusion. A detailed understanding of the sequential stages of angiogenesis and their underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms has opened new avenues for the development of therapeutic interventions targeting angiogenesis.

Key molecular regulators of angiogenesis and vascular remodeling

Angiogenesis is regulated by a complex network of organ- and cell-type-specific mechanisms. Single-cell profiling has revealed distinct molecular signatures in venous versus arterial ECs [8], and in tissue-specific tip cells, such as those in the heart [9] and brain [10]. Key signalling pathways include vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), Notch/Delta, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and angiopoietins (Ang1/Ang2) with their receptor Tie2 (Fig. 1). Among them, VEGF—particularly the VEGFA165 isoform—is the principal driver of angiogenesis and vascular permeability [11]. Acting primarily through VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2), VEGF stimulates EC proliferation, filopodial extension, migration, and vascular permeability via downstream MAPK signaling [12–16]. Different VEGF isoforms display divergent functions and bioavailability during angiogenic processes [17, 18]. Dysregulated VEGF signaling is implicated in many vascular disorders [19, 20], and anti-VEGF therapies are widely used in oncology and ophthalmology. However, treatment resistance is common due to compensatory activation of alternative angiogenic pathways [21].

Notch signaling pathway, particularly DLL4-Notch 1, modulates tip/stalk cell specification and restricts excessive sprouting [22–24]. There is extensive crosstalk between Notch/Delta and VEGF signaling: VEGF regulates DLL4 and Notch-1 expressions [25], while Notch activity is modulated by proteins like multiple PDZ domain protein (MPDZ) [26] and transcription factors such as Sp1/Sp3 [27]. Galectin-3 (Gal3), secreted by activated microglia, promotes pathological angiogenesis by binding to Jagged 1, therefore disrupting Notch signaling [28]. Emerging therapeutic approaches—such as hydrogel-based Jagged 1 delivery [29], DLL4 blocking antibodies, and DLL4/VEGF bispecific antibodies [30]—have demonstrated significant promise in pre-clinical models, laying the groundwork for their evaluation in ongoing clinical trials. Specifically, bispecific antibodies like dilpacimab and navicixizumab, which concurrently target DLL4/VEGF, have demonstrated synergistic action in clinical trials by disrupting the tumor vasculature and inhibiting tumor growth [31, 32]. In hypertension, clinical evidence confirms that aerobic exercise was shown to promote microvascular angiogenesis by enhancing the angiogenic capacity of endothelial progenitor cells through inhibition of Notch1 signaling [33].

Beyond vessel sprouting, maturation and stabilization are governed by factors like PDGF, Angiopoietin (Ang)/Tie, and TGF-β [34–36]. PDGF is essential for mural cell recruitment and ECM deposition [37], and its delivery via hydrogel promotes neovascularization and cardiac repair [38]. The Ang/Tie axis balances vessel stabilization and remodeling [39, 40]. Ang-1 activates Tie2 to promote vascular quiescence [41], whereas Ang-2 functions in a context dependent manner that can antagonize Tie2 signalling [42], thereby facilitating vascular remodeling under stress conditions [43, 44]. Disruption of this balance contributes to vascular pathology. TGF-β promotes pericyte differentiation, endothelial-pericyte interactions via N-cadherin, and enhances overall vessel stability [35, 36, 45, 46]. Furthermore, TGF-β/ALK5 signaling maintains cerebrovascular integrity by epigenetically repressing Ang-2 expression in pericytes [47]. Aberrant TGF-β signaling leads to endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), vascular dysfunction, and inflammation [48–51].

Another critical mediator of vessel integrity is Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), which enhances endothelial junctions and pericyte interactions [52, 53] via endothelial differentiation sphingolipid G-protein-coupled receptor-1 (EDG1) and EDG3 subtypes of G protein-coupled receptors on ECs [54]. Loss of S1P signaling leads to vessel instability and haemorrhage [55]. Additional stabilizing factors, such as extracellular matrix glycoprotein elastin microfibril interfacer 2 (Emilin2), further promote EC-pericyte interactions [56].

Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) [57, 58] also contributes to angiogenesis by promoting EC proliferation and modulating inflammation [59–63]. Unlike VEGF, bFGF alone is insufficient to drive vasculogenesis [64] but often compensates for VEGF inhibition [65]. Innovations like micelle-encapsulated bFGF [66] and Ag nanocomposite hydrogel (Ag-NCH) [67] have been developed to treat ischemic injury and wound healing.

Together, these molecular pathways orchestrate angiogenesis and vascular remodeling. Among them, the Wnt signaling pathway [68–71] and its endogenous regulators, the secreted frizzled-related proteins (SFRPs), have emerged as critical modulators of vascular biology, and their roles form the central focus of this review.

Wnt signaling and its regulation

Overview

Wnt signaling is indispensable in vascular biology, governing key EC processes, such as proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Evidence from diverse model organisms, from zebrafish to humans, underscores its conserved role in physiological and pathological neovascularization [68, 70]. Elucidating the crosstalk between Wnt signaling and other signaling pathways has provided valuable insights into vascular physiology and pathology, while uncovering novel therapeutic targets with significant translational potential [72, 73]. For example, Wnt7a enhances the VEGF signaling pathway through its receptor ROR2, which is essential for initiating tip cell formation and promoting angiogenesis [74]. Similarly, Wnt3a promotes EC migration and tube formation by upregulating Ang-2, illustrating that the Wnt-Ang-2 signaling axis is critical for vascular repair [70]. Dysregulation of this pathway contributes to vascular disorders, including atherosclerosis, hypertension, and vascular malformations [68–71].

The Wnt family comprises secreted glycoproteins that initiate signaling by binding to Frizzled (FzD) receptors and co-receptors such as low-density lipoprotein receptor-related proteins 5 and 6 (LRP5/6), or receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) like Ryk and Ror [75, 76]. These interactions trigger intracellular signaling cascades that are broadly categorized into two major branches: the canonical (β-catenin-dependent) and non-canonical (β-catenin-independent) pathways [68, 77, 78]. In canonical signaling, typically activated by ligands such as Wnt1, Wnt2, Wnt3, Wnt7, Wnt8, and Wnt10, ligand binding leads to β-catenin stabilization, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional activation of Wnt target genes. In contrast, the non-canonical pathways, primarily associated with Wnt5 and Wnt11, regulate cytoskeletal organization, calcium signaling, and cell polarity [79–81].

Regulation of Wnt signaling

Wnt signaling is tightly controlled through post-translational modifications (PTMs) and protein interaction dynamics [82–85]. These mechanisms collectively regulate the stability, localization, and activity of core Wnt pathway components, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis and ensuring the fidelity of Wnt-mediated responses in vascular development and pathology.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs)

Wnt ligands undergo key PTMs, most notably palmitoylation and glycosylation. Palmitoylation, mediated by the membrane-bound acyltransferase Porcupine (PORCN) in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [86], is essential for Wnt secretion and biological activities [86–88]. Wnt ligands are also glycosylated at multiple residues [89, 90], though the functional relevance remains less well-defined. Extracellular enzymes further modulate Wnt activities. Notum inactivates Wnts or their stabilizers through deacylation [91–93], while metalloprotease Tiki selectively cleaves Wnt3a, Xenopus Wnt8, and Wnt5 [94, 95], inactivating them in a FZD-dependent manner [96].

Within the canonical pathway, β-catenin stability is regulated by a phosphorylation-dependent destruction complex. In the absence of Wnt, β-catenin is sequentially phosphorylated by Casein Kinase 1 α (CK1α) and Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3 (GSK3), marking it for β-TrCP-mediated ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation, thereby preventing the Wnt target gene activation. Wnt ligand binding disrupts this complex and stabilizes cytosolic β-catenin, which translocates to the nucleus and activates nuclear translocation to T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer-binding factor (TCF/LEF)-dependent transcription [76]. β-catenin can also be degraded through a ligand-independent mechanisms, such as by an SCF-like E3 ligase complex containing Siah-1, in response to DNA damage [97–99]. Similarly, Hakai promotes ubiquitination of membrane-associated, E-cadherin-bound β-catenin, disrupting cell–cell adhesion and promoting motility [100].

Cellular sensitivity to Wnt ligands is modulated by a tightly regulated ubiquitylation/deubiquitylation cycle. The RING-domain ubiquitylases RNF43 and ZNRF3 control Fzds and LRP5/6 membrane abundance, and are themselves inhibited by R-spondins [101]. K63-linked ubiquitination of β-catenin drives vascular calcification (VC) by activating osteogenic signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [102]. Conversely, deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) like ubiquitin-specific protease 6 (USP6) deubiquitinates FzDs, enhancing their membrane stability and Wnt signaling [103]. Other DUBs, such as Usp28, Usp13, Usp15, and Trabid, stabilize β-catenin and related Wnt pathway components, including FOXM1 [104], WISP1, APC, and Axin [84, 105]. DUBs can also repress Wnt signalling. For example, XBP1u promotes β-catenin degradation and represses β-catenin/TCF-mediated transcription of Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and Msx2 [106].

Besides ubiquitylation, kinases and phosphatases further fine-tune β-catenin stability and activity. Protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) reduces β-catenin levels through the APC complex [107, 108], whereas inhibiting Protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) promotes β-catenin degradation by enhancing the phosphorylation of Axin[109]. Conversely, RTKs enhance β-catenin transcriptional activity via Ser552 phosphorylation [85]. Notably, the family with sequence similarity 83 member A (FAM83A) directly binds β-catenin and prevents its degradation by impeding the destruction complex formation [110]. Protein kinase A (PKA) phosphorylates β-catenin at Ser675 to enhance its transcriptional activity [111], whereas protein kinase C (PKC) promotes β-catenin degradation via TRIM33. Other kinases, including JAK3, PAK, CK2, PKD1, and BCR-ABL, also dynamically regulate β-catenin stability and transcriptional output [112, 113]. In summary, PTMs act as a key mechanism for regulating Wnt signaling.

Protein interaction dynamics

In addition to intracellular modifications, Wnt signaling is finely regulated by complex protein–protein interactions at the extracellular and membrane levels. Key extracellular modulators include SFRPs, DKKs, and Wnt inhibitory factor (WIF), which bind to Wnt ligands or their receptors [96, 114–116]. The transmembrane protein TMEM132A stabilizes Wnt ligands and promotes their interaction with Wntless, which is essential for Wnt trafficking and signal transduction [117]. TMEM132A also interacts with FzD to control Wnt signaling [118]. Additionally, the GPI-anchored matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) inhibitor Reck (reversion-inducing-cysteine-rich protein with Kazal motifs) and an orphan receptor of the adhesion GPCR family Gpr124 form a co-receptor complex that stabilizes Wnt7a/Wnt7b and enhances canonical Wnt signaling [119, 120]. Other co-receptors, such as ROR, RYK, and glypicans, can compete with FzDs for Wnt binding, activating alternative pathways [121–123]. Intracellularly, Dvl/Dsh interacts with Naked cuticle to prevent β-catenin accumulation [124]. In the nucleus, transcriptional repressors such as Groucho bind TCF/LEF to silence Wnt target genes [121].

Secreted frizzled-related proteins (SFRPs)

Overview

SFRPs constitute a family of secreted glycoproteins that modulate Wnt signaling by binding either to Wnt ligands or FzDs via their conserved frizzled-like cysteine-rich domain (CRD). Through these interactions, SFRPs regulate the bioavailability of Wnt ligands and the overall activity of the Wnt signalling pathway [114, 125, 126]. The SFRP family was first identified nearly three decades ago with the discovery of FRZB/SFRP3 in articular cartilage and SFRP1 in conditioned media [126, 127]. Subsequently, additional family members, SFRP2, SFRP4, and SFRP5, were identified, each exhibiting a context-dependent and functionally distinct role (Table 1). Notably, SFRPs can exhibit dual functionality: while traditionally viewed as Wnt antagonists, they can also act as Wnt agonists under specific conditions. A key example of this is the biphasic activity of SFRP1, which enhances Wnt signaling at low concentrations but suppresses it at high concentrations [116], depending on the cellular environment and FzD expression profile [117]. Structural characteristics and classification

All SFRPs share conserved structural features that include a signal peptide required for secretion, a netrin domain (NTR), and a CRD. The NTR domain contains six conserved cysteine residues that form three disulfide bonds and shares homology with netrin-like domains found in complement proteins (C3, C4, C5), type I procollagen C-proteinase enhancer proteins, and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases. The CRD, structurally similar to the extracellular Wnt-binding domain of FzD receptors [130], enables SFRPs to interact with both Wnt ligands and FzD [131–133].

Despite structural similarity, SFRPs exhibit distinct ligand specificities. SFRP1 (FrzA) preferentially binds Wnt1 but not Wnt5a [114]. While SFRP1, SFRP2, SFRP3, and SFRP4 can bind Wnt3a with nanomolar affinities, only SFRP1 and SFRP2 effectively antagonize Wnt3a-induced β-catenin accumulation in mouse fibroblasts [134]. SFRP2 specifically antagonizes Wnt3a signaling during mesoderm differentiation in embryonic stem cells [134]. SFRP5 preferentially regulates Wnt5a in a mouse model of type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated with myocardial infarction (T2DM-MI) [135].

Expression patterns in human tissues

Expression in normal tissues

SFRPs display tissue-specific expression patterns across human tissues (Fig. 2). Among them, SFRP1, SFRP2, and SFRP3 are broadly expressed, while SFRP4 and SFRP5 exhibit more restricted expression profiles. Specifically, SFRP1 is highly expressed in the endometrium, adipose tissue, salivary glands, and kidneys. SFRP2 is enriched in the urinary bladder, gall bladder, and esophagus. SFRP3 is highest in the spleen, gall bladder, and perimetrium. SFRP4 is most abundant in the endometrium, with moderate expression in the bladder. SFRP5, generally expressed at lower levels, is relatively high in the pancreas, duodenum, and small intestine [136]. Additional cell- and tissue-specific expression if SFRP transcripts have been reported: SFRP1 in human lung fibroblasts [137], esophageal lamina propria [138], osteoblasts and chondrocytes [139]; SFRP3-5 in articular cartilage and bone [140–142]; SFRP2 [143, 144] and SFRP5 in the retina [145]; and SFRP4 in cardiac vasculature [146]. Immunohistochemical studies have further confirmed the high expression of SFRP1 and SFRP3 proteins in human placental tissues, particularly in ECs [147]. SFRP Expression in disease contexts

Altered SFRP expression has been reported in various pathological conditions, particularly in cancer, vascular diseases, metabolic dysfunction, and inflammatory disorders (Table 2). For example, SFRP1 [147] and SFRP3 [148] expression is significantly reduced in trophoblastic tumors and glioblastoma multiforme [149]. Conversely, SFRP1 expression is elevated in the placentas of smoking mothers and pregnant mice exposed to carbon monoxide analogs [150]. SFRP2 is overexpressed in several malignancies, including breast cancer [151], melanoma [152], multiple myeloma [153], and osteosarcoma (OS) [154], implicating it in tumourigenesis. SFRP3 shows biphasic expression patterns in disease contexts. It is upregulated in rat aortic smooth muscle cells (RASMCs) following aorta balloon injury [155], yet significantly reduced in the serum of osteosarcoma patients [148]. SFRP4 is significantly upregulated during the mid-to-late proliferative phases of the endometrial cycle, suggesting potential roles in endometrium remodeling, angiogenesis, and inflammation [156]. However, it is downregulated in fetal cardiac tissues in a non-human primate model of fetal inflammatory response syndrome [146]. SFRP5 expression increases in the aortas of aged rats and in the plasma of elderly individuals [157]. Elevated plasma SFRP5 levels have also been observed in patients with acute aortic dissection (AAD) [158], whereas lower SFRP5 levels are associated with metabolic dysfunction in obesity [159]. In preclinical models, altered SFRP expression has also been linked to impaired renal function [160]. SFRP expression patterns in pathological conditions

Regulation of SFRP expression

Transcriptional and post-translational control

Epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation, oxidative stress, and PTMs, play important roles in regulating SFRP gene expression [161–163]. In cancer, promoter methylation frequently silences SFRP genes, contributing to tumour progression and invasiveness, as observed in gastric [164], multiple myeloma [165], and colorectal cancers [166]. Notably, knockdown of DNA methyltransferase-1 (Dnmt1) restores SFRP1 expression and inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by downregulating β-catenin and Wnt target genes [163]. Silencing of methyl CpG binding domain protein 2 (MBD2) and epidermal homolog enhancer 2 (EZH2) has been shown to reduce SFRP promoter methylation, thereby reactivating expression of SFRP1, SFRP2, SFRP4, and SFRP5 in cancer cells [167].

Pharmacological agents can also influence SFRP expression. For instance, glucocorticoids and cyclosporine A suppress SFRP expression and enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling [168, 169], whereas methotrexate induces SFRP1 mRNA expression [170]. These findings suggest that SFRP regulation is responsive to hormonal and chemical stimuli, though the precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Roles of noncoding RNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), ~ 22-nucleotide noncoding RNAs, regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to complementary sequences in target mRNA [171]. Several miRNAs directly target SFRPs, particularly in osteogenesis. These include miR-27a, miR-218, miR-454-3p, and miR-542-3p [172–174]. Along these, miR-27a modulates angiogenesis via SFRP1 [175]. In a canine peri-implantitis model, miR-27a overexpression enhanced angiogenesis and osteogenesis by downregulating SFRP1 and upregulating VEGF and Ang-1 in peri-implant soft tissues and bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) [175]. Similarly, miR-27a-3p promotes osteogenic differentiation of murine BMSCs by targeting SFRP1 in an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model of osteoporosis [176]. In addition, the long noncoding RNA DLG5-AS1 promotes cell proliferation and invasion via the EZH2-mediated H3 histone methylation [177]. However, regulatory interactions between other SFRP family members and noncoding RNAs remain largely unexplored.

SFRP-regulated signaling in vascular cells

Wnt-dependent pathways

SFRPs regulate angiogenesis through both Wnt-dependent and Wnt-independent mechanisms, with effects that are highly context- and cell-type-specific. Their canonical role as Wnt antagonists involves blocking the interaction between Wnt ligands and FzDs [114, 125, 178, 179]. For example, SFRP-1 inhibits Wnt signaling in ECs by interacting with FzD-4 and FzD-7 in cooperation with GSK-3β [180]. Similarly, SFRP4 antagonizes canonical Wnt signaling by reversing lithium chloride-induced GSK-3β inhibition and angiogenic gene expression in HUVECs [181].

Beyond simple antagonism, SFRPs can also modulate Wnt signalling in more complex and dynamic ways. RPE-derived SFRP1 activates non-canonical Wnt signaling by elevating intracellular Ca2+ levels and phosphorylated CaMKII in choroidal capillaries and ECs [182]. SFRP2 has been shown to activate the canonical Wnt signaling pathway in lung cancer cells [183] and to promote angiogenesis through the Wnt/Ca2⁺/NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells) signaling pathway [184]. Likewise, SFRP4 exhibits dual roles, activating Wnt/Ca2+ signaling while simultaneously inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, in HUVECs [181].

SFRPs also regulate β-catenin directly. They can bind β-catenin via their NTR and CRD domains, thereby regulating its transcriptional activities in a domain-specific manner [185, 186]. In addition, SFRPs influence Wnt ligand trafficking. For example, SFRP2 forms stable heterodimers with Wnt3a and Wnt-5a to enhance their cell surface accumulation and exosome-mediated re-secretion [187], processes that have been implicated in myocardial fibrosis and VC [188].

Wnt-independent pathways

In addition to modulating Wnt signalling, SFRPs also act through Wnt-independent mechanisms. For instance, SFRP1 reduces GSK-3β phosphorylation without altering cytoplasmic β-catenin levels [189]. SFRP2 inhibits the activity of Xolloid-related (Xlr), a metalloproteinase involved in regulating BMP signaling during Xenopus development, independent of Wnt signaling [190]. In smooth muscle cells, SFRP5 inhibits PDGF-BB-induced P38 MAPK phosphorylation, thereby exerting protective effects against arterial aging [157].

In summary, SFRPs exhibit remarkable functional diversity and context-dependent signalling versatility in vascular biology. Through both Wnt-dependent and Wnt-independent mechanisms, they fine-tune endothelial and smooth muscle cell function, emphasizing their potential as therapeutic targets for vascular disorders. SFRPs in physiological and pathological angiogenesis

Overview

While Wnt signaling has long been recognised as a key regulator of vascular development and angiogenesis [10, 152, 191–194], the specific functions of SFRPs in these processes remain incompletely understood. This session synthesizes recent advances that address this knowledge gap and highlights the emerging therapeutic potential of SFRP-mediated regulation in vascular diseases.

Roles in embryonic angiogenesis

Embryonic angiogenesis is essential for establishing a functional vascular network during development [195]. SFRP1 plays a pro-angiogenic role, acting primarily via Wnt1a, rather than Wnt-5a [114]. In the chicken embryo chorioallantois membrane (CAM) model, SFRP-1 promotes vascular maturation independent of VEGF, FGF-2, Ang-1, or Akt activation, likely through reduced phosphorylation of GSK-3 [196]. In mice, SFRP1 is expressed in the heart and visceral yolk sac during development, where it co-localizes with Wnt8, suggesting a role in cardiac morphogenesis and multilegance regulation [197].

SFRP2 also promotes angiogenesis in the CAM model, enhancing vascular branching and tube formation [198], while SFRP4 demonstrates anti-angiogenic properties, significantly inhibiting vessel formation [181, 199]. Although SFRP5 can bind FzD5 and stimulate angiogenesis [200], its specific contribution to embryonic vascular development remains unclear.

These findings underscore the context-dependent roles of SFRPs in embryonic vascular patterning.

Bone-related angiogenesis

Angiogenesis plays a pivotal role in bone development and remodeling [201]. Vascular ECs regulate mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) differentiation into cartilage and support bone mineralization [61, 202]. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling promotes both angiogenesis and osteogenesis, in part through epidermal growth factor-like domain-containing protein 6 (EGFL6)-mediated angiogenesis and osteogenesis [203], enhances stress fiber formation in bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) [204], and contributes to VC by facilitating the transformation of VSMCs into osteoblast-like cells [205, 206].

Among SFRPs, SFRP5 is best studied in bone-related vascular regulation [207–209]. In a rat model of chronic kidney disease (CKD) induced by a high-phosphate diet (HPD), aortic VC and bone loss were associated with downregulated SFRP5 and Wnt5a alongside upregulated Runx2. Intriguingly, adenovirus-mediated overexpression of SFRP5 attenuated aortic calcification, and the expression of Runx2 and BMP-2 [210], whereas anti-SFRP5 treatment reduced phosphorylated Rho-associated coiled-coil-containing protein kinase 2 (ROCK2) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) levels in VSMCs [207–209].

Additionally, SFRP2 expression is elevated in rats with advanced VC [211] and confers protective effects [212]. However, the mechanism underlying these expression changes remains unclear.

Tumour-associated angiogenesis

Angiogenesis supports tumour growth, invasion, and metastasis [213]. Among the SFRP family members, SFRP1, SFRP2, and SFRP4 have been implicated in this process.

SFRP1 overexpression in gastric cancer cells promotes angiogenesis as evidenced by increased human umbilical cord vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) tube formation, migration, and VEGF expression. Consistent with this observation, SFRP1 overexpression leads to increased tumour size, enhanced invasiveness, and elevated microvessel density [196, 214]. SFRP2 acts as a pro-angiogenic factor in several cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer, angiosarcoma, OS, and melanoma [132, 152, 154, 184, 199, 215]. It is overexpressed in osteoblasts (OBs) derived from Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) patient induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and conditioned medium from LFS patient iPSC-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) promotes HUVEC tube formation, whereas the knockdown of SFRP2 in tuomur cells inhibits tumour growth in a mouse xenograft model [154].

In melanoma, SFRP2 overexpression in dermal fibroblasts drives angiogenesis, metastasis, and resistance to targeted therapies [152]. Notably, an SFRP2 antibody suppresses melanoma angiogenesis in aged mice [216].

Conversely, SFRP4 exhibits anti-angiogenic activity by inducing EC apoptosis and suppressing proliferation, thereby restricting tumour growth [181, 217]. Synthetic peptides derived from its CRD domain induce endothelial dysfunctions without triggering apoptosis, suggesting a multifaceted anti-angiogenic mechanism [181, 217].

Angiogenesis in other pathological conditions

SFRPs also modulate angiogenesis in non-cancerous pathologies. In myopia, SFRP1 is upregulated in retinal pigmented epithelial (RPE) cells in response to Cochlin, an ECM protein that impairs choroidal vessel perfusion [182]. In addition, SFRP1 induces Ca2+ influx and CaMKII (α and β isoforms) phosphorylation in choroidal ECs, leading to apoptosis and migration. These events could be reversed by an SFRP1 inhibitor or Wnt3a activator, and intravitreal injection of lentiviral particles carrying SFRP1 inhibitors restores choroidal vessel perfusion in a rat form-deprived myopia (FDM) model.

SFRP5 is also expressed in the RPE cells [218], though its role in ocular angiogenesis remains unexplored. In high glucose-treated HUVECs and cardiac tissues of a T2DM-MI model, SFRP5 expression is reduced. On the other hand, overexpression of SFRP5 enhances HUVEC migration and angiogenesis by regulating Bax, caspase-3, and Bcl-2 levels [135]. In a mouse model of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), SFRP5 deficiency exacerbates reperfusion injury and capillary damage by promoting Wnt5a expression in macrophages, which induces the production of the anti-angiogenic mVEGF-A165b splice variant via the Ror2/JNK/SC35 axis [19].

Cellular and molecular mechanisms of SFRPs in vascular cells

Overview

Angiogenesis entails coordinated cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation, engaging various cell types such as ECs, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), pericytes, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and perivascular macrophages [34]. SFRPs play vital roles in regulating these cellular processes, making them attractive therapeutic targets for treating vascular disorders.

Regulation of cell migration and differentiation

SFRPs are critical modulators of cell migration, particularly in ECs, though their effects are highly context-dependent. For example, SFRP1 promotes EC migration and angiogenesis in vitro but can also suppress EC migration and angiogenesis when exposed to CD133-containing conditioned medium from the human fetal aorta [219]. Similarly, SFRP2 promotes EC migration [184, 199, 215], whereas SFRP4 inhibits HUVEC migration [181].

In the perivascular niche, MSCs differentiate into pericytes or fibroblasts and contribute to vascular repair and regeneration through paracrine signaling. SFRP1 and SFRP2 stimulate CXCL5 expression and secretion in human MSCs, potentially enhancing angiogenesis during bone repair [220]. In pathological states, adventitial fibroblasts may differentiate into myofibroblasts, contributing to ECM production and vessel remodeling. Interestingly, SFRP1 exhibits differential expression in invading versus non-invading fibroblasts [221].

Effects on cell proliferation and apoptosis

SFRPs exert diverse, sometimes opposing, effects on vascular cell proliferation and apoptosis. SFRP1 and SFRP2 are generally anti-apoptotic and protective in ECs [196]. Paradoxically, recombinant SFRP1 can also induce cell cycle arrest in ECs and SMCs by modulating cyclin/cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) activity and increasing cytosolic phospho-β-catenin levels [222]. Cigarette smoke exposure upregulates SFRP1 in embryonic lung tissue, downregulates Cyclin D1, and suppresses mesenchymal proliferation [223]. In cardiac fibroblasts, SFRP1 deficiency results in increased α-SMA expression and proliferation [224]. Additionally, SFRP1 is decreased in myofibroblasts of ulcerative colitis (UC) samples, whereas recombinant SFRP1 inhibits EC proliferation in a UC model [225]. SFRP2 inhibits EC apoptosis under hypoxic conditions [199], and neuron-derived SFRP2 promotes EC survival [226]. Consistently, anti-SFRP2 antibody promotes apoptosis and inhibits the proliferation of ECs [227]. SFRP4, on the other hand, disrupts the stability of endothelial rings and suppresses EC proliferation [181]. SFRP5 significantly inhibits PDGF‑BB‑induced SMC proliferation in vitro [157].

In summary, SFRPs influence vascular cell proliferation and survival in a cell-type- and context-dependent manner, potentially modulated by signals present in tissue microenvironments [228].

Regulation of tube formation

SFRP1 and SFRP2 are prominent regulators of tube formation in angiogenesis. SFRP1 promotes tube formation in vitro and enhances functional vessel formation in chick CAM assays, mesenchymal cell graft models, and malignant glioblastoma tumor models [196]. Transgenic mice overexpressing SFRP1 exhibit more muscularized vessels with a larger lumen area [229], potentially mediated by Fzd-4 and Fzd-7 [180]. SFRP1 downregulation impairs endothelial planar polarity via Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (TET1)-dependent demethylation, disrupting vascular integrity [230]. SFRP2 facilitates endothelial tube branching and tube formation via Fzd5 [200], whereas hSFRP2mAb effectively inhibits tube formation [227]. In addition, cardiac-specific overexpression of SFRP2 enhances capillary density and cardiac angiogenesis. Interestingly, direct application of SFRP2 fails to induce angiogenesis in HUVECs, but conditioned medium from SFRP2-treated myocytes does, highlighting a paracrine mechanism or the dependence on the presence of other angiogenic molecules [231].

Clinical implications and future directions

Therapeutic potential

Anti-angiogenic therapy aiming at disrupting abnormal vasculature to induce avascular dormancy or enhancing vessel normalization is an established strategy in oncology, ophthalmology, and some other life-threatening diseases [232–234]. SFRPs are emerging as key biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Elevated serum SFRP5 levels are associated with favourable cardiometabolic profiles, such as lower blood pressure, higher HDL cholesterol, and lower triglycerides [235]. Serum SFRP5 also predicts coronary artery disease (CAD) severity and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [236]. Altered plasma SFRP levels were observed in patients with acute aortic dissection, suggesting their prognostic potential [237].

Given their roles in angiogenesis, SFRP-targeting therapies are especially promising in oncology. SFRP2 polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies reduce angiogenesis, tumour volume, and β-catenin and Nuclear Factor of Activated T-cells cytoplasmic 3 (NFATc3) activation in SVR angiosarcoma and MDA-MB-231 breast carcinoma xenograft models in mice [184]. A humanized monoclonal antibody against SFRP2 (hSFRP2 mAb) not only inhibited angiogenesis and tumor growth but also promoted tumor apoptosis in a triple-negative breast carcinoma model in mice [227]. In a hamster model of cardiomyopathy, SFRP2 antibodies promoted angiogenesis, improved cardiac function, and increased myocardial VEGF and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) levels [238].

Emerging tools and therapeutic strategies

New platforms such as gene editing, noncoding RNAs, and exosome-mediated delivery are transforming angiogenesis therapies [239]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, enable precise delivery of therapeutic molecules [240], with innovations like magnetic nanoparticle-loaded exosomes that release cargo in acidic environments showing promise for ischemic tissue repair [241]. For example, MSC-derived exosomes effectively deliver miR-205 in the eye, leading to reduced cytotoxicity and abnormal blood vessel formation in the mouse retina and choroid [242]. Bacteria-derived nanovesicles [243], biodegradable TFRD-loaded porous scaffold [244], and non-pharmacological interventions such as electroacupuncture [245], can further expand delivery options for SFRP-based therapeutics.

In summary, these emerging technologies may facilitate the efficient and targeted delivery of SFRP-based therapeutics for treating vascular-related diseases.

Research Gap, translational challenges, and future directions

Angiogenesis is a tightly regulated, multistep process involving complex crosstalk among various signaling pathways and cell types. This complexity poses a major challenge for single-target therapies, which often show limited efficacy and may lead to resistance [2, 246]. A combinatorial treatment strategy that targets multiple signaling axes simultaneously may improve therapeutic outcomes in angiogenesis-related diseases.

While originally identified as Wnt antagonists [222], SFRPs regulate cellular processes through both Wnt-dependent and Wnt-independent pathways, positioning them as promising therapeutic targets to complement or enhance existing VEGF-based therapeutics. However, functional outcomes of SFRPs in angiogenesis appear to be highly context-dependent, influenced by factors such as concentration gradient, receptor expression profiles, crosstalk with other signaling pathways, and disease state. Moreover, their expression is governed by multiple layers of regulation, epigenetic mechanisms like DNA methylation and post-transcriptional mechanisms, many of which remain poorly characterized [163].

To advance SFRP-targeted therapies, there is a pressing need for systematic mapping of 1) expression levels and mutational profiles of SFRPs in disease-specific contexts; 2) their interaction networks and functional partners; 3) correlation between SFRP dysregulation and resistance to current anti-angiogenic agents; 4) biomarkers predictive of therapeutic response or disease progression. Emerging multi-omics platforms, including single-cell RNA sequencing, proteomics, epigenomics, and spatial transcriptomics, offer powerful tools to build this knowledge base. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) will play a critical enabling role in this process.

Conclusion

SFRPs constitute a versatile family of secreted proteins with increasing relevance in vascular diseases. Their ability to regulate angiogenesis and vascular remodeling positions them as promising therapeutic targets. While their mechanistic roles remain incompletely understood, mounting evidence underscores their context-specific, dual-modulatory roles. Ongoing investigation into their molecular interactions, regulatory control, and translational potential may pave the way for the development of novel therapeutic strategies against a wide spectrum of vascular-related diseases.
