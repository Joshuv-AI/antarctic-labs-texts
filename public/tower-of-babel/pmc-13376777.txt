Bletilla striata polysaccharides promote the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts on titanium surfaces via the PI3K/AKT pathway: a preliminary in vitro study
Authors: Fengtong Liu, Xuan Li, Rong Rong, Cong Zhou, Jianyong Dong, Jun Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the effects of Bletilla striata polysaccharides (BSPs) on the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) on titanium surfaces and clarify the regulatory role of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Primary HGFs were isolated from healthy gingival tissues and subjected to hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining and immunofluorescence (purity ≥ 95%). HGFs were cocultured with titanium sheets and divided into control, BSP (800 µg/mL, optimal concentration), and BSP + wortmannin (WM, PI3K inhibitor) groups. Cell proliferation was evaluated by a CCK-8 assay, live/dead staining, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) for Ki67 and PCNA mRNA expression. Cell migration was detected by scratch wound healing and Transwell assays. ELISA was used to measure the secretion of fibronectin (FN) and type I collagen (COLI) in the culture medium. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the cell morphology on the titanium surfaces. HE staining and immunofluorescence confirmed the successful isolation and identification of HGFs. BSPs significantly promoted HGF proliferation, as evidenced by increased CCK-8 absorbance, a higher live cell ratio, an increased proportion of Ki67/PCNA double-positive cells, and increased mRNA and protein expression levels of Ki67 and PCNA (P < 0.01). Scratch and Transwell assays revealed that BSPs enhanced HGF migratory capacity, accompanied by increased secretion of FN and COLI (P < 0.01). SEM revealed that BSPs improved cell spreading and surface coverage on titanium. Mechanistically, BSPs activated the PI3K/AKT pathway, and WM cotreatment abolished both the pathway activation and the proproliferative and promigratory effects of BSPs. BSPs promote the proliferation and migration of HGFs on titanium surfaces by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is associated with the upregulation of Ki67 and PCNA expression and increased FN and COLI secretion. This study provides experimental evidence for the potential application of BSPs as bioactive agents in peri-implant soft tissue regeneration, but these findings require further in vivo validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53360-3.Keywords: Bletilla striata polysaccharides (BSPs), Human gingival fibroblasts, Titanium surface, Cell proliferation, Cell migration, PI3K/AKT signaling pathwaySubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Osseointegration is a prerequisite for the long-term stability and functional performance of dental implants1. Studies have confirmed that a stable gingival structure around implants, namely, the peri-implant soft tissue seal, is highly important for achieving osseointegration2. The formation of the peri-implant soft tissue seal can effectively block microbial invasion, chemical penetration, and mechanical damage, thereby protecting the bone tissue, reducing the incidence of peri-implant diseases, and significantly improving the long-term survival rate of implants3.

Human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) are the main cellular component of gingival connective tissue and maintain gingival tissue homeostasis through proliferation and migration4,5. When the gingiva is damaged, HGFs rapidly transition from the interphase to the mitotic phase, recruit immune cells to generate an immune response, and synthesize collagen to promote tissue repair and wound healing6,7. The proliferation and migration of HGFs are the foundation for the formation of a peri-implant soft tissue seal8.

As a treasure of traditional Chinese medicine, TCM embodies profound cultural heritage and medical wisdom. Studies have shown that Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP), the active component of the TCM herb Bletilla striata, has anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and wound-healing properties9. BSP has characteristics such as bioadhesion, biodegradability, and biosafety and has been formulated into various dosage forms, including nanoparticles, microspheres, microneedles, and microcapsules, increasing its clinical application routes10. However, its effectiveness in promoting the peri-implant soft tissue seal and the underlying mechanism remain incompletely understood.

In this study, an in vitro coculture model of HGFs and titanium sheets was established to systematically investigate the effects of BSP on the proliferation and migration of HGFs for the first time, providing new insights for promoting the formation of the peri-implant soft tissue seal. Through an experimental design using wortmannin (WM) to specifically inhibit the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway11, this study aimed to clarify whether BSP regulates the proliferation and migration of HGFs through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, provide a theoretical basis for the development of new peri-implant healing agents, and promote the translational application of TCM in stomatology.

Materials and methods

Main materials, reagents, and instruments

(China). Medical-grade smooth TA1 titanium sheets (Ra = 1.6 ± 0.5 μm, diameter 10.0 mm, thickness 0.1 mm) were obtained from Haiyuan Scientific Research Metal (China), and PCR primers were custom synthesized by Anshengda (China). The key reagents used included a CCK-8 Assay Kit (Biosharp, China), high-glucose DMEM (Biosharp, USA), fetal bovine serum (Biosharp, USA), penicillin‒streptomycin–amphotericin B mixed solution (Solarbio, China), 4% paraformaldehyde fixative (Biosharp, China), an HE staining kit (Solarbio, China), a vimentin antibody (Cat. No.: GB11192-50), a cytokeratin 8 antibody (Cat. No.: GB150081-50), a fibronectin antibody, and a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (Wuhan Savel, China). The protein-related reagents used included PMSF, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Jiangsu KeyGen, China), RIPA lysis buffer, a BCA protein quantification kit (Solarbio, China), PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA), an SDS‒PAGE gel preparation kit (CWBIO, China), electrophoresis/transfer/TBST buffers (Wuhan Savel, China), quick blocking buffer (Yamei, China), and ECL reagent (Bio-Rad, USA). All the instruments were regularly calibrated to ensure data reliability.

Preparation of key materials

Titanium sheets were ultrasonically cleaned in 75% ethanol and distilled water for 15 min each (repeated three times), dried in a 60 °C oven, and sterilized under ultraviolet radiation for 30 min prior to the cell culture experiments. BSP was dissolved in complete medium (DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% double antibody) and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min to confirm complete dissolution. Based on solubility limits and preexperimental results, BSP concentrations of 0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1000 µg/mL were prepared for subsequent experiments.

Methods

Isolation and culture of HGFs

Primary human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were isolated from healthy attached gingival tissues of patients undergoing impacted third molar extraction (Ethics Approval No.: JNSKQYY-2023-005), trimmed to remove epithelial components, cut into small fragments, and cultured in primary medium at 37 °C with 5% CO₂; after 4 h of inverted incubation, the tissues were submerged in medium, which was refreshed every 3 days. When the cells reached 80% confluence, they were digested with trypsin, which was terminated with complete medium, after which the cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended and subcultured in new flasks; the medium was changed every 3 days. For cryopreservation, confluent cells were digested, centrifuged, resuspended in cryopreservation solution, labeled, and stored at − 80 °C; cryopreservation tubes were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath, disinfected, centrifuged, resuspended in complete medium, and inoculated into flasks, with experiments performed when the cells reached 80% confluence.

Cell identification

HE staining: HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 6-well plates to 80% confluence, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, sequentially stained with hematoxylin (10 min) and eosin (5 min), and observed under an optical microscope after rinsing with PBS.

Immunofluorescence staining: HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates to 80% confluence, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with serum for 30 min. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies (vimentin, cytokeratin, and fibronectin) at 4 °C overnight, then with a fluorescent secondary antibody for 2 h in the dark, stained with DAPI for 30 min, and observed under a fluorescence microscope after being rinsed and mounted.

Determination of the effect of BSP on HGF proliferation (CCK-8)

HGFs (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h and then treated with BSP (0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, or 1000 µg/mL; 6 replicates per group) for 24–72 h. After rinsing, 10% CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for 2 h in the dark; the absorbance at 450 nm was measured to determine the optimal BSP concentration.

Determination of the cytotoxicity of BSP to HGFs (live/dead staining)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates for 24 h, treated with 0/800 µg/mL BSP for 72 h, rinsed with assay buffer, and stained with calcein-AM (20–25 min) and PI (5 min) in the dark. The cells were subsequently centrifuged (450 g, 5 min), resuspended in PBS, and observed under a fluorescence microscope (490 ± 10 nm excitation) for live/dead cell identification.

Determination of the effect of BSP on HGF migration (scratch and transwell assays)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were subjected to scratch and Transwell assays to assess the effect of BSP on migration: for the scratch assay, cells were seeded in 24-well plates to full confluence in serum-free medium, scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip and then treated with 0, 100–800 µg/mL BSP (4 replicates per group) for 24–72 h. The migration rate was calculated as follows: [(initial area − area at time t)/initial area]×100%. For the Transwell assay, HGFs were seeded in serum-free upper Transwell chambers with lower chambers supplemented with 0, 100–800 µg/mL BSP, incubated for 36 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet; the upper layer cells were wiped off, and the migrated cells on the lower membrane were counted in 3 random fields under an optical microscope.

Determination of the effect of BSP on COLⅠ/FN secretion (ELISA)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h and treated with 0/100/800 µg/mL BSP for 24/72 h. The culture supernatant was collected and added to ELISA plates, followed by incubation with blocking solution, enzyme-labeled antibody and substrate solution. The reaction was terminated with stop solution, the OD value at 450 nm was measured, and the protein concentration was calculated via a standard curve.

Determination of the effect of BSP on ki67/PCNA mRNA expression (qRT–PCR)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h and treated with 0/100/800 µg/mL BSP for 24/72 h. Total RNA was extracted and quantified (purity 1.8–2.0) and then reverse transcribed (37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s). qRT‒PCR was performed with GAPDH as an internal reference (40 cycles: 95 °C for 3–10 s and 60 °C for 10–30 s), and relative mRNA expression was calculated via the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method (Table 1).

Determination of the effect of WM on HGF proliferation (CCK-8)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h and treated with WM (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, or 8 µM; 6 replicates per group) for 24–72 h. The CCK-8 assay was performed as described in 1.3.3, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Determination of the effect of WM cytotoxicity on HGFs (live/dead staining)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with 0/1 µM WM for 72 h. The live/dead staining assay was performed as described in 1.3.4, and the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Determination of the effect of WM on HGF migration (scratch assay)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates to full confluence (serum-free medium), scratched, and treated with 0/1 µM WM (4 replicates per group) for 24/72 h. The migration rate was calculated as described in 1.3.5.

Determination of the effect of BSP on p-PI3K/p-AKT expression with WM intervention (WB)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h and treated with 0/800 µg/mL BSP and 800 µg/mL BSP + 1 µM WM for 24/72 h. Total protein was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer, and the concentration was determined via the BCA method. Protein samples (20 µg) were denatured, separated by SDS‒PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with serum, and incubated sequentially with primary/secondary antibodies. The bands were developed and quantified for relative protein expression via ImageJ.

Determination of the effect of BSP on HGF proliferation with WM intervention (CCK-8 and scratch assay)

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h and treated with 0/800 µg/mL BSP/800 µg/mL BSP + 1 µM WM (6 replicates per group) for 24/72 h. The CCK-8 assay was performed as described in 1.3.3, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The scratch assay migration rate was calculated as described in 1.3.5.

Determination of the effect of BSP on HGF proliferation/migration on titanium surfaces

HGFs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded on titanium sheets in 24-well plates and treated with 0/100/800 µg/mL BSP for 24/72 h. The samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, critical point dried and gold-sputtered. The number, distribution and morphology of cells on the titanium surfaces were observed under an electron microscope, and the proliferation/migration ability was quantified via ImageJ.

Statistical methods

We used the inferential statistical methods provided by SPSS 23.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 for data analysis and performed the Shapiro‒Wilk test for normality. The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). For data that conformed to a normal distribution, the t test was used; for data that did not conform to a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. One-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among multiple groups. The confidence interval was set at 95%, and the statistical significance level was defined as α = 0.05.

Results

Isolation, culture and identification of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs)

Gingival tissue explants were cultured for 7 days after adherence, and a large number of spindle-shaped cells with a typical whirlpool-like distribution around the explants were observed under an inverted microscope (Fig. 1a), exhibiting the morphological characteristics of fibroblasts. The cells were further cultured to 90% confluence (achieved at 21 days, Fig. 1b) for trypsin digestion and subculture. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that the cells were elongated and spindle shaped, with multiple nucleoli in the nucleus and abundant cytoplasm with two or more cytoplasmic processes (Fig. 1c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the cells expressed fibronectin (nuclei blue, cytoplasm red; Fig. 1d) and vimentin (nuclei blue, cytoplasm red; Fig. 1e) positively, whereas cytokeratin 8 (CK8) staining was negative (nuclei blue, cytoplasm unstained; Fig. 1f). ImageJ-based quantitative analysis of positive cells revealed that the positive staining rates of fibronectin and vimentin were both ≥ 95% (Fig. 1g). In combination with the morphological, histological, and immunophenotypic characteristics and tissue anatomical origin, the isolated cells were identified as high-purity HGFs.

Isolation, culture, and identification of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs): a) culture on Day 7; b) culture on Day 21; c) hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining; d) positive immunostaining for fibronectin; e) positive immunostaining for vimentin; f) negative immunostaining for cytokeratin 8 (CK8).

Screening of the optimal concentration of BSP for regulating HGFs on titanium surfaces

The adhesion and proliferation of HGFs on titanium surfaces were detected by CCK-8 assays at 12 and 72 h after treatment with different concentrations of BSP (0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL) (Fig. 2). The absorbance values of the BSP-treated groups (100–800 µg/mL) were significantly greater than those of the control group (0 µg/mL) at all time points, indicating a concentration-dependent promotion effect. The 800 µg/mL BSP group exhibited the most significant increases in HGF adhesion and proliferation (***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 vs. control). However, compared with the 800 µg/mL group, the 1000 µg/mL BSP group showed a slight decrease in cell activity, indicating a mild inhibitory effect at high concentrations. Therefore, 800 µg/mL BSP was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. All the data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3 biological replicates, 6 technical replicates per group).

Comparison of the adhesion of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) on titanium surfaces following treatment with varying concentrations of BSP and incubation for different time periods. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

Cytotoxicity of BSP on HGFs on titanium surfaces

Live/dead staining was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of 800 µg/mL BSP on HGFs after 72 h of culture, with 75% ethanol used as the positive control (cell death group) and complete medium used as the negative control (normal growth group). The negative control group showed nearly 100% cell survival, with green fluorescent live cells as the main population (Fig. 3a). The 800 µg/mL BSP group also had a cell survival rate close to 100%, and the cell density and green fluorescence intensity were significantly greater than those of the negative control group (Fig. 3b); ImageJ quantitative analysis confirmed that the fluorescence intensity of the BSP group was 1.82-fold greater than that of the control group. The positive control group treated with 75% ethanol for 10 min showed almost 0% cell survival, with only red fluorescent dead cells observed (Fig. 3c). The results of the CCK-8 assay were consistent with the live/dead staining results, confirming that 800 µg/mL BSP had no cytotoxic effect on HGFs and promoted cell proliferation. All the data were analyzed by Student’s t test and are expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates).

Cytotoxicity of BSP: a) negative control group; b) experimental group; c) positive control group.

Effect of BSP on the migration of HGFs on titanium surfaces (scratch and transwell assays)

Scratch and Transwell assays were combined to evaluate the migratory capacity of HGFs on titanium surfaces after treatment with 100 and 800 µg/mL BSP, with serum-free medium as the control group; for the scratch assay, the scratch area was measured at 0, 24 and 72 h after scratching, the initial scratch boundaries were marked with yellow lines for intuitive comparison (Fig. 4a), statistical analysis revealed that the scratch areas of BSP-treated groups were significantly smaller than those of the control group at both 24 and 72 h (****P < 0.0001), and the migratory effect of 800 µg/mL BSP was more pronounced than that of 100 µg/mL BSP, at 24 h, cells in the control group presented a round morphology with shortened cytoplasmic processes, whereas cells in the BSP groups showed an elongated shape with directional migration toward the scratch area; after 72 h of BSP treatment, the scratch area in the 800 µg/mL BSP group was almost completely filled by migrated cells with nearly closed gaps, whereas approximately 80% of the scratch area in the control group remained unoccupied (Fig. 4a). Quantitative analysis of migration rate showed that the migration rate of the 800 µg/mL BSP group at 72 h reached (92.36 ± 3.15)%, which was significantly higher than the rate of (21.58 ± 2.87)% achieved by the control group (Fig. 4b), indicating that BSP could promote the migration of HGFs. However, the potential interference of cell proliferation on the scratch assay results needed to be excluded. To further verify the migratory effect of BSP on HGFs and eliminate the interference of cell proliferation, a Transwell migration assay was performed. The number of cells migrating to the lower chamber of the Transwell insert was counted after 36 h of culture (Fig. 4c), and the results showed that the number of migrated cells in the BSP groups was significantly higher than that in the control group (**P < 0.01), with the migratory-promoting effect of 800 µg/mL BSP superior to that of 100 µg/mL BSP (Fig. 4d). Collectively, the combined results of the scratch and Transwell assays confirmed that BSP could significantly promote the migration of HGFs on titanium surfaces in a concentration-dependent manner, with 800 µg/mL BSP as the optimal concentration for enhancing HGF migration. All data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc test, expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates, 4 technical replicates per group for the scratch assay; n = 3 biological replicates, 3 technical replicates per group for the Transwell assay).

a and b) Results of the scratch test evaluating the effect of BSP on HGF migration on a titanium surface, along with a quantitative comparison. Compared with that in the control group, the wound area in the BSP-treated group was markedly reduced (scale bar: 200 μm). c and d) The Transwell migration assay further demonstrated that BSP significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of HGFs (scale bar: 400 μm). **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Effect of BSP on the expression of extracellular matrix-related proteins (COLI, FN) in HGFs on titanium surfaces (ELISA)

ELISA was performed to detect the protein secretion levels of COLⅠ and FN in HGFs on titanium surfaces after 24 and 72 h of culture with 100 and 800 µg/mL BSP (Fig. 5). ELISA revealed that the secretion levels of COLⅠ and FN in the culture supernatant of the 800 µg/mL BSP group were significantly greater than those in the control group at 72 h, with the concentrations of COLⅠ and FN being 1.21- and 1.65-fold greater than those in the control group, respectively. The ELISA results were calculated based on the standard curve, and all the data were analyzed by Student’s t test and are expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates, 3 technical replicates per group).

Quantification of COLI and fibronectin (FN) protein expression levels by ELISA. Statistical significance: **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Effect of BSP on the mRNA expression of proliferation-related genes (Ki67, PCNA) in HGFs on titanium surfaces (qRT‒PCR)

qRT‒PCR was used to detect the mRNA expression levels of Ki67 and PCNA in HGFs on titanium surfaces after 24 and 72 h of culture with 800 µg/mL BSP, with GAPDH as the internal reference, and the relative expression level was calculated by the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method (Fig. 6). Melt curve analysis revealed single peaks for all genes, confirming the specificity of amplification. The mRNA expression levels of Ki67 and PCNA in the BSP group were significantly upregulated compared with those in the control group at both time points (****P < 0.0001). At 24 h, the relative expression levels of Ki67 and PCNA in the 800 µg/mL BSP group were 2.23- and 1.68-fold greater than those in the control group, respectively; at 72 h, the relative expression levels were 4.26- and 2.72-fold greater than those in the control group, respectively, indicating a time-dependent upregulation trend. All the data were analyzed by Student’s t test and are expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates; 3 technical replicates per group).

Analysis of the expression levels of the PCNA and Ki67 genes in human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR). **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Screening of the optimal concentration of the PI3K/AKT inhibitor wortmannin (WM) for HGFs

Effects of different WM concentrations on HGF proliferation (CCK-8 and live/dead staining)

A CCK-8 assay was used to determine the effects of different concentrations of WM (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 µM) on HGF proliferation at 24 and 72 h (Fig. 7a). At 24 h, there was no significant difference in cell activity between the 0 and 2 µM WM groups and the control group (P > 0.05), whereas the 4 µM and 8 µM groups showed significant decreases in activity (**P < 0.01). At 72 h, the 0–1 µM WM treatments had no significant effect on cell proliferation (P > 0.05), whereas the 2–8 µM treatments significantly inhibited cell proliferation (**P < 0.01). Live/dead staining revealed that after 72 h of treatment with 1 µM WM, the cell survival rate was 100%, with only green fluorescent live cells observed and no red fluorescent dead cells observed (Fig. 7b). Therefore, 1 µM WM was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent pathway inhibition experiments, as it had no significant effect on HGF proliferation or viability.

Screening of the optimal WM concentration for HGFs. (a) CCK-8 analysis of the effects of different WM concentrations on HGF proliferation at 24 and 72 h. (b) Live/dead staining of HGFs treated with 1 µM WM for 72 h (scale bar: 500 μm). (c) Scratch assay images of the control and 1 µM WM groups at 72 h (scale bar: 200 μm). (d) Quantitative analysis of the migration rate. **P < 0.01 vs. control; ns: no significant difference (Student’s t test; n = 3).

Effect of 1 µM WM on HGF migration (scratch assay)

Scratch assays showed that after 72 h of culture with 1 µM WM, the scratch area and migration rate did not significantly differ between the WM and control groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 7c and d). The cells in both groups were mostly round with shortened cytoplasmic processes, and the scratch area was not covered by cells, with loose intercellular connections. These findings confirmed that 1 µM WM had no significant effect on HGF migration. All the data were analyzed by Student’s t test and are expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates).

BSP promotes the proliferation and migration of HGFs on titanium surfaces via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (WB)

WB was used to detect the expression levels of p-PI3K, PI3K, p-AKT and AKT in HGFs on titanium surfaces after 24 and 72 h of culture with 800 µg/mL BSP alone or in combination with 1 µM WM (Fig. 8). The p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios were used to evaluate the phosphorylation level of the PI3K/AKT pathway. At both 24 and 72 h, the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in the BSP group were significantly greater than those in the control group. The phosphorylation levels in the BSP + WM group were significantly lower than those in the BSP group and were close to those in the control group. These findings indicated that BSP could significantly activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in HGFs and that this activation could be inhibited by WM. All the data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with the LSD post hoc test and are expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3 biological replicates).

Effect of BSP on the phosphorylation level of PI3K/AKT in HGFs on titanium surfaces with or without WM intervention (WB).

WM attenuates the migration-promoting effect of BSP on HGFs (scratch assay)

Scratch assays revealed that at 24 and 72 h, the migration rate of the BSP group was significantly greater than that of the control group, whereas the migration rate of the BSP + WM group was significantly lower than that of the BSP group. The migration rate of the BSP + WM group was significantly greater than that of the control group at both time points, indicating that WM only partially inhibited the migration-promoting effect of BSP, suggesting that BSP may regulate HGF migration through multiple pathways and that the PI3K/AKT pathway is one of the important pathways involved. All the data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with the LSD post hoc test and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3 biological replicates, 4 technical replicates per group) (Fig. 9a and b).

Effects of WM on the proliferation- and migration-promoting effects of BSP on HGFs on titanium surfaces (scratch assay and CCK-8). (a) Representative images at 24 and 72 h (scale bar: 200 μm). (b) Quantitative analysis of the migration rate. (c) CCK-8 analysis of HGF proliferation. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 (one-way ANOVA with the LSD post hoc test; n = 3).

WM attenuates the proliferation-promoting effect of BSP on HGFs (CCK-8)

CCK-8 assays revealed that at 24 and 72 h, the cell activity of the BSP group was significantly greater than that of the control group (***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001), whereas the cell activity of the BSP + WM group was significantly lower than that of the BSP group (***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, Fig. 9c). At 24 h, there was no significant difference in cell activity between the BSP + WM and control groups (P > 0.05); at 72 h, the cell activity of the BSP + WM group was slightly greater than that of the control group (P < 0.05) but significantly lower than that of the BSP group. These findings indicated that WM could significantly attenuate the proliferation-promoting effect of BSP on HGFs, suggesting that the PI3K/AKT pathway is one of the key pathways through which BSP promotes HGF proliferation. All the data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with the LSD post hoc test and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3 biological replicates, 6 technical replicates per group).

Morphological observation of HGFs on titanium surfaces (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the morphological characteristics, adhesion state and spreading degree of HGFs on the titanium surfaces after 24 and 72 h of culture with 800 µg/mL BSP. All SEM images are provided with scale bars, and the images are arranged vertically by time points and horizontally by groups for clear comparison. At 24 h, the cells in both the control and BSP groups were contracted and aggregated, with small adhesion areas and no pseudopod extension (Fig. 10a and b). At 72 h, the cells in both groups were further extended into an irregular polygonal shape with more cytoplasmic processes and pseudopodia; compared with those in the control group, the cells in the BSP group had a more extensive spreading area, more abundant pseudopodia and more obvious intercellular connections (Fig. 10c and d). The SEM results confirmed that BSP could significantly improve the adhesion and spreading of HGFs on titanium surfaces, which was consistent with the above functional experimental results.

Discussion

The peri-implant soft tissue seal consists of a coronal junctional epithelium forming an epithelial cuff and an apical connective tissue, with a height of approximately 3–4 mm. It functions to resist external stimuli and isolate the oral environment from the underlying bone tissue12. The junctional epithelium attaches to the implant via hemidesmosomes and defends against external stimuli through physical barriers, immune surveillance, and proinflammatory responses13. However, the junctional epithelium can migrate apically, and bacteria can invade apically accordingly. The connective tissue supports the overlying junctional epithelium, prevents its apical migration, maintains the gingival structure around the implant neck, and protects the underlying bone tissue, thereby sustaining peri-implant osseointegration14.

There are differences between the peri-implant gingival structure and that of natural teeth. The connective tissue layer of the peri-implant gingiva has a low fibroblast content, lacks a blood supply derived from the periodontal ligament, and the implant surface has no cementum. Collagen fibers run parallel to the implant and lack vertical Sharpey fibers15. Microleakage at the abutment‒crown interface and micromotion caused by functional loading render the peri-implant gingival structure susceptible to damage, which ultimately affects implant osseointegration and leads to the occurrence of peri-implant diseases16,17. Alveolar bone is vulnerable to bacterial invasion in periodontal tissues. Located apical to the peri-implant soft tissue seal, alveolar bone has a low cellular component and insufficient blood supply, resulting in weak cellular and humoral immune capabilities18,19. Once bacteria break through the peri-implant soft tissue seal to the alveolar crest, they are prone to inducing alveolar bone resorption with a poor prognosis. Therefore, the connective tissue is crucial for protecting the underlying alveolar bone and supporting the overlying junctional epithelium20, and fibroblasts play a key role in connective tissue repair21.

Currently, the commonly used mouthwashes after implant surgery are chlorhexidine and Kangfuxin solution. Chlorhexidine has antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects22 but is associated with adverse effects such as taste disturbance, tooth discoloration, and cytotoxicity and can cause severe side effects such as IgE-mediated urticaria/anaphylactic shock23–26. Kangfuxin solution is an ethanol extract preparation of Periplaneta americana and has wound-healing-promoting effects; however, some patients have psychological aversions to cockroaches and ethanol, as well as gastrointestinal discomfort and allergic reactions27. Therefore, the development of safe, convenient, and effective oral preparations based on traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has become important.

Human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) are the primary cellular component of gingival connective tissue and maintain gingival tissue homeostasis through proliferation and migration4,28. After dental implant surgery, HGFs rapidly transition from a quiescent state to an activated state. Through proliferation and migration, they promote the restoration of gingival morphology and function, achieve stable integration with the implant, form a peri-implant soft tissue seal, and protect the implant from the oral environment29. Therefore, the activity of fibroblasts at the implant‒gingiva interface plays a crucial role in the formation of the peri-implant soft tissue seal.

The formation of the peri-implant soft tissue seal is influenced by multiple comprehensive factors30. This study aims to identify a safe and convenient method to increase the activity of human gingival fibroblasts for potential clinical application. As a treasure of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), Chinese herbal medicines embody profound cultural heritage and medical wisdom and have garnered increasing attention in recent years. Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP), a bioactive component derived from the TCM herb Bletilla striata, possesses stable physicochemical properties, excellent biological activity, outstanding biocompatibility, and various pharmacological effects, such as anti-inflammatory, hemostatic, antibacterial, and cell proliferation-promoting effects31. In this study, BSP was selected as the active ingredient to treat human gingival fibroblasts, and it promoted the proliferation and migration of HGFs.

Studies have shown that BSP affects the proliferation and migration of various cell types. Specifically, 80 µg/mL BSP can promote the proliferation and migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells32; 100 µg/mL BSP enhances the proliferation and migration of human tenocytes33; 5 µg/mL BSP promotes the proliferation and migration of mouse fibroblasts, whereas 200 µg/mL BSP inhibits these processes34, and wrapping 3 mg BSP around autologous jugular vein grafts in rats inhibits vascular endothelial cell proliferation and effectively prevents vein graft restenosis35. In the present study, 100 µg/mL BSP promoted the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts, with 800 µg/mL identified as the optimal concentration. However, the proliferation-promoting effect decreased at 1000 µg/mL, possibly due to the solubility limitations of BSP, as insoluble components inhibit cell proliferation. It is speculated that further increases in BSP concentration will lead to inhibitory effects on the proliferation of human gingival fibroblasts.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway regulates biological processes such as cell proliferation and migration. PI3K plays central roles in cell proliferation and migration, and AKT, as a key downstream effector of PI3K, is involved in cell growth and cell cycle regulation36. As a bioactive natural product, BSP exhibits complex concentration-dependent effects on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. BSP accelerates the proliferation and migration of human tenocytes by promoting AKT phosphorylation33. In this study, BSP activated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts, with the most significant activation occurring at 800 µg/mL BSP. It is speculated that further increases in concentration will result in the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by BSP. This study confirms that BSP promotes the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts by regulating the activity of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, but this mechanism involves indirect regulation. The direct drug targets of BSP and the detailed mechanisms underlying its interaction with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway remain to be further explored. In summary, although the mechanism by which BSP promotes the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts has been initially clarified, further research is needed to explore its direct targets and interactions with other signaling pathways to fully understand the biological effects and potential clinical application value of BSP.

This study revealed that BSP promotes the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts on titanium surfaces and increases the secretion of type I collagen (COLI) and fibronectin (FN). As the main component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), type I collagen accounts for approximately 70% of the total ECM mass and provides a stable scaffold for cell growth37. Fibronectin is an adhesion molecule in the ECM that tightly connects the collagen fiber network to cytoskeletal microfilaments through transmembrane receptors such as integrins, thereby strengthening the interaction between cells and the ECM. This tight connection is crucial for maintaining normal cell function and morphology38. During the healing process of peri-implant soft tissues, collagen and fibronectin play vital roles in promoting the attachment of HGFs to the implant abutment surface, accelerating ECM remodeling, and laying a solid foundation for the formation of the peri-implant soft tissue seal39.

Exploring ways to promote the proliferation and migration of HGFs on titanium surfaces not only relies on the direct regulation of HGF biological behavior but also involves the optimization of the physicochemical properties of the titanium surface. Surface characteristics such as the roughness, chemical composition, and hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity of titanium have been confirmed to significantly affect HGF behavior40,41. These surface properties influence the healing process of the implant–soft tissue interface by regulating cell responses. In clinical practice, the integration between the implant neck or abutment and HGFs is an extremely complex biological process. This process is not only restricted by the material properties of the implant and the biological characteristics of the HGFs themselves but also involves intricate interactions between surrounding cells (such as osteoblasts and immune cells) and cytokines (such as growth factors and inflammatory mediators)42.

This study has several limitations. All the experiments were conducted in an in vitro coculture system of human gingival fibroblasts and smooth titanium sheets, which cannot fully simulate the complex in vivo peri-implant microenvironment. Only one optimal concentration of BSP was used, and the observation time points were relatively short. In addition, this study only preliminarily verified the role of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway; other potential mechanisms and direct molecular targets remain unclear. Furthermore, only smooth titanium surfaces were investigated, and no in vivo animal experiments or clinical trials were performed. Therefore, further in-depth studies are needed to verify the long-term efficacy, biosafety, and clinical translational value of BSP.

Conclusions

This study revealed that Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP), a natural component of traditional Chinese medicine, can promote the proliferation and migration of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. It also exerts similar proproliferative and promigratory effects on HGFs cultured on smooth titanium surfaces. BSP holds promising potential as a clinical therapeutic agent for enhancing peri-implant soft tissue sealing. However, the current research is limited to in vitro cellular experiments, and further in-depth studies and clinical validations are needed before its clinical application.
