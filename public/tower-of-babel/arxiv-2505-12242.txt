ZenFlow: Enabling Stall-Free Offloading for LLM Training
Tingfeng Lan, Yusen Wu, Bin Ma, Zhaoyuan Su, Rui Yang, Tekin Bicer, Masahiro Tanaka, Olatunji Ruwase, Dong Li, Yue Cheng

Abstract
. Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) often exceeds GPU memory limits, prompting systems to offload model states to CPU memory. However, existing offloaded training frameworks like ZeRO-Offload treat all parameters equally and update the full model on the CPU, causing severe GPU stalls, where fast, expensive GPUs sit idle, waiting for slow CPU updates and limited-bandwidth PCIe transfers. We present ZenFlow , a new offloaded training framework that decouples updates between GPU and CPU by prioritizing important gradients. ZenFlow performs in-place updates of important gradients on GPU while offloading and accumulating less important ones on CPU without blocking training iterations, fully overlapping CPU work with GPU computation. To make this importance-aware design scalable, ZenFlow leverages a novel spatio-temporal locality of important gradients—concentrated in specific positions that remain stable across iterations—to make important gradient identification efficient and lightweight while preserving fidelity. Evaluation shows that ZenFlow achieves up to 5 × \times end-to-end speedup, 2 × \times lower PCIe traffic, 85% less GPU stalls, and up to 72% lower cloud GPU cost, all while preserving accuracy.

1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have become the foundation of modern natural language processing applications, powering tasks from text generation to code synthesis
(
Radford et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2021
;
Adiwardana et al., 2020
;
Karpukhin et al., 2020
)
. While pretrained models
(
Radford et al., 2019
;
Brown et al., 2020
;
Touvron et al., 2023a
;
Touvron et al., 2023b
)
offer general-purpose capabilities, fine-tuning them on domain-specific data is often essential for achieving high performance on downstream tasks.
However, as LLM sizes grow,
the memory demands of fine-tuning far exceed the capacity of a single GPU, making large-scale training increasingly challenging
(
Cottier et al., 2025
;
Shen et al., 2023
;
Bai et al., 2024
)
.

To address this memory bottleneck, offloading-based training systems, such as ZeRO-Offload
(
Ren et al., 2021b
)
, ZeRO-Infinity
(
Rajbhandari et al., 2021
)
, and StrongHold
(
Sun et al., 2022
)
, have emerged as promising solutions. These systems reduce GPU memory consumption by offloading model states (e.g., gradients and optimizer states) to CPU memory or NVMe storage and performing optimizer updates on the CPU. However, the CPU-GPU performance gap has widened rapidly (see Fig.
1
), increasing from 28 TFLOPS to 2248 TFLOPS in the last couple of years,
so CPU-side optimizer updates and PCIe transfers are largely exposed to the critical path, causing long GPU stalls (Fig.
2
).
For example, on fine-tuning a Llama-2-7B
(
Touvron et al., 2023b
)
model with
4\times
A100 GPUs, one training step time experiences a dramatic increase from 0.5s to 7s when enabling offloading, a 14
\times
slowdown.

We identify two major sources of inefficiency in existing offloaded training systems:
(1)
Long stalls caused by slow CPU-side updates
, which delay the next training iteration and leave GPUs idle.
(2)
High I/O cost
, as each iteration requires transferring the full set of gradients and updated parameters between GPU and CPU.

These performance bottlenecks stem from critical limitations of current offloading systems:
(1) They adopt a uniform strategy that treats all parameters and gradients as equally important, regardless of how much each individual gradient actually contributes to learning.
(2) They rely on slow CPU with limited computation and PCIe bandwidth for updating and transferring entire model states.

In practice, this causes slow CPU to bottleneck fast and more expensive GPU. As shown in Fig.
2
, during three consecutive training steps, ZeRO-Offload suffers from repeated GPU stalls—each lasting up to 5 seconds within a 7-second step—where GPU utilization drops to nearly 0%. These prolonged idle periods dominate the training time, resulting in severe underutilization of GPU resources.

However, our analysis and former study
(
Aji and Heafield, 2017
;
Aghajanyan et al., 2020
;
Lin et al., 2017
;
Jang et al., 2024
)
reveal that
LLM gradients are highly imbalanced
: the top 1% of gradients account for over 90% of the total gradient norm. Consequently, existing hardware-agnostic treatment of gradients leads to wasted computation and communication on low-impact updates, stalling overall training throughput.

We introduce
ZenFlow
, the first offloaded training system that decouples parameter updates based on both hardware heterogeneity and learning dynamics (e.g., the gradient importance).
The key insight behind
ZenFlow
is to
treat important and unimportant updates differently
: by retaining the small but critical subset of gradients on GPU for immediate updates,
ZenFlow
avoids GPU stalls and ensures
fast
updates on important parameters. Meanwhile, remaining less important gradients are offloaded to the
slower
CPU, where they are slowly
accumulated and updated. This strategy preserves the learning contribution of less important gradients while avoiding frequent update and I/O cost. By amortizing CPU-side updates over multiple GPU iterations,
ZenFlow
effectively combines the high speed of GPU with the cost efficiency of CPU. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, this design enables
ZenFlow
to eliminate repeated GPU stalls seen in ZeRO-Offload, resulting in consistently high GPU utilization and significantly improved end-to-end efficiency.

A key challenge in realizing
ZenFlow
is determining which parameters are important enough to be updated immediately on GPUs. This becomes particularly difficult in distributed training settings such as ZeRO
(
Rajbhandari et al., 2020
)
,
where each GPU holds a shard of the full model and its corresponding gradients. Performing an
AllGather

to collect the full gradient matrix across GPUs would incur prohibitive communication and memory cost.
To address this,
ZenFlow
leverages a novel observation:

to our best knowledge, we are the first to identify that important gradients in LLM fine-tuning exhibit strong spatial and temporal locality.

Specifically, we find that a small subset of input dimensions (i.e., channels) consistently carries high-magnitude gradients across training steps. By tracking and reusing this compact set of important channels,
ZenFlow
enables efficient, scalable importance-aware training without expensive global synchronization.

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We discover and characterize a novel spatio-temporal locality property of important gradients in LLM fine-tuning.

•

We design a lightweight method to identify important gradients in distributed training without costly global synchronization.

•

We build
ZenFlow
, a fine-grained CPU-GPU pipeline that decouples parameter updates with different importance to minimize GPU stalls and I/O overhead.

•

We build
ZenFlow
on DeepSpeed
(
Rasley et al., 2020
)
and evaluate it thoroughly across single-GPU and multi-GPU settings.
ZenFlow
achieves 3.6-5
\times
end-to-end speedup compared to state-of-the-art offloading systems while maintaining accuracy across diverse LLMs and fine-tuning tasks.

Open-source release.

ZenFlow
has been integrated into and is open-sourced at

deepspeedai/DeepSpeed
.

Background and Motivation

2.1.
Distributed Training Systems

Basics of Distributed Training.

Deep learning model training typically consists of millions of iterations performed across multiple training epochs
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Touvron et al., 2023a
;
Touvron et al., 2023b
)
. Each iteration mainly involves three stages: forward propagation (
FP
), backward propagation (
BP
), and parameter update (
UP
). In the
FP
stage, a batch of training data is passed through the model to compute the output and loss based on an objective function. In the
BP
stage, the model propagates the loss value reversely through model layers to compute gradients for each model parameter. Finally, in the
UP
stage, model parameters are updated using the computed gradients through an optimization algorithm by the optimizer (e.g., SGD
(
Bottou, 2010
)
, Adam
(
Kingma and Ba, 2017
)
).

Distributed Training.

To train large models efficiently, distributed parallelism is widely adopted. When models fit in GPU memory, data parallelism
(
Li et al., 2014
)
is commonly used, replicating the model across devices and sharding/distributing input batches. For larger models that exceed memory capacity, model parallelism
(
Shoeybi et al., 2019
)
and pipeline parallelism
(
Huang et al., 2019
;
Narayanan et al., 2019
)
partition model layers across devices to utilize aggregate memory.
Furthermore, ZeRO
(
Rasley et al., 2020
)
extends data parallelism by sharding model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across devices. ZeRO can be regarded as a hybrid of data and model parallelism: each device processes a full batch in data parallelism style while applying model-parallelism-like partitioning to memory-intensive states. This design enables training models far beyond single-device memory limits and has become foundational in modern systems such as DeepSpeed
(
Rasley et al., 2020
)
, Megatron-LM
(
Shoeybi et al., 2019
)
, and PyTorch FSDP
(
Zhao et al., 2023
)
.

2.2.
Memory-Efficient Training

Training memory is dominated by persistent states (i.e., parameters, gradients, and optimizer states), while activations are short-lived. In
BF16
/
FP16
, if parameters take
M
, gradients add another
M
, and optimizer states add
2M
, approximately
4M
in total (see Table
2
). For Llama2-7B, that is 14GB each for parameters and gradients, and 28GB for optimizer states—totaling 56GB, which easily exceeds the memory limit of an A100 40GB GPU.

Offloaded Training.
To mitigate the GPU memory bottleneck, some offloading techniques
(
Ren et al., 2021b
;
Rajbhandari et al., 2021
;
Huang et al., 2020
;
Ren et al., 2021a
)
have been proposed to transfer model parameters and optimizer states from the GPU memory to other storage, such as CPU DRAM or NVMe storage. Those methods reduce the GPU memory consumption and enable “out-of-core” training of larger models. ZeRO-Offload
(
Ren et al., 2021b
)
in the DeepSpeed ZeRO series
(
Ren et al., 2021b
;
Rajbhandari et al., 2020
;
Rajbhandari et al., 2021
)
, is one of those state-of-the-art systems.

Fig.
3
(a) illustrates the workflow of ZeRO-Offload. In each training iteration (
{Step}_{I}
), the forward pass (
{FP}_{I}
) and backward pass (
{BP}_{I}
) are executed on the GPU. During the backward pass, the gradients are computed and then transferred from GPU to CPU memory, where the CPU performs the parameter update (
{UP}_{I}
). Once the update is complete, the updated parameters
\Delta W_{I}
are fetched back to the GPU for the forward pass in the next training iteration. During the
UP
stage (the parameter updating), the GPU is idle, waiting for the updated parameters to be fetched from the CPU.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT).
As summarized in Table
1
, PEFT methods reduce training cost by updating only a small subset of parameters instead of all parameters. Typical PEFT approaches include adapters
(
He et al., 2022
)
, prefix-tuning
(
Li and Liang, 2021
)
, LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
;
Dettmers et al., 2023
)
, and GaLore
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
. Among them, LoRA is the most widely used: it trains low-rank adapters while freezing the original weights, updating less than 1% of parameters. This design enables low memory usage and fast training. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of representational fidelity. PEFT methods are structurally incapable of full-parameter adaptation, which can lead to suboptimal convergence and fail to capture the deep weight shifts required for domain specialization
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Liao et al., 2023
;
Coleman et al., 2025
;
Dai et al., 2024
;
Patel, 2025
)
. In this paper, we target full fine-tuning to maximize generality. Nevertheless, ZenFlow remains compatible with PEFT methods and can be combined when parameter efficiency is required.

2.3.
Problems and Insights

Problem: Fast GPU execution is frequently stalled by slow CPU-side update.
To understand the performance bottleneck of offloading-based fine-tuning, we break down per-iteration time when fine-tuning Qwen2.5 and Llama2 model series with various sizes (see Fig.
5
). We use ZeRO-Offload with fully parallelized
CPUAdam
optimizer.

Each iteration is broken down into four stages: forward pass (
FP
), backward pass (
BP
), gradient offloading (
GO
), and parameter update (
UP
).
With ZeRO-Offload, the forward and backward passes run on the GPU, the gradients are transferred to the CPU, and the update phase is computed entirely on the CPU with the
CPUAdam
optimizer.
Despite parallelizing the
CPUAdam
optimizer across 128 CPU threads, the CPU-side update is a significant bottleneck (see Fig.
5
). For example, when fine-tuning a Llama2-7B model, the update stage on the CPU takes
\sim
4,600
ms
—over twice as long as the backward pass time of 2,000
ms
, which leads to GPU idling, as the GPU must wait for the CPU to complete its update computation before proceeding to the next iteration.

To improve GPU utilization, one natural solution from StrongHold
(
Sun et al., 2022
)
is to overlap CPU updates with GPU computation using layer-wise scheduling
(Fig.
3
(b)). In this strategy, each layer’s gradients are offloaded and updated sequentially during the backward pass, allowing the CPU to begin updating earlier layers while the GPU continues computing later ones. This pipelined execution enables partial overlap between CPU-side
updates and the GPU-side backward computation. For example, as soon as the gradient for layer
l_{n}
is computed, it can be offloaded and the corresponding update initiated on the CPU, rather than waiting for the full backward pass (for all remaining layers from
l_{n-1}
to
l_{0}
) to complete.
This allows updated parameters to be uploaded back to the GPU earlier, reducing GPU idle time.

Although layer-wise scheduling can partially overlap CPU and GPU tasks, the CPU update phase is too long to be fully hidden (4,600
ms
on the CPU vs. 2,000
ms
on the GPU).

As a result,
GPU execution is frequently stalled, waiting for the CPU update even with aggressive multi-threaded optimization and optimal overlap.

Furthermore, parameter upload introduces additional stalls. While gradient offloading overlaps naturally with the backward pass (since gradients can be streamed asynchronously to CPU), the next iteration must wait for updated parameters to return from CPU via PCIe. This adds additional 500
ms
stall per iteration as shown in Fig.
3
(c).

Next, we present insights to address the core limitation of existing offloaded training systems.

Insight #1: Gradients/parameters with different importance should be treated differently to decouple GPU/ CPU execution and reduce communication.

It has been
observed that the gradients of different parameters have different importance in deep neural network training
(
Aji and Heafield, 2017
;
Ström, 2015
;
Lin et al., 2017
;
Dryden et al., 2016
)
. For example, Fig.
5
shows that top 1% of gradients account for
\sim
90% of the gradient norm. However, the current offloading techniques
overlook
this difference in gradients, treating them equally and offloading all of them to the CPU regardless of their impacts. This uniform treatment introduces unnecessary inefficiencies.
Take Llama2-7B as an example. An important subset of its parameters could be updated and transferred in
\sim
50
ms
, but under the uniform scheme they stall until the full CPU update (4,600
ms
) and parameter transfer (500
ms
) complete. In other words, the few important parameters that drive learning are forced to wait through
\sim
5
s
of unnecessary work dominated by unimportant ones.

This observation leads to a key research question:
Can we decouple important and less important gradients, and assign them to different hardware resources accordingly?
To mitigate unnecessary stalls, we propose updating important gradients directly on the GPU—leveraging its high computing bandwidth—while offloading and accumulating the remaining less important gradients on the CPU. This design relaxes the tight coupling between GPU execution and the full CPU update cycle, allowing the GPU to proceed without waiting on low-priority updates. The CPU-side accumulation proceeds
concurrently
and is typically fast enough (e.g.,
\sim
500
ms
) to be hidden within the backward pass, thereby avoiding additional delay and improving GPU utilization.

Challenge: Full gradient view is expensive in fully sharded distributed training.

To leverage gradient importance during training, selecting important gradients correctly and efficiently is a critical step.

A simple yet effective approach is top-
k
selection, which retains the gradients with the highest magnitudes. This strategy has been widely studied and applied in prior work
(
Aji and Heafield, 2017
;
Fang et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2025
;
Jang et al., 2024
)
. However, the top-
k
selection assumes access to the full set of gradients—what we refer to as a
global gradient view
.

In fully sharded distributed training, this assumption breaks. Each GPU holds only a fraction of the model parameters and computes gradients for its local shard, making global top-
k
selection
expensive
, because constructing a fully global view would require gathering and synchronizing gradients across all devices, incurring significant communication overhead and causing peak memory usage spikes due to gathered full gradient matrix.
Therefore, directly applying existing global top-
k
selection is not practical.

Insight #2: Important LLM gradients show both spatial and temporal locality.
To address the challenge posed by the lack of a global gradient view in fully sharded distributed training, we next investigate an important research question:
Do important gradients in LLM fine-tuning exhibit spatial or temporal locality that we can exploit to approximate global top-
k
selection more efficiently?

To answer this, we analyze the gradient importance distribution during fine-tuning. As a representative case, we use Qwen2.5-0.5B on the
Alpaca52K
dataset to illustrate. We also evaluate whether this locality generalizes across model architectures and optimizers in
§

6.6
.

First, we observe clear
spatial locality
in the gradient distribution. As shown in Fig.
6
(a), the top 1% of gradients (highlighted in orange) are not uniformly distributed but instead concentrated in a narrow subset of columns, each corresponding to a specific input channel. This pattern persists across iterations
(see Fig.
6
(b) and discussed next).

This result suggests that a small set of input features consistently receive large updates during fine-tuning.
This is because transformer activations are often localized across channels during inference
(
Jaszczur et al., 2021
;
Shen et al., 2020
)
, and such patterns propagate backward to the gradients.

Based on this, instead of performing the expensive global top-
k
selection over the entire parameter matrix,
we can approximate gradient importance by identifying and tracking a small set of important input channels—an approach significantly cheaper in both computation and communication.

Second, we find that these important input channels also exhibit
temporal locality
. As shown in Fig.
6
(b), the top 1% of important input-channel indices remain stable across iterations, forming persistent horizontal bands over time. This indicates that the same small subset of input channels consistently receives large updates during fine-tuning: these channels contribute more to the learning process than others, and their importance remains stable over time.
While occasional deviations occur—likely due to exploration of alternative subspaces—the overall importance remains stable.
Empirically, the top-1% channels exhibit a retention rate above 90%, while the retention rate approaches 100% under a looser threshold (e.g., top-5%). Therefore, we can efficiently track important gradients by maintaining a stable set of channels.

This spatio-temporal locality enables a lightweight approximation to global top-
k
selection: rather than recomputing gradient importance every iteration, we can approximate important gradients efficiently using
a slowly-updated set of important channels.
This drastically reduces communication and synchronization overhead, while preserving high fidelity in identifying important updates.

3.
ZenFlow
Design

The challenges and insights discussed in
§

2
motivate
ZenFlow
, a system that leverages gradient importance and gradients’ spatio-temporal locality to mitigate the I/O bottleneck and GPU stalls in offloading training.
ZenFlow
decouples gradient updates across heterogeneous hardware and minimizes communication overhead without sacrificing accuracy.
This section presents the design of
ZenFlow
with the following goals:

\bullet

Goal #1.
Minimize GPU stalls caused by slow CPU (i.e., I/O and computation overhead associated with unimportant gradients and parameters).

\bullet

Goal #2.
Ensure training reproducibility with deterministically scheduled update process.

\bullet

Goal #3.
Guarantee training accuracy by preserving all (important and less important) gradient information.

3.1.
Importance-aware Offloading Workflow

We begin by presenting the overall workflow of
ZenFlow
to provide a high-level view of how it decouples gradient updates across GPU and CPU. In this subsection, we assume that an important subset of gradients and their corresponding parameters have already been identified. The mechanism for efficiently selecting this subset is described later in
§

3.3
.
Here, we focus on how
ZenFlow
manages the importance-aware offloading and update process once the selection is in place.

Important updates: fast GPU path.
As shown in Fig.
3
(d),
ZenFlow
assigns the important gradient and parameters (highlighted in purple) to the GPU, while offloading the unimportant ones (shown in gray) to the CPU.
At each iteration,
ZenFlow
performs a standard forward and backward pass with the full set of parameters.
Once gradients are computed, the pre-identified important gradients remain on the GPU, where a
selective-optimizer
, initialized only with corresponding parameter subsets, performs an
in-place
update.
This GPU-side update is lightweight, as it operates on a small subset of parameters, and it completes on the GPU without introducing stalls between iterations.

Unimportant updates: slow CPU path.
Less important gradients are offloaded to the CPU and gradually accumulated over several iterations.
These gradients are
not
discarded. They are simply delayed until they become important enough—a process of what we call
gradient accumulation
.
Once the accumulated gradients become large enough to matter,
ZenFlow
performs a full parameter update on the CPU. In practice, this trigger is implemented via a fixed accumulation interval or an adaptive threshold (
§

3.2
,
§

6.11
), ensuring bounded staleness.
This CPU-side update, though being slow, is carefully scheduled, so that it
(1) perfectly overlaps with GPU computation to avoid stalls
(see
§

3.2
),
and (2) ensures training reproducibility and accuracy (see
§

4
).

In effect,
ZenFlow
updates important parameters more frequently using
fast
GPU, while updating less important ones less often on
slow
CPU, but with full gradient information.

This design achieves performance gains from three parts:
(1) In each iteration, only a small subset of parameters are updated efficiently in place on GPU, and these updates are independent from the slow CPU-side.
(2) The slow and compute-intensive CPU updates for large unimportant gradients are fully overlapped with multiple iterations of GPU computation, effectively hiding their latency.
(3) Update I/O overhead for unimportant parameters is amortized as
ZenFlow
only updates and transfers them to GPU when they become large enough.
Together, these optimizations minimize stalls.

3.2.
Zero-stall Pipeline

While the importance-aware offloading design has the potential to minimize GPU stalls by selectively updating a small set of parameters, the CPU-side update can still become a bottleneck if not carefully overlapped with GPU computation.
Moreover, naïvely creating an extra
selective-optimizer
on the GPU may incur unnecessary memory overhead.
We next describe how
ZenFlow
addresses both challenges, by hiding CPU update latency and managing memory efficiently, to realize a truly zero-stall pipeline.

Modeling I/O Efficiency.
We now provide an analytical comparison of the offloading workflow I/O traffic between
ZenFlow
and DeepSpeed ZeRO-Offload, demonstrating that
ZenFlow
’s pipeline significantly reduces communication overhead. For this analysis, we consider the case of fine-tuning with half-precision (
BF16
/
FP16
).
In each iteration, ZeRO-Offload transfers the gradient generated from the GPU to the CPU, equivalent to one model copy (
M
).
After the CPU optimizer completes the update, the updated parameters are transferred back to the GPU—another model copy (
M
). Therefore, the total I/O traffic per iteration is
2M
.

In contrast,
ZenFlow
offloads only the gradients for less important parameters. Let
k
denote the top-
k
ratio (i.e., the fraction of gradients considered important) and
S
the number of accumulation rounds for the unimportant gradients. In each iteration,
ZenFlow
transfers only the
(1-k)\cdot M
unimportant gradients to the CPU. After
S
iterations, the CPU performs a parameter update and sends back the corresponding
(1-k)\cdot M
updated parameters. Therefore, the average I/O traffic per iteration in
ZenFlow
is:
\frac{(S+1)\cdot(1-k)\cdot M}{S}
.

Take
S=4
(one representative configuration of the accumulation rounds from our analysis for hiding CPU-side updates we will discuss shortly), and
k=0.1
as an example, the average I/O traffic per iteration becomes
1.125M
, which is nearly a
2\times
reduction compared to
ZeRO-Offload.

Hiding CPU-side Updates.

In addition to reducing I/O traffic,
ZenFlow
also hides CPU-side update latency by overlapping it with the GPU computation. Our profiling shows that, for a Llama2-7B model, CPU update latency is approximately 4,600
ms
with 128 CPU threads (fully parallelized on our GPU node) and
\sim
6,200
ms
with limited resources (e.g., with only 32 CPU threads).
Uploading updated parameters to the GPU typically takes
\sim
500
ms
.
In contrast, the GPU forward pass (
\sim
45
ms
) and backward pass (
\sim
2,000
ms
) take around
\sim
2,045
ms
. This means the CPU update can be overlapped with
2.3\times
to
3.1\times
the GPU compute time.

Critically, we observe that unimportant gradients usually require 4-6 accumulation steps before crossing the importance threshold for update (see
§

6.11
). This enables their CPU-side updates to be effectively masked by 4-6 iterations of GPU forward/backward computation, achieving near-complete overlap without additional delay. Empirically, we set the accumulation interval to 4 steps (
S=4
), which is sufficient to hide CPU update latency in most cases while bounding staleness (see
§

4
for theoretical analysis).

To achieve this,
ZenFlow
employs a double-buffering strategy on the CPU. It maintains two gradient buffers: one for concurrently accumulating gradients from the current iteration, and one for holding previously accumulated gradients used for parameter updates. While the GPU executes the forward and backward passes and sends new gradients, the CPU concurrently applies updates accumulated in one buffer. See the step of
\textit{UP}_{R1}
in Fig.
7
, where parameters are updated using gradients accumulated from both
\textit{Step}_{I}
and
\textit{Step}_{I+1}
.

Once the update is complete, the buffers are swapped, and the buffer used for parameter updates is cleared in order to store gradients in the next accumulation cycle.
This design allows CPU updates to be completely hidden and run transparently in the background, fully overlapping with GPU training without introducing stalls.

Swapping out/in and Layer-wise Scheduling.

To avoid excessive GPU memory consumption from the GPU-side
selective-optimizer
,
ZenFlow
swaps out its optimizer states to CPU and swaps back in before next update on GPU. These states are relatively small and can be transferred efficiently. To further reduce memory cost, swapping is performed in a layer-wise manner, ensuring that only one layer’s optimizer state resides on GPU at any time. This design maintains high memory efficiency by preventing additional overhead beyond what is required for a single layer. The same layer-wise scheduling is applied to gradient offloading and CPU-side updates to fully overlap communication and computation.

Hyperparameter Auto-tuning.
To fully leverage the locality of important gradients while maintaining accuracy, we design
ZenFlow-auto
, which adaptively tunes the update interval based on observed learning dynamics. Specifically,
ZenFlow-auto
monitors gradient changes across GPUs using a lightweight coordination proxy (detailed in
§

3.3
and
§

6.11
).
For unimportant gradient part,
ZenFlow-auto
tracks the average accumulated channel gradient norm and compares it to the average one of the important part. Once the unimportant gradient part becomes comparable to important ones,
ZenFlow-auto
immediately triggers its CPU-side update, ensuring timely parameter refresh and stable convergence.

3.3.
Gradient Selection

We now turn to the key question:
how to select important gradients for GPU-side updates in a scalable, low-overhead manner.

A natural approach is to prioritize gradients with large magnitudes, as they typically contribute more to learning. However, in fully sharded training, computing and comparing all gradient norms globally is
expensive. To make this feasible in distributed training,
ZenFlow
introduces a lightweight yet effective approximation that leverages the spatio-temporal
locality of important gradients discussed in
§

2.3
.

Lightweight Proxy for Gradient Ranking.

In fully sharded training, selecting important gradients across GPUs poses a major communication challenge. A straightforward solution is to rank all gradients globally by magnitude and prioritize the top-
k
for updates. However, this requires collecting all gradients across devices via expensive
AllGather
.

For example, consider fine-tuning Llama2-7B across 4 GPUs shown in Fig.
8
(left). A naïve global ranking would require exchanging tens of gigabytes of gradients per iteration, incurring significant communication overhead.
Even a single weight matrix imposes non-trivial communication overhead. Consider
q_proj.weight
in a transformer layer, shaped
[4096,4096]
, containing 16.8M parameters. In a 4-way sharded setup, each device holds a
[1024,4096]
partition with 4.2M parameters. Using
BF16/FP16
, this amounts to 8MB per GPU. Aggregating gradients for this matrix across all devices requires 96MB of data transfer per iteration. Extrapolating to a 7B-parameter model, this results in a total gradient communication volume of 40GB per iteration.

To address this bottleneck,
ZenFlow
employs a communication efficient proxy: instead of gathering full
(n\times m)
gradients, where
n
is the output dimension and
m
is the input dimension of a weight matrix, each GPU computes and shares per-column gradient norms squared (i.e., the sum of squared gradient values within each column). This reduces both communication and top-
k
selection complexity from
O(nm)
to
O(m)
while preserving gradient magnitude information and omitting only directional components. For example, consider a
q_proj.weight
from Llama2-7B with shape
4096\times 4096
. Rather than transferring the full 32MB gradient matrix in BF16, each GPU shares a 4096-dimensional vector (8KB), reducing communication volume by over 4,000
\times
with negligible impact on importance estimation (Fig.
8
(right)).

Spatial Locality.

We empirically validate the effectiveness of the approximation. Fig.
9
(a) shows the cumulative distribution of top-
k
gradients and per-channel gradient norms during Qwen2.5-0.5B fine-tuning on
Alpaca52K
. Channels are sorted by how frequently they contain top-
k
gradients. The results show that 60-90% of top-
k
gradients are concentrated in just the top 10% of channels, and that per-channel gradient norms are strongly correlated with this top-
k
density. This confirms that channel-level summaries are a reliable indicator of gradient importance.

Temporal Locality.

To further reduce the selection overhead, we examine whether important channels persist across iterations. Fig.
9
(b) reports the retention ratio—the fraction of steps where previously selected channels remain within the important set. We find that once the top-1% of channels are tracked, the retention ratio already approaches 100%, and stays nearly perfect as the threshold widens to 3-5%. This strong temporal locality shows that caching and reusing channel indices is sufficient to cover important updates, avoiding repeated computation. In multi-GPU settings, this further reduces cross-device coordination and amortizes selection cost, enabling scalable importance-aware training.

4.
Accuracy and Reproducibility Guarantee

To ensure that
ZenFlow
training delivers robust quality, we explicitly address the requirements of convergence and reproducibility across runs.

4.1.
Convergence.

We now show that
ZenFlow
’s asynchronous offloading design does not affect the convergence property of existing optimizers.

Under standard assumptions commonly used in asynchronous optimization analysis
(
Dai et al., 2018
;
Lian et al., 2015
;
Zhang et al., 2015b
;
Zhou et al., 2016
)
, we show that
ZenFlow
preserves the convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
with a bounded staleness factor where
T
is the total number of iterations. Such a convergence rate is the same as the ideal rate of synchronous SGD
(
Dai et al., 2018
;
Zhang et al., 2015b
)
.

Why prioritizing high-norm channels.
Prioritizing high-norm gradient channels is consistent with standard descent analysis. For an
L
-smooth objective, one SGD step satisfies

f(w_{t+1})\leq f(w_{t})-\eta\langle\nabla f(w_{t}),g_{t}\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|g_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Channels with larger gradient norm therefore have disproportionate influence on the descent direction. Existing top-
K
sparsification results further show that retaining large-norm channels with error compensation can preserve convergence behavior
(
Alistarh et al., 2018
;
Stich et al., 2018
)
.

Partial staleness in asynchronous training.
We consider a mixed asynchronous training setup where the parameters are partitioned into two disjoint sets:
\theta=[\theta^{(g)},\theta^{(c)}]
. The gradients w.r.t.
\theta^{(g)}
are computed and applied
immediately
on the GPU every iteration, while the gradients w.r.t.
\theta^{(c)}
are
accumulated on the CPU
over
S
iterations (typically
S=4
) and then applied synchronously.
Formally, at each training step
t
, we have the following.

\theta_{t+1}^{(g)}=\theta_{t}^{(g)}-\alpha_{t}\nabla_{\theta^{(g)}}L(\theta_{t})

\theta_{t+1}^{(c)}=\begin{cases}\theta_{t}^{(c)}-\alpha_{t}\cdot\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=t-S+1}^{t}\nabla_{\theta^{(c)}}L(\theta_{i}),&\text{if }t\bmod S=0\\
\theta_{t}^{(c)},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{t}
is the learning rate and
L(\theta)
is the objective loss function. The GPU handles the forward and backward passes for both
\theta^{(g)}
and
\theta^{(c)}
, but only updates
\theta^{(g)}
immediately. The CPU accumulates the gradients for
\theta^{(c)}
over
S
iterations before applying the update.

Next, we model a partially stale update system with a bounded delay. Let

\rho=\frac{\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\theta^{(c)}}L(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}{\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}
,
representing the fraction of total gradient-norm energy that resides in the delayed coordinates (on the CPU). Empirically, we observe
\rho\approx 0.10
, as the GPU handles 90% of the gradient energy.

Bounded-staleness result.
With common assumptions of unbiased stochastic gradients, bounded variance, and Lipschitz smoothness
(
Koloskova et al., 2022
;
Lian et al., 2015
;
Zhou et al., 2016
;
Dai et al., 2018
)
, and letting
\rho
denote the fraction of gradient energy at the
CPU side, we have the following:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla L(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{1+\rho S}{T}}\Bigr).

The term
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{T}}\Bigr)
is the standard SGD rate; the factor
\sqrt{1+\rho S}
quantifies the extra cost of staleness. With
S=4
and
\rho\approx 0.10
, this factor is

\sqrt{1.4}\approx 1.18
, i.e., an 18 % slowdown relative to ideal
synchronous SGD.

Warmup mitigates early-stage staleness.
The convergence bound under the partial staleness includes a penalty term with the form
\sqrt{1+\rho S}
, where
\rho
is the fraction of gradient-norm energy in the delayed coordinates and
S
is the accumulation interval. This bound assumes uniform gradient energy across training steps.
However, in practice, the gradients are
not
uniformly distributed. Early training steps contribute disproportionately to optimization progress, as the gradient norms are significantly larger during this phase. Empirical and theoretical studies suggest that the gradient energy often decays as
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(\theta_{t})\|^{2}]\sim t^{-\beta}
, with
(0<\beta<1)

(
Luo et al., 2025
;
Hoffmann et al., 2022
;
Kaplan et al., 2020
)
.

ZenFlow
exploits this observation by applying synchronous updates (i.e., no staleness) during the initial
\tau
warmup steps, and then switches to asynchronous offloading for the remaining
T-\tau
steps.
This strategy eliminates staleness where it causes the most harm, while preserving efficiency later when gradients are smaller and more stable.

To quantify the effect, we compute the
gradient-weighted
penalty using a continuous approximation of the
p
-series.

\text{Penalty}(\beta)\approx\sqrt{1+\rho S\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{\tau}{T}\right)^{1-\beta}\right)}.

This closed form captures the diminishing impact of the delayed gradients as training progresses.

For example, with a 5% warmup (
\tau/T=0.05
),
S=4
,
\rho=0.1
, and
\beta=0.6
(typically ranging from 0.4 to 0.6
(
Kaplan et al., 2020
;
Luo et al., 2025
)
), the multiplicative penalty factor decreases from approximately 1.18 without warmup to 1.13 with warmup—equivalently, the excess penalty decreases from 18.3% to 13.1%.

Thus, the bound for
ZenFlow
incurs only about 13% overhead relative to the ideal SGD rate. Modern optimizers such as Adam and AdamW may further mitigate gradient staleness through momentum and adaptive learning rates
(
Lin et al., 2017
;
Kingma and Ba, 2017
;
Shazeer and Stern, 2018
;
Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017
)
. With this modest theoretical overhead,
ZenFlow
achieves up to 5
\times
end-to-end speedup, a 2
\times
reduction in I/O traffic, and near-zero stalls. We present the end-to-end speedup and a detailed breakdown of the performance gains in
§

6
.

We use SGD in the derivation because it provides a general and transparent explanation of the delayed-update effect. While Adam and AdamW introduce momentum and adaptive per-coordinate scaling, ZenFlow relies on channel-level gradient-energy concentration rather than optimizer-specific update dynamics. Our cross-optimizer measurements in Table
4
further show that this locality remains strong under AdamW.

4.2.
Determinism.

ZenFlow
’s decoupled updating process is fundamentally different from traditional nondeterministic asynchronous training methods
(
Zhang et al., 2015a
;
Lian et al., 2018
;
Li et al., 2014
;
Xing et al., 2015
)
, where update timing depends on variable factors such as system latency.
ZenFlow
ensures determinism: Given fixed seeds and configurations, the gradient selection, update and offloading schedules follow a
predetermined order
, making runs fully reproducible.

In multi-GPU training, ZenFlow overlaps important and unimportant channel computation only within each GPU; inter-GPU synchronization still follows standard ZeRO semantics with a barrier at every step. Consequently, per-stage execution latency does not affect the deterministic ordering of parameter updates observed across workers. We use deterministic CUDA kernels and apply a fixed channel-ranking pipeline. Together, these guarantees ensure that ZenFlow produces bit-exact identical training results under the same configuration.

5.
Implementation

We build
ZenFlow
on DeepSpeed
(
Rasley et al., 2020
)
with
\sim
11K lines of Python code, without requiring any change to user training code.
Our design extends DeepSpeed’s ZeRO-Offload
(
Ren et al., 2021b
)
and ZeRO-Infinity
(
Rajbhandari et al., 2021
)
backends by integrating importance-aware gradient selection and
selective-optimizer
into the DeepSpeed runtime. We describe the key implementation components of
ZenFlow
below.

Fully Segmented Gradient Selection.

In fully sharded distributed training, model states such as gradients are partitioned across GPUs. A gradient tensor may be flattened and split across multiple GPUs, so a single input-channel tensor can span two devices. To support fine-grained importance tracking, we introduce a segment mapping table that uses
(segment_id, offset)
tuples to index and manage gradient metadata. Each
segment_id
corresponds to one selected important channel. This structure enables flexible tracking of important channels during training even when they are scattered across devices. Additionally, we customize PyTorch’s
flatten
operations
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
to reorganize gradient storage from a row-major to a column-major layout, improving channel-wise access and manipulation.

Concurrent CPU-side Optimizer.

To overlap GPU computation with CPU-side updates,
ZenFlow
initializes multiple CPU optimizer instances at setup. Each optimizer updates its assigned gradients—grouped into I/O-efficient buckets—as soon as data arrives from the GPU.
Updates run in dedicated processes, with double-buffering and
shared_memory
enabling zero-copy communication. Concurrency is carefully managed to minimize synchronization overhead.

Selective GPU-side Optimizer.

We extend PyTorch’s Adam and AdamW optimizers to support in-place updates using selected important gradients and enable fast swap-out and swap-in for extra optimizer state tensors.

extends the model scale beyond what GPU ZeRO-only can support (e.g., 7B parameters on A6000), enabling fine-tuning of larger models without using more expensive hardware. In addition,
ZenFlow
improves efficiency: by leveraging offloading to support larger batch sizes and overlap updates,
ZenFlow
achieves higher end-to-end throughput than both ZeRO and ZeRO-Offload. This results in faster completion of the
Alpaca52K
fine-tuning task and lower overall cost. Even when the optimizer overhead is not fully amortized (e.g., 7B on A100), the larger batch sizes enabled by
ZenFlow
still reduce cost compared to traditional GPU training. Overall,
ZenFlow
reduces cost by 35–60% compared to ZeRO-Offload and by 18–72% compared to GPU-only ZeRO, demonstrating the practical benefits of importance-aware offloaded training.
Compared with GPU-only ZeRO, ZenFlow trades limited CPU offloading overhead for larger feasible batches and trainability when the model would otherwise run out of GPU memory.

6.11.
Sensitivity of Hyperparameters

We investigate the impact of hyperparameters in
ZenFlow
, specifically the top-
k
ratio and the CPU-side update interval. By assigning longer update intervals to less important gradients,
ZenFlow
further reduces GPU-side stalls. However, this selective staleness can slightly degrade accuracy when training iterations are limited—e.g., using
S{=}16
results in a noticeable 0.02 drop in accuracy within just 3 epochs as shown in Fig.
16
(a) . This tradeoff can be mitigated with extended training iterations, as stale gradients gradually catch up due to
ZenFlow
’s faster per-iteration execution.

From Fig.
16
(a) we observe that
ZenFlow
is generally robust to variations in the top-
k
ratio in most cases, as
ZenFlow
preserves all gradients with bounded staleness. Increasing the update interval typically improves speedup. However, using an overly aggressive update interval (e.g.,
S{=}16
), a smaller ratio may degrade accuracy due to increased staleness penalty (e.g., from 0.881 to 0.873 with 1% top-
k
). In general, we find that a 1% ratio with
S{=}4
strikes a good balance between accuracy and efficiency (see
§

2.3
).

7.
Related Work

Tensor Offloading for Training.

Prior work
(
Ren et al., 2021b
;
Rajbhandari et al., 2021
;
Sun et al., 2022
;
Beaumont et al., 2021
)
offloads tensors to CPU memory to scale training but largely overlooks CPU–GPU pipeline interactions, leaving stalls from CPU latency and PCIe transfers
(
Maurya et al., 2024
)
. These systems make large models trainable under GPU memory constraints, yet generally update all parameters uniformly despite the hardware imbalance. StrongHold
(
Sun et al., 2022
)
overlaps layer-wise CPU and GPU computation, but the performance gap between the devices still exposes CPU-induced stalls; overlap alone therefore cannot fully hide slow CPU updates. Storage-focused systems such as SwapAdvisor
(
Huang et al., 2020
)
and G10
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
optimize placement across GPU, CPU, and SSD while treating the CPU primarily as storage rather than explicitly coordinating its computation with GPU updates. Recent systems exploit high-bandwidth CPU–GPU superchips for efficient offloading
(
Lian et al., 2025
;
Luo et al., 2026
)
,
but rely on specialized interconnects such as NVLink-C2C that are unavailable on widely deployed A100 and H100 GPUs.
In contrast,
ZenFlow
is importance- and hardware-aware, decoupling important and non-important updates to minimize stalls.

Gradient Sparsity and Compression.

Prior studies show that gradients exhibit sparsity and can be filtered using threshold-based selection
(
Ström, 2015
;
Dryden et al., 2016
;
Aji and Heafield, 2017
;
Fang et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2025
;
Lin et al., 2017
)
; transmitting only top-
k
or above-threshold gradients can preserve training quality while reducing communication. In offloading settings, this observation motivates prioritizing useful gradients instead of moving every gradient with the same urgency. Smart-Infinity
(
Jang et al., 2024
)
and LSP-Offload
(
Chen et al., 2025
)
reduce offloading I/O through lossy compression, dropping small gradients or using learned projections at the cost of gradient fidelity.
ZenFlow
instead retains the remaining gradients through CPU-side accumulation while scheduling important updates on the GPU. Compression remains orthogonal to
ZenFlow
and can further optimize its scheduling pipeline.

Asynchronous Training.
Asynchrony can accelerate distributed training
(
Zhang et al., 2015a
;
Lian et al., 2018
;
Li et al., 2014
;
Xing et al., 2015
;
Yu et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Chai et al., 2021
)
, but non-deterministic execution introduces stale gradients that can degrade convergence and reproducibility
(
Dai et al., 2018
;
Lian et al., 2015
;
Zhang et al., 2015b
;
Zhou et al., 2016
)
. In typical schemes, workers update shared state according to unpredictable completion times, making the resulting update sequence difficult to reproduce. Prior work therefore bounds staleness
(
Yu et al., 2024
;
Lian et al., 2015
;
Chai et al., 2021
;
Cui et al., 2014
)
.
ZenFlow
instead uses bounded asynchronous execution specifically to hide CPU-side latency while retaining a predetermined, repeatable update order.

Parameter-Efficient vs. Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning.
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods (e.g., adapters
(
He et al., 2022
)
, prefix-tuning
(
Li and Liang, 2021
)
, LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
, Q-LoRA
(
Dettmers et al., 2023
)
, mLoRA
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
, GaLore
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
) reduce fine-tuning cost by updating only a small subset of parameters, significantly lowering memory and optimizer overhead.
However, this efficiency comes at a cost: the constrained parameter space can bottleneck the model’s ability to learn from diverse datasets or sustain long-term training
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Liao et al., 2023
;
Coleman et al., 2025
;
Dai et al., 2024
;
Patel, 2025
)
.
Unlike PEFT,
ZenFlow
targets full-parameter fine-tuning rather than restricting the trainable parameter space, preserving full-weight flexibility while improving system performance. The two approaches address different bottlenecks:
ZenFlow
is complementary to PEFT and can be integrated with low-rank methods.

8.
Conclusion

ZenFlow
decouples fast GPU and slow CPU updates to reduce I/O and GPU stalls in LLM fine-tuning. It overlaps in-place GPU updates of important gradients with CPU accumulation of the remainder. By exploiting gradients’ spatial and temporal locality for scalable importance estimation,
ZenFlow
accelerates fine-tuning without global synchronization while preserving accuracy and reproducibility.
ZenFlow
has been integrated into DeepSpeed.
