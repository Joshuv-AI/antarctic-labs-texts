Control and optimization of a half-car active suspension system with classical and modified PID-based controllers using metaheuristic techniques
Authors: Faruk Emre Aysal, Onur Özdal Mengi, Aykut Fatih Güven, Mohit Bajaj, Vojtech Blazek, Iryna Hunko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In automobiles and road vehicles such as buses, high-performance suspension systems are essential to ensure passenger comfort by minimizing the effects of road disturbances. Therefore, effective control of suspension systems is of great importance. This study investigates the control of a half-car active suspension system using Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID), Fractional Order Proportional-Integral-Derivative (FOPID), Fractional Order Proportional-Integral + Fractional Order Derivative (FOPI + FOPD), Tilt-Integral-Derivative (TID), and Proportional-Integral-Derivative Acceleration (PIDA) controllers. The tuning of these controllers was performed using five different metaheuristic optimization algorithms: the Artemis Optimizer (AO), Escape Algorithm (ESC), Genghis Khan Shark Optimizer (GKSO), A Novel Weighted Mean of Vectors (INFO), and Moss Growth Optimization (MGO). The aim of this work is to determine the most efficient controller-algorithm combination for optimal system performance. Each controller was individually paired with each optimization algorithm, resulting in 25 distinct simulations. Performance was evaluated using the Integral Time-weighted Absolute Error (ITAE) as the objective function. Among all configurations, the ESC algorithm achieved the lowest ITAE value. Although the more complex controller structures were expected to outperform the classical PID, the PID controller achieved the best overall result, surpassing expectations. This outcome highlights that simple controller architectures, when properly tuned, can still deliver highly effective performance and should not be underestimated in control system design.

Introduction

Suspension systems play a critical role in minimizing the impact of road irregularities on vehicle body motion, thereby enabling a safe and comfortable driving experience. Designed to enhance ride dynamics and improve road holding capabilities, these systems are composed of complex mechanical structures1,2. Functioning as the link between the vehicle body and the wheels, suspension systems allow the vehicle to effectively adapt to varying road conditions and enhance passenger comfort. Among the various suspension technologies that have evolved over time, the MacPherson suspension—still widely used today—is considered one of the foundational components of modern suspension systems3.

Conventional vehicle suspension systems mainly consist of two components: coil springs and dampers. This configuration offers several advantages, including high road-holding capability, driving comfort, and safety. From a control theory perspective, suspension systems can be classified into three main categories: passive, semi-active, and active systems4. In passive suspension systems, the dynamic characteristics are fixed and therefore cannot adequately respond to sudden changes in road conditions or vehicle speed. Due to this limited adaptability, passive suspensions fail to deliver high-performance outcomes5–7. To overcome these limitations, researchers have focused on the development of semi-active and active suspension systems (Fig. 1). Semi-active systems can dynamically adjust the damping force based on real-time road feedback. Techniques such as orifice-based dampers, viscoelastic or elasto-plastic fluids, and other controllable media are often employed in these systems8,9. While both passive and semi-active suspension systems are directly affected by road disturbances, significant improvements in ride performance have been achieved through semi-active approaches9–12. However, when evaluated under the ISO-2631 standard on “Mechanical Vibration and Shock,” these improvements have been found insufficient to fully meet comfort requirements12. Consequently, research efforts have increasingly focused on developing active suspension systems that offer greater control effectiveness13. Nevertheless, research into semi-active systems, particularly those based on magnetorheological fluids, continues.

Active suspension systems utilize various sensors to monitor vehicle movements in real time. Based on the sensor feedback, suspension parameters are continuously adjusted. These systems can quickly and effectively respond to both road disturbances and driver inputs, thereby enhancing ride comfort as well as vehicle stability and handling performance14. Numerous studies in the literature have investigated the potential of active suspension systems to improve passenger comfort, often using the ISO-2631 standard as a benchmark15–18. The effective implementation of both active and semi-active suspension systems is highly dependent on the design of appropriate control strategies and their integration into the vehicle. To this end, both the automotive industry and academic researchers have developed a wide range of control methods for active suspension design. Common control techniques investigated in the literature include Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers19, Linear Quadratic Regulators (LQR)20, Linear Quadratic Integral (LQI) controllers21, Fuzzy Logic Control22, and Model Predictive Control (MPC)23.

Literature review

Studies on active suspension systems for passenger vehicles in the literature exhibit significant variations in terms of the mathematical models employed, the types of controllers used, the optimization algorithms applied for controller parameter tuning, and the performance evaluation criteria adopted. Within this context, recent studies focusing on half-car models have been reviewed. For clarity and ease of understanding, a systematic classification of these recent studies is presented in Table 1.

An analysis of the research conducted on half-car models reveals that most researchers prefer the conventional half-car model as the basic mathematical framework24–49. In some studies, however, this model has been modified and integrated with more complex structures to enhance passenger comfort and improve model accuracy. For instance, certain studies incorporate driver and passenger seats into the half-car model24–31. Moreover, as an alternative to the traditional linear single-track vehicle model, some researchers have employed more comprehensive two-track vertical dynamic models that also account for aerodynamic effects25,26,29–31. This modeling approach enables the simulation of vehicle rolling motion, in contrast to the pitching motion typically represented by the conventional half-car model.

Despite these developments, the classical half-car model remains the most widely used approach in the literature. The primary reason for its continued prevalence is its usefulness as a foundational model for analyzing the dynamics of an entire vehicle undergoing linear motion. In studies where vehicle rolling motion must also be simulated, full-car models are generally preferred due to their ability to more accurately represent multi-axis dynamics.

A brief literature survey.

An evaluation of existing studies from the perspective of control strategies reveals that classical PID controllers and their derivatives remain the most commonly employed techniques, as illustrated in Table 1. In several cases, hybrid control structures have been developed to improve system performance; for instance, combinations of PID control with fuzzy logic have been proposed to exploit the advantages of both methods25,27–29,32,37,41,46. In parallel, there has been a noticeable increase in the use of more sophisticated approaches such as SMC, MPC, LQR, and various optimal control frameworks, particularly in recent studies26,30,31,33–36.

From the standpoint of optimization techniques, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) has emerged as one of the most prominent methods for tuning control parameters in active suspension systems24,28,29,35,46,47. Furthermore, other bio-inspired algorithms, including Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), have been successfully employed to enhance controller performance26,27,37. Nevertheless, a significant number of studies have focused on evaluating the control schemes directly, without incorporating any optimization techniques25,30–34,36,38–45,48,49.

As for performance evaluation metrics, the Root Mean Square (RMS) value is the most frequently adopted criterion across the literature24–49. Additionally, alternative metrics such as the Integral of Absolute Error (IAE) and the Integral of Time-weighted Absolute Error (ITAE) have been reported in selected studies to further quantify control effectiveness36,41.

Motivation

The literature review has revealed the existence of various half-car models with differing structural and dynamic complexities. While numerous studies have employed different control techniques, relatively few have focused on the optimization of controller parameters using advanced algorithms. It is well recognized that not only the design but also the precise control of active suspension systems plays a critical role in achieving high-performance outcomes. In this study, multiple controllers were employed to manage the dynamics of a half-car active suspension system. To ensure a rigorous evaluation of each controller’s performance, their parameters were optimized using selected metaheuristic algorithms. By combining diverse controller structures and optimization techniques identified from the literature, a comprehensive set of configurations was tested. The obtained results, particularly in terms of controller effectiveness, revealed some unexpected findings and provided new insights into the relative performance of conventional versus complex control schemes.

Problem formulation

The overall methodology proposed in this study is illustrated in Fig. 3, which presents the conceptual framework for modeling, control, and optimization of a half-car active suspension system. The system incorporates five controller architectures: PID, Fractional Order PID (FOPID), a hybrid FOPI + FOPD configuration, Tilt Integral Derivative (TID), and Proportional–Integral–Derivative Acceleration (PIDA).

To achieve optimal controller performance, five recent metaheuristic algorithms were employed: AO, ESC, GKSO, INFO, and MGO. Each controller was individually tuned using these optimization techniques. The tuning process aimed to minimize ITAE, a widely accepted performance criterion in suspension control applications.

This combined control-optimization scheme enables a comprehensive comparison across 25 distinct controller-algorithm pairs. The obtained results offer valuable insights into which combinations yield the most favorable dynamic responses under road disturbance inputs.

Half car active suspension system

The half-car model is widely used in vehicle dynamics and suspension system simulations due to its relatively simple and interpretable structure50. Despite its simplicity, simulations based on the half-car configuration can capture many critical characteristics of a full vehicle, providing high levels of accuracy51. The model represents a longitudinal section of a full vehicle, incorporating both front and rear suspension structures.

In its passive form, the half-car model typically consists of an unsprung mass, two springs, and a damper located in both the front and rear suspension assemblies. An actuator positioned between the vehicle body and the axle introduces active behavior into the system. These actuators generate vertical forces in both directions, allowing for dynamic control of the suspension response. Both suspension units are connected to the sprung mass, which represents the main body of the vehicle.

By applying active control, the adverse effects of road disturbances and driving inputs on the vehicle body and passengers can be significantly minimized52. Moreover, active suspension systems have been shown to improve overall vehicle dynamics and handling performance53. The active half-car model employed in this study features four degrees of freedom and is used to evaluate the impact of different control strategies on ride comfort and dynamic behavior. A schematic representation of this model is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Proposed optimization algorithm

This section provides an in-depth explanation of the ESC, which was selected as the primary optimization technique in this study. Although five metaheuristic algorithms—AO, ESC, GKSO, INFO, and MGO—were initially evaluated, ESC consistently outperformed the others across all controller types in minimizing the ITAE. Due to its superior accuracy and robustness, only ESC is discussed in detail in the following subsections. Comparative results of the remaining algorithms are presented in the Results and Discussion section.

Overview of the escape algorithm (ESC)

The ESC draws its conceptual foundation from the study of human behavioral dynamics during emergency evacuations. Rather than relying on purely mathematical models, the algorithm mimics real-world crowd responses, particularly those observed under high-stress scenarios. It integrates diverse behavioral modes—such as calm decision-making, herding behavior, and panic-driven reactions—to effectively balance global exploration and local exploitation. This adaptive mechanism enables the ESC to tackle complex optimization problems with greater flexibility and efficiency58.

Origin of inspiration

The development of the ESC is rooted in the evolution of research on crowd behavior during emergency scenarios. Insights gained from such studies have played a crucial role in shaping the algorithm’s structure. Its theoretical framework arises from a comprehensive investigation into the behavioral variability displayed by individuals under high-stress conditions59. In crises like natural disasters or man-made hazards, human reactions are often erratic, influenced by fear, limited mobility, and the collective movement of surrounding individuals60–62. These unplanned responses can either accelerate or hinder evacuation processes, offering a meaningful reference for designing adaptive and intelligent optimization methods. The ESC models this behavioral complexity by transforming real-life human response patterns into an effective computational metaheuristic suitable for solving a wide range of optimization problems.

A key behavioral foundation of the ESC algorithm is the “leader–follower” mechanism frequently observed in group evacuation scenarios63,64. In such contexts, certain individuals naturally or situationally assume leadership roles, influencing the direction and decisions of others who follow based on spatial proximity or perceived authority. ESC reflects this emergent structure by categorizing agents into three behaviorally distinct roles: calm, herding-oriented, and panic-driven agents65. Each behavioral type contributes differently to the algorithm’s search process, and their collective interaction enhances the system’s ability to explore the solution space effectively and converge toward optimal results.

Calm Agents: This category simulates individuals who remain composed under pressure and tend to process situations logically. To emulate such dynamic conditions, ESC employs a structural mechanism called the Elite Pool, which maintains a set of the best-performing candidate solutions. This pool reflects the most favorable escape routes, akin to those identified by evacuees in real-world situations. By incorporating this mechanism, the algorithm improves its capacity for diverse and extensive exploration of the search space, thereby reducing the likelihood of premature convergence and helping escape from local optima58.

In this formulation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{j}\sim\:N(0,{\sigma\:}^{2})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{j}\sim\:N\left(\text{0,1}\right)$$\end{document}, where σ is derived from a gamma-based scaling rule that ensures the resulting step sizes follow a heavy-tailed Lévy distribution. This dynamic adjustment causes the algorithm to exhibit high variability in the early stages—mimicking panic-like movement—and gradually shift toward precision-focused updates, akin to individuals settling into rational evacuation behavior. The adaptive weighting, therefore, supports both global search efficacy and local refinement, allowing the algorithm to efficiently avoid local optima while accelerating toward high-quality solutions.

Fitness evaluation and elite pool update

During each cycle of the ESC algorithm, the quality of updated individuals is reassessed to determine whether a position change leads to performance improvement. This process relies on a greedy selection criterion that retains only the better of the current and newly proposed positions. The goal is to ensure that the search process consistently advances toward superior solutions without regression.

The fitness of the modified candidate is calculated using the objective function, as shown in Eq. This logic ensures that only improvements are adopted, fostering a monotonic enhancement in solution quality across iterations and minimizing the risk of backtracking into inferior regions of the search space.

In parallel, the Elite Pool—a dynamic archive of the most successful candidates—is updated after each iteration. It functions as a memory mechanism, storing the best-performing individuals and serving as a reference for guiding the population in subsequent steps. By continuously refining this elite subset, the algorithm maintains convergence efficiency and enhances its ability to locate global optima, while retaining enough diversity to prevent premature stagnation.

Although the ESC is not proposed in this study, its computational characteristics are relevant for understanding runtime performance. ESC has been reported to exhibit a slightly higher computational burden compared to lightweight optimizers such as harris hawks optimization, primarily due to its behavioral group stratification and population sorting procedures66,67. Nevertheless, this additional cost is often offset by its improved convergence capabilities and robust global search behavior, which justify its application in complex control problems like the one addressed here.

Simulation and results

In the simulation study, a total of 25 distinct experiments were conducted by combining five different controllers—PID, FOPID, FOPI + FOPD, TID, and PIDA—with five optimization algorithms—AO, ESC, GKSO, INFO, and MGO. Each simulation was configured with a population size of 50 and 100 iterations, ensuring consistency across all experimental conditions. For each of these 25 scenarios, 5000 trials were executed to determine the optimal tuning coefficients for the controllers. The primary performance metric used for evaluation was the ITAE, chosen due to its widespread use and proven effectiveness in the control systems literature. The aim of the optimization process was to minimize the ITAE value, reflecting faster and more accurate system response with minimal error over time.

In addition to ITAE, the simulations also produced values for other widely recognized error performance metrics—ITSE, IAE, and ISE—which were plotted to offer a more comprehensive assessment of system behavior under different control and optimization strategies.

A similar study in the literature70 employed the same system configuration and compared PID, fuzzy PID, and MPC techniques. That study reported MPC as the best-performing controller, achieving a minimum ITAE value of 0.5395. In contrast, our study demonstrated that the PID controller tuned via the ESC algorithm achieved a significantly lower ITAE value of 0.3388. Moreover, with the exception of the INFO algorithm, all other four algorithms surpassed this benchmark when used to optimize the PID controller.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study has presented a comprehensive investigation into the control of a half-car active suspension system by employing various controller structures and optimizing their parameters using metaheuristic algorithms. The controllers evaluated include PID, FOPID, FOPI + FOPD, TID, and PIDA. The parameters of these controllers were optimized using five different algorithms: AO, ESC, GKSO, INFO ve MGO.

Although the literature presents a vast number of both control strategies and optimization algorithms, it is not feasible to test each one individually. Therefore, selecting a subset of promising controllers and optimization techniques—based on prior research or informed expectations—is necessary. However, this selection process itself represents a significant research challenge.

This study was based on the initial hypothesis that more complex controller architectures would yield superior performance compared to basic ones, such as the classical PID controller. Contrary to this expectation, the results demonstrated that the simple PID controller outperformed the more advanced structures, such as FOPID and PIDA, even when their parameters were finely tuned. Despite its extended structure, the FOPID controller did not achieve better performance than PID.

Researchers who plan to work in this field are advised to carefully consider these findings when selecting their controller structures. Furthermore, the choice of optimization algorithm and the design of the search space should be guided by preliminary analysis. It is highly recommended that parameter bounds and algorithm configurations be fine-tuned using a trial-and-error approach before the actual optimization process is executed. This preparatory phase can significantly enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the final results.

In summary, this study demonstrates that simpler controllers with well-optimized parameters—specifically the PID controller optimized using the ESC algorithm—can yield superior performance in active suspension applications, contrary to the widespread assumption that complexity necessarily leads to better control.
