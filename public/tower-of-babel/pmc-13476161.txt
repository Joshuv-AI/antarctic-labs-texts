Peripheral immune dynamics during treatment predict prognosis in HCC receiving TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF/TKIs
Authors: Lihao Qin, Linzhou Zhu, Hao Yang, Wanci Li, Shuai Zhang, Jian Shen, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the prognostic value of peripheral immune markers and their static and dynamic changes during different treatment stages in patients with hepatitis B virus (HBV)‐related unresectable hepatocellula carcinoma (uHCC) receiving transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) antibodies or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).MethodsThis single‐centre retrospective study included patients with HBV‐related uHCC treated with TACE combined with ICls and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs between July 2019 and July 2024. Peripheral blood immune indicators were collected at baseline (Cycle 0), the second treatment cycle (Cycle 2) and the fourth treatment cycle (Cycle 4). The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), while secondary outcomes included progression‐free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR), with tumour response assessed at the first treatment evaluation (at the end of the fourth treatment cycle) based on mRECIST criteria. Longitudinal changes between adjacent time points were calculated (Δ: Cycle 2 ‐ Cycle 0; Δ2: Cycle 4 ‐ Cycle 2). Both static values and dynamic changes in immune markers were subjected to LASSO‐Cox and stepwise multivariate Cox regression analyses to identify independent predictors of OS. Based on the significant variables, a prognostic nomogram and an immune‐based risk score model were developed. To further assess prognostic heterogeneity, patients were stratified by K‐means clustering according to the temporal patterns of the most predictive immune marker, and survival outcomes were compared across the resulting subgroups.ResultsA total of 67 HBV‐related uHCC patients treated with TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs were included. The cohort had a median follow‐up of 18.2 months, with a median overall survival (mOS) of 30.4 months and a median progression‐free survival (mPFS) of 11.0 months. At the first evaluation, the objective response rate (ORR) was 53.7%, and the disease control rate (DCR) was 86.6%. LASSO‐Cox regression identified several static and dynamic immune markers associated with OS. Ultimately, CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio at Cycle 4 (HR = 0.58, p = 0.035), Δ2 NLR (HR = 1.66, p = 0.014), and Δ2 CD3⁺CD4⁺ (HR = 0.04, p = 0.007) were confirmed by stepwise multivariate Cox regression. A prognostic nomogram incorporating these variables demonstrated favourable predictive performance (C‐index = 0.72; AUCs = 0.750, 0.722 and 0.854 for 1‐, 2‐ and 3‐year OS, respectively). The derived immune‐based risk score effectively stratified patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups with significantly different OS (11.5 vs. 34.0 months, p < 0.001) and PFS (7.5 vs. 13.8 months, p = 0.006). The risk score showed a positive correlation with Δ2 NLR (r = 0.50) and negative correlations with CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio (r = −0.64) and Δ2 CD3⁺CD4⁺ (r = −0.45). Based on CD3⁺CD4⁺ trajectory clustering, three distinct immune dynamic subtypes were identified, among which the continuous decline group exhibited the poorest prognosis (p = 0.042).ConclusionThis study demonstrates the prognostic relevance of peripheral immune markers in patients with HBV‐related uHCC receiving combination therapy. Immune dynamics observed during later treatment stages, rather than baseline measurements, were more strongly associated with overall survival. A risk model based on the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio at Cycle 4, Δ2 NLR and Δ2 CD3⁺CD4⁺ enabled effective prognostic stratification and may provide a useful framework for risk assessment in this patient population.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide, posing a significant threat to global health. 1 Although surgical resection and liver transplantation offer curative potential for patients diagnosed at an early stage, the majority of patients present with intermediate or advanced disease at diagnosis, limiting treatment options and resulting in poor overall prognosis. 2 , 3 Therefore, effective treatment strategies for patients with unresectable HCC remain a major clinical challenge. In recent years, transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) has been established as the standard locoregional therapy for intermediate‐stage HCC. In addition, its combination with systemic treatments, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and anti‐angiogenic agents, including bevacizumab and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), has emerged as a new standard for advanced‐stage HCC. 4 Furthermore, landmark trials, such as IMbrave150, ORIENT‐32 and CHANCE2201 have demonstrated that TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs therapies can exert synergistic effects in suppressing tumor progression and improving long‐term survival. 5 , 6 , 7

However, the therapeutic response to combination therapy remains highly heterogeneous. 8 While some patients derive durable clinical benefit, others experience rapid disease progression. 9 This highlights the urgent need for robust immunological assessment tools to identify poor responders early and guide personalised treatment strategies. In this context, peripheral blood immune markers, including lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, complement components and inflammatory cytokines, have emerged as promising tools for real‐time immune monitoring because of their accessibility and reproducibility. 10 , 11 Several studies have shown that baseline immune parameters, such as the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels, are associated with immune status and clinical outcomes in HCC. 12 Nevertheless, static immune measurements at a single time point often fail to reflect the dynamic immunological remodelling that occurs throughout treatment and its relationship with survival.

Importantly, the immune status of cancer patients is a dynamic and continuously evolving process shaped by therapeutic interventions and host immune responses. longitudinal monitoring of immune marker dynamics may provide greater biological and clinical predictive value than static assessments at a single time point. In recent years, accumulating studies have further advanced biomarker research, immune‐related investigation and prognostic modelling in HCC, underscoring the growing interest in refining risk stratification and treatment monitoring in this disease. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, although such dynamic approaches have been explored in other solid tumors, including non‐small cell lung cancer and melanoma, systematic studies focusing on longitudinal changes in peripheral immunity and their prognostic significance in HCC patients receiving TACE combined with ICls and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs remain limited.

To address this gap, we collected 15 peripheral immune markers from patients with HBV‐related unresectable HCC undergoing TACE combined with immunotherapy and anti‐VEGF agents at three key treatment cycles. We systematically analysed their dynamic changes and used Lasso and stepwise Cox regression to identify markers associated with overall survival (OS). Furthermore, we evaluated the prognostic impact of immune marker trajectories and developed a dynamic immune‐based risk model and nomogram to support individualised outcome prediction and treatment optimisation in HCC.

Results

Patient characteristics

From 1 July 2019 to 1 July 2024, a total of 187 uHCC patients undergoing TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs were screened. After applying the exclusion criteria, 120 patients were excluded for the following reasons: (1) histologically diagnosed with ICC, mixed hepatocellular‐cholangiocarcinoma, sarcomatoid HCC or fibrolamellar HCC (n = 5); (2) presence of other concomitant malignancies (n = 6); (3) history of prior systemic therapy (n = 16); (4) incomplete blood cell count or peripheral immunity data (n = 87); (5) follow‐up duration less than 3 months after the initiation of combination therapy (n = 6). Finally, 67 patients with complete clinical, immunological and follow‐up data who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled for further analysis. Efficacy and safety

As of the last follow‐up on 1 January 2025, the median follow‐up period for the study cohort was 18.2 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 11.5–29.9 months). The median overall survival (mOS) was 30.4 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 18.5–34.9 months) and the median progression‐free survival (mPFS) was 11.0 months (95% CI: 8.6–12.9 months). At the initial treatment assessment, the ORR was 53.7%, and the DCR reached 86.6%. Treatment‐related AEs were generally tolerable and manageable, with no therapy‐associated deaths reported. Overall, 59.7% (40/67) of patients experienced AEs. The most frequently observed events included fever (56.7%), abdominal discomfort (46.3%), liver function abnormalities (19.4%), hand‐foot skin reaction (22.4%), hypertension (14.9%), proteinuria (16.4%), thrombocytopenia (9.0%) and a single case of immune‐related myocarditis (1.5%).

Dynamic changes in key immune markers during treatment

As shown in Figure 1, several humoral immune markers demonstrated significant dynamic changes across treatment cycles. The proportion of CD3+CD19+ B cells decreased significantly from Cycle 0 to Cycle 2 (P < 0.001) and Cycle 4 (P = 0.010). Igλ levels increased significantly from Cycle 0 to Cycle 2 (P = 0.001) and remained elevated at Cycle 4 (P = 0.017). Igκ levels showed a similar pattern, with significant increases from Cycle 0 to Cycle 2 (P = 0.001) and Cycle 4 (P = 0.014). IgA levels rose progressively during treatment, with significant increases observed at Cycle 2 (P = 0.001) and Cycle 4 (P = 0.045). IgG levels also increased significantly from Cycle 0 to Cycle 2 (P = 0.003) and Cycle 4 (P = 0.020). CFB levels were significantly higher at Cycle 4 than at Cycle 0 (P = 0.031), while no significant difference was observed between Cycle 0 and Cycle 2 (Figure 1).

Prognostic immune marker selection based on LASSO‐Cox regression analysis

In the static immune marker model (Figure 2a and b), LASSO‐Cox regression identified several variables with non‐zero coefficients, including CD3−CD19+ (%), Igκ (mg/dL), IgA (g/L), NLR, CD4+/CD8+ (%), C4 (g/L) and IgA at different time points. These candidate predictors and their corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and P‐values are shown in the forest plot (Figure 2e). Among these, NLR at Cycle 4 (HR = 1.10, P = 0.028), CD4+/CD8+ ratio at Cycle 4 (HR = 0.56, P = 0.036) and C4 at Cycle 4 (HR = 2.20, P = 0.009) were statistically significant. The Harrell's concordance index (C‐index) of the static model was 0.79.

Collinearity diagnosis analysis

To evaluate potential multicollinearity among the variables intended for inclusion in the stepwise multivariate Cox regression model [4 NLR, 4 CD4+/CD8+ (%), 4 C4 (g/L), Δ2 NLR and Δ2 CD3+CD4+ (%)], we conducted a collinearity diagnosis using the VIF. In this study, all variables had VIF values < 5, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity. These results confirmed the appropriateness of including these variables in the stepwise Cox regression analysis (Table 2).

Construction and validation of an immune‐based prognostic model

To identify the final prognostic factors and develop a clinically applicable risk prediction tool, we performed a stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis incorporating candidate variables identified from the LASSO‐Cox models. As shown in the regression table (Table 3), three variables were independently associated with OS: 4 CD4+/CD8+ (HR = 0.58, P = 0.035), relative change in NLR between Cycle 2 and Cycle 4 (Δ2 NLR; HR = 1.66, P = 0.014) and relative change in CD3+CD4+ over the same interval (Δ2 CD3+CD4+; HR = 0.04, P = 0.007). This model demonstrated good discriminative power, with a C‐index of 0.714.

Construction of a risk score model and survival stratification based on longitudinal immune indicators

To stratify patients based on immune‐related prognostic factors, a risk score was calculated for each individual using the coefficients derived from the stepwise multivariate Cox regression model. The formula was expressed as a weighted sum of the selected immune features, with each coefficient representing the natural logarithm of the corresponding hazard ratio (log HR). The formula was as follows: Risk Score=−0.458×Cycle4CD4+/CD8++0.611×ΔNLR−3.203×Δ2 CD3+CD4+.

The immune‐based risk score and nomogram were derived from the same stepwise multivariate Cox regression model, and thus reflect equivalent prognostic capacity through different formats. The distribution of risk scores ranged from −13.84 to 3.31, with a median of −1.27. Using the optimal cut‐off value (cut‐off = −0.1455) determined by the maximally selected rank statistics (implemented via the survminer package in R), patients were stratified into a high‐risk group (n = 14) and a low‐risk group (n = 53).

The distribution of the calculated risk scores is shown in Figure 4a and b, with a range from −13.84 to 3.31. The high‐risk group exhibited significantly higher risk scores (P < 0.001, Figure 4a) and poorer immune profiles (Figure 4d), including significantly higher Δ2 NLR (mean: 1.43 vs. 0.22, P = 0.011), markedly lower 4 CD4+/CD8+ ratio at Cycle 4 (mean: 1.13 vs. 1.73, P < 0.001), and a trend towards reduced Δ2 CD3+CD4+ levels (mean: 0.14 vs. 0.59, P = 0.054), consistent with an immunosuppressive state. The correlation heatmap (Figure 4e) demonstrated that the constructed risk score was negatively correlated with 4 CD4+/CD8+ (r = −0.64) and Δ2 CD3+CD4+ (r = −0.45), while positively correlated with ΔNLR (r = 0.50), validating the immunological relevance of the scoring system. The risk score showed a significant inverse correlation with OS time (Pearson r = −0.37, P = 0.002, Figure 4c), and displayed a clear clustering pattern in the heatmap of immune indicators and risk score sorted by survival status (Figure 4f), where survivors typically exhibited higher 4 CD4+/CD8+ ratios, lower Δ2 NLR and greater increases in Δ2 CD3+CD4+, while non‐survivors were associated with an opposite immunological profile and higher composite risk scores. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis confirmed the prognostic utility of the model: the high‐risk group had significantly shorter OS [median OS: 11.5 months (95% CI: 6.6–18.5 months) vs. 34.0 months (95% CI: 23.1–NR months), P < 0.001, Figure 4g] and PFS [median OS: 7.5 months (95% CI: 3.5–11.0 months) vs. 13.8 months (95% CI: 11.7–NR months), P = 0.006, Figure 4h] compared to the low‐risk group.

Prognostic stratification based on CD3 + CD4 + T‐cell dynamic patterns

Given that Δ2 CD3+CD4+ exhibited the most significant association with OS in multivariate Cox regression analysis (HR = 0.04096, 95% CI: 0.003958–0.4239, P = 0.007, Table 3), we selected CD3+CD4+ T‐cell dynamics as the basis for further stratification. To explore the prognostic relevance of its temporal patterns, unsupervised K‐means clustering was performed based on CD3+CD4+ T‐cell proportions across three treatment cycles (Cycle 0, Cycle 2 and Cycle 4). Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the dynamic changes in peripheral immune parameters at baseline and different treatment cycles in patients with HCC receiving TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs. By integrating longitudinal immune profiling with survival analyses, we demonstrated a close association between the temporal evolution of peripheral immune status and OS. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the immune system undergoes substantial remodelling during the course of combination therapy, and that the late‐stage immune status and its trajectory provide stronger prognostic information than baseline measurements, thereby underscoring the time‐dependent and dynamic value of peripheral immune monitoring.

We found that the immune status at the late treatment stage (Cycle 4) exhibited superior prognostic relevance compared to that at baseline (Cycle 0). This observation likely reflects the cumulative immunomodulatory effects of combination therapy over time, wherein the true capacity of an individual's immune system to respond to intervention becomes evident only during the mid‐to‐late phases of treatment. Immune parameters at baseline (Cycle 0) primarily represent the baseline immune milieu, which is often shaped by underlying chronic liver disease, viral infections, tumor burden and prior therapies. 17 As a result, this baseline profile tends to reflect an immunosuppressive state and may not reliably predict immunotherapeutic responsiveness. In contrast, as treatment progresses, TACE induces tumor antigen release, ICIs alleviate T‐cell inhibition and anti‐VEGF agents remodel tumor vasculature to enhance immune cell infiltration, collectively driving a transition from immune suppression to activation and eventual reconstruction. 18 This immunologic transformation stabilises over several treatment cycles, and the immune profile captured at Cycle 4 likely represents a new immune equilibrium established under therapeutic pressure. While the initial immune state may indicate potential for response, it fails to reflect the durability and quality of subsequent immune activation, particularly in HCC, a tumor characterised by profound immunological heterogeneity. 19 , 20 Therefore, the late‐phase immune signature, as represented by Cycle 4, provides a more accurate reflection of systemic immune reconstitution and serves as a more robust foundation for prognostic modelling.

At this treatment stage, we observed that the increase of the CD4+/CD8+ ratio was strongly associated with improved prognosis. This ratio, a well‐recognised indicator of T‐cell subset balance and functional reconstitution, has been validated in multiple solid tumors as a surrogate marker of immune responsiveness to therapy. 21 , 22 An elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratio generally reflects enhanced activity of CD4+ helper T cells, which not only play a pivotal role in initiating antigen‐specific immune responses but also contribute to sustained anti‐tumor immunity by promoting dendritic cell maturation, secreting cytokines, such as IL‐2 and IFN‐γ, and maintaining the memory phenotype of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. 23 , 24 In the context of HCC, chronic inflammation and immune evasion mechanisms often result in CD4+ T‐cell dysfunction or depletion; thus, an increased CD4+/CD8+ ratio following combination therapy may serve as a key immunological indicator of successful reversal of immunosuppression and effective immune reconstitution. 25 In parallel, the dynamic change in NLR from Cycle 2 to Cycle 4 also demonstrated significant prognostic relevance. NLR is an integrative marker of systemic inflammation and immune competence; an increasing NLR often reflects a predominance of neutrophil‐mediated pro‐inflammatory responses, while a concurrent reduction in lymphocyte counts signals impaired anti‐tumor immunity. 26 , 27 This imbalance may represent a shift towards an inflammation‐driven, immune‐failure trajectory that facilitates tumor progression and immune escape. By focusing on the temporal trend of NLR rather than static measurements alone, our model offers a more refined understanding of immunological dynamics during treatment. This approach enhances the ability to distinguish between true immune non‐responders and patients with pseudo‐progression, thereby improving the interpretability of immune‐based biomarkers in clinical decision‐making.

In the further analysis of dynamic changes in CD3+CD4+ T cells, we found that patients with a sustained increase in peripheral CD4+ T‐cell levels exhibited significantly longer OS. This finding suggests that CD4+ T cells may play a critical immunoregulatory role in the context of TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF therapy. Previous studies have shown that CD4+ T cells enhance anti‐tumor immunity by activating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, promoting the secretion of effector molecules, such as granzyme B and perforin, and supporting the maintenance of cytotoxic function. 28 , 29 , 30 They also secrete IFN‐γ to enhance the activity of antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) and upregulate MHC class I and II expression, thereby improving tumor antigen recognition. 31 , 32 Additionally, CD4+ T cells contribute to the clonal expansion and sustained activation of CD8+ T cells through IL‐2 secretion, preventing their exhaustion. 33 Conversely, a continuous decline in CD4+ T cells may indicate effector T‐cell depletion, insufficient antigenic stimulation or the activation of immune escape mechanisms, such as increased regulatory T cells (Tregs) or persistent inhibitory signalling through pathways like PD‐1 and CTLA‐4, all of which can impair the efficacy of immunotherapy. 34 , 35

Notably, in this study, both an elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratio at Cycle 4 and a sustained increase in CD3+CD4+ T cells were associated with favorable prognosis. Although derived from different analytical dimensions, these two indicators are not contradictory; instead, they reflect complementary aspects of immune system remodelling at both structural and functional levels. The CD4+/CD8+ ratio serves as a surrogate marker of systemic immune balance and T‐cell subset coordination. 36 Its elevation often indicates a relative increase in helper T‐cell populations, resolution of immunosuppression and restoration of immune homeostasis, which may support the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells and delay immune exhaustion. 37 , 38 Meanwhile, a progressive increase in CD3+CD4+ T cells suggests enhanced functional activation and sustained immune responsiveness, reflecting a durable immune engagement during the later phase of treatment. 39 Their combined association with improved outcomes underlines the pivotal role of CD4+ T cells not only in quantitative reconstitution but also in the functional orchestration of anti‐tumor immunity. 40 , 41

In the context of clinical application, the dynamic immune indicators identified in this study can serve as valuable tools for early assessment of treatment response and real‐time monitoring of immune dynamics, enabling clinicians to track immunological trends throughout therapy and optimise clinical decision‐making accordingly. Conventional efficacy evaluations, often based on imaging findings or tumor markers, such as AFP, are inherently delayed and do not directly reflect the actual immune response. In contrast, dynamic immune parameters, such as changes in CD3+CD4+ T‐cell counts, CD4+/CD8+ ratio and trends in NLR ratio, offer a forward‐looking biological window that can help predict treatment outcomes even before radiological progression becomes evident, thereby facilitating proactive intervention. Particular attention should be paid to patients who exhibit persistently declining CD4+ T‐cell levels during treatment, as this may reflect underlying factors, such as excessive immune suppression, gut microbiota dysregulation or chronic viral co‐infection. 42 , 43 These immune‐compromised patients may benefit from personalised immunomodulatory strategies, including supplementation with cytokines, such as IL‐7 or IL‐15, use of metabolic modulators or therapeutic agents targeting immune exhaustion pathways to restore immune competence. 44 , 45 In addition, modifying the timing and schedule of immunotherapy, for example, shortening dosing intervals, extending the duration of ICIs, or optimising the sequence and timing of TACE, may further enhance therapeutic synergy by aligning treatment with the dynamics of CD4+ T‐cell recovery. 46 The trajectory of CD4+ T‐cell changes also offers a rational framework for tailoring follow‐up schedules and defining re‐evaluation time points in clinical practice.

For patients presenting with immunosuppressive profiles during treatment, such as persistently declining CD4+ T cells or rising NLR, this may suggest the emergence of immune escape mechanisms, dominance of chronic inflammatory responses or progression towards T‐cell exhaustion. In such cases, additional immune‐enhancing strategies could be considered, including IL‐2 or IL‐7 support, metabolic intervention or therapies targeting key immune‐regulatory checkpoints. 47 For those with increased systemic inflammation, concurrent anti‐inflammatory approaches (e.g. low‐dose corticosteroids or IL‐6 receptor antagonists) may be necessary to avoid progression to inflammation‐driven immune dysfunction. 48 Furthermore, for patients with underlying dysbiosis or chronic infections, adjunctive strategies such as microbiota modulation or antiviral therapy may help to restore a favorable immune microenvironment from the source. 42 , 43 Conversely, patients who demonstrate sustained immune activation, such as a consistent increase in CD3+CD4+ T cells, recovery of CD4+/CD8+ ratio and a decrease in NLR, are likely to have entered a post‐treatment immune re‐equilibration phase. These patients may be highly responsive to current combination therapy and could potentially benefit from treatment de‐intensification strategies, such as extending treatment intervals, shortening the TACE cycle or cautiously reducing ICI dosing, in order to minimise immune‐related AEs while preserving therapeutic efficacy. This type of immune response‐guided adaptive treatment strategy represents a key step towards the precision management of HCC patients undergoing interventional‐immunotherapy combinations.

This study has several limitations. First, it was based on a single‐centre cohort with a relatively limited sample size, and most enrolled patients had HBV‐related HCC, which may affect the generalisability of the findings. In addition, because this study focused on longitudinal immune dynamics, complete and time‐standardised peripheral immune data at Cycle 0, Cycle 2 and Cycle 4 were required. Therefore, patients with missing blood samples at one or more predefined time points or samples collected outside the predefined time window were excluded, which may have introduced potential selection bias related to retrospective data availability. External validation in larger, multi‐centre prospective cohorts is needed to confirm the robustness and generalisability of the risk model and the stability of the data‐derived risk score cut‐off. The cut‐off value of −0.1455 was determined from the present cohort and should therefore be considered exploratory rather than a definitive clinical threshold. Second, peripheral immune markers, while convenient and minimally invasive, may not fully represent the immune activity within the tumor microenvironment. Future studies incorporating tumor tissue analysis would help validate the correlation between systemic and local immune responses. Third, although key time‐dependent immune indicators were identified, the mechanistic basis underlying these dynamic changes remains to be clarified. Further research using multi‐omics and single‐cell approaches may provide deeper insight into the immune remodelling process during combination therapy.

In conclusion, this study, conducted in the clinical context of TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs, systematically revealed the dynamic evolution of peripheral immune status and its significant association with prognosis in patients with HBV‐related unresectable HCC. It highlights the time‐dependent nature and prognostic relevance of dynamic immune monitoring during treatment. By integrating multi‐timepoint immune data, we developed a dynamic risk scoring model and a visual prognostic tool that may facilitate refined risk stratification and individualised prognostic assessment in this patient population, although further validation is still needed before broader clinical application.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee. A portion of the patients included in this study has been reported previously; however, the present analysis addresses a distinct research objective and outcomes under the same ethics approval. Given its retrospective nature, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Between 1 July 2019 and 1 July 2024, patients diagnosed with unresectable HCC (uHCC) who received TACE in combination with ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HCC diagnosis confirmed histologically or clinically in accordance with the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines; (2) Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C; (3) liver function classified as Child‐Pugh A or B, without uncontrolled ascites or hepatic encephalopathy; (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1; and (5) treatment with a combination regimen, including TACE, ICIs and anti‐VEGF agents or TKIs.

Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) diagnosis of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), combined hepatocellular‐cholangiocarcinoma, sarcomatoid HCC or fibrolamellar HCC; (2) coexistence of other malignancies aside from HCC; (3) prior exposure to any systemic anti‐cancer therapy for HCC before initiation of the index TACE plus ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs regimen (including, but not limited to, ICIs, anti‐VEGF therapies, TKIs); (4) missing hematologic or immunologic data that prevented evaluation of clinical outcomes or longitudinal immune dynamics; and (5) a follow‐up period of < 3 months following the initiation of combination treatment.

Peripheral immune markers

Immune profiling included the assessment of lymphocyte subsets, specifically the percentages of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ helper T cells, CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, CD3−CD16+56+ natural killer (NK) cells and CD3−CD19+ B cells, along with the CD4+/CD8+ ratio. Additional immunological parameters measured included immunoglobulin λ (Igλ), immunoglobulin κ (Igκ), complement components C3 and C4, immunoglobulin classes M (IgM), A (IgA) and G (IgG), as well as complement factor B (CFB).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via density gradient centrifugation using the Ficoll–Paque method and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Flow cytometric analysis of PBMCs was performed using a FACScan Caliber system (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Serum immunoglobulin levels and complement components were quantified using the immune turbidimetric assay, while NLR was calculated from routine complete blood count results.

Study design

This study was designed to explore the prognostic value of dynamic peripheral immune markers in patients with uHCC receiving combination therapy with TACE, ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies or TKIs. Peripheral blood samples were collected at three predefined treatment stages: baseline (Cycle 0), second treatment cycle (Cycle 2) and fourth treatment cycle (Cycle 4). Follow‐up and data collection

The primary outcome was OS, while secondary outcomes included progression‐free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR). Following the initiation of combination therapy, patients underwent follow‐up assessments every 6–9 weeks. These evaluations included imaging studies, such as contrast‐enhanced abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and non‐contrast chest‐abdomen computed tomography (CT) scans, as well as laboratory analyses, including complete blood counts, comprehensive biochemical profiles, tumor marker measurements, HBV‐DNA quantification, cytokine profiling, immune status assessments, chest pain panels and thyroid function tests. ICIs were administered at 3‐week intervals, with pre‐treatment laboratory evaluations conducted prior to each cycle, covering hematologic parameters, biochemical tests, immune indices, chest pain panels and thyroid function assessments. Tumor response was assessed according to mRECIST 49 using contrast‐enhanced dynamic liver MRI or multiphasic contrast‐enhanced liver CT by two experienced radiologists, with discrepant cases resolved by consensus. The baseline, second and fourth cycles were referred to as Cycle 0, Cycle 2 and Cycle 4, respectively. Immune and biochemical data were collected at Cycle 0, Cycle 2, Cycle 4, the first radiological evaluation time point, and, when applicable, at progression disease (PD), for subsequent longitudinal analyses.

TACE procedure

All TACE procedures were performed by two senior interventional radiologists, each with over a decade of clinical experience. To reduce inter‐operator variability and improve embolisation precision, a standardised precision TACE protocol was implemented. 50 Subsequent TACE treatments (typically evaluated after three to four treatment cycles) were administered on a demand‐driven basis, depending on changes in tumor markers and imaging evaluations. TACE was discontinued if any of the following conditions were met: (1) progression to Child‐Pugh class C, accompanied by complications, such as refractory ascites, significant jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy or hepatorenal syndrome; (2) an ECOG performance status score exceeding 2; or (3) continuous progression of target lesions after three consecutive TACE sessions, as determined by the Mrecist. In patients with extrahepatic spread or macrovascular invasion, TACE was used with liver‐directed palliative intent as part of combination therapy to control intrahepatic tumor burden and support ongoing systemic treatment. Selection was based on multidisciplinary evaluation within the Chinese guideline framework, with priority given to patients with liver‐dominant disease, Child‐Pugh A/B liver function, ECOG 0–2 and adequate procedural feasibility.

ICIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies/TKIs administration

Patients included in this study received guideline‐based combination therapy consisting of an ICI (camrelizumab or sintilimab) plus either bevacizumab or a TKI (lenvatinib or sorafenib). These systemic agents were all approved by the National Medical Products Administration and were routinely available in China. Treatment choice and sequencing were guided by standard national guideline recommendations and routine clinical protocols, and were further individualised based on multidisciplinary team discussion or physician judgement in real‐world clinical practice. To ensure procedural consistency, all TACE procedures were performed using a standardised precise/superselective approach. 49 In patients treated with oral TKIs, dosing was temporarily withheld for 2–3 days before and after each TACE procedure to minimise the risk of TACE‐related complications. All medications were administered at standard dosages and intervals, in accordance with national treatment guidelines and institutional protocols. The combined regimen of ICIs and anti‐angiogenic agents was continued until radiologic confirmation of disease progression or the development of intolerable adverse effects. Therefore, although some regimen variation existed, the overall treatment framework was relatively consistent and guideline‐based, consisting of TACE combined with ICIs and anti‐angiogenic agents/TKIs.

Molecular targeted agents, including TKIs and anti‐VEGF antibodies, were administered at their recommended standard doses and schedules. Oral TKIs were temporarily withheld for 2 days before and after each TACE procedure if no significant post‐procedural symptoms were observed. Dose adjustments for TKIs and anti‐VEGF agents were allowed based on product guidelines and clinical judgement to manage treatment‐related adverse events (AEs). ICIs were given at fixed doses and intervals without dose reductions. Temporary suspension of ICIs was permitted for immune‐related AEs of grade ≥ 2 and resumed once toxicity resolved to grade ≤ 1, with a maximum interruption duration of 12 weeks. TKIs were paused in cases of grade ≥ 2 AEs, such as hypertension, proteinuria or hand‐foot skin reactions, and reintroduced at either the original or a reduced dose upon recovery. All treatment modifications adhered to established protocols and were individualised according to patient tolerance and organ function. AEs were classified and graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. 51

Relevant definitions

The criteria for initiating combination therapy were defined as the administration of the first dose of ICIs either concurrently with the initial TACE procedure or within a 1‐month interval. TKIs were required to be administered concurrently with either TACE or ICIs. Patients were required to receive a minimum of four cycles of ICI therapy (with one cycle defined as a 3‐week interval) following the initial TACE session. Survival data were updated through January 2025. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause or the last follow‐up, while PFS was defined as the duration from diagnosis to either documented disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. Treatment response was evaluated according to the mRECIST at the fourth treatment cycle or approximately 3 months after therapy initiation. This first radiologic assessment typically occurred at the third‐month mark or the fourth ICI cycle. Based on the initial treatment response, patients were stratified into PD, stable disease (SD) or objective response (OR) groups, the latter including complete response (CR) and partial response (PR). The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, while the DCR was defined as the proportion of patients with CR, PR or SD. All enrolled HCC patients had chronic HBV infection as the underlying aetiology. Clinically meaningful HBV activation was defined as a serum HBV DNA level exceeding 60 IU/mL in the presence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity. 52 All enrolled patients had HBV‐related HCC as the underlying aetiology, reflecting the predominant etiologic pattern observed in routine clinical practice at our centre.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U‐test, as appropriate. Paired longitudinal continuous variables were compared using the paired Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test. LASSO‐Cox regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation was used to identify immune markers associated with OS, followed by stepwise multivariate Cox regression to identify independent prognostic factors. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A nomogram and immune risk score model were constructed based on the selected variables. The optimal cut‐off value for the risk score was determined using maximally selected rank statistics via the surv_cutpoint () function in the R package survminer with OS time and status as the response variables. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log‐rank test. Time‐dependent ROC curves, calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate model performance. All tests were two‐sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
