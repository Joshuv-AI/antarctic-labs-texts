The impact of economic complexity on green technology innovation in China
Authors: Xiang Ruan, Shanlang Lin, Junpei Huang, Chenghan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Green technology innovation (GTI) plays a pivotal role in mitigating global climate change and advancing environmental sustainability. Economic complexity, which reveals the diversity and sophistication of economic structures, provides a novel analytical perspective for improving the GTI. This research explores the potential influence of economic complexity on GTI advancement. This study constructs a dual fixed effects model to empirically analyze the influence of economic complexity on GTI, using panel data from 288 Chinese cities over the period 2000–2016. The results show that economic complexity significantly promotes GTI. On average, a 1% increase in economic complexity leads to a 1.84% increase in GTI. In addition, the impact of economic complexity is heterogeneous, with cities in less developed regions and central-western areas deriving greater marginal benefits from economic complexity growth. Additionally, we find that economic complexity can promote GTI through knowledge spillover and industrial structure upgrading. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the increase in economic complexity drives the “quantity-and-quality improvement” of GTI. Finally, using data sourced from China’s A-share listed companies, we provide micro-level evidence of the connection between economic complexity and the GTI.

As the world grapples with increasingly severe environmental challenges, such as climate change, resource depletion, and ecological degradation, the pursuit of sustainable growth has garnered heightened attention from governments and international organizations1. Green technology innovation (GTI) has become a vital approach to promoting sustainable development2. Consequently, many countries have introduced policies that not only encourage sustainability through enhanced GTI but also impose stricter environmental regulations, which are found to significantly stimulate innovation among highly polluting enterprises3–5. Green technologies not only help reduce environmental pollution and greenhouse gas emissions but also contribute to economic growth and social well-being6. The core of GTI lies in addressing the environmental issues arising from current production and consumption patterns, thereby promoting sustainable economic growth7. As the world’s second-largest economy, China’s progress in GTI is pivotal for its environmental sustainability and holds significant implications for global environmental protection8.

Relying on a single or overly simple economic model may not be sufficient to address the increasingly complex global challenges we face and effectively advance sustainable development. Instead, a more complex and diversified economic structure is necessary to support the innovation and implementation of new technologies9. As a significant application of scale-free network theory in economics, economic complexity theory provides an important tool for analyzing economic structures, identifying key technical nodes, and understanding economic system dynamics10. The latest advancements in complex network science and the data revolution have enriched the theory of economic complexity.

Economic complexity underscores the role of technology, skills, and knowledge in the functioning and growth of economic systems11. It reflects the sophistication of production structure and the diversity of the products12. Higher economic complexity indicates a wider range of high-value-added and technology-intensive goods that a country produces and exports globally. This centrality in the global knowledge network enables the country to access, utilize, and innovate with knowledge more effectively13. Nations that exhibit greater economic complexity typically possess more advanced and diversified industrial structures, which facilitate knowledge flow and technological convergence across sectors, thereby stimulating new innovations. Such complexity is often linked to advanced technological knowledge and innovative capabilities2. Complex products, which integrate extensive technologies, skills, and knowledge processes, drive economic growth and GTI through their scalability and extensibility.

Although economic complexity and GTI have garnered substantial attention individually14,15, the GTI effect of economic complexity remains underexplored. To address this gap, this study investigates the causal relationship between economic complexity and GTI. Using panel data from 288 prefecture-level cities in China covering the period 2000–2016, we construct the economic complexity index based on the fitness and complexity algorithm and employ a dual fixed-effects model for estimation. The results show that economic complexity significantly promotes GTI, with its positive effect being stronger in regions with lower levels of economic development and in central and western areas. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that economic complexity fosters GTI through two main mechanisms: knowledge spillovers and industrial structure upgrading. Finally, we extend the analysis to the enterprise level and confirm that economic complexity also promotes green innovation among firms, thereby providing solid micro-level evidence in support of our findings.

This paper makes the following marginal contributions. First, from the research perspective, prior studies on economic complexity have concentrated on green economic growth9, energy efficiency16, greenhouse gas emissions17, and green development efficiency13. However, the relationship between economic complexity and GTI has remained underexplored. This paper fills this gap by identifying the causal effect of economic complexity on GTI, thus extending the application of economic complexity theory and offering a new perspective for understanding the drivers of GTI. Second, from the data perspective, most existing research has measured economic complexity at the national or provincial level13,18. This paper employs city-level data from China, which enables a more detailed analysis of regional differences in the effect of economic complexity. This urban-level research enriches empirical evidence and provides a theoretical basis for developing regionally differentiated policies for GTI. Third, from the mechanism perspective, this study integrates economic complexity, GTI, knowledge spillovers, and industrial structure upgrading into a unified explanatory framework. Specifically, it demonstrates that economic complexity fosters GTI through two key channels: knowledge spillover and industrial structure upgrading. This mechanism-based analysis advances theoretical understanding and highlights the pathways through which economic complexity contributes to sustainable development. Fourth, from the evaluation perspective, this paper examines both the quantity and quality of GTI. This dual focus allows for a more comprehensive assessment of innovation performance and offers policymakers richer reference indicators to support the promotion of high-quality GTI.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

Economic complexity and green technology innovation

Economic complexity can be conceptualized as an application of scale-free networks9 that foster the innovation and implementation of green technologies through key characteristics such as node connectivity, network structure, centrality, and modularity. In such networks, the high connectivity of nodes improves the efficiency of information dissemination and the breadth of knowledge flow, thereby accelerating interdisciplinary technological development and collaboration19. The network’s connectivity, clustering, and small-world effects further expedite the diffusion, adoption, and commercialization of green technologies20. Strategic nodes, such as large corporations, key research institutions, and government agencies, play a pivotal role in the diffusion of green technologies due to their influence, thereby facilitating technology transfer and innovation21. Moreover, the modular structure of the network creates closely interconnected sub-networks that accelerate innovation within specific fields or regions by enabling resource integration22.

Economic complexity directly promotes GTI through the diversity and complementarity of technological capabilities. Complex economies possess a wide range of interrelated technologies, which allow firms to explore multiple innovation pathways and develop environmentally friendly solutions11,23. Firms located at the core of global value chains can leverage their technological reserves and ability to integrate global resources to transform complex technologies into practical green innovations9. This internal diversity enables more effective collaborative research, technology transfer, and academic exchange24. Furthermore, economic complexity facilitates the adoption of environmentally friendly production techniques25, supporting the transition from energy-intensive to knowledge-intensive economies26 and establishing a robust foundation for sustainable development. By providing multiple technological combinations and adaptive innovation paths, economic complexity enhances the efficiency and scope of green technology development.

Based on this, we put forward the following research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Economic complexity can enhance GTI.

Economic complexity, knowledge spillover and green technology innovation

Economic complexity reflects the high complementarity of economies in terms of capabilities and knowledge, providing enterprises with a multi-dimensional learning and interactive environment. Industrial adjacency enables enterprises to gain experience and inspiration from related industries, reducing the cost of knowledge absorption10. The mobility of talents promotes the diffusion of technological capabilities among enterprises and achieves optimal allocation27. The cooperation among industry, academia and research brings cutting-edge research and development achievements and practical guidance to enterprises, promoting the effective application of knowledge among different entities9. Shared production facilities and R&D resources further support enterprises in integrating external information and applying it to innovation. In complex economies, a high level of capability integration enables enterprises to absorb, apply and combine external knowledge more efficiently, thereby forming a continuous and extensive knowledge diffusion and laying a solid foundation for innovation activities2,18.

Knowledge spillover enhances GTI by reducing firms’ R&D risks and uncertainty, enabling them to allocate resources more efficiently to green projects9,28. It strengthens absorptive capacity, allowing enterprises to internalize external expertise and transform it into practical eco-innovations. Knowledge spillover also promotes cross-sectoral knowledge recombination, generating breakthroughs in cleaner production processes and renewable substitutes7,16. In addition, it accelerates the formation of shared technical standards, which lowers diffusion costs and facilitates the large-scale adoption of green technologies29. Through these focused pathways, knowledge spillover not only speeds up the innovation process but also deepens the quality and sustainability of GTI.

Based on this, we put forward the following research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Economic complexity promotes GTI by facilitating knowledge spillover.

Economic complexity, industrial structure upgrading and green technology innovation

Economic complexity enhances a country’s diversity and specialization at the industrial level, enabling the economic system to accumulate a rich combination of knowledge and skills and driving industries to continuously rise along the value chain9,10. Economic complexity makes it easier to integrate and transform knowledge and technologies among industries, creating conditions for the development of emerging industries9. A complex economy not only enhances the technological content of traditional industries but also accelerates the growth of high-tech industries and service sectors2,18. During the process of industrial upgrading, resources are constantly flowing from low-efficiency sectors to high-efficiency ones, and a complex industrial structure can accelerate this adjustment. The cumulative effect of knowledge enables economic complexity to provide continuous impetus for industrial upgrading and lay a solid foundation for GTI.

Industrial structure upgrading promotes GTI by reshaping the composition of industries and reallocating resources9. The rise of high-tech industries and advanced manufacturing increases the demand for energy-saving production and clean technologies, directly stimulating firms’ green R&D activities. The expansion of the service sector strengthens knowledge-intensive activities, which provide broader application scenarios for new green technologies30. The concentration of capital, technology, and skilled labor in advanced sectors raises innovation efficiency and accelerates the commercialization of green solutions31. Industrial upgrading also improves the market acceptance of green products, creating favorable conditions for the large-scale diffusion of sustainable technologies7. Overall, the optimization of industrial structure not only accelerates the pace of GTI but also enhances its scale and sustainability.

Based on this, we put forward the following research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Economic complexity promotes GTI by facilitating industrial structure upgrading.

Variables

Explained variable

In this research, GTI was adopted as the explained variable and was measured by the log-transformed number of green patent applications at the prefecture-city level. Green patents provide a concrete and measurable reflection of innovative activities aimed at environmental protection, resource efficiency, and sustainable development. Unlike general R&D inputs, which may not directly translate into technological breakthroughs, patent applications capture the actual output of inventive efforts with a clear technological orientation34. Specifically, green patents record novel technical solutions with explicit environmental benefits, thereby serving as a reliable proxy for GTI. Prior studies have also recognized patent statistics as robust indicators of GTI8,35,36, and focusing on green patent applications allows this study to assess both the intensity and the environmental orientation of regional innovation activities.

Green patents in this study are screened and extracted from the National Intellectual Property Administration database in accordance with the International Patent Classification (IPC) Green List codes developed by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) in 2010. The IPC Green Catalogue classifies patents related to environmentally friendly technologies into seven major categories, including alternative energy production, transportation, energy conservation, waste management, agriculture and forestry, administration/regulation/design, and nuclear power generation, each with detailed classification codes. Compared with granted patents, patent applications are used here as they provide a more timely and sensitive measure of innovation dynamics9. The approval process for patent grants is often lengthy and influenced by administrative or political factors, which may reduce their stability as indicators. Moreover, patent applications can already reflect the direction and intensity of technological innovation, as many innovations start to influence markets and policy discussions once the application is filed, well before formal approval.

Core explanatory variable

The core explanatory variable in this research is economic complexity indicator (ECI). The existing literature identifies two primary measures: the Method of Reflection and the Fitness and Complexity algorithm. Hidalgo and Hausmann11 proposed the Method of Reflection, which quantifies economic complexity based on the average complexity of products within a region’s export basket. However, due to the averaging process, the measures derived from the Method of Reflection lose critical information regarding regional diversification and product ubiquity37. Additionally, it underestimates the significance of highly diversified economies38.

Higher economic complexity indicates that a country is able to produce more complex goods than only a few countries can produce, which is usually associated with higher levels of knowledge intensity and technology17. The FC algorithm proposed by Tacchella, et al39. more accurately captures the essence of economic complexity. Figure 3 shows that from 2000 to 2016, the overall value of China’s economic complexity rose significantly, indicating that more and more cities have become complex in terms of industrial structure and knowledge capacity9. In 2000, economic complexity was mainly concentrated in a few developed coastal cities. By 2016, the number of cities with higher complexity had increased and spread inland. Most western cities remained light-colored and had lower complexity in 201613. One possible reason is that the Yangtze River Delta, the Pearl River Delta and the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region have always been high-value areas of economic complexity. These regions have developed manufacturing and service industry clusters, complete industrial chains, and strong spillover effects of foreign capital and technology, which have driven the accumulation of complexity. In the western region, the reason is that the industries are mainly resource-based and primary processing-based, lacking high-end manufacturing and innovation networks. By 2016, the gap between the high-value area in the east and the low-value area in the west had become more obvious. It indicates that complexity, under the path dependence of “the strong getting stronger”, is concentrated in a few core areas.

Mechanism variables

Knowledge spillover (KS). Within the research framework of this paper, knowledge spillover is regarded as a key mechanism through which economic complexity drives GTI. Cities with higher levels of economic complexity tend to concentrate a greater variety and depth of knowledge resources. As the core vehicles for knowledge creation and dissemination, universities can promote knowledge diffusion through talent cultivation, the commercialization of research outcomes, and industry-academia-research collaboration. Therefore, this paper selects the number of general higher education institutions in a city as the mechanism variable to measure the level of knowledge spillover at the city level. On the one hand, a greater number of universities indicates a higher potential for knowledge production and accumulation within a region, facilitating diverse knowledge exchange and cross-application of technologies. On the other hand, interactions between universities, businesses, and governments generate a continuous flow of knowledge spillover effects, thereby playing a crucial role in transmitting the relationship between economic complexity and GTI.

Industrial structure upgrading (ISU). The greater the proportion of industries with higher labor productivity, the higher the level of industrial structure9. Accordingly, we measure ISU as the weighted product of the proportional share of each industry and its corresponding labor productivity30. Control variables

To account for additional factors influencing GTI beyond economic complexity, this research incorporates a series of control variables into the regression model:

(1) Economic development level (EDL). Per capita GDP serves as a key indicator of a region’s economic development level. A higher level of economic development tends to create a more favorable environment for GTI40, thereby facilitating its advancement. In this study, the logarithm of per capita GDP is employed to measure the level of economic development.

(2) Population density (POP). China’s rapidly growing population has contributed to environmental pollution, and as population size continues to expand, pollution is expected to intensify. This escalating environmental challenge generates an urgent demand for green technologies, thereby fostering GTI40. In this research, population density is quantified as the number of individuals per square kilometer.

(3) Share of the tertiary industry (IND). Existing studies have established a link between the agglomeration of productive service industries and technological innovation41. Research indicates that while diversified agglomeration within the tertiary sector enhances both local and neighboring GTI, specialized agglomeration primarily benefits local GTI. In this research, the share of the tertiary industry is calculated as the ratio of value added by the tertiary sector to GDP.

(4) Foreign direct investment (FDI). As China has become more open to the global economy, substantial inflows of foreign enterprises, capital, and technology have significantly influenced local GTI, industrial structure, and economic growth42. The “pollution halo” theory suggests that foreign investment enhances local GTI through demonstration, spillover, and competition effects, thereby improving environmental quality. In this research, the total amount of actual foreign investment is used to measure openness to the global economy.

(5) Environmental governance (EG). Environmental infrastructure investment, industrial pollution control, and compliance with the “three simultaneous” policy significantly impact GTI43. This research focuses on environmental pollution control investments in prefecture-level cities, using the total investment in pollution control as a proxy for environmental governance.

(6) R&D investment (RD). R&D investment plays a crucial role in driving GTI in China, fostering enterprise productivity while mitigating the financial constraints associated with the R&D process44. This research quantifies R&D investment by calculating the share of science and technology expenditure as a proportion of total fiscal expenditure.

(7) Human capital (HR). Human capital plays a pivotal role in technological advancement and regional innovation. Talent quality directly influences the depth and scope of innovation, while the accumulation of human capital serves as a key driver of GTI development45. This research employs the number of college students per ten thousand people as a proxy for human capital.

Does economic complexity enhance GTI?

Baseline regression results

Table 2 presents the baseline regression results, with GTI as the dependent variable and economic complexity (ECI) as the core explanatory variable. Across all model specifications, ECI consistently shows a significant positive effect on GTI at the 1% level. This finding suggests that a more complex economic structure, characterized by diversified production capabilities and sophisticated industrial linkages, is conducive to fostering GTI. From an economic perspective, regions with higher economic complexity tend to accumulate more tacit knowledge and technological capabilities, which lowers the marginal cost of green innovation and facilitates knowledge spillover across industries. These results are in line with existing literature emphasizing that economic complexity not only reflects industrial upgrading and diversification but also serves as an important driver of sustainable innovation, thereby reinforcing regional GTI capacity9,18.

The control variables also demonstrate economically meaningful results. Higher levels of economic development (EDL) and population density (POP) provide broader market demand and innovation resources, which can stimulate the development of green technologies. The positive coefficient for the proportion of the tertiary industry (IND) indicates that service-oriented economic transformation supports GTI by enhancing knowledge-intensive activities. Foreign direct investment (FDI) significantly promotes GTI, consistent with the view that international capital brings advanced technologies and management experience that diffuse locally. R&D investment (RD) and human capital (HR) show strong positive effects, confirming that innovation inputs and skilled labor are fundamental drivers of green innovation. Moreover, the positive influence of environmental governance (EG) suggests that stricter environmental policies and regulatory investments not only do not crowd out innovation, but instead create incentives for firms to pursue GTI.

Robustness tests

Replace the explained variable

In the baseline regression, the number of green patent applications serves as a proxy for the explained variable. This metric reflects total technology submissions accepted by patent offices, with higher application counts indicating greater innovation activity. However, not all applied-for patents are granted, leading to time lags and discrepancies between applications and approvals. To address this, the robustness test employs the number of granted green patents as an alternative GTI measure to reassess the coefficients. The results, presented in columns (1)-(2) of Table 3, show that the coefficient of economic complexity remains significantly positive at the 1% confidence level, aligning with the findings of the baseline regression. Moreover, the coefficient for the number of granted green patents is 1.94, which surpasses its effect on green patent applications. Given that the patent review process is highly rigorous—typically requiring 1 to 2 years for approval, with not all applications ultimately granted—these findings suggest that economic complexity has a stronger influence on the outcomes of GTI than on the initial innovation process. This further reinforces the crucial role of economic complexity in shaping GTI capacity.

Replace the core explanatory variable

To examine the robustness of our findings, we converted the continuous economic complexity variable into a discrete variable by creating a dummy indicator for high economic complexity (ECI_d), defined based on its mean value. The corresponding results are displayed in columns (3) and (4) of Table 3. The findings reveal that the coefficient of the economic complexity dummy remains significantly positive at the 1% confidence level, consistent with the baseline regression results. This confirms the robustness of our main conclusions.

Endogeneity

This research may encounter endogeneity issues that present challenges for identification. First, while economic complexity influences GTI, the increase in product diversity resulting from higher degrees of green innovation may, in turn, affect economic complexity, that is, there is a reverse causality issue. Second, there are many factors that influence GTI, and there may be some factors that we cannot capture and cannot be included in the model, leading to the problem of omitted variables. This research utilizes the instrumental variables approach to solve the potential endogeneity issue. Two instrumental variables are employed. The first is the predicted ECI for the period 2006–2016, obtained using the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model trained on data from 2000 to 2005 (ECI_PRE). The application of a LSTM model to construct instrumental variables offers notable advantages in addressing endogeneity concerns46. The advantage of using LSTM is that it can effectively capture complex time dependencies and nonlinear dynamics in the historical evolution of economic complexity, thereby generating a more accurate prediction of the subsequent ECI series. This predicted measure is strongly correlated with the actual ECI but is exogenous to current green technology innovation activities, making it a suitable instrument.

This study employs the LSTM model, a type of recurrent neural network specifically designed to handle sequential data. Unlike traditional models that often suffer from vanishing gradients, LSTM incorporates a gating mechanism consisting of forget, input, and output gates that regulate the flow of information. This design enables the model to selectively retain relevant signals from past periods while discarding noise or less important information, thus capturing both short-term fluctuations and long-term dependencies in economic complexity data. As a result, LSTM is particularly effective in producing reliable predictions of ECI over time. The architecture of the LSTM network applied in this study is presented in Fig. 4.

Historically, railway density shaped regional industrial layouts and trade flows, which are strongly correlated with the formation of economic complexity. However, given the long time span since 1962, it is implausible that this historical measure directly affects today’s GTI outcomes. GTI is driven primarily by current technological capabilities, policy incentives, and market demand for environmental sustainability, none of which can be reasonably traced back to the initial distribution of railways more than half a century ago. Therefore, the railway density variable influences GTI only indirectly, through its impact on the evolution of economic complexity, satisfying the exclusion restriction requirement for an instrumental variable. To address data consistency, it is important to note that the railway density information is available only as a cross-sectional measure. Heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity in economic development level

This research classifies cities across the country into three groups based on per capita GDP—high, medium, and low economic development and performs sub-sample regressions to examine the regional heterogeneity in the influence of economic complexity on GTI. Figure 4 presents the results of this heterogeneity analysis by economic development level. The findings show that the positive effect of economic complexity is most pronounced in cities with low per capita GDP, followed by those with medium economic development, while the impact is smallest in cities with high economic development. This difference may be related to the economic endowments of cities. In cities with high degrees of economic development, where the economy is advanced and the capacity for GTI is already well-developed, with limited room for upward growth47, and usually require more significant investment to achieve26. Additionally, these developed cities already exhibit a relatively high degree of economic complexity, resulting in a weaker marginal effect of further enhancement. In contrast, in cities with lower degrees of economic development, where the foundation for GTI is less developed, an increase in economic complexity can more effectively facilitate the diffusion of knowledge and the spillover of technology, thereby significantly boosting the capacity for GTI. The findings suggest that the enhancement of economic complexity can help reduce disparities in GTI between more developed and less developed cities, thereby promoting balanced regional development.

Heterogeneity in geographical location

Referring to Wang, et al13. research, this paper divides the sample into eastern, central, and western regions based on geographical heterogeneity. As shown in Fig. 5, the coefficient value for the central region is the highest, and the confidence interval is relatively concentrated, indicating that economic complexity in the central region can significantly and stably drive GTI. This phenomenon may be closely related to the central region’s recent efforts to attract industrial transfers, promote industrial diversification, and strengthen the aggregation of innovative elements, enabling complex industrial structures and knowledge networks to be more effectively converted into green technology outcomes. In contrast, although the western region has a relatively weak overall economic foundation, economic complexity still exhibits a relatively positive influence, which may be attributed to policy and industrial support resulting from national regional development strategies and the transformation of resource-based cities. In the eastern region, despite its overall high level of economic complexity, its marginal promotional effect on GTI is relatively weak. This may be because eastern cities already have relatively well-established innovation systems, and green innovation relies more on factors such as policy environments, market demand, and international cooperation, rather than solely on improvements in economic complexity.

How can economic complexity promote GTI?

As shown in Table 5, ECI has a significant positive impact on KS in Mechanism I (23.6362, p < 0.01), and KS further significantly promotes GTI (0.0184, p < 0.01), indicating that economic complexity strengthens the R&D process of green technology by promoting the dissemination and reuse of knowledge and technology. In Mechanism II, ECI also exhibits a significant positive effect on ISU (0.3444, p < 0.01), while ISU significantly promotes GTI (0.3748, p < 0.05). Together, they reveal the differentiated transmission pathways through which economic complexity promotes GTI.

Does economic complexity promote GTI quality?

Exploring the quality, rather than just the quantity of GTI can more accurately reflect the actual benefits and technological impact of innovations48. Previous studies show that relying solely on the number of patents to capture GTI may neglect the significance of GTI quality. High-quality GTI not only enhances the market competitiveness of enterprises, but also promotes environmental protection and resource efficiency49.

This research has analyzed the positive influence of economic complexity on the number of green patents, so can the improvement of economic complexity also improve the quality of GTI? Invention patents, which possess a high degree of technological sophistication, more effectively capture the value of GTI and thus serve as a key indicator of patent quality. Accordingly, we utilize the number of green invention patent applications as a proxy for the quality of GTI to examine how economic complexity influences the quality of GTI.

Table 6 reports the results examining the influence of economic complexity on the quality of GTI. The findings reveal that the coefficients for economic complexity are all significantly positive at the 1% confidence level, suggesting that increases in economic complexity have a substantial positive effect on GTI quality. This implies that economic complexity not only fosters growth in the number of green patents but also improves their quality, thereby facilitating the “quantity and quality” advancement of GTI.

Economic complexity and green innovation quality.

Micro evidence from A-share listed companies

We have verified that economic complexity positively contributes to the promotion of GTI in prefecture-level cities. To further investigate the micro-effects driving changes in GTI at the enterprise level50, this study integrates data from A-share listed companies on the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges with patent data spanning from 2000 to 2016. Green patents from listed companies are identified and extracted based on the Green List of International Patent Classification published by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), enabling an empirical analysis of the influence of economic complexity on corporate GTI. The control variables include corporate scale (SCALE), corporate debt (DEBT), the shareholding ratio of the largest shareholder (SHARE), corporate age (AGE), Tobin’s Q (TBQ), and the level of regional economic development (EDL). Corporate scale, measured by the logarithm of total assets, and corporate debt, measured by the logarithm of the total debt, are widely recognized as important determinants of firms’ innovation capacity, as larger firms with stronger financial capacity are better positioned to undertake risky green R&D projects. Ownership concentration, captured by the shareholding ratio of the largest shareholder, may affect managerial discretion and strategic decision-making related to innovation. Corporate age, measured as the logarithm of years since establishment, is introduced to capture the potential effects of organizational maturity and inertia on GTI, while Tobin’s Q reflects firms’ growth opportunities and market expectations. Finally, regional economic development, measured by the logarithm of per capita GDP, is included to account for differences in the external innovation environment across regions.

The first column of Table 7 includes only economic complexity, and the results show that economic complexity has a significant positive impact on corporate GTI. After adding control variables in the second column, economic complexity remains significant, suggesting a robust relationship. In columns three to five, regression analysis is conducted using the first- period, second- period, and third-period lagged variables of economic complexity, and the results consistently show that economic complexity significantly promotes corporate GTI. These findings not only strengthen the causal interpretation of the results by alleviating endogeneity concerns but also provide solid micro-level evidence that economic complexity fosters corporate GTI. Among the control variables, corporate scale and debt display strong positive coefficients, which can be explained by the fact that larger firms with more resources and higher financial capacity are better able to absorb the costs and risks associated with GTI. In contrast, the largest shareholder’s shareholding ratio and corporate age have significant negative effects, indicating that concentrated ownership may reduce incentives for long-term innovation, while organizational inertia in older firms tends to hinder the adoption of green technologies. Tobin’s Q and regional economic development both show positive but statistically insignificant coefficients, suggesting that while market expectations and external economic conditions may encourage innovation, their direct impact on corporate GTI is limited. The models also incorporate region and time fixed effects, and the R-squared values gradually increase with the inclusion of additional variables, reflecting improved explanatory power and robustness of the empirical results.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusion

This research integrates economic complexity and GTI into a coherent research framework to examine the influence of economic complexity on GTI. The conclusions drawn are as follows (Fig. 6). First, based on a dual fixed effects model, the empirical results show that increases in economic complexity significantly enhance GTI capacity. Robustness tests using instrumental variables and two-stage least squares confirm this conclusion. This is consistent with Lin, et al9., who emphasize the role of complexity in fostering knowledge recombination, and extends Mealy and Teytelboym18 by showing that complexity also supports GTI. This result indicates that fostering complex industrial capabilities is conducive to green transformation and achieving high-quality development, which is in line with the current policy direction of China to accelerate the development of new quality productive forces51. Second, we conducted a sub-sample regression analysis to examine the heterogeneity of the impact of economic complexity on GTI. The results show that the promotional effect of economic complexity on GTI is stronger in regions with lower levels of economic development and in central and western regions. Less developed regions benefit more from accumulated capabilities. This discovery indicates that economic complexity not only helps maintain the forefront of innovation in developed regions but also creates a more pronounced incremental effect in less developed areas9,13. Third, mechanism tests reveal that economic complexity promotes GTI through knowledge spillovers and industrial structure upgrading. The mechanisms demonstrate that structural transformation and knowledge flows are key drivers of green-oriented new quality productive forces29,30,51. Fourth, when examining GTI quality, the results show that rising economic complexity not only increases the quantity of GTI but also enhances its quality. Fifth, firm-level evidence from A-share listed companies in Shanghai and Shenzhen shows that rising economic complexity significantly enhances corporate GTI, reinforcing the regional-level findings. The consistency at both regional and firm levels confirms that complexity strengthens innovation across scales, supporting firms’ role in building new quality productive forces.

Policy recommendations

Based on the main findings of this research, the relevant policy recommendations are summarized as follows. First, policies should focus on strengthening knowledge spillovers by creating platforms that connect universities, research institutes, and enterprises. Establishing regional innovation hubs and technology transfer centers can promote the diffusion of specialized knowledge across sectors. Support should also be provided for joint research projects and open-access databases to accelerate the dissemination of green technologies. These measures will enhance the knowledge spillover mechanism identified in this study and transform scientific research into productive applications more effectively.

Second, governments should promote industrial upgrading to provide a stronger foundation for GTI. This can be achieved by supporting the integration of green technologies into traditional industries, encouraging advanced manufacturing, and fostering strategic emerging industries. Local governments can introduce pilot programs that link green innovation projects with industrial transformation strategies, ensuring that economic complexity translates into long-term structural advantages. This policy direction corresponds to the mechanism test showing that industrial upgrading channels the positive effect of economic complexity on GTI.

Third, differentiated support policies should be implemented to reflect regional heterogeneity. In less developed and central-western regions, targeted measures such as larger financial subsidies, preferential tax treatment, and infrastructure investment should be introduced to enhance their economic complexity and maximize the benefits of GTI. In more developed regions, policies can emphasize the quality and international competitiveness of green innovations by encouraging high-value patents and global collaboration. These tailored measures directly respond to the finding that economic complexity has stronger effects on GTI in less developed regions.

Fourth, special funds should be established to accelerate the commercialization of high-quality green invention patents, especially for startups and small enterprises. Beyond financial support, services such as fast-track patent approval, intellectual property protection, and market-matching platforms should be provided. Training programs in green technology and advanced manufacturing skills should also be implemented to ensure that the workforce can meet the demands of more complex industries. By improving both the quantity and quality of GTI, these measures will support the development of new quality productive forces as emphasized in China’s current policy agenda.

Limitations and future research

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the measure of economic complexity is derived from the China Customs Database, which, while authoritative, restricts the analysis to trade-related dimensions of complexity and may not fully capture broader aspects of industrial and technological development. In addition, the sample period of 2000–2016, although valuable for historical analysis, is somewhat outdated and relatively short, making it difficult to reflect the latest dynamics of GTI in China. Second, although dual fixed effects models and instrumental variable methods were applied to mitigate endogeneity, the possibility of measurement errors and omitted variables cannot be completely ruled out. Third, the external validity of the findings is limited, as the analysis is based on the Chinese context and may not be directly generalizable to other economies with different institutional settings. Future research could extend the time frame to include more recent data, integrate alternative sources such as firm-level surveys and patent databases, and apply advanced causal inference methods. Comparative studies across countries and further exploration of network structures, regional collaboration, and global value chains would also provide a more comprehensive understanding of how economic complexity influences GTI.
