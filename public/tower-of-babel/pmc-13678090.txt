Genetic and biochemical screens identify MGAT1 as a druggable glycosyltransferase target in STK11-mutant lung cancer
Authors: Kiera M Vassallo, Ying-nan P Chen, Katarzyna B Handing, Alvin Z Lu, Samuel R Meier, Shangtao Liu, Binzhang Shen, Teng Teng, Yi Yu, Lei Ji, Katherine Lazarides, Alan Huang, Chengyin Min, Brian B Haines, William D Mallender, Sining Sun, Mu-Sen Liu, Alvaro J Amor, Douglas A Whittington, Rebecca Salerno, Frank J Bruzzese, John P Maxwell, Andrew Maynard, Patrick McCarren, Jon H Come, Scott Throner, Jannik N Andersen, Wenhai Zhang, Serge Gueroussov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCheckpoint inhibitors are standard-of-care therapies for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but their efficacy is limited in tumors with STK11 mutations, highlighting the need for new therapeutic strategies. Here, we performed complementary in vivo and in vitro CRISPR-Cas9 functional genomic screens to identify genes whose loss restores sensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy. We found that loss of MGAT1, a Golgi glycosyltransferase critical for the maturation of high-mannose N-glycans into hybrid and complex glycan structures, reversed resistance to anti-PD-1 treatment in syngeneic mouse tumor models harboring STK11 mutations. Parallel co-culture screens with antigen-matched CD8+ T cells further showed that disruption of N-glycosylation strongly sensitized tumor cells to T cell–mediated killing. Genetic rescue studies demonstrated that this immune-evasion phenotype depends on MGAT1 catalytic activity, supporting direct biochemical interrogation of the enzyme. Using purified human MGAT1 and a UDP-Glo glycosyltransferase assay, we established a tractable screening platform and performed a 500,000-compound biochemical high-throughput screen, identifying an initial hit (Compound 1; IC50 = 197 μM). Subsequent medicinal chemistry optimization delivered progressively more potent analogs, including TNG-9333 (0.814 μM) and TNG-2673 (0.043 μM) and represented a >1000-fold improvement in biochemical potency from the starting hit. Crystal structures of human MGAT1 in apo, UDP-bound, UDP-GlcNAc-bound, and inhibitor-bound states, together with SPR and DSF analyses, revealed that this chemical series engages a previously unrecognized allosteric pocket and inhibits MGAT1 through a UDP-noncompetitive mechanism. Collectively, our work implicates N-glycosylation as a key mediator of immune evasion and establishes MGAT1 as a ligandable, structurally tractable target for small-molecule drug discovery.Keywords: glycosyltransferase, high-throughput screen (HTS), medicinal chemistry, crystal structure, lung cancer, enzyme inhibitor, immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance, MGAT1 inhibition, STK11, N-glycosylation pathway, CRISPR/Cas9 target discovery, allosteric small-molecule inhibitor, tumor immune evasion, T cell–mediated killing sensitization, immunotherapy potentiation

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved the treatment landscape of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet many patients fail to benefit (1, 2, 3). Characterizing mechanisms of resistance therefore remains critical to informing targeted strategies that can broaden the impact of these therapies. STK11 loss-of-function mutations occur in 15 to 20% of NSCLC cases, at lower frequencies in other cancer types, and have been associated with reduced response to immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment (4, 5, 6, 7, 8). Consistently, STK11 loss of function in human and preclinical models has been associated with lower T cell infiltration (9, 10), higher levels of immunosuppressive neutrophils (10), attenuated STING signaling (11), and reduced antigen presentation (12, 13). A critical need therefore remains for novel therapeutic targets capable of overcoming STK11-driven immune evasion in NSCLC.

Functional genomic screening is a powerful approach for the discovery of novel drug targets and has been applied broadly to identify genes that govern recognition and elimination of tumors by innate and adaptive immunity (14, 15). We recently applied in vivo CRISPR-Cas9 screening in the classically anti-PD-1-sensitive MC38 syngeneic mouse model. Knockout of STK11 in this model led to increased tumor growth and resistance to anti-PD-1 blockade (16). We further found that knockout of HDAC1 reversed STK11-driven anti-PD-1 resistance, ultimately leading to the development of TNG260, a CoREST complex inhibitor that has entered phase I clinical trials (16). This result motivated our search for additional, mechanistically distinct targets that could overcome STK11-driven immune evasion.

In the present study we applied a combination of in vivo and in vitro CRISPR screens and identified mannosyl (alpha-1,3-)-glycoprotein beta-1,2-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase (MGAT1) as an important tumor-intrinsic regulator of immune evasion in STK11-mutant and, more broadly, in solid tumors. MGAT1 is a Golgi glycosyltransferase that catalyzes the transfer of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) from UDP-GlcNAc to an α-1,3-linked mannose residue on high-mannose N-glycans, an essential step in the maturation of complex N-glycans chains (17). MGAT1 was previously implicated in cell-intrinsic cancer growth (18). In addition, two recent studies implicated MGAT1 and Alpha-mannosidase 2 (MAN2A1), the enzyme acting directly downstream of MGAT1 in N-glycan maturation, as tumor intrinsic regulators of immune evasion (19, 20). More broadly, glycosylation has gained increasing attention as a therapeutically actionable process in autoimmunity and cancer (21, 22).

We found that knockout of MGAT1 was sufficient to overcome STK11-driven adaptive immune resistance in MC38 tumors in vivo, indicating that this enzyme may be especially important in this tumor subtype. In parallel, in vitro CD8+ T cell co-culture screens identified MGAT1, together with additional protein glycosylation genes, as a regulator of tumor susceptibility to T cell attack and T cell receptor (TCR) engagement at the tumor cell surface. We further demonstrated that these immune-evasion phenotypes depend on MGAT1 enzymatic activity, establishing a rationale for direct biochemical interrogation of the enzyme. Accordingly, we developed biochemical high-throughput screen (HTS) assays, identified the first allosteric inhibitors of MGAT1, and used medicinal chemistry, biophysical analyses, and structural studies to define a tractable binding pocket. Together, these findings establish MGAT1 as a tumor-intrinsic mediator of immune evasion and a biochemically tractable glycosyltransferase target for small-molecule discovery.

Results

In vivo CRISPR screens identify knockout of MGAT1 as an immune sensitizer in STK11-deficient tumors

Our previous in vivo CRISPR screens corroborated clinical data (5, 8, 23), showing that knockout of STK11 in MC38 murine colon adenocarcinoma cells conferred a growth advantage in immune-competent mice and resistance to anti-PD-1 blockade (7, 16). Here, we sought to discover additional tractable targets in this setting by performing target discovery screens in STK11 wild-type and STK11 knockout cells (Fig. 1A). The immune evasion phenotype associated with STK11 loss was consistent with prior observations (Fig. S1A), and we focused on identifying genes whose loss could specifically sensitize STK11 mutant tumors to the adaptive immune system. We looked for guides that were selectively depleted in STK11-knockout cells treated with anti-PD-1 compared with (1) knockout cells in nude mice, and (2) wild-type cells treated with anti-PD-1. The top hit in both comparisons was MGAT1 (Fig. 1B). A deeper analysis of guide dropout showed that three of the four MGAT1-targeting sgRNAs in the library showed preferential depletion in the sgSTK11 background, with the magnitude of dropout increasing with higher immune pressure (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the 400 non-targeting control (NTC) guides in the library showed no significant change across conditions (Fig. S1B). Guides targeting PD-L1 were depleted specifically in wild-type tumors in C57BL/6 mice compared to the sgSTK11 tumors, consistent with the loss of anti-PD-1 sensitivity in sgSTK11 tumors (Fig. S1C).

MGAT1 loss enhances anti-PD-1 sensitivity in STK11-deficient MC38 tumors

To further validate these findings, we performed single-gene knockout of MGAT1 in both sgNTC and sgSTK11 MC38 cells and implanted these tumor cells into C57BL/6 mice. We measured Phytohemagglutinin-L (PHA-L) lectin binding to complex N-glycans on the cell surface to confirm that MGAT1 depletion led to glycan loss in both parental MC38 and STK11 mutant backgrounds (Fig. S1D). The in vivo tumor growth data were consistent with the primary screen where MGAT1 knockout uniquely inhibited growth in the sgSTK11 background (Fig. 1D). To assess whether MGAT1 knockout could synergize with anti-PD-1 treatment, we compared responses in MC38 sgSTK11 tumors with either sgNTC or sgMGAT1 following anti-PD-1 treatment. The data show that loss of MGAT1 could enhance the modest sensitivity of STK11-deficient tumors to anti-PD-1 blockade, supporting MGAT1 as a candidate immunotherapeutic target for pharmacological modulation in this setting (Figs. 1E and S1E).

Co-culture CRISPR screening links N-glycosylation to resistance to antigen-specific T cell-mediated killing in vitro

Our in vivo findings showed that knockout of MGAT1 could modestly mitigate tumor growth in nude mice, consistent with its previously reported role in regulating cell-autonomous tumorigenicity or sensitization to innate immunity. However, the antitumor effect was greatly enhanced in the presence of adaptive immunity and anti-PD-1 treatment (Fig. 1C). Prior studies have implicated N-glycosylation in resistance to T cell-mediated killing through a variety of mechanisms (24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31). To further define the role of MGAT1 and N-glycosylation in driving resistance to T cell-mediated killing, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR screen in an in vitro co-culture system using primary human CD8+ T cells and tumor cells (Fig. 2A). This unbiased approach could provide a pathway view by evaluating the contribution of glycan biology relative to other known mechanisms, such as antigen presentation or cytokine signaling.

We isolated and expanded primary human CD8+ T cells and transduced them with the NYESO1 TCR (32). Human tumor cell lines were selected based on endogenous expression of the HLA-A∗02:01 allele and the cognate NYESO1 antigen (CTAG1A/B) (Fig. S2A). We confirmed antigen-specific cytotoxicity of our engineered T cells against the candidate tumor lines (Fig. S2B) and selected NCI-H1703 cells for the genome-wide co-culture screen. The screen recovered many known regulators of T cell-mediated killing (Fig. 2B). Notably, many of the sensitizer genes identified in the co-culture screen, including MGAT1, regulate cell-surface glycosylation (Fig. 2B, highlighted). These hits spanned multiple steps in the N-glycosylation pathway, including enzymes involved in precursor-sugar biosynthesis, transporters that deliver these substrates into the endomembrane system, and glycosyltransferases that build glycan chains. We prioritized MGAT1 because it encodes a potential druggable enzyme and occupies a key upstream position in the pathway as the rate-limiting step in complex N-glycan synthesis (Fig. 2C). Moreover, MGAT1 was the most distal gene in the N-glycosylation pathway that scored as a sensitizer in our screen, suggesting that its inhibition could produce the broadest downstream impact on N-glycan maturation.

MGAT1 loss sensitizes human tumor models to T cell-mediated killing in vitro and in vivo

To further evaluate the role of MGAT1 in sensitizing tumor cells to T cell-mediated killing, and to expand our work into additional cell models including STK11 mutant NSCLC, we carried out targeted co-culture assays using an in vitro competition format (Fig. S2C). Knockout of MGAT1 led to reduced binding to PHA-L using diverse guides and across our chosen cell models (Fig. S2D). In the co-culture setting, MGAT1 knockout sensitized both STK11-wild-type NCI-H1703 and STK11-mutant NCI-H1755 cells to NYESO1 TCR+ CD8+ T cell–mediated killing, which occurred in an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio–dependent manner (Fig. 2, D and E).

To determine whether the sensitization could be recapitulated in vivo, we established an adoptive cell transfer model using subcutaneous A375 (an HLA-A∗02:01+, NYESO1+ cell line) xenografts in NOG mice treated with our NYESO1 TCR+ CD8+ human T cells. Dose-dependent in vivo expansion (Fig. S2E) and anti-tumor activity of T cells against A375 tumors were confirmed (Fig. S2F). We then compared responses of sgNTC or sgMGAT1 A375 tumors to a low dose of CD8+ T cells. While baseline tumor growth was equivalent, MGAT1 knockout (Fig. S2G) significantly enhanced tumor sensitivity to T cell-mediated killing in vivo (Fig. 2F).

MGAT1 knockout blocks complex N-glycan maturation and alters glycosylation of immune effector molecules PD-L1 and CD73

MGAT1 catalyzes transfer of GlcNAc from UDP-GlcNAc onto an α-1,3-linked mannose substrate, an essential step in the conversion of immature high-mannose N-glycans into hybrid and complex structures terminating in lactosamine and sialic acid (Fig. 2C). MGAT1 knockout would thus be expected to block N-glycan maturation, thereby altering the activity or presentation of key regulatory molecules such as PD-L1 (33), or more recently reported CD73 (20, 30) and broadly reducing the immunosuppressive effects of N-linked sialic acid (34, 35).

To confirm functional MGAT1 loss, PHA-L binding was measured in A375 and NCI-H1703 MGAT1 knockout populations (Figs. 2G and S2H). MGAT1 knockout cells had low PHA-L binding, indicative of reduced complex N-glycan structures. Additionally, lectin binding with Galanthus nivalis agglutinin (GNA), which is specific to high-mannose N-glycan structures, increased upon MGAT1 knockout (Figs. 2H and S2I), consistent with pathway modulation upon MGAT1 loss (19). To characterize the global impact of MGAT1 loss, we performed mass spectrometry-based glycoproteomic analysis on two sets of MGAT1 isogenic cell line pairs (A375 and NCI-H1703), detecting 2906 glycosylated peptides mapping to 524 unique proteins. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the glycoproteomic dataset demonstrated clear separation between MGAT1 knockout and NTC samples and high reproducibility among biological replicates (Fig. 2I). Indeed, immunoblot analysis revealed molecular-weight gel shifts in PD-L1 and CD73 (Fig. 2, J and K), consistent with altered mature glycosylation levels and implicating MGAT1 as a regulator of anti-cancer immunity.

Biochemical and structural studies define the human MGAT1 catalytic site

Given its favorable genetic validation, we wanted to evaluate MGAT1 as a potential target for small molecule inhibition, focusing on its enzymatic activity. MGAT1 is a Golgi-resident, type II membrane protein and to assess enzyme activity in an in vitro system, we expressed and purified the MGAT1 luminal domain (T30-N445) (Fig. S3A). As a glycosyltransferase, MGAT1 transfers GlcNAc from UDP-GlcNAc to its high-mannose substrate, producing UDP as a product (Fig. S3B). Enzyme activity was measured with a UDP-Glo assay kit, which converts UDP production into a luminescent readout (Fig. S3C). 3α,6α-mannopentose served as a surrogate substrate, enabling a sensitive, high-throughput assessment of MGAT1 catalytic activity.

To understand key features of the enzyme active site involved in catalysis, we examined published protein structures of rabbit MGAT1 (94% sequence identity between rabbit and human MGAT1 catalytic domains) and determined the first human MGAT1 crystal structures in the apo form (1.7 Å), in complex with UDP (1.6 Å), and in complex with UDP-GlcNAc (2.0 Å). In the UDP-bound structure, uracil is stabilized in the active site by hydrogen-bonding interactions with side chains of H188 and D142, and by hydrophobic interaction with the side chain of I185 (Fig. 3A). The hydroxyl group from the ribose forms a hydrogen bond interaction with the backbone of I111. The phosphate groups directly interact with the backbone of V319, the backbone and side chain of S320, and the side chain of R115, as well as a catalytically essential manganese ion. The manganese ion adopts an octahedral coordination geometry, interacting with two phosphate oxygens from UDP, three water molecules, and the side chain oxygen of D211. Superposition of the UDP-bound structure with the UDP-GlcNAc-bound structure illustrates that both molecules share the same binding site, with the same key residues responsible for coordinating the UDP moiety in the active site. The beta phosphate shifts 0.8 Å to accommodate the GlcNAc moiety in the UDP-GlcNAc-bound structure. The GlcNAc moiety extends further towards the opening of the pocket and forms hydrogen-bonding interactions with side chains of E209 and W288, but the global protein conformation remains unchanged (Fig. 3B). The same overall conformation is observed when comparing the apo and UDP-bound structures, with the exception of a flexible loop region (R316–F331) that is disordered and unresolved in the apo state and becomes partially ordered upon UDP binding (Fig. 3C). This loop folds over the ligand and is stabilized by direct interactions with UDP, suggesting a ligand-induced conformational change that may be important for catalysis.

Guided by these structural insights into the active site, and based on prior literature, we next sought to determine the functional importance of specific residues for MGAT1 catalytic activity. We identified two residues likely to be critical. D289 is positioned to function as the catalytic base, deprotonating the incoming electrophile of the acceptor substrate in glycosyltransferases; in MGAT5, the equivalent E297A mutation abolishes enzymatic activity (36). D211 is part of the conserved DXD motif characteristic of this enzyme class. It is the only residue in our structure directly coordinating the catalytic Mn2+ ion, which is essential for MGAT1 activity (Fig. S3D). We therefore generated three MGAT1 luminal domain mutants (D211A, D289A, and the D211A/D289A double mutant) and tested their enzymatic activity using the UDP-Glo assay. Compared with wild-type MGAT1, all three mutant proteins exhibited a marked loss of glycosyltransferase activity, confirming that both D211 and D289 are critical for catalysis (Fig. 3D).

MGAT1 catalytic activity is required for immune evasion phenotypes

We next performed a series of functional rescue experiments to test whether MGAT1 catalytic activity is required for its role in immune evasion. First, we repeated the in vitro T cell co-culture assays and re-expressed CRISPR-resistant wild-type or catalytically inactive MGAT1 in MGAT1 knockout backgrounds. Re-expression of wild-type but not mutant MGAT1 restored PHA-L binding, indicating functional glycosylation; in contrast, catalytically inactive mutants failed to rescue this phenotype (Figs. 4A and S4A). The co-culture results mimicked the PHA-L data. Only wild-type MGAT1 was able to rescue the increased sensitivity of NCI-H1703 MGAT1 knockout cells to T cell killing (Fig. 4B).

We next tested whether the same requirement for catalytic activity was true for protecting MC38 tumors from immune attack in vivo. We carried out three steps of genetic engineering to generate cells for comparison (Figs. 4, C, D and S4B). First, we knocked out MGAT1 using a non-targeting guide as a control. To ensure full penetrance of MGAT1 knockout, we isolated a single knockout clone. We then knocked out STK11 and rescued MGAT1 expression with CRISPR-resistant wild-type or catalytically inactive cDNA. Consistent with the previous rescue experiments in human cells, only wild-type MGAT1 was able to rescue PHA-L binding. When these cells were implanted into immune-competent mice, we observed that wild-type, but not catalytically dead, MGAT1 cDNA could restore the growth advantage of MGAT1/STK11 double knockout cells (Fig. 4E). MGAT1 catalytic activity is thus critical for its function in protecting cancer cells from immune attack in vitro and in vivo, making it a suitable target for small-molecule drug development.

HTS and medicinal chemistry identify potent MGAT1 inhibitors

Having established the role of MGAT1 in tumor immune recognition and T cell–mediated killing, and having confirmed that enzymatic activity is required for this phenotype, we next sought to identify small molecule inhibitors of MGAT1 as starting points for medicinal chemistry optimization. In a UDP-Glo–based HTS of 500,000 compounds measuring inhibition of MGAT1 enzymatic activity, Compound 1 emerged as an initial hit with an IC50 of 197 μM (Table 1, Figs. 5A and S5A). Though weakly potent, Compound 1 was an attractive starting point for hit expansion and optimization, employing the same UDP-Glo biochemical assay for compound screening to assess MGAT1 inhibition. Multiple approaches were undertaken to define the inhibitor pharmacophore and improve potency, including a “methyl walk” (37, 38, 39) on the open positions of both the pyridine core and anilide ring. Though methyl substitution in most positions was poorly tolerated, methylation of the six-position on the pyridine ring imparted significant potency enhancement, yielding TNG-3153 with an IC50 of 25.6 μM. Building off this promising finding, we sought to further probe and occupy this space through expansion of the pyridine ring to a quinoline, leading to TNG-5988, which afforded a further 10-fold improvement in biochemical potency (IC50 = 2.02 μM). Maintaining the quinoline scaffold, we then diversified the anilide ring via the incorporation of polar functionality to scout for additional binding interactions. This strategy proved successful, as exemplified by TNG-9084, wherein the incorporation of a meta-aminomethyl side chain moiety improved potency (IC50 = 0.543 μM) with concomitant increase in solubility. Further refinement of the quinoline core and dimethyl substitution of the triazole moiety led to TNG-2673 with an IC50 of 0.043 μM, a >1000-fold potency improvement in the inhibition of MGAT1 enzymatic activity from the initial HTS starting point Compound 1, while maintaining good physicochemical properties (Table 1, Figs. 5A and S5A). As part of a parallel medicinal chemistry effort, we also pursued bioisosteric replacements (40) of the triazole moiety (e.g., triazole to substituted acetamides (41) and concurrent conversion of the anilide phenyl ring to a pyridine ring to assess the impact on potency and overall properties. An illustrative example is analog TNG-9333, with an IC50 of 0.814 μM, which encapsulated both alterations. Although unable to achieve the level of potency observed in the triazole series, such analogs retained high solubility and low in vitro metabolic clearance. Across both series, potencies determined in the biochemical activity assay correlated well with binding affinities measured by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), indicating that improved inhibition of MGAT1 activity tracked with increased binding affinity and target engagement (Fig. 5B).

To benchmark our inhibitors against previously reported MGAT1 chemical matter (20), we evaluated Compounds 8 and 9 in both biochemical and biophysical assays. In our UDP-Glo assay, both compounds displayed only weak inhibitory activity (Fig. S6). Moreover, neither compound showed detectable binding to purified MGAT1 by SPR under the conditions tested. These results contrast with the robust biochemical, biophysical, and structural validation observed for our inhibitor series and highlight the improved potency and target engagement achieved through our optimization campaign.

Biophysical and structural analyses reveal a novel allosteric pocket and UDP-noncompetitive inhibition

Next, to gain insight into the compound’s mode of action, we performed mechanistic studies using SPR. Since the endogenous reaction product UDP yielded stronger responses in SPR than the natural substrate UDP-GlcNAc, and both bind in the same binding site (Fig. 3B), we selected UDP for subsequent analyses. These experiments revealed that the saturating concentrations of the HTS-derived series compounds did not significantly affect UDP binding affinity, and vice versa. (Figs. 5C and S5, B–C). These results indicate that the UDP and discovered inhibitors bind independently at distinct sites on MGAT1, with the HTS compound series likely engaging an allosteric pocket. This conclusion was supported by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF): both UDP and the HTS compound individually increased MGAT1 thermal stability, and the stabilizing effect was additive when the two ligands were combined, consistent with non-competitive binding relative to UDP (Figs. 5D and S5D).

Definitive structural insight came from two X-ray structures: MGAT1 bound to TNG-9333, as well as the ternary complex of MGAT1 bound to both UDP and TNG-9333. The structure of MGAT1 in complex with TNG-9333 revealed a novel binding site distinct from the active site (Fig. 5E). This pocket is formed by β-turn L407–R413, α-helix R391–L401, and the C-terminus of the protein. The quinoline ring of TNG-9333 is buried within the pocket, sandwiched between G390 and W437 via hydrophobic interactions, while the pyridine ring remains solvent-exposed. The compound also forms a hydrogen bond with the backbone oxygen of G439 and a buried, crystallographically conserved water molecule, further stabilizing its binding pose. The ternary structure of MGAT1 in complex with TNG-9333 and UDP corroborated the SPR and DSF findings, demonstrating that the two molecules can simultaneously bind to distinct sites on MGAT1 (Fig. 5F). Although no substantial conformational changes were observed in the ternary complex relative to the UDP-bound structure, with the exception of a minor shift in the D406-G410 loop (Fig. S5E), we propose that inhibitor binding impedes productive glycan substrate engagement through steric occlusion and/or dynamic effects not captured in the crystallographic state.

A limitation of the current mechanistic analysis is that the native oligomannose substrate (Man5) cannot be employed at sufficiently high concentrations in either biochemical or biophysical assays due to solubility and assay constraints. As a result, classical steady-state substrate titration and direct biophysical competition experiments with Man5 could not be performed to fully define the mode of inhibition. As such, the proposed allosteric mechanism is inferred solely from the lack of identifiable evidence of active-site interaction. Development of improved substrate reagents or alternative assay formats will be required to further resolve the molecular mechanism of MGAT1 inhibition.

Discussion

In this study, we identify MGAT1 as a tumor-intrinsic mediator of immune evasion, particularly in the context of STK11-mutant cancers, and establish its catalytic activity as essential for this phenotype. Using a combination of in vivo CRISPR screening, in vitro T cell co-culture assays, and glycoproteomics, we demonstrate that MGAT1-dependent N-glycan maturation plays a pivotal role in modulating tumor immune recognition and resistance to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Our findings nominate MGAT1 as a promising target for immunotherapy, especially in tumors harboring STK11 loss-of-function mutations, a genomic context currently associated with poor responses to immune checkpoint blockades. These findings support the existing literature highlighting the importance of protein glycosylation in immune regulation (20, 24, 42), and MGAT1 as a particularly favorable target due to its global effect on N-glycosylation.

Over a decade of treating lung cancer patients with immune checkpoint inhibitors has led to a wealth of knowledge about mechanisms of primary and acquired resistance (2). To increase rates of response, next-generation therapies must better understand and address these mechanisms (43, 44). Genetic biomarkers are particularly useful, as sequencing has become widely implemented in the clinical setting. Tumor-driving mutations, including classic oncogenes such as KRAS, are being implicated in the response of cancer immunotherapy (45). Defining these mechanisms may enable rational drug combinations to be selected at the time of diagnosis. STK11 loss-of-function mutations are well recognized drivers of poor response to immune checkpoint therapy, and multiple combinations have been proposed by us and others (7, 9, 16). Here we propose that STK11 loss creates a unique dependency on N-glycosylation as an immune evasion mechanism. This connection may be due to the role of STK11 in metabolic rewiring, and others have shown that STK11 loss may specifically alter metabolic flux to increase availability of UDP-GlcNAc (46, 47).

Our in vitro screen uncovered that knockout of a broad set of genes related to cell-surface glycosylation sensitizes tumor cells to immune attack, consistent with an established role for glycans and glycan-binding proteins in regulating immune recognition (22). In vitro, MGAT1 was important in protecting both STK11 wild-type and mutant tumors. Given that other studies consistently show a role for MGAT1 and other genes downstream in N-glycan synthesis in immune protection (19, 20, 48, 49), we hypothesize that dependence on this pathway becomes more pronounced in vivo, where nutrients become limiting, and STK11-dependent metabolic rewiring becomes more pronounced. Other strategies aimed at targeting immunosuppressive proteoglycans involve tumor antigen-directed elimination of sialic acid (24) and targeting of various glycan binding immune checkpoints (50). We propose that MGAT1 is a promising target for therapeutic development, given that it scored as a sensitizer in multiple screens and would more deeply impact cell-surface glycans than downstream targets while potentially sparing non-cancer cell viability. Moreover, as other studies have implicated N-glycosylation as limiting immune cell-intrinsic activity (51, 52, 53), inhibition of MGAT1 could provide a dual impact of sensitizing tumor cells to immune attack, while also boosting the efficacy of the immune cells themselves. Such an approach has been proposed by others and is being clinically evaluated with PTPN1/2 inhibitors (54, 55).

We demonstrate that MGAT1’s catalytic activity is required for its role in immune evasion, underscoring the critical need to discover inhibitors of MGAT1. Regulation of MGAT1 activity by interaction with the C-terminus of MGAT4D-L has been described before in a biological context (56), but there are only limited reports on discovering small-molecule inhibitors against MGAT1 (20). At the molecular level, MGAT1 catalyzes the initial rate-limiting step in the synthesis and maturation of complex N-linked glycans (57). The reaction product is subsequently branched by downstream glycan-processing enzymes such as MGAT2 and MGAT5, ultimately yielding functional complex glycan chains, further emphasizing MGAT1’s central role in this ordered, multistep process. Notably, MGAT5 has been implicated as a potential drug target for immunotherapy further highlighting the importance of this biosynthesis route (58). To date, there are no clinically approved inhibitors of glycosyltransferases within the N-linked glycan synthesis pathway suggesting this enzyme class may be inherently difficult to target.

Our biochemical HTS of MGAT1 resulted in identification of hits with weak potency. However, through iterative medicinal chemistry optimization using robust assays and structural insights from high-resolution crystal structures, we achieved >1000-fold improvement in potency relative to initial hits, leading to nanomolar inhibitors of MGAT1 activity. Notably, other previously reported MGAT1-directed compounds (20) with reported cellular activity showed only weak biochemical activity in our assay format and failed to demonstrate convincing orthogonal biophysical validation by SPR or structural studies under the conditions tested. Accordingly, these observations should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect differences between compound behavior in biochemical versus cellular assay formats, target engagement, or off-target cellular effects rather than definitive evidence against MGAT1-directed activity. Taken together, however, both the prior literature and our results support the conclusion that MGAT1 is amenable to small-molecule modulation, while our work provides direct biochemical, biophysical, and structural validation that MGAT1 is a ligandable target and that potent inhibitors can be developed through classical drug-discovery approaches.

Importantly, structural studies of MGAT1–inhibitor complexes reveal that our compounds engage a previously unrecognized allosteric site, distinct from the UDP-binding active site. Complementary biophysical analyses confirm a UDP-non-competitive mechanism of action, a property that may confer selectivity advantages over other enzymes from this class and circumvent challenges associated with targeting conserved active sites. Our compounds therefore demonstrate that MGAT1 can be inhibited through an allosteric mechanism by small molecules. Together, these findings establish allosteric inhibition of MGAT1 as a novel and tractable strategy to block its activity. While further development is required to advance compounds identified in this study toward therapeutic application, the discovery of potent allosteric inhibitors provides a strong foundation for future structure-based optimization and supports the broader feasibility of N-glycosylation-targeting immunotherapy strategies (48, 59).

Experimental procedures

Compounds

Compound 1 was purchased from Enamine (Z1536619032). TNG-3153, TNG-5988, TNG-9084, TNG-2673 and TNG-9333 were synthesized as described in the Supporting Information.

Cell culture

Cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), validated by STR profiling, and routinely tested for mycoplasma. A549, A375, and MC38 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco #12491) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma #12306C). All other cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco #11875) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma #12306C) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco #15240062). Adherent cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA (Gibco #25200056) and maintained at 37 °C, humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

DNA constructs and generation of engineered cell lines

Lentivirus was generated by transiently transfecting 293T cells with lentiviral packaging mix (Cellecta #CPCPK2A), Lipofectamine transfection reagent (Thermo L3000015), and plasmid DNA diluted in Opti-MEM (Thermo 31985062). Viral supernatants were collected after transfection, filtered with a 0.45 μm filter and used to infect target cells in the presence of polybrene. Transduced cells were selected with the appropriate antibiotic.

In vivo CRISPR screen

The target identification screen used two sequential lentiviral infections. MC38 cells were first infected with an sgRNA library targeting a curated subset of the druggable genome. Library-containing MC38 cells were then divided into two groups and infected with an all-in-one viral vector containing Cas9 and either a non-targeting control sgRNA or an STK11-targeting sgRNA. Infected cells were implanted subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice (n = 30), nude mice (n = 15), or maintained in vitro. When tumors in C57BL/6 mice reached 50 mm3, mice were treated twice weekly with either anti-IgG2a (BioXCell; #BE0089) or anti-PD-1 (BioXCell; #BE0146) at 5 mg/kg. Tumors were collected for next generation sequencing (NGS) when the average tumor volume of the anti-IgG2a group reached 1000 mm3. DNA sequencing and computational analysis were performed as previously described (60).

Primary human CD8+ T-cell isolation and activation

Leukopaks from healthy donors were obtained from Precision For Medicine. Upon receipt, PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (CYTIVA, Cat# 17544203), cryopreserved, and stored in liquid nitrogen at 2 × 108 cells/vial. For T cell preparation (Day −1), 6-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with anti-human CD3 (10 μg/ml; clone UCHT1, Cat #40–0038) and anti-human CD28 (5 μg/ml; clone CD28.2, Tonbo, Cat #40–0289) in PBS. On Day 0, CD8+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs using magnetic beads (MILTENYI #130-096-495) and used immediately without refreezing. Cells were resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/ml in X-Vivo medium consisting of X-Vivo15 medium (Lonza, Cat #04–418Q), 5% Fetal Calf Serum, 50 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10 mM N-Acetyl L-Cysteine, and human IL-2 (STEMCELL # 78036) at 50 U/ml, and plated onto CD3/CD28 coated plates (2 ml/well). After 24 h (Day 1), cells were transduced with NYESO1 (1G4)-encoding lentivirus to achieve >90% receptor expression. On Day 3, 2 ml of fresh media was added to each well. On Days 4 and 6, cells were pooled, counted, spun down, and resuspended with fresh medium at 1 × 106 cells/ml. On Day 8, cells were assessed for TCR expression and frozen down for future use.

In vitro co-culture screen

NCI-H1703 cells were first engineered to stably express Cas9 using a lentiviral vector containing the blasticidin resistance marker. Cells were then infected with a whole genome library comprising four guides per gene for a total of ∼78,000 plasmids containing a puromycin resistance marker seeding 2.6 × 108 cells 16 h before infection. Virus was added to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ∼0.3, equal to ∼1000× library coverage. At 48 h post infection, infection efficiency was assessed by RFP expression. Puromycin (1 μg/ml) was added to select infected cells, and selection continued for ∼5 days until >97% of cells were RFP-positive. Selected tumor cells were then seeded at 8 × 107 cells per 5-layer flask 16 h before addition of CD8+ T cells. NYESO1 TCR+ CD8+ T cells were added at a ratio of 0.5 T cells per tumor cell for 24 h. T cells were removed by washing, and tumor cells harvested for gDNA extraction. DNA sequencing and computational analysis were performed as previously described (60).

In vitro T cell cytotoxicity competition assays

For competition assays, knockout tumor cells or nontargeting control cells (RFP positive) were mixed with parental tumor cells (RFP negative) at a 1:1 ratio and co-cultured with NYESO1 TCR+ CD8+ T cells at the indicated effector-to-target ratios. After 24 h, T cells were removed by washing, and the percentage of RFP-positive tumor cells was determined by flow cytometry. Residual T cells were gated out based on forward and side scatter and confirmed by GFP expression from the TCR construct.

In vivo genetic studies

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuxi in accordance with the guidance of the Association of Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.

For syngeneic studies, mice were acclimatized for 7 days, before subcutaneous implantation of 0.1 million indicated MC38 cells in 0.1 ml of PBS into the right flank. For antibody treatment, when tumors reached 50 mm3, animals were randomized to receive anti-IgG2a (BioXCell, Clone BE0089) or anti-PD-1 (BioXCell, Clone RMP1–14) at 10 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection twice weekly. Tumor volume was measured with calipers and calculated as (length × width × width)/2.

For adoptive cell transfer studies, five million A375 tumor cells were mixed with Matrigel (Corning) and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of NOG mice. Tumor growth was monitored three times per week with calipers and when the size reached 150 mm3, mice were randomized in a blinded manner inoculated intravenously via the tail vein with the indicated numbers of thawed CD8+ T cells. T cell persistence was supported by intraperitoneal administration of 100,000 IU IL-2 (Proleukin, Novartis) once daily for three consecutive days.

Flow cytometry and lectin staining

Cells were dissociated with Accutase (Invitrogen #00-4555-56), neutralized with complete medium, and transferred to a 96-well plate (Corning #3363). Cells were spun down at 1600 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 50 μl of 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated for at least 30 min at 25 °C. Cells were spun down and washed three times with staining buffer (2% BSA + DPBS). Biotinylated GNA (Vector laboratories #B-1245-2) or biotinylated PHA-L (Vector laboratories #B-1115-2) was added to a final concentration of 10 μg/ml, and samples were incubated for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were washed again with staining buffer and incubated with Alexa Fluor 790-conjugated streptavidin (Thermo #S11378) to a final concentration of 1.5 μg/ml for 30 min in the dark, washed, and analyzed on an iQue3 FACS instrument (Sartorius).

Immunoblotting

Protein lysates were prepared from cultured cells or frozen tumor tissue in RIPA buffer. Protein concentrations were normalized using Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce #A53225). Proteins were separated on NuPAGE 4 to 12% Bis-Tris Midi Protein Gels (Invitrogen; WG1402BOX) and analyzed by immunoblotting using antibodies against STK11 (Invitrogen #MA5-15729), β-actin (CST #4967), MGAT1 (Abcam # ab180578), PD-L1 (CST #13684), CD276 (CST #14058), and GAPDH (CST #2118).

sgRNA sequences

The following sgRNA and control guide sequences were used in this study (5′→3′): NC77 (ACCCATCGGGTGCGATATGG), sgSTK11 (CTCAAATGGGTACAGGCCCG; mouse), sgMGAT1_4 (CAGCCAACAGCAGCCATCCA; mouse), ITC control (ATTTGTAACATCTCTTCACG), B2M sgRNA3 (GGCCGAGATGTCTCGCTCCG; human), MGAT1_1 (GTTCCGAGAGCTGGCTACCG; human), MGAT1_2 (ACTGGAAGGTGACAATACCC; human), MGAT1_3 (GCCCGACCTGAGCAGCATTG; human), MGAT1_4 (GGCCAGGGAAAAAGTCGGTG; human), NTC-1 (AGTATGAGACTCATAGGGTG), NTC-2 (GAAGGCCTTAACACGTCGAC), PD-L1_sg1 (GGTTCCCAAGGACCTATATG; human), PD-L1_sg2 (ACTGCTTGTCCAGATGACTT; human), and sgPTPN2 (CCATGACTATCCTCATAGAG; human). All guide sequences were 20 bp in length.

Glycoproteomics

Pellets of four replicates of human cell lines were grinded with liquid nitrogen into cell powder and lysed in four volumes of lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail), followed by sonication for 3 min on ice using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz). The remaining debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000g at 4 °C for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined with BCA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The sample was slowly added to the final concentration of 20% (m/v) TCA to precipitate protein, then vortexed and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 4500g for 5 min at 4 °C. The precipitated protein was washed cold acetone 3 times and dried for 1 min. The protein sample was then redissolved in 200 mM TEAB and ultrasonically dispersed. Trypsin was added at 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion overnight. The sample was reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 °C and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. The peptides were then desalted by Strata X SPE column.

Tryptic peptides were dissolved in 0.5 M TEAB. Each channel of peptide was labeled with their respective TMT reagent (based on manufacturers protocol; ThermoFisher Scientific) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. 5 μl of each sample was pooled, desalted, and analyzed by MS to check labeling efficiency. Samples were then quenched by adding 5% hydroxylamine. The pooled samples were desalted with Strata X C18 SPE column (Phenomenex) and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

The sample was fractionated by high pH reverse-phase HPLC using Agilent 300 Extend C18 column (5 μm particles, 4.6 mm i.d., 250 mm length). Briefly, peptides were separated with a gradient of 8% to 32% acetonitrile solution of pH 9 over 60 min into 60 fractions. Then, the peptides were combined into four fractions and dried by vacuum centrifuging.

Tryptic peptides were redissolved in 200 μl washing buffer (80% ACN, 5% TFA) and then loaded onto the column and washed with washing buffer. Glycopeptides were eluted with 0.1% TFA, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 50% ACN. The eluted glycopeptides were desalted using C18 Zip Tips according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then dried for further MS analysis.

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile/in water), directly loaded onto a home-made reversed-phase analytical column (25-cm length, 100 μm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A and solvent B (0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile/in water). Peptides were separated with the following gradient: 0 to 60 min, 2% to 25% B; 60 to 84 min, 25% to 35% B; 84 to 87 min, 35%-80% B; 87 to 90 min, 80% B, and all at a constant flow rate of 500 nl/min on an EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific). The separated peptides were analyzed in Q Exactive HF-X with a nano-electrospray ion source. The electrospray voltage applied was 2100 V. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the Orbitrap detector. The full MS scan resolution was set to 120,000 for a scan range of 700 to 2000 m/z. The fragments were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 45,000 and fixed first mass was set as 100.0 m/z. Up to 10 most abundant precursors were then selected for further MS/MS analyses with 15.0 s dynamic exclusion. The HCD fragmentation was performed at a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 20, 30, 40%. Automatic gain control (AGC) target was set at 2e5, with an intensity threshold of 2.5e4 ions/s and a maximum injection time of 200 ms.

The raw data from the mass spectrometer was imported into the library search software, and the corresponding analysis parameters were set according to the experimental plan. Raw data were processed using MSFragger (v.3.4) software, tandem mass spectra were searched against Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20230103. fasta (20,389 entries) concatenated with reverse decoy database. Enzyme was set to stricttrypsin, missed cleavage was set to 2, respectively. The length range of the peptide was set to 7 to 50. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as fixed modification. N-Glycosylation on asparagine, oxidation on methionine were specified as variable modifications. Set mass offsets to the default list of glycosylated modifications. The quantitative method was set to TMT-Pro. False discovery rate (FDR) of protein, peptide and PSM was adjusted to < 1%.

Further data filtering is required for the search database analysis results. The accuracy FDR of identification at the three levels of spectrum, peptide and protein is set to 1%; the identification of protein needs to contain at least one unique peptide.

Recombinant MGAT expression and purification

The MGAT1 construct for activity assays and thermal stability studies (UniProt #P26572; residues T30–N445, lacking the N-terminal transmembrane helix) was cloned into pFastBac (Genscript) with an N-terminal His tag and a TEV protease cleavage site. Cloning and purification were performed at Biortus Biosciences Co., Ltd. His-TEV–MGAT1(T30–N445) was expressed in Hi-5 insect cells (Gibco #B85502) using baculovirus at 27 °C for 48 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the pellets were resuspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, supplemented with 1 mM MgCl2, nuclease (Biortus, BP23157-00A, 500 U/g cell pellet), and complete Protease Inhibitor Tablet (Roche, #4693132001). Cells were lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer (650 bar, four passes), and lysates were clarified by centrifugation for 60 min at 18,000 rpm.

Purification was performed by Ni affinity chromatography (Bestchrom, AA0053), overnight TEV digestion followed by GST affinity cleanup (Bestchrom, AA0073), Mono S ion-exchange chromatography, and size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva, 17104302) equilibrated in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM DTT, and 150 mM NaCl. Final protein was concentrated to 5 mg/ml and stored in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM DTT, and 150 mM NaCl. Mutant D211A, D289A, and D211A/D289A were purified similarly, except that 0.5 mM TCEP was included in the resuspension buffer, 1 mM DTT was added to Mono S equilibration buffer, and 5% glycerol was added to storage buffer.

The crystallographic construct comprised residues A104–N445 and lacked both the N-terminal transmembrane helix and an additional N-terminal helix. This construct was cloned and expressed as described above, except that the His tag was retained in the final protein, yielding His-TEV–MGAT1(A104–N445) for crystallization.

For SPR studies, the A104–N445 construct was modified by insertion of an Avi tag and a seven-repeat Gly-Ser linker were inserted between the His tag and the protein sequence, generating 6His-TEV–Avi–(GS)7–MGAT1(A104–N445). This construct was expressed in Sf9 cells rather than Hi-5 cells. After TEV digestion and GST affinity cleanup, the protein was biotinylated overnight at 4 °C in 50 mM Bicine (pH 8.3), 10 mM ATP, 10 mM MgOAc, 100 μM D-biotin, and BirA ligase (Biortus, #BP02784-01A) at a 1:100 M ratio to protein. Biotinylation efficiency was confirmed by LC–MS. Biotinylated protein was captured on a Strep-Tactin XT column (IBA, 2-5030-500) equilibrated in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF (Solarbio, P8340-25g), eluted with the same buffer containing 75 mM biotin, further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 pg (Cytiva, 17104302), concentrated to 5.3 mg/ml, and stored in the same storage buffer.

HTS hit identification

Initial MGAT1 inhibitors were identified by screening the Tango HTS library (∼500,000 compounds) at 50 μM using the UDP-Glo biochemical assay. Screening was performed by Evotec. Confirmed hits were orthogonally validated for MGAT1 inhibition in single-point and dose-response UDP-Glo assays and for direct binding to MGAT1 by SPR.

UDP-Glo activity assays with WT and mutant protein

UDP-GlcNAc transferase reactions were performed at room temperature in freshly prepared 1× reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 1 mM MnCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.005% Triton-X100, and 1 mM BSA. MGAT1 WT or catalytic-dead mutants (D211A, D289A, or D211A/D289A) were assayed in a dose-response format using a five-point, 2-fold serial dilution starting at 50 nM (2× concentration). Reactions were initiated by adding 5 μl of 50 nM MGAT1 (T30–N445) (25 nM final) with 5 μl of 100 μM 3α,6α-mannopentaose (Sigma, #M0925-5MG, 50 μM final) and 260 μM UDP-GlcNAc (Promega Corporation, V7072, final concentration: 130 μM). Reactions were incubated for 60 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of 10 μl of UDP-Glo Glycosyltransferase Assay Reagent (Promega Corporation, #V6963) was added to each reaction and incubated for an additional 60 min. Luminescence, corresponding to UDP production, was measured using a PHERAstar plate reader (BMG Labtech) using ProxiPlate 384-shallow well Plus white plates (Revvity, #6008280).

Biochemical inhibitor potency assay

Compound potency was measured using a related UDP-Glo assay format. Test compounds were prepared as 10 mM stock solutions in DMSO and dispensed into assay plates (Revvity, Part# 6008280) as 10-point, 3-fold serial dilution using Echo Acoustic Liquid Handler (Beckman, model 655), with a top final reaction concentration of 100 μM and a dispense volume of 0.5 μl/well. Enzyme inhibition reactions were conducted at room temperature in 1× reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 0.015 mM EDTA, 2 mM MnCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.008% Triton X-100 and 0.01% BSA. Reactions were initiated by adding 2 μl of 12.5 nM MGAT1(T30–N445) (5 nM final) to 5 μl of 100 μM 3α,6α-mannopentaose (50 μM final) and 260 μM UDP-GlcNAc (130 μM final). After 60 min at room temperature, UDP production was measured using the UDP-Glo reagent (Promega Corporation, V6963) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with an additional 60 min incubation, and luminescence was measured on a PHERAstar plate reader (BMG Labtech). IC50 values were determined in Genedata Screener with a standard dose–response curve fit with top and bottom plateaus constrained to 0 and -100, respectively.

The apo structure of MGAT1 was obtained similarly, except that only MGAT1 protein (16.1 mg/ml) was used in the drop, the reservoir contained 8% PEG 8000 and 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) and seeds from the MGAT1-UDP crystals were used instead of lysozyme seed stock. Diffraction data were collected to 1.7 Å resolution at the National Synchrotron Light Source II (beamline 17-ID-1) and data processing and structure determination followed the workflow used for the MGAT1-UDP complex.

The MGAT1-UDP-GlcNAc complex was obtained by soaking apo crystals with 10 mM UDP-GlcNAc and 10 mM MnCl2 for 2 h. Apo crystals for soaking were prepared as for the MGAT1-UDP structure, except that protein alone was used to set up the drops and MGAT1-UDP crystals for seeding instead of lysozyme crystals. Diffraction data were collected to 2.0 Å resolution at the Canadian Light Source (beamline CMCFID). Data processing and structure determination followed the workflow used for the MGAT1–UDP complex except that the apo MGAT structure was used for molecular replacement.

Crystals of the MGAT1-TNG-9333 complex were obtained by sitting-drop vapor diffusion after incubation of MGAT1 at 21.7 mg/ml with 1 mM TNG-9333 (10% DMSO final) on ice for 1 h. Drops containing 200 nl of the protein–compound mixture were combined with 180 nl of reservoir solution composed of 0.2 M triammonium citrate (pH 7.0), 20% PEG 3350, along with 20 nl of seed stock from MGAT1 apo crystals. Drops were equilibrated over 20 μl of reservoir solution at 20 °C. Well-diffracting crystals grew within days. A complete 1.9 Å dataset was collected at the Super Photon Ring – 8 GeV synchrotron facility (beamline BL45XU). Data processing and structure determination followed the same workflow used for the MGAT1-UDP-GlcNAc complex.

Crystals of the MGAT1 ternary complex with TNG-9333 and UDP were obtained as above, except that protein at 23.12 mg/ml was incubated with 1 mM TNG-9333, 10 mM UDP, and 2 mM MnCl2 (10% DMSO final), and the reservoir contained 0.1 M HEPES NaOH (pH7.5), 20% PEG 8000. A complete 2.0 Å dataset was collected at the Australian Synchrotron facility (beamline ANSTO-MX2). Data processing and structure determination followed the workflow used for the MGAT1–UDP-GlcNac complex.

SPR binding assay

The binding between compounds and MGAT1 was evaluated using Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) via a Biacore 8K (Cytiva) at 25 °C. Biotinylated AVI-tagged MGAT1 (A104-N445) was immobilized on a SA S Series sensor chip (Cytiva, BR100531) in Immobilization Buffer (25 mM Bicine (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, 0.05% P20), using the manufacturer's protocol. Protein was immobilized to ∼9000 response units (RU). Compounds were prepared in an 11-point, 1:1 serial dilution starting at 10 μM for TNG-9333 and 250 μM for UDP and injected at a flow rate of 30 μl/min using Assa Buffer (25 mM Bicine (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, 0.05% P20, 5% DMSO). Each compound concentration was tested using a 60-s association injection of the sample at a flow rate of 30 μl/min and a 240-s dissociation measurement in Assay Buffer. Data were analyzed using the Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva) with a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine steady-state and kinetic parameters.

SPR competition assay

The competition of binding to MGAT1 between UDP and TNG-9333 were performed using an A-B-A injection protocol on Biacore S200 instrument (Cytiva) at 25 °C. Biotinylated AVI-tagged MGAT1 (A104-N445) was immobilized on the SA S Series sensorchip (Cytiva, BR100531) in Immobilization Buffer (25 mM Bicine (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, 0.05% P20), using the manufacturer's protocol.

For the competition study with UDP (J&K Scientific, 155296), the protein was immobilized to ∼3600 RUs. UDP (at 5000 μM) was brought to steady state with MGAT1 (A104-N445) via pre- and post-injection at saturating levels (Injection A) in Assay Buffer (25 mM Bicine (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MnCl2, 2 mM DTT, 0.05% P20, 5% DMSO). TNG-9333 was prepared in 10-point, 1:1 serial dilution starting at 10 μM top concentration in 5000 μM UDP (Injection B). Each concentration of compound was tested using a 60-s association injection of the sample at a flow rate of 30 μl/min and a 240-s dissociation measurement in Assay Buffer. Data were analyzed using the Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva).

For the competition study with TNG-9333, the protein was immobilized to ∼ 3500 RUs. TNG-9333 (20 μM) was brought to a steady-state with MGAT1 (A104-N445) via pre- and post-injection at saturating levels (Injection A) in Assay Buffer. UDP was prepared in a 10-point, 1:1 serial dilution starting at 250 μM top concentration in 20 μM TNG-9333 (Injection B). UDP dilutions were injected using the same parameters and buffer conditions as described for UDP competition.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Thermal stability was measured in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 15 μM EDTA, 2 mM MnCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 5% DMSO. A 5 μl aliquot of 18.6 nM MGAT1(T30–N445) (9.3 final μM) was mixed with 5 μl of 200 μM TNG-9333 (100 μM final) or 10,000 μM UDP (5000 μM final), or both ligands at those final concentrations in the 384-well plates (Roch, 4729749001). SYPRO Orange dye was added using an Echo Acoustic Liquid Handler (Beckman, model 650) as 20 nl of a 5000× stock to achieve a final concentration of 10×. Plates were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h with shaking. Melting curves were recorded on a LightCycler 480II real-time PCR (Roche) by increasing the temperature from 20 °C and 99 °C at 1 °C/min. Data were analyzed using Protein Thermal Shift Software 1.4 (Thermo).
