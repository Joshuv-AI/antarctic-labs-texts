Metrnl in diabetes and its complications: divergent evidence and translational prospects
Authors: Shengwei Zhang, Jiayang Tang, Guiyu Liu, Biying Meng, Kang Hu, Yongqiang Zhou, Xiaoliang Yang, Qingyun Tang, Yuyang Liu, Ling Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeteorin-like protein (Metrnl) is a novel secreted protein produced by multiple tissues, which is involved in metabolic regulation, immune homeostasis, and tissue repair. In recent years, research on the role of Metrnl in diabetes has advanced significantly. However, substantial controversies persist regarding its expression dynamics, protective roles, and underlying molecular mechanisms in diabetes and its complications.Main bodyThis review synthesizes current evidence to systematically analyze the complex roles of Metrnl in diabetes. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that Metrnl primarily improves insulin resistance, protects pancreatic β-cells, and mitigates diabetes related damage to kidney, heart, and skin through multiple pathways, including the KIT receptor, AMPK and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways.In contrast, clinical data exhibit significant heterogeneity. Reports on circulating Metrnl levels in patients with type 2 diabetes are highly inconsistent, with findings of decreased, increased, or no significant changes. Such discrepancies are largely attributed to methodological inconsistencies and clinical confounders. Similar inconsistencies are observed in studies on gestational diabetes. Importantly, Metrnl also displays markedly different or even opposing expression patterns across various diabetic complications, highlighting its tissue-specific regulatory characteristics.ConclusionsIn summary, Metrnl is a pleiotropic molecule involved in the pathophysiological processes of diabetes and its complications, and it holds potential as a candidate biomarker or therapeutic target. However, its clinical translation faces key challenges, including insufficient standardization of detection methods, unclear characterization of receptor systems, and undefined pharmacological properties. Therefore, although Metrnl shows promise in preclinical models, its clinical utility requires rigorous validation, and its roles should be interpreted cautiously based on the current body of evidence.Keywords: Metrnl, Diabetes mellitus, Diabetic complications, Insulin resistance

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder that has reached pandemic proportions worldwide. Recent epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 589 million adults were living with diabetes by 2024, representing 11.11% of the global adult population, and this number is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [1]. Persistent hyperglycemia drives the development of numerous complications, which constitute the leading causes of diabetes-related disability and mortality, and contribute significantly to socioeconomic burdens [2, 3]. Although current therapeutic strategies—including oral hypoglycemic agents, insulin, and newer pharmacological formulations—can ameliorate hyperglycemia, a substantial number of patients continue to experience inadequate glycemic control and the progression of diabetic complications [4, 5]. Consequently, the identification of novel therapeutic targets, particularly molecules with multiorgan protective properties, represents a critical avenue for improving the clinical outcomes of diabetes and its associated complications.

Meteorin-like (Metrnl) is a secreted protein that was initially identified in 2009 and is expressed in and secreted by a range of cell types, including adipocytes, endothelial cells, mononuclear macrophages, and myocytes [6, 7]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Metrnl participates in the regulation of diverse pathophysiological processes, such as metabolic, cardiovascular, and autoimmune disorders, suggesting its potential role as a key mediator of interorgan crosstalk and systemic homeostasis [8–10]. While existing reviews have focused predominantly on the role of Metrnl in cardiovascular diseases [11–14], emerging evidence highlights its critical involvement in the pathogenesis and progression of diabetes and its complications [15, 16]. However, the current findings in the context of diabetes remain inconsistent, particularly regarding its expression dynamics, seemingly dual protective and detrimental effects, and the underlying molecular mechanisms, highlighting a lack of systematic synthesis.

This review aims to systematically summarize the latest research progress on the role of Metrnl in diabetes and its complications, focusing on its expression characteristics in circulation and local tissues, controversies surrounding its protective or damaging roles, and the related signaling pathway mechanisms. By integrating preclinical and clinical evidence, this review provides a comprehensive perspective for a deeper understanding of the role of Metrnl in diabetic pathophysiology and discusses its translational prospects as a potential therapeutic target or biomarker, along with future research directions.

Overview of Metrnl

Gene and protein structure of Metrnl

The human Metrnl gene is located on chromosome 17 (17q25.3), while its mouse ortholog resides on chromosome 11 (11qE2) [17]. This subtelomeric region is characterized by high gene density and genetic instability and has been implicated in certain rare genetic syndromes [18]. The Metrnl protein is evolutionarily conserved, sharing approximately 77% amino acid sequence identity between humans and mice, with nearly complete conservation of core functional domains—a feature that supports the translational relevance of murine models in preclinical research [19].

The primary human Metrnl translation product consists of 311 amino acids (Fig. 1A). Its N-terminus harbors a 45-amino acid signal peptide that directs the protein to the classical endoplasmic reticulum–Golgi secretory pathway [20, 21]. Following signal peptide cleavage, the mature secreted form comprises 266 amino acids with a predicted molecular weight of ~ 29 kDa. Structural predictions suggest that Metrnl contains 9 α-helices, 15 β-sheets, 15 loop regions, and 5 conserved disulfide bonds [22]. The mature protein is organized into two principal domains: an N-terminal CUB (Complement C1r/C1s, Uegf, Bmp1) domain (46–175 aa) and a C-terminal NTR (Netrin-like) domain (189–311 aa). Structural bioinformatic analyses suggest that specific loop regions (L5 and L10) in the CUB domain, along with the α-6 helix and β-13 sheet in the NTR domain, are involved in KIT receptor binding. The flexible hinge (176-188aa) displays high conformational dynamics that mediate conformational shifts between the CUB and NTR domains, thereby enabling interactions with multiple ligand partners (Fig. 1B). For instance, the CUB and NTR domains of Metrnl each bind to an individual KIT receptor, and putative conformational changes within the hinge region drive KIT dimerization and subsequent activation of KIT signaling [21]. Ten high-risk missense SNPs have been identified in Metrnl: G78A, G87S, R163W, L172V, E195A, C201S, D204N, T202P, A285G, and R293G. These variants markedly destabilize the protein, promote its aggregation or misfolding, and consequently compromise protein expression and biological functions (Fig. 1C).

Together, Metrnl and the neurotrophic factor Metrn (Meteorin) constitute an evolutionarily conserved two-member protein family that shares approximately 40% amino acid sequence identity [23]. Despite structural similarities, they exhibit significant differences in expression patterns and functions. Metrn is expressed primarily in the nervous system and is involved in neural development and plasticity, nerve injury repair, and embryonic development [24–28]. In contrast, Metrnl has a broader expression pattern and is particularly enriched in metabolically active tissues and barrier organs, with functions more focused on regulating metabolism, immunity, and tissue repair [29].

Metrnl undergoes post-translational modifications. Mouse Metrnl contains an N-glycosylation site at Asn103, which increases its effective molecular weight and may promote its cellular secretion. Notably, this glycosylation site is not conserved in human Metrnl, where the corresponding residue at position 103 is histidine (His103) [30]. Ubiquitination represents another regulatory mechanism that leads to the proteasomal degradation of Metrnl under high glucose conditions [31].

Cellular sources and tissue distribution of Metrnl

Metrnl exhibits a distinct tissue- and cell-type-specific expression pattern, notably enriched in key target organs of diabetes and its complications. Data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project provide an overview of its distribution across human tissues [32] (Fig. 2). A precise understanding of this spatial expression and the relative contributions of different sources to circulating Metrnl is fundamental to deciphering its multifaceted biological functions.

Metrnl can act locally through paracrine or autocrine mechanisms in diabetic target organs. In metabolic tissues, pancreatic islet β-cells express Metrnl, which may contribute to maintaining β-cell homeostasis [33]. In adipose tissue, Metrnl expression is approximately 3–5 times higher in subcutaneous white adipose tissue (sWAT) than in mesenteric or perivascular depots, with comparable levels in adipocytes and stromal vascular cells [30]. Adipocyte-derived Metrnl modulates local inflammation and differentiation, thereby influencing insulin sensitivity [34, 35]. Similarly, skeletal muscle secretes Metrnl in response to contraction, activating intramuscular pathways to promote glucose uptake [36]. Within complication-prone organs, locally produced Metrnl plays critical roles: renal tubular expression is involved in lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function in diabetic kidney disease [37], whereas keratinocyte-derived Metrnl is essential for diabetic wound re-epithelialization [38]. Additionally, activated macrophages, particularly those in the M2 phenotype, serve as a significant source, linking Metrnl to diabetes-associated inflammation [38], and cardiomyocyte-derived Metrnl functions autocrinely to protect against diabetic cardiomyopathy [39].

In contrast, the circulating pool of Metrnl is primarily sustained by vascular endothelial cells. Although high expression is observed in classic metabolic tissues such as sWAT adipocytes/stromal cells [20, 30, 40], gastrointestinal epithelium [20, 40, 41], and hepatocytes [42], tissue-specific knockout models indicate that intestinal, hepatic, and adipocyte sources contribute minimally to basal plasma levels. Conversely, endothelial-specific deletion reduces circulating Metrnl levels by approximately 75% in mice [20], and β-cell-specific knockout also significantly decreases Metrnl levels [33]. Metrnl expression is dynamic and responds to physiological and pathological stimuli. Cold exposure induces its expression in adipose tissue, increasing its circulating levels [34]. As a myokine, its basal expression and secretion from muscle are markedly increased by exercise, leading to a multifold increase in plasma concentration in both mice and humans [36, 43, 44]. These findings collectively position the endothelium as the principal source of systemic Metrnl, clarifying its role as a circulating indicator of metabolic and inflammatory status.

Notably, substantial discrepancies have been reported in circulating Metrnl levels among human diabetic cohorts [45–47], representing a central controversy in the field. This inconsistency is closely associated with the cellular origin and tissue distribution of Metrnl. On one hand, Metrnl expression is highly dynamic and can be regulated by diverse physiological and pathological stimuli, such as cold exposure, exercise, metabolic state, and inflammatory levels, thereby causing fluctuations in circulating concentrations. On the other hand, the extent of tissue damage induced by hyperglycemia varies significantly among individuals, and the relative contribution of Metrnl from different tissues to the total circulating concentration also differs substantially. As a result, distinct differences in Metrnl levels are observed across various diabetic complications.

In summary, Metrnl operates through a dual “systemic–local” secretory architecture. Circulating Metrnl, which is largely of endothelial origin, may mediate endocrine effects, while locally produced Metrnl in target tissues executes precise protective functions within their microenvironments. This integrated network allows Metrnl to coordinate metabolic homeostasis, inflammatory responses, and tissue repair in diabetes. Its widespread, stimulus-responsive expression profile classifies Metrnl not as a classic glandular hormone but as a pleiotropic messenger released by diverse cell types, integrating systemic physiology through autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine actions.

Physiological functions and signal transduction of Metrnl

A critical step in elucidating the molecular mechanisms of Metrnl is the identification of its specific cell surface receptor. A key breakthrough was the discovery that Metrnl binds with high affinity to the extracellular domain of the stem cell factor (SCF) receptor KIT on endothelial cells. This interaction induces KIT dimerization and tyrosine autophosphorylation, leading to activation of the downstream SRC/AKT1 signaling pathway [48]. Mechanistically, Metrnl may exploit its intervening hinge region to facilitate KIT receptor dimerization, thereby functionally mimicking the SCF homodimer to initiate KIT signaling [22].

In addition to the KIT-SRC/AKT1 axis, Metrnl modulates several other key signaling pathways, including LKB1/AMPK, Sirt3/AMPK, AMPK/PGC1α, and Wnt/β-catenin [33, 37, 39, 49]. The integrated activity of these pathways underlies the pleiotropic functions of Metrnl. In metabolic regulation, it promotes white adipose tissue browning and enhances skeletal muscle glucose uptake, collectively improving whole-body insulin sensitivity [34, 49]. With respect to immune homeostasis, Metrnl drives macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production, and increases the activity of anti-inflammatory mediators, thereby mitigating tissue inflammation [34, 50]. Furthermore, Metrnl facilitates tissue repair processes by promoting angiogenesis, accelerating wound healing, and improving muscle regeneration [6, 31, 38]. These multifaceted physiological functions enable Metrnl to exert coordinated, multiorgan protective effects throughout the pathogenesis of diabetes and its complications.

Although Metrnl generally exhibits protective effects in metabolic disorders, its wide tissue distribution and pleiotropic functions indicate that excessive or dysregulated Metrnl signaling may give rise to potential off-target risks. For instance, Jackson et al. [51] reported that Metrnl may inhibit anti-tumor CD8 + T cell responses by disrupting mitochondrial function. Liu et al. [52] further demonstrated that Metrnl can impair fungal phagocytosis and killing capacity in macrophages, thereby negatively regulating host antifungal immunity. Therefore, further studies are needed to systematically characterize its safety profile, off-target interactions, and context-specific detrimental effects in a tissue specific manner.

Metrnl and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

As the most common form of diabetes, T2DM is pathologically defined by insulin resistance coupled with a progressive decline in pancreatic β-cell function, culminating in chronic hyperglycemia [53]. This section systematically examines the role of Metrnl in T2DM pathophysiology, with a focus on the expression patterns of circulating Metrnl in patient populations and its association with the core pathogenic mechanisms of the disease.

Expression profile of Metrnl in T2DM

Despite consistent preclinical evidence showing reduced levels of circulating Metrnl in diabetic animal models and demonstrating its protective effects on insulin sensitivity and complications [37–39, 54], clinical findings in T2DM patients are markedly heterogeneous and often contradictory (Table 1). An analysis of 19 clinical studies revealed a lack of consensus: nine reported significantly lower circulating Metrnl levels in T2DM patients [55–63], seven reported elevated levels [64–70], and three reported no significant difference [38, 71, 72]. This inconsistency is further reflected in meta-analyses, with two reporting no significant difference between T2DM patients and controls [45, 46], while a more recent and comprehensive analysis by Xie et al. [47] concluded that circulating Metrnl is significantly reduced in T2DM patients. This significant heterogeneity points to the influence of multiple methodological and biological confounders.

Methodological variability

Commercial ELISA kits used in different studies cover at least nine distinct brands, which vary considerably in reliability of standard curve fitting and detection sensitivity, which represents one of the major technical sources of heterogeneity in reported measurements. For instance, Zheng et al. [57] directly compared two widely used kits and found that the R&D Systems DY7867-05 kit exhibited significantly better curve fitting, assay reproducibility, and antigen specificity than the Aviscera Bioscience SK00478-02 kit. Critically, Metrnl concentrations measured by the two kits showed no meaningful correlation, directly rendering data across studies incomparable.

The reported absolute concentrations of circulating Metrnl vary strikingly, by more than 2000-fold (e.g., 2.01 pg/mL in some studies versus 4.41 ng/mL in others), a range far exceeding plausible physiological and pathological ranges. This indicates that the observed variability primarily stems from systematic methodological bias rather than genuine biological differences. Notably, according to manufacturer specifications, the detection range of the CusaBio Biotech (China) CSB-EL013718HU kit is 156 pg/mL-10,000 pg/mL, and that of the human Metrnl kit from Bioassay Technology Laboratory (China) is 50 pg/mL-15,000 pg/mL. However, the median Metrnl concentrations reported in several studies fall outside the certified ranges of the corresponding kits, suggesting potential failure to rigorously exclude outliers and a heightened risk of assay bias. In future meta analyses, such datasets may require careful screening and exclusion to improve analytical reliability.

Sample type also introduces non-negligible detection bias. Studies using serum samples more frequently report reduced circulating Metrnl levels, whereas those using plasma tend to show elevated values [47]. This discrepancy implies that sample processing procedures may affect the analytical stability of Metrnl and thereby distort measured concentrations.

Furthermore, Metrnl protein exhibits poor stability. As validated by Zheng et al. [57], substantial degradation occurs within days when samples are stored at room temperature or 4℃, leading to artifactual underestimation of measured concentrations.

Biological and clinical heterogeneity

Pharmacologic intervention represents the most critical modifiable confounding factor. Statin therapy has been consistently associated with elevated circulating Metrnl levels [59], likely through mechanisms involving regulated adipocyte metabolism. In contrast, the effect of metformin on Metrnl is controversial and exhibits clear time dependence. Long-term metformin treatment (3 months) has been shown to reduce Metrnl levels [68], whereas short-term administration (2 weeks) caused no significant change [56], possibly because metabolic effects had not fully emerged. This indicates that duration of pharmacotherapy should be carefully controlled as a key variable in future studies.

Beyond medication, even studies restricted to newly diagnosed, drug-naïve patients to circumvent medication effects still yield divergent results, reporting either decreased [56–58, 60, 62], increased [65, 68], or unchanged levels [72]. This suggests that intrinsic features such as disease severity independently modulate Metrnl expression and secretion.

Furthermore, the relationship between Metrnl and dysglycemia is nonlinear. Several studies have demonstrated significant differences in Metrnl levels between prediabetic individuals and those with established T2DM [56, 58, 65], whereas no difference was found between T2DM and those with IGT [59]. This suggests the existence of a potential threshold effect in Metrnl regulation that warrants validation in larger cohorts.

Importantly, changes in circulating Metrnl levels are closely related to the type of diabetic complication. Compared to T2DM patients without complications, Metrnl levels have been reported to be elevated in those with diabetic retinopathy (DR) [71], unchanged or decreased in patients with diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) [38, 70], and decreased in patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) [69] or progressive diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [61, 73]. Such complication-specific divergence further contributes to inconsistent findings across studies.

Selection bias in study populations also cannot be overlooked. Most available studies have been conducted in Chinese and Turkish cohorts, limiting the generalizability of findings to other ethnic and genetic backgrounds worldwide. In addition, some studies focused on highly specific subgroups such as patients with sarcopenic obesity [64], introducing further selection bias.

Finally, exercise and cold exposure are well-established potent physiological inducers of Metrnl [74, 75] that markedly upregulate its expression and secretion. However, these factors are rarely controlled or matched in observational studies.

In summary, the conflicting evidence regarding circulating Metrnl levels in T2DM primarily stems from a lack of methodological standardization and considerable clinical heterogeneity. Consequently, the available evidence precludes its current use as a reliable, standalone diagnostic biomarker for T2DM.

Metrnl and insulin resistance

Insulin resistance is a pathological condition in which peripheral target tissues—primarily skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue—exhibit diminished sensitivity to insulin [76]. This impairment leads to reduced glucose uptake and utilization, necessitating compensatory hyperinsulinemia to maintain euglycemia [77]. Its severity is typically quantified using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [78]. While numerous clinical studies have explored the association between circulating Metrnl and HOMA-IR as a surrogate for its insulin-sensitizing effects, the evidence remains contentious.

Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated a significant inverse correlation between circulating Metrnl levels and HOMA-IR in populations including both diabetic and nondiabetic individuals [56, 58–60, 62, 63, 79]. Notably, one study revealed that after adjusting for confounding factors, the inverse association between serum Metrnl and HOMA-IR remained significant only in T2DM patients but not in healthy controls, implying that the insulin-sensitizing of Metrnl effect may be particularly relevant under conditions of established metabolic dysregulation [55]. Conversely, other studies have reported a positive correlation between Metrnl and HOMA-IR, suggesting that elevated Metrnl may paradoxically be linked to, or even contribute to, insulin resistance in certain contexts [65, 66].

Interventional studies provide further insight into the dynamic relationship between Metrnl and insulin sensitivity. An 8-week resistance training regimen was shown to significantly increase circulating Metrnl levels in patients with T2DM, accompanied by a concurrent improvement in HOMA-IR [80]. Additionally, elevated postoperative Metrnl levels have been associated with reduced HOMA-IR in T2DM patients following bariatric surgery [81]. These findings suggest that Metrnl may act as a potential mediator of improved insulin sensitivity in response to both exercise and metabolic surgery.

The mechanisms underlying the ability of Metrnl to ameliorate insulin resistance involve multiple organ-specific activities. Studies have revealed distinct modes of action in adipose tissue. Spiegelman et al. [34] demonstrated that acute Metrnl administration to high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice improves systemic insulin sensitivity through the recruitment of eosinophils to adipose tissue. These cells secrete IL-4 and IL-13, which activate the STAT6 pathway in resident macrophages, promoting their polarization to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. The M2 macrophages subsequently induce white adipose tissue browning and increase energy expenditure through catecholamine signaling.

In contrast to the eosinophil-dependent mechanism, Li et al. [35] demonstrated through adipocyte-specific Metrnl knockout models that, under conditions of a HFD or leptin deficiency, adipocyte-derived Metrnl acts predominantly in an autocrine/paracrine manner. Through PPARγ pathway activation, Metrnl promotes adipocyte differentiation and suppresses inflammation, improving insulin sensitivity via a process independent of adipose browning or immune cell recruitment. A key finding was that systemic administration of recombinant Metrnl failed to reverse insulin resistance in adipocyte-specific knockout mice, highlighting the essential autocrine/paracrine role of locally produced Metrnl within adipose tissue.

Skeletal muscle represents another key site of Metrnl activity. Jung et al. [49]showed in palmitate-treated C2C12 myotubes and HFD-fed mice that Metrnl activates the AMPK–PGC-1α and PPARδ–PGC-1α axes, upregulating genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and improving insulin resistance. Lee et al. [36] utilized electrically stimulated C2C12 cells along with HFD and db/db mouse models to show that Metrnl activates the calcium-dependent AMPKα2 pathway. This leads to the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK pathway and HDAC5, relieving its repression of the GLUT4 gene, and the simultaneous phosphorylation of TBC1D1, promoting the translocation of the GLUT4 to the plasma membrane. This dual action synergistically enhances glucose uptake into skeletal muscle at both the transcriptional and posttranslational levels.

Metrnl and pancreatic β-cell protection

A progressive decline in pancreatic β-cell function represents another core pathological mechanism in the development and progression of T2DM [82]. Studies have indicated that Metrnl is predominantly expressed within the β-cells of pancreatic islets [33] and exerts protective effects by maintaining β-cell functional identity and regulating the balance between β-cell proliferation and apoptosis.

With respect to the maintenance of β-cell function, Zhou et al. [33] demonstrated that Metrnl is essential for sustaining the expression of key β-cell identity genes (Pdx1 and Mafa) and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. In db/db and HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice, the loss of Metrnl triggered β-cell transdifferentiation into α-cells via upregulation of the transcription factor c-Jun, thereby reducing the functional β-cell mass and impairing insulin secretion. This study also identified the transcription factor KLF6 as a potential upstream regulator of METRNL expression in islets. In addition to maintaining cell identity, Metrnl also enhances β-cell survival and proliferation. Hu et al. [83] showed in MIN6 β-cells and db/db mice that Metrnl activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, significantly inhibiting high glucose-induced apoptosis and stimulating β-cell proliferation, which collectively improved insulin secretion under hyperglycemic conditions.

Despite compelling preclinical evidence, the clinical relevance of circulating Metrnl as a biomarker for β-cell function remains uncertain. In contrast to the functional data, Wang et al. [66] reported no significant correlation between serum Metrnl levels and established indices of β-cell function (fasting C-peptide, AUC0−2 and HOMA2-%B) in T2DM patients. This discrepancy between basic and clinical observations may arise from several factors, including whether systemic Metrnl levels accurately reflect its local paracrine/autocrine actions within islets, clinical heterogeneity, and the influence of different diabetes stages. Thus, while preclinical studies strongly suggest that Metrnl is a key local defender of β-cell health, the utility of its circulating level as a reliable clinical biomarker for human β-cell function warrants further rigorous investigation.

Collectively, while preclinical studies have outlined key protective roles of Metrnl in T2DM, nearly all mechanistic evidence originates from rodent models and in vitro systems. Translational evidence in humans remains limited, and caution should be observed when extending these findings to human pathophysiology and potential therapeutic applications.

Metrnl and type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM)

T1DM is an autoimmune disorder characterized by the aberrant activation of autoreactive T cells that infiltrate pancreatic islets and specifically destroy β-cells, leading to an absolute deficiency of insulin secretion [84, 85]. Emerging evidence indicates that Metrnl may confer protection against T1DM progression through a dual mechanism: immunomodulation to suppress autoimmune attack, and direct enhancement of β-cell resilience under inflammatory stress.

From an immunomodulatory perspective, Yao et al. [86] reported that intravenous Metrnl administration markedly attenuated pancreatic inflammation in nonobese diabetic mice. Metrnl treatment promoted the proliferation and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), suppressed the activation of autoreactive T cells, and shifted the cytokine profile from proinflammatory (e.g., IFN-γ and IL-17) to anti-inflammatory (e.g., IL-4 and IL-10), thereby delaying the progression of T1DM. These immunomodulatory effects may be further enhanced by phenolic compounds derived from licorice root [87].

Notably, several important scientific questions remain unresolved in current research. First, the observed immunomodulatory effects are based on systemic administration of Metrnl, which cannot distinguish between pancreas-specific local immune regulation and systemic immune modulation. Sustained systemic immunomodulation may therefore carry potential risks of long-term immunosuppression. For example, Liu et al. [52] reported that Metrnl can negatively regulate host antifungal immunity. Second, available preclinical studies are limited to short-term intervention and lack long-term safety data. It remains unclear whether the immune effects are durable, preventive rather than therapeutic, or dose-dependent.

Metrnl also has direct β-cell protective effects. Costa-Junior et al. [50] reported that Metrnl inhibited the expression and secretion of CXCL10 induced by inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-1β in human EndoC-βH1 β-cells and iPSC-derived islet cells, thereby mitigating the direct cytotoxic effects of inflammatory signaling on β-cells and promoting their survival. Furthermore, Tayebi et al. [36] reported in an STZ-induced T1DM rat model that an intervention combining cold-water swimming and cinnamon significantly increased systemic METRNL levels, which was accompanied by the downregulation of HDAC5 (a transcriptional repressor of GLUT4) and decreased fasting blood glucose levels, suggesting a potential glucose-lowering role of Metrnl in T1DM.

Metrnl and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)

GDM is defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy and is typically diagnosed at 24–28 weeks of gestation [88, 89]. Metrnl has been detected in breast milk and umbilical cord blood, with mammary alveolar tissue identified as its primary secretion source in milk. However, findings regarding circulating Metrnl levels in patients with GDM remain inconsistent.

Some studies report elevated serum Metrnl levels in GDM patients. Yavuzkir et al. [90] reported that between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, serum Metrnl levels were significantly higher in GDM patients than in those with normal glucose tolerance (NGT), and this elevation persisted postpartum. Additionally, umbilical cord blood Metrnl levels were also significantly greater, suggesting a potential fetal origin or maternofetal transport mechanism. Yavuz et al. [91] further reported that compared with those in non-GDM mothers, Metrnl concentrations in the plasma and all stages of breast milk (colostrum, transitional milk, and mature milk) were higher in GDM mothers, with milk concentrations generally exceeding those in plasma.

However, other studies have reported the opposite trend. Reis et al. [92] and Damirova et al. [93] both observed that serum Metrnl levels were significantly lower in GDM women than in NGT women at 24–28 weeks of gestation, suggesting that low Metrnl levels may be associated with an increased risk of GDM onset.

Lappas et al. [94], controlling for delivery mode (all by elective cesarean section), reported that GDM did not affect maternal plasma Metrnl levels but did significantly reduce umbilical cord blood Metrnl levels. Furthermore, umbilical cord Metrnl levels were positively correlated with maternal-fetal glucose levels, and the concentration of Metrnl was generally greater in umbilical cord than in maternal blood. These findings suggest that the placenta or the fetus itself may be the primary source of umbilical cord Metrnl, and that GDM may disrupt this local secretory or metabolic process.

In summary, the association between GDM and circulating Metrnl levels remains controversial. In terms of gestational endocrine regulation, maternal estrogen and progesterone levels increase progressively with gestational age, which can influence Metrnl expression in various tissues, which leads to notable variability even when sampling is performed at the standard diagnostic gestational window.

At the fetal and placental level, findings from Lappas et al. [94]suggest that the fetus and placenta may serve as important local sources of Metrnl in maternal circulation. Hyperglycemia and inflammatory stress associated with GDM exert bidirectional regulatory effects on placental Metrnl expression, and compromised placental barrier function further contributes to the inconsistent observations.

Moreover, similar to research in T2DM, methodological variations and clinical confounders exacerbate heterogeneity in GDM studies. At the assay level, commonly used ELISA kits have not been rigorously validated in pregnancy-specific samples with high hormonal background; clinically, key confounding factors including pre-pregnancy BMI, intervention strategies, and physical activity status directly compromise the reliability of Metrnl measurements. Whether Metrnl can be applied for early screening of GDM high-risk individuals and predict long-term metabolic health in their offspring requires further investigation.

Metrnl and DKD

DKD is a prevalent and severe microvascular complication of diabetes and a major cause of end-stage renal disease. Current evidence consistently points to a protective role of Metrnl in the pathogenesis of DKD [95].

Two clinical studies have investigated the association between serum Metrnl levels and DKD. Wang et al. [61] reported that serum Metrnl levels were significantly lower in the macroalbuminuria group than in the normoalbuminuria and microalbuminuria groups, with no significant difference observed between the latter two groups. In contrast, Chen et al. [73] reported that serum Metrnl levels decreased in a graded manner with increasing urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) categories, and the differences between these categories were statistically significant. The primary distinction in clinical studies is the varying pattern of serum Metrnl changes in relation to UACR, which may be associated with the disease stage, sample size, and inclusion criteria.

Tissue-level studies further revealed abnormalities in Metrnl expression in DKD. Zhou et al. [37] and Lin et al. [54] both reported significantly reduced Metrnl expression in kidney tissue, particularly in renal tubular epithelial cells, which was negatively correlated with renal function indicators, suggesting the synchronized dysregulation of circulating and local tissue Metrnl expression in DKD.

With respect to mechanisms, Zhou et al. [37] demonstrated in diabetic mouse models (db/db and STZ/HFD-induced) that downregulation of renal Metrnl is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive lipid accumulation. Intervention via either exogenous recombinant Metrnl supplementation or AAV9-mediated kidney-specific overexpression activated the PGC-1α/Sirt3 signaling pathway. This activation promoted AMPK phosphorylation to enhance mitophagy and sustain mitochondrial homeostasis, while concurrently upregulating the key thermogenic protein UCP1 to stimulate lipid consumption, collectively mitigating renal lipid deposition. Furthermore, Lin et al. [54] revealed the role of Metrnl in anti-renal fibrosis. Systemic overexpression of Metrnl via tail vein injection of adenovirus suppressed the activity of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway and reduced the production of fibrotic molecules such as α-SMA, thereby ameliorating renal pathological injury in db/db mice. These studies collectively indicate that Metrnl may protect against the pathological progression of DKD through the regulation of lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function and fibrotic signaling pathways.

Metrnl and diabetic cardiovascular complications

Diabetes is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, with atherosclerosis (AS) and diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) being two primary diabetic cardiovascular complications [96].

With respect to AS, multiple clinical studies suggest a close association between Metrnl and vascular functional status. El-Ashmawy et al. [58] reported that in individuals with NGT, IGT, and T2DM, serum Metrnl levels were negatively correlated with markers of endothelial dysfunction (ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and E-selectin) and clinical indicators of AS (CIMT and baPWV). Fadaei et al. [59] further revealed a gradient in this association: in T2DM patients, serum Metrnl was negatively correlated with ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and E-selectin; in prediabetic individuals, it correlated significantly only with sE-selectin; and no significant correlation was observed in NGT individuals. In Contrast, Li et al. [97] reported that compared with those without AS, T2DM patients with carotid AS had significantly higher serum Metrnl levels. The discrepancies among these clinical findings may arise from differences in the disease stages of enrolled patients. In the study by Li et al. [97], participants with carotid atherosclerosis likely included a higher proportion of patients in the advanced stages, whereas the studies by El-Ashmawy et al. [58] and Fadaei et al. [59] involved more early-stage cases. Collectively, these results suggest that the association between Metrnl and endothelial function may evolve with the progression of glucose metabolism disorders. Nevertheless, whether Metrnl directly regulates endothelial function requires further experimental validation.

With respect to DCM, Lu et al. [39] identified a direct cardioprotective role of Metrnl. In both the STZ-induced T1DM and db/db T2DM mouse models, cardiac Metrnl expression was significantly downregulated. Cardiac-specific knockout of Metrnl exacerbated hyperglycemia-induced myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, apoptosis, and oxidative stress injury. Conversely, cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of Metrnl effectively reversed these pathological alterations, an effect independent of changes in blood glucose or lipid levels. Mechanistic investigations revealed that Metrnl activates the LKB1/AMPK signaling pathway, leading to ULK1 phosphorylation and subsequent translocation of the STING protein to mitochondria. Within mitochondria, STING interacts with TRAF2 and undergoes ubiquitination and degradation, ultimately enhancing cardiomyocyte autophagy and conferring cardiac protection.

Metrnl and DFU

Given its high expression in skin and mucosal tissues, Metrnl is implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of DFU. Current evidence collectively indicates that Metrnl exerts protective effects involving multiple targets and cell types during diabetic wound healing, highlighting its potential as a novel bioactive molecule for DFU treatment.

At the circulatory level, Erdogan et al. [70] showed that serum Metrnl levels were significantly greater in DFU patients than in those without DFU, but its level was not significantly associated with the presence of infection or the type of infecting microorganism in DFU patients, suggesting that the role of Metrnl in DFU might be independent of the infection process. In contrast, Song et al. [38] reported that circulating Metrnl levels remained unchanged in the presence of skin wounds in both T2DM patients and diabetic mouse models. The two studies included only 31 and 11 DFU patients, respectively, and such limited sample sizes may reduce the representativeness of the findings. Moreover, unadjusted confounders, such as disease duration, underlying comorbidities, and medication use, may have influenced the measurement of circulating Metrnl levels, thereby contributing to the divergent conclusions.

At the tissue level, Metrnl is expressed primarily in epidermal keratinocytes in both human and mouse skin [31]. Two independent studies consistently demonstrated the downregulation of local Metrnl expression in wound tissues from STZ, HFD/STZ, and db/db diabetic mouse models, as well as in human DFU specimens, compared with non-diabetic controls. Diabetic conditions were shown to suppress the physiological upregulation of Metrnl expression during normal wound healing [31, 38]. Song et al. [38] further elucidated that high glucose conditions promote ubiquitination-mediated degradation of the Metrnl protein, leading to its reduced expression in diabetic wounds.

In functional intervention studies, topical application of recombinant Metrnl hydrogel significantly accelerated wound healing in STZ, HFD/STZ, and db/db diabetic mice without affecting plasma Metrnl levels, systemic insulin sensitivity, or observed hepatorenal toxicity [38]. Zhao et al. [98] also confirmed the promoting effect of topically applied recombinant Metrnl on wound healing in nondiabetic mouse models, suggesting that its activity is independent of the diabetic metabolic background.

Mechanistically, Metrnl participates in the regulation of diabetic wound repair through multiple coordinated pathways. Song et al. [38] reported that Metrnl, via the LKB1/AMPK signaling pathway, phosphorylates and activates AMPK, inhibits mitochondrial damage, and upregulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes (SOD and GSH) and the key ferroptosis inhibitory protein GPX4, thereby reducing oxidative stress and inhibiting ferroptosis in diabetic wounds. Meng et al. [31] showed that keratinocyte-derived Metrnl promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, and that M2 macrophages can, in turn, secrete Metrnl, forming a positive feedback loop that enhances the local repair microenvironment. Simultaneously, Metrnl, by binding to its receptor c-Kit, activates AKT (S473) phosphorylation, thereby promoting the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and vascular endothelial cells and accelerating re-epithelialization and angiogenesis in diabetic wounds [38].

Metrnl and DPN

DPN involves hyperglycemia-induced neuronal damage, axonal degeneration, and disruption of the myelin structure, ultimately leading to impaired nerve conduction function, which clinically manifests as symmetric distal sensory abnormalities, pain, and sensory loss [99].

At the circulatory level, Yao et al. [69] reported that serum Metrnl levels are significantly lower in T2DM patients with DPN than in those without DPN, identifying low Metrnl levels as an independent risk factor. However, it remains unclear whether the reduction in circulating Metrnl contributes to disease pathogenesis, reflects a compensatory protective response, or represents a secondary epiphenomenon. Moreover, consistent with findings in other diabetic complications, whether circulating Metrnl levels accurately reflect local Metrnl expression and bioactivity in neurons and peripheral nerves has not yet been clarified.

Mechanistically, Yuksel et al. [100] found that Metrnl increased DRG neuronal cell viability and alleviate high glucose-induced neuronal damage in vitro via activation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. In a STZ-induced diabetic mouse model, intraperitoneal administration of Metrnl significantly improved hyperalgesia and allodynia behaviors, further supporting its neuroprotective function in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons. Notably, MAPK signaling exhibits strong cell-type specificity, with distinct functional effects across different cell types in the peripheral nerve microenvironment, and the observed protective effect was validated only in DRG neurons.

Despite these encouraging findings, neither a causal relationship nor therapeutic viability has been established. The available data only demonstrate a protective association and preclinical neuroprotective efficacy of Metrnl, rather than proven therapeutic activity.

Metrnl and DR

Research on the role of Metrnl in DR is still in its preliminary stages. One clinical study measured Metrnl levels in the plasma and aqueous humor of patients who underwent cataract surgery. The results revealed that compared with healthy controls, patients with DR had significantly higher Metrnl concentrations in both the plasma and aqueous humor, whereas the Metrnl levels in the diabetic group without retinopathy did not significantly differ from those in the healthy group [71]. This expression pattern is opposite to the decreased circulating Metrnl levels observed in DPN patients, indicating that Metrnl expression is regulated in a manner specific to tissue type, disease type, and disease stage across different diabetic complications.

The elevation of Metrnl in DR has two potential biological interpretations: a compensatory protective action against retinal pathological damage under hyperglycemic conditions, or an indicator of pathological stress in the progression of DR. Current evidence on Metrnl in DR is limited to a single clinical observational study, with no interventional research to confirm its causal role in DR pathogenesis. Further studies are needed to clarify the specific biological function, molecular mechanism and clinical significance of Metrnl in DR.

Limitations of current evidence

Despite accumulating evidence supporting the biological roles of Metrnl in diabetes and its complications, critical research gaps still limits its clinical translation.

The primary limitation of current clinical research is the lack of methodological standardization. Inconsistent detection assays, variable sample processing, and heterogeneous patient characteristics lead to highly divergent results for circulating Metrnl levels and poor comparability across clinical datasets.

A fundamental unresolved issue is the incomplete characterization of Metrnl receptors. KIT is the only validated binding partner, and this interaction has only been verified in endothelial cells. The expression and downstream signaling of the Metrnl - KIT axis in other diabetes-relevant cell types remain unvalidated.

More importantly, the vast majority of existing clinical studies employ cross-sectional or retrospective observational designs, which can only reveal statistical correlations, but cannot establish temporal sequence or causal relationships. Across various diabetic complications, while multiple studies consistently report associations between abnormal Metrnl expression and injury severity, they still fail to address the core question: whether increased or decreased Metrnl represents an initiating driver of complication progression, an adaptive response of tissues to hyperglycemia, or merely a bystander change with no critical functional significance. Several correlations are significantly attenuated after adjustment for confounding factors [46, 66, 72], suggesting that existing evidence is prone to overinterpretation as causal effects. Therefore, Metrnl should not be simply classified as a definitive driver or protective factor for diabetes in the absence of confirmation from prospective cohorts, Mendelian randomization, or specific intervention studies.

Several challenges also persist at the clinical translation stage. On the one hand, the safety profile of Metrnl interventions has not been fully elucidated, and critical pharmacological properties, including its pharmacokinetic characteristics, optimal administration route, long-term systemic safety, and potential off-target effects, remain unclear. On the other hand, there is a complete lack of human interventional studies of Metrnl in diabetic populations. All proposed therapeutic effects are derived exclusively from in vitro experiments and animal studies.

Current research on Metrnl in diabetes remains largely focused on preclinical models of T2DM, with significant evidence gaps in other diabetes subtypes and complications. Clinical studies in GDM, DPN, and DR are particularly limited by small sample sizes and inadequate mechanistic validation.

Future perspectives

To address the above limitations and advance the translational potential of Metrnl in diabetes and its complications, targeted and prioritized research directions are proposed as follows.

First, methodological standardization should be established. A unified protocol for Metrnl detection and sample processing should be established to reduce heterogeneity. Large, multicenter prospective cohorts are then needed to define dynamic changes in Metrnl levels and its clinical value.

Second, comprehensive characterization of Metrnl receptor is required. Further studies are needed to validate the expression, function and downstream signaling of the Metrnl–KIT axis in major diabetes-related cell types, including pancreatic β-cells, adipocytes, myocytes and renal tubular cells. Exploration of potential alternative receptors will also help clarify the tissue-specific mechanisms of Metrnl.

Third, systematic preclinical pharmacological and safety evaluation should be performed. Key pharmacokinetic properties and potential off-target effects of Metrnl-based interventions must be rigorously assessed, especially given the involvement of KIT in multiple physiological processes. Local delivery strategies may be explored to minimize systemic risks.

Fourth, large scale multicenter prospective cohort studies, combined with Mendelian randomization and specific intervention experiments, are essential to clarify the causal role of changes in Metrnl levels and fundamentally resolve the core controversy regarding unclear causality. Priority should be given to early phase human interventional trials of Metrnl interventions, which can connect cellular experiments, animal models, and clinical applications, verify safety, tolerability, and biological activity in diabetic patients, and bridge the gap between preclinical research and clinical practice.

Finally, larger clinical cohorts and in-depth mechanistic studies are warranted for GDM, DPN, DR and other understudied fields. For GDM in particular, long-term mother-child cohort studies are needed to explore its predictive value and potential effects on offspring metabolic health.

Conclusions

This review systematically summarizes recent advances regarding the structure, tissue distribution, physiological functions, and emerging roles of Metrnl in the pathogenesis and progression of diabetes and its related complications. Current evidence suggests that Metrnl, as a pleiotropic secreted factor, participates in the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, islet β-cell function, inflammatory balance, oxidative stress, and tissue repair, and may exert organ-protective actions in various diabetic complications. This review also highlights key controversies and inconsistencies in the field, including heterogeneous circulating Metrnl levels, divergent expression patterns across different diabetic complications, and multiple unresolved mechanistic and translational questions.

Given the limited and predominantly preclinical nature of available data, Metrnl should be cautiously interpreted as a promising molecule of biological interest rather than an established biomarker or validated therapeutic target. Further rigorous clinical and mechanistic studies are essential to clarify its true physiological and pathological roles, validate its clinical utility, and determine whether it can be safely and effectively translated into clinical applications for diabetes and its complications.
