Rapid diagnosis of celiac disease based on serum infrared spectroscopy combined with deep learning
Authors: Yan Feng, Jiahe Li, Cheng Chen, Chen Chen, Xiaoyi Lv, Ting Li, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeliac disease (CD) and its early-stage variant, potential celiac disease (PCD), pose considerable diagnostic challenges due to their overlapping serological and symptomatic features, and the lack of villous atrophy in PCD. Accurate and non-invasive differentiation between CD, PCD, and healthy controls (HC) is crucial for timely intervention and effective management. In this study, we propose a novel hybrid dual-attention deep learning model for the classification of CD, PCD, and HC using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of serum samples. The proposed model integrates two heterogeneous attention mechanisms in a dual-branch architecture: a self-attention module to capture long-range global dependencies across the spectral dimensions, and a channel-wise attention mechanism to highlight locally significant spectral features. The fusion of global and local information enables the model to sensitively detect subtle spectral variations associated with PCD and to robustly discriminate it from both CD and HC, which is a significant advancement over conventional methods. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that our model achieves superior classification performance compared to baseline architectures, with notably improved sensitivity in distinguishing PCD from CD. This framework offers a promising, non-invasive, and interpretable approach for early and precise diagnosis of celiac spectrum disorders, and may serve as a decision support tool in clinical settings.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Biomarkers, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that occurs in genetically susceptible individuals, where ingestion of foods containing gliadin (Gluten) triggers an abnormal immune response that results in atrophy and chronic inflammation of the villi of the small intestine. The global prevalence of celiac disease is approximately 1%, but the diversity of clinical presentations, ranging from typical gastrointestinal symptoms to extraintestinal manifestations such as anemia, osteoporosis, and neurological abnormalities, has led to a long history of underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of the disease. Early and accurate diagnosis is important to prevent disease progression and complications, and lifelong adherence to a wheat-gelatin-free diet remains the only effective treatment available. Currently, there are two types of celiac disease: confirmed CD and flow-modulated potential PCD. The small intestinal mucosa of patients with PCD may appear completely normal (Marsh 0) or may have only mild inflammatory changes, such as increased intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) (Marsh 1), without significant villous atrophy (Marsh 2/3).The presence of lymphocytes in the intestinal epithelium may also be associated with an increase in the number of lymphocytes in the intestinal epithelium. Some individuals with PCD may have no clinically significant symptoms, especially in the pediatric population where the presentation is more insidious, whereas adult patients are more likely to present with predominantly extraintestinal manifestations, such as anemia, elevated liver enzymes, or decreased bone density.

In recent years, as celiac disease screening strategies have become more widespread and serologic testing techniques have improved, an increasing number of patients exhibit serologic and genetic features of celiac disease (e.g., positive IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase [anti-tTG] and anti-endothelial [EmA] antibodies, harboring either the HLA-DQ2 or the HLA-DQ8 gene) but do not have the typical villi of the small intestinal mucosa. atrophic lesions. These patients are categorized as PCD, as opposed to CD. Despite the high degree of concordance between the two in terms of immunologic manifestations and genetic background, significant differences exist in terms of pathomorphology, clinical symptoms, and management strategies. Of interest, although patients with PCD do not meet the criteria for histologic confirmation of the diagnosis, some individuals may exhibit typical gastrointestinal or extraintestinal symptoms1 and are at risk for progression to CD during follow-up2. Before intervention, patients with celiac disease (CD) typically present with classic clinical symptoms of malabsorption, including but not limited to diarrhea, abdominal bloating, weight loss, anemia, and fatigue. Clinically, CD patients exhibit damage to the small intestinal mucosa; a duodenal biopsy will reveal significant small intestinal villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and an increase in intraepithelial lymphocytes, which is typically classified as a Marsh grade III lesion. PCD is considered to be a very early stage of the CD autoimmune process. Clinically, patients with PCD may be completely asymptomatic or may exhibit mild, non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms. Similar to patients with confirmed CD, their autoimmune response is active; however, the mucosal architecture of the small intestine in PCD patients is largely normal, with biopsies showing no significant villous atrophy (typically Marsh grade 0-I).

After intervention, specifically following the implementation of a strict gluten-free diet (GFD), the clinical symptoms in most patients are significantly alleviated or even disappear completely. As the immune trigger—gluten—is removed, autoantibody titers gradually decrease and eventually return to normal. Concurrently, the small intestinal mucosa begins to heal, damaged villi progressively regenerate, and inflammation subsides. The current diagnostic pathway often relies on repeated endoscopies and biopsies to monitor the progression of PCD. Our rapid diagnostic method, by reliably assessing the disease state, has the potential to reduce the number of further endoscopic and biopsy procedures that patients need to undergo. Therefore, developing a diagnostic tool that can rapidly and accurately distinguish between confirmed CD and PCD holds significant clinical value for guiding personalized treatment, avoiding unnecessary interventions, and determining patient prognosis. Such a tool would not only help to improve early identification rates but also promote a more refined and proactive management strategy across the entire course of celiac disease3.

Traditional diagnostic methods for celiac disease rely on serologic tests (e.g., anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies, anti-endothelial antibodies) and small bowel biopsy as the gold standard. However, these methods have certain limitations, such as sensitivity and specificity that are highly affected by individual differences and invasive biopsy procedures. In recent years, spectroscopic techniques, especially Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, have shown great potential in the field of medical diagnostics.FTIR spectroscopy is the study of the interaction between matter and electromagnetic radiation in the form of a spectrum. Each molecule has a spectral spectrum that makes it unique and allows it to be distinguished from other molecules.

FTIR enables rapid, non-destructive and non-invasive detection of molecular compositional changes in biological samples such as serum and plasma, thus obtaining infrared spectral fingerprint information that is closely related to disease states. Spectroscopic detection technology has the advantages of high sensitivity and rapidity, and plays an important role in substance identification and chemical composition identification. It has been used to diagnose diseases such as keratitis, Alzheimer’s disease and celiac disease. Raman spectroscopy is simple, non-destructive and very sensitive to non-polar groups and has a wide range of applications in the study of molecular structure. Similarly, infrared spectroscopy, considered as a molecular fingerprint, can be used for biomedical diagnostics. FTIR spectroscopy can characterize a wide range of diseases of human organs, as well as quantify a number of different biomolecules, such as proteins4, nucleic acids5,6, and lipids7. Zarnowiec et al.8 have used FTIR spectroscopy to identify a number of biological variations, and have used linear discriminant analysis to for the detection and identification of bacterial pathogens. Vitaly Erukhimovitch9 examined Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) as an easy, fast and reliable technique to differentiate between bacterial and fungal infections in humans and other animals. Under the analysis of this technique, the characteristic peaks of bacteria and fungi were clearly distinguishable in the infrared spectra of mixed infected samples. Mostaco-Guidolin et al.10 characterized two types of melanomas by FTIR spectroscopy for mouse melanoma and human melanoma, and biochemical correlations existed for all absorption bands identified in the study.

Machine learning, as the core technology of data-driven artificial intelligence, extracts potential laws from complex data and builds predictive models through feature engineering and pattern recognition algorithms. In recent years, thanks to the significant increase in computer arithmetic power, the association of this technology with Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) has shown broad prospects in the field of biomedical testing, and some of the application scenarios have already realized the technological iteration of traditional diagnostic methods. It can even replace the traditional methods used to diagnose various diseases. However, due to the drawback of low signal-to-noise ratio of FTIR spectral signals11, it is sometimes difficult to observe the differences between different spectra, which may lead to low diagnostic performance. Therefore, designing a method to achieve high-precision diagnosis of infrared spectroscopically classified diseases by spectral analysis is of significant research value. Typical chemometrics techniques such as K nearest neighbor (KNN), support vector machine (SVM) and principal component analysis (PCA) have been widely used for spectral analysis12,13. Nevertheless, when FTIR spectra from different sources are so similar or even so complex that the human eye cannot distinguish the differences, the results obtained by these methods must fulfill the diagnostic requirements. More sophisticated deep learning models are needed to make the classification results meet the diagnostic requirements.

Traditional CNN models show high performance and robustness in raw spectral data processing. zeng et al.14 applied CNN based on serum Raman spectroscopy to rapidly diagnose HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancers with an accuracy of up to 91.11%. Wu et al.15 combined a deep learning model with Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to achieve the diagnostic results for ankylosing spondylitis (AS), rheumatoid spondylitis (RSI), and rheumatoid spondylitis (RS). Based on Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) combined with a deep learning model, Wu et al.15 realized the non-invasive, fast and accurate differentiation of ankylosing spondylitis (AS), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), osteoarthritis (OA) and healthy controls.

Shi et al.16 used Raman spectroscopy combined with various deep learning algorithms to classify celiac disease, and they found that deep learning outperforms traditional machine learning methods in diagnosing breast cancer on many datasets; therefore, automatic analysis of FTIR spectral data using deep learning techniques provides a new way of thinking about noninvasive, efficient, and accurate diagnosis of celiac disease. However, spectral data usually have strong noise interference, and traditional CNN models can only extract single-scale features, which may lead to poor model classification performance. In addition, the noise-induced differences between the training and test sets can reduce the accuracy of spectral classification.

To address the limitations of traditional spectral classification models—such as their focus on a single feature scale, insufficient utilization of both global and local features, and high sensitivity to noise—this study proposes a deep learning framework for the diagnosis of celiac disease. We have constructed a spectral feature classification model based on a dual-path attention mechanism to accurately differentiate among CD patients, PCD patients, and healthy controls. In contrast to conventional chemometric methods that often rely on pre-selected spectral regions or manual feature engineering, our proposed deep learning approach is designed to more comprehensively capture and utilize the subtle and complex patterns across the entire high-dimensional spectral data. By improving the model’s diagnostic accuracy and reducing the reliance on invasive procedures, this work provides robust support for the early screening and clinical auxiliary diagnosis of celiac disease.

Experimental materials sample preparation

All serum samples were derived from freshly drawn venous blood collected in anticoagulant-free tubes. To standardize collection conditions and minimize potential confounding variables, particularly dietary influences, phlebotomy procedures were consistently performed during morning hours following an overnight fasting period of approximately 12 h. A standardized volume of 3 ml was drawn from each study participant. Sample processing involved immediate centrifugation at 4 °C with a relative centrifugal force equivalent to 4000 revolutions per minute. This protocol yielded acellular serum supernatant, which was carefully aliquoted for subsequent spectroscopic analysis while maintaining cold chain protocols throughout handling. The serum infrared spectroscopy equipment is a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTlR-850, Guangdong scientific, China) with a spectral wavelength range of 700–4000 cm− 1, a resolution of 4 cm− 1, and a scan number of 32. It is obtained at constant temperature and air humidity, Samples were thawed at room temperature for 15 min before measurement, Then, 8 µl of the sample was dropped onto the lens, placed on the spectrometer, and allowed to dry for 10 min. After each measurement, the lens was cleaned with alcohol. To minimize errors during spectral acquisition, three spectra were recorded at different positions on each sample, and the average spectrum for each sample was obtained for further analysis. Finally, 342 infrared spectra of serum from the three sample categories were collected. The average values of the original infrared spectra are shown in Fig. 1.

Methods

To fully exploit the discriminative features at different scales and levels in infrared spectral data, this paper proposes a heterogeneous dual-attention mechanism deep neural network architecture, whose overall structure is shown in Fig. 2. The model takes one-dimensional FTIR spectral vectors as input and first performs preliminary feature extraction on the raw data through a basic feature transformation layer (Conv1D + BatchNorm). The features are then routed into two structurally similar yet functionally complementary branches: the upper branch (Global Feature Branch) focuses on global trends and long-range dependencies in the spectrum; the lower branch (Local Feature Branch) concentrates on capturing local feature details and short-range changes.

The overall architecture of the proposed Heterogeneous Dual-path Multimodal Network (HDMN). a Global Feature Branch and a Local Feature Branch. The Global branch utilizes a Self-Attention mechanism to capture long-range dependencies, while the Local branch employs an SENet module for channel-wise recalibration of key features. Following processing through their respective Layernorm and Residual Block layers, the outputs from both branches are flattened and fused via concatenation (Concat). A final Dense (fully-connected) layer performs the classification task. The inset on the right details the internal structure of the SENet module.

The model first feeds the input infrared spectrum vector into the Conv1D and BatchNorm layers for preliminary feature extraction. The features are then distributed to two parallel subpaths: the Global Feature Branch and the Local Feature Branch. Both branches are processed through structurally identical modules: each module contains an attention mechanism (Self-Attention or Channel-Attention), LayerNorm, and a residual block to extract spectral information from different perspectives. The self-attention mechanism primarily focuses on local details, such as peak positions and shape changes in the spectrum, aiding in the detection of subtle disease markers. Channel attention, on the other hand, emphasizes extracting global contextual information, including overall energy distribution trends and broad-range dependencies in the spectrum.

The input to the SENet attention mechanism network consists of x convolutional layer feature data. The first fully connected layer compresses the global information obtained from global pooling. The second layer expands the compressed feature vector, restoring it to the same feature vector as after the global pooling layer. In summary, through global average pooling, feature compression, and feature expansion, each convolutional layer feature data automatically learns a weight wx. Then, wx is multiplied by the corresponding input features to amplify the spectral data with significant differences, thereby improving the classification performance of the original architecture. This attention module further extracts features from the spectral feature signals obtained from each network convolutional layer and acquires key information from the celiac disease infrared spectral signals. The lower branch introduces a lightweight channel attention mechanism (Squeeze-and-Excitation, SE) to emphasize the importance of spectral features across different channels (i.e., local convolutional responses or spectral band clusters). This mechanism extracts a global description of each channel through global average pooling and learns channel weights through two fully connected layers with Sigmoid activation functions. Finally, the weights are multiplied by the original features channel by channel to achieve feature enhancement and re-calibration.

After the two paths output their respective features, they are input after passing through a Layer Normalization layer to enhance training stability. The Residual Block enhances deep feature learning capabilities and preserves original information, followed by feature concatenation and fusion to complete multi-path information integration, thereby achieving collaborative modeling of local and global features and enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish complex spectral information. The outputs from the two subpaths are flattened via Flatten and concatenated, then fed into a Softmax activation function to output the probability distributions corresponding to three diagnostic results (celiac disease, potential celiac disease patients, and healthy controls). This structure effectively integrates local and global feature representations, improves the model’s ability to distinguish fine-grained features in spectral data, and enhances its discriminative performance for spectral data such as celiac disease, particularly suitable for multi-classification tasks involving infrared spectral data.

Model evaluation and overfitting control

Given the small size of the dataset, a one-time training/test split may introduce significant random error, leading to unreliable evaluation results. To obtain a more robust and unbiased assessment of model performance, we adopted a k-fold cross-validation strategy. In each iteration, we used k-1 folds as the training set and the remaining 1 fold as the test set. This method ensures that all samples are involved in training and testing, greatly reducing the randomness of evaluation results and improving model performance stability.

To directly limit model complexity and prevent overfitting on the training set, we integrated multiple regularization techniques into the model architecture and training process. After the fully connected layers (Dense layers), we added a Dropout layer with a dropout rate set to 0.5. At each training step, Dropout randomly sets the outputs of a portion of the neurons to zero, forcing the network to learn more robust and redundant feature representations, effectively preventing complex synergistic adaptation between neurons. Additionally, we applied L2 regularization to the weight parameters of all convolutional layers and fully connected layers, with the regularization coefficient λ set to 0.001. This suppresses the excessive growth of network weights by adding a penalty term related to weight magnitude in the loss function, making the model more inclined to learn simple patterns. A batch normalization layer is used after each convolutional layer. In addition to accelerating convergence, batch normalization also introduces slight noise into each batch of data, thereby achieving a certain regularization effect.

Experimental setup

During training, the cross-entropy loss function is used as the optimization objective, with the Adam optimizer selected. A learning rate scheduler is combined to achieve dynamic learning rate updates, thereby improving model convergence efficiency. After model training, the model performance is comprehensively evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and specificity on the test set. Additionally, a confusion matrix and ROC curve are plotted for visual analysis of classification performance.

Our dataset contains 342 spectral data points. To objectively and reliably evaluate the model’s performance, we randomly split the entire dataset into a training set and a test set at a 7:3 ratio. Comparison with existing technologies

The current clinical diagnosis of celiac disease primarily relies on serological testing and duodenal biopsy, which is a multi-step, high-cost, and time-consuming process. These inherent limitations of the technology exist globally, but they become particularly pronounced in regions with limited medical resources or insufficient awareness of celiac disease.

Duodenal biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosis, but it is an expensive and invasive endoscopic procedure associated with anesthesia and procedural risks. In many primary healthcare facilities, the conditions for conducting endoscopic examinations are limited, and patients often have low acceptance due to its invasiveness. The entire diagnostic process requires collaboration among multiple specialists, including gastroenterologists, endoscopists, and pathologists, which places high demands on medical infrastructure and professional skills. In underdeveloped regions, the lack of these specialized resources directly results in the absence of diagnostic capabilities. From the initial serological test to the final biopsy pathology report, the entire diagnostic cycle can take several weeks or even months, preventing patients from receiving timely intervention and treatment. As we have noted, in many regions where CD is uncommon, due to insufficient awareness among both doctors and patients, many patients with atypical symptoms are unable to even undergo the initial serological screening step, leading to a significant number of missed diagnoses.

The spectral analysis combined with deep learning method we propose aims to provide a rapid (< 1 h), low-cost, and pathologist-independent diagnostic assistance solution through a single minimally invasive blood sample collection. This technology is particularly suitable for large-scale preliminary screening in primary healthcare facilities, enabling the rapid identification of high-risk individuals who can then be referred to specialized centers for confirmation. This holds significant public health implications for improving diagnostic rates in regions with low awareness of CD and limited medical resources. Some studies have used traditional machine learning models (such as support vector machines (SVM) and random forests (RF)) combined with clinical indicators or serological data for diagnosis. While these models demonstrate some efficacy, they typically rely on manual feature engineering and may fail to fully capture the complex patterns inherent in high-dimensional, nonlinear data such as spectral data. While technologies like mass spectrometry metabolomics are powerful, they are often expensive, complex to operate, and difficult to standardize, making them hard to implement in grassroots or resource-limited settings. Our FTIR spectroscopy technology, however, offers advantages such as relatively low equipment costs, ease of operation, and technical maturity. In summary, the method we propose not only demonstrates competitive performance but, more importantly, provides a unique solution to the pain points of existing clinical diagnostic workflows (especially in resource-scarce regions) in terms of diagnostic efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and technological accessibility. Compared to other emerging research technologies, our method achieves a better balance between deep feature extraction and clinical implementation potential.

Model comparative analysis

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the HDMN model, 11 baseline models were selected for comparison. Among these, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Trees (DT), and Logistic Regression (LR) are five classical machine learning models, while Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), ResNet, AlexNet, GoogleNet, and VGG are six classical deep learning models widely applied in the field of deep learning. In the Hybrid Dual-Modality Network (HDMN) framework proposed in this study, FTIR spectral data is preprocessed and then fed into two structurally distinct feature extraction paths. The upper branch incorporates a self-attention mechanism to capture long-range dependencies between spectral dimensions, enhancing the ability to model global patterns; the lower branch combines a lightweight channel attention mechanism to emphasize the ability to model local spectral details. Each path embeds a residual connection module to enhance the nonlinear expression of features and the ability to retain original information. Finally, the two feature streams are concatenated and fused before being fed into a fully connected classifier to predict outputs for three categories: CD, PCD, and HC. Experimental results are shown in Table 4. In terms of overall classification performance, the HDMN model achieved the best results in five metrics: accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, demonstrating significant performance advantages. Specifically, the HDMN model achieved an accuracy rate of 94.17%, far exceeding that of multiple traditional models and the ResNet model’s 86.41%, indicating its superior performance in comprehensive discriminative capability. In terms of precision and sensitivity, the HDMN model achieved 94.12% in both metrics. In contrast, the ResNet model achieved a precision rate of 86.76% and a sensitivity of 86.39%. Although the ResNet model showed some improvement, it still lagged behind the HDMN model. This indicates that the HDMN model is more sensitive in capturing disease-related features and can effectively identify fine-grained differences between different categories of samples. Additionally, the HDMN model’s AUC value reached 95.50%, not only outperforming ResNet’s 90.40% but also significantly surpassing multiple traditional models, demonstrating its superior discriminative and generalization capabilities on multi-category spectral data. Ablation study

To systematically evaluate the contribution of each component module of the proposed heterogeneous dual-path attention deep neural network model to the final classification performance, this section designs a series of ablation experiments. By progressively removing or replacing key structures within the model, including the heterogeneous dual-path attention mechanism, residual connections, and path fusion strategies, we analyze the impact of each module on model performance. The experiments include: using a single-path MLP structure (without attention or residual connections), a single-path Self-Attention + residual connection (Model-A) without dual paths; using a dual-path structure but removing the Self-Attention module while retaining residual connections (Model-B); using a dual-path Self-Attention structure but removing the residual module (Model-C); and the complete structure with dual-path Self-Attention + residual connections + multi-path fusion (HDMN). The performance of the four models was evaluated under the same training and testing set partitioning and training settings, with results shown in Table 5.

In the ablation experiments, we primarily reported Accuracy, Precision, Sensitivity, Specificity, and AUC values to ensure the accuracy of the experiments. As shown in Table 5, in the three-classification experiment on the celiac disease dataset, the model demonstrates excellent performance on infrared spectra. In the baseline model, which only includes the most basic fully connected network structure without introducing attention mechanisms or residual structures, the overall performance is relatively low, with an accuracy rate of 73.79%, and an AUC value of only 81.00%, indicating that traditional shallow structures struggle to effectively extract deep discriminative features from infrared spectra. After introducing residual connections and attention mechanisms, classification accuracy and other metrics showed significant improvements, demonstrating its capability in feature learning, but it still faces limitations in generalization ability. Furthermore, a dual-path self-attention mechanism was adopted, but removing the residual modules did not result in a significant performance improvement, and there is still a certain gap compared to the HDMN model proposed in this paper, indicating that deep structures are beneficial for capturing complex features in spectra. The HDMN model integrates a dual-path self-attention mechanism with residual connections, effectively enhancing feature expression capabilities while fully modeling both global and local information in spectra. Ultimately, it achieved the best results in terms of accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, with an accuracy rate as high as 94.17% and an AUC of 95.50%, significantly outperforming other comparison models. The experimental results validate that the design of dual-path attention mechanism + residual module + path fusion can effectively enhance the model’s ability to characterize FTIR spectral features.

Conclusion

In this study, a deep learning model with dual-path attention mechanism for FTIR)data is proposed for high-precision classification between celiac disease, potentially celiac disease and healthy individuals. Aiming at the performance degradation of traditional models in the face of high-dimensional spectral noise and single feature expression, this method introduces multi-scale feature modeling and heterogeneous attention mechanisms: by designing a dual-branch path with symmetric structure but complementary mechanisms, the global dependencies are modeled using Self-Attention and the local critical information is strengthened by Channel Attention to strengthen local key information, thus realizing the effective integration of local and global information at the feature expression level. In addition, modules such as residual connection, layer normalization and feature splicing are integrated in the model structure, which further improves the expression ability and training stability of the deep network. The experimental results show that this method outperforms the traditional convolutional neural network and the single attention mechanism model in the triple classification task, and especially exhibits higher sensitivity and accuracy in distinguishing PCD groups, which are the most difficult to recognize in clinical practice. This study not only provides an effective non-invasive strategy for spectroscopy-assisted early screening and accurate typing of celiac disease, but also provides new ideas and model references for in-depth modeling of disease spectral data and multi-scale feature fusion methods.
