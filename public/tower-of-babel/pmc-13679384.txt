The Fleming Fund: Key Features of Design and Approach to Strengthening Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
Authors: Tim Shorten, Jemima Clarke, Archibald Drake, Ankur Gupta-Wright, Milena von und zur Mühlen, Evelyn Wesangula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper examines the Fleming Fund as a major long-term investment in strengthening antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance in low- and middle-income countries. Established by the UK Department of Health and Social Care in 2015, the Fund committed up to £498 million to support surveillance capacity across 25 countries in Africa and Asia through a One Health approach spanning human, animal and environmental health. The paper situates the Fleming Fund within wider global concern about AMR and highlights surveillance as essential for stewardship, policy and international coordination. It describes the Fleming Fund's design logic, governance arrangements, grant architecture and evolving strategic priorities, with particular attention to how the program combined laboratory strengthening, workforce development, data systems, quality assurance, sampling pathways and multisectoral coordination to generate credible and usable AMR data. The analysis also examines the Phase 2 shift from an initial emphasis on data generation toward greater attention to data interpretation, dissemination and use in policy and practice. Drawing on program documentation, evaluation evidence and stakeholder perspectives, the paper presents the Fleming Fund as an important case of large-scale surveillance system strengthening in resource-constrained settings. It argues that the Fund offers wider lessons on the time, coordination and institutional embedding required to move from externally supported capacity building toward sustainable national surveillance functions. These lessons are relevant to future AMR investments and to broader debates on global health security, health systems strengthening and One Health implementation.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, AMR surveillance, health systems strengthening, one health, evidence to action, LMIC, Fleming Fund, global health security

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health challenge that threatens progress toward universal health coverage, health security, and the Sustainable Development Goals. Since the 2016 O’Neill report “Tackling Drug-Resistant Infections Globally” [1], this assessment has gained traction internationally, including with the World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations.

A consistent theme in global AMR policy has been the centrality of surveillance: without routine, comparable data on resistance patterns and antimicrobial use, governments and partners lack the evidence needed to prioritize interventions, target stewardship, and monitor progress [1]. The O’Neill report also underlined the need for a multisector or One Health response [1] implying surveillance capacity across human health, animal health and environmental sectors.

Strengthening AMR surveillance systems is technically complex and resource-intensive. It requires sustained investment in laboratory infrastructure, a skilled workforce, governance and coordination, and interoperable data systems. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the burden of AMR is highest [2, 3], these foundational elements have historically been under-resourced or supported through fragmented, time-bound external initiatives, limiting continuity, standardization, and country ownership.

It is in this context that the Fleming Fund was established: as one of the largest long-term investments in AMR surveillance in LMICs, and large-scale effort to build national One Health surveillance capabilities at scale. Implemented across diverse national and regional contexts in Africa and Asia, the Fleming Fund sought to address long-standing constraints in laboratory microbiology capacity, data generation, workforce development and coordination across the human, animal and environmental health sectors, while linking country systems to regional and global surveillance systems.

The Fleming Fund was conceived as a long-term commitment, established in 2015 and implemented between 2017 and 2025 (with some activities continuing into early 2026), with a commitment up to £498 million. Its scale and duration (supporting work in 25 countries) make it a significant case for examining what it takes to build microbiology and AMR surveillance functions, integrate One Health considerations, and move from capacity building toward routine generation and use of surveillance data.

ABOUT THIS SERIES

This series brings together evidence and learning from one of the largest sustained investments in AMR surveillance in LMICs. It aims to contribute to wider learning on how complex global health programs translate technical capacity into institutional and policy change, and to strengthen the use of evaluative approaches within UK-funded AMR programming and the wider literature.

The papers in this journal series were commissioned and funded by DHSC, delivered through a drafting group process convened by Itad (the Fleming Fund independent evaluation supplier) to collaborate on the content for each paper. The purpose is to synthesize learning from the program implementation and evaluation to inform future AMR surveillance and action, further strengthening the Fleming Fund's legacy. Rather than treating the Fleming Fund as a program assessed in isolation, the series uses it as a lens on broader questions. These include how AMR surveillance systems are built, strengthened, and used in resource-constrained settings.

The series draws on this experience to examine how surveillance capacities are developed, how data and information flows can be established, and the conditions under which surveillance outputs are interpreted and used in decision-making. Particular attention is given to the interface between technical capability and governance, recognizing that the production of surveillance data alone is insufficient to ensure its routine or effective use. In this respect, the papers place greater emphasis on institutional, governance and political economy dimensions of AMR surveillance than is typical in a literature often focused primarily on technical system components.

The analysis is based on a structured narrative synthesis of program documentation, evaluation evidence and stakeholder perspectives. This approach enables cross-cutting analysis across diverse country contexts and components of the Fleming Fund, but it is subject to recognized limitations, including reliance on available program documentation and constrained ability to establish causal attribution.

METHODOLOGY

Approach

This series presents a structured synthesis of learning from the implementation of AMR surveillance strengthening activities supported through the Fleming Fund. The selection of papers is structured to maximize relevance to the intended audience, framed by the Fleming Fund's theory of change (ToC) and covering complementary components of surveillance system development and data use.

Evidence Base

The analysis draws on program documentation, independent evaluation outputs and selected academic and grey literature, including surveillance reporting to international platforms. Evidence was selected purposively rather than comprehensively. Sources were prioritized where they provided insight into system functionality, implementation experience and reported outputs.

Authorship and Drafting Process

Writing groups were convened through a structured invitation process designed to bring together contributors with extensive implementation experience from across the Fleming Fund. This process was led by a lead or coordinating author from the Itad evaluation team (Itad, www.itad.com, was the independent evaluation and learning partner for the Fleming Fund from 2016 to 2026; DHSC asked Itad to coordinate and facilitate the production of this series as an extension to this role), with support from an Itad “secretariat” function.

Contributors included country-based implementers, policy-makers, laboratory specialists, clinicians and program partners across human, animal and environmental health sectors, alongside regional and global experts. Collectively, contributors brought experience from the design, implementation and evaluation of AMR surveillance systems across multiple countries, sectors and stages of system development. Drafts were developed through successive rounds of discussion, review and revision, allowing contributors to challenge interpretations, refine arguments, and agree conclusions.

Scope and Limitations

The series is intended as a practice-informed synthesis rather than an independent evaluation. It does not seek to establish causal attribution or provide a comprehensive account of program performance; rather, it uses available evidence to identify lessons and insights relevant to the AMR community. This entails inherent limitations, including potential biases associated with program-linked authorship, purposive evidence selection, and reliance on documented and reported experience. Where available, evidence on constraints, partial implementation, and areas of limited progress was considered and presented, although decisions about inclusion and emphasis were ultimately made through discussion and consensus within the drafting groups. The drafting groups were mostly led by UK-based authors, but group membership was intentionally broader, ensuring that synthesis and interpretation were grounded in contributions from country-level implementers and decision-makers. These considerations were recognized in the design of the series and are presented transparently, alongside explicit statements of conflict of interest.

PURPOSE AND OBJECTIVES OF THE FLEMING FUND

The Fleming Fund was established in 2015 by the UK Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) as a multiyear investment to strengthen the generation, analysis, sharing and use of AMR surveillance data in LMICs.

The Fleming Fund's objectives can be summarized as follows:

to strengthen laboratory capacity and surveillance systems for AMR, antimicrobial consumption, and antimicrobial use across One Health sectors;

to strengthen laboratory capacity and surveillance systems for AMR, antimicrobial consumption, and antimicrobial use across One Health sectors;

to support countries to generate high-quality, standardized AMR data aligned with international reporting systems, including WHO's GLASS;

to support countries to generate high-quality, standardized AMR data aligned with international reporting systems, including WHO's GLASS;

to improve the analysis, interpretation, sharing, and use of AMR data to inform policy, clinical practice, and investment decisions;

to improve the analysis, interpretation, sharing, and use of AMR data to inform policy, clinical practice, and investment decisions;

to contribute to sustainable national surveillance systems that are embedded within broader health systems, governance arrangements, and national AMR action plans.

to contribute to sustainable national surveillance systems that are embedded within broader health systems, governance arrangements, and national AMR action plans.

The Fleming Fund was designed to generate benefits at national, global, and UK levels simultaneously. For partner countries, investments focus on strengthening laboratory capacity, surveillance systems, and the routine generation and use of AMR data to inform policy, clinical practice, and stewardship. At the global level, these investments contribute to a more complete and comparable evidence base on AMR, supporting international reporting systems and collective action. For the United Kingdom, the rationale is rooted in the transboundary nature of antimicrobial resistance: improving the scope, quality and use of surveillance data in LMICs strengthens the global evidence base on which the UK depends to understand emerging resistance patterns, assess risks, and inform its own public health and policy responses. Taken together, the Fleming Fund reflects an approach in which national capacity strengthening and global health security are closely aligned, rather than separate objectives.

These objectives were pursued through a One Health approach, recognizing the interconnected nature of human, animal, and environmental drivers of AMR and the need for coordinated governance across sectors. One Health formed an underpinning principle for the Fleming Fund, alongside country ownership, alignment, sustainability, gender, and equity.

STRUCTURE AND GOVERNANCE OF THE FLEMING FUND

Governance

The Fleming Fund was funded and overseen by DHSC, through a London-based team, with leadership from the UK's Special Envoy on AMR, Dame Sally Davies. The DHSC team leveraged existing diplomatic, technical and partner networks at country level; a program board in DHSC supported on management and accountability, and a Technical Advisory Group [9] guided the Fleming Fund's approach.

The majority of Fleming Fund investments (c.70%; see Figure 1) were managed by Mott Macdonald as the Fleming Fund's Management Agent (MA) through a core team in London and regional hubs in Accra, Kampala, New Delhi and Bangkok. The MA was responsible for designing, procuring, and managing a portfolio of country and regional grants alongside a Fellowship scheme (described in more detail in the following section). The DHSC team also managed a set of direct grants, described below, to complement the MA-managed investments.

An independent evaluation function was embedded within the program from its inception, managed by Itad, reflecting commitment to both accountability, learning and adaptation as essential in complex systems strengthening initiatives.

THE FLEMING FUND'S APPROACH TO AMR SURVEILLANCE

Design Logic and Foundations

The Fleming Fund's technical scope was defined around the surveillance pillar of the WHO Global Action Plan on AMR (2015), focusing on the generation, analysis, sharing and use of AMR surveillance data rather than broader AMR control or stewardship interventions. A core objective was to help countries generate data compatible with international systems, particularly GLASS [18], while embedding surveillance within national One Health AMR action plans.

The Fleming Fund's approach was developed during an inception period, informed by DHSC design parameters. A set of program aims and exclusions were set out by DHSC at the start of the Fleming Fund, focused on six key areas:

2015 scoping studies, and the LSHTM roadmap for AMR surveillance LMICs also informed the Fund's design [19]; the roadmap conceptualized AMR surveillance as a staged, system-wide capability requiring coordinated investment across laboratories, workforce, data systems, governance and data use, with sentinel surveillance systems expanding stepwise as capacity developed.

At the time of program design, operational guidance for multisectoral and One Health surveillance was limited, requiring pragmatic design choices and adaptive delivery.

A program-level ToC was developed, after the initial laboratory-focused design, and evolved over time. It articulated (Figure 3) a phased contribution pathway: Phase 1 focused on the foundational components required to generate credible AMR data; Phase 2 on the interpretation and routine use of those data to inform policy, planning and practice, albeit with longer-term outcomes left relatively vague and action toward them necessarily exploratory; and Phase 3 on action on AMR, understood as sustained changes in policy or clinical practice.

Fleming fund program ToC [25].

Sustainability was central to the Fleming Fund's intent because AMR surveillance is a recurring public function dependent on reliable financing, institutional anchoring, skilled staff, and sustained demand for data. The program's experience highlights the tension between accelerating capacity in the short to medium term and embedding systems that can be maintained and routinely used over the longer-term. The fund incorporated sustainability as an underpinning principle from the outset and considered what could be sustained as support to laboratories was defined; specific sustainability and exit plans were developed by all grantees in Phase 2. Related issues are therefore taken up across the series.

Although conceived as a 10-year investment, the effective period for country-level delivery was closer to seven years once phased start-up, sequencing and replanning are taken into account (Figure 4). This matters for interpreting observed results that depend on institutionalization, routine data use, and sustained financing.

Core System Components for AMR Data Generation (Phase 1 Outputs)

Consistent with the ToC, Phase 1 focused on strengthening interdependent system components required to generate reliable, comparable and interpretable AMR surveillance data, with most investment directed toward laboratory strengthening, supported by workforce development, data systems, governance, and sampling pathways.

Laboratory Strengthening for Quality Data Generation

Laboratory strengthening formed the largest component of investment and focused on establishing routine bacteriology and antimicrobial susceptibility testing for priority pathogen–antimicrobial combinations aligned with WHO GLASS and One Health priorities. Interventions typically included laboratory renovation and equipment, standardized testing methods and workflows, strengthened reference laboratory functions, and essential consumables. Quality assurance and standardization were embedded through standard operating procedures, external quality assessment, quality management systems, and accreditation readiness, complemented by regional QA initiatives.

Workforce Development

Workforce development supported laboratory scientists, epidemiologists, data analysts and policy actors through in-service training, mentorship, supervision, multidisciplinary surveillance teams, and the Fleming Fellowship program (Box 1).

Fleming Fellows

The Fleming Fund Fellowship scheme complemented country and regional grants by strengthening human capacity for AMR surveillance. It supported government and public-sector professionals in ministries, national reference laboratories, public health institutes, and related agencies, hosted by technical institutions domestically or internationally. Fellowship profiles spanned laboratory science, epidemiology, data analysis, AMU/C, governance, and policy.

Data Systems and Management

Data systems investments supported aggregation, analysis, and reporting at facility and national levels, including implementation or upgrading of laboratory information management systems; use of tools such as WHONET [20] for data analysis; development of national data platforms; and reporting to international systems including GLASS, InFARM [21], and ANIMUSE [22].

Sampling Pathways and the Clinical Interface

Because data quality depends on how samples are generated, the Fleming Fund supported improvements in sampling practices and the interface between laboratories and clinicians. This included guidance on appropriate specimen collection with associated clinical and demographic data; strengthening of diagnostic stewardship and clinician–laboratory interaction; and in some contexts (particularly animal health), targeted active sampling to complement routine testing. Across sectors, surveillance prioritized feasible, standardized approaches over statistically representative sampling.

Governance and Multisectoral Coordination

Governance and coordination support, in line with AMR National Action Plans, included establishment or strengthening of national AMR coordination committees or equivalent bodies; support to secretariat functions, coordination processes and alignment with National Action Plans; and development of structures for multisectoral engagement and data sharing across human, animal and environmental sectors.

Global and Regional Complementary Interventions

Regional and global grants reinforced country-level investments by supporting functions that could not be efficiently delivered at the national level, including quality assurance, proficiency testing, reference laboratory functions, and global surveillance standards and platforms.

From Data Generation to Use (Phase 2 Strategic Shifts)

During Phase 2, the Fleming Fund placed greater emphasis on interpretation, dissemination and use of AMR data (Box 2), reflecting recognition that data generation alone is insufficient to influence policy or practice (note that the shift on substandard and falsified medicines was the focus of a Fleming Fund grant to WHO, but neither reflected in the ToC (Figure 3) nor part of the independent evaluation scope and so not captured in this series).

Tailoring Delivery to Country Contexts

Delivery was tailored to country context. From 2018 to 2025, the Fleming Fund supported 25 countries (Figure 5) across four regions, which were grouped using an A–D categorization reflecting the maturity of AMR national action plans and surveillance systems (Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, Cameroon, Eswatini, Ghana, India, Indonesia, Kenya, Laos, Malawi, Nepal, Nigeria, Pakistan, Papua New Guinea (PNG), Rwanda, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Sri Lanka, Tanzania, Timor-Leste, Uganda, Vietnam, Zambia and Zimbabwe. Note that additional countries, beyond those listed here, were supported through other grant mechanisms such as the MPTF or work in the Caribbean). This informed sequencing, pacing and scale.

Fleming Fund focus countries. Figure 5 refers to A-D categories which the MA used to assess the strength of each country’s AMR surveillance capabilities at the start of Phase 1 and the start of Phase 2. These categories were described as follows: Category A comprised countries without a National Action Plan (NAP), or where a NAP was at an early or draft stage and not close to finalization; such countries typically lacked functional coordination committees, may not have undertaken situation analyses, and showed limited engagement with the AMR agenda, and were therefore not favored for investment at that time. Category B included countries with a NAP in place but without an operational surveillance system; these countries had often received external support to develop AMR plans and recognized the need for surveillance, but implementation progress was limited largely due to resource constraints rather than an absence of political will, and they were the primary focus of Fleming Fund investments. Category C covered countries where modest surveillance arrangements existed—predominantly in human health—but were small-scale, fragmented and constrained by gaps in quality assurance, analytical capacity, laboratory capability, and sustainability, with weak cross-sectoral integration for One Health. Category D referred to countries with relatively more advanced surveillance systems, in some cases including animal health, where support was expected to be more targeted and additive, aligned with government and other donor investments, and focused on nuanced contributions and strengthening One Health approaches.

In each country, the MA undertook situational analyses of laboratory and surveillance capacity, covering equipment, human resources, data management, quality assurance systems and throughput. These analyses informed priorities, balancing capacity gaps, sustainability, and alignment with national AMR action plans. In many settings, National Action Plans were new and informed primarily by global guidance rather than country-specific surveillance data, so baseline needs were not always well understood.

Laboratory support commonly included infrastructure renovation and equipment provision during Phase 1, including installation of automated blood culture and AST systems in health facility laboratories. In animal health fewer laboratories were supported, reflecting the greater reliance on active surveillance approaches (see other papers in this series for further discussion) [4, 6].

EMERGING RESULTS

Program monitoring and independent evaluation [24, 25] indicate that the Fleming Fund contributed substantially to strengthening AMR surveillance capacity in diverse LMICs. Reported improvements have been observed in laboratory performance, workforce competence, governance arrangements, and participation in international reporting systems such as GLASS.

Progress was uneven, reflecting differences in starting conditions, political commitment, and external shocks including COVID-19. Nonetheless, the volume of AMR data generated increased across much of the portfolio, alongside improvements in analysis and sharing at national, regional and international levels.

Use of AMR data remained limited and uneven. Where observed, it was clearest at facility level, informing clinical practice, stewardship and infection prevention. At higher system levels, data more often contribute to technical deliberation, priority-setting and periodic policy processes, with less evidence of routine integration into regulatory, financing or system-wide decision-making. This reflects the time required to embed data use and the need for sustained investment in the analytical, governance and decision-making capacities.

The Fleming Fund also helped position AMR surveillance as a routine public health function and has contributed to a global evidence base for stewardship, policy dialogue and international prioritization.

On this basis the Fleming Fund can be understood as a sustained effort to strengthen the foundational systems required for AMR surveillance across diverse contexts, operating at the intersection of national capacity development and global health security. The design choices, implementation experience and constraints described here provide the context for the analyses that follow in this series. The papers that follow examine how these investments have contributed to data generation, data use, multisectoral coordination and future directions for AMR surveillance and action.
