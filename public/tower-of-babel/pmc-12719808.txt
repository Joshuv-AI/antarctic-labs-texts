Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling shows dysregulated immune and metabolic pathways in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Deniz Akdis, Lukas Weidmann, Paolo Nanni, Anna Gaertner, Hendrik Milting, Peter Bode, Corinna Brunckhorst, Liang Chen, Felix C. Tanner, Frank Ruschitzka, Ardan M. Saguner, Firat Duru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsArrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a hereditary heart disease characterized by fibrofatty myocardial replacement and a predisposition to malignant ventricular arrhythmias. The underlying pathomechanisms remain incompletely understood, and specific disease markers are sparse. This study aimed to characterize the myocardial transcriptome and proteome in ACM patients and assess whether key identified molecules were also detectable in plasma and tissue samples.Methods and resultsMyocardial tissues were obtained from ACM and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) patients as well as healthy controls (n = 10/group). Transcriptomic profiling was performed by RNA sequencing and proteomic profiling by label‐free liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses were performed to identify key biological processes. Selected targets were validated by tissue immunofluorescence and plasma ELISA. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 3030 dysregulated mRNAs in ACM versus healthy controls and 120 versus DCM. Enriched clusters in ACM versus healthy controls were related to immune activation, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodelling and mitochondrial stress; redox and metabolic processes, cell junction regulation and immune responses were enriched clusters in ACM versus DCM. Proteomics identified 206 and 65 differentially expressed proteins, respectively. Three novel proteins, UCHL1, OCIAD1 and desmoyokin, were consistently up‐regulated at transcript and protein levels. The latter two were confirmed in myocardial tissue staining and showed elevated plasma levels in ACM compared with DCM and controls.ConclusionThis integrated transcriptomic and proteomic study of myocardial tissue and plasma from patients with ACM compared with those with DCM and healthy controls, identified key dysregulated pathways, involving immune response, inflammation and oxidative and mitochondrial stress. Desmoyokin and OCIAD1, in particular, may represent specific candidate biomarkers for ACM.

Introduction

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is an inherited cardiac disorder characterized by fibrofatty infiltration of the myocardium and life‐threatening ventricular arrhythmias. 1 Initially recognized as a disease of the right ventricle (RV), ARVC is now encompassed within the broader term arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), which includes biventricular and left ventricular (LV) phenotypes. 2 Due to its highly variable clinical expression, ACM remains a diagnostic challenge. The diagnosis is based on structural, electrical and histological criteria alongside family history and genetic testing. 3 Most forms exhibit autosomal dominant inheritance. Identifiable genetic variants are present in approximately two‐thirds of patients, with five desmosomal variants constituting up to 60%. 4 Non‐desmosomal variants, particularly in phospholamban (PLN), desmin (DES) and transmembrane protein (TMEM)‐43 are associated with LV involvement, a heightened risk for developing heart failure, and arrhythmias. 5

At the molecular level, ACM pathogenesis involves the disruption of desmosomal integrity, which compromises mechanical and electrical coupling within the intercalated discs. The structural abnormalities can lead to mislocalization and down‐regulation of key intercalated disc proteins such as connexin 43, disrupting gap junction communication and contributing to the arrhythmogenic substrate. 6 , 7 This concept has been examined in more detail by studies using patient‐derived stem cell cardiomyocyte models, which demonstrated that desmosomal defects are linked to impaired sodium and calcium handling and increased vulnerability to adrenergic stimulation and also proposed novel arrhythmia‐promoting pathways. 8 , 9 ACM hearts may also exhibit inflammation, often linked to viral infections or immune triggers. 10 Building on this, biomarker studies have highlighted the prognostic relevance of molecules related to myocardial stress and inflammation, such as soluble suppression of tumorigenicity‐2 (sST2) and growth differentiation factor‐15 (GDF‐15). 11

To further understand the underlying disease mechanisms, current research has also shifted towards transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. While methodologies vary, previous studies, including our own, have highlighted dysregulated molecular pathways related to calcium channel regulation, apoptosis and fibrosis. 12 , 13 Early comparative transcriptomic analyses have demonstrated distinct gene expression profiles in ACM compared with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) hearts. 14 A spatial transcriptomics approach identified key regulating proteins in zones of fibro‐fatty replacement that induced autophagy and cell death 15 and a recent comprehensive study combining transcriptomic and proteomic analysis examined the impact of desmosomal gene loss on calcium dynamics in HL1 cardiomyocyte models. 16 These complementary findings highlight the value of integrating multi‐level molecular data to demonstrate complex disease mechanisms. Building on these observations, the present study aims to perform a comprehensive analysis of the myocardial transcriptome and proteome in ACM patients, compared with healthy controls and DCM patients. This design attempts to identify disease‐specific pathways and potential biomarkers and to allow a more precise separation of ACM‐specific mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample collection

Myocardial tissue samples of 10 patients with definite ACM according to the 2010 modified Task Force Criteria (TFC) 17 and 10 DCM patients were collected during heart transplantation, excluding endocardial and epicardial portions of the heart. All DCM patients had a confirmed clinical or histological diagnosis of non‐ischaemic DCM, with no known family history of the disease. Additionally, autopsy samples from 10 individuals without heart disease were collected from the RV wall within 5–24 h postmortem. Absence of disease was confirmed by clinical, macroscopic and histological evaluation. Tissues were either preserved in RNAlater (Life Technologies, Zug, Switzerland), snap‐frozen at −80°C for molecular analysis or formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded for immunostaining.

Plasma biomarker levels were measured in 20 definite ACM patients, all of whom underwent clinical evaluation including history and echocardiography. Diagnosis was confirmed at the time of blood withdrawal. Sixteen healthy and 12 non‐ischaemic DCM controls were included. Venous blood was collected, centrifuged and stored at −80°C. Clinical follow‐up after blood sampling assessed major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as ventricular fibrillation, heart transplantation or death.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich (approval number PB_2016_02109).

Informed consent was obtained for prospective inclusion, and retrospective analysis was approved.

Genetic analysis

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood cells. Most patients underwent exome‐based sequencing with a focus on 96 cardiomyopathy‐ and channelopathy‐associated genes, while in three patients targeted sequencing with the TruSight™ Cardio panel covering 174 genes was applied. Variants were validated by Sanger sequencing and classified according to international standards. (Detailed description of the sequencing procedures, bioinformatic tools and a list of analysed genes and panels are provided in Supporting information Methods section and in Table S1).

High‐throughput RNA sequencing and mass spectrometry‐based proteomics

For details, see Supporting information Methods section and Table S2.

Briefly, RNA quality was assessed using a Qubit® Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Germany); samples with acceptable purity and integrity were processed (ACM n = 8, DCM n = 10, healthy controls n = 9). Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, USA), and sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 (paired‐end 2 × 101 bp or single‐end 100 bp) using the TruSeq SBS Kit HS4000. Reads were aligned with STAR (v2.5.3a) to the Ensembl GRCh38 reference genome (release 80). Differential expression was analysed using a negative binomial model in EdgeR (R v3.3.2, EdgeR v3.14.0).

Tissue lysis and protein digestion were performed using COMPLETE protease inhibitor (Roche, Switzerland) and Trypsin (Promega, USA, 1:50). Peptides (1 μL) were analysed on a QExactive with a nano Easy 1000 LC (Thermo Fisher) using an Acclaim PepMap™ Trap and PepMap™ RSLC C18 Snail Column in data‐dependent acquisition mode using HCD fragmentation. Data were analysed using MaxQuant (v1.4.1.2) and Andromeda with Ensembl GRCh38 release 80 and a reversed decoy database. Label‐free quantification was enabled in all samples. Protein abundances were calculated from summed precursor intensities, hyperbolic arcsine transformed, imputed (missForest) and median‐normalized.

Tissue immunofluorescence and plasma protein validation using ELISA

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded myocardial tissue samples were cut into 5‐μm sections. After deparaffinization with xylene and 2‐propanol, the slides were pressure‐heated in citrate buffer and blocked for antigen retrieval. The samples and respective isotype controls (Life Technologies) were incubated with primary antibodies AHNAK/desmoyokin (Clone EM‐09, Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan) and OCIA domain‐containing protein 1 (OCIAD1) (polyclonal, Merck & Cie Buchs Switzerland) as well as secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The samples were later fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde and covered with ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent containing 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Life Technologies). Tissue sections were imaged using a Leica TCS SPE microscope (Leica Microsystems, Heerbrugg, Switzerland). For ELISA, the following kits were used for OCIAD‐1 (EIAab Science Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) and for AHNAK/desmoyokin (Cloud‐Clone Corp, TX, USA).

Statistical analysis

Detailed statistical analysis of the different molecular sequencing datasets is described above. Further statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 8‐10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel (version 16.95, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile ranges (IQR) or mean with standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Comparison analyses were performed by the Mann–Whitney U test. If not specified otherwise, a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

Among the 10 ACM patients from whom myocardial samples were obtained, all fulfilled definite TFC; the median age at the time of tissue collection (heart transplantation) was 57 years (IQR: 47–62 years); all were male. Genetic analysis revealed that 8 patients (80%) carried at least one desmosomal variant. PKP2 variants were identified in 6 cases and DSP variants in two cases. Two patients (20%) had no associated variants (gene elusive). (Investigated genes and panels are provided in Table S1.) All patients exhibited significant RV dysfunction, with a RV fractional area change (FAC) below 35%. As a comparator group, myocardial samples from age and gender matched patients with DCM and healthy controls were analysed.

The 20 ACM patients, from whom plasma was collected, also had definite ACM, with a median score of 8. The median age at the time of sampling was 51 years (IQR 43–57 years), and 40% of the cohort were female. The most common clinical symptom was sustained ventricular tachycardia (18 patients, 90%). After a mean follow‐up of 78 months, eight patients (40%) experienced a MACE as defined above, of which two were cardiovascular deaths. Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated a median RV FAC of 26% and a median tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 17 mm. LV function was impaired in eight patients (40%), defined as a LV ejection fraction (LVEF) below 52% in men and below 54% in women, as assessed by the Simpson's biplane method. 18

Beta‐blockers were the most frequently prescribed medications used in 60% of patients. Classes I and III antiarrhythmic drugs were prescribed in 35% of cases. Implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators (ICDs) were present in 13 patients (65%) (Table 1).

Myocardial tissue transcriptomics reveals dysregulated mRNAs in ACM versus healthy controls and DCM

A total of 21 174 genes were quantified. Of those, 3162 mRNAs were identified as significantly differentially expressed in patients with ACM as compared with healthy controls, with P < 0.01 and FDR = 0.042, indicating robust transcriptomic alterations after correction for multiple testing. When applying an additional log2 fold‐change (FC) threshold of ≥0.5 (corresponding to FC ≥ 1.41) to ensure biological relevance, 3030 mRNAs remained significant (Figure 1A). A total of 204 genes were identified as significantly differentially expressed when comparing ACM to DCM, with P ≤ 0.01 and FDR = 0.6498; however, the high FDR suggests a substantial proportion of potential false positives. When applying an additional log2 FC threshold of ≥0.5 (corresponding to FC ≥ 1.41) to ensure biological relevance, 120 genes remained significant (Figure 1B).

One hundred twenty mRNAs were significantly differentially expressed between ACM and DCM (P ≤ 0.01, FDR = 0.6498, log2 FC ≥ 0.5). Transcriptomic clustering demonstrates inflammatory, pro‐fibrotic and metabolic signatures in ACM

To investigate the biological processes dysregulated in ACM versus healthy controls, enrichment analysis was performed on the top 2000 differentially expressed genes (P ≤ 0.01, log2 FC ≥ 0.5). We analysed gene ontology (GO) terms that were grouped into clusters based on gene overlap and hierarchical clustering. Six distinct clusters reflecting key pathological pathways in ACM were revealed (Figure 1B). Cluster 1 (yellow) included regulation of NF‐κB signalling, mechanical stress responses, cell adhesion and B cell activation. Enriched terms included positive regulation of I‐κB kinase/NF‐κB signalling, homophilic cell adhesion via plasma membrane adhesion molecules, cellular response to mechanical stimulus and B cell activation. Cluster 2 (red) involved extracellular matrix remodelling processes, including extracellular matrix organization, disassembly, platelet degranulation and angiogenesis. Cluster 3 (blue) represented chronic inflammation, with inflammatory response, leukocyte migration, chronic inflammatory response and positive regulation of inflammatory response. Cluster 4 (green) captured innate and adaptive immune processes: inflammatory response, immune response, innate immune response and regulation of immune response. Cluster 5 (turquoise) involved mitochondrial and metabolic activity, with cellular metabolic processes, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), mitochondrial electron transport and respiratory electron transport chain. Cluster 6 (orange), although smaller, included mitochondrial‐specific functions such as respiratory chain complex IV assembly and oxidation–reduction processes (Figure 1C). To compare biological differences between ACM and DCM, GO enrichment analysis of 120 significantly differentially expressed genes (P ≤ 0.01, |log2 FC| ≥0.5) revealed five clusters. Cluster 1 involved processes such as NADPH oxidation and zinc ion import into organelles. Cluster 2 was enriched for prostanoid and prostaglandin metabolism, indicating dysregulated lipid signalling. Cluster 3 highlighted protein localization to cell junctions, positive regulation of signal transduction and antiviral innate immune responses. Cluster 4 comprised pathways related to angiogenesis, apoptotic regulation and interleukin signalling. Cluster 5 highlighted key inflammatory processes, with strong enrichment of response to interleukin‐1, tumour necrosis factor and lipopolysaccharide (Table S3).

Structural remodelling and immune activation dominate the proteomic signature of ACM myocardium

Using an FC threshold of 1.5 and a significance threshold of P < 0.05, 206 out of 2316 proteins were found to be dysregulated in ACM tissues compared with healthy controls. Additionally, 65 proteins were differentially expressed in ACM compared with DCM (Figure 2A,B and Table S4A,B; list of all significant proteins and identified proteins). In comparison of ACM versus healthy controls, the top 25 dysregulated proteins included desmoyokin (also known as AHNAK), fibulin‐5 (FBLN5), OCIA domain‐containing protein 1 (OCIAD1), ubiquitin carboxyl‐terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1), synaptopodin (SYNPO), synaptopodin‐2 (SYNPO2), LIM and calponin homology domains‐containing protein 1 (LIMCH1), ribosomal protein L7 (RPL7), ribosomal protein S2 (RPS2), ribosomal protein S3A (RPS3A), ribosomal protein L27A (RPL27A), major histocompatibility complex, class II, DR alpha (HLA‐DRA) and S100 calcium‐binding protein A9 (S100A9) (Figure 2C). Focusing on the top 25 differentially expressed proteins in ACM versus DCM, desmoyokin remained consistently up‐regulated in ACM. Furthermore, PKP2 and desmoglein‐2 (DSG2) were significantly down‐regulated versus DCM. Other proteins included filamin A (FLNA), caldesmon 1 (CALD1), microtubule‐associated tumour suppressor candidate 2 (MTUS2), apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1), apolipoprotein A2 (APOA2) and apolipoprotein H (APOH) (Figure 2D). We also wanted to observe the tissue protein expression of the proteins encoded by genes of the eight most common variants in ACM. Among the eight proteins analysed, as mentioned, only PKP2 and DSG2 were significantly down‐regulated in ACM compared with DCM. No significant dysregulation of these proteins was observed when comparing ACM to healthy controls. The remaining proteins did not show significant differential expression in either comparison (Figure 2E,F).

Multiomic analysis highlights desmoyokin, OCIAD1 and UCHL1 as specifically dysregulated in ACM

mRNA expression levels in normalized counts and corresponding protein abundance were individually assessed for selected candidate molecules. For OCIAD1, mRNA levels were elevated in ACM compared with healthy controls (P = 0.006) and had a trend towards lower levels compared with DCM (P = 0.083). A similar trend was observed in healthy controls versus DCM (P = 0.053). Protein levels of OCIAD1 were highly increased in ACM versus healthy controls (P < 0.0001), and also in ACM versus DCM (P = 0.004) (Figure 3A). For UCHL1, significant up‐regulation was observed in ACM versus healthy controls at both the mRNA (P = 0.021) and protein levels (P < 0.0001); no significant differences were observed between ACM and DCM for UCHL1 (Figure 3B). For desmoyokin, both mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased in ACM compared with DCM (mRNA: P = 0.0005; protein: P = 0.0007) and compared with healthy controls (mRNA: P < 0.0001; protein: P = 0.001) (Figure 3C).

Immunostaining and plasma protein measurements show distinct localization of desmoyokin and OCIAD1 as well as an up‐regulation specifically in ACM

Desmoyokin immunostaining was observed both intracellularly and at the intercalated discs. Its expression appeared increased in ACM samples compared with controls and DCM (Figure 4A). We also measured the absolute plasma desmoyokin levels in 20 ACM patients and compared the levels to those of 14 healthy controls and 12 DCM plasma samples. Although the P values did not reach statistical significance, there was a trend towards increased desmoyokin levels in ACM compared with healthy controls (P = 0.056) and to DCM (P = 0.059), respectively (Figure 4B).

OCIAD1 also exhibited intracellular, especially perinuclear localization and appeared to be strongly expressed in ACM samples (Figure 4C). The plasma concentrations of 16 ACM patients were compared with those of 12 healthy controls and 10 DCM samples. Again, there was a trend towards elevated levels in ACM (ACM vs. healthy controls P = 0.053, ACM vs. DCM P = 0.06) (Figure 4D).

As an exploratory observation, desmoyokin and OCIAD1 plasma levels were reported in patients who experienced MACE. Patients with MACE had a trend towards higher desmoyokin levels (P = 0.057); this was not observed with OCIAD1 (P > 0.9) (Figure S1).

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive multi‐level analysis of ACM to uncover disease‐specific molecular signatures. Using label‐free transcriptomics and proteomics, we quantitatively assessed mRNA and protein expression from myocardial tissue and plasma. This novel approach yielded several key findings. Differential gene expression and GO enrichment revealed six functional clusters in ACM compared with healthy controls that reflected the major dysregulated biological processes, including immune response, extracellular matrix remodelling and inflammation, as well as mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, all of which converge to promote fibrosis. To better characterize ACM's distinct pathophysiology, we included patients with DCM for comparison. Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between ACM and DCM identified five additional clusters. These were mainly related to redox processes, dysregulated lipid signalling, intracellular communication, regulation of cell junctions and inflammation. Together, these findings emphasize the distinct molecular profile of ACM and suggest potential epigenetic and environmental regulatory influences. Proteomic analysis confirmed ACM‐specific protein signatures. Desmosomal proteins PKP2 and DSG2 were down‐regulated in ACM compared with DCM. The novel molecule desmoyokin was consistently up‐regulated in both myocardial tissue and plasma compared with healthy controls and DCM, and higher levels may be associated with MACE. OCIAD1 was also up‐regulated at the protein level, and UCHL1 was elevated in ACM versus healthy controls at both transcript and protein levels.

Focusing on mitochondrial metabolic processes, the transcriptomic signatures of ACM myocardium revealed distinct clusters, including the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and respiratory chain assembly. This is consistent with our proteomics findings, where the up‐regulation of certain proteins such as the mitochondrial membrane protein OCIAD1 may support mitochondrial dysfunction. The up‐regulation of S100A9, linked to inflammation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and of desmoyokin, regulating calcium homeostasis, further indicates disrupted mitochondrial signalling and oxidative stress. 19 , 20 Previous studies have identified mitochondrial impairment as a driver of ACM pathogenesis, linking defective ATP synthesis and excess ROS to disrupted calcium handling, altered conduction and fibrotic remodelling. 21 In PKP2‐deficient hearts and mouse models, nuclear envelope damage with oxidative stress and a dysregulation of electron transport chain proteins were observed, all pointing to early mitochondrial dysfunction. 22 More recently, mitochondrial ROS production and impaired energetics were shown to affect calcium signalling, ion channel function and gap junction integrity, promoting arrhythmogenicity. 23

Our data also highlight a strong inflammatory component in ACM pathogenesis, with enrichment of immune activation pathways such as chronic inflammation, B cell activation and interferon signalling. At the protein level, this was reflected by the up‐regulation of immune‐related proteins such as HLA‐DRA and again S100A9 in ACM. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that PKP2 deficiency may intrinsically activate inflammatory and immune gene expression in cardiomyocytes. 24 The pathophysiology has been described as a convergence of inflammation, mechanical stress and fibrosis, a model reinforced in our data by the co‐enrichment of NF‐κB signalling, mechanical stimulus response and extracellular matrix related pathways. 25 Further supporting the role of profibrotic mechanisms, we also identified UCHL1 among the top up‐regulated proteins in ACM versus healthy tissue, mirroring earlier findings, where UCHL1 promoted cardiac fibrosis, suggesting a conserved fibrotic mechanism. 26

In our study, we did not observe a uniform down‐regulation of commonly mutated genes associated with ACM, such as PKP2 or DSG2. This likely reflects the heterogeneity of underlying genetic variants within our cohort. Nonetheless, certain desmosomal dysregulation was evident in ACM compared with DCM, in line with previous studies showing variant‐specific down‐regulation of desmosomal components. 16

A central finding was the consistent up‐regulation of three novel proteins UCHL1, OCIAD1 and desmoyokin, with pronounced expression changes in the latter two, at both transcript and protein levels in ACM tissue. UCHL1, a deubiquitinating enzyme, can promote fibrotic remodelling by degrading GRP78, thereby increasing endoplasmic reticulum stress and activating pro‐fibrotic pathways. 26 In post‐myocardial infarction models, UCHL1 exacerbates fibrosis and impairs cardiac function. Additionally, UCHL1 activates NF‐κB signalling in cardiac fibroblasts, further driving collagen expression and fibrotic progression. 27 Therefore, in ACM but also in DCM, it could be a driving factor of fibrosis. OCIAD1 is a mitochondrial membrane protein regulating oxidative phosphorylation and calcium homeostasis. 28 Its role in the regulation of mitochondrial function and differentiation has been studied in animal models and elevated circulating levels have been reported in neurodegenerative diseases, where they were also linked to inflammatory responses and mitochondrial dysfunction. Evidence suggests that OCIAD1 expression is responsive to acute stress and may largely be influenced by comorbid conditions. 20 , 29 Taken together, its role in cardiomyopathies is largely unknown, and further investigation is required before considering OCIAD1 as a disease‐specific biomarker. Among the key molecules identified, desmoyokin was the most specifically up‐regulated in ACM across transcriptomic, proteomic and plasma analyses. Desmoyokin is a scaffold protein that anchors the actin cytoskeleton and regulates L‐type calcium channel localization, thereby influencing calcium homeostasis. 30 Originally linked to epithelial adhesion, it exhibits dynamic localization in cardiomyocytes. 31 In muscle dystrophies, its release in vesicles under conditions of membrane stress has been demonstrated. 32 This suggests that intercalated disc disruption amplifying calcium and structural dysregulation in ACM may underlie its extracellular accumulation. 30 Under adrenergic stress, these disturbances can be further aggravated, with impaired calcium handling compromising mitochondrial function and enhancing inflammatory signalling, directly contributing to electrophysiological instability and arrhythmogenesis. 8 , 9 Taken together, the up‐regulation of desmoyokin, OCIAD1 and UCHL1 points towards a converging model in which alterations in calcium homeostasis, cytoskeletal organization and protein quality control drive mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation and fibrosis.

Our study has several limitations: The relatively small number of included patients may limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding the robustness of the biomarkers. The heterogenous genetic background of ACM patients may contribute to data variability. Given the overlapping clinical and molecular features of ACM and DCM, especially in advanced stages, this may also have affected the specificity of our findings. Direct statistical multiomics integration was not performed, as statistical robustness could not be ensured.

Nonetheless, our comprehensive multi‐level analysis revealed coherent and specific findings, showing that ACM disease mechanisms involve distinct molecular alterations affecting cellular signalling, energy metabolism and immune responses. These changes may contribute to tissue remodelling and fibrosis. Key molecules such as desmoyokin, OCIAD1 and UCHL1 are up‐regulated in ACM and distinguish it from DCM and healthy controls. These molecules are likely associated with disease‐specific pathological pathways and could be used as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
