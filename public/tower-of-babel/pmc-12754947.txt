Introduction

Taekwondo has evolved into a modern-day Olympic combat sport, where matches are typically structured as three 2 min rounds with a 1 min interval [1]. Athletes execute high-intensity attack sequences (7–39 attempts per match, 70–80% of them lasting < 2 s) [1], alternating with low-intensity phases, demonstrating work: rest (W: R) ratios ranging between 1:2 and 1:7 [2]. These contests elicit near maximal heart rate (HRmax) responses (>90 HRmax), high lactate concentrations([La−], 6.1–14 mmol/L), and high oxygen uptake (VO2, 46.9–63.0 ml/kg/min) [1, 3]. Metabolic analyses revealed that the aerobic system accounts for 58.4–66.0% to the total energy contribution during simulated match, phosphagen system accounts for 25.9–30.0%, and the glycolytic system 4.0–4.5% [4–6].

High-intensity interval training (HIIT), characterized by brief bouts of intense exercise alternated with recovery periods, was widely recognized for enhancing athletes’ cardiorespiratory fitness and metabolic efficiency [7]. In combat sports such as judo, karate, and wrestling, HIIT is commonly used to optimize both aerobic and anaerobic energy systems [8–10].For taekwondo, previous studies have examined HIIT protocols with exercise durations ranging from 10 s to 3 min, mirroring the sport’s high-intensity, intermittent demands [11, 12]. Notably, taekwondo-specific HIIT has been proposed based on competition time structures and physiological responses [13], with evidence suggesting it elicits similar heart rate, perceived exertion, and training loads as traditional interval running [14]. Furthermore, research indicates that such protocols can enhance maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max), anaerobic capacity, and agility [15, 16]. However, significant variations exist across studies in terms of W: R ratios (e.g., 1:1, 1:2, 1:3), each bout duration (6–30 s), and rest intervals (1–4 min), making it challenging to establish an optimal HIIT protocol for taekwondo athletes.

At least nine variables can be manipulated to prescribe different HIIT sessions [17], of which the intensity and duration of work and rest intervals are the key influencing factors [18]. Rogers et al. [19] demonstrated that shorter rest durations (60 s vs. 90 s vs. 120 s) during repeated 6 s sprints significantly reduced mean power and caloric expenditure while increasing rating of perceived exertion (RPE). Training should replicate the demands of the actual competition and at some instances create conditions even more difficult than those during the actual competition. In practice, taekwondo athletes typically use a work duration of 10 s with 1:0.5, 1:1 and 1:2 W: R ratio for taekwondo-specific HIIT [20, 21]. Notably, in this context, large W: R ratios (e.g., 1:5, 1:7) were rarely adopted. However, the acute physiological responses, neuromuscular fatigue (e.g., power output decline), and perceived exertion induced by particular HIIT protocols with the aforementioned W: R ratios in taekwondo athletes remain unclear.

During the preparatory and competitive phases of the season, coaches or practitioners formulate long-term training protocols and exercise prescriptions to enhance athletes’ competitive abilities and performance. Different HIIT protocols will impose varying internal loads or stresses on athletes, including physiological responses, neuromuscular fatigue, and perceived exertion [18]. Understanding the differences among them is of utmost significance and value in aspects such as training periodization, load monitoring, preventing overuse, or overtraining to avoid injuries or promote recovery.

Given that taekwondo-specific HIIT with a 1:2 W: R ratio has already been explored in our previous study [22]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare the physiological responses, neuromuscular fatigue, and perceived exertion between two W: R ratios (1:0.5 and 1:5) during taekwondo-specific HIIT. It was hypothesized that 1:0.5 W: R ratio would elicit higher physiological responses, neuromuscular fatigue, and perceived exertion.

Methods

Participants

A sample size of 15 was determined a priori using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2) based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance using a within factors design (alpha = 0.05, power = 0.8, effect size = 0.25, correlation among repeated measures = 0.6) [22]. Thirteen moderate-trained (between tier 2 and tier 3) college-level taekwondo athletes [23], including nine males (age 22.2 ± 2.5 years; body mass 70.8 ± 7.1 kg; height 176 ± 6 cm) and four females (age 20.7 ± 2.4 years; body mass 65.2 ± 8.4 kg; height 1.70 ± 4.0 cm) volunteered to participate in this study. They were all training 10 to 15 h per week and had been practicing taekwondo for 7 ± 2 years, reporting no injuries or illnesses in the 6 months preceding the study. Participants were instructed not to consume alcoholic beverages for one week, suspend resistance training for 72 h, and avoid high-intensity exercise for 24 h prior to the trials. Before the trials, athletes were informed of the procedures, including the possible risks involved, and signed an informed consent form. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (102772021RT32).

Design

During the first visit to the laboratory, the demographics and other information of the participants were collected, and the participants were familiarized with the trial process. Following that, they completed the frequency speed of kick test, which is a 10 s maximum capacity of the roundhouse kick test [24]. This test exhibits a high test-retest intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between test-retest (ICC = 0.95).with a test-retest coefficient of variation(CV) of 2.9% [24].During this session, participants performed roundhouse kicks as fast as possible on a free-standing punching bag and the test result was 23 ± 1 kicks. Simple randomization was used in this study, with a randomization table designed by Microsoft Excel. On subsequent visits to the laboratory, participants randomly performed two taekwondo-specific HIIT. The design of the two trials was consistent with the competition, which is divided into three rounds of 2 min roundhouse kicks and 1 min rest between rounds. During the HIIT, the work duration was constant at 10 s but W: R ratios varied. Two configurations of W: R ratios were used;10 s:5 s and 10 s:50 s. To ensure that participants get sufficient rest and recovery, testing sessions were separated by at least 24 h of rest. All tests were conducted in the laboratory during the winter and the environmental conditions were similar in all trials (temperature:14–18 °C, relative humidity: 43–55%, and barometric air pressure:1013–1030 mbar).

Experimental sessions

Prior to each trial, participants completed a standardized 10 min warm-up protocol consisting of running drills, dynamic stretching exercises, countermovement jumps (CMJs), and roundhouse kick practice. Following this, a 10 min resting period was allowed during which participants were instrumented with a heart rate monitor (Polar Accurex Plus, Polar Electro, Finland) and a portable gas analyzer (Metamax 3B, Cortex, Germany). The portable gas exchange analyze was shown to provide reliable measurements of metabolic demand, with typical errors of 2–3% forVO2, VCO2, and VE [25]. A pre-test sample of blood for lactate concentration determination [La−] was collected and the baseline CMJ performance was recorded. Trial comprised three 2 min rounds of continuous alternating roundhouse kicks performed at a standardized cadence of 2 beats/s, cued by an audio signal. This cadence was predetermined during preliminary testing. Kicks were directed at a free-standing punching bag equipped with an electronic scoring system (JS-JT01, Jijin Smart, China), with an assistant stabilizing the bag throughout testing. Kick performance was objectively recorded via the electronic point scoring system. During the rest intervals following each round, [La−], RPE, and CMJ variables were collected. Post-exercise oxygen consumption was measured during a 6 min seated recovery period following test completion.(Fig. 1)

Experimental protocol

Cardiopulmonary measures

Prior to each test, the gas analyzer was calibrated according to the operating manual using a 3-L syringe and a gas of defined composition (O2 15.00%, CO2 5.00%). HR and VO2 were continuously monitored throughout the testing. The mean heart rate (HRmean) and oxygen uptake (VO2mean) during exercise were calculated based on the averages recorded over 2 min of each round. The peak heart rate (HRpeak) was defined as the highest value during each round, while peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) was defined as the highest 15 s average value of VO2 measured during each round [26].

Blood sampling

Capillary blood samples (10 µL each) were obtained from the earlobe at rest (baseline), immediately after round 1 and 2 of exercise, and at 0, 3, 5, and 7 min post-exercise and analyzed using a lactate analyzer (Biosen S_Line, EKF Diagnostics, Germany). The coefficient of variation for this analysis is less than 3% (https://www.ekfdiagnostics.com/point-of-care/diabetes/biosen/).

Energy system contribution calculation

Estimates of aerobic, anaerobic lactic, and anaerobic alactic system use was carried out through the measurement of oxygen consumption during activity, the peak blood lactate concentration, and the fast phase of excess oxygen consumption after exercise (EPOCfast), respectively [5]. Aerobic energy (Waer) was calculated from VO2 above rest, caloric-equivalent-adjusted for the corresponding respiratory-exchange ratio and body mass using the formula Waer (J) = VO2 (ml) × caloric equivalent (J/ml). The resting VO2 was defined as 3.5 ml/kg/min for females and 4.0 ml/kg/min for males [27]. Anaerobic lactic energy (W[la−]) was determined from changes in pre-and post-round blood lactate (△[la−]), O2-lactate equivalent, caloric equivalent, and body mass using the formula W[la−] (J) = △[la−] (mmol/L) × O2-lactate equivalent (ml/kg/mmol/L) × caloric equivalent (J/ml) × body mass (kg) with the O2-lactate equivalent of 3 ml/kg/mmol/L and a caloric equivalent of 21.131 J/mL [28].

Repayment of high-energy phosphates (Wpcr) was assumed to correspond to the fast component of post round oxygen uptake (VO2pcr) and calculated from the latter and its caloric equivalent of 21.1311 J/ml: Wpcr (J) = VO2pcr (ml) × caloric equivalent (J/ml) [28]. Wpcr was estimated considering the oxygen consumption during the interval between rounds and the EPOCfast after round 3 [28]. The time course of the VO2 during the post-exercise recovery was modeled using a bi-exponential function as described by: VO2(t) = a e−t/τa + b e−t/τa + c [29]. where VO2(t) is the oxygen uptake at time t, a and b are the amplitudes of the fast and slow components, respectively, τa and τb the corresponding time constants and c is the VO2 at rest.

Countermovement jump

CMJ performance assessment was conducted pre-trial and after each round utilizing a wireless portable jump test mat which shows a good reliability [(ICC) = 0.89, 95% confidence intervals (CI): 0.76–0.95] (Smart Jump, Canberra, Australia) [30]. Participants assumed a standardized starting position with an upright posture and hands placed firmly on the hips. Upon hearing the auditory cue, they performed a rapid countermovement squat followed immediately by a maximal vertical jump. Throughout the flight phase, they maintained strict leg alignment at 90° relative to the ground surface. Each landing was executed with proper absorption mechanics to ensure force dissipation, followed by a complete return to the initial standing position. Two CMJs were performed, separated by 10 s, and the average value was used in the results.

Rating of perceived exertion

The RPE was assessed after each interval training session using the Borg Scale [6–20, 31], and its ICC was 0.94 [32], ensuring ratings reflected the actual exercise intensity [33]. Prior to the study, all participants underwent standardized training on proper RPE scale usage to guarantee the accuracy of self-reported ratings.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS Institute, Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution. All data was normally distributed. The variables were compared using a two-way (trial × round) analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measurements. The wholeness of the data for all study variables was verified in accordance with the Mauchly test, and the Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment was used when the sphericity assumption was violated. The Bonferroni test was used as post hoc test. Discussion

The main findings of the present study partially confirm our hypotheses. Specifically, Two W: R ratios of taekwondo-specific HIIT showed that the relative aerobic energy contribution increases with the increase in the number of rounds. At the same time, the relative glycolytic energy contribution decreases while the relative phosphagen energy contribution remains unchanged. Compared with the 1:5 W: R ratio, the 1:0.5 W: R ratio elicits a greater physiological response and perceived exertion.

In the present study, under the same total training time, two W: R ratios led to variations in the number of roundhouse kicks (i.e., 160 kicks and 40 kicks) and rest times (i.e., 40 s and 100 s) per round. For the two W: R ratio, after the Waer reaches its peak in round 2, it remains stable in round 3. W[la−] shows a continuous downward trend with the increase of rounds. Wpcr remains stable in round 1 and 2 but begins to decline in round 3. This result is consistent with the energy contribution changes in amateur boxing studied by Davis et al. [28] and badminton training drills studied by Edel et al. [35] Moreover, the results of the energy system relative contribution with the progress of exercise are consistent with those reported by Gaitanos et al. [36] Using a different exercise protocol compared to our study-repeated sprints on the cycle ergometer-their results showed an increase in the contribution of aerobic metabolism to the total energy production from the first to the last sprint [36]. In our study, even when the W: R ratio of taekwondo-specific HIIT changes from 1:0.5 to 1:5, the aerobic system is the main contributor to energy production (62.0% − 72.5%), supplemented by phosphagen (21.5% − 24.7%), while the contribution of the glycolytic system is the lowest (4.8% − 13.3%). Compared with competitions or simulated competitions [4–6], the relative phosphagen energy contribution in this study is lower, while the glycolytic energy contribution is more. These findings provide a reference for the training of taekwondo and other combat sports. Under the premise of the same total training duration, a 1:0.5 W: R ratio led to a greater number of kicks, resulting in a greater absolute energy contribution, but did not significantly alter the proportion of contribution of energy systems to the total energy production. This indicates that the 1:0.5 W: R ratio can increase the training volume without changing the relative energy system contribution. Therefore, coaches can adjust the W: R ratio during HIIT to more precisely develop personalized training programs according to the individual needs of athletes and competition requirements. For example, in order to enhance the endurance and overall training volume of athletes, it is recommended to use 1:0.5 W: R ratios. Conversely, in order to optimize the energy recovery process of athletes after HIIT, it is recommended to choose a 1:5 W: R ratio.

HR, VO2, and [La−] for the two W: R ratios in the present taekwondo-specific HIIT showed an upward trend with increasing rounds. There were significant differences in HR and VO2 between 1:0.5 and 1:5 W: R ratios, which was consistent with the early results of Nicolo et al. [37]. Higher VO2 and [La−] were also shown in trained cyclists in response to a 40 s: 20 s vs. 30 s: 30 s cycling regimen performed at 135% maximum power output. These findings suggest that the1:0.5 W: R ratio imposes a higher physiological demand on cardiovascular and metabolic systems, potentially influencing training adaptations and recovery strategies in athletes.

The CMJ has an eccentric component and is an indicator of slow stretch-shortening cycle performance [38]. Chiodo et al. [39] found that the CMJ height of taekwondo athletes did not decrease after the competition, but increased. Vuorimaa et al. [40] reported that under the same W: R ratio, when changing the duration of a single bout of work, the CMJ height of national level long-distance runners did not decrease. Previous research has suggested the use of RPE as an indicator of training intensity, including physiological (VO2, HR, ventilation, beta endorphin, circulating glucose concentration, and glycogen depletion) and psychological factors during HIIT [41, 42]. RPE increases as a result of the combination of exercise and rest duration. Research indicated that increasing the recovery time (60 s vs. 90 s vs. 120 s) during a protocol consisting of 10 × 6 s sprints led to a decrease in RPE [19]. Another research also confirmed that with the rest duration kept constant at 30 s, increasing the running duration from 30 s to 60 s and 90 s significantly increased the athletes’ RPE [43]. Based on these findings, taekwondo-specific HIIT with a higher W: R ratio may stimulate greater glycolytic activity, leading to increased [La⁻] and H⁺ accumulation, thereby elevating RPE [44]. Additionally, further analysis indicated that the changes in RPE during 1:5 W: R ratio were consistent with those reported by Bridge et al. in the international taekwondo competitions32. Specifically, the RPE ranged from 11 in round 1 to 13 in round 2, and then to 14 in round 3, with perceived exertion levels ranging from “light” to “hard”.

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the current findings. First, the sample size was relatively small (N = 13), including both male and female participants. This limited sample size may affect the generalizability of the results, and physiological differences between genders could have influenced the outcomes. Second, this study specifically examined the roundhouse kick — a fundamental taekwondo technique frequently employed in competition. While different techniques activate various muscle groups, these findings may offer preliminary insights applicable to other movements, though further research is needed to validate such extrapolation. Additionally, resting oxygen consumption was estimated rather than directly measured, which introduces potential errors in calculations of the energy systems contribution.

Conclusions

During 2 min × 3 rounds taekwondo-specific HIIT, athletes exhibited progressively higher physiological responses and RPE across rounds as W: R ratio increased. Compared with 1:5 W: R, the 1:0.5 W: R elicited greater physiological responses and RPE. In terms of energy metabolism, two W: R ratios elicited similar relative energy contribution, with aerobic metabolism serving as the primary energy source, followed by the phosphagen system, while glycolytic contributions remained minimal. Coaches and athletes need to recognize the differences in training stimuli among taekwondo-specific HIIT with different W: R ratios and select appropriate training methods according to the competition period and training objectives. In addition, neuromuscular responses seem to be independent of physiological responses and RPE.
