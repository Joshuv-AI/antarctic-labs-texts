Prediction models for dynamic respiratory muscle strength in COPD and asthma patients based on fixed-pressure inspiratory muscle performance
Authors: Yasemin Ari Yilmaz, Mehmet Ismail Tosun, Erkan Demirkan, Meryem Yalcinkaya, Abdulsamet Efdal, Hilal Boyacı, Hazel Kara Gunaydin, Mustafa Arici, Mert Ayranci, Milan Marković
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDynamic inspiratory muscle strength, assessed using the S-Index, reflects pressure generation during flow-dependent inspiration and may provide functional information beyond static respiratory muscle strength measures. However, its routine clinical use is limited by the need for specialized equipment. This study aimed to develop and internally validate prediction models for estimating S-Index from accessible pulmonary function, body composition, and fixed-pressure inspiratory performance measures in male patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or asthma.MethodsIn this cross-sectional predictive modeling study, 123 male patients with COPD or asthma underwent S-Index assessment using a single-breath test. Demographic characteristics, body composition, spirometry variables, and inspiratory performance data from a fixed-pressure task-to-failure protocol at 30 cmH₂O were evaluated as candidate predictors. Linear, regularized, robust, support vector, quantile, and multilayer perceptron regression models were developed. Missing spirometry values were handled using 20 multiply imputed datasets. Model performance was evaluated using repeated nested five-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions and assessed using mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R²), with bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals.ResultsPeak inspiratory flow rate and the number of inspiratory repetitions showed the strongest correlations with S-Index (r = 0.83 and r = 0.70, respectively). In the full predictor scenario, regularized and linear regression models showed broadly comparable internal predictive performance. LassoLars numerically achieved the lowest prediction error, with an RMSE of 7.156 cmH₂O (95% CI: 6.108–8.285), MAE of 5.538 cmH₂O (95% CI: 4.778–6.352), and R² of 0.758 (95% CI: 0.651–0.829). Lasso and Elastic Net produced similar results. Models using only demographic, anthropometric, and body composition variables explained approximately 44–46% of the variance, whereas adding spirometry and inspiratory flow variables increased explained variance to approximately 70%. Complete-case sensitivity analysis yielded broadly consistent findings.ConclusionsIn this pooled single-center cohort of male patients with COPD or asthma, S-Index could be estimated with useful internal predictive performance from fixed-pressure inspiratory performance, spirometry, and body composition variables. These models should be interpreted as complementary to, rather than substitutes for, direct S-Index measurement. External validation is required before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04579-3.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma are major global health burdens, associated with impaired respiratory muscle function and reduced exercise capacity [1, 2]. These diseases impair both lung structure and respiratory muscle function, reducing strength, endurance, and neuromuscular coordination [1, 3, 4]. These alterations contribute to impaired ventilatory efficiency and increased symptom burden [5, 6]. Functional insufficiency of the diaphragm and accessory inspiratory muscles leads to reduced ventilatory capacity, increased dyspnea, and early fatigue during daily activities [7]. In this context, the accurate and sensitive assessment of respiratory muscle function is crucial for effective clinical monitoring and the planning of individualized rehabilitation strategies.

COPD and asthma are among the most prevalent chronic respiratory disorders worldwide and account for a substantial proportion of respiratory-related morbidity and healthcare burden [8, 9]. Both conditions are characterized by airflow limitation, increased airway resistance, and altered ventilatory mechanics, although their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms differ [10, 11]. In COPD, inflammation and hyperinflation shorten the diaphragm, placing the inspiratory muscles at a mechanical disadvantage [12–14]. This altered length–tension relationship may compromise pressure-generating capacity and contribute to increased work of breathing and persistent dyspnea. Furthermore, systemic inflammation and physical deconditioning, which are frequently observed in COPD, may exacerbate peripheral and respiratory muscle dysfunction [4, 15]. In asthma, recurrent episodes of bronchoconstriction and airway hyperresponsiveness increase inspiratory load and may impose repetitive stress on the respiratory muscles [16, 17]. Even in clinically stable phases, subtle alterations in breathing pattern, dynamic airflow limitation, and episodic hyperinflation may affect inspiratory muscle performance [13, 14]. Although static respiratory muscle strength may appear preserved in some individuals, dynamic measures may reveal functional limitations not detected by conventional maximal inspiratory pressure assessments [14, 18]. Therefore, in both COPD and asthma, evaluation of dynamic inspiratory muscle strength is clinically relevant for understanding symptom burden, exercise intolerance, and response to rehabilitation interventions [12, 16].

Maximal inspiratory pressure and maximal expiratory pressure measurements have traditionally been widely used in the evaluation of respiratory muscle function [19]. However, because these measurements largely rely on static and volitional maximal efforts, they provide limited insight into the dynamic performance and functional capacity of the respiratory muscles [20, 21]. In daily life and during exercise, respiratory muscles function under dynamic contractions with continuous airflow rather than under static conditions [22]. Therefore, in recent years, interest has increased in alternative parameters that reflect the dynamic force-generating capacity of the respiratory muscles, such as the S-Index and peak inspiratory flow rate (PIFR) [21]. Notably, both have been reported to be significantly lower in patients and considered relevant in pulmonary rehabilitation, particularly as a strategy to maintain or increase physical activity [23].

The S-Index is defined as the highest pressure value achieved during a maximal and rapid inspiratory effort in the dynamic phase of inspiration, when airflow is maintained, and has emerged as a parameter used to evaluate the dynamic strength characteristics of the inspiratory muscles [24]. Although PIFR and S-Index are both obtained during a rapid inspiratory maneuver, they characterize different components of inspiratory performance. PIFR represents the maximal airflow achieved, whereas the S-Index reflects the dynamic inspiratory pressure generated while airflow is maintained, thereby incorporating the pressure–flow interaction throughout inspiration [25, 26]. The S-Index measurement requires specialized devices. The high cost of these devices and their limited accessibility in field conditions significantly restrict the routine use of the S-Index in clinical practice and large patient populations [24]. On the other hand, inspiratory pressure threshold loading (IPTL) devices are often used in rehabilitation programs due to their ease of use, cost-effectiveness, and widespread availability in clinical settings [27]. However, studies directly relating performance outputs obtained through these devices to parameters reflecting dynamic respiratory muscle strength remain limited. Current methods used to assess dynamic respiratory muscle function require advanced technological equipment, limiting their applicability in routine clinical practice. This situation reduces feasibility, particularly in busy clinical centers and in resource-limited settings. Therefore, the ability to estimate a dynamic parameter such as the S-Index using repetition-based performance data obtained from widely used IPTL devices could make a significant contribution to clinical evaluation and monitoring processes. Such an approach may reduce measurement costs and enable the assessment of dynamic respiratory muscle performance in broader patient populations.

The aim of this study is to develop predictive models for S-Index values based on inspiratory repetitions performed to task failure using a fixed-pressure IMT device and anthropometry and spirometry parameters in individuals with COPD and asthma. The present research seeks to provide an alternative, feasible, and clinically meaningful approach to advanced technology-dependent measurement methods. In this context, we aim to establish a feasible approach to estimating dynamic inspiratory muscle strength in individuals with COPD and asthma.

Methods

The present study was approved by the Hitit University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee on January 28, 2026 (Application Number: 2026-0028; Decision Number: 2026-01). Clinical trial registration was not applicable because this was an observational, cross-sectional predictive modeling study and did not involve the prospective assignment of participants to an intervention.

Participants

We prospectively recruited consecutive patients who met the inclusion criteria from three clinics at Çorum Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital, Hitit University, Turkey, between 02/02/2026 and 27/03/2026. The inclusion criteria were as follows: being 18 years of age or older; having a clinical diagnosis of COPD or asthma; being able to perform fixed-pressure inspiratory muscle loading and S-Index measurements in accordance with the instructions and with correct technique; absence of any acute clinical condition during the 12 weeks before enrollment that could affect respiratory function beyond the existing diagnosis of COPD or asthma; and having signed the Informed Consent Form approved by the Ethics Committee. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: current smoking; inability to perform fixed-pressure inspiratory muscle loading or dynamic respiratory muscle strength measurements in accordance with the technical requirements of the measurement protocols; inability to obtain reliable data because of insufficient coordination, motivation, or volitional effort; presence of a musculoskeletal injury or surgical history affecting the diaphragm, thorax, spine, or abdominal region that could significantly impair respiratory mechanics; and the presence of neurological conditions that could affect respiratory muscle function, such as neuromuscular diseases, peripheral nerve damage, or central nervous system disorders. The required sample size was calculated a priori, resulting in a minimum sample of 86 participants (multiple linear regression with 12 predictors, significance level α = 0.05, power [1 − β] = 0.95, effect size ƒ² = 0.35; G*Power version 3.1.9.6, Kiel, Germany). However, all eligible and willing patients recruited during the study period were included to allow for possible exclusions arising from incomplete assessments or spirometry maneuvers that did not meet ATS/ERS acceptability and repeatability criteria and to improve the precision of the study estimates [28].

Experimental design

The research was designed as a cross-sectional predictive modeling study aiming to develop a multivariable regression-based estimation model for the S-Index. Individuals who were invited to participate and met the inclusion criteria attended a detailed information and familiarization session two days prior to the scheduled measurement date. During this session, all tests to be applied within the scope of the study were explained in detail, measurement protocols were demonstrated, and trial measurements were conducted to ensure that participants could perform the tests using correct technique. In addition, participants were verbally informed about behaviors to be avoided prior to the test day.

Demographic and body composition variables, including age (years), height (cm), body weight (kg), body mass index (kg/m²), fat-free mass (kg), fat mass (kg), and body fat percentage (%), as well as respiratory function and performance parameters, including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV₁), FEV₁/FVC ratio, peak expiratory flow (PEF), peak inspiratory flow rate (PIFR), inspiratory volume (IV), the number of repetitions performed to task failure during the fixed-pressure inspiratory loading test, and test completion time, were considered as candidate predictors. These variables were selected to represent complementary aspects of body size and composition, pulmonary function, inspiratory flow, and performance under fixed-pressure loading, while remaining obtainable through routine clinical or performance-based assessment. They were used to estimate the S-Index as a measure of dynamic inspiratory muscle strength.

The individual measurements were completed within a single day. On the measurement day, following the recording of participants’ demographic information, dynamic respiratory muscle strength measurement was performed first. Subsequently, body fat percentage was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis, followed by standard pulmonary function tests. In the final stage of the measurement protocol, an inspiratory muscle performance test to task failure was conducted using a fixed-pressure inspiratory muscle training device (POWERbreathe Classic Light Resistance, POWERbreathe International Ltd., Kent, United Kingdom). At this stage, all participants performed inspiratory maneuvers for the maximum duration they could sustain using the device set at a resistance level of 30 cmH₂O.

All evaluations were administered by experienced healthcare professionals (MD and PhD) and researchers specialized in sports sciences (PhD) who were familiar with the relevant measurement techniques. To minimize evaluator-dependent variability, each type of measurement was performed by the same researchers throughout the study.

Measurements

Respiratory variables

S-Index measurement was performed using a portable inspiratory muscle training and assessment device, the POWERbreathe K5 (POWERbreathe International Ltd., Kent, United Kingdom), via the device’s single-breath test mode [29]. In this mode, the device evaluates the dynamic respiratory muscle strength generated during a single maximal inspiratory maneuver and calculates the S-Index value.

During each trial, participants were instructed to first perform a full expiration to residual volume and then, through the device’s single-use, bacteria-filtered mouthpiece, to execute the fastest and most forceful possible inspiration until their lungs were completely filled. Following each single-breath measurement, the device displayed the S-Index value instantaneously in cmH₂O on its screen, and the obtained data were recorded. No warm-up protocol was applied prior to testing. Measurements were performed with participants in an upright standing position, and a nose clip was used throughout the test to prevent air leakage. Each participant performed eight consecutive maximal inspiratory efforts [25]. Short rest intervals were provided between trials when deemed necessary, and the highest S-Index value obtained for each individual was used for analysis [24]. All measurements were completed on the same day within a single session, and the S-Index assessment was completed in approximately 15 min.

Pulmonary function measurements were performed using a calibrated spirometry device (Beijing M&B Electronic Instruments Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in accordance with the spirometry technical standards published by the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society [30]. Forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV₁), FEV₁/FVC ratio, and peak expiratory flow (PEF) were obtained through standardized forced respiratory maneuvers. Prior to testing, standardized verbal instructions and demonstrations were provided to ensure correct technique. Each maneuver was initiated following normal tidal breathing with a maximal inspiration to total lung capacity; participants were then instructed to perform a rapid, explosive, and maximal-effort forced expiration (“blast” expiration) without delay. The forced expiration was continued until no further volume change occurred or end-of-test plateau criteria were met; subsequently, a maximal inspiratory maneuver was performed to complete the flow–volume curve. In accordance with ATS/ERS standards, at least three acceptable maneuvers were performed for each participant [28, 30]. Acceptability criteria included a rapid start of expiration, absence of cough within the first second, no early termination, and adequate effort throughout the maneuver. Repeatability criteria were evaluated according to ATS/ERS recommendations, and strong verbal encouragement was provided to ensure maximal performance during testing [30, 31]. For analysis, the highest FVC and highest FEV₁ values obtained from acceptable trials were used. PEF values were derived from the maximal flow points of the expiratory phase of the flow–volume curve. PIFR was measured separately using a Spirolab Plus device (MIR—Medical International Research S.p.A., Rome, Italy). Peak inspiratory flow rate (PIFR) represents the maximum airflow achieved during inspiration and reflects inspiratory flow performance, which is influenced by inspiratory muscle function and airway mechanics [25, 29]. All measurements were completed within a single session under standard laboratory conditions.

Body composition assessment

Body composition was assessed using the MC-580 Multi-Frequency Segmental Body Composition Scale with Column (Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan), based on the principle of multi-frequency segmental bioelectrical impedance analysis [32]. The device measures segmental impedance values by transmitting low-level alternating current at different frequencies through hand and foot electrodes and performs separate analyses for the trunk, right/left upper extremities, and right/left lower extremities. Using manufacturer algorithms, it instantaneously calculates parameters such as total body fat percentage, fat-free mass, muscle mass, and total body water. To enhance measurement validity, participants followed a standardized pre-test preparation protocol. They verbally confirmed that they maintained their normal hydration status. Considering that bioelectrical impedance measurements may be influenced by hydration status and gastrointestinal content, measurements were performed at the same time of day, and participants were instructed to empty their bladder prior to testing and were in a fasted state.

During measurement, participants stood barefoot on the platform electrodes and grasped the hand electrodes with both hands, maintaining an upright posture without movement or speaking, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Metal accessories were removed prior to measurement, and room temperature was maintained under standard laboratory conditions. Individual parameters such as age, sex, and height were entered into the device system before measurement, and the analysis was completed within approximately 20–60 s [33].

Inspiratory muscle training repetition test

To evaluate inspiratory muscle repetition performance, an inspiratory muscle exercise to task failure was performed using the POWERbreathe Classic Light Resistance device (POWERbreathe International Ltd., United Kingdom). Prior to measurements, all devices were cleaned with appropriate disinfectant solutions in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, and standard hygiene conditions were ensured for each participant. To prevent nasal breathing, participants’ noses were occluded with a standard nose clip throughout the test.

The exercise was performed with participants in a seated position, with back support and an upright posture. Participants were instructed to perform continuous, rhythmic, and deep inspiratory and expiratory maneuvers through the device at a normal breathing tempo. Standardized verbal instructions were provided throughout the test to ensure maintenance of correct breathing technique. Exercise duration was measured using a stopwatch, and the time elapsed from the start of the test to the point of task failure was recorded.

Task failure was determined by supervising researchers according to the same predefined criteria for all participants. These criteria included inability to maintain a rhythmic breathing pattern, a marked reduction in inspiratory effort, repeated technical errors despite verbal correction, or voluntary termination by the participant because of an inability or unwillingness to continue. The total number of valid respiratory repetitions completed before task failure was recorded as an indicator of inspiratory muscle performance.

Safety and termination criteria

All measurement and exercise protocols were planned and implemented with consideration of potential respiratory risks, particularly in individuals diagnosed with COPD and asthma. Throughout testing, participants were continuously monitored by healthcare professionals (MD., PhD.) experienced in respiratory physiology and clinical evaluation, as well as by researchers in sports sciences (PhD).

Prior to testing, participants were provided with detailed information regarding possible symptoms that might occur during the test and were informed that they could terminate the test immediately if they experienced any discomfort. To ensure safe test administration, perceived dyspnea and exertion levels were monitored using the Modified BORG Dyspnea Scale [34]. Borg scores were evaluated before and during the test in conjunction with clinical observation; testing was terminated in the event of a sudden and marked increase in perceived dyspnea. Participants were continuously monitored, and testing was terminated in the presence of clinically significant symptoms (severe dyspnea, dizziness, chest pain) or inability to maintain proper technique. In addition, a marked reduction in inspiratory effort, inability to maintain a rhythmic breathing pattern, or loss of adherence to the measurement protocol were accepted as technical termination criteria. For participants diagnosed with COPD, disease-specific safety criteria were defined as a sudden increase in dyspnea, excessive use of accessory respiratory muscles, pronounced prolongation of expiration, increased wheezing, or signs of exercise-induced respiratory instability. For individuals with asthma, wheezing, chest tightness, coughing attacks, sudden decreases in inspiratory or expiratory flow, or clinical signs suggestive of bronchospasm were considered sufficient for immediate test termination. A participant’s request to discontinue the test, without providing any reason, was accepted as sufficient for termination. After testing, participants were kept under observation until complete resolution of any symptoms was confirmed and, when considered necessary, were referred to the relevant healthcare unit for further clinical evaluation. No adverse clinical events or safety issues requiring application of the predefined termination criteria were observed during the measurement and exercise protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and the development of regression-based models to estimate the S-Index. Three prespecified predictor scenarios were examined to represent different levels of clinical feasibility. The full scenario included demographic characteristics, body composition, spirometry variables, and fixed-pressure inspiratory performance measures. Scenario 01 included demographic, anthropometric, and body composition variables only, whereas Scenario 02 additionally included pulmonary function and inspiratory flow variables but excluded fixed-pressure repetition performance and time to exhaustion.

The categorical disease variable was represented using binary numerical encoding, with asthma coded as 0 and COPD as 1. Seven participants had concurrently missing values for FVC, FEV₁, FEV₁/FVC, and PEF. The primary analyses retained all 123 participants using 20 multiply imputed datasets generated through stochastic chained equations with Bayesian ridge regression. GOLD and GINA severity levels were not included in the imputation models, model development, hyperparameter optimization, or prediction procedures. Because Scenario 01 contained no missing predictor values, imputation was not required for that scenario. A complete-case sensitivity analysis was additionally performed using the 116 participants with complete predictor data.

Linear Regression, Ridge, Lasso, LassoLars, Elastic Net, Huber Regression, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Quantile Regression, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) regression models were evaluated. These approaches were selected to compare an unpenalized linear benchmark with regularized and robust linear methods and with models capable of representing nonlinear relationships. Model hyperparameters were optimized using the Optuna framework.

Model development and internal validation were conducted using repeated nested five-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions. Within each outer validation iteration, multiple imputation, standardization, and hyperparameter optimization were performed exclusively using the corresponding training data. Hyperparameters were selected using five-fold cross-validation within the inner loop, after which the optimized model was evaluated on the held-out outer validation fold. Thus, all reported performance estimates were based on out-of-fold predictions generated for participants who were not included in the corresponding model-fitting process.

Model performance was evaluated using mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). Models were compared primarily according to RMSE, with MAE and R² considered complementary measures of prediction error and explained variance. Participant-level out-of-fold predictions obtained across the repeated validation cycles were averaged, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples.

Before model development, relationships between S-Index and demographic characteristics, body composition variables, pulmonary function parameters, and inspiratory performance indicators were examined using Pearson correlation analysis and Random Forest–based permutation importance. Model interpretability was further examined using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to characterize the relative contribution and direction of the predictors. Statistical significance for correlation analyses was set at α = 0.05. All analyses were conducted in Python 3.10 using the scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, Optuna, and SHAP libraries.

Regression methods used for S-Index prediction

Linear Regression was used as an unpenalized benchmark model [35]. Ridge, Lasso, LassoLars, and Elastic Net were included to examine whether coefficient shrinkage and, where applicable, variable selection improved model stability in the presence of correlated predictors. Ridge applies an L2 penalty that reduces coefficient magnitude without eliminating predictors [36], whereas Lasso applies an L1 penalty that can shrink selected coefficients to zero [37]. LassoLars provides an efficient procedure for estimating the Lasso solution path [38], and Elastic Net combines L1 and L2 penalties to balance variable selection with coefficient stability [39].

Huber Regression was used to reduce sensitivity to influential residuals and extreme observations [40]. SVR and MLP regression were evaluated to determine whether allowing nonlinear relationships improved prediction beyond the linear approaches [41, 42]. Quantile Regression was included as a robust alternative that estimates conditional quantiles of the outcome rather than focusing exclusively on the conditional mean [43].

The primary analysis retained all 123 participants through multiple imputation. Model development and internal validation were conducted using repeated nested five-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions. All preprocessing procedures, including multiple imputation and standardization, were fitted exclusively using the corresponding training data. Model hyperparameters were optimized in the inner cross-validation loop, and performance was evaluated using out-of-fold predictions from the outer validation folds. Overall, reducing the number of input variables resulted in a progressive decline in predictive performance. Models based solely on demographic, anthropometric, and body composition variables in Scenario 01 explained approximately 44–46% of the variance in S-Index. Adding pulmonary function and inspiratory flow variables in Scenario 02 increased the explained variance to approximately 70%. However, the full models, which additionally included fixed-pressure inspiratory repetition performance and time to exhaustion, demonstrated the highest predictive performance and explained approximately 75–76% of the variance.

These findings demonstrate that pulmonary function and inspiratory flow variables provide substantial predictive information beyond demographic and body composition characteristics. The additional improvement observed in the full models further indicates that fixed-pressure inspiratory performance measures contribute complementary information to the estimation of dynamic inspiratory muscle strength.

Discussion

The present findings indicate that, within this pooled cohort of male patients with COPD or asthma, S-Index could be estimated from accessible clinical and fixed-pressure inspiratory performance measures. PIFR and inspiratory repetition performance accounted for a substantial proportion of the variability in S-Index, while regularized linear models produced the lowest numerical prediction errors during internal validation. Under the repeated nested cross-validation framework, the best-performing model explained approximately 76% of the variance in S-Index. The complete-case sensitivity analysis yielded broadly consistent findings, supporting the robustness of the results to the method used for handling missing spirometry values. These findings suggest that fixed-pressure inspiratory performance measures may provide useful information about dynamic inspiratory muscle strength when direct S-Index assessment is unavailable, although external validation is required before clinical application.

From a physiological perspective, the prominence of PIFR as a predictor is noteworthy. Although PIFR and S-Index are both obtained during rapid inspiratory maneuvers, they characterize different components of inspiratory performance: PIFR quantifies the maximal inspiratory airflow achieved, whereas the S-Index reflects the dynamic pressure generated while airflow is maintained. Their strong association is therefore physiologically plausible, but the two measures should not be regarded as interchangeable [33].

Furthermore, the substantial contribution of the number of inspiratory repetitions sustained under fixed resistance supports the concept that inspiratory muscle performance reflects not only maximal strength but also a functional endurance component [44, 45]. This finding underscores the multidimensional nature of inspiratory muscle function and indicates that dynamic performance measures may capture these components simultaneously. At the same time, long duration of fixed-pressure inspiratory effort has been associated with headaches and dizziness, particularly in females [46, 47]. These suggest caution when implementing such testing in fragile populations, and may be a practical limitation during introduction of the examined approach in clinical practice.

The positive association observed with fat-free mass and the negative association with body fat percentage suggest that respiratory muscle performance may be influenced by peripheral muscle mass and thoracic mechanical loading. Increased fat mass may impose additional mechanical load on the respiratory system, potentially affecting ventilatory efficiency, whereas higher fat-free mass may contribute to greater force-generating capacity of the inspiratory muscles [48, 49]. In this context, the resulting model structure supports the notion that respiratory muscle function reflects an integrated interaction between ventilatory flow characteristics and overall body composition.

Another important finding of this study was the broadly similar performance of the evaluated regularized and linear regression models. LassoLars, Lasso, and Elastic Net produced the most favorable numerical results, although their bootstrap-derived confidence intervals substantially overlapped with those of Linear Regression, Ridge, and Huber Regression. Therefore, the small differences between models should not be interpreted as evidence of clear superiority. The overall pattern suggests that the relationships between the examined predictors and S-Index were largely captured by linear structures within the observed range, limiting the additional benefit of more complex nonlinear approaches. Regularization may nevertheless remain advantageous in datasets with correlated predictors and limited sample sizes by reducing coefficient instability and overfitting [50]. From a clinical perspective, relatively simple and interpretable models may offer practical advantages, provided that their performance is confirmed through external validation.

In a study conducted by Cordeiro et al. [51] in children with asthma, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) values were similar to predicted norms for healthy individuals, whereas S-Index values were significantly lower compared with healthy controls. These findings suggest that reductions in respiratory muscle strength not detected by static MIP measurements may be revealed through S-Index assessment. Strong associations between S-Index and lung volumes and pulmonary function parameters have also been reported [51]. In a study by Arslantürk et al. [52] involving 30 healthy young adult university students who were not competitive athletes, S-Index showed strong positive correlations with FVC and FEV₁ and very strong positive correlations with PEF and MVV (r = 0.600–0.770, p < 0.05). These data suggest that S-Index is associated not only with respiratory strength, but also with pulmonary function and respiratory endurance.

Because it is measured under open airway conditions, it may reduce hemodynamic load and hyperventilation risks that can accompany MIP testing, particularly in individuals with severe COPD or asthma exacerbations [25, 53]. As S-Index is based on a dynamic inspiratory maneuver, it enables evaluation of inspiratory muscle performance under conditions more closely resembling actual breathing. In patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, an increase in S-Index following eight weeks of inspiratory muscle training has been reported, suggesting its potential utility in monitoring treatment response [54]. Inspiratory muscle training is widely applied in pulmonary rehabilitation [55–57]. In such programs, assessing baseline strength and monitoring training-induced improvements are essential. In IMT protocols, S-Index may be used to adjust exercise load and monitor treatment response. As S-Index reflects the dynamic pressure response generated during maximal inspiration with ongoing airflow, it may reveal functional performance differences not apparent in static maximal pressure measurements and may contribute to tracking changes throughout the rehabilitation process.

The study is not free from limitations. The model was developed using a single-center dataset and was not externally validated, which may limit generalizability. Although repeated nested five-fold cross-validation with bootstrap confidence intervals provided a rigorous assessment of internal predictive performance, this approach does not establish model performance in other centers or in COPD and asthma as separate diagnostic groups. In addition, four spirometry variables were missing concurrently in seven participants. Multiple imputation was used to retain the full sample, and the complete-case sensitivity analysis produced broadly consistent results; nevertheless, some uncertainty related to the missing-data mechanism cannot be excluded. Since bioelectrical impedance analysis can be affected by hydration status, potential deviations in body composition measurements should be considered. Additionally, factors such as disease severity, pharmacological treatment status, and clinical stability were not included in the model. Since these variables may influence respiratory muscle performance, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to different clinical subgroups. Furthermore, the study is based on a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about temporal dynamics or causality. Future longitudinal investigations are needed to determine whether the discussed variables can reliably track changes in the S-Index over time. Finally, multiple between-sex differences in the structure and function of the respiratory system exist, including size of airways and lungs, shape of the rib-cage, as well as differences in dynamic respiratory muscle strength [21, 58]. Accordingly, the findings should not be extrapolated to women or to populations with substantially different demographic or clinical characteristics. Future research should include diverse populations, comprehensive calibration assessment, and implementation of clinical decision analysis protocols. Moreover, examining applicability of S-Index results in clinical practice, particularly within longitudinal design to inform risk stratification policies, seems warranted.

Conclusion

In this pooled single-center cohort of male patients with COPD or asthma, S-Index could be estimated from fixed-pressure inspiratory muscle performance and spirometry variables, with regularized linear models demonstrating useful and broadly comparable internal predictive performance. These findings support further evaluation of interpretable prediction tools for settings in which direct S-Index measurement is unavailable. External validation and calibration assessment are required before clinical implementation.

Clinical implications

The presented findings suggest that dynamic inspiratory muscle strength, represented by the S-Index, could be estimated with useful internal predictive performance when respiratory function parameters and task-based inspiratory performance measures are available. Models based only on anthropometric variables showed markedly lower accuracy, whereas the addition of spirometry and inspiratory flow variables improved predictive performance but remained less accurate than the full models. From a clinical perspective, the best-performing models may provide a practical alternative in settings where direct dynamic inspiratory strength assessment is not feasible due to equipment availability, time constraints, or cost limitations. This may be particularly relevant for screening contexts, remote monitoring settings, or resource-limited clinical environments where dynamic inspiratory testing devices are not routinely available. The ability to approximate S-Index values through commonly obtainable measures such as inspiratory flow characteristics and performance-based breathing tasks could facilitate more frequent monitoring of respiratory muscle function in patients with chronic respiratory diseases, including COPD and asthma. To support practical use, an Excel-based calculator based on the numerically best-performing LassoLars model was prepared as Supplementary File 2, allowing users to enter raw clinical and performance values and obtain the corresponding predicted S-Index value without manually standardizing the input variables. Furthermore, the best-performing models may support the further development of individualized respiratory muscle assessment strategies in settings where specialized dynamic testing devices are not available. However, these findings should be interpreted as complementary rather than substitutive to direct measurements, and external validation is required before routine clinical implementation.
