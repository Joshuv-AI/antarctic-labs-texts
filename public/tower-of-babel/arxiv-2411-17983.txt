Optimized Conformal Selection: Powerful Selective Inference After Data-Dependent Score Construction
Tian Bai, Ying Jin

Abstract
We study data-driven score construction in conformal selection, which uses conformal p-values and multiple testing to select “interesting” instances with large unobserved labels from a pool of unlabeled data, while controlling the FDR in finite sample. While existing solutions require the score function to be constructed independent of the calibration and test data, it is desirable to select or construct powerful conformity score in a data-driven manner and to leverage all labeled data for score training when presented with many score choices and limited labeled data. We propose Optimized Conformal Selection (OptCS), a general framework that allows valid multiple testing after flexible data-driven score construction. We introduce general conditions under which OptCS constructs valid conformal p-values despite substantial data reuse and handles complex p-value dependencies to maintain finite-sample FDR control. We instantiate this general recipe to propose three FDR-controlling procedures, each optimizing the models differently: (i) selecting a powerful score function among multiple candidates, (ii) training the score with all labeled data without sample splitting, and (iii) combining full-sample training and model selection. We demonstrate the efficacy of our methods via simulation studies and real applications in drug discovery and large language models.



Keywords:
Conformal inference, conformal p-value, model selection, FDR control, sample splitting.

1
Introduction

Conformal inference is a versatile, distribution-free framework for predictive inference
(
Vovk et al., 2005
)
.
Given any prediction model, it employs any user-specified conformity score to measure how well the outcomes “conform” to the predictions, and then leverages the exchangeability among scores to deliver valid statistical evidence (p-value). Its strength lies in its flexibility as a “wrapper” for valid inference, regardless of the score function used. This principle underlies its use in various tasks, including prediction set construction
(
Lei et al., 2018
)
and recent extensions in two-sample testing
(
Hu and Lei, 2024
)
and multiple testing
(
Bates et al., 2023
;
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
.
However, despite this flexibility, a common challenge is score construction
(
Liang et al., 2024a
)
: determining the appropriate conformity score and/or prediction model to improve task-specific performance.

We study data-driven score construction in the context of model-free selective inference
(
Jin and Candès, 2023a
)
, which aims to identify samples with large unobserved response variables with error control.
Formally, given labeled data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and unlabeled samples
\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the goal is to select a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
such that most of them obey
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
, where
c_{n+j}
are user-specified thresholds above which the outcomes are considered interesting. This is formalized by false discovery rate (FDR) control:

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}:=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in{\mathcal{S}},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|{\mathcal{S}}|}\Bigg]\leq q,

(1)

where
q\in(0,1)
is a user-specified nominal level.
Since the FDR measures the expected proportion of incorrectly selected instances, rigorous FDR control is crucial for the trustworthiness of screening procedures and the efficiency of downstream investigations on the selected units (see representative applications below).

Conformal selection
(
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
addresses this problem by extending conformal inference ideas to multiple test samples (see also extensions to covariate shift
(
Jin and Candès, 2023a
)
and online settings
(
Xu and Ramdas, 2024
)
, and with constraints and objectives such as diversity
(
Wu et al., 2023
;
Huo et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Nair et al., 2025
)
).
Given any pre-specified conformity score function (wrapped around any prediction model), conformal selection is an automatic pipeline: it compares each test score to the scores on the labeled (calibration) data, constructs p-values for the test units, and determines the selection set among the unlabeled (test) data. Its performance thus relies on the choice of the conformity score function (which implicitly depends on the prediction model).

To maintain FDR control, the score function construction is required to be independent of the calibration and test data it applies to. Akin to the celebrated split conformal prediction idea
(
Lei et al., 2018
)
, if the score needs to be trained, tuned, or selected, this is usually done on a separate training fold (often a subset of labeled data). This prevents practitioners from (i) data-driven model selection, where one selects a score based on its downstream selection performance such as the resulting selection-set size, and (ii) full-data training, where all labeled data are used for model training without sample splitting. We illustrate the need for both forms of data-dependent score construction through two typical applications.

•

Drug discovery
. Early stages of drug discovery aim to find highly viable drug candidates—molecules, antibiotics, or proteins—with desirable biological properties
(
Szymański et al., 2011
;
Macarron et al., 2011
)
. Such tasks can be characterized by finding drug candidates
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
with
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
for an unknown property
Y_{n+j}
and thresholds
c_{n+j}
. Given any model that predicts
Y_{n+j}
based on physical/chemical features
X_{n+j}
, conformal selection can be used to shortlist drug candidates with FDR control, enabling efficient pre-evaluation before costly validations
(
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
.
However, with numerous pretrained models by recent advances in machine learning
(
Xiong et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2021
;
Huang et al., 2020
;
Carracedo-Reboredo et al., 2021
)
, it is often unclear a priori which conformity score (and the pretrained model it relies on) will be most effective for the downstream FDR-controlled selection. Rather than fixing this choice in advance, a scientist may desire to use the available data to select a score with strong
downstream selection performance.

•

LLM abstention
. Large language models (LLMs) show tremendous potential in high-stakes tasks such as generating radiology reports, but issues like hallucination pose serious risks
(
Huang et al., 2023b
;
Weidinger et al., 2021
)
. In this context, conformal selection can be used to select radiology images
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
for which the generated reports align with experts, represented by an unknown “alignment indicator”
Y_{n+j}=1
.
Gui et al. (2024)
uses a set of labeled data (images and human reports) to train an “alignment predictor” that predicts
Y_{n+j}
based on features of LLM outputs
X_{n+j}
, and applies conformal selection—needing a separate labeled calibration dataset—to select new images with “aligned” outputs. Since the expert-labeled data are often scarce, it is naturally desirable to train the predictor on as many labeled data as possible to improve the selection power.

The challenge in performing such data-driven score construction is that it is often incompatible with the exchangeability requirement. Once the calibration and test data are involved in selecting and training the score function, the resulting scores may no longer be exchangeable. In the context of conformal selection, it remains unclear how the score can be constructed using the data it applies to while maintaining valid FDR control.

We propose Optimized Conformal Selection (OptCS), a general framework for FDR-controlled conformal selection after data-dependent score construction. Here, score

2
Preliminaries

This section reviews the key ideas of conformal selection, and discusses the challenges in data-driven model optimization, followed by
a brief overview of related works.

2.1
Recap: Conformal selection

Assume access to labeled data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{label}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and unlabeled test data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}=\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
whose responses
\{Y_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are unobserved.
Conformal selection begins by randomly splitting the labeled data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{label}}
into two disjoint subsets, the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}:=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}
and the calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}:=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}
. First,
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}
is used to train a model and build a
monotone
conformity score function
v:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
represent the sample space of
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{Y_{i}\}
.

Definition 1
.

A score function
v\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is monotone if
v(x,y)\leq v(x,y^{\prime})
whenever
y\leq y^{\prime}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

A monotone score function
v
can wrap around any prediction model obtained in the training step, e.g.,
v(x,y)=y-\widehat{\mu}(x)
where
\widehat{\mu}(x)
is a point prediction for
y
given
x
. Other popular choices include those based on quantile regression
(
Romano et al., 2019
)
and conditional cumulative distribution functions
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2021
)
.
We then compute the calibration scores
V_{i}=v(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=n_{1}+1,\dots,n
, and test scores
\widehat{V}_{n+j}=v(X_{n+j},c_{n+j})
,
j=1,\dots,m
. These scores can be understood as “test statistics” for large outcomes.
Conformal selection then builds a conformal p-value for each test point:

\displaystyle p_{j}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+j}\}}{n_{2}+1},\quad j=1,\dots,m,

(2)

where
n_{2}=n-n_{1}
.
Intuitively, a small p-value suggests
\widehat{V}_{n+j}=v(X_{n+j},c_{n+j})
is unusually small compared to
\{V(X_{i},Y_{i})\}
, indicating that
Y_{n+j}
is likely to exceed
c_{n+j}
, i.e., is “of interest”.
The selection set
{\mathcal{S}}
is then obtained by applying the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure
(
Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995
)
to
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
at nominal level
q
.
As long as
(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
is exchangeable with
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}
, it controls FDR in finite sample
(
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
.
From now on, we refer to this procedure as Split Conformal Selection (SCS) for convenience.
Following
Jin and Candès (2023b)
, we exclusively focus on the powerful “clipped”-type scores.

Assumption 1
.

The thresholds
\{c_{i}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}
are observed for the labeled data, such that
\{(X_{i},c_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n+m}
are exchangeable. The score functions considered are clipped, i.e., they are monotone and obey
v(X_{i},Y_{i})=v(X_{i},c_{i})
when
Y_{i}\leq c_{i}
.

The condition
v(X_{i},Y_{i})=v(X_{i},c_{i})
for
Y_{i}\leq c_{i}
means that the score is clipped
at the threshold: any response below
c_{i}
is treated as
c_{i}
. This pushes the scores
V_{i}
to the largest values possible (subject to monotonicity) to yield small p-values (
2
) and powerful selection.
As pointed out in
Jin and Candès (2023b)
, any monotone score can be transformed to a clipped score with strictly higher power once
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are observed. As we shall see later, the fact that
v(X_{i},Y_{i})=v(X_{i},c_{i})
when
Y_{i}\leq c_{i}
is crucial for flexible score construction without knowing the test labels. Although only Assumption
1
is needed for the validity of OptCS, following
Jin and Candès (2023b)
we typically use scores of the form
v(X_{i},Y_{i})=M\cdot\ind\{Y_{i}>c_{i}\}-f(X_{i})
, where
M
is a large constant and
f
is a point predictor.
The score implicitly depends on the thresholds, i.e.,
v(x,y,c)
, yet we suppress the dependence on
c
for convenience.

2.2
Challenges in optimizing conformity score with data reuse

We take a moment to review the theoretical underpinning of SCS and discuss the challenges in preserving FDR control while permitting data-driven score construction. At a high level, the FDR control of SCS relies on two facts:

(i)

Valid p-values
. Should
\widehat{V}_{n+j}
be replaced by
v(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
, the p-value would follow a super-uniform distribution due to exchangeability. Thus, a small value of
p_{j}
indicates evidence for
\widehat{V}_{n+j}<v(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
and hence
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
due to monotonicity.

(ii)

Favorable p-value dependence
. While the conformal p-values are dependent since they use the same set of calibration data, their positive dependence
(
Bates et al., 2023
;
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
ensures FDR control with the BH procedure.

Ensuring the independence between the score function
v
and the data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}
it applies to is essential for both facts above.
However, this requirement prevents two natural forms of data reuse in score construction: selecting a score with strong downstream selection performance from a pre-specified finite candidate class, and training a score using calibration data involved in p-value construction.

We illustrate the challenge via a naive attempt at the first form, model selection.
Consider
\{v{(\cdot,\cdot;k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
where each
v(\cdot,\cdot;k)\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is a pre-specified conformity score function. A natural idea is to choose the score that leads to the largest SCS set, i.e.,

\widehat{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{k\in[K]}|{\mathcal{S}}{(k)}|,

where
{\mathcal{S}}{(k)}
is the SCS output with the score
v{(\cdot\,,\cdot\,;k)}
.
The final selection set is

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}{(\widehat{k})}
, which is equivalent to applying BH to p-values

p_{j}=\frac{\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{v(X_{i},Y_{i};\widehat{k})\leq v(X_{n+j},c_{n+j};\widehat{k})\}+1}{n_{2}+1}.

Here,
\widehat{k}
depends on
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{calib}}
and
(X_{n+j},c_{n+j})
non-symmetrically. Thus,
v(X_{i},Y_{i};\widehat{k})
and
v(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j};\widehat{k})
are no longer exchangeable, yielding invalid p-values. It is also hard to justify the positive dependence, since
\widehat{k}
is a complex function of the calibration and test data. Indeed, we observe drastic violation of FDR using such a “greedy” approach in experiments.

Analogously, for full-data training, involving a calibration sample in training the function
v
can easily break the exchangeability between
v(X_{i},Y_{i})
and
v(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j})
.
In general, involving the calibration and test data in score construction introduces substantial data reuse which invalidates the p-values and complicates FDR control.
To address these challenges, we (i) construct valid p-values under data reuse, and (ii) develop multiple testing with FDR control despite intricate p-value dependence.

2.3
Related work

Conformal selection and model optimization.

This work builds upon the conformal selection framework
(
Jin and Candès, 2023b
)
, motivated by the need for model optimization in applications such as drug discovery
(
Bai et al., 2025
)
and language models
(
Gui et al., 2024
)
.
More broadly, it adds to the literature on model selection and optimization in conformal inference, which covers (i) prediction set construction for a single test point and (ii) selecting multiple test points. In (i), recent work primarily performs data-driven score optimization in a separate fold
with
data splitting
(
Stutz et al., 2022
;
Yang and Kuchibhotla, 2024
;
Huang et al., 2023a
;
Xie et al., 2024
)
.
Strategies in conformal prediction to avoid sample splitting, such as full conformal prediction and
Liang et al. (2024a)
, often rely on maintaining certain permutation invariance, which is connected to the key ideas of OptCS.
Works for (ii) are limited to outlier and novelty detection
(
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Marandon et al., 2024
;
Liang et al., 2024b
;
Lee et al., 2025
)
, which we discuss below.

Multiple testing with dependent p-values.

Our method calibrates individual selection thresholds to control the FDR under complicated dependence. This strategy builds upon the conditional calibration method of
Fithian and Lei (2022)
, which tests deterministic hypotheses with dependent p-values by finding an individual threshold for each p-value, typically via Monte Carlo, conditional on a sufficient statistic whose conditional distribution is known.
In OptCS, the unordered set of the calibration data and the
j
-th test point serves as the conditioning statistic; since our p-values are valid conditional on it, the individual thresholds (Section
3.1
) apply exactly without Monte Carlo approximation. Our setting also differs in that the hypotheses are random, and we additionally introduce homogeneous and deterministic pruning; see Remark
3.3
.
A similar strategy is used in
Jin and Candès (2023a)
to address the dependence among weighted conformal p-values under covariate shift, which relies on a conditional independence and permutation invariance structure distinct from ours; here, the thresholds and p-values must be carefully designed to accommodate the dependence induced by data reuse and model selection.

Data reuse in outlier and novelty detection.

The idea of reusing data for both model fitting and p-value construction while maintaining validity connects to several recent works on outlier detection.
AutoMS
(
Zhang et al., 2022
)
selects among candidate novelty detectors the one yielding the most discoveries, using leave-one-out Jackknife scores, which is conceptually related to OptCS-MSel and OptCS-Full-MSel; its validity relies on learning stability and yields a high-probability bound on the FDP and asymptotic FDR control, whereas OptCS controls the FDR in finite samples.
AdaDetect
(
Marandon et al., 2024
)
sets aside a fold of the (inlier) calibration data as a “null” class and trains outlier detectors by contrasting it with the remaining calibration and test data, ensuring validity through permutation invariance across the latter. Its training step is close to OptCS-Full in that both involve test samples in training, but OptCS-Full uses all calibration data for training and instead imputes the unknown test labels as nulls without compromising validity. Moreover, evaluating the performance of each candidate model makes the roles of calibration and test data intrinsically asymmetric, so OptCS only requires permutation invariance among the calibration data and the
j
-th test point. That said, the PRDS analysis of AdaDetect can be adapted to an alternative proof of FDR control for OptCS-Full (Appendix
A.3
).

Liang et al. (2024b)
use labeled outliers to train and select one-class classifiers in a way that is permutation invariant to the calibration data and each test point, and they also use conditional calibration for FDR control; their training strategies and integrated p-values are specialized to outlier detection, whereas OptCS accommodates general, possibly heterogeneous thresholds
c_{n+j}
and general score optimization, including full-sample training.
Concurrently,
Lee et al. (2025)
construct full-conformal e-values relative to a null reference sample for novelty detection and apply the e-BH procedure. In contrast, OptCS-Full uses both labeled positives and negatives to train a supervised score, and follows a p-value and conditional calibration route (see Appendix
A.1.3
).

Other related methods.

Gazin et al. (2025)
study the selection of informative conformal prediction sets, which involves selection followed by prediction set construction; their selective-conditional method constructs p-values with only the preselected calibration subset, whereas OptCS uses the entire calibration set in both training and calibration.
The recent ACS method
(
Gui et al., 2025
)
improves power by an interactive testing process that allows a subset of calibration data to enter training.
Numerical comparisons in Appendix
D.3
show that ACS and the outlier detection methods above can be less powerful when adapted to our problems.
A more detailed discussion of these connections is in Appendix
A.1
.

Remark 3.3

(Connection to

The pruning step extends the idea of conditional calibration

4.1
OptCS-MSel: Model selection over trained models

The first scenario we consider is when multiple pre-trained models have been obtained in a separate training stage, and a scientist aims to use data at hand to select a powerful model and produce an FDR-controlling selection set.

Such scenarios are common in modern machine learning tasks, such as drug discovery where pre-trained models for drug properties are available yet too costly to retrain, or computer vision and natural language processing where it is standard to employ powerful pre-trained foundation models. OptCS-MSel “approximates” the selection performance of all the model choices and selects the best while preserving validity.

Figure 4
:

4.2
OptCS-Full: Conformal selection without data splitting

In this part, we introduce OptCS-Full, a procedure that avoids sample splitting when model training with labeled data is needed. Setting aside the model selection issue, we consider a fixed model class: the prediction model will be trained through a given process and wrapped by a conformity score in a given way.
The goal is to improve power by training a more accurate model with all labeled data.

The training process is an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that inputs a set of labeled data and outputs a trained model, e.g.,

\mathcal{A}\colon\cup_{N\geq 0}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N}\to\{\textrm{measurable functions}~\widehat{\mu}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\}.

We require that
\mathcal{A}

Remark 4.4

(Design rationale)
.

Given the

The main intuition for power improvement with OptCS-Full is that the model

We apply OptCS to two representative applications: drug discovery (Section

6.1
Drug discovery with model selection

We first apply OptCS to drug discovery tasks for selecting drug candidates with favorable biological properties. In this task, controlling the FDR of the selection set ensures that subsequent investigations, such as wet-lab validation of their properties, are resource-efficient.

Concretely, in this problem, each sample is a drug candidate (such as a small molecule, an antibiotic, or a protein), whose physical and chemical features are encoded in
X\in\mathcal{X}
, and we are interested in a certain biological property
Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. While the ground-truth knowledge of
Y
typically needs to be evaluated via processes such as high-throughput screening (HTS) or wet-lab experiments
(
Lloyd,
;
Macarron et al., 2011
)
, machine learning models are increasingly used to predict the properties to identify potentially viable candidates before such costly evaluations
(
Huang, 2007
)
.
Given test drugs
\{X_{n+j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, we aim to select promising ones with
Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}
for pre-determined thresholds
c_{n+j}
while controlling the FDR.

A.1
Detailed related works

A.1.1
Connection to conditional calibration

A key technique for maintaining FDR control in our procedures is to use an individual threshold
s_{j}=q\widehat{R}_{j}/m
for each test p-value to prune the final selection set.
This is largely inspired by the conditional calibration method of
Fithian and Lei [2022]
, whose technique is also extended in
Jin and Candès [2023a]
to handle unknown p-value dependency in conformal selection under covariate shift. We discuss the connections and distinctions here.

Fithian and Lei [2022]
study the classical multiple testing setting where
m
deterministic hypotheses
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are tested with dependent p-values
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
. To achieve FDR control, they assume there exist sufficient statistics
\{S_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
such that for each
j
, the distribution of data conditional on
S_{j}
is known. They then use Monte Carlo to find individual thresholds
\widehat{\tau}_{j}
and estimator
\widehat{R}_{j}
of the BH selection set, such that
\mathbb{E}[\ind\{p_{j}\leq\widehat{\tau}_{j}\}/\widehat{R}_{j}]\leq q/m
. Finally, they use the same pruning method as ours with heterogeneous
\{\xi_{j}\}
with
\widehat{\tau}_{j}
in the place of our
s_{j}
.
Our method can be viewed as a generalization of this approach, where
\widehat{R}_{j}
is chosen as a permutation invariant function of the calibration and the
j
-th test data, and we take
\widehat{\tau}_{j}=q\widehat{R}_{j}/m
without resorting to Monte Carlo simulation. This is because we leverage the conditional validity of the p-value to theoretically ensure that
\mathbb{E}[\ind\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m\}/\widehat{R}_{j}]\leq q/m
(omitting the dependence on the null event here for the clarity of comparison). As such, the problem structure leveraged in our procedures is quite different from theirs.
Apart from this key distinction, we also differ in terms of the hypothesis testing setting (since we address random hypotheses) and pruning methods (since we also introduce deterministic and homogeneous pruning).

Such a generalization is also used in
Jin and Candès [2023a]
who calibrates
s_{j}=q\widehat{R}_{j}/m
to address dependent weighted conformal p-values under covariate shift. However, the strategy there relies on a distinct conditional independence and permutation invariance structure than ours. Our setting requires delicate design of
\widehat{R}_{j}
and
p_{j}
to accommodate complicated dependence due to data re-use and model selection.

A.1.2
Connection to adaptive outlier detection

The general ideas of reusing data while maintaining inference validity for outlier detection are connected to several recent works, which we discuss in more detail here.

Zhang et al. [2022]
propose AutoMS for automatic model selection in novelty detection with FDR control. Given a reference sample of inliers, AutoMS uses leave-one-out Jackknife scores to estimate the null score distribution for each candidate detector, constructs an FDR-based detection threshold, and selects the detector yielding the largest number of discoveries. This is conceptually related to OptCS-MSel and OptCS-Full-MSel, which likewise select among candidate scores according to downstream selection performance while allowing substantial data reuse. However, the validity theory of AutoMS relies on learning stability and uniform convergence of the Jackknife estimate, yielding a non-asymptotic high-probability bound on the FDP and asymptotic FDR control under density, stability, and rate conditions.

Marandon et al. [2024]
proposes AdaDetect which uses calibration and test data for better outlier detection model training. In the context of outlier detection where the calibration data consists of inliers and the test data is a mixture of inliers and outliers with unknown distributions, AdaDetect splits a set of calibration data as a “null” class to create a two-class structure between this subfold and the test data for model training, while using the remaining calibration data for both model training and conformal p-value construction.
First, their training step is close to our OptCS-Full variant in that both include test samples for model training. However, since the problem settings differ, the strategies are quite distinct: we do not need to set aside a subset of calibration data and can use all of them in model training; we need to impute the unknown test labels without compromising validity, while the data are fully observed in outlier detection.
Second, apart from OptCS-Full, the other two variants of OptCS also reuse calibration data for model choice and p-value construction. In their work, the validity is guaranteed by ensuring (global) permutation invariance across the entire set of (second fold of) calibration and test data. However, since we need to evaluate the performance of each model, the role of test and calibration data is intrinsically asymmetric, and it is difficult to maintain global permutation invariance. In contrast, we only require certain permutation invariance among the calibration data and the
j
-th test point, thereby allowing the use of flexible model selection and training processes. As such, our multiple testing step and theoretical analysis differ significantly from theirs.
Finally, we remark that the alternative proof of FDR control for OptCS-Full (Appendix
A.3
) relies on a similar PRDS property of the conformal p-values here, similar to AdaDetect.

Similar ideas of data splitting are discussed in
Gazin et al. [2025]
in the context of selecting informative conformal prediction sets, which involves both selection and prediction set construction afterwards.
Since the problems addressed are different, our procedures differ naturally.
Although their InfoSP procedure recovers (split) conformal selection in the selection step, their selective-conditional method (InfoSCOP) diverges significantly from OptCS. In addition, in the construction of p-values, InfoSCOP only uses the preselected calibration subset, whereas all of our procedures can leverage the entire calibration dataset (so all of them are used both in training and calibration).

Finally,
Liang et al. [2024b]
also study model optimization and selection in outlier detection. Their method improves upon one-class classifiers as conformity scores in conformal outlier detection by leveraging labeled outliers. Their main method splits the inliers and outliers into two folds; the first training fold is used to train the models, and related to our methods, they use median difference to select the models in a way that is permutation invariant to the calibration data and each test point. The second calibration fold is used to construct two preliminary p-values, which are integrated into a single p-value. In a similar spirit to ours, they use a conditional calibration strategy to ensure FDR control.
Our work differs mainly in scope and in the type of data-dependent optimization being enabled. OptCS is developed for conformal selection problems with potentially heterogeneous thresholds
c_{n+j}

and general score/model optimization steps (including full-sample training and/or model selection) given any score function, whereas the training strategies, such as the proposal of integrated p-values, are highly specialized to outlier detection settings.
That said, the two works are conceptually related in the shared motivation of improving power by model selection with data reuse. Beyond sample splitting,
Liang et al. [2024b]
discuss a more expensive transductive/CV
+
-type variant to reduce the loss and instability introduced by
sample splitting, at the cost of additional model fits (which also renders conditional calibration prohibitively expensive). In our work, Appendix
A.4
discusses a more
computationally intensive variant of OptCS-Full similar in spirit; it is designed to mitigate the effect of training with many
imputed null test samples (our OptCS-Full and OptCS-Full-MSel reduce computation costs by simultaneous null imputation of all test points). Both developments illustrate that
pursuing stronger data reuse often requires leave-out-style constructions with additional
computation. In general, our problem setting, specific training and model selection strategies, and the technical framework (with general permutation-based conditions) are sharply distinct.

A.1.3
Connection to full-conformal novelty detection

We discuss the connection between OptCS and the full-conformal novelty detection
(FCND) framework of
Lee et al. [2025]
. FCND is an independent and concurrent work that
develops full-conformal e-values for novelty detection relative to a null/inlier reference
distribution, and then applies e-BH-type procedures for FDR control.
If the binary reduction is applied, FCND becomes a close e-value-based comparator (similar to AdaDetect and IC methods). However,
the connection is limited to the binarized full-data-training branch of OptCS; the general OptCS
framework also covers model/score selection and score constructions based on regression,
quantile, or clipped scores without reducing to binary
classification.

One key difference between OptCS and FCND is in data usage.
FCND constructs full-conformal e-values relative to a
null/inlier reference sample
. In contrast, OptCS-Full remains in the supervised
setting: it uses both labeled positives and labeled negatives to train a supervised score, and
imputes test labels as null to preserve validity. Thus, the direct FCND adaptation uses labeled
nulls as a reference sample, while OptCS-Full exploits labeled positive information at the
cost of possible signal dilution from null-imputed test samples.

The two methods also use different validity architectures. FCND follows an e-value/e-BH
route, whereas OptCS follows a p-value/conditional-calibration route: the score-generating
map
\mathcal{V}
produces valid data-dependent conformal p-values, and the auxiliary map

\mathcal{R}
calibrates the dependent p-values for FDR control. The default
K=1
FCND
construction and the non-LOO version of OptCS-Full are structurally analogous one-fit
procedures; both can be made more robust through LOO-style constructions at additional
computational cost. Hence, the similarities reflect a common computation–power tradeoff
in full-data conformal methods, while the data usage and evidence objects remain different.
Targeted numerical comparisons with the official
fcnd
implementation are reported
in Appendix
D.3.3
.

A.2
Design of auxiliary selection function

In this part, we formalize the discussion in Remark
3.6
. For any p-values
\{p_{j}\}
and auxiliary selection sizes
\{\widehat{R}_{j}\}
, define the selection set with heterogeneous, homogeneous, and deterministic pruning as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{hete}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq s_{j},~\xi_{j}\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r_{\textnormal{hete}}^{*}\big\},\quad

\displaystyle r_{\textnormal{hete}}^{*}:=\max\Big\{r\colon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},\,\xi_{j}\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r\}}\geq r\Big\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{homo}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq s_{j},~\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r_{\textnormal{homo}}^{*}\big\},\quad

\displaystyle r_{\textnormal{homo}}^{*}:=\max\Big\{r\colon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r\}}\geq r\Big\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{dtm}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq s_{j},~\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r_{\textnormal{dtm}}^{*}\big\},\quad

\displaystyle r_{\textnormal{dtm}}^{*}:=\max\Big\{r\colon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},\,\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r\}}\geq r\Big\}.

Here,
s_{j}=q\widehat{R}_{j}/m
, and
\xi_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\textnormal{Unif}([0,1])
and
\xi\sim\textnormal{Unif}([0,1])
are all independent of the data.
Also, define the output of the BH procedure applied to
\{p_{j}\}
at level
q\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{BH}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq qr_{\textnormal{BH}}^{*}/m\big\},\quad

\displaystyle r_{\textnormal{BH}}^{*}:=\max\Big\{r\colon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq qr/m\}}\geq r\Big\}.

The following proposition formalizes Remark
3.6
.

Proposition A.1
.

For any values of p-values
\{p_{j}\}
and any values
\{\widehat{R}_{j}\}
, the following statements hold:

(i)

{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{dtm}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{BH}}
.

(ii)

If
\widehat{R}_{j}\equiv|{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{BH}}|
, then
{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{hete}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{homo}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{dtm}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{BH}}
.

(iii)

{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{dtm}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{hete}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{dtm}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{homo}}
.

A.1
.

Fix any values

\{p_{j}\}
,
\{\widehat{R}_{j}\}
, and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{1}(r;\vec{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\{j\in[m]\colon p_{j}\leq s_{j},\lambda_{j}\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r\}

\displaystyle=\Big\{j\in[m]\colon p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m~\text{and}~\lambda_{j}\cdot q\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq qr/m\Big\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{2}(r)=\{j\in[m]\colon p_{j}\leq qr/m\}.

For any fixed

Define

A.3
An alternative proof for OptCS-Full

We provide an alternative proof for the validity of OptCS-Full variant under slightly stronger assumptions, based on the PRDS property of conformal p-values
[
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
,
Bates et al., 2023
,
Jin and Candès, 2023b
]
.
All distributional assumptions below are
understood conditionally on the preparatory data
Z_{1:n_{1}}
,
if any. This property is characterized by the following result:

Proposition A.2
.

Assume that the calibration and test data
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n+m}
are i.i.d., and the data in
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\cup\{Z_{n+j}\}
are mutually independent for any
j\in[m]
.
Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
be the conformal p-values defined in (
4
), with the
OptCS-Full
scores defined in (
9
). For each fixed
j
, let
\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{*,j}
be the
oracle LOO model obtained from
\mathbf{D}_{-i}
by replacing
\widehat{Z}_{n+j}^{T}
with
Z_{n+j}^{T}
in the augmented training sample before leaving out
observation
i
. Define the oracle scores

V_{i}^{*,j}=\begin{cases}v(Z_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{*,j}),&i\in\{n_{1}+1,\ldots,n,n+j\},\\
v(\widehat{Z}_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{*,j}),&i\in\{n+1,\ldots,n+m\}\setminus\{n+j\}.\end{cases}

Assume these oracle scores have no ties almost surely for every
j\in[m]
. Define the oracle conformal p-values

\displaystyle p_{\ell}^{*,j}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}^{*,j}\leq V_{n+\ell}^{*,j}\}}{n_{2}+1},\qquad\ell\in[m],

(13)

and write
p_{j}^{*}=p_{j}^{*,j}
.
Then, conditionally on the preparatory data,
the vector
(p_{1}^{*,j},\dots,p_{m}^{*,j})
is PRDS on
p_{j}^{*}
, and
p_{j}^{*}
is super-uniform, for every
j\in[m]
.

Assuming the above proposition, the validity of OptCS-Full
can be directly established following the technique of
Theorem 2.3 in
Jin and Candès [2023b]
, since the output
of the pruning step is identical to that of directly
running BH on the p-values by Proposition
A.1
.
Specifically, denote by
{\mathcal{S}}
the OptCS-Full output and
by
{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}
the BH selection set obtained from

(p_{1}^{*,j},\dots,p_{m}^{*,j})
.
On
\{Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}
, null compatibility gives

Z_{n+j}^{T}=\widehat{Z}_{n+j}^{T}
, so all observed and
oracle fitted models coincide. By the clipped-score
property, the entire score vectors also coincide.
Consequently,

(p_{1}^{*,j},\dots,p_{m}^{*,j})=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m}),\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}={\mathcal{S}}\quad\text{on }\{Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}.

It follows that

FDR

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\ind\{p_{j}\leq q|{\mathcal{S}}|/m,\,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{|{\mathcal{S}}|\vee 1}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq q|{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}|/m,\,Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{|{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}|\vee 1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\ind\{p_{j}^{*}\leq q|{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}|/m\}}{|{\mathcal{S}}_{j\to*}|\vee 1}\bigg]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{q}{m}=q.

The last inequality follows from the standard
BH bound under PRDS and super-uniformity
at coordinate
j
, applied conditionally on the
preparatory data and then averaged.

We now return to the proof of Proposition
A.2
, which is based on Theorem 3.3 of
Marandon et al. [2024]
.

Marandon et al. [2024]
considers an outlier detection setting where the calibration data are identically distributed from some distribution
\mathbb{P}
, and the goal is to identify test data drawn from different distributions. The score-exchangeability argument in that theorem yields the following coordinatewise result, with the score direction reversed to match our notation:

Lemma A.3
.

Let
W_{n_{1}+1},\dots,W_{n}\sim\mathbb{P}
be the
calibration data and
W_{n+1},\dots,W_{n+m}

be the test samples. For each
j\in[m]
, the null hypothesis is
H_{j}^{*}:W_{n+j}\sim\mathbb{P}
.
We denote the sets of inliers and outliers by

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{j:W_{n+j}\sim\mathbb{P}\}
and

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\{j:W_{n+j}\not\sim\mathbb{P}\}
, respectively.
Given any mechanism generating scores

\{V_{i}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n+m}
with no ties almost surely,
construct

\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{*}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}\leq V_{n+\ell}\}}{n_{2}+1},\qquad\ell\in[m].

If, for a given
j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
,

\{V_{i}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\cup\{V_{n+j}\}

is exchangeable conditionally on

\{V_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}
, then

(\widetilde{p}_{1}^{*},\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}^{*})
is PRDS on

\widetilde{p}_{j}^{*}
, and
\widetilde{p}_{j}^{*}
is super-uniform.
In particular, if this condition holds for every

j\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the vector is PRDS on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Lemma
A.3
allows any construction
of the scores satisfying the stated conditional
exchangeability and no-ties conditions.
We note that this is the case for the oracle OptCS-Full
scores. Fix
j\in[m]
and instantiate the construction
with

W_{i}=Z_{i}\quad(i=n_{1}+1,\dots,n),\qquad W_{n+j}=Z_{n+j},\qquad W_{n+\ell}=\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\quad(\ell\neq j).

Here,
j
is an inlier coordinate; no restriction on the
distributions of the remaining test observations is
needed for the coordinatewise conclusion.

By the assumptions, the calibration observations and

W_{n+j}
are exchangeable conditionally on the
remaining inputs. The fixed observationwise
transformation
T
commutes with permutations, and
the symmetry of
\mathcal{A}
makes the corresponding LOO
scores permutation equivariant. Moreover, permuting
the calibration observations and
W_{n+j}
leaves
the scores at all other test indices unchanged.
Thus,
\{V_{i}^{*,j}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\cup\{V_{n+j}^{*,j}\}
is exchangeable conditionally on
\{V_{n+\ell}^{*,j}\}_{\ell\neq j}
.
Applying Lemma
A.3
, we obtain

(\widetilde{p}_{1}^{*},\dots,\widetilde{p}_{m}^{*})=(p_{1}^{*,j},\dots,p_{m}^{*,j})
,
which is PRDS on

\widetilde{p}_{j}^{*}=p_{j}^{*,j}=p_{j}^{*}
, with
p_{j}^{*}

super-uniform. This concludes the proof of
Proposition
A.2
.

A.4
A second variant of OptCS-Full(-MSel)

In this part, we present two additional variants of OptCS that avoid using too many imputed null samples in the leave-one-out training idea of OptCS-Full and OptCS-Full-MSel. We call these variants OptCS-Full_sep and OptCS-Full-MSel_sep.

Method with fixed model class.

We begin by the setting of Section
4.2
with a fixed model class. We assume a training process
\mathcal{A}
, and write the conformity score function via

v(x,y{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu})\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
,
where the functional dependence on a trained model is encapsulated in
\widehat{\mu}
representing a general model.
We introduce the procedure of OptCS-Full_sep by specifying the two subroutines in Lines
3
and
5
in Algorithm
1
.
First, for each
j\in[m]
, we define the leave-one-out training set (again without splitting the labeled data)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j)}=\{Z_{i}^{T}\}_{i=1}^{n}\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+j}^{T}\}\backslash\{\widetilde{Z}^{T}_{\ell}\},

where
\widetilde{Z}^{T}_{\ell}=Z^{T}_{\ell}
if
\ell\leq n
and
\widetilde{Z}^{T}_{\ell}=\widehat{Z}^{T}_{n+j}
if
\ell=n+j
.
Then, for each
\ell\in[n]\cup\{n+j\}
, we train a model

\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{(j)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j)})

using the leave-one-out data. Note that each
\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j)}
contains at most one imputed null data point.
We then specify the score generating functional in Line
3
of Algorithm
1
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{(j)}=(V_{n_{1}+1}^{(j)},\dots,V_{n}^{(j)},\widehat{V}_{n+1}^{(j)},\dots,\widehat{V}_{n+m}^{(j)}),

\displaystyle\textrm{where}\quad V_{i}^{(j)}=v(X_{i},Y_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{(j)}),\quad\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}^{(j)}=v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{n+j}^{(j)}).

In our experiments, we find that running the BH procedure using p-values (
4
) based on the above scores always controls the empirical FDR. Therefore, we recommend using such a relaxed procedure for practical use.
However, if one aims for rigorous theoretical control of FDR in finite samples, we specify the auxiliary selection function
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
in Line
5
of Algorithm
1
as follows. We define the auxiliary p-values

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}=\frac{\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}^{(j)}\leq v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{(j)})\}+\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}^{(j)}\leq v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{n+j}^{(j)})\}}{n_{2}+1},

and set
\widehat{R}_{j}
as the size of the BH procedure output applied to
\{\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}\}_{\ell\neq j}\cup\{0\}
. Similar to the proof ideas of Proposition
4.5
, we can show that these definitions obey the properties of
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
required for finite-sample FDR control in Theorem
3.4
.

Method with model selection.

The variant with model selection follows a similar idea as above. Following Section
4.3
, we consider
K
training processes
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
K
conformity scores whose dependence on a trained model is represented by

v(\cdot,\cdot{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{k},k)
.
With the same idea as before, we obtain the leave-one-out model (using the
T_{k}
encoding)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\ell,k}^{(j)}=\mathcal{A}_{k}(\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j,k)}),\quad\ell\in[n]\cup\{n+j\}.

Then, we use ideas similar to OptCS-MSel to select an optimal model for the
j
-th test point in a permutation-invariant fashion. Specifically, we define the scores

V_{i,k}^{(j)}=v(X_{i},Y_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i,k}^{(j)},k)
for
i\in[n_{1}+1:n]
, and
\widehat{V}_{n+\ell,i,k}^{(j)}=v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i,k}^{(j)},k)
for
\ell\in[m]
and
i\in[n_{1}+1:n]\cup\{n+j\}
. We define the auxiliary p-values

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{\ell,k}^{(j)}=\frac{\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j,n+j,k}^{(j)}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell,n+j,k}^{(j)}\}+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i,k}^{(j)}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell,i,k}^{(j)}\}}{1+n_{2}},

and let
\widetilde{S}_{k}^{(j)}
be the selection set of the BH procedure applied to
\{0\}\cup\{\widetilde{p}_{\ell,k}^{(j)}\}_{\ell\neq j}
. The selected model index is then

\widehat{k}_{j}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{k\in[K]}|\widetilde{S}_{k}^{(j)}|
.
Finally, we
specify the score generating functional in Line
3
of Algorithm
1
via

\displaystyle V_{i}^{(j)}=v(X_{i},Y_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i,\widehat{k}_{j}}^{(j)},\widehat{k}_{j}),\quad\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}^{(j)}=v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{n+j,\widehat{k}_{j}}^{(j)},\widehat{k}_{j}),

and the auxiliary selection sizes are given by
\widehat{R}_{j}=|\widetilde{S}_{\widehat{k}_{j}}^{(j)}|
. Following similar ideas as before, one can show that the conditions needed in Theorem
3.4
are fulfilled by this construction, and applying OptCS calibration yields valid FDR control.

Intermediate solution:
K_{0}
-fold split.

The above two methods are suitable in settings where practitioners are willing to commit intensive computation efforts to fully exploit the labeled data. Finally, we discuss an intermediate solution based on
K_{0}
-fold splitting that sits between the all-test null imputation in the main text and the intensive variant here. For brevity, we introduce the method with a fixed model class, following the setup in Section
4.2
.

This variant still preserves all the labeled data in training, but reduces the number of imputed null test samples compared with OptCS-Full. Let
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be the number of folds (such as 5 or 10). We randomly split the
test
data into
K_{0}
folds,
I_{1},\dots,I_{K_{0}}\subseteq[m]
.
For each
j\in I_{k_{0}}
,
k_{0}\in[K_{0}]
, we define the leave-one-out datasets

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j)}=\{Z_{i}^{T}\}_{i=1}^{n}\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+j^{\prime}}^{T}\}_{j^{\prime}\in I_{k_{0}}}\backslash\{\widetilde{Z}_{\ell}^{T}\},\quad\ell\in\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n\}\cup\{n+j^{\prime}\}_{j^{\prime}\in I_{k_{0}}}.

The corresponding trained model is then

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\ell}^{(j)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(j)}),\quad\ell\in\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n\}\cup\{n+j^{\prime}\}_{j^{\prime}\in I_{k_{0}}}.

It incur
|I_{k_{0}}|+n_{2}
times of model fitting for each
k_{0}
, totaling
m+n_{2}\cdot K_{0}
times of model fitting.
We define the score-generating functional in Line
3
of Algorithm
1
via

\displaystyle V_{i}^{(j)}=v(X_{i},Y_{i}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{(j)}),\quad\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}^{(j)}=v(X_{n+\ell},c_{n+\ell}{\,|\,}\widehat{\mu}_{n+\ell}^{(j)}),\quad\ell\in I_{k_{0}}.

Note that
V_{i}^{(j)}=V_{i}^{(j^{\prime})}
and
\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}^{(j)}=\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}^{(j^{\prime})}
for any
j,j^{\prime}\in I_{k_{0}}
by definition.
We define the p-values for
j\in I_{k_{0}}
via

\displaystyle p_{j}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}^{(j)}\leq\widehat{V}_{n+j}^{(j)}\}}{n_{2}+1}.

It then suffices to set
\widehat{R}_{j}
as the size of the BH selection set applied to
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in I_{k_{0}}}
at level
q
. One can show that this construction satisfies the conditions needed for Theorem
3.4
(using exactly the same proof idea as that of OptCS-Full).

A.5
Conceptual justification of (binarized) null imputation

This appendix provides a theoretical intuition for why
all-test null imputation
in OptCS-Full
need not hurt
asymptotic
power, even when a non-negligible fraction of test points are truly non-null.
The key observation is that, under the standard setup of OptCS (i.e., calibration and test
covariates share the same marginal distribution), the optimal score under the “mixed” training
distribution induced by null imputation becomes a
global rescaling
of the optimal score under the
correct data distribution. Since the p-values depend on the
relative ordering

(ranks) of scores rather than their absolute values, such a rescaling does not affect the limiting behavior.
We emphasize that this argument is asymptotic and abstracts away finite-sample training error; the latter is
addressed empirically in Appendix
D.2
.

A.5.1
Setup and the induced mixed distribution

We present the argument under the binary reduction used in OptCS-Full.
Let
Y\in\{0,1\}
denote the reduced label, where
Y=1
corresponds to “non-null” (e.g.,
Y>c
in the original
problem) and
Y=0
corresponds to “null”. Let
P
denote the joint distribution of a labeled pair
(X,Y)
, and
write the true conditional probability

p(x):=P(Y=1\mid X=x).

OptCS assumes that calibration and test covariates share the same marginal distribution.

OptCS-Full (with all-test null imputation) trains its predictive model on the augmented dataset consisting of
(i) labeled samples
(X,Y)\sim P
and (ii) imputed test samples
(X,0)
whose covariates follow the same marginal

P_{X}
. At the population level, this corresponds to a
mixture
distribution
\widetilde{P}
over
(X,Y)
:

\widetilde{P}\;:=\;\frac{n}{n+m}\,P\;+\;\frac{m}{n+m}\,(P_{X}\otimes\delta_{0}),

(14)

where
n
is the number of labeled samples used for training and
m
is the number of test samples imputed as
null. (Under LOO fitting, leaving out one point only changes the mixture weights by
o(1)
and does not affect
the asymptotic discussion.)

A.5.2
Optimal score under
\widetilde{P}
is a global rescaling

According to
Jin and Candès [2023b]
, the “oracle” score under the true distribution
P
that leads to the most powerful selection is based on
p(x)=P(Y=1{\,|\,}X=x)
, namely, the clipped score
V(x,y)=My-p(x)
.
A direct calculation shows that under
\widetilde{P}
, the oracle score is a

global scaling
of
p(x)
.
Indeed, the
X
-marginal under
\widetilde{P}
is still
P_{X}
, since both components in
(
14
) share the same
X
-marginal.
Moreover, the joint mass of
(X,Y=1)
under
\widetilde{P}
only comes from the labeled component:

\widetilde{p}(x)\;:=\;\widetilde{P}(Y=1\mid X=x)\;=\;\frac{n}{n+m}\,P(Y=1\mid X=x)\;=\;\frac{n}{n+m}\,p(x).

(15)

Consequently, the oracle score under the mixed distribution is simply a constant multiple of
the oracle score under the correct labeled distribution.
In particular, the
ordering
of the score function is preserved.

A.5.3
Unchanged asymptotic behavior for conformal p-values

Conformal selection and the OptCS p-values depend on score comparisons of the form

\mathbb{I}\{V_{i}^{(j)}\leq V_{n+j}^{(j)}\}
(see (
4
) and the definitions in Section
3
).
Thus, the
rank
of the test score among calibration scores is the key object.

To make the order-preserving argument transparent, consider the clipped score commonly used for the binary
reduction
[
Jin and Candès, 2023b
]

v(x,y\mid\mu):=My-\mu(x),

with
M
chosen large enough so that
v(x,1\mid\mu)>v(x^{\prime},0\mid\mu)
for all
x,x^{\prime}
(this ensures that only
null calibration points contribute to the relevant comparisons).
For a null-labeled point (
y=0
), the score reduces to
v(x,0\mid\mu)=-\mu(x)
, so the ordering of scores among
null points is exactly the reverse ordering of
\mu(x)
.

Oracle-level statement.

If we could plug in the oracle score, then by (
15
),

v(x,0\mid\widetilde{p})=-\widetilde{p}(x)=-\frac{n}{n+m}p(x),

which differs from
-p(x)
by a positive constant factor. Therefore, the induced ranking (hence the resulting conformal p-values) is identical to that obtained from the oracle
score under
P
.
Concretely, the conformal p-value for test point
j
can be written as

p_{j}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\!\left\{v(X_{i},Y_{i}\mid\mu_{i})\leq v(X_{n+j},0\mid\mu_{n+j})\right\}}{1+n_{2}},

(16)

where
\mu_{i}
denotes the fitted model used for scoring point
i
(LOO fits in OptCS-Full).
If
\mu_{i}
were replaced by the
oracle
\widetilde{p}
(or
p
), the indicator event depends only on the ordering of
\widetilde{p}(X_{i})
and

\widetilde{p}(X_{n+j})
, hence equals the corresponding event with
p(X_{i})
and
p(X_{n+j})
. Thus, the oracle p-values coincide.

Plug-in statement.

Suppose the learning algorithm used in OptCS-Full is
consistent
for the mixed distribution in the sense
that, under LOO fitting,
\mu_{i}(\cdot)
converges (in an appropriate mode, e.g., in probability in
L_{2}(P_{X})
or
uniformly on compact sets) to
\widetilde{p}(\cdot)
.
Then the score
v(\cdot,0\mid\mu_{i})
converges to
v(\cdot,0\mid\widetilde{p})
. Since (
16
) depends only on ranks,
the resulting conformal p-values converge to the same limiting p-values as those obtained from the correct
labeled distribution. Consequently, with similar ideas as in the asymptotic analysis of, e.g., Appendix
B.6
, we can show the power converges to the asymptotic power.

A.5.4
Remarks and limitations

Other score mappings.

The argument above does not rely on the specific clipped score; any score construction in OptCS-Full that is
a fixed (strictly) monotone transform of a consistent estimate of
\widetilde{p}(x)
yields the same order
preservation and hence the same asymptotic implication.

Class-imbalance corrections.

Many standard imbalance corrections (e.g., constant class weights or random over/under-sampling) effectively
change class fractions while keeping in-class distributions unchanged. One can show that at the population level, the
resulting
oracle score
remains a monotone function of
p(x)
, and thus also preserves ordering; this is
why such corrections do not alter the conceptual asymptotic picture, while potentially improving finite-sample
training.

Finite-sample caveat.

While
\widetilde{p}(x)
preserves ordering, the learning problem can become harder when
m
is large due to
severe class imbalance and signal dilution, which may degrade finite-sample training quality. This motivates
our use of imbalance correction (e.g., over-sampling) and also motivates the per-test variant in Appendix
A.4
.
We provide targeted simulations and practical guidance for this computation–power tradeoff in Appendix
D.2
.

B.1
Proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Fix a
j\in[m]

Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}

\mathcal{V}

j

\widehat{Z}_{n+j}

\displaystyle\big(\bar{V}^{(j)}_{n_{1}:n},\bar{V}_{n+1:n+j-1}^{(j)},\bar{V}_{n+j}^{(j)},\bar{V}_{n+j+1:n+m}^{(j)}\big)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{V}^{(j)}(Z_{1:n_{1}},Z_{n_{1}+1:n},\widehat{Z}_{n+1:n+j-1},Z_{n+j},\widehat{Z}_{n+j+1:n+m}).

We will compare this new set of scores with (
3

Since
\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\bar{V}^{(j)}_{i}={V}^{(j)}_{i}\text{ for }i\leq n,\quad\bar{V}^{(j)}_{n+i}=\widehat{V}^{(j)}_{n+i}\text{ for }i\leq m,i\neq j,\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{V}^{(j)}_{n+j}\leq\widehat{V}^{(j)}_{n+j}.

We now define the oracle p-value as

\displaystyle p_{j}^{*}:=\frac{1+\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\ind\{\bar{V}_{i}^{(j)}\leq\bar{V}^{(j)}_{n+j}\}}{n_{2}+1}.

By definition,
p_{j}^{*}\leq p_{j}
on the null event that
Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}

p_{j}^{*}
by an exchangeability argument, and as a result,
p_{j}

Denote
\mathcal{Z}_{j}=(Z_{n_{1}+1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{n+j})

[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]=[Z_{n_{1}+1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{n+j}]

We will show that the set of scores

Technically, we are to show that for any values
(v_{n_{1}+1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+j})

\pi

\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n,n+j\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\bar{V}_{n_{1}+1}^{(j)}=v_{n_{1}+1},\dots,\bar{V}_{n}^{(j)}=v_{n},\bar{V}_{n+j}^{(j)}=v_{n+j}\,\Big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bar{V}_{n_{1}+1}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n_{1}+1)},\dots,\bar{V}_{n}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n)},\bar{V}_{n+j}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n+j)}\,\Big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\Big),

(17)

[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]

[z]=[z_{n_{1}+1},\dots,z_{n},z_{n+j}]

\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}

\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{-j}

\{[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]=[z],~\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}=\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{-j}\}

The permutation equivariance of
\mathcal{V}
(Definition
4

[\{\bar{V}_{i}^{(j)}\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n}\cup\{\bar{V}^{(j)}_{n+j}\}]

[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]
and
\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}

\displaystyle(v_{n_{1}+1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+j})=\mathcal{V}^{(j)}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\widehat{z}_{n+1:n+j-1},z_{n+j},\widehat{z}_{n+j+1:n+m}).

(v_{n_{1}+1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+j})

[z_{1},\dots,z_{n},z_{n+j}]

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{n+j}

Since
(Z_{n_{1}+1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{n+j})

\pi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(Z_{n_{1}+1}=z_{\pi^{\prime}(n_{1}+1)},\dots,Z_{n}=z_{\pi^{\prime}(n)},Z_{n+j}=z_{\pi^{\prime}(n+j)}\,\Big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]=[z],~\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}=\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{-j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+n_{2})!}.

For any permutation
\pi
of
\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n,n+j\}

4

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\bar{V}_{n_{1}+1}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n_{1}+1)},\dots,\bar{V}_{n}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n)},\bar{V}_{n+j}^{(j)}=v_{\pi(n+j)}\,\Big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(Z_{n_{1}+1}=z_{\pi(n_{1}+1)},\dots,Z_{n}=z_{\pi(n)},Z_{n+j}=z_{\pi(n+j)}\,\Big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\Big)=\frac{1}{(1+n_{2})!}.

This proves (
17

\displaystyle p_{j}^{*}\,\big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\quad\sim\quad\textrm{Unif}\big(\{1/(n_{2}+1),\dots,n_{2}/(n_{2}+1),1\}\big).

In other words, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(p_{j}^{*}\leq t\,\big|\,[\mathcal{Z}_{j}]\cup\{\widehat{Z}_{n+\ell}\}_{\ell\neq j}\big)\leq t.

B.5
Proof of Lemma
3.5

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

We prove the result for three pruning options separately.

Case 1: Heterogeneous pruning
. By definition, we have

FDR

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in{\mathcal{S}},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|{\mathcal{S}}|}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}\ind\{\xi_{j}\leq|{\mathcal{S}}|/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{1\vee|{\mathcal{S}}|}\Bigg]

where the second inequality relies on that
r^{*}=|{\mathcal{S}}|
, a property that follows from the proof of Proposition
A.1
. Fix any
j\in[m]
. Once
j\in{\mathcal{S}}
, the rejection set of our pruning method will not change if
\xi_{j}
is sent to 0 while keeping other
\xi
’s unchanged, by the step-up nature of the pruning process. Denoting
{\mathcal{S}}_{\xi_{j}\rightarrow 0}
as the rejection set obtained by replacing
\xi_{j}
by 0 in the pruning step, we have

FDR

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|=k\}}{k}\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}\ind\{\xi_{j}\leq k/\widehat{R}_{j}\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}_{\xi_{j}\rightarrow 0}|=k\}}{k}\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}\ind\{\xi_{j}\leq k/\widehat{R}_{j}\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}_{\xi_{j}\rightarrow 0}|=k\}}{\widehat{R}_{j}}\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{\widehat{R}_{j}}\Bigg]

where the second equality follows from the independence between
\xi_{j}
and
({\mathcal{S}}_{\xi_{j}\rightarrow 0},p_{j},\widehat{R}_{j})
. This shows the heterogeneous case.

Case 2: Homogeneous pruning
. In this case, we use a conditional PRDS property of
\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
which we will specify later. Let
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
be the conditional expectation given all the data, so the only randomness lies in
\xi
. By the independence of
\xi
, we would still have
\xi\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
after conditioning. We define the conditional FDR:

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in{\mathcal{S}},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|{\mathcal{S}}|}\Bigg]

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
is with respect to the randomness in
\xi
conditional on
\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{label}}\cup\{(X_{n+j},Y_{n+j},c_{n+j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
. Note that
\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j}\}
are deterministic conditional on the data. We denote
{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}:=\{j:p_{j}\leq s_{j}\}
as the first-step rejection set, and
\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\{j:Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}
as the set of null hypotheses, both being deterministic given the data. Then, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|=k\}\frac{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}}\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\}}{k}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\frac{\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\}}{k}(\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\}-\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k-1\})\Bigg].

(19)

For any
j\in[m]
, we define
\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D},j)
as the summand in (
19
).
Since
|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq|{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}|
deterministically, we know

\displaystyle\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\}(\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\}-\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k-1\})=0

for any
k>|{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}|=:m_{1}
. The case
m_{1}=0
is trivial, so assume
m_{1}\geq 1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D},j)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{k}\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\}\cdot(\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\}-\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k-1\})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min\{1,m_{1}/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{m_{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\}\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\frac{1}{k+1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\ind\{\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k+1\}\ind\{|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min\{1,m_{1}/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{m_{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\Bigg\{\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k)}{k}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k+1)}{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k+1\Big)\Bigg\}.

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
is with respect to the conditional distribution of
\xi
given
\mathcal{D}
. Now, we would rely on the
positive regression dependence on a subset
(PRDS) property
[
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
]
of
\xi\widehat{R}_{j}
as follows to control
\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D},j)
. For completeness, we include the definition of PRDS below. Lemma
B.1
is the same as Lemma C.2 of
Jin and Candès [2023a]
, hence we omit the proof here.

Definition 6

(PRDS)
.

A random vector
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
is PRDS on a subset
\mathcal{I}
if for any
i\in\mathcal{I}
and any increasing set
D
,
the probability
\mathbb{P}(X\in D{\,|\,}X_{i}=x)
is increasing in
x
.

Lemma B.1

(Lemma C.2 of
Jin and Candès [2023a]
)
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
be nonnegative fixed constants, and let
\xi\sim\textnormal{Unif}([0,1])
. Then, the random variables
\{a_{1}\xi,\dots,a_{j-1}\xi,a_{j+1}\xi,\dots,a_{k}\xi\}
are PRDS in
a_{j}\xi
for any
j\in[k]
.

By Lemma
B.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k+1\Big).

Since
\xi
is independent from
\widehat{R}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D},j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min\{1,m_{1}/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{m_{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\bigg\{\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k)}{k}-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k+1)}{k+1}\bigg\}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\min\{1,m_{1}/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{m_{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\bigg\{\frac{\min\{1,k/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{k}-\frac{\min\{1,(k+1)/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{k+1}\bigg\}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big(|{\mathcal{S}}|\leq k\,\Big|\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq k\Big),

Since
\frac{\min\{1,k/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{k}=\min\{1/k,1/\widehat{R}_{j}\}
is non-increasing in
k
, the coefficients in the last summation is non-negative. Bounding the conditional probabilities by
1
gives

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D},j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min\{1,m_{1}/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{m_{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}\left\{\frac{\min\{1,k/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{k}-\frac{\min\{1,(k+1)/\widehat{R}_{j}\}}{k+1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\{1,1/\widehat{R}_{j}\}\leq\frac{1}{\widehat{R}_{j}}.

Putting above bounds together, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}(\mathcal{D})\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)}\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}}\frac{1}{\widehat{R}_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{\widehat{R}_{j}}.

Finally, marginalizing over the randomness in
\mathcal{D}
concludes the proof of the homogeneous case.

Case 3: Deterministic pruning
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=\{j:p_{j}\leq s_{j},\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r^{*}\}\quad\text{and}\quad r^{*}=|{\mathcal{S}}|.

by Proposition
A.1
. Therefore, for any
j\in{\mathcal{S}}
, we have
\widehat{R}_{j}\leq|{\mathcal{S}}|
, and

\displaystyle\textnormal{FDR}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in{\mathcal{S}},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{1\vee|{\mathcal{S}}|}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{j\in{\mathcal{S}},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{\widehat{R}_{j}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq s_{j},Y_{n+j}\leq c_{n+j}\}}{\widehat{R}_{j}}\Bigg].

This concludes the proof of the deterministic case and hence also the proof of Lemma
3.5
.
∎

B.6
Proof of Proposition

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

For each
k\in[K]
and
\ell\in[m]
, we define

\displaystyle p_{\ell}^{*}(k)=F(\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}(k);k),

where
F(s;k)=\mathbb{P}(v(X_{i},Y_{i};k)\leq s)
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in[K]
, and recall that

\displaystyle G(t;k)=\mathbb{P}\Big(F(v(X_{i},c_{i};k);k)\leq t\Big),\quad t\in[0,1].

We first characterize the selection performance of SCS with each conformity score function. By definition and using the stopping-time interpretation of the BH procedure in
Storey et al. [2004]
, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{k,\textnormal{SCS}}=\{j\in[m]\colon p_{j}(k)\leq\widehat{\tau}(k)\}
, where (since
n_{2}=n
)

\displaystyle p_{j}(k)=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}(k)\leq\widehat{V}_{n+j}(k)\}}{n+1},

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}(k)=\sup\bigg\{t\in[0,1]\colon\frac{mt}{1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}(k)\leq t\}}\leq q\bigg\}.

The uniform law of large numbers implies that as
m,n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[m],k\in[K]}\big|p_{j}(k)-p_{j}^{*}(k)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0,

and since
\{p_{j}^{*}(k)\}_{j=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. random variables, applying the uniform law of large numbers to them implies

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\bigg|\frac{1\vee\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}(k)\leq t\}}{m}-G(t;k)\bigg|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

Recall that
t^{*}_{k}=\sup\{t\colon t/G(t;k)\leq q\}
. Due to the continuity of
G(t;k)
near
t_{k}^{*}
and the fact that there exists
t^{\prime}\in(t_{k}^{*}-\epsilon,t_{k}^{*})
with
t^{\prime}/G(t^{\prime};k)<q
for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, we know
t_{k}^{*}=q\cdot G(t_{k}^{*};k)
, and

\displaystyle\sup_{k}\big|\widehat{\tau}(k)-t_{k}^{*}\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

The uniform law of large numbers thus implies

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[m]}\sup_{k\in[K]}\big||{\mathcal{S}}_{k,\textnormal{SCS}}|/m-G(t_{k}^{*};k)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

Since the maximizer
k^{*}\in[K]
of
G(t_{k}^{*};k)
is unique, taking any constant
\epsilon<(G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\max_{k\neq k^{*}}G(t_{k}^{*},k))/2
, the above a.s. convergence implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{k\in[K]}|{\mathcal{S}}_{k,\textnormal{SCS}}|=k^{*}\big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{S}}_{k^{*},\textnormal{SCS}}|/m>G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\epsilon,

\displaystyle\max_{k\neq k^{*}}|{\mathcal{S}}_{k,\textnormal{SCS}}|/m<\max_{k\neq k^{*}}G(t_{k}^{*};k)+\epsilon\Big)\to 1

for any subsequence of
(m,n)
as
m,n\to\infty
.
Also, defining
\widehat{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{k\in[K]}|{\mathcal{S}}_{k^{*},\textnormal{SCS}}|
, the above analysis implies

\displaystyle\textnormal{Power}({\mathcal{S}}_{\widehat{k},\textnormal{SCS}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}(\widehat{k})\leq\widehat{\tau}(\widehat{k}),~Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\mathbb{P}\Big(F(\widehat{V}_{n+j}(k^{*});k^{*})\leq t_{k}^{*},~Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\Big).

(20)

We then proceed to characterize the proxy selection sets
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k)
, the individual thresholds
\widehat{R}_{j}=\max_{k\in[K]}|{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k)|
, and the final selection set of OptCS-MSel. By definition and using the stopping-time interpretation of the BH procedure in
Storey et al. [2004]
, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k)=\{\ell\in[m]\colon\allowbreak\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq\widehat{\tau}_{j}(k)\}
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{j}(k)=\sup\bigg\{t\in[0,1]\colon\frac{mt}{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq t\}}\leq q\bigg\}.

Recalling the definition (since
n_{2}=n
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}(k)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{V_{i}(k)\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}(k)\}+\ind\{\widehat{V}_{n+j}(k)\leq\widehat{V}_{n+\ell}(k)\}}{n+1}.

The uniform law of large numbers implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in[K]}\sup_{j\in[m],\ell\in[m]}\big|\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}(k)-p_{\ell}^{*}(k)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

Since
\{p_{\ell}^{*}(k)\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. random variables, the above convergence, together with the uniform law of large numbers, implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\sup_{j\in[m]}\bigg|\frac{1+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\ind\{\widetilde{p}_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq t\}}{m}-G(t;k)\bigg|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

With similar arguments as in our analysis of
{\mathcal{S}}_{k,\textnormal{SCS}}
, we thus know that

\sup_{k\in[K],j\in[m]}\big|\widehat{\tau}_{j}(k)-t_{k}^{*}\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0
.
The uniform law of large numbers thus implies

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[m]}\sup_{k\in[K]}\big||{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k)|/m-G(t_{k}^{*};k)\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

Since the maximizer
k^{*}\in[K]
of
G(t_{k}^{*};k)
is unique, taking any constant
\epsilon<(G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\max_{k\neq k^{*}}G(t_{k}^{*},k))/2
, the above a.s. convergence implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\,\widehat{k}_{j}=k^{*},~\forall j\in[m]\big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(

\displaystyle\min_{j\in[m]}|{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k^{*})|/m>G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\epsilon,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[m],k\neq k^{*}}|{\mathcal{S}}_{j}(k)|/m<\max_{k\neq k^{*}}G(t_{k}^{*};k)+\epsilon\Big)\to 1

for any subsequence of
(m,n)
as
m,n\to\infty
. On the other hand, the above analysis also implies that

\sup_{j\in[m]}\big|\widehat{R}_{j}/m-G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0
.
Recall the definition of the OptCS p-values

\displaystyle p_{j}=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind\{v(X_{i},Y_{i};\widehat{k}_{j})\leq v(X_{n+j},c_{n+j};\widehat{k}_{j})\}}{n+1}.

Based on the above analysis, it is not difficult to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[m]}\big|p_{j}-p_{j}^{*}(k^{*})\big|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0,

(21)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\bigg|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq t\}}-G(t;k^{*})\bigg|~{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{a.s.}}}{{\to}}}~0.

(22)

We now proceed to analyze the final selection set of OptCS with homogeneous pruning, following similar ideas as in the proof of Proposition 3.4 in
Jin and Candès [2023a]
. The homogeneous pruning yields

{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{OptCS}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,~\xi\widehat{R}_{j}\leq r^{*}\big\},

where

\displaystyle r^{*}:=\max\bigg\{r\colon\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r/m\}}\geq r/m\bigg\}.

Equivalently, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{OptCS}}=\big\{j\colon p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,~\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r^{**}\big\}
, where

\displaystyle r^{**}=\max\bigg\{r\in[0,1]\colon\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r\}}\geq r\bigg\}.

Fixing any
\delta>0
, we denote the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\delta):=\Bigg\{\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\bigg|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq t\}}-G(t;k^{*})\bigg|>\delta\Bigg\}\bigcup\Bigg\{\sup_{j\in[m]}\big|\widehat{R}_{j}/m-G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})\big|>\delta\Bigg\}.

The above analysis implies that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(\delta))\to 0
for any subsequence of
(m,n)
as
m,n\to\infty
. Also recall that
t_{k^{*}}^{*}=q\cdot G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})
. Thus, on the event
\mathcal{E}(\delta)^{c}
, for any
r\in(0,1)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\Big\{p_{j}\leq qG(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})+q\delta,\,\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\Big\{p_{j}\leq t_{k^{*}}^{*}+q\delta,\,\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}+q\delta;k^{*})+\delta\Big)\ind\Big\{\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}\leq G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}+q\delta;k^{*})+\delta,

and on the other hand, also

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind{\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,\,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r\}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\Big\{p_{j}\leq qG(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})-q\delta,\,\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})+\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\Big\{p_{j}\leq t_{k^{*}}^{*}-q\delta,\,\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})+\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\Big(G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}-q\delta;k^{*})-\delta\Big)\ind\Big\{\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})+\delta\big]\leq r\Big\}.

These imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(r^{**}\geq G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}-q\delta;k^{*})-\delta\Big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{E}(\delta)^{c}~\text{and}~\xi\big[G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})+\delta\big]\leq G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}-q\delta;k^{*})-\delta\Big)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(r^{**}\leq G(t_{k^{*}}^{*}+q\delta;k^{*})+\delta\Big)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(\delta)).

Taking
\delta\downarrow 0
, we know that

r^{**}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}G(t_{k^{*}}^{*};k^{*})
. Thus, with similar ideas we can show that

\displaystyle\text{Power}({\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{OptCS}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ind\{p_{j}\leq q\widehat{R}_{j}/m,\xi\widehat{R}_{j}/m\leq r^{**},Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\mathbb{P}\Big(p_{j}^{*}(k^{*})\leq t_{k}^{*},~Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(F(\widehat{V}_{n+j}(k^{*});k^{*})\leq t_{k}^{*},~Y_{n+j}>c_{n+j}\Big).

(23)

Combining (
20
) and (
23
), we thus have
\text{Power}({\mathcal{S}}_{\textnormal{OptCS}})-\text{Power}({\mathcal{S}}_{\widehat{k},\textnormal{SCS}})=o_{P}(1)
. We thus conclude the proof of Proposition
4.2
.
∎

D.3.3
Comparing OptCS-Full and full-conformal novelty detection

In this section, we provide a targeted comparison with the full-conformal novelty detection
(FCND). As discussed in Appendix
A.1.3
, after the binary reduction
used by OptCS-Full, FCND becomes a close e-value-based comparator. The goal of this
section is to evaluate the direct FCND adaptation in our supervised conformal-selection
setting, using the official
fcnd
implementation.

Adapting FCND to our setting.

The adaptation of FCND to our setting is analogous to AdaDetect and IC. We take the labeled null units

\{i:Y_{i}\leq c_{i}\}

as the reference/inlier sample, and treating the unlabeled test units as the test sample. The
true test labels are used only for evaluating empirical FDR and power. This is the natural
mapping from the thresholded conformal-selection problem to FCND’s novelty-detection
formulation. However, this adaptation uses the data differently
from OptCS-Full: the labeled positives
\{i\colon Y_{i}>c_{i}\}
are not part of the null reference sample and are not natively used in
the same supervised way. In contrast, OptCS-Full uses both labeled positives and labeled
negatives to train the supervised score, and imputes test labels as null to preserve validity.
We compare the following methods:

•

OptCS-Full: our default full-data method with leave-one-out (LOO) training.

•

OptCS-Full-Non-LOO: the non-LOO variant of OptCS-Full, which requires only
one model fit.

•

FCND: the default 1-FC e-BH procedure implemented in the official

fcnd
package.

•

FCND-LOO: the LOO-1-FC e-BH procedure implemented in the official

fcnd
package.

The one-fit procedures, FCND and OptCS-Full-Non-LOO, represent the computationally light
full-data regime, whereas FCND-LOO and OptCS-Full represent the corresponding LOO
regime. For FCND, we report the one-class novelty-detection scores implemented in the
official package, including Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, and one-class SVM. Since
the raw e-BH procedure can underuse the FDR budget, we also implement the null-fraction
correction described by
Lee et al. [2025]
. Unless otherwise stated, the FCND variants shown
below use this null-fraction correction. This correction improves FDR-budget utilization,
but it does not change the underlying data-use pattern: the score is still constructed from a
null-reference sample rather than from both labeled positives and labeled negatives.

Simulation studies.

We first evaluate the methods in Jin’s settings used in Section
5.2
. We fix the FDR target
level at
q=0.2
, vary the number of labeled samples

n\in\{100,200,500\}
, and vary the test sample size

m\in\{20,50,100,200,500\}
. For OptCS-Full and OptCS-Full-Non-LOO, we use a random
forest classifier from
scikit-learn
as a representative supervised learner. For FCND,
we use the official one-class score implementations described above. Each configuration is
averaged over
N=200
runs.

The results are shown in Figure
28
. The FCND variants control FDR, but
remain substantially less powerful than OptCS-Full across the displayed settings. The
null-fraction correction improves the FDR-budget side of FCND, but does not close the
power gap. This supports the data-use interpretation above: in these supervised conformal
selection problems, labeled positives carry useful information for score construction, and
OptCS-Full directly exploits this information through supervised score training. This shows the distinction of data usage and validity mechanism. The
non-LOO variant of OptCS-Full can be competitive in some low-label regimes, but it may
lose power when the fitted supervised model is strong, illustrating the computation–power
tradeoff behind the LOO construction.

D.5
Runtime Comparison for OptCS-Non-LOO

In Appendix
A.4
, we presented additional variants of OptCS-Full and OptCS-Full-MSel for settings where computational resources are not a primary concern and the goal is to maximize selective power. At the other end of the spectrum, one may face more stringent computational constraints while still seeking improved power over the naïve baselines. This can be achieved using the OptCS-Full-Non-LOO and OptCS-Full-MSel-Non-LOO variants, in which each model
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell,k}
is trained on the full dataset rather than the leave-one-out dataset
\mathbf{D}_{-\ell}^{(k)}
. As a result, the entire procedure requires fitting only
K
models, instead of
(n_{2}+m)\times K
models.

As discussed in the main text and illustrated in Appendix
D.1
, this modification may entail a modest loss in power: the prediction
\widehat{\mu}_{\ell,k}(X_{\ell})
is obtained from a model trained on
Z_{\ell}^{T_{k}}
itself, which may make it less informative for constructing the corresponding score. Nevertheless, avoiding repeated leave-one-out fitting can substantially reduce computational cost. Table
5
reports the runtimes of the split baseline, OptCS-Full-MSel, and OptCS-Full-MSel-Non-LOO using the first case in Liang’s settings, with various sample sizes. Runtimes are averaged over 100 repetitions and include selection at all 19 nominal levels. All computations in this section were performed on an Intel Core i7-12700KF CPU.
