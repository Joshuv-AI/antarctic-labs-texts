The Orbit-Summable Fixed Point Criterion and its Relation to Caristi’s Theorem
Robledo Mak’s Miranda Sette

Abstract
The relationship between geometric and variational principles remains central to Nonlinear Analysis. This paper introduces the Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Criterion - a novel, purely dynamical condition—and establishes its profound connection to Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem in complete metric spaces. Our criterion, which requires only that the total displacement along a single orbit be finite (the orbit-summability property), provides a practical and concrete tool for checking the existence of fixed points without relying on the construction of an abstract potential function. We demonstrate that, under a minimal regularity assumption involving the function f f and the metric d d , the Orbit-Summability Criterion is precisely equivalent to Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem. This equivalence is conceptually significant as it creates a direct bridge between the geometric principle of dynamical gap summability (akin to the core idea in Banach’s Contraction Principle) and the variational principle of Caristi. As a direct consequence of this equivalence, the classical Banach Contraction Principle is recovered as a straightforward corollary . The methodology is designed to merge key elements from the Banach proof (orbit convergence via summability) with the structural requirements of Caristi (majorization by a well-behaved functional), effectively providing a unifying framework for these fundamental results. Thus, the Orbit-Summability Criterion offers an attractive and accessible perspective on these established cornerstones of Fixed Point Theory.



1
Introduction

Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem stands among The Fundamental Role of Fixed Point Theory.

Fixed Point Theory occupies a foundational position in modern Mathematics, serving as a critical tool across fields ranging from
Nonlinear Analysis
and
Topology to Applied Mathematics
,
Economics
, and
Optimization
. The existence of a fixed point - a solution
x
to the equation
f(x)=x
for a given self-map
f
on a space
X
- often corresponds to the solution of fundamental problems, such as the solvability of differential and integral equations or the existence of equilibria in dynamical systems.

Historically, the theory gained prominence with the introduction of the
Banach Contraction Principle
in 1922, which provided a constructive and widely applicable criterion for fixed point existence in complete metric spaces, relying on the map’s contractive property. This principle not only guaranteed existence but also uniqueness and rapid convergence through iteration, defining the classical framework for metric fixed point theory.

A major conceptual leap occurred with the realization that fixed point theorems could be linked to variational or minimization principles. The landmark
Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem
(1974) represents the pinnacle of this development. It introduced a necessary condition - the existence of a real-valued potential function
\varphi
that majorizes the distance between
x
and
f(x)
- which significantly generalized the Banach principle. Crucially, the power of Caristi’s result stems from its rigorous equivalence to the
Ekeland’s Variational Principle
, thus embedding the existence of fixed points deep within the technical framework of
optimization and variational analysis
. This equivalence allows fixed point techniques to be rigorously employed in proving the existence of minima for complex functionals, ensuring its pervasive influence across modern Nonlinear Analysis and the study of critical points.

Despite the conceptual elegance of the Caristi-Ekeland equivalence, verifying the existence of the potential function
\varphi
can often be abstract or difficult in practice. The purpose of this paper is to isolate and study a new fixed point criterion arising solely from the
geometric dynamics
of the forward orbit of a point under the iteration of a self-map.

Rather than relying on the global potential
\varphi
, the
Orbit-Summability Condition
asks only whether the cumulative displacement between successive iterates is finite:

\sum_{n\geq 0}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x))<\infty.

This summability requirement frequently admits direct calculation, especially in dynamical contexts where the metric and the map
f
possess explicit structure, thereby offering a practical and concrete diagnostic tool.

We demonstrate the high conceptual significance of this new criterion by establishing a rigorous equivalence: under a mild regularity assumption on the map
f
, the Orbit-Summability Theorem is
precisely equivalent
to Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem. This result creates a fundamental conceptual bridge, showing that the geometric requirement of
dynamical gap summability
- the very notion that underpins the proof of Banach’s principle - is sufficient to satisfy the variational requirements of Caristi’s theorem. Consequently,
the Banach Contraction Principle is obtained as a direct and immediate corollary
of our new criterion.

Thus, the Orbit-Summability Criterion provides an alternative, more dynamical, and geometrically accessible pathway toward verifying the existence of fixed points, simultaneously unifying the classical metric approach of Banach with the modern variational approach of Caristi.

2
Preliminaries

Let
(M,d)
be a metric space and let
f:M\to M
be any map.
For each
n\geq 0
define the
n
-th orbit gap function

d_{f}^{n}(x)=d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x)),\qquad x\in M.

Lemma 2.1

(Lower semicontinuity of the orbit)
.

Define

\varphi_{f}(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x)).

If each
d_{f}^{n}
is lower semicontinuous, then
\varphi_{f}
is also lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

For each
N
put

\varphi_{f}^{N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}d_{f}^{n}(x).

Each
\varphi_{f}^{N}
is l.s.c. and
\varphi_{f}^{N}\nearrow\varphi_{f}
pointwise.
Fix
x_{k}\to x
in
M
.
By Fatou’s lemma applied to the nonnegative array
a_{n,k}=d_{f}^{n}(x_{k})
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\liminf_{k\to\infty}a_{n,k}\;\leq\;\liminf_{k\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n,k}.

Since
\liminf_{k}a_{n,k}\geq d_{f}^{n}(x)
for each
n
, the left-hand side is at least
\varphi_{f}(x)
, giving

\varphi_{f}(x)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\varphi_{f}(x_{k}),

so
\varphi_{f}
is l.s.c.
∎

Definition 2.1

(Proper functions)
.

A function
\varphi:M\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is called
proper
if

\varphi(x)<+\infty
for at least one point
x\in M
.

Definition 2.2

(Contractions)
.

A map
f:(M,d)\to(M,d)
is a
strong contraction
if

d(f(x),f(y))\leq c\cdot d(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in M)

for some
c\in[0,1)
, and a
weak contraction
if the inequality holds with
c=1
.

3
The Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem

Theorem 3.1

(Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem)
.

Let
(M,d)
be a complete metric space and let
f:M\to M
be a map.
Assume that for every
n\geq 0
the function
d_{f}^{n}
is lower semicontinuous.
Then
f
has a fixed point if and only if there exists
x\in M
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x))<+\infty.

Proof.

Suppose the series converges for some
x\in M
.
Set
x_{n}=f^{n}(x)
, with the convention that
f^{0}(x)=x
.
Convergence of the series implies

d(x_{n},x_{m})\leq\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j},x_{j+1})\xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{}0,

so
(x_{n})
is Cauchy. Completeness of
M
yields
x_{n}\to p\in M
for some
p
. Define the orbit potential

\varphi_{f}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d(f^{j}(y),f^{j+1}(y))\in[0,+\infty].

Using the identity

\varphi_{f}(x_{n})=\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}d(x_{j},x_{j+1}),

we see that
\varphi_{f}(x_{n})\to 0
.
By Lemma
2.1
,
\varphi_{f}
is l.s.c., so

\varphi_{f}(p)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{f}(x_{n})=0.

Hence
\varphi_{f}(p)=0
.
As
\varphi_{f}(p)
is a sum of nonnegative terms, each term vanishes:

d(p,f(p))=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f(p)=p.

Conversely, if
p
is a fixed point then each gap
d(f^{n}(p),f^{n+1}(p))=0
, so the series trivially converges.
∎

Corollary 3.1

(Banach Contraction Principle)
.

Every strong contraction on a complete metric space has a unique fixed point.

Proof.

If
f
is a strong contraction with constant
c<1
, then

d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x))\leq c^{n}\cdot d(x,f(x)),\quad n\geq 0,

the geometric series
\sum c^{n}
converges and each
d_{f}^{n}
is continuous and therefore lower semicontinuous.
Thus the orbit is summable for every
x
, and the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
are satisfied.
Existence follows from the theorem and uniqueness is standard.
∎

4
Relations Between Caristi and the Orbit-Summability Criterion

4.1
Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Caristi’s Fixed Point Theorem)
.

Let
(M,d)
be complete and let
f:M\to M
.
Let
\varphi:M\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be proper, bounded below, and lower semicontinuous.
Assume

d(x,f(x))\leq\varphi(x)-\varphi(f(x))\qquad(x\in M).

Then
f
has a fixed point.

4.2
(Orbit-Summability +
d_{f}^{n}
l.s.c.)
\Rightarrow
Caristi

Theorem 4.2
.

If
d_{f}^{n}(\cdot):=d(f^{n}(\cdot),f^{n+1}(\cdot))
is lower semicontinuous for all
n\geq 0
and satisfies Caristi’s inequality with a proper,
bounded-below, l.s.c. potential
\varphi
, then
f
has a fixed point.

Proof.

Let
m=\inf_{M}\varphi>-\infty
.
Choose
x
with
\varphi(x)<+\infty
.
From Caristi’s inequality applied to
f^{j}(x)
,

d(f^{j}(x),f^{j+1}(x))\leq\varphi(f^{j}(x))-\varphi(f^{j+1}(x)).

Summing from
j=0
to
N
gives

\sum_{j=0}^{N}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j+1}(x))\leq\varphi(x)-\varphi(f^{N+1}(x))\leq\varphi(x)-m.

Letting
N\to\infty
,

\varphi_{f}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j+1}(x))\leq\varphi(x)-m<+\infty.

Thus
x
has a summable orbit.
Since
d_{f}^{n}
is l.s.c. for all
n\geq 0
, Theorem
3.1
applies and yields a fixed point.
∎

4.3
Caristi +
d_{f}^{n}
l.s.c.
\Rightarrow
Orbit-Summability

Theorem 4.3
.

If
f
satisfies Caristi’s inequality with a proper, bounded-below, l.s.c. potential and in addition
d_{f}^{n}
is l.s.c. for every
n\geq 0
,
then
f
admits at least one summable orbit and thus, a fixed point.

Proof.

Identical to the summability argument in the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The orbit-summability criterion provides a concrete dynamical test for the existence
of fixed points.
In many applications the quantities
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x))
can be estimated explicitly,
so the condition

\sum_{n\geq 0}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n+1}(x))<\infty

becomes computationally accessible, particularly when
f
admits explicit iterates or the metric
encodes geometric decay.
This offers a complementary viewpoint to potential-based methods such as Caristi’s theorem.

5
Convergence of Non-Expansive Algorithms via Orbit-Summability

5.1
The General Convergence Problem for Non-Expansive Iterations

Let
(H,\|\cdot\|)
be a Hilbert space and let
T:H\to H
be a non-expansive mapping, that is,

\|T(x)-T(y)\|\leq\|x-y\|\qquad(x,y\in H).

Consider the fixed point iteration

x_{n+1}=T(x_{n}),\qquad x_{0}\in H.

Such iterations arise naturally in convex optimization, variational inequalities, and splitting methods.
However, in contrast to the contractive case, non-expansivity alone does not guarantee convergence.
Classical tools typically rely on additional assumptions such as compactness, Opial-type conditions,
uniform convexity, or the existence of a global Lyapunov functional.
The central question is therefore the following:

Under which verifiable and purely geometric conditions does a non-expansive iteration converge to a fixed point?

The Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem provides a direct and intrinsic answer to this question.

5.2
Orbit-Summability as a Convergence Mechanism

Given a non-expansive map
T:H\to H
and an initial point
x_{0}\in H
, define the orbit

x_{n}=T^{n}(x_{0}).

The orbit is said to be
summable
if

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|x_{n+1}-x_{n}\|<\infty.

This condition has a clear geometric meaning: the total displacement of the orbit is finite.
In particular, summability implies that
(x_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence, hence convergent in the complete
space
H
.

The Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem shows that, under a minimal lower semicontinuity assumption on
the orbit gaps, the limit of such an orbit must necessarily be a fixed point of
T
.
Importantly, this conclusion is obtained without invoking any global variational principle.

5.3
Application to the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann Algorithm

We now illustrate the applicability of this framework by considering the classical

Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration
, defined by

x_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_{n})x_{n}+\alpha_{n}T(x_{n}),

where
(\alpha_{n})\subset(0,1)
is a relaxation sequence.

This algorithm plays a fundamental role in fixed point theory and serves as a prototype
for many modern splitting schemes.

5.3.1
Orbit Gaps and Summability

A direct computation shows that

x_{n+1}-x_{n}=\alpha_{n}\bigl(T(x_{n})-x_{n}\bigr),

and therefore

\|x_{n+1}-x_{n}\|=\alpha_{n}\|T(x_{n})-x_{n}\|.

Hence, the orbit-summability condition becomes

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\|T(x_{n})-x_{n}\|<\infty.

This condition is
local along the orbit
and can often be verified using
energy estimates or problem-specific decay properties, even when no global potential
function is available.

5.3.2
Convergence Result

Assume that:

•

T:H\to H
is non-expansive (which implies the functions
x\mapsto\|T^{n+1}(x)-T^{n}(x)\|
are lower semicontinuous for all
n\geq 0
);

•

there exists
x_{0}\in H
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\|T(x_{n})-x_{n}\|<\infty.

Then the Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem applies and yields the following conclusion:

x_{n}\longrightarrow p\in H,\qquad T(p)=p.

Thus, the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration converges strongly to a fixed point of
T

under a condition that depends solely on the summability of the orbit increments.

5.4
Extensions to Other Algorithms

The reasoning above is not specific to the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann scheme.
Any iterative method that generates a non-expansive (or quasi-non-expansive) orbit
and admits explicit control of the increments
\|x_{n+1}-x_{n}\|
falls within the scope
of the Orbit-Summability Criterion.
Notable examples include:

•

The Proximal Point Algorithm;

•

Forward–Backward and Douglas–Rachford splitting methods;

•

alternating projections onto closed convex sets;

•

relaxed and inertial fixed point schemes.

In all these cases, convergence can be established by verifying that the total
geometric displacement of the orbit is finite, bypassing the need for global
variational arguments.

5.5
Conceptual Significance

This application highlights a key philosophical shift:
instead of constructing a global Lyapunov or potential function,
one can certify convergence by inspecting the
dynamical geometry of a single orbit
.

The Orbit-Summability Fixed Point Theorem thus provides a unifying and flexible
framework for convergence analysis in non-expansive dynamics,
bridging classical metric fixed point theory with modern algorithmic practice.
