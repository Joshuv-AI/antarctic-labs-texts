Costs and benefits of routine labs in hospital patients: iatrogenic anaemia and undiagnosed acute kidney injury
Authors: Dawson Dean
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceGuidelines recommend avoiding unnecessary laboratory tests to minimise risks of anaemia in hospitalised patients as well as reduce costs, but there are costs to skipping routine labs including missing acute kidney injury.ObjectiveQuantify the costs and benefits of routine labs in dollar costs as well as mortality.Evidence reviewThis is a retrospective analysis of 48 204 admissions at University of Kentucky Hospitals and simulates different strategies for skipping labs.FindingsIn a simplified estimate of pure dollar costs, the costs of daily labs appear to outweigh the costs of missing acute kidney injury.Conclusions and relevanceIn both dollar costs and the number of patients with mortality effects, the benefits of randomly skipping labs appear to significantly outweigh the costs, but the costs are not insignificant.

Introduction

The laboratory test is the microscope of the internist and illuminates processes such as renal arteriole blood flow or distal tubule transporter activity that are invisible to the gross findings of a physical examination or history. However, the Choosing Wisely guidelines recommend clinicians ‘Don’t perform laboratory blood testing unless clinically indicated so that the risk of iatrogenic anaemia is minimized’.1 Furthermore, the Society of Hospital Medicine Choosing Wisely guidelines recommend, ‘Additionally, reducing the frequency of daily unnecessary phlebotomy can result in significant cost savings for hospitals’.2 British guidelines3 recommend ‘urea and electrolytes (U&E) checked on admission and within 24 hour’ for patients with kidney injury, but this is only for patients with known kidney injury and does not recommend routine or periodically repeated labs after the first recheck. Those same guidelines also recommend ‘daily monitoring of U&E and glucose’ for stable patients on IV fluids, but level of evidence is D.

There are risks to not monitoring a patient’s laboratory values from newly arising conditions such as acute kidney injury (AKI), but the guidelines leave open the question of what defines clinically indicated and do not specify the costs and savings of routine lab tests. Like many medical decisions, choosing how frequently to monitor laboratory values benefits from comparing costs and benefits of routine lab draws, and this paper attempts to quantify these risks and benefits.

Methods

This study is a retrospective analysis of 48 204 admissions at University of Kentucky Hospitals, which are teaching hospitals serving central and eastern Kentucky in the USA. All patients were admitted to general Internal Medicine Teams between 2015 and 2019 inclusive. Patients were admitted for a variety of medical issues, usually not specifically for anaemia or renal injury. All patients were at least 18 years old, had at least 3 days of laboratory values collected and had an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) that exceeded 20 at least once.

All data analysis was performed with Python software running on Python V.3.7.04 using standard numerical libraries.5 The source code for all analysis is open source and may be downloaded at http://www.dawsondean.com/pub/routineLabsAndAKISrcCode.tar

Acute kidney injury

AKI is defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines6:

KDIGO I: creatinine increase by 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or increase in creatinine to 1.5–1.9 times baseline or urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6–12 hours.

KDIGO I: creatinine increase by 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or increase in creatinine to 1.5–1.9 times baseline or urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6–12 hours.

KDIGO 2: creatinine increase to 2.0–2.9 times baseline or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for at least 12 hours.

KDIGO 2: creatinine increase to 2.0–2.9 times baseline or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for at least 12 hours.

KDIGO 3: creatinine increase to 3.0 times or more baseline or creatinine increase to 4.0 or greater or urine output <0.3 mL/kg/hour for at least 24 hours or Anuria for at least 12 hours.

KDIGO 3: creatinine increase to 3.0 times or more baseline or creatinine increase to 4.0 or greater or urine output <0.3 mL/kg/hour for at least 24 hours or Anuria for at least 12 hours.

In all of these criteria, the baseline creatinine is assumed to have occurred within the prior 7 days. The current study omits the restriction of 7 days, and instead considers the baseline to be the lowest creatinine measured during the admission prior to each new lab. Additionally, because urine output is often not recorded reliably, this study only uses changes in creatinine to define AKI. This study also limits the criteria of creatinine rising 0.3 mg/dL to patients whose previous creatinine was less than 1.7 mg/dL to avoid false positives from normal variation in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD). This study also ignores any patient whose estimated GFR never rises above 20 mL/min, because such patients may be on haemodialysis. All GFR values are computed using the CKD-Epi equation.7

AKI and skipping labs

The question of whether skipping lab values will lead to undiagnosed AKIs is difficult because there is no way to describe the results of laboratory tests that were never performed. An approximation is to use ‘virtual admissions’, which are any sequence of 3 or more hospital days with daily labs. There may be several virtual admissions in one actual admission, and each sequence of daily labs is considered a separate virtual admission (figure 1).

Several virtual hospital admissions in one actual hospital admission.

This study simulates skipping labs by ignoring some values and estimates sensitivity by comparing the number of AKIs observed with and without skipping labs:

Sensitivity = number of AKIs with skipped labs/number of AKIs with no skipped labs

Ideally, we skip enough labs to avoid unnecessary blood draws but not so many labs that we miss episodes of AKI. There are many methods to make this trade-off and several were tested in this study, including:

Randomly skipping labs starting on the first day of a hospitalisation.

Randomly skipping labs after the 14th day of a hospitalisation (emphasises stable patients who remain inpatient due to placement issues).

Randomly skipping labs only when creatinine has been stable—the most recent creatinine is between 0.7 and 1.3 x of the 3-day running average.

Anaemia

The WHO definition of anaemia is absolute haemoglobin below 13.0 g/dL for men and 12.0 g/dL for women,8 but this difference can also be calculated in several ways. Some papers investigating anaemia9 10 use the difference between haemoglobin on admission and discharge, and only consider patients with normal haemoglobin on admission. This approach, however, excludes acute on chronic anaemia that happen during the middle of an admission. Instead, this study expands the WHO definitions to use comparable drops in haemoglobin between the highest and lowest values recorded during the hospital admission:

No anaemia: haemoglobin drop less than 1.0 g/dL.

No anaemia: haemoglobin drop less than 1.0 g/dL.

Mild anaemia: haemoglobin drop 1.0 g/dL–3.0 g/dL.

Mild anaemia: haemoglobin drop 1.0 g/dL–3.0 g/dL.

Moderate anaemia: haemoglobin drop 3.0 g/dL–5.0 g/dL.

Moderate anaemia: haemoglobin drop 3.0 g/dL–5.0 g/dL.

Severe anaemia: haemoglobin drop over 5.0 g/dL.

Severe anaemia: haemoglobin drop over 5.0 g/dL.

Additionally, anaemia can also happen after a transfusion, so this study defines peak haemoglobin as:

Peak haemoglobin=peak measured haemoglobin+(0.9×number transfusions up until that peak)

This haemoglobin drop will be designated as Peak-To-Trough, while the drop in haemoglobin measured only at admission and discharge will be designated as Admission-to-Discharge. When the type of anaemia is not specified, it will be Peak-To-Trough.

Results

This study is a retrospective analysis of 48 204 admissions at University of Kentucky Hospitals who were admitted between 2015 and 2019 inclusive.

Acute kidney injury

For AKI analysis, this study examined 43 683 patient admissions at the University of Kentucky between 2015 and 2019 with an eGFR that was at least 20 at some time during the admission. Among these non-dialysis hospital admissions, 18.2% (n=7935) had any AKI, 12.7% (n=5566) had a KDIGO-1 AKI, 3.6% (n=1568) had a KDIGO-2 AKI and 1.3% (n=801) had a KDIGO-3 AKI. Significant Elixhauser11 12 statistics of University of Kentucky patients with AKI in the Hospital are described in online supplemental table 2. Diagnoses like hypertension and valvular cardiomyopathy seem to be more associated with higher levels of kidney injury. However, only 46% of patients with KDIGO 3 AKI based on their creatinine had an Elixhauser ICD code related to AKI.

Most cases of KDIGO 2 and KDIGO 3 AKI were present on admission (79% of KDIGO 2 and 75% of KDIGO 3), while only 52% of KDIGO 1 AKI happened before admission, so 48% of KDIGO 1 AKI happened after admission (figure 2). Most patients who developed AKI in the hospital had a baseline creatinine between 0.5 and 0.75, and many patients with AKI were receiving nephrotoxic medication—15% were prescribed a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, 46% received vancomycin and 41% were on a diuretic (online supplemental figures).

Fraction of each class of AKI that happens before and after admission. AKI, acute kidney injury.

The test for creatinine, however, has limits in reproducibility and possibly accuracy. Occasionally, patients in the hospital have identical labs drawn at nearly the same time, and two labs drawn within 15 min apart should be very similar. Comparing the results from these near simultaneous tests suggests the reproducibility of the overall test and may be indicative of its accuracy. The University of Kentucky uses the Roche Cobas 8000/702 (Jaffee method) assay for creatinine, and 254 pairs of creatinine labs were collected within 15 min apart. Of these near simultaneous lab pairs, only 43% of results were identical, but 90% of pairs were within a range of −0.3 and 0.3 apart (figure 3).

AKI and skipping labs

We estimate the sensitivity for detecting AKI with and without daily labs by using virtual admissions. There were 11 116 non-ESRD virtual admissions, and the average virtual length of stay was 8.25 days. A comparable number of AKIs were found in actual and virtual admissions (online supplemental figures).

Random skipping labs will miss AKIs in a dose-dependent relationship with the probability of skipping labs (figure 4). Similarly, randomly skipping labs only after 14 days in the hospital will also miss AKIs, but misses fewer of the more severe AKIs. Finally, skipping labs only when recent creatinine values have been stable will also miss AKIs. However, fewer labs are skipped, and so fewer AKIs are undiagnosed. Hemoglobin

Of the initial patient population, 29 009 admissions had three or more haemoglobin collected and were used for analysis. The average haemoglobin drop (Peak-To-Trough) was 1.64 g/dL, while average haemoglobin drop (Admission-to-Discharge) was 0.35 g/dL. Classifying anaemia by Peak-To-Trough drop detected 9762 more cases of mild anaemia, 6166 more cases of moderate anaemia and 3654 more cases of severe anaemia than Admission-to-Discharge (online supplemental figures). All other statistics will use Peak-To-Trough haemoglobin drops.

The most common Elixhauser statistics11 12 for patients based on ICD codes are described in online supplemental table 1. As expected, diagnoses of deficiency anaemia and coagulopathy are more common in patients with anaemia based on lab values, but only approximately 20% of patients with anaemia based on lab values also had ICD codes for anaemia.

Many patients already had some chronic anaemia on admission: 11% had haemoglobin below 8, 9% below 9, 13% below 11, 13% below 12 and 12% below 13 (online supplemental figures). However, anaemia occurs both on admission and during the hospitalisation. For example, 11% of admissions had severe anaemia on admission, but 19% of admissions had severe anaemia at some point during the hospitalisation (online supplemental figures).

Anaemia that develops in the hospital tends to be slow and progressive, and patients typically do not have large changes in haemoglobin in the middle of an admission. The average daily changes in haemoglobin are negative and range between −0.07 g/dL and −0.2 g/dL daily after day 2 in the hospital (online supplemental figures). This drop in haemoglobin does not frequently rise to the level requiring transfusions. For example, the number of transfusions declines with both length of stay and with the number of labs collected (online supplemental figures).

However, haemoglobin testing in daily hospital practice may not be accurate enough to draw precise conclusions. The University of Kentucky uses the Haemoglobin Sysmex XN9000 assay for measuring serum haemoglobin, and in the data collected, there were 770 pairs of tests drawn within 15 min apart. Only 17% of near simultaneous lab pairs had perfect agreement, and the error range to include 90% of the collected labs was −0.8 g/dL to 0.8 g/dL, while the error range to include 95% of tests was between −1.5 g/dL and 1.5 g/dL (figure 5).

Difference between simultaneous tests of haemoglobin.

The cause of anaemia while in the hospital is likely multifactorial. Lab draws is likely one cause, and the Spearman correlation between haemoglobin decline and the total number of complete blood count (CBC) or basic metabolic panel (BMP) labs collected is 0.8116, while the correlation between haemoglobin drop and length of stay is 0.6335. Additionally, the Spearman correlation of haemoglobin decline with platelet decline is 0.7922, which suggests a loss of whole blood and not a limited underproduction of red blood cells. As with haemoglobin, the Spearman correlation between platelets decline and the number of labs collected is 0.8084 and between platelets decline and the length of stay is 0.6789.

Other factors besides lab draws may also contribute to iatrogenic anaemia, and these are not affected by skipping labs. The Spearman coefficient between the number of surgeries and drop in haemoglobin is 0.4352, and the Spearman coefficient between number of endoscopies or colonoscopies and drop in haemoglobin is 0.2013. Nonetheless, 62% of patients with severe anaemia and 40% of patients with moderate anaemia also underwent surgery. Similarly, the Spearman coefficient between the highest white cell count and haemoglobin decline is 0.5033, while the Spearman coefficient between haemoglobin decline and receiving antibiotics including vancomycin is 0.3608 (online supplemental figures and table 3).

Discussion

We model the effects of reducing lab utilisation by 25%, and the interested reader may use an online calculator to try different policies and assumptions, https://www.dawsondean.com/pub/CostOfRoutineLabs.html

Costs of skipping labs

We will consider only the effects of skipping labs on iatrogenic AKI, since AKI present on admission would not be affected by lab policy. If we skip 25% of labs then we skip the same number of creatinine levels annually. Using a random sampling algorithm, we will miss 185 instances of KDIGO 1 AKI annually, 22 instances of KDIGO 2 AKI annually and 14 instances of KDIGO 3 AKI annually, or a total of 222 cases of undiagnosed AKI annually.

Chertow13 estimated the costs of AKI; an increase in creatinine over 0.5 mg/dL was associated with 6.5-fold increase in the odds of death, a 3.5-day increase in length of stay, and $8902 in excess hospital costs. Ishani14 showed that patients with AKI and CKD have increased risk for ESRD relative to patients without kidney disease (HR 41.2), and patients with AKI but no previous CKD also have increased risk for ESRD (HR 13.0).

Admission to the University of Kentucky Hospital costs $1687 per day. If each AKI adds 3.5 additional days to the admission, then there is a total cost of $1 315 498. The cost is $1 127 569 if each AKI adds three additional days to the admission, $751 713 if each AKI adds 2 days and $375 856 if each AKI adds 1 day.

Cost–benefit comparison

The benefits of skipping labs are the cost savings from skipped labs as well as the avoided impacts of anaemia due to those labs:

Total avoided cost=avoided cost of skipped labs+avoided cost of iatrogenic anaemia due to lab draws not meeting transfusion criteria+avoided cost of unnecessary transfusions+avoided cost of adverse effects due to unnecessary transfusions

We assume each CBC costs $184, each BMP costs $187 and each transfusion costs $1900. Reducing lab draws by 25% would provide a saving that is the sum of $5 133 470 in saved labs+$1 795 500 for avoided transfusion costs+711 patients have reduced 12-month mortality

In this simplified estimate of pure dollar costs, we save significantly more from reduced lab costs than we would gain by detecting AKI earlier, at least partly because iatrogenic AKI seems to be relatively uncommon. However, this calculation makes several assumptions such as reducing routine labs by 25% will also reduce transfusions and anaemia by the exact same percentage. The real lesson is that there are both benefits and costs to skipping labs. Quality improvement efforts often focus only on the costs in dollars and millilitres of blood of skipped labs, but what we measure affects how we behave, and we should just as carefully track the costs of skipping routine labs.

In the current study, creatinine was appropriately monitored more closely when it was observed to be rising. A histogram of days until the next creatinine was drawn shows labs were usually collected 1 day after it rises. The problem with skipping labs is missing the first rise, not monitoring a previously observed AKI.

Other studies have examined routine labs and iatrogenic anaemia. Salisbury et al9 studied 2909 adult patients hospitalised with myocardial infarction and found patients with moderate or severe anaemia (based on discharge haemoglobin) had higher risk of 12-month mortality. The study did not find a causal effect between lab tests and iatrogenic anaemia, and noted, ‘Finally, these observational data do not allow us to draw conclusions about causal relationships between HAA and mortality, and it remains unclear whether HAA is a marker for or a mediator of, poor outcomes’.

Koch et al10 studied 188 447 hospitalisations, and also found patients with moderate or severe anaemia had higher 12-month mortality. Again, this did not draw a clear causal connection between blood draws and anaemia, and the anaemia may be explained by other factors.

Makam et al15 studied 29 045 hospitalisations and found higher 30-day mortality in patients with hospital anaemia, but again it is not clear whether anaemia was the cause of mortality or a marker for other injury. The authors stated ‘Thus, blood loss due to phlebotomy, 1 of the more modifiable aetiologies of HAA, was unlikely to have been the primary driver for most patients who developed severe HAA’.

This paper has several strengths: it uses virtual admissions to estimate skipping labs while still knowing the results of those labs, and attempts to quantify both the benefits and risks of skipping labs. There are however several weaknesses. AKI is identified only by changes in creatinine, not urine output. Any patient with a GFR that never exceeds 20 mL/min is excluded because of possible haemodialysis. This study did not examine whether a creatinine rises several days in a row without detection because we cannot simulate consecutive missed lab tests. In this study, ‘skipped labs’ are only simulated, and the actual lab test is still performed and monitored by a physician who could respond to an AKI by adjusting fluids or medications or other interventions. After the first rise in creatinine, the second creatinine may be elevated or normal, but that may only reflect a response to the physicians’ interventions. Additionally, simulating skipping labs was done only on patients who had daily labs, so a clinician was possibly already concerned about renal injury. This study also assumes that any haemoglobin is collected as part of a CBC and any creatinine is collected as part of a BMP. This study also uses the same categories of costs of anaemia as other studies, but with slightly broader definitions (includes acute on chronic anaemia). For the purpose of comparing costs and benefits, there were average 52 930 CBCs and 57 726 BMPs collected annually.
