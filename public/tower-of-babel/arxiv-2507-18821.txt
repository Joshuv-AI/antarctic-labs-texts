On Similarity Structure Groups and their W ∗ and C ∗ -algebras
Eli Bashwinger, Patrick DeBonis

Abstract
. Finite Similarity Structure (FSS) groups are a class of generalized Thompson groups first introduced by Farley and Hughes. In this paper, we study the properties of a new subclass of the more general Countable Similarity Structure (CSS) groups, which we call CSS ∗ groups. The Higman-Thompson groups V d , r V_{d,r} , the countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups V d ​ ( G ) V_{d}(G) , and the topological full groups of irreducible one-sided subshifts of finite type studied by Matui are all examples of CSS ∗ groups. One overarching theme is to isolate a class of CSS groups with the necessary properties to generalize the main result of arXiv:2312.08345 – primeness of the group von Neumann algebra of V d V_{d} . We achieve this aim for CSS ∗ groups and in the process prove that CSS ∗ groups are non-inner amenable and many are properly proximal, which are new results for V d , r V_{d,r} , V d ​ ( G ) V_{d}(G) , and the groups studied by Matui. A second theme is to produce a dichotomy within CSS ∗ groups; those that are C ∗ C^{*} -simple and possess a simple commutator subgroup, and those lacking both properties. In particular, we extend the C ∗ C^{*} -simplicity results of V V and V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) of arXiv:1605.01651 , recover the simple commutator subgroup results of arXiv:2008.04791 and arXiv:1210.5800 , and give examples that lack both properties. Lastly, we observe that CSS ∗ groups are not acylindrically hyperbolic, motivating the need to prove many of these results by other methods.



1.
Introduction

The Thompson groups
F\leq T\leq V
and their generalizations have attracted wide-spread attention, particularly in the last few decades, serving as examples and counterexamples for many results in group theory, topology, and logic. Although there have been various generalizations of these groups, three notable sources for systemically producing Thompson-like groups come from the cloning system construction, first introduced by Witzel and Zaremsky in
[
WZ18
]
, Jones Technology elaborated on in
[
Bro23
]
, and similarity structures on compact ultrametric spaces, first introduced by Hughes in
[
Hug09
]
. A
similarity structure
on a compact ultrametric space
X
is a function that associates a set of local similarities between any two closed balls of
X
. When the sets are finite, this is called a
finite similarity structure
or FSS. The largest group determined by a similarity structure is called an
FSS group
and is a subgroup of
\text{LS}(X)
, the group of local similarities of the compact ultrametric space. In this paper, we isolate a special class of FSS groups and their natural generalization,
countable similarity structure
(CSS) groups, for which we are able to study their analytic properties that shed light on the structure of the resulting group von Neumann algebra and reduced group
C^{*}
-algebra.

Studying the operator algebras of the extended class of generalized Thompson groups began with Jolissaint in
[
Jol98
]
, where he proved that the group von Neumann algebra
L(F)
for the Thompson group
F
is McDuff, meaning
L(F)\cong L(F)\bar{\otimes}R
, where
R
is the hyperfinite II
1
factor. Later, in
[
BZ23
]
, the first named author and Zaremsky initiated the study of the von Neumann algebras of Thompson-like groups from cloning systems, where they generalized Jolissaint’s result and proved that many “
F
-like” groups give rise to group von Neumann algebras that are McDuff factors and therefore are inner-amenable. In
[
Bas23
]
, the first named author went on to prove that the preponderance of these cloning system groups give rise to inclusions of von Neumann algebras satisfying the weak asymptotic homomorphism property with respect to the subalgebra
L(F_{d})
, where
F_{d}
is the smallest of the Higman-Thompson groups (a very natural generalization of
F
). In
[
dSDK23
]
, the second named author, de Santiago, and Khan proved that many of the
F
-like groups coming from cloning systems are stable in the sense of Jones and Schmidt, meaning they admit a free ergodic p.m.p action on a probability space such that the resulting crossed product von Neumann algebra is a McDuff factor. They also showed that for the Higman-Thompson groups
T_{d}
and
V_{d}
, the group von Neumann algebras
L(T_{d})
and
L(V_{d})
are prime. A II
1
factor is
prime
if
M\not\cong P\bar{\otimes}Q
for any II
1
factors
P
and
Q
. One of the main motivations of this present paper was to generalize this result to a suitable class of CSS groups.

Primeness for von Neumann algebras is a property that is intensive to demonstrate. Popa exhibited the first prime von Neumann algebra in
[
Pop83
]
, and in
[
Ge96
]
Ge used the techniques of Voiculescu’s free probability theory to prove that the free group factors
L(\mathbb{F}_{n})
are prime. Ozawa then generalized this to all non-elementary hyperbolic groups and bi-exact groups in
[
Oza04
]
and
[
BO08
]
. In fact, Ozawa proved a stronger property: that they yield solid von Neumann Algebras. A von Neumann algebra
M
is
solid
if for any diffuse subalgebra
A\subset M
,
A^{\prime}\cap M
is amenable. Related properties, such as strong solidity and its generalizations, were developed later (see for example
[
OP10a
]
and
[
HJKE25
]
). As Popa’s deformation/rigidity theory revolutionized the study of II
1
factors, this framework was used to prove other classes of groups are prime, solid, or strongly solid. After Popa reproved Ozawa’s solidity result for free group factors in
[
Pop07
]
, Peterson used the techniques of Popa’s deformation/rigidity theory to prove groups admitting a weakly-
\ell^{2}
unbounded 1-cocycle produce solid group von Neumann algebras, in
[
Pet09
]
. The main result of
[
dSDK23
]
(referenced as Theorem
7.1
in Section
7
) can then be understood as a generalization of Peterson’s work, where it was shown that under particular conditions, groups admitting a 1-cocycle into a quasi-regular representation will produce a prime von Neumann algebra that is generally
not
solid. In particular, the second named author, de Santiago, and Khan proved Theorem
7.1
and that the Higman-Thompson groups
T_{d}
and
V_{d}
satisfy the necessary assumptions. The original motivation of this current paper was to understand which generalized Thompson groups satisfy Theorem
7.1
, and more precisely, which FSS groups.

In
[
Hug09
]
, Hughes introduced FSS groups as a generalized class of Thompson groups that specifically possess the Haagerup property. In particular, Hughes generalized the proper 1-cocycle of Farley from
[
Far03
]
and actually showed that all FSS groups act properly by isometries on CAT
(0)
cubical complexes. In
[
FH15
]
, Farley and Hughes studied the finiteness properties of these groups and identified a subclass that are all type
F_{\infty}
. In an earlier version of
[
FH15
]
, they also used the language of FSS groups to give some isomorphism class of
V_{d}(H)
for
H
finite, and identified simple subgroups related to Nekrashevych work in
[
Nek04
]
. In
[
HF17
]
, they isolated a connection between braided diagram groups and FSS groups, and showed the Houghton groups and a particular group of quasi-automorphisms of the infinite binary tree fall into both classes. Later, Farley and Hughes generalized FSS groups further in an effort to provide a unifying framework for the finiteness properties for generalized Thompson groups in
[
FH24
]
and
[
Far25
]
. Their efforts included the Brin-Thompson groups
nV
, among many others, but do not apply to some in the cloning system family, like the braided Higman-Thompson group
bV_{d}
. Additionally, Farley studied the context-free co-word problem for similarity structure groups in
[
Far18
]
and Sauer and Thumann
[
ST14
]
showed certain similarity structure groups are
\ell^{2}
-invisible. In summary, apart from the Haagerup property result of Hughes, many analytic aspects of these groups necessary for operator algebraic results remained unexamined, and in this paper we initiate a more robust study of such properties of certain similarity structure groups.

Statement of Results

The class of CSS groups we study are referred to as
CSS
∗
groups
and are defined more precisely in Definition
2.8
. Morally speaking, CSS
∗
groups are those that possess a “local homogeneity” property in the corresponding compact ultrametric space
X
. Given any closed ball
B
in
X
and an infinite ball
D
in
X
, this local homogeneity translates to always being able to find a closed subball
D_{0}\subseteq D
such that there exists a surjective similarity
h:B\to D_{0}
. This turns out to be enough to establish a rich structure within the CSS
∗
groups. We can now state our main results, many of which are used to prove the following motivating theorem, yet are important in their own right.

Theorem A

(Theorem
7.7
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Then
L(\Gamma)
is a prime II
1
factor, but is not solid.

Proving this theorem requires numerous explicit constructions to show CSS
∗
groups satisfy the conditions of Theorem
7.1
. The starting point is reinterpreting and restricting the Farley-Hughes cocycle to a quasi-regular representation with respect to a specific subgroup. In tandem with the CSS
∗
property, we leverage the cocycle to prove non-inner amenability (Theorem
B
) to confirm that
L(\Gamma)
does not have property Gamma for any CSS
∗
group
\Gamma
.

Example 1.1
.

The class of CSS
∗
is quite robust and includes the following groups.

(1)

The Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}
, first defined in
[
Hig74
]
and
[
Bro87
]
.

(2)

Countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups
V_{d}(G)
, first defined and in
[
Sco84a
]
,
[
Röv99
]
,
[
Nek04
]
.

(3)

QAut
(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})
, a certain class of self-bijections of the infinite ordered rooted binary tree
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, first defined in
[
Leh08
]
.

(4)

\text{LS}(X)
, where
X
is a locally rigid compact ultrametric space, like the Fibonacci Space, first defined in
[
Hug09
]
.

(5)

The CSS group for any non-periodic irreducible subshift of finite type, studied in
[
Mat15
]
.

In
[
BZ22
]
, Zaremsky and the first named author proved the Higman-Thompson groups
T_{d}
and
V_{d}
are non-inner amenable, extending a result of Haagerup and Olesen in
[
HO17
]
.

In this current paper, we take a different approach, utilizing a result of Chifan, Sinclair, and Udrea, from
[
CSU16
]
to prove that all CSS
∗
groups are non-inner amenable. Given that countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups are CSS
∗
groups, this resolves a conjecture stated in
[
BZ22
]
for the countable setting.

Theorem B

(Theorem
6.6
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Then
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable.

The result from
[
CSU16
]
in part requires the group to admit an unbounded cocycle into a non-amenable representation. We prove the quasi-regular representation
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
is non-amenable by explicitly constructing a paradoxical decomposition of the coset space
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}
in Theorem
6.4
. This is an interesting result in its own right, as it produces a paradoxical decomposition of the Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}
that do not depend directly on free subgroups.

When the cocycle is additionally proper (which is the case for FSS
∗
groups) then the non-amenability of the representation implies the groups are properly proximal by a result of Boutonnet, Ioana, and Peterson
[
BIP21
]
. The notion of proper proximality was introduced in the same paper as a generalization of bi-exact groups, particularly to study free ergodic p.m.p actions of such groups and their resulting von Neumann algebras. Since then, proper proximality has become an important property for both groups and von Neumann algebras (for which an analogous definition is given in
[
DKEP23
]
). Properly proximal groups also serve as a large class of non-inner amenable groups and a wide variety of groups have been shown to be properly proximal (see for example
[
DKE24
]
and
[
HHL23
]
).

Corollary C

(Corollary
6.7
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a FSS
∗
group. Then
\Gamma
is properly proximal.

A group
G
is said to be
C^{*}
-simple
if its reduced
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}_{\text{red}}(G)
is simple as a
C^{*}
-algebra, meaning it has no non-trivial two-sided norm closed ideals.
C^{*}
-simplicity is also characterized by the property that all unitary representations weakly contained in the left regular representation are actually weakly equivalent to the left regular representation. In
[
KK17
]
and
[
BKKO17
]
, new characterizations of
C^{*}
-simplicity were given, including if
G
admits some topologically free boundary action. This led to renewed attention and new classes of groups with this property. The
C^{*}
-simplicity of the Thompson groups became an important questions after it was established in
[
HO17
]
and
[
LBMB18
]
that
F
is non-amenable if and only if
F
is
C^{*}
-simple if and only if
T
is
C^{*}
-simple. We do not address that question here, but instead show that a certain class of CSS
∗
groups and their commutator subgroups are
C^{*}
-simple, generalizing Le Boudec and Matte Bon’s result that
V
and
V(G)
are
C^{*}
-simple
[
LBMB18
]
.

Theorem D

(Corollary
7.6
and Corollary
8.9
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group acting on a compact ultrametric space with no finite balls. Then
\Gamma
and its commutator subgroup
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
are
C^{*}
-simple.

By restricting the class of CSS
∗
groups to those whose ultrametric space contains no finite balls, we can guarantee the rigid stabilizer subgroups are all non-amenable and so the result follows from
[
LBMB18
, Corollary 1.3]
. In fact, a compact ultrametric space with no finite balls will be homeomorphic to the Cantor space. By showing the centralizer of
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
in
\Gamma
is trivial, a result of
[
Urs22
]
allows the
C^{*}
-simplicity to pass to the commutator subgroup. The Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}
, the countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups
V_{d}(G)
, and the CSS
∗
groups arising from irreducible subshifts of finite type all satisfy the conditions of Theorem
D
, whereas it was only shown that
V_{2}
and
V_{2}(G)
are
C^{*}
-simple in
[
LBMB18
]
.

For the same subclass of CSS
∗
groups, we also show that the commutator subgroup is simple.

Theorem E

(Corollary
8.4
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group acting on a compact ultrametric space with no finite balls. Then
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
is a simple group.

We prove this by adapting the methods of
[
Mat15
]
and
[
BEH24
]
to the language of compact ultrametric spaces and similarity structure groups. While Theorem
E
recovers Matui’s result about the commutator subgroup of the CSS
∗
group arising from non-periodic irreducible shifts of finite type, it only has partial overlap with the simplicity result from
[
BEH24
]
. In particular, the Brin-Thompson groups can be viewed as homeomorphism groups of the Cantor space but are not CSS groups.

The assumption that
X
contain no finite balls from Theorem
D
and Theorem
E
is necessary. Indeed, a compact ultrametric space with finite balls allows for a nontrivial normal subgroup of
\Gamma
of elements that fix all but finitely many points in
X
(we say such an element has finite support). In Section
8
, we prove that the elements of finite support form a normal amenable subgroup, meaning that Theorem
D
and Theorem
E
will not hold for a general CSS
∗
group and, in particular, fails for
\operatorname{QAut}(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})
from Example
4.15
.

Finally, we prove that CSS
∗
groups are not acylindrically hyperbolic.

Corollary F

(Corollary
8.7
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
. Then
\Gamma
is not acylindrically hyperbolic.

The last corollary is interesting and relevant for a few reasons. First, acylindrically hyperbolic groups form a large and important class of groups studied in geometric group theory, so it is interesting that CSS
∗
groups do not possess this property, yet many of them have some aspects of non-positive curvature. We also note that if these groups were acylindrically hyperbolic, then several of these results would be more or less trivial. Indeed, ICC acylindrically hyperbolic groups are already known to be non-inner amenable and
C^{*}
-simple by
[
DGO17
]
. Moreover, it is conjectured by Osin that acylindrically hyperbolic groups should yield prime factors. We also note that acylindrically hyperbolic groups cannot yield McDuff factors, yet there are many Thompson-like groups yielding McDuff factors (the
F
-like groups from cloning systems we referenced earlier). Hence, Thompson-like groups have a spectrum of somewhat unusual properties, and this shows that new tools, techniques, and ideas are needed to establish results of this type for these groups.

We also note that there is a very general criterion from
[
HHL23
]
for proving groups are properly proximal—namely, showing that a group admits a proper action by isometries on a CAT
(0)
space with a rank 1 element. Hughes’ original result from
[
Hug09
]
showed FSS groups act properly by isometries on a CAT
(0)
space, though the same action is no longer necessarily proper for a general CSS group (see Section
5
). However, Sisto proved in
[
Sis18
]
that every group which acts properly on a CAT
(0)
space with rank 1 elements, is either virtually cyclic or acylindrically hyperbolic. Hence, FSS
∗
groups do not contain rank 1 elements. While neither the result of
[
HHL23
]
nor Theorem
C
proves in full generality that CSS
∗
groups are properly proximal, we suspect it should still be true, however we leave it for future work to settle.

2.
Similarity Structure Groups

A similarity structure group is a certain subgroup of
\text{LS}(X)
, the group of local similarities of some metric space
X
, usually a compact ultrametric space. For example, Thompson’s group
V
acts by homeomorphism on the Cantor set, and more precisely, can be viewed as a subgroup of the local similarity group of the Cantor set. The Cantor set is a compact ultrametric space; one way to see this is to realize it as the end space of the ordered regular rooted binary tree. The direction Hughes took in
[
Hug09
]
to generalize the Thompson groups was to isolate the structure on the Cantor set that produces them and then generalize the structure on the same or different compact ultrametric spaces.

In this section, we review some basic facts about compact ultrametric spaces, define a similarity structure and the FSS groups of Hughes
[
Hug09
]
, and generalize these groups to the larger class of CSS groups. Finally, we introduce CSS
∗
groups, the class of groups that we study in this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is called an
ultrametric space
if

d(x,y)\leq\text{max}\{d(x,z),d(z,y)\}

for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Ultrametric spaces and compact ultrametric spaces, in particular, arise naturally in many different areas of math. The p-adic integers form an ultrametric space, under the metric induced by the
p
-adic norm,
|x|_{p}:=p^{-1\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}\>|\>p^{n}\>\text{divides}\>x\}}
. For this paper, the prototypical example is the end space of a tree. In fact, Hughes proved a categorical equivalence between complete ultrametric spaces and the end space of geodesically complete
\mathbb{R}
-trees
[
Hug04
]
(see the beginning of Section
4
for definitions). Another class of interesting examples that arises in symbolic dynamics is subshifts of finite type, sometimes referred to as topological Markov shifts. These closed sets of infinite sequences also turn out to be the end space of certain
\mathbb{R}
-trees.

Example 2.2

(
[
Hug09
]
, Example 2.2)
.

Let
(T,d)
be a locally finite simplicial tree equipped with the natural unique metric, making
(T,d)
into an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. The distance between two vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
is the shortest path of edges connecting the two. More precisely, it is the minimum number of edges in a sequences
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
with
v_{1}\in e_{0}
,
v_{2}\in e_{n}
and
e_{i}\cap e_{i+1}\neq\emptyset
for
0\leq i\leq n-1
. Given a root, or base vertex,
v\in T
the
end space
of
(T,v)
is defined by

\text{end}(T,v)=\{x:[0,\infty)\rightarrow T\>|\>x(0)=v\>\text{and}\>x\>\text{is an isometric embedding}\}.

For any
x,y\in
end
(T,v)
define the following metric

d_{e}(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\>x=y,\\
1/e^{t_{0}}&\text{if}\>x\neq y\>\text{and}\ t_{0}=\sup\{t\geq 0\>|\>x(t)=y(t)\}\end{cases}

which makes
(\text{end}(T,v),d_{e})
into a compact ultrametric space with diameter less than one.

The following are standard facts about ultrametric spaces, which appear in this form in
[
FH15
, Lemma 3.2]
for instance.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be an ultrametric space.

(1)

Given two open balls
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
in
X
, then either one contains the other, or the balls are disjoint.

(2)

Every open ball in
X
is a closed set, and every closed ball in
X
is an open set.

(3)

Let
B_{r}(x)
be an open ball in
X
. If
y\in B_{r}(x)
, then
B_{r}(x)=B_{r}(y)
.

(4)

If
X
is compact, then each open ball
B
is contained in at most finitely many distinct open balls of
X
.

(5)

If
X
is compact, then each open ball in
X
is a closed ball and each closed ball is an open ball.

(6)

If
X
is compact and
x
is not an isolated point, then each open ball
B_{r}(x)
is partitioned by its maximal proper open subballs, of which there are only finitely many.

Convention
.

As a convention, throughout this paper, if
(X,d)
is a compact ultrametric space, we will assume every ball is closed, and properly contained in
X
, unless specified otherwise.

Before we introduce similarity structures on metric spaces, and the associated maximal subgroup determined by the similarity structure, let us define some basic terms.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces.

(1)

For
\lambda>0
, a map
f:X\to Y
is called a
\lambda
-similarity
provided
d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))=\lambda d_{X}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
. A map between metric spaces is simply called a similarity if there exists such a constant.

(2)

A homeomorphism
f:X\to Y
is a
local similarity
if for every
x\in X
, there exist constants
r,\lambda>0
such that the restriction
f|_{B(x,r)}:B(x,r)\to B(f(x),\lambda r)
is a surjective
\lambda
-similarity.

(3)

Let LS
(X)
be the
group of all local similarities from X onto X
.

We can now define a similarity structure on a metric space.

Definition 2.5

(
[
Hug09
]
)
.

A
similarity structure
for
X
is a function, Sim, that assigns to each pair
B_{1},B_{2}
of closed balls in
X
a (possible empty) set
\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
of surjective similarities
B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}
such that whenever
B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
are closed balls in
X
, the following properties hold:

(1)

(Identities) id
{}_{B_{1}}\in

\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{1})
.

(2)

(Inverses) If
h\in

\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
, then
h^{-1}\in

\text{Sim}(B_{2},B_{1})
.

(3)

(Composition) If
h_{1}\in

\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
and
h_{2}\in

\text{Sim}(B_{2},B_{3})
, then the composition
h_{2}h_{1}\in\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{3})

(4)

(Restrictions) If
h\in\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
and
B_{3}\subseteq B_{1}
, then
h|_{B_{3}}\in\text{Sim}(B_{3},h(B_{3}))
.

If the set
\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
is always finite, we call this a
finite similarity structure
. If instead
\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
is countable, we call this a
countable similarity structure
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
B
be a closed ball in a metric space
(X,d)
endowed with a similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X}
. An embedding
h:B\to X
is
locally determined
by
\text{Sim}_{X}
provided for any
x\in B
, there exists a closed ball
B^{\prime}
in
B
such that
x\in B^{\prime}
,
h(B^{\prime})
is a closed ball in
X
, and
h|_{B^{\prime}}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B^{\prime},h(B^{\prime}))
.

Given a similarity structure on a metric space, we can associate a natural group to it whose elements are determined by the similarity structure in the above sense.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact ultrametric space, and let
\text{Sim}_{X}
be a given similarity structure. The group
\Gamma:=\Gamma(\text{Sim}_{X})
associated to
\text{Sim}_{X}
is the set of all homeomorphisms of
X
locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
. We call
\Gamma
the similarity structure group.

(1)

If
\text{Sim}_{X}
is a finite similarity structure for
X
, then we call
\Gamma
a FSS group.

(2)

If
\text{Sim}_{X}
is a countable similarity structure for
X
, then we call
\Gamma
a CSS group.

In either case,
\Gamma
is the unique largest subgroup of LS
(X)
locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
(see
[
Hug09
, Proposition 3.5]
).

In this paper, we consider a natural class of CSS groups for which our main results hold. In particular, our results hold for CSS groups, whose compact ultrametric space possesses a local “self-similar” property. That is, given any proper ball in
X
and there will be infinitely many balls homeomorphic to it. We call this class of groups CSS
∗
groups, which we now define precisely.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X
be an infinite compact ultrametric space and
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
a CSS or FSS group. If the following two conditions hold, we say
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
or FSS
∗
group, respectively. Notice that FSS
{}^{*}\subset
CSS
∗
.

(1)

Any infinite ball in
X
has at least two disjoint infinite subballs.

(2)

For any proper closed ball
B
and infinite proper closed ball
D
in
X
, there exists a subball
D_{0}\subseteq D
such that
\text{Sim}(B,D_{0})
is non-empty.

In Section
4
we prove the Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}
, countable Röver- Nekrashevych groups, and some braided diagram groups give rise to FSS
∗
or CSS
∗
groups. We also show there is a natural similarity structure that can be placed on a large class of irreducible subshifts of finite type so that we can consider FSS
∗
groups inside the group of local similarities. We encourage the reader to consult
[
Hug09
]
or visit Section
4
if they are not familiar with FSS groups.

Lemma 3.3

The next lemma is an important tool for constructing elements

\Gamma
from local similarities. In practice, the pasting lemma is often paired with Proposition

and the definition of CSS

∗

Lemma 3.7

(Pasting Lemma)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS group and let
\{B_{1},...,B_{n}\}
and
\{\widetilde{B}_{1},...,\widetilde{B}_{n}\}
be two partitions of
X
such that
\text{Sim}(B_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i})
contains a similarity
f_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Then the element
f:X\to X
defined by
f(x)=f_{i}(x)
if
x\in B_{i}
belongs to
\Gamma
.

Proof.

First, the mapping
x\mapsto f_{i}^{-1}(x)
for
x\in\widetilde{B}_{i}
, where
f_{i}^{-1}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(\widetilde{B}_{i},B_{i})
, is clearly the inverse
f
and is of the same form as
f
itself. A simple exercise shows that
f
is continuous, and hence
f^{-1}
is also continuous. Hence,
f
is indeed a homeomorphism. Now, let us prove that
f
is locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
.

Let
x\in X
. Since the
B_{i}
partition
X
, there exists a unique
i=1,\dots,n
such that
x\in B_{i}
. Because
f_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i})
, there exists
\lambda>0
such that
f_{i}:B_{i}\to\widetilde{B}_{i}
is a surjective
\lambda
-similarity. Because
B_{i}
and
\widetilde{B}_{i}
are closed balls containing
x
and
f_{i}(x)
, respectively, we can choose
r_{i},\delta_{i}>0
such that
B_{i}=\overline{B}(x,r_{i})
and
\widetilde{B}_{i}=\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\delta_{i})
. Choose
r>0
such that
r<r_{i}
and
\lambda r<\delta_{i}
, which will ensure that
\overline{B}(x,r)\subseteq B_{i}
and
\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)\subseteq\widetilde{B}_{i}
. We claim that
f_{i}(\overline{B}(x,r))=\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)
.

First, given
y\in\overline{B}(x,r)
, observe that

d(f_{i}x,f_{i}y)=\lambda d(x,y)\leq\lambda r,

which proves that
f_{i}(\overline{B}(x,r))\subseteq\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)
. Now given
z\in\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)
, by surjectivity of
f_{i}
, there exists
y\in B_{i}
such that
z=f_{i}y
. We want to show more precisely that
y\in\overline{B}(x,r)
. But observe

d(x,y)=\frac{1}{\lambda}d(f_{i}x,f_{i}y)=\frac{1}{\lambda}d(f_{i}x,z)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\lambda r=r,

which proves that
\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)\subseteq f_{i}(\overline{B}(x,r))
.

Finally, by the restriction property of Sim, we have that

f_{i}|\in\text{Sim}(\overline{B}(x,r),f_{i}(\overline{B}(x,r))=\text{Sim}(\overline{B}(x,r),\overline{B}(f_{i}x,\lambda r)).

But
f
and
f_{i}
equal when restricted to
\overline{B}(x,r)\subseteq B_{i}
, so

f|\in\text{Sim}(\overline{B}(x,r),\overline{B}(f(x),\lambda r)),

which proves that
f\in\Gamma
.

∎

The converse of the pasting lemma is also true and appeared first in the work of Farley and Hughes
[
HF17
, Proposition 3.11]
.

The following corollary is a special case of the extension property that works for finite balls. We isolate it because it sometimes is enough to prove the results we need.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Let
B
be an arbitrary ball in
X
and
D
an infinite ball in
X
disjoint from
B
. Then there exists a ball
D_{0}\subseteq D
and an involution
h\in\Gamma
such that
h(B)=D_{0}
and
h(D_{0})=B
.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group, there exists
D_{0}\subseteq D
, such that
\text{Sim}_{X}(B,D_{0})
is non-empty. Since
X\setminus(B\cup D_{0})
compact, there exists closed balls
B_{1},\dots B_{n}
such that
\{B,D_{0},B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
partition
X
. Let
f\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B,D_{0})
and, therefore,
f^{-1}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(D_{0},B)
. Let
f_{i}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B_{i},B_{i})
be the identity. Define
h:X\to X
by

h(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&x\in B\\
f^{-1}(x),&x\in D_{0}\\
f_{i}(x),&x\in B_{i}\\
\end{cases}

By the pasting lemma (Lemma
3.7
), we know that
h
is a surjective similarity locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
, meaning that
h\in\Gamma
, and clearly
h(B)=D_{0}
and
h(D_{0})=B
.
∎

The following proposition proves that if
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group, then given some number of local similarities between infinite balls determined by the similarity structure corresponding to
\Gamma
, one can always recover an element of
\Gamma
that agrees with these local similarities.

Proposition 3.9

(Extension Property)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and let
S_{1}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
and
S_{2}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\}
be two sets of infinite balls in
X
that are respectively pairwise disjoint. Assume that neither set is a complete partition of
X
. Suppose that there exists
h_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{i},D_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Then there exists an element
g
in
\Gamma
such that
g|_{B_{i}}=h_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

If
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset
, then we can simply take a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
X
such that
S_{1},S_{2}\subset\mathcal{P}
and use the Pasting Lemma to define
g
of
\Gamma
satisfying the proposition to be

g(x)=\begin{cases}h_{i}(x)&x\in B_{i}\\
h_{i}^{-1}(x)&x\in D_{i}\\
x&x\in X\backslash\{S_{1},S_{2}\}\end{cases}.

Note that since
X\backslash\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
is still a compact ultrametric, there exists a partition of
X\backslash\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
into closed balls where we are simply taking the identity similarity as is done in Corollary
3.8
. This property is used repeatedly throughout, and we do not write down partitions where the identity similarity is being used.

When
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\neq\emptyset
, the general process to construct a
g\in\Gamma
such that
g|_{B_{i}}=h_{i}
, is as follows. Let
B_{i_{1}}\cap D_{j_{1}}\neq\emptyset
,
i_{1},j_{1}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, be the first non-empty intersection. If
B_{i_{i}}\subset D_{j_{1}}
, choose a partition
\{B_{i_{1}},\tilde{B}_{1}\dots,\tilde{B}_{r}\}
of
D_{j_{1}}
and choose a ball
A_{1}
such that
A_{1}\cap\{S_{1},S_{2}\}=\emptyset
. Since
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group, there exists
\tilde{A}_{1}\subseteq A_{1}
such that there is a non-trivial
f_{1}\in\text{Sim}(D_{j_{1}}\tilde{A}_{1})
. Then, using the pasting lemma, construct an element
g\in\Gamma
such that
g=h_{i_{1}}
on
B_{i_{1}}
,
g=f|_{\tilde{B}_{i}}
on
\tilde{B}_{i}
and
f_{1}|_{B_{i_{1}}}h^{-1}_{i_{1}}f^{-1}_{1}
on
\tilde{A}_{1}
. If there are no other intersections, stop and extend
g
to be an element on
\Gamma
, by letting
g
be the identity on
X\setminus\{S_{1},S_{2},\tilde{A}\}
. Otherwise, proceed to
B_{i_{2}}\cap D_{j_{2}}\neq\emptyset
and repeat step 1, noting that since
B_{i_{2}}=B_{i_{1}}
and
D_{j_{2}}=D_{j_{1}}
is possible, as well as different combinations of inclusion
D_{j_{2}}\subset B_{j_{2}}
, we explicitly write out the specific cases that will contribute to building
g
for an arbitrary amount and types of intersections between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Note that there are only
n
types of intersections possible that go into the construction of
g
.

By considering the case where
S_{1}=\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
and
S_{2}=\{D_{1},D_{2}\}
, we can exhibit all of the different ways to construct

depending on how
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
intersect. Thus, we consider the following cases.

Case 1
: Suppose that
B_{1}\subseteq D_{1}
and
B_{2}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset
. If
B_{1}=D_{1}
, this is is still covered by the disjoint setting with
h_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{1},D_{1})
being the identity, so suppose
B_{1}\subset D_{1}
is a proper inclusion. Let
\{B_{1},\tilde{B}_{1},\dots\tilde{B}_{r}\}
be a partition of
D_{1}
into balls that includes
B_{1}
and pick a ball

disjoint from both

and
S_{2}
. Since
\Gamma
is a CSS

group, there exists a subball

is non-empty. Pick

in

. By the restriction property, we have that

and

4.
Examples of FSS
∗
and CSS
∗
Groups

In this section, we introduce examples of FSS
∗
and CSS
∗
groups as well as natural FSS groups that are not FSS
∗
. Apart from the groups arising from irreducible subshifts of finite type, all of these examples were first identified as FSS groups in the work of Farley and Hughes
[
Hug09
]
,
[
HF17
]
.

4.1.
From d-ary trees

For each non-empty finite set
A
, or
alphabet
as we will call it, one can associate an infinite
d
-ary tree where
d=|A|
. We call any ordered sequence of elements of
A
a
word
. Let
A^{*}
be the set of all finite words in the alphabet
A
and let
A^{\omega}
be the subset of
A^{*}
of all infinite words. Let
\mathcal{T}_{A}
be the tree associated to
A
, where the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{A}
are all the finite words in
A
. Notice that two words are connected by an edge if one of the words contains the other. More precisely, if
v
and
w
are words in
A
, then there is an edge
\mathcal{T}_{A}
connecting
v
and
w
if and only if
w=vx
or
v=wx
for some other word
x
in
A
. Letting the empty word be the root, we can see
A^{\omega}=
end
(\mathcal{T}_{A},\emptyset)
is a compact ultrametric space under the metric described in Example
2.2
. The induced total order on
A^{\omega}
is the usual lexicographic order.
Metric balls in
A^{\omega}
are of the form
wA^{\omega}
, where
w
is a word in
A^{*}
. Balls can be thought of as sets of words that all have the same prefix up to a certain length.

More generally, under this same metric one can define compact ultrametric spaces from the end space of
\mathbb{R}
-trees. In
fact, Hughes proved in
[
Hug04
]
that there is a categorical equivalence between complete ultrametric spaces and the end
space of geodesically complete
\mathbb{R}
-trees. An
\mathbb{R}
-
tree
is a metric space
(T,d)
where any two points
x,y\in T
are uniquely arcwise connected, meaning there exists a unique path from
x
to
y
isometric to the subinterval
[0,d(x,y)]
of
\mathbb{R}
. A rooted tree
(T,v)
is
geodesically complete
, if every vertex, except possibly the root, lies in at least two edges. More precisely, every isometric embedding
x:[0,t]\rightarrow T
,
t\geq 0
such that
x(0)=v
extends to an isometric embedding
\tilde{x}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow T
. In other words, there are no dead ends in the tree. See
[
Hug12
, Section 5]
for more on the discussion of
\mathbb{R}
-trees in this context.

Example 4.1

(Higman-Thompson Groups
V_{d}
)
.

Let
A
be an alphabet with
|A|=d
, and let
A^{\omega}
, as defined above, be our compact ultrametric space. Consider
\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(A^{\omega})\leq\text{LS}(A^{\omega})
to be the group of all local order-preserving similarities. That is,
h\in\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(A^{\omega})
if for every
x\in A^{\omega}
, there exists a ball
B\subset A^{\omega}
such that
h|_{B}
is an order preserving similarity from
B
to
h(B)
.
Notice that every ball in
A^{\omega}
is of the form
wA^{\omega}
for some word

, and moreover,
wA^{\omega}=\text{end}(\mathcal{T}_{A},w)
. In particular,
(\mathcal{T}_{A},w)
and
(\mathcal{T}_{A},\emptyset)
are homeomorphic trees, and thus, there is a unique order-preserving similarity from any ball onto
A^{\omega}
. Therefore, between any two balls in
A^{\omega}
there exists a unique order-preserving similarity. Thus, we define the similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{A^{\omega}}
such that
g\in\text{Sim}(wA^{\omega},vA^{\omega})
if and only if

is the unique order-preserving similarity

. This similarity structure exactly produces
\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(A^{\omega})
as the corresponding FSS group and is isomorphic to the Higman-Thompson group
V_{d}
.

Observation 4.2
.

The Higman-Thompson group
V_{d}
is FSS
∗
group.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the previous example. Indeed, every ball in
V_{d}
is infinite and contains at least two disjoint balls (in fact they contain arbitrarily many). For instance, if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are distinct in
A
, and
wA^{\omega}
is a ball in
A^{\omega}
, then
wu_{1}A^{\omega}
and
wu_{2}A^{\omega}
are two disjoint balls in
wA^{\omega}
. Since any two balls in
A^{\omega}
have a surjective similarity between them
\text{Sim}(wA^{\omega},vA^{\omega})
is non-empty for any two balls
wA^{\omega}
and
vA^{\omega}
in
A^{\omega}
. Therefore,
V_{d}\cong\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(A^{\omega})
is an FSS
∗
group.
∎

Example 4.3

(Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}
and Generalizations of
\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(X)
)
.

For any rooted, ordered, proper
\mathbb{R}
-tree
(T,v)
, we can consider the compact ultrametric space

and the group
\text{LS}_{\text{lop}}(X)\leq\text{LS}(X)
. The similarity structure is not as uniform as in the case where

. The balls in

are of the form
\text{end}(T,w)
for some vertex

on

. However, there only exists an order-preserving similarity between two balls

and

if

and

are homeomorphic as trees. This is no longer a given for a general ordered, proper

-tree, like it is for the

-ary tree
(\mathcal{T}_{A},\emptyset)
. Thus, whenever
(T,w_{1})
and
(T,w_{2})
are not isomorphic, we set

to be empty, and when they are isomorphic we take it to be the unique order-preserving similarity between the two balls. With that adjustment made,

is an FSS group, as in the previous example.

For a particular example, we see that the Higman-Thompson groups

for
d
and

\mathbb{R}
-tree

. The tree can be described as follows. Fix a root

and connect

r

r
edges, attach a

-ary tree and define

to be the resulting tree. It follows that

is an FSS group in

. We can also conclude

is an FSS

is, given that every subtree of

-ary tree.

In general, the branching pattern of a rooted, ordered, proper

-tree can be quite complicated, so

should realize a vast variety of groups depending on the tree you start with.

Remark 4.4
.

In
[
Hug09
]
, Hughes actually studied a larger class of groups than FSS groups, which he called “locally finitely determined groups of local similarities on a compact ultrametric space.” In particular, such a group can be understood as any subgroup of

. What this means is that the Higman-Thompson groups

and

are also locally finitely determined groups of local similarities but are not FSS groups because they appear as subgroups of

Example 4.5

(Röver-Nekrashevych groups

)
.

The Röver-Nekrashevych groups

were first defined and studied by Scott in
[

the corresponding

between any two closed balls in

is a homogeneous space. Now since

, it can be viewed as a self-similar subtree of

, and any

induces a surjective similarity between

In

, Hughes requires

is now countable and not finite. In fact, the countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups were the motivation for us to define CSS groups.

We also note that if

from this similarity structure as well. In particular, every self-similar subgroup

Part of the motivation for Hughes to define FSS groups instead of CSS groups in

We also remark that we can only prove

4.2.

In this section, we describe how one can define an FSS group arising from the compact ultrametric spaces that – together with the shift transformation — define the dynamical systems known as a subshift of finite type. We start by recalling the definition of a subshift of finite type and direct the reader to

Consider a finite alphabet

is homeomorphic to

We now define the subshifts of finite type. Let
B
be an

with entry values either

B_{x_{i}x_{i+1}}=1
, meaning the sequence is completely determined by the matrix
B
. We can also view

as an adjacency matrix of a direct graph
(G_{B},V)
, where the vertices

agree with the symbols

. If every directed path on

is infinite, then we say the corresponding subspace
X_{B}
of

is a
one-side subshift of finite type
. The matrix

is usually called the transition matrix for
X_{B}
and since
X_{B}
is a closed subspace of
X_{n}
it is also a compact ultrametric space under the same metric. Viewing
X_{B}
, as a subspace of
\text{end}(\mathcal{T}_{A},\emptyset)
, we can see it corresponds to the end space of a subtree of
(\mathcal{T}_{A},\emptyset)
of starting from the same root. Call this subtree
(\mathcal{T}_{B},\emptyset)
.

To define the similarity structure on
X_{B}
, note that
\mathcal{T}_{B}
still has the lexicographic order inherited from
\mathcal{T}_{A}
, which induces an order on
X_{B}
. Therefore, every vertex in
\mathcal{T}_{B}
corresponds to a word which is a finite prefix to an infinite sequence in
X_{B}
. We call this set of words

. Every ball in
X_{B}
is of the form
wX_{B}
, with
w\in A^{*}_{B}
. For any symbol

, we define its

as the set of symbols that are allowed to follow

according to the transition matrix B. Now, given two balls
wX_{B}
and
vX_{B}
, with
|w|=n
and
|v|=m
, write
w=w^{\prime}u
and

, where
w^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in A^{*}_{B}
with
|w^{\prime}|=n-1
,
|v^{\prime}|=m-1
and
u,u^{\prime}
symbols in

. If

and

have the same follower set, then the subtrees of
(\mathcal{T}_{B},\emptyset)

(\mathcal{T}_{B},w)
and
(\mathcal{T}_{B},v)

, which is a surjective similarity. We then set
\text{Sim}(wX_{B},vX_{B})=\{\hat{h}\}

and

.

Now that we have an FSS group

, the goal is to understand when

will be an FSS
∗
groups. The obvious place to look is the well-studied irreducible subshift of finite type. We say the transition matrix

for some positive integer

is the

\ell

It is a well-known fact that an irreducible subshift of finite type

, defined on a finite alphabet, is either one periodic orbit (and hence finite) or homeomorphic to the Cantor set. Therefore, to rule out the periodic case, it suffices to consider irreducible subshifts of finite type where at least one symbol has two other symbols that can immediately follow it. We call such a symbol a

.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
X_{B}
be an irreducible one-sided subshift of finite type on a finite set of symbols
A
. Assume there exists at least one 2-followed symbol. Let
\text{Sim}_{X_{B}}
be the similarity structure defined above. Then
\Gamma(\text{Sim}_{X_{B}})\leq\text{LS}(X_{B})
is an FSS
∗
group.

Proof.

Since every ball in
X_{B}
is of the form
wX_{B}
for some
w\in A^{*}_{B}
, every ball is infinite. Now because
G_{B}
is strongly connected, any two symbols have a path connecting them. Let
u
be the symbol in
A
that is 2-followed by, say,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in
A
. For any ball
wX_{B}
in
X_{B}
, pass to a subball
w^{\prime}X_{B}
, such that
w^{\prime}=vu
for some
v\in A^{*}_{B}
. Note that
w^{\prime}X_{B}\subseteq wX_{B}
, means
|w^{\prime}|\geq|w|
and

is a subword of
w^{\prime}
. Then
w^{\prime}X_{B}
contains two disjoint infinite balls,
w^{\prime}u_{1}X_{B}
and
w^{\prime}u_{2}X_{B}
. Therefore,
\Gamma
satisfies the first condition of being an FSS
∗
group.

To show
\Gamma
satisfies the second FSS
∗
condition, consider two balls
wX_{B}
and
vX_{B}
. Write
w=w^{\prime}u
, so we know what the last symbol of

is. If
v=v^{\prime}u
for some

, then by the definition of the similarity structure above we know
\text{Sim}(wX_{B},vX_{B})
is non-empty. If

is not of this form, we note that there exists some word of the form
v\ell u\in A^{*}_{B}
for some
\ell\in A^{*}_{B}
, because

is strongly connected. Therefore,
v\ell uX_{B}
is a subball of
vX_{B}
such that
\text{Sim}(wX_{B},v\ell uX_{B})
is non-empty.

4.3.
Braided Diagram Groups

In
[
HF17
, Theorem 4.12]
, Farley and Hughes proved that if
D_{b}(\mathcal{P},x)
is a braided diagram group with a tree-like semigroup presentation
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma|\mathcal{R}\rangle
, there exists a compact ultrametric space
X_{\mathcal{P}}
and a finite similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}
such that
D_{b}(\mathcal{P},x)
is isomorphic to the FSS group
\Gamma(\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}})
. In particular, they proved that the Houghton groups
H_{n}
and
\text{QAut}(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})
, a particular group of quasi-automorphisms of the infinite binary tree, are FSS groups by first showing that they are braided diagram groups coming from a tree-like semigroup presentation. Given that the Houghton groups are elementary amenable (see
[
Lee12
]
),
H_{n}
provides an example of FSS groups that are not in FSS
∗
. On the other hand, we prove in Example
4.15
that
\text{QAut}(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})
is an FSS
∗
group.

We briefly recall braided diagram groups and the general construction of Farley and Hughes, which associates a compact ultrametric space to a tree-like semigroup presentation.

Given an alphabet
\Sigma
, the
free semigroup

\Sigma^{+}
, is the set of all non-empty strings of elements of
\Sigma
. A
semigroup presentation
,
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma|\mathcal{R}\rangle
, is the alphabet
\Sigma
, together with the set of relations
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\Sigma^{+}\times\Sigma^{+}
. We write
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
instead of equality, because there will be need to distinguish the left and right side.

We avoid actually defining a braided diagram group and refer to
[
HF17
]
for a precise definition and
[
GS97
]
for a more general background on diagram and braided diagram groups. However, it should be emphasized that braided diagram groups do not contain braids in the intuitive sense and have little to do with actual braid groups. Instead, we recall how Farley and Hughes constructed a rooted tree (and thus a compact ultrametric space) from a tree-like semigroup presentation.

Definition 4.10

(
[
HF17
]
)
.

A semigroup presentation
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma,\mathcal{R}\rangle
is said to be
tree-like
if

(1)

every
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
is such that
|w_{1}|=1
and
|w_{2}|>1
;

(2)

if
(a,w_{1}),(a,w_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
, then
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are equal as reduced words.

The goal now is to construct the compact ultrametric space that a braided diagram group with a tree-like semigroup presentation is the FSS group for. To that end, we recall the following construction that appears in the proof of
[
HF17
, Theorem 4.12]
(Proposition
4.12
).

Let
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma|\mathcal{R}\rangle
be a tree-like semigroup presentation and
x\in\Sigma
. Set this
x
to be the root vertex of the tree and, by the definition of a tree-like semigroup presentation, there is at most one relation where
x
is on the left side of the relation. If
x
is not the left side of any relation, the tree is just a single vertex. Assume then that we have
(x,x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{k})
in
\mathcal{R}
,
k\geq 2
. Construct
k
edges coming down from the vertex labeled
x
, and label each edge with
x_{1}
to
x_{k}
, so that if you read across the edges from left to right, you will see the word
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{k}
. Now whenever
x_{i}
is not the left side of any relation, that edge becomes a dead end in the tree. If instead we have
(x_{i},y_{1}\dots y_{n})
in
\mathcal{R}
for
n\geq 2
, draw
n
edges coming down from
x_{i}
and, as before, label them
y_{1}
to
y_{k}
. Repeat this process indefinitely or until every branch of the tree terminates. See figures
2
and
3
for examples.

Now let Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
be the set of all paths
p
along the edges in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)}
, as defined above, that start at the root and do not backtrack. Notice that
p
is either infinite or terminates at a dead end in the tree (a vertex with no children). Given
p,p^{\prime}
in Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
that have exactly
m
edges in common, define a metric on Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
by setting
d(p,p^{\prime})=e^{-m}
. This makes Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
into a compact ultrametric space. Notice, this is similar to the end space of an infinite rooted tree however, it is not the same since Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
can have finite paths caused by dead ends in the tree (see Remark
4.11
). The balls in Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
are labeled by a vertex in
\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)}
and given by
B_{v}=\{p\in\text{Ends}(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)}):v\;\text{lies on}\>p\}
. Notice that if the vertex
v
has no children, then
B_{v}
is actually a finite set.

The finite similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}
on Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
is then defined as follows. For any two balls
B_{v}
and
B_{v^{\prime}}
in Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
, set
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}(B_{v},B_{v^{\prime}})=0
if
v
and
v^{\prime}
have different labels from
\Sigma
. If
v
and
v^{\prime}
have the same label
y\in\Sigma
, then there is a label- and order- preserving isomorphism
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{v}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{v^{\prime}}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{v}
and
\mathcal{T}_{v^{\prime}}
are subtrees of
\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)}
that start a the vertex
v
and
v^{\prime}
respectively. Note that any
p\in B_{v}
is of the form
p_{v}q
, where
p_{v}
is the unique non-backtracking path in
\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)}
from the root to
v
and
q
is a non-backtracking path in
\mathcal{T}_{v}
. Then define
\hat{\psi}:B_{v}\rightarrow B_{v^{\prime}}
by
\hat{\psi}(p_{v}q)=p_{v^{\prime}}\psi(q)
, which is a surjective similarity. Therefore, we set
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}(B_{v},B_{v^{\prime}})=\{\hat{\psi}\}
to obtain a finite similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}
on Ends
(\mathcal{T}_{(\mathcal{P},x)})
. The similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}
is further called

because
|\text{Sim}_{X_{\mathcal{P}}}(B_{v},B_{v^{\prime}})|\leq 1
for all balls
B_{v}
and
B_{v^{\prime}}
in

.

Remark 4.11
.

For an infinite rooted tree
(\mathcal{T},v)
the end space

requires all non-backtracking paths to be infinite, while

does not. In particular, the way Ends

is defined (which is how it is defined in

trivially relates to itself. What this means is that instead of having a dead end at

5.
An Unbounded Cocycle for CSS
∗
groups

In this section, we prove that CSS groups admit unbounded cocycles by generalizing Hughes’ result from
[
Hug09
]
, and more specifically, prove that CSS
∗
groups admit an unbounded cocycle into a quasi-regular representation.

Recall that for a discrete group
\Gamma
, a 1-cocycle is a continuous map
b:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
into a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, together with a unitary representation
\pi:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
, such that for all
g,h\in\Gamma
,
b
satisfies the cocycle identity:
b(gh)=b(g)+\pi(g)b(h)
. When we refer to a 1-cocycle or cocycle, we are referring to the triple
(b,\pi,\mathcal{H})
. A 1-cocycle is said to be
proper
if the set
\{g\in\Gamma:\|b(g)\|\leq r\}
is compact for every
r>0
. One of the many equivalent definitions for a group
\Gamma
to have the
Haagerup property
, is if it admits a proper 1-cocycle
b
. In
[
CCJ+01
, Proposition 7.5.1]
, Valette also shows that if a countable, discrete group
\Gamma
admits a zipper action, it also admits a proper 1-cocycle. We say
\Gamma
admits a
zipper action
if there exists a left action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
on a set
\mathcal{E}
and a subset
Z\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that for every
g\in\Gamma
,
|gZ\triangle Z|
is finite, and for every
r>0
, the set
\{g\in\Gamma|\>|gZ\triangle Z|\leq r\}
is finite.

For our use, we are interested in groups that admit unbounded 1-cocycles. A cocycle is
unbounded
if there exists a sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
such that
\|b(g_{n})\|\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Notice that a proper cocycle is unbounded, but an unbounded cocycle need not be proper. To that end, we relax the conditions of a zipper action and arrive at the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

A discrete group
\Gamma
has a
generalized zipper action
if there exists a left action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
on a set
\mathcal{E}
and an infinite subset
Z\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that for every
g\in\Gamma
, the symmetric difference
gZ\triangle Z
is finite.

Valette’s result still holds if we consider a generalized zipper action and a cocycle that is not necessarily proper.

Proposition 5.2
.

If a discrete group
\Gamma
admits a generalized zipper action, then
\Gamma
admits a 1-cocycle
(b,\pi,\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E}))
.

Proof.

Assume
\Gamma
admits a generalized zipper action and define
b:\Gamma\rightarrow\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{E})
by
b(g)=\chi_{gZ}-\chi_{Z}
. Note that the support of
b(g)
is
gZ\triangle Z
, so
b(g)
is finitely supported and thus
b(g)
is more precisely in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
for all
g\in\Gamma
. The action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
induces a unitary left representation
\Gamma
on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
,
\pi:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})),
given by
\pi(g)\delta_{E}=\delta_{gE}
, for all
g\in\Gamma
and
\delta_{E}
a basis vector for
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. One can then check that
b
satisfies the cocycle condition for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in\Gamma

b(g_{1}g_{2})=\pi(g_{1})b(g_{2})+b(g_{1})
.

∎

The idea now is to show CSS groups have a generalized zipper action and moreover, the resulting cocycle is unbounded.

Let
\Gamma
be a CSS group and let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of equivalences classes of pairs
(f,B)
where
B
is a closed ball
X
and
f:B\rightarrow X
is a local similarity embedding determined by Sim. Two pairs
(f_{1},B_{1})
and
(f_{2},B_{2})
are equivalent, whenever their exists
h\in\text{Sim}(B_{1},B_{2})
such that
f_{2}h=f_{1}
. So equivalences implies
f_{1}(B_{1})=f_{2}(B_{2})
. Denote the equivalence classes by
[f,B]
.

Define a subset of
\mathcal{E}
to be
Z=\{[f,B]\in\mathcal{E}:f(B)
is a closed ball in
X
and
f\in\text{Sim}(B,f(B))\}
. Alternatively,
Z=\{[\text{incl}_{B},B]\in\mathcal{E}:B
is a closed ball in
X\}
. Intuitively,
Z
is the set of pairs
[f,B]
where
f(B)
is a single closed ball. For a general element
[f,B]
in
\mathcal{E}
,
f(B)
is only guaranteed to be a finite union of closed balls.

In
[
Hug09
, Theorem 6.1]
, Hughes proved the that every FSS group has a zipper action, and thus a proper 1-cocycle defined as,
b:\Gamma\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
where
b(g)=\chi_{gZ}-\chi_{Z}
for all
g\in\Gamma
. To prove our main results about CSS
∗
groups, we need to show that the set of equivalence classes
\mathcal{E}
can be interpreted differently.

The group
\Gamma
acts naturally on the set
\mathcal{E}
defined by
g\cdot[f,B]=[gf,B]
for all
g\in\Gamma
, where the composition and restriction properties of the similarity structure guarantee
[gf,B]\in\mathcal{E}
. For each
[f,B]\in\mathcal{E}
, recall that we can define the stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{U}
for any closed set
U
in
X
, thus
\Gamma_{B}
, makes sense for any closed balls. Now, the orbits of
\Gamma
acting on
\mathcal{E}
,
\Gamma\cdot[f,B]=\{g\cdot[f,B]:g\in\Gamma\}
can actually all be written as
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
for some ball
B
in
X
, given that
[f,B]=f\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
. Then, by the orbit-stabilizer theorem, we know
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
is in one-to-one correspondence with the coset
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}
. Therefore, we can decompose
\mathcal{E}
as,

\mathcal{E}=\bigsqcup\limits_{B\subset X}\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\leftrightarrow\bigsqcup\limits_{B\subset X}\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}.

We also observe that it is not actually a union over every ball in
X
because many elements of
\mathcal{E}
can have the same orbit. In fact, for
\Gamma=V_{d}
, there is only one orbit, and the description of
\mathcal{E}
completely agrees with
V_{d}/V_{[0,1.d)}
from
[
Far03
]
. The set
Z\subset\mathcal{E}
can also be written as a disjoint union. Define

Z_{B_{0}}=\{[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\in\mathcal{E}\cap\big(\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B_{0}},B_{0}]\big):B\>\text{is a closed ball in}\>X\}.

So
Z_{B_{0}}\subset Z
and we have the useful identification,

Z_{B_{0}}\cong\{f\Gamma_{B_{0}}:f\in\Gamma\>\text{and}\>f(B_{0})\>\text{is a closed ball}\}\subset\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{0}}.

Moreover, it follows that
Z=\sqcup_{B\subset X}Z_{B}
, where the disjoint union is still determined by the orbits
\Gamma\cdot[f,B]
.

The following theorem is only a slight generalization of
[
Hug09
, Theorem 6.1]
. By moving from FSS to CSS groups,
\text{Sim}(B,D)
can now be infinite for
B
and
D
balls in
X
. However, the only part of Hughes’ proof that changes is that the zipper action is no longer metrically proper. We prove Hughes’ result in our context, essentially reusing Lemma 6.2 and Lemma 6.3 from
[
Hug09
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(c.f.
[
Hug09
]
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq LS(X)
be a CSS group. Then
\Gamma
has a generalized zipper action.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\leq LS(X)
be a CSS group and consider
\mathcal{E}
the set of equivalence classes of the form
[f,B]
, defined earlier in the section. Take as the zipper set
Z=\{[\text{incl}_{B},B]\in\mathcal{E}:B
is a closed ball in
X\}
, also defined earlier. Since it is clear
Z
is infinite, showing
gZ\triangle Z
is finite is enough to conclude the natural left action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
defined by
g\cdot[f,B]=[gf,B]
is a generalized zipper action.

Claim:
Let
B
be a closed ball in
X
and
g\in\Gamma
. Then
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\in Z\backslash gZ
if and only if
B
properly contains a maximum region of
g^{-1}
.

To prove this claim, first, assume
[\text{incl}_{B},B]\in Z\backslash gZ
and that there exists a maximum region
R
for
g^{-1}
containing
B
. We see
g^{-1}(R)
is a ball and
g^{-1}|_{R}\in\text{Sim}(R,g^{-1}(R))
. By the Restriction property,
g^{-1}|_{B}\in\text{Sim}(B,g^{-1}(B))
and
[g^{-1}|_{B},B]
is in
Z
. However,
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]=g[g^{-1}|_{B},B]\in gZ
, which is a contradiction.

Now assume
R
is a maximum region of
g^{-1}
properly contained in
B
. Suppose
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\in gZ
. Then there is
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\in Z
such that
[\text{incl}|_{B_{1}},B_{1}]=g[\text{incl}|_{B_{1}},B_{1}]=[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, meaning
g(B_{1})=B
. This equality also implies that
g|:B_{1}\rightarrow B
is in
\text{Sim}(B_{1},B)
. Using the Inverse Property, we see
g^{-1}|:B\rightarrow B_{1}
is in
\text{Sim}(B,B_{1}).
Therefore,
B
is a region for
g^{-1}
, which contradicts the maximality of
R
. So,
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\not\in gZ
proving the claim.

Note that the claim also implies that for each
g\in\Gamma
, there is a bijection between
Z\backslash gZ
and the set of closed balls of
X
properly containing maximum regions of
g^{-1}
given by the function function
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\mapsto B
. We also note that since
g[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\mapsto[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
is a bijection from
gZ\backslash Z
to
Z\backslash g^{-1}Z
, the claim then also implies
g[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\mapsto B
is a bijection from
gZ\backslash Z
to the set of closed balls of
X
properly containing maximum regions of
g
.

Finally, since there are only finitely many closed balls containing a maximum region of
g
or
g^{-1}
, the previous two bijections show that
Z\backslash gZ
and
gZ\backslash Z
are both finite, so we can conclude that for each
g\in\Gamma
,
gZ\triangle Z
is finite and
\Gamma
has a generalized zipper action.

∎

Combining Theorem
5.3
and Proposition
5.2
, it is clear that a CSS group
\Gamma
admits a cocycle into
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. However, the cocycle is also unbounded. Indeed, for the cocycle
b:\Gamma\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
given in Proposition
5.2
, we have
\|b(g)\|_{2}^{2}=|gZ\triangle Z|
for all
g\in\Gamma
. Since
|gZ\triangle Z|
is finite by Theorem
5.3
, we know
b(g)
is finitely supported in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. In particular,
\|b(g)\|_{2}^{2}
can be thought of as counting the number of closed balls in
X
containing maximal regions for either
g
or
g^{-1}
. Since
X
is compact,
\|b(g)\|_{2}
is always finite. However, taking a sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Gamma
such that the partition
\mathscr{P}_{+}
for
g_{n}
and
\mathscr{P}_{-}
for
g_{n}^{-1}
are such that
|\mathscr{P}_{+}|=|\mathscr{P}_{-}|=n
, then
\|b(g_{n})\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore,
b
is an unbounded cocycle.

To understand why
b
is not proper for CSS groups that are not also FSS groups, we remark that at the end of the proof of Theorem
5.3
, it was shown that the set
Z\backslash gZ
is in bijection with the set of closed balls properly containing maximum regions of
g^{-1}
and
gZ\backslash Z
is in bijection with the set of closed balls properly containing maximum regions of
g^{-1}
. Therefore, the size of the set
\{g\in\Gamma:\|b(g)\|_{2}\leq r\}
, depends on how many
h\in\Gamma
have maximal regions containing the maximal region of
g
. In other words,
|\{g\in\Gamma:\|b(g)\|_{2}\leq r\}|
, roughly speaking, counts how many
g\in\Gamma
have a maximal partition no greater than a certain finite cardinality. However, as we can see from Lemma
3.4
,
\Gamma(\mathscr{P}_{\pm})
being infinite, or the fact that some
\text{Sim}(B,D)
is infinite when
\Gamma
is a CSS group that is not an FSS group, that there are infinitely many
g\in\Gamma
with maximal partition of the same cardinality, so
\{g\in\Gamma|\|b(g)\|_{2}\leq r\}
will always be an infinite set.

Corollary 5.4
.

If
\Gamma\leq LS(X)
is a CSS group, then it is admits an unbounded, but not necessarily proper, 1-cocycle
(b,\pi,\mathcal{\ell}^{2}(\mathcal{E}))
.

Ultimately, we want the cocycle to be associated to a quasi-regular representation corresponding to a subgroup of
\Gamma
. It turns out that the CSS
∗
condition is sufficient to arrive at such a cocycle. We first state a useful lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\Gamma\leq LS(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
the canonical action defined previously. Then the orbit of
[f,B]\in\mathcal{E}
is infinite, for any proper ball
B\subset X
.

Proof.

Assume
B\subset X
is an infinite proper ball. We first claim it is enough to consider orbits of the form
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
. Indeed, since
f^{-1}\cdot[f,B]=[id,B]=[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, we have that
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\in\Gamma\cdot[f,B]
. So consider the orbit
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
and an infinite subball
D\subsetneq B
. Since
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group, there exists a subball
D_{0}\subseteq D
and some
g\in\text{Sim}(B,D_{0})
. Moreover, by the extension property (Proposition
3.9
), we can replace
g
by an element of
\Gamma
and also call it
g
. Then
g\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]=[g,B]
is in
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
.

We next claim
[g,B]=[\text{incl}|_{D_{0}},D_{0}]
. We need to show there exists and
h\in\text{Sim}(B,D_{0})
such that
\text{incl}|_{D_{0}}\circ h=g
. However, we can simply take
h=g
. Now, by the first property of being a CSS
∗
group, we know that there exists a proper subball
D_{1}\subsetneq D_{0}
. Choose
g_{1}\in\text{Sim}(D_{0},D_{1})
, replacing
D_{1}
with a subball if necessary, where we again use the fact that
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group. Again, use the extension property to view
g_{1}
as an element of
\Gamma
. By the previous argument, we then have that
[g_{1},D_{0}]=[\text{incl}|_{D_{1}},D_{1}]
. Now we claim
[g_{1},D_{0}]\neq[g,B]
. Suppose instead that the two were equal, then there exists
h\in\text{Sim}(D_{0},B)
such that
g\circ h(D_{0})=g_{1}(D_{0})=D_{1}
, however this is a contradiction, because
g\circ h(D_{0})=g(B)=D_{0}\neq D_{1}
. Therefore,
g\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]=[g,B]
and
g_{1}g\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]=[\text{incl}|_{D_{1}},D_{1}]=[g_{1},D_{0}]
are two distinct elements of
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
. Next, choose a proper subbball
D_{2}\subsetneq D_{1}
and some
g_{2}\in\text{Sim}(D_{1},D_{2})
, replacing
D_{2}
with a subball if necessary. By the same argument as above,
[g_{2},D_{1}]=[\text{incl}|_{D_{2}},D_{2}]
,
[g_{2},D_{1}]\neq[g_{1},D_{0}]
, and
[g_{2},D_{1}]\neq[g,B]
. Repeating this process indefinitely, we get

\displaystyle\{g_{n}g_{n-1}\cdots g_{1}\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle=\{[g_{n},D_{n-1}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle=\{[\text{incl}|_{D_{n}},D_{n}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B],

meaning every orbit of
[f,B]\in\mathcal{E}
is infinite when
B
is infinite.

Now assume
B\subset X
is a finite ball. Since
X
is infinite, pick some infinite ball
D_{1}\subset X\setminus B
, then by the CSS
∗
property, there exists some ball
\widetilde{D}_{1}\subset D_{1}
and some
f_{1}\in\text{Sim}(B,\widetilde{D}_{1})
. Note that
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\neq[f_{1},B]
. By Corollary
3.8
, we can replace
f_{1}
by an involution in
\Gamma
, so
f_{1}\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]=[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
is in the orbit of
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
. Next, pick and infinite ball
D_{2}\subset X\setminus(B\cup\widetilde{D}_{1})
. By the CSS
∗
property, there exists some ball
\widetilde{D}_{1}\subset D_{1}
and some
f_{1}\in\text{Sim}(\widetilde{D}_{1},\widetilde{D}_{2})
. Using Corollary
3.8
to view
f_{2}
as an involution in
\Gamma
, we observe the following

[\text{incl}|_{B},B]\neq[f_{1},\widetilde{D}_{1}]\neq[f_{2},\widetilde{D}_{2}]=f_{2}f_{1}\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B].

Continuing this process, we see that
[f_{n},\widetilde{D}_{n}]=f_{n}f_{n-1}\cdots f_{1}\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
is in the orbit of
[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
for all
n
(and is distinct for all
n
), and hence the orbit of
[g,B]\in\mathcal{E}
is infinite for every finite ball
B
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\Gamma\leq LS(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Then for each orbit
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
of an infinite ball
B\subset X
, there exists an unbounded cocycle
(b_{B},\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
, where
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
is the quasi-regular representation for the stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{B}\leq\Gamma
. If moreover,
\Gamma\in
FSS
∗
, then
b_{B}
is proper.

Proof.

Theorem
5.3
and Proposition
5.2
prove that a CSS
∗
group admits an unbounded cocycle into
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
. Now identify
\mathcal{E}
with it’s orbits, as was explained earlier in the section. Then, for each distinct orbit
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, let
P_{B}:\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B])
, be the projection of
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{E})
onto
\ell^{2}(\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B])
. We then claim
b_{B}:=P_{B}b:\Gamma\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B])
is an unbounded cocycle. From the definition of
b
, we have that
b_{B}(g)=P_{B}b(g)=P_{B}(\chi_{gZ}-\chi_{Z})=\chi_{gZ_{B}}-\chi_{Z_{B}}
, since
P_{B}\chi_{Z}=\chi_{Z_{B}}
.

Then, for each orbit
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, we use Lemma
5.5
to find a sequence of balls
(D_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
B\supsetneq D_{0}\supsetneq D_{1}\supsetneq\dots\supsetneq D_{n}
and
g_{n}\in\text{Sim}(D_{n-1},D_{n})
such that
\{[g_{n},D_{n-1}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
. Now, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
S_{n,1}=\{B_{n,1},\dots,B_{n,n}\}
and
S_{n,2}=\{A_{n,1},\dots,A_{n,n}\}
be two sets of balls in
X
that are respectively pairwise disjoint and such that
S_{n,1}\cap S_{n,2}=\emptyset
and
B\cap\{S_{n,1},S_{n,2}\}=\emptyset
. Passing to subballs of each
A_{n,i}
if necessary, choose
h_{n,i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{n,i},A_{n,i})
. Then following the procedure Proposition
3.9
and using the pasting lemma, define

to be:

f_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}h_{n,i}(x)&x\in B_{n,i}\\
h^{-1}_{n,i}(x)&x\in A_{n,i}\\
g_{n}|_{D_{n}-1}(x)&x\in D_{n-1}\\
x&x\in X\backslash\{S_{n,1},S_{n,2},D_{n-1}\}\end{cases}.

Then
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
with maximal partition

such that

. In particular,
\|b_{B}(g_{n})\|_{2}^{2}=|g_{n}Z_{B}\triangle Z_{B}|\rightarrow\infty
as

. Therefore,

is unbounded. Since
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
is in one-to-one correspondence with

, and

For
\Gamma\in
FSS
∗
, we know that

is also proper, given that the cocycle

[

.

6.
Non-inner Amenability of CSS
∗
Groups

In this section, we prove that CSS
∗
groups are non-inner amenable. Recall that a countable discrete group
G
is said to be
inner amenable
if there exists a finite additive probability measure
\mu
on the subsets of
G\setminus\{e\}
such that
\mu(g^{-1}Eg)=\mu(E)
for all
E\subset G\setminus\{e\}
. Given that we now know CSS
∗
group admits an unbounded but not proper cocycle into the quasi-regular representation
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
, we turn to leveraging the following result to prove non-inner amenability.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
CSU16
]
Theorem 4.5)
.

Let
\Gamma
be an ICC, countable, discrete group together with a family of subgroups
\mathcal{G}
so that
\Gamma
is ICC over every subgroup
\Sigma\in\mathcal{G}
. If
\Gamma
admits an unbounded cocycle into a non-amenable orthogonal representation which is mixing relative to
\mathcal{G}
, then
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable.

The rest of this section is spent showing CSS
∗
groups satisfy the above theorem of Chifan, Sinclair, and Udrea. We start by recalling some relevant definitions.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable group and let
\Sigma<\Gamma
be a subgroup. We say that
\Gamma
is
ICC over

\Sigma
if for every finite subset
F\subset\Gamma
there exists
g\in\Gamma
such that
g(F\Sigma F^{-1})g^{-1}\cap F\Sigma F^{-1}=\{e\}
.

Notice (and it is proved in
[
CSU16
]
) that
\Gamma
is ICC if and only if it is ICC over the trivial subgroup. It is also straightforward to check that
\Gamma
will be ICC over any malnormal subgroup. It turns out that a CSS
∗
will be ICC over any stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{B}
of a closed ball
B
in
X
, though we appeal to the structure of
\Gamma_{B}
to prove it in Lemma
6.5
.

Since any CSS
∗
group
\Gamma
admits an unbounded cocycle,
(b_{B},\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
, we now need to show
(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
is a non-amenable representation and is mixing relative to
\mathcal{G}=\{\Gamma_{B}\}
.

Recall, a representation
(\pi,\mathcal{H})
for a discrete group
G
is
mixing relative to

\mathcal{G}
if for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi,\eta\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n},h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in G
and subgroup
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\in\mathcal{G}
such that for any
g\in G\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}H_{i}h_{i}
we have
|\langle\pi(g)\xi,\eta\rangle|<\varepsilon
. When
\mathcal{G}
is the trivial group, then this is equivalent to mixing.
Moreover, the quasi-regular representation
(\lambda_{G/H},\ell^{2}(G/H))
is always mixing relative to
\mathcal{G}=\{H\}
.

The main obstacle to applying Theorem
6.1
is showing
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
is non-amenable. It is well known that showing
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
is non-amenable is equivalent to showing
\Gamma_{B}
is not co-amenable in
\Gamma
(see for example
[
Bek90
]
). Recall that for a discrete group
G
with subgroup
H
, we say
H
is co-amenable to
G
if there exists a finite additive probability measure
\mu
on the subsets of
G/H\setminus\{e\}
that is
G
-invariant. Thus, to prove
\Gamma_{B}
is not co-amenable in
\Gamma
, it is enough to construct a
\Gamma
-paradoxical decomposition of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}
.

Definition 6.3
.

We say a group
G\curvearrowright X
acting on a set
X
, admits a
paradoxical decomposition
if there exist positive integers
m
and
n
, disjoint subsets
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
of
X
, and subsets
S_{1}=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\}
,
S_{2}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\}
of
G
such that

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=X_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup X_{m}\sqcup Y_{1}\dots\sqcup Y_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}X_{i}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}Y_{i}

The sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are the
translating sets
of the paradoxical decomposition.

The
Tarski number
,
\mathscr{T}(G\curvearrowright X)
is the smallest possible value of
m+n
in a paradoxical decomposition of
G\curvearrowright X
. A classical result due to Jónsson and Dekker states that for a group
G
,
\mathscr{T}(G\curvearrowright G)=4
if and only if
G
contains a non-abelian free subgroup. Wagon proved in
[
Wag85
, Theorem 4.8]
a more general result that
\mathscr{T}(G\curvearrowright X)=4
if and only if
G
contains a non-abelian free subgroup
F
such that the restriction of the action to
F
has abelian point stabilizers. However, for a subgroup
H\leq G
and
X=G/H
a coset space, the action
G\curvearrowright G/H
will generally not have abelian point stabilizers, and so Wagon’s result is not easily applied. Therefore, when
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
is a CSS
∗
group, we instead give an explicit paradoxical decomposition of
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma
, with more than
4
pieces, such that it factors through to the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}
for a specific proper ball
B
in
X
. That is, we construct a paradoxical decomposition of the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}
. To that end, we introduce more structure inherent to CSS
∗
groups and their compact ultrametric spaces.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS group. Recall from Definition
3.2
that every
f\in\Gamma
has a unique maximum partition
\mathcal{P}_{f}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{m}\}
of
X
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where each
D_{i}
is a maximum region for
f
, meaning,
f|_{D_{i}}\in\text{Sim}(D_{i},f(D_{i}))
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, and for any ball
D\supset D_{i}
,
f(D)
is not a ball. For every compact ultrametric space we can consider the ball depth for
B\subset X
, denoted
d_{X}(B)=n
, where by
ball depth
, we mean the number of balls properly containing
B
. Since
X
is a compact ultrametric space,
d_{X}(B)
is always finite. We will use integrally that every compact ultrametric space
X
has a partition,

\mathcal{P}_{X}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}

where
d_{X}(B_{i})=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. We call such a partition the
minimal ball partition of
X
and
|\mathcal{P}_{X}|=n
, the
ball width
of
X
. Moreover, every compact ultrametric space can be equipped with a total order on the ball structure, which is induced by the lexicographic order on the end space of the tree that arises in the categorical equivalence of
X
with the end space of a rooted locally finite geodesically complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree (See
[
Hug12
, Section 12.3]
). With this in mind, we can assume both
\mathcal{P}_{f}
and
\mathcal{P}_{X}
are ordered partitions, with
D_{1}
(respectively
B_{1}
) being called the
maximally ordered ball
in
\mathcal{P}_{f}
(respectively
\mathcal{P}_{X}
). Since this is the lexicographic order, this can be read as “left most” ball. We now consider
B_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
for
1\leq j\leq n
that are infinite and the intersection
\mathcal{P}_{f}\cap B_{j}
, which is necessarily always nonempty. Then we set

(6.0.1)

D_{f,j}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m\>:\>D_{i}\>\text{infinite}}\{{D_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}\cap B_{j}}\},

where in words,
D_{f,j}
is the infinite ball in
\mathcal{P}_{f}\cap B_{j}
of maximal ball order with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{f}
. We now make a few observations about the balls
D_{f,j}
and
f(D_{f,j})
.

(1)

For every
f\in\Gamma
, the ball
D_{f,j}\subseteq B_{j}
exists and is unique for
f
.

(2)

The ball
f(D_{f,j})
can be located only in
B_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
, where
B_{i}
is infinite.

(3)

If
D_{j}=B_{j}
and
f|_{D_{j}}=id
, then
f(D_{f,j})=f(D_{j})=B_{j}
.

(4)

Given
f,g\in\Gamma
and
f|_{D_{f,j}}=g|_{D_{g,j}}
, then it is possible, and in fact common, that
D_{f,j}\neq D_{g,j}
.

(5)

Given
f,g\in\Gamma
, then
gf|_{D_{gf,j}}=f|_{D_{f,j}}
and
D_{gf,j}=D_{f,j}
if and only if

g|_{f(D_{f,j})}\in\text{Sim}\big(f(D_{f,j}),g(f(D_{f,j}))\big).

Given a CSS
∗
group
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
, we can now use
f(D_{f,j})
to define a partition of
\Gamma
. For convenience, from this point forward, we assume
B_{1}
is infinite and fix
B_{j}=B_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
and write
D_{f}
for
D_{f,1}
. Now for each infinite
B_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
, choose
B_{i,1},B_{i,2}\subset B_{i}
infinite such that

(1)

B_{i,1}\cap B_{i,2}=\emptyset
, and

(2)

\text{Sim}(B_{i},B_{i,1})
and
\text{Sim}(B_{i},B_{i,2})
are both non-empty.

This is always possible for a CSS
∗
group. Indeed, by the second CSS
∗
condition, there are at least two disjoint subballs
B_{i,1}
and
B_{i,2}
of
B_{i}
, and by the first CSS
∗
condition, there exists some proper subball
\tilde{B}_{i,1}\subseteq B_{i,1}
and some
\tilde{B}_{i,2}\subseteq B_{i,2}
such that
\text{Sim}(B_{i},\tilde{B}_{i,1})
and
\text{Sim}(B_{i},\tilde{B}_{i,2})
are non-empty. For simplicity we relabel the subballs as
B_{i,1}
and
B_{i,2}
. Now for all
1\leq i\leq n
such that
B_{i}
, is infinite, define the following subsets of
\Gamma

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\{f\in\Gamma:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,1}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle=\{f\in\Gamma:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i},\>f(D_{f})\not\subset B_{i,1},\>\text{and}\>f(D_{f})\not\subset B_{i,2}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{3}_{i}

\displaystyle=\{f\in\Gamma:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,2}\}.

By construction,
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1},\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}
are all pairwise disjoint. Moreover, since each
C_{i}^{j}
is defined with respect to the infinite balls
B_{i}
in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
(which by definition are disjoint from each other), we know for each
f
in
\Gamma
,
f(D_{f})
can only be in one
B_{i}
, so we also see that
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}\cap\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime}}^{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,2,3\}
whenever
i\neq i^{\prime}
or
j\neq j^{\prime}
. In particular, every
f\in\Gamma
can be identified with a single
f(D_{f})
and
f(D_{f})
can only be contained in a single
\mathcal{C}^{j}_{i}
. Therefore,

\Gamma=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}

is a disjoint union of
\Gamma
into
3n
disjoint sets, where
n
is the ball width of
X
and is finite because
X
is a compact ultrametric space.

We now consider the coset space
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
. Let
f_{1}\Gamma_{B_{1}}\equiv f_{2}\Gamma_{B_{1}}
, then necessarily,
f_{1}|_{B_{1}}=f_{2}|_{B_{1}}
. Therefore, the natural left action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
given by
g\cdot f\Gamma_{B_{1}}=gf\Gamma_{B_{1}}
produces a new coset, which is only distinguished by
(gf)|_{B_{1}}
, where
f
can be any coset representative of
f\Gamma_{B_{1}}
. Setting
\overline{f}:=f\Gamma_{B_{1}}
, we define the following sets

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}^{1}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\{\overline{f}\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,1}\}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}^{2}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\{\overline{f}\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i},\>f(D_{f})\not\subset B_{i,1},\>\text{and}\>f(D_{f})\not\subset B_{i,2}\}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}^{3}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\{\overline{f}\in\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}:f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,2}\},

and observe that the images
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}}
of
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}
under the quotient still define a disjoint partition of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
precisely because for every
f\in\Gamma
,
D_{f}\subseteq B_{1}
. Indeed, if
f_{1}(D_{f_{1}})\neq f_{2}(D_{f_{2}})
, then
f_{1}|_{B_{1}}\neq f_{2}|_{B_{2}}
. So
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
cannot determine the same element in
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
, and so
f_{1}\Gamma_{B_{1}}\neq f_{2}\Gamma_{B_{1}}
. Therefore, if
f_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime}}^{j^{\prime}}
for either
i\neq i^{\prime}
or
j\neq j^{\prime}
, we have that
f_{1}\Gamma_{B_{1}}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}}
and
f_{2}\Gamma_{B_{1}}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime}}^{j^{\prime}}}
, so the disjointness of
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}
is preserved under the quotient. Thus, we have the following decomposition of the coset space of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
,

\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}.

We are now ready to prove that this decomposition of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
is paradoxical for the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and
\mathcal{P}_{X}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
be the minimal ball partition of
X
. Assume
B_{1}
is infinite and let
\Gamma_{B_{1}}
be the corresponding stabilizer subgroup for
B_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Then
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
has a paradoxical decomposition of size
3n
, where
n
is the number of infinite balls in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
. In particular,
\Gamma_{B_{1}}
is not co-amenable to
\Gamma
and
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}}
is a non-amenable representation.

Proof.

First, consider the subset of balls in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
that are infinite and relabel them
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
for
n\leq m
, for convenience. We then define a set of paradoxical translates,
g_{i},h_{i}\in\Gamma
, for
1\leq i\leq n
. By construction of
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}
, there exists some
\tilde{g}_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{i},B_{i,1})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Hence, by the extension property (Proposition
3.9
), we know there exists a
g_{i}\in\Gamma
such that
g_{i}|_{B_{i,1}}=\tilde{g}_{i}^{-1}
. Therefore, we fix such a
g_{i}\in\Gamma
, so that
g_{i}|_{B_{i,1}}:B_{i,1}\to B_{i}
. We then claim that

g_{i}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}}.

Indeed, if
f\Gamma_{B_{1}}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}
, then
f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,1}
. So,
g_{i}f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i}
and
D_{g_{i}f}=D_{f}
. In particular,
g_{i}\cdot\overline{f}=\overline{g_{i}f}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}}
for some
j\in\{1,2,3\}
. Conversely,
g_{i}^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}
, since
g_{i}^{-1}f(D_{f})\subseteq B_{i,1}
and
D_{g_{i}^{-1}f}=D_{f}
for any
\overline{f}\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}}
. By construction of
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{j}
, there also exists some
\tilde{h}_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{i},B_{i,2})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Hence, by the extension property, we know there exists a
h_{i}\in\Gamma
such that
h_{i}|_{B_{i,2}}=\tilde{h}_{i}^{-1}
. Therefore, we fix such a
h_{i}\in\Gamma
, so that
h_{i}|_{B_{i,2}}:B_{i,2}\to B_{i}
. Then by a symmetric argument to the first claim, we have that

h_{i}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}}=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}}.

We now define the two subsets of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
,

S_{1}=\Big\{\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}},\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}\Big\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}=\Big\{\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}}\Big\}_{1\leq i\leq n}.

Then we have,

\displaystyle\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{1}}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{i}^{3}},

which means
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}=S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}
is a paradoxical decomposition of
\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
of size
3n
, where
n
is the number of infinite balls in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Therefore,
\Gamma_{B_{1}}
is not co-amenable to
\Gamma
and
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}}
is a non-amenable representation.

∎

We observe that this paradoxical decomposition of
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}}
depends only on
n
, the number of infinite balls in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
, which in many standard examples is the ball width of the compact ultrametric space
X
. Given a CSS
∗
group
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
where
\mathcal{P}_{X}
only consists of infinite balls and
|\mathcal{P}_{X}|=n
, we also do not know if
3n
is the Tarski number
\mathscr{T}(\Gamma\curvearrowright\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{1}})
, though it would be interesting to know if it is. Additionally, Theorem
6.4
technically only proves
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B_{j}}}
is non-amenable when
d_{X}(B_{j})=0
and
B_{j}
is infinite, however that is sufficient for our uses given that
\Gamma
admits an unbounded cocycle
(b_{B},\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
for any proper infinite ball
B\subset X
.

At this point, we have proven that the quasi-regular representation
\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}}
, for
\Gamma
a CSS
∗
group, is non-amenable. To further conclude
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable, by-way-of Theorem
6.1
(the result of Chifan, Sinclair, and Udrea) we need to prove
\Gamma
is ICC over
\Gamma_{B}
for any CSS
∗
group
\Gamma
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and
\Gamma_{B}
the stabilizer subgroup for a proper infinite ball
B
in
X
. Then
\Gamma
is ICC over
\Gamma_{B}
.

Proof.

Let
B\subset X
be a proper infinite ball and suppose
\Gamma
is not ICC over
\Gamma_{B}
, then there exists a finite set
F\subset\Gamma
such that for all
g\in\Gamma
,
gF\Gamma_{B}F^{-1}g^{-1}\cap F\Gamma_{B}F^{-1}\neq\{e\}
, or equivalently

F^{-1}gF\Gamma_{B}F^{-1}g^{-1}F\cap\Gamma_{B}\neq\{e\}

In particular, for every
g\in\Gamma
and every
h\in\Gamma_{B}
there exists
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\in F
such that

f_{1}^{-1}gf_{2}hf^{-1}_{3}g^{-1}f_{4}\in\Gamma_{B}.

By the definition of
\Gamma_{B}
, this implies
(f_{1}^{-1}gf_{2}hf^{-1}_{3}g^{-1}f_{4})|_{B}=id
, which is not possible, given that there are only finitely many
f_{i}\in F
to choose from. Indeed, first note that
F=\{id\}
would imply
\Gamma_{B}
is normal, which is easily seen to be false. Now, for any other finite set
F\subset\Gamma
, fix
\mathcal{P}_{m}:=\mathcal{P}_{f}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is the maximal partition for
f
and
|\mathcal{P}_{m}|\geq|\mathcal{P}_{f}|
for all
f\in F
. We also let
m
be the number of infinite balls in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. For each infinite
D_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
take
m
infinite subballs
\{D_{i,1},\dots,D_{i,m}\}
that do not partition
D_{i}
. Send each
D_{i,j}
into a ball inside
D_{j}
disjoint from
\{D_{j,1},\dots,D_{j,m}\}
. By the extension property (Proposition
3.9
) define an element
g\in\Gamma
that does just this. Then
|\mathcal{P}_{g}|>m^{2}
. Now choose
h_{1}\in\Gamma_{B}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{h_{1}}|_{X\setminus B}\subset\mathcal{P}_{g}
. Suppose
|\mathcal{P}_{h_{1}}|_{X\setminus B}|=r
. Then for every infinite ball
\tilde{D}_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{h_{1}}|_{X\setminus B}
, repeat the same process for defining
g
and call this new element
\tilde{h}
. Then it is not possible for
(f_{1}^{-1}gf_{2}\tilde{h}f^{-1}_{3}g^{-1}f_{4})|_{B}=id
for any
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\in F
. Therefore,
\Gamma
is ICC over
\Gamma_{B}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6

(Theorem
B
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group.
Let
B
be a closed ball of
X
and
\Gamma_{B}
the corresponding subgroup of
\Gamma
. Then the quasi-regular representation
(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
for
\Gamma
is non-amenable. Moreover,
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable.

Proof.

The fact that the quasi-regular representation
(\lambda_{\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}},\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B}))
is non-amenable follows Theorem
6.4
. To further conclude
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable, we appeal to Theorem
6.1
, the result of Chifan, Sinclair, and Udrea, for
\mathcal{G}=\{\Gamma_{B}\}
. Indeed, by Theorem
5.6
we know
\Gamma
admits an unbounded cocycle into
\ell^{2}(\Gamma/\Gamma_{B})
, which is always mixing relative to
\Gamma_{B}
. Then by Lemma
6.5
we see
\Gamma
is ICC over
\Gamma_{B}
.
∎

It is worth noting that in the FSS
∗
case where the cocycle from Theorem
5.6
is proper, Theorem
6.1
can be replaced with
[
OP10b
, Proposition 2.1]
. The result of Ozawa and Popa states that a group that admits a proper cocycle into a non-amenable representation is non-inner amenable. However, this approach does not hold for the general CSS
∗
case, where the cocycle is not proper.

In Example
4.5
we observe that countable Röver-Nekrashevych groups
V_{d}(G)
are CSS
∗
groups, so in light of Theorem
7.7
we can conclude
V_{d}(G)
is non-inner amenable for any countable self-similar group
G
. In particular, this answers a question of the first named author and Zaremsky posed in
[
BZ22
]
in the countable setting. We also recover the result of
[
HO17
]
and
[
BZ22
]
that
V_{d}
is non-inner amenable for
d\geq 2
(see Example
4.1
). In light of Example
4.3
, we also prove that
V_{d,r}
is non-inner amenable, which does not seem to have appeared in the literature before. The class of FSS
∗
groups coming from irreducible subshifts of finite type from Section
4.2
and QAut
(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})
from Example
4.15
both appear to be new examples of non-inner amenable groups as well.

Corollary 6.7

(Corollary
C
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be an FSS
∗
group. Then
\Gamma
is properly proximal.

Proof.

In
[
BIP21
]
, Boutonnet, Ioana, and Peterson show that a group admitting a proper 1-cocylce into a non-amenable representation is properly proximal; hence, combing Theorem
5.6
, Lemma
7.5
(1), and Theorem
6.4
also proves FSS
∗
groups are properly proximal.
∎

However, the proof of
[
BIP21
, Theorem 4.7]
does not generalize to the unbounded 1-cocycle case, because the boundary piece

X:=\{\omega\in\Delta\Gamma\>|\>\lim_{g\rightarrow\omega}\|b(g)\|=+\infty\}

is no longer the entire boundary
\partial\Gamma
of
\Gamma
when the cocycle is unbounded, but not proper. Therefore, we do not know if CSS
∗
groups in their full generality are properly proximal.

Given that the only examples we have of CSS
∗
groups that are not FSS
∗
groups are

for
G
infinite self-similar, we recover that all the other examples just mentioned above are new examples of properly proximal groups, in addition to
V_{d}(H)
, where
H
is a finite self-similar group.

7.
Prime Factors from CSS Groups

In this section, we prove the motivating result of this paper, Theorem
7.7
, that the group von Neumann algebra for any CSS
∗
group is a prime II
1
factor. In particular, we use Theorem
5.6
and Theorem

to show that CSS
∗
groups satisfy the assumptions of the following theorem of the second named author, de Santiago, and Khan in
[
dSDK23
]
. Therefore, this result can be understood as the culmination of our work so far. As a consequence of the ping-pong lemma, we also prove that certain CSS

Theorem 7.1

(
[
dSDK23
]
Theorem 4.10)
.

Let
G
be a countable discrete ICC group such that
L(G)
does not have property Gamma. Suppose there exists an ICC subgroup
H\leq G
such that
H
is weakly malnormal and
L(H)
is weakly bicentralized in
L(G)
.
If
G
admits an unbounded 1-cocycle into the quasi-regular representation
(\lambda_{G/H},\ell^{2}(G/H))
, then
L(G)
is prime.

The condition of
L(H)
being weakly bicentralized in
L(G)
is really about weak containment of bimodules, and is used to great effect in the proof of the previous theorem. However, if
N\subset M
is a containment of II
1
factors, then
N
being equal to its own relative bicommutant, that is,
(N^{\prime}\cap M)^{\prime}\cap M=N
, is sufficient for

to be weakly bicentralized in
M
by
[
BMO20
, Lemma 3.4]
. Historically,

is said to be
normal
in
M
. To understand when
L(\Gamma_{B})
is normal in
L(\Gamma)
, we recall some facts that relate relative commutants to subgroups. Recall, the centralizer of a subgroup
H
in
G
is

\mathcal{C}_{G}(H):=\{g\in G:h^{-1}gh=g\,\forall h\in H\},

while the virtual centralizer of
H
in
G
is defined as

\mathcal{VC}_{G}(H):=\{g\in G:|\{h^{-1}gh:h\in H\}|<\infty\}.

In general, the commutant of
L(H)
inside
L(G)
is given by the following

L(H)^{\prime}\cap L(G)=\left\{\sum_{g\in G}c_{g}\lambda_{g}\in L(G):g\in\mathcal{VC}_{G}(H),\,c_{g}=c_{h^{-1}gh}\,\forall g\in G,h\in H\right\}.

However, when
\mathcal{C}_{G}(H)=\mathcal{VC}_{G}(H)
we know that
L(H)^{\prime}\cap L(G)=L(\mathcal{C}_{G}(H))
. The goal now is to show that the centralizer and virtual centralizer agree for
\Gamma_{B}
in
\Gamma
. We fix the notation
g^{\Gamma_{B}}:=\{h^{-1}gh:h\in\Gamma_{B}\}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and
B\subset X
any proper closed ball. Then for the stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{B}
of
\Gamma
, the following hold:

(1)

\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})=\mathcal{VC}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})=\Gamma_{X\backslash B}
and

(2)

\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{X\backslash B})=\mathcal{VC}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{X\backslash B})=\Gamma_{B}
.

In particular,
\big(L(\Gamma_{B})^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)\big)^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)=L(\Gamma_{B})
.

Proof.

First, we can write
X\backslash B
as a disjoint union of closed balls,
B_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup B_{n}
, where
n
is a positive integer. We now claim that
\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})=\Gamma_{X\backslash B}
. Indeed,
g\in\Gamma
commutes with

if and only if

is the identity on

, which happens exactly when
g\in\Gamma_{X\backslash B}
. Now to prove
\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})=\mathcal{VC}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})
, it is enough to show that for all
g\in\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})
,
|g^{\Gamma_{B}}|=\infty
.

We can achieve this with a variation of Theorem
3.11
. First, suppose
g\in\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})
and
g(X\backslash B)\neq B
. Then there exists an
x\in X\backslash B
such that
g|_{X\backslash B}\neq id
and
g(x)\not\in B
. Then the proof of Corollary
3.12
applies here and importantly,
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Gamma_{B}
, where
\{h_{n}^{-1}gh_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence of distinct elements. Therefore,
|g^{\Gamma_{B}}|=\infty
.

The second case to consider is if
g\in\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{B})
and
g(X\backslash B)=B
, resulting in

. Let
A,D_{1}
be infinite disjoint balls in
X\backslash B
. Choose
f_{1}\in\text{Sim}(A,D_{1})
, replace
D_{1}
with a subball if necessary, and extend
f_{1}
to be an element of
\Gamma
, as in Corollary
3.8
. Note that
f_{1}\in\Gamma_{B}
. Choose
D_{2}\subset D_{1}
and
f_{2}\in\text{Sim}(A,D_{2})
, again replacing
D_{2}
with a subball if necessary and extending

to be an element of
\Gamma
. Continue this process for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim
f_{n}^{-1}gf_{n}\neq f_{m}^{-1}gf_{m}
for
n\neq m
. Indeed, observe that
f_{n}^{-1}gf_{n}(D_{1})=g(D_{n})
. Suppose now that
g(D_{n})=g(D_{m})
. Then since

, this implies
D_{n}=D_{m}
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\{f_{n}^{-1}gf_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence of distinct elements in
\Gamma_{B}
and
|g^{\Gamma_{B}}|=\infty
.

The proof of
2
follows from a symmetric argument. We also see that
1
implies
L(\Gamma_{B})^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)=L(\Gamma_{X\backslash B})
and
2
implies

\big(L(\Gamma_{B})^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)\big)^{\prime}\cap L(\Gamma)=L(\Gamma_{B})
.

∎

Recall that a subgroup
H
of

is said to be

if there exists
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G\backslash H
such that
|\cap_{i=1}^{n}(H\cap g_{i}^{-1}Hg_{i})|<\infty
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group and
B\subset X
any proper closed ball. Then the stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{B}
is weakly malnormal in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
B\subset X
be any proper infinite ball in
X
. Pick a partition
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
of
X
containing

B_{1}:=B
. Then pick
D_{1},\dots,D_{n-1}
pairwise disjoint balls in

. Moving to subballs if necessary, choose
g_{i}\in\text{Sim}(B_{i+1},D_{i})
for

g(x)=\begin{cases}g_{i}(x),&x\in B_{i+1}\\
g_{i}^{-1}(x),&x\in D_{i}\\
x,&x\in X\setminus\{B_{2},\dots,B_{n},D_{1},\dots,D_{n-1}\},\end{cases}

which by the pasting lemma (Lemma

) is an element of

. We now claim
\Gamma_{B_{1}}\cap g^{-1}\Gamma_{B_{1}}g=\{1\}
. Indeed, choose

. If

. Next, we use the fact that

to see that

\displaystyle f(B_{i+1})=g^{-1}hg(B_{i+1})

\displaystyle=g^{-1}hg_{i}(B_{i+1})

\displaystyle=g^{-1}h(D_{i})

\displaystyle=g^{-1}(D_{i})=g_{i}^{-1}(D_{i})=B_{i+1}

∎

Theorem

Let

\displaystyle\in\text{Sim}(B^{+}_{1},B_{1,a}^{+}),\quad g_{1}\in\text{Sim}(B^{-}_{1},B_{2,a}^{+})

8.
Dynamics and Properties of the Commutator Subgroup

In
[
Mat15
]
Matui showed the commutator subgroup of the topological full group of a non-periodic irreducible subshift of finite type is simple. The authors of
[
BEH24
]
later generalized this result to the commutator subgroup of so-called vigorous subgroups of the homeomorphism group of the Cantor set. As remarked previously, the CSS
∗
groups discussed in Section
4.2
are exactly the topological full groups Matui studied and many (though not all) of the vigorous homeomorphism groups of the Cantor set are also CSS
∗
groups, so it was natural to see which properties from
[
Mat15
]
and
[
BEH24
]
hold for the class of CSS
∗
groups. In particular, we prove some CSS
∗
group have a simple commutator subgroup and some do not. We then go on to characterize the inclusion of the group von Neumann algebras and reduced group C
∗
-algebras arising from
\Gamma
and its commutator subgroup when it is simple. Throughout this section, we recall and introduce relevant definitions as they arise.

Recall that a homeomorphism
h
of topological space
X
is said to
locally agree
with a group
\Gamma\leq\text{Homeo}(X)
if for every point
x\in X
, there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x
and an element
g\in\Gamma
such that
h|_{U}=g|_{U}
(see
[
Olu21
]
). The group
\Gamma
is said to be
full
if every homeomorphism of
X
which locally agrees with an element of
\Gamma
is, in fact, an element of
\Gamma
. This definition appears in
[
Olu21
]
and is more general than the definition of full from
[
BEH24
]
, and implies it. However, the converse does not hold be true.

Theorem 8.1
.

If
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
is a CSS group, then it is full.

Proof.

Let
h
be a homeomorphism of the compact ultrametric space
X
that locally agrees with
\Gamma
. To show that
h
belongs to
\Gamma
, we need to show that it is locally determined by the similarity structure
\text{Sim}_{X}
. To this end, let
x\in X
. Then there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x
and a group element
g\in\Gamma
such that
h|_{U}=g|_{U}
. Because closed balls form a basis, there exists a closed ball
B_{0}
such that
x\in B_{0}\subseteq U
. Now, because
g
is locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
, there exists a closed ball
B_{1}
such that
x\in B_{1}
,
g(B_{1})
is a closed ball, and
g|_{B_{1}}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B_{1},g(B_{1}))
. Finally, pick a closed ball
B_{2}
such that
x\in B_{2}\subseteq B_{0}\cap B_{1}
. Since
B_{2}\subseteq B_{1}
, the restriction property tells us that
g|_{B_{2}}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B_{2},g(B_{2}))
. Moreover, because
B_{2}\subseteq U
, we have that
h|_{B_{2}}=g|_{B_{2}}
. So, in reality, we have that
h|_{B_{2}}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B_{2},h(B_{2}))
, thereby proving
h
is locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
. Hence,
h\in\Gamma
.
∎

We now recall from
[
BEH24
]
that a homeomorphism of a metric space
(X,d)
is said to have
small support
if its support is contained in a proper clopen subset of
X
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS

group. Then
\Gamma
is generated by its elements of small support. In fact, every element of
\Gamma
is the product of two elements of small support.

Proof.

Let
g\in\Gamma
be a non-trivial element. Then there exists a point
x\in X
such that
g(x)\neq x
. Pick closed balls
B
and
D
disjoint such that
x\in B
and
g(x)\in D
. Because
g
is locally determined by
\text{Sim}_{X}
, we can find a closed ball
B^{\prime}
containing
x
such that
g(B^{\prime})
is a closed ball and
g|_{B^{\prime}}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(B^{\prime},g(B^{\prime}))
. Pick
\epsilon>0
small enough so that
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)\subseteq B\cap B^{\prime}
,
g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon))\subseteq D\cap g(B^{\prime})
, and
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)\cup g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon))
is a proper subset of
X
. By the restriction property, we know that
g|_{\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)}\in\text{Sim}_{X}(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon),g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)))
. Because
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)\subseteq B
and
g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon))\subseteq D
, we know that
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)
and
g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon))
are disjoint closed balls. Since they are disjoint, we know by Corollary

that the similarity
g|_{\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)}
can be extended to an element
h\in\Gamma
such that

agrees with

on
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)

on

\text{Supp}(h^{-1})=\text{Supp}(h)\subseteq\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)\cup g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon))
, and that

acts as the identity on
\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)
, meaning that
\text{Supp}(hg)\subseteq X\setminus(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)\cup g(\overline{B}(x,\epsilon)))
. Hence,
g=h^{-1}(hg)
is a product of elements of small support.
∎

From
[

]

are clopen subsets of

and

. Note that this definition only makes sense in a compact ultrametric space without finite balls, which in turn is homeomorphic to the Cantor space. Consequently, the class of CSS

Theorem 8.3
.

Let

be a compact ultrametric space with no finite balls, and let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS

\Gamma
is vigorous.

Proof.

Let
A\subset X
be a clopen subset and let

and

be proper clopen subsets of

. Then we can write

as a finite union of balls,
B=B_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup B_{n}
. If

there is nothing to do, so assume

. By the CSS
∗

, that do not partition

, such that
\text{Sim}(B_{i},C_{i})
contains a similarity

. Then by the extension property (Proposition

), there exists some

such that
g|_{B_{i}}=h_{i}
and
\text{Supp}(g)\subset A
and
g(B)\subset C
.

∎

Putting the last few properties together, we can determine when the commutator subgroup of a CSS

Corollary 8.4

(Theorem

.

Let

Proof.

Since

is a non-trivial normal subgroup of
\Gamma

[

, Lemma 4.14]
that the action of
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
on

[

, Lemma 4.15]
, we see that

is generated by its elements of small support. By Theorem

,
\Gamma

, Lemma 4.17]

.

∎

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Let
\Lambda
denote the set of elements of
\Gamma
with finite support. Then
\Lambda
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
and
\Lambda\cap[\Gamma,\Gamma]
is a normal subgroup of
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
.

Proof.

The fact that
\Lambda
is a subgroup follows from the observation that
\text{Supp}(gh)\subset\text{Supp}(g)\cup\text{Supp}(h)
for any
g,h\in\Gamma
. To see that
\Lambda
is normal, consider
h\in\Lambda
and
g\in\Gamma
. Then
x\in\text{Supp}(g^{-1}hg)
if and only if
g^{-1}hg(x)\neq x
if and only if
hg(x)\neq g(x)
if and only if
g(x)\in\text{Supp}(h)
if and only if
x\in g^{-1}(\text{Supp}(h))
. Therefore,
\text{Supp}(g^{-1}hg)=g^{-1}(\text{Supp}(h))
, implying
g^{-1}hg
has finite support.

∎

If
\Gamma
is a CSS
∗
group, and
\text{Sim}(B,D)
is non-empty for two finite balls

and

, then it follows from the definition of being a CSS

is non-trivial. Therefore, when
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
is a CSS

Theorem 8.6
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Then the subgroup
\Lambda
of elements of finite support is isomorphic to an
n
-fold product of the group
S_{\infty}
, where
n
is any positive integer or
\infty
. Moreover, neither
\Gamma
or
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
are
C^{*}
-simple.

Proof.

Let
f\in\Lambda
. Then
\text{Supp}(f)
is a finite clopen set. Take
\text{Supp}(f)=B_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup B_{m}
to be the partition such that there exists no proper subball
D\subset B_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. In particular, this is the partition of
\text{Supp}(f)
containing the most possible balls. Necessarily,
f
is permuting the balls
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
. Now consider the action of
\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{E}
from Section
5
. Then every
B_{i}
in the partition of
\text{Supp}(f)
will appear in some orbit
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, where
B
is a finite ball that does not contain proper subballs. It follows from Lemma
5.5
that each such orbit is infinite. Moreover, the balls contained in these orbits are disjoint from each other because this is just the set of finite balls with no proper subballs. Therefore,
\Lambda
is isomorphic to an
n
-fold direct product of
S_{\infty}
, where
n
is the number of distinct orbits
\Gamma\cdot[\text{incl}|_{B},B]
, where
B
is a finite ball that does not contain proper subballs. When
n
is finite,
\Lambda
is amenable. To see the isomorphism, fix the lexicographical order for the balls in each orbit. Then
f\in\Lambda
is a product of permutations; one permutation for each orbit represented in
\text{Supp}(f)=B_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup B_{m}
. Conversely, if
(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{\infty}
, we can use the pasting lemma (Lemma
3.7
) to define
f\in\Lambda\leq\Gamma
such that each
\sigma_{i}
is represented by how

permutes the

in
\text{Supp}(f)
of the same orbit.

To conclude that
\Gamma
and
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
are not

-simple when

is finite, we appeal to the well known result that a

-simple discrete group does not contain any nontrivial normal amenable subgroups (see for example
[
dlH07
]
). When
n
is finite,
\Lambda
is a normal amenable subgroup of
\Gamma
and
[\Lambda,\Lambda]
is a normal amenable subgroup of
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
. When
n
is infinite, then
\Lambda
is an infinite direct product of copies of
S_{\infty}
. Therefore,
\Lambda
has many normal amenable subgroups and so is not
C^{*}
-simple. Since
\Lambda
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, then
\Gamma
is also not
C^{*}
-simple, by
[
BKKO17
, Theorem 1.4]
. It also follows that
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
is not
C^{*}
-simple.

∎

Since the only finite balls in the compact ultrametric space
\operatorname{QAut}(\mathcal{T}_{2,c})

Corollary 8.7

(Corollary
F
)
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group. Then
\Gamma
is not acylindrically hyperbolic.

Proof.

First we assume,
X
contains no finite balls, then simplicity of
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
entails that all proper quotients of a CSS
∗
group, whose compact ultrametric space contains no finite balls, are abelian. Indeed, let
N
be a normal subgroup of a CSS
∗
group
\Gamma
, and pick a non-trivial element
g\in N
. Since CSS
∗
groups have trivial center (because they are ICC), there must be a non-trivial element
h\in\Gamma
such that the commutator
[h,g]
is non-trivial. But
[h,g]=(h^{-1}g^{-1}h)g
which must lie in
N
because
h^{-1}g^{-1}h
lies in
N
due to
N
being closed under conjugation, and the product
(h^{-1}g^{-1}h)g
lies in
N
due to
N
being a subgroup. Hence,
N\cap[\Gamma,\Gamma]
is a non-trivial normal subgroup of the simple group
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
which implies
[\Gamma,\Gamma]=N\cap[\Gamma,\Gamma]\leq N
. Hence,
\Gamma/N
is abelian. From this we conclude that
\Gamma
is not SQ-universal and therefore not acylindrically hyperbolic by
[
DGO17
]
.

If we now assume that
X
contains finite balls, then by Theorem
8.6
the corresponding CSS
∗
group
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
is not
C^{*}
-simple. By
[

, Theorem 8.14]
,
\Gamma

\Gamma
being

-simple, yet Theorem

implies
\Gamma
is non-inner amenable, even when

is nontrivial (and hence
\Gamma
is not

-simple), thus we have a contradiction. It follows that no CSS

∎

We now focus on CSS

contains no finite balls, and the commutator subgroup of

is simple. In this setting, we can characterize the types of inclusions of both the group von Neumann algebras and reduced group

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS
∗
group acting on a compact ultrametric space with no finite balls. If
g\in\Gamma
commutes with everything in the commutator subgroup
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
, then
g
is the identity element, i.e.
C_{\Gamma}([\Gamma,\Gamma])
is trivial.

Proof.

Since
g
commutes with everything in
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
, it must stabilize the support of any element in
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
. By way of contradiction, assume that
g
is not the identity element. Then there exists
x_{0}\in X
such that
g(x_{0})\neq x_{0}
. Pick closed disjoint closed balls
B
and
C
such that
x_{0}\in B
and
g(x_{0})\in C
. Pick closed disjoint closed balls
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
that are also disjoint from
B
and
C
. Pick a similarity in
\text{Sim}_{X}(B,D_{i})
and extend it to an element
h_{i}\in\Gamma
such that it maps
B
to
D_{i}
,
D_{i}
to
B
, and is the identity outside of these closed balls. In particular, because these balls are disjoint from
C
, it follows that
h_{i}
is the identity on
C
. Hence, clearly the commutator
f:=[h_{1},h_{2}]
is the identity on
C
, meaning the support of
f
is contained in
X\setminus C
. Moreover, because
f(x)\in D_{1}
, and
D_{1}
is disjoint from
B
,
f(x)
must be distinct from
x
, meaning that the point
x
lies in the support

. Hence, because
x\in\text{Supp}(f)\subseteq X\setminus C
, it follows that

g(x)\in g(\text{Supp}(f))\subseteq\text{Supp}(f)\subseteq X\setminus C.

But this is a contradiction. Hence,
g
must be the identity element.
∎

Let

is

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\text{LS}(X)
be a CSS

Proof.

The inclusion being irreducible is equivalent to every non-trivial element of

having infinitely many
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
-conjugates. This is in turn equivalent to every non-trivial element of
\Gamma
having infinite index centralizer in
[\Gamma,\Gamma]

have finite-index centralizer in

. Because
[\Gamma,\Gamma]
is an infinite simple group, it cannot have any proper finite-index subgroups, meaning that the centralizer of

must equal all of

. Hence,
g
must commute with every commutator, and therefore

must be the identity element by Lemma

.
∎
