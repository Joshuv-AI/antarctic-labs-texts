Aberrant left vertebral artery arising distal to the left subclavian artery: a rare anatomic variant demonstrated on computed tomographic angiography
Authors: Sz-Han Yu, I Hui Wu
Published: 2026

Summary

A 56-year-old male presenting with infrarenal aortic dissection was found to have a rare aortic arch variant: a left vertebral artery (LVA) originating distal to the left subclavian artery (LSCA). This ‘Images in Cardiology’ report highlights the anatomical characteristics and clinical significance of this 3.9% rare variant for vascular and neuro-interventional procedures.1

Case description

A 56-year-old asymptomatic man with a history of coronary artery disease and prior coronary artery angioplasty underwent a chest-to-pelvic CT scan for surveillance, which incidentally revealed a focal infrarenal abdominal aortic dissection. While the dissection was limited to the abdominal aorta and had no direct haemodynamic impact on the supra-aortic vessels, the computed tomographic angiography (CTA) provided a detailed view of the aortic arch anatomy.

The CTA demonstrated an aberrant LVA arising directly from the aortic arch, positioned as the fourth branch, distal to the LSCA (Figure 1). The LVA followed a cephalad course posterior to the LSCA and entered the foramen transversarium at the C7 level, rather than the more common C6 level.2 The right vertebral artery followed a typical course.

Three-dimensional CTA reconstruction of the aortic arch. The aberrant left vertebral artery (arrow) is seen originating from the aortic arch distal to the left subclavian artery.

Aberrant vertebral arteries typically arise between the left common carotid and the LSCA. An origin distal to the LSCA is significantly rarer, occurring in only approximately 3.9% of vertebral artery variants. Clinically, an LVA with a direct arch origin is associated with altered haemodynamics that may increase the risk of dissection or atherosclerotic disease, potentially leading to neurologic events.

Furthermore, identifying this variant is critical for procedural planning. In thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or supra-aortic bypass, failure to recognize a distal LVA could lead to unintentional occlusion, resulting in posterior circulation ischaemia. Similarly, during neuro-interventional catheterization, the unusual acute angle of the LVA origin poses a challenge for vessel cannulation.
