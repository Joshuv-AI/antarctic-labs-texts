Do not underestimate cryptosporidium: case report of a rare cause of acute liver failure in transplant recipients
Authors: Anne-Cécile Brunac, Claudie Thouron, Pierre Guy, Joelle Guitard, Olivier Marion, Janick Selves, Xavier Iriart, Charline Zadro, Nassim Kamar, Arnaud Del Bello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCryptosporidium infection, usually self-limited in immunocompetent individuals, can lead to severe complications in immunosuppressed patients, such as solid organ transplant recipients. Biliary tract infection is a rare but potentially devastating complication, with limited therapeutic options.Case presentationWe report the case of a woman who developed fatal liver failure secondary to Cryptosporidium-induced cholangiopathy fifteen years after liver transplantation. The patient, maintained on triple immunosuppression, initially presented with diarrhea and acute renal failure. PCR on stool identified Cryptosporidium spp., and immunosuppression minimization combined with specific therapies were initiated with temporary improvement. Despite sequential antiparasitic therapies, recurrent diarrhea and progressive liver enzyme elevation ensued. Serial liver biopsies revealed portal and lobular inflammation with granulomas and biliary injury, evolving toward hepatic failure and fibrosis. Profound and persistent lymphopenia was observed throughout the course.ConclusionsThis case underscores the underestimated risk of Cryptosporidium infection in liver transplant recipients and its potential to cause irreversible biliary damage and liver failure. Early recognition, and prompt targeted therapy are crucial to improve the outcomes in this life-threatening complication.

Background

Solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients require long-term immunosuppression and infections remain a leading cause of complications [1–3]. Opportunistic infections typically occur between 1 to 6 months post transplantation, but delayed infections are more and more frequent with modern immunosuppression and persistent lymphopenia [4, 5]. Furthermore, delayed opportunistic infections depend on the presence, intensity and virulence of pathogen exposure [6]. Among them, cryptosporidium-induced diarrhea represents a major cause of opportunistic disease [7].

Cryptosporidium species, waterborne intracellular protozoa, are a common cause of watery diarrhea, in immunocompetent travelers in endemic regions [8]. Nonetheless, this is an emerging infection in Europe. [9–11],. [12] The infection is passed via fecal-oral route from infected hosts, or in contaminated water or food comprising cryptosporidium oocysts. [12] When ingested, sporozoites are released from oocysts in the distal ileum where they invade enterocytes. Although more than 40 species of Cryptosporidium have been identified, human infections are mainly caused by Cryptosporidium parvum and Cryptosporidium hominis [6]. Immune response to cryptosporidium is complex. Enterocytes represent a first barrier. Local adaptative immune response is mainly involved with resident memory T cell, but also B cells, that can develop antibody mediated responses after activation and differenciation.[13]

Although self-limited in immunocompetent, cholera-like diarrhea or disseminated disease could occur in immunosuppressed patients, associated with fever and abdominal pain [12, 14–16]. A French registry of SOT recipients diagnosed with cryptosporidia, extra-intestinal infections were described as relatively rare (8%). Among these complications, biliary tract infection represents one of the most dramatical.

A reduction of immunosuppression is classically proposed as first line therapy [17]. There is currently only a single approved drug (nitazoxanide) for the treatment of Cryptosporidium, but in addition to the drug availability issues, doubts remain as to the effectiveness in immunocompromised patients.

In the present case, we describe and discuss the occurrence of a liver transplant recipient who developed a liver failure rapidly after cryptosporidium induced diarrhea.

Case presentation

A woman in her thirties underwent liver transplantation for fulminant autoimmune hepatitis. Three months after transplantation, she developed biopsy-proven acute antibody-mediated rejection, successfully treated with polyclonal antibodies, plasma exchanges, steroid pulses, and rituximab.

Her maintenance immunosuppression included tacrolimus (C0: 3–5 ng/mL), mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily, and prednisone 5 mg/day.

Her follow-up remained uneventful for fifteen years, except for the detection of an asymptomatic biliary stenosis (without infection or abnormal liver tests).

She was then hospitalized for acute renal failure secondary to diarrhea persisting for two weeks. Liver tests were normal (Fig. 1). She presented a discrete lymphopenia (total lymphocyte 1091/mm3, CD4: 591/mm3, CD8: 351/mm3), and NK cell diminution in absolute number (CD3–CD16+CD56+: 40/mm3). Stool analysis identified by PCR Cryptosporidium sp. and Bothriocephalus, treated with nitazoxanide (500 mg twice daily for one month) and a single dose of praziquantel (10 mg/kg). Patient interview confirmed an environmental exposure (she lived in rural area, with farm animals). Although her digestive symptoms improved rapidly, she was readmitted for recurrence of diarrhea, with Cryptosporidium sp. detected in stool. This time, elevated liver enzymes were noted (Fig. 1). Other causes of chronic diarrhea: viral infections (HEV, ADV, HBV, HAV, HCV, HIV, CMV), bacterial infections (blood and stool cultures, and stool enteropathogen PCR assays), and others fungal and parasitic pathogen infections (blood and stool cultures, stool PCR assays) were excluded. However, one month later, she was readmitted for recurrent diarrhea and elevated liver enzymes, associated with Cryptosporidium sp. infection detected in stool (Fig. 1). Paromomycin and azithromycin (500 mg/day) were given for one month, followed by an additional month of paromomycin alone. Despite resolution of diarrhea, liver tests remained abnormal.

A second biopsy revealed more pronounced inflammatory changes. No significant fibrosis or cirrhosis were seen. The infiltrate was denser, portal and lobular, lymphohistiocytic with numerous neutrophils and associated with microgranulomas lacking caseous necrosis. Some bile ducts showed subacute cholangitis. Mild ductopenia, involving 3 of 11 portal tracts, and mild cholestasis were observed, without ductular reaction. These findings were confirmed by CK7 immunostaining, which also revealed features of chronic cholestasis. Endotheliitis had resolved. The overall picture remained nonspecific but compatible with an infectious process or mild biliary obstruction (Fig. 3). Autoimmune markers remained negative in blood. To rule out chronic biliary obstruction, an endobiliary stent was placed, and nitazoxanide was reintroduced. A marked lymphopenia was observed (total lymphocyte 680/mm3, CD4: 309/mm3, CD8: 251/mm3), and persistent NK cell diminution in absolute number (CD3–CD16+CD56+: 35/mm3). An excess of exhausted memory CD4+PD1+TIGIT+ was observed (mCD4+:50% of total CD4+; mCD4+PD1+TIGIT+:30.5%).

Three months later (nine months after the first detection of Cryptosporidium), the patient developed clinically significant portal hypertension and transudate ascites, along with laboratory evidence of liver failure (factor V: 50%). A persistent lymphopenia was noted (total lymphocyte 552/mm3, CD4: 318/mm3, CD8: 145/mm3), and persistent NK cell diminution in absolute number (CD3–CD16+CD56+: 28/mm3), in the absence of auto-immune markers detection in blood. Several paracenteses confirmed a sterile low-protein ascitic fluid. MRI demonstrated segmental de novo strictures throughout the biliary tree (Fig. 2). A third biopsy confirmed further progression of the same pattern. Portal tracts showed mild edema and dense polymorphous inflammation rich in neutrophils. Within the lobules scattered lymphohistiocytic microgranulomas and foci of hepatocellular necrosis were observed. Bile ducts were present, but largely damaged, showing features of acute cholangitis and, less frequently, lymphocytic cholangitis. CK7 staining confirmed the absence of ductopenia but revealed a mild ductular reaction and features of chronic cholestasis (Fig. 3). To note, across all biopsies, no Cryptosporidium organisms were identified on H&E and Ziehl–Neelsen stains or PCR, particularly in biliary tracts. CMV, VHE, and EBER in situ hybridization remained also negative. C4d immunostains was negative. Despite nitazoxanide and improvement of digestive symptoms (with negation of cryptosporidium detection in stool), she developed renal failure consistent with hepatorenal syndrome. She was relisted for retransplantation but died from hemorrhagic shock secondary to post-paracentesis complications.

Discussion and conclusions

Here, we report the case of a liver transplant recipient who developed acute liver failure secondary to a biliary complication induced by Cryptosporidium infection. Cryptosporidium is a well-recognized cause of gastrointestinal disease in SOT recipients and is generally associated with favorable outcomes. However, disseminated infections can occur in some cases [18, 19].

Cryptosporidium species infect intestinal epithelial cells and are highly resistant to acidic environments, which explains their frequent involvement in biliary tract infections [20]. Cryptosporidium-associated cholangiopathy was first described in patients with AIDS [21]. In SOT recipients, biliary complications have been reported regardless of the transplanted organ [17, 18], but prognosis appears to be particularly poor in liver transplant patients [17, 22].

In the French registry of Cryptosporidium infections after SOT, two liver transplant recipients were included, and one developed a biliary tract infection. Similarly, Fischer et al. reported six pediatric liver transplant patients with Cryptosporidium infection, half of whom developed biliary tract involvement during follow-up [22].

Reduction of immunosuppressive therapy is commonly recommended as an initial strategy to restore T-cell function [17]. However, this approach remains poorly defined, as spontaneous clearance of Cryptosporidium may occur independently of immunosuppression tapering. Moreover, such reduction may expose patients to the risk of graft rejection [22].

Antiparasitic therapy is also not well standardized. Nitazoxanide is currently the only approved drug in this setting [23] and has been shown to shorten the duration of diarrhea. Its mechanism involves inhibition of pyruvate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase, an enzyme essential for anaerobic electron transport. Other agents such as azithromycin, paromomycin, and fluoroquinolones have shown variable efficacy [23]. Notably, unlike other drugs, nitazoxanide has demonstrated activity against Cryptosporidium infections of the biliary tract [23]. Combination therapies may be considered in cases of disseminated infection.

In the present case, despite the absence of detectable parasites in liver tissue, the histological features, together with the clinical course and the confirmed presence of Cryptosporidium DNA in stool samples, are consistent with biliary involvement by cryptosporidiosis, as previously described [21, 24]. The limited sensitivity of PCR in tissues [25], particularly during a systemic treatment probably explains the absence cryptosporidium detection in the liver biopsy in the case presented here. The absence of any alternative toxic, infectious, neoplastic, mechanical, allo or autoimmune cause, suggests that the increase in bilirubin observed during this period may be attributable to direct biliary tract involvement by microsporidial infection. To note, antiparasitic drug–induced hepatotoxicity in this patient cannot be excluded. However, this hypothesis appears unlikely given the absence of clear evidence in the literature supporting liver toxicity associated with nitazoxanide or paromomycin [26]. Despite reduction of immunosuppression and combined antiparasitic therapy, the patient developed acute liver failure. This highlights the importance of early recognition of Cryptosporidium infection and prompt initiation of targeted treatment. Although rare, reversal or stabilization of Cryptosporidium-induced cholangitis has been described [18, 22, 27, 28].

Finally, environmental risk factors for Cryptosporidium infection should be systematically investigated in SOT patients presenting with diarrhea. In this case, the patient lived in a rural area with nearby animal farms. Other common sources of exposure include ingestion of contaminated water or food, and travel to endemic regions [29].

In conclusion, this case emphasizes the potential for severe biliary complications associated with Cryptosporidium infection in liver transplant recipients, underlining the need for early diagnosis and rapid initiation of specific therapy.
