Technology for helping people with neuromotor, intellectual, and speech disabilities engage in leisure and communication activities: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Giulio E. Lancioni, Jorge Navarro, Antonella Mellino, Elena Bortolozzi, Nirbhay N. Singh, Mark F. O’Reilly, Jeff Sigafoos, Gloria Alberti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We extended the assessment of our recently developed technology to help people with neuromotor, intellectual, and speech disabilities access (listen to) music, communicate with distant partners, and answer questions. The technology included a tablet (1) fitted with an Internet connection, a SIM card, WhatsApp Messenger, and MacroDroid application, and (2) interfaced with different pairs of response sensors. Six participants were included in the study, which was carried out according to a nonconcurrent multiple baseline design across participants. During the baseline phase (5–8 sessions carried out over 1–2 weeks), participants were unable to access music, make video calls, or answer questions using a regular tablet. During the intervention phase (50–102 sessions carried out with the technology over 2–4 months), however, they activated means of 4.7–7.6 songs and 0.4–3.6 video calls per session. Moreover, they answered correctly a mean of 69–96% of the questions presented to them. These results suggest that our technology solution is a viable and potentially valuable resource for people with extensive disabilities.

Introduction

People with extensive neuromotor impairment and intellectual and speech disabilities often have major difficulty engaging in leisure activities or communicating over a device (e.g. telephone or tablet) [1–3]. The need to alleviate this situation and the apparent inability/inadequacy of conventional treatments (e.g. physiotherapy and speech therapy) to foster rapid improvement have prompted the development of technology-aided intervention programs [3–7]. Several of those programs have focused on the use of simple technology solutions to enable people to independently make choices, such as accessing preferred songs or engaging in communication with distant partners [2,3,8,9]. Recently, an extended program was developed in which people were also asked to answer questions (e.g. about objects, animals, and humans) [10,11]. Answering those questions was considered to be a valuable activity suitable for people who are unable to engage in the use of objects and tend to be passive and detached [12,13].

The aim of this study was to continue the evaluation of the aforementioned extended program after an update of its technology. The update (1) included new arrangements and activation conditions for the sensors the participants were to use to access songs and video calls and answer questions, and (2) served to make the technology suit the situation of the six participants involved.

Method

Participants

The participants here identified with the pseudonyms of Casey, Jonah, Colin, Jett, Peter, and Karin were 26–64 years old. They were unable to walk and manipulate/use objects and had no speech skills or could utter only a few difficult-to-understand words. Their intellectual disability was estimated (by the psychological service of the rehabilitation and care center they attended) to be within the moderate range. No intelligence quotient scores were available. Their Vineland age equivalents [14] were between about 2.5 years and slightly below 6 years on receptive communication and between below 1 year and nearly 2 years on daily living skills (personal subdomain). Their complex condition was due to traumatic brain injury (Casey) or congenital encephalopathy (all others).

Setting and technology

The study was carried out in quiet rooms of the rehabilitation and care center the participants attended. The technology involved a tablet with an Android operating system, which was (1) fitted with an Internet connection, a SIM card, WhatsApp Messenger, and the MacroDroid application (regulating its functioning), and (2) interfaced with pairs of response sensors. Participants used one of three possible pairs of sensors specifically arranged for them (see Fig. 1a–c). The first pair (Fig. 1a) included an optic sensor and a touch sensor that were activated with the fingers and the back of the right hand, respectively. The second (Fig. 1b) included two touch sensors that were activated by inward movements of the left and right hand/arm. The third (Fig. 1c) included two pressure sensors that were activated by a light finger push.

(a–c) Schematic representation of the three pairs of sensors, of the arrangement of the sensors in front of the tablet, and of the participants’ hand(s) position in relation to the sensors (i.e. to activate them). The tablet screen shows the images of music and telephone.

Every session started with the tablet presenting a music note and a telephone image (see Fig. 1) and asking the participants whether they wanted to listen to music or call somebody. If the participants activated the sensor in front of the music note/image, the tablet presented the photos of two singers and asked the participants whether they wanted to listen to singer name one or singer name two. Activating the sensor in front of one of the singers led the tablet to play a song by that singer for 1.5 min. Thereafter, the smartphone returned to step one to present the images of music and telephone and repeat the same question as above. If the participants activated the sensor in front of the telephone image, the tablet presented the photos of two preferred communication partners and asked whether they wanted to call partner name one or partner name two. Activating one of the sensors led the tablet to start a video call with the partner corresponding to that sensor.

Following the participants’ completion of two or three choice sequences leading to the activation of two or three songs and calls, the tablet presented a series of five questions (based on the participants’ estimated skills). Each question was accompanied by the appearance of two images on the tablet screen. For example, the question ‘Which one is a girl?’ could occur while the tablet showed the images of a girl and another person (e.g. a lady). If the participants answered correctly (activating the sensor in front of the girl), the tablet delivered verbal approval and presented the next question. If they were not correct, the tablet remained idle until the correct answer occurred. Once all questions of the series had been answered, a new choice period started with two or three new choice sequences involving other singers and communication partners. A session contained four choice periods interspersed with three series of questions.

Experimental conditions

A nonconcurrent multiple baseline design across participants was used [15]. Participants started with 5–8 baseline sessions, which occurred over 1–2 weeks and tested whether they could access music, make video calls, or answer questions using a regular tablet. Then, they received 50–102 intervention sessions, which were carried out over 2–4 months with the use of the technology system working as described earlier. The intervention sessions were preceded by four familiarization/practice sessions.

The data recorded concerned the frequency of songs and video calls activated, the frequency of questions answered correctly at the first attempt, and the sessions’ length. The percentage of interrater agreement (computed over at least 20% of the sessions by dividing the number of sessions in which the two raters reported the same frequencies and session lengths differing less than 1.5 min by the total number of sessions in which two raters were available and multiplying by 100%) was above 90% for all participants.

Results and discussion

Figure 2 summarizes the results for the six participants. The black dots and empty circles represent the mean frequency of songs and video calls activated per session, respectively, over blocks of two sessions during baseline and three sessions during the intervention. The asterisks represent the mean percentage of questions answered correctly at the first attempt over the same blocks of sessions. During the baseline, the participants were unable to use the regular tablet, and the sessions were interrupted after failure on the first two choice sequences and on the first question (i.e. to minimize frustration). During the intervention, their mean frequency of songs and calls activated ranged from 4.7 (Peter) to 7.6 (Jonah) and from 0.4 (Jonah) to 3.6 (Peter) per session, respectively. Their mean percentage of questions answered correctly at the first attempt ranged from 69% (Casey) to 96% (Peter). These percentages were significantly above the 50% chance level (P < 0.01) as indicated by a binomial test. The mean session length across participants was about 18 min.

The black dots and empty circles represent the mean frequency of songs and video calls activated per session, respectively, over blocks of two sessions during baseline and three sessions during the intervention. Blocks with different numbers of sessions are marked with a numeral indicating how many sessions they include. The asterisks represent the mean percentage of questions answered correctly at the first attempt over the same blocks of sessions.

These results suggest that the participants used the developed technology to engage in leisure and communication activities and to answer verbal questions. Based on these results, one may argue that similar technology solutions could be adopted to improve the condition and autonomy of people with extensive disabilities [11,13,16]. Having people answer verbal questions through technology might be viewed as a functional activity that can supplement their leisure and communication engagement and help reduce their passivity and withdrawal [13,17]. Providing people with specific (personalized) sensor arrangements may be critical to ensure their responding.

The main limitations of this study concern the failure to assess whether (1) the activations of songs and video calls reflected participants’ purposeful choices, (2) the impact of the technology could generalize across settings, and (3) participants and staff liked the technology [18–21]. Future research may address the aforementioned limitations and further upgrade the technology to make it more easily applicable across people and settings [22].
