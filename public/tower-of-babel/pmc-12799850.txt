ZAR1 pathogenic variants disrupt maternal mRNA storage and cause oocyte maturation defects in humans
Authors: Xiu-Quan Liao, Shuai Zhao, Chang-Long Zhang, Yan Zhu, Jun Wen, Yang Wang, Shao-Yuan Liu, Wei Su, Jiang-Tao Zhang, Qiong-Wen Lu, Fei Gong, Ge Lin, Heng-Yu Fan, Wei Zheng, Han Zhao, Qian-Qian Sha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Zygote arrest 1 (Zar1) was one of the earliest identified maternal-effect genes that function during the maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) in mice. However, the role of human ZAR1 remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the association of ZAR1 gene variants with infertility in women, focusing on their impact on oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. Using whole-exome sequencing, we identified homozygous and compound heterozygous ZAR1 variants in two independent families with female patients diagnosed with primary infertility owing to oocyte maturation and early embryonic arrest. Functional analysis revealed that the V118A variant disrupted the localization of ZAR1 to the mitochondria-associated ribonucleoprotein domain (MARDO) structure, which is a key mRNA storage site in oocytes. The R397Q and S121* mutations impaired ZAR1’s RNA-binding capability, indirectly affecting its subcellular localization and the integrity of MARDO. These variants disrupted MARDO formation and function, leading to defective oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. This study highlights the critical roles of ZAR1 and MARDO in human reproduction and provides insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying some forms of female infertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06000-4.

Introduction

Infertility affects millions of individuals of reproductive age globally, and assisted reproductive technologies (ART) are commonly used to treat it [1, 2]. The number of children conceived via ART has been steadily increasing [3–5], with in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) emerging as the two most prevalent techniques. Oocyte maturation, including nuclear and cytoplasmic maturation, is the first and most pivotal process that ensures precise cleavage after fertilization [6, 7]. Cytoplasmic maturation encompasses subcellular processes such as cytoskeleton assembly, organelle distribution, maternal mRNA decay, and mRNA storage [8–11]. Defects in these processes can also cause recurrent preimplantation embryo developmental arrest (RPEA) [12, 13].

Oocyte meiotic maturation and early embryonic development occur in a transcriptionally inactive state [14], controlled by maternally stored mRNAs. Embryonic gene transcription only resumes with zygotic genome activation (ZGA) following fertilization [8, 15]. Thus, appropriate storage and degradation of maternal mRNA are essential for the development of fully grown oocytes and pre-ZGA embryos [16]. Maternal mRNAs typically undergo degradation during maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT), a phase in which developmental control shifts from the maternal transcriptome to the zygotic transcriptome [17, 18]. Dysregulation of maternal mRNA clearance is associated with various reproductive disorders such as delayed follicular growth, oocyte maturation defects, early embryonic arrest, oocyte aging, and infertility [19–21].

Mutations in various genes related to the cell cycle and meiosis result in the arrest of embryo development [22]. Cell division cycle 20 (CDC20), a co-activator of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), aids in the transition from metaphase to anaphase. Biallelic mutations in CDC20 cause female infertility, characterized by oocyte maturation defects, fertilization failure, and abnormal early embryonic development in humans [23, 24]. The thyroid hormone receptor interactor 13 (TRIP13) serves as a spindle assembly checkpoint. Its mutations lead to human oocyte maturation arrest and cause zygotic cleavage failure [25, 26]. F-box protein 43 (FBXO43), also known as endogenous meiotic inhibitor 2 (EMI2), helps maintain oocyte arrest and early embryonic development. Mutations in FBXO43 result in early embryonic arrest in humans [27]. The subcortical maternal complex (SCMC), a multimeric protein complex uniquely expressed in mammalian oocytes and early embryos, is critical for zygote formation after the initial embryonic cell division. Mutations in SCMC genes, including KH domain-containing 3-like (KHDC3L) [28], NLR family, pyrin domain-containing 2 (NLRP2) [29] and 5 (NLRP5) [29], peptidylarginine deiminase 6 (PADI6) [28], and transducin-like enhancer of split 6 (TLE6) [28], are directly linked to embryonic developmental arrest in humans. Overall, these studies highlight the importance of the cytoskeleton in oocyte meiotic maturation and the MZT.

Mutations in genes associated with mRNA decay and storage cause arrest during embryonic development. B-cell translocation gene 4 (BTG4), a crucial adaptor of the carbon catabolite repression 4 (CCR4)-negative on TATA-less (NOT) complex, regulates maternal mRNA decay in mice and humans [20, 30, 31]. Four previously reported homozygous mutations in BTG4 were shown to disrupt its interaction with CNOT7, leading to zygotic cleavage failure in human embryos [31]. ZFP36L2 recruits the CCR4-NOT complex to degrade maternal transcripts during meiotic maturation; however, biallelic ZFP36L2 gene variants hinder this process, causing embryonic developmental arrest by preserving maternal mRNA in fertilized eggs [12, 19]. PATL2 modulates the expression and degradation of maternal mRNAs by interacting with EIF4E and CPEB1 [32, 33]. The Y-box binding protein 2 (YBX2) forms a reversible sponge-like cortical partition with maternal mRNA in mammalian oocytes, serving as a reservoir to store and safeguard mRNA [34]. Biallelic mutations in human MOS affect extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) phosphorylation and mRNA clearance [35–37]. LSM family member 14B (LSM14B) functions as a component of P-bodies and the mitochondria-associated ribonucleoprotein domain (MARDO) [38–41]. Genetic knockout of Lsm14b hinders proper storage and clearance of mRNAs, thereby affecting oocyte growth and meiotic maturation. Taken together, these analyses of human oocytes and embryos suggest the presence of regulators similar to those in mice, indicating the potential involvement of specific factors in controlling maternal mRNA stability in humans [30, 42].

In addition to MZT-coupled maternal mRNA decay, recent studies have made significant progress in our understanding of RNA storage in developing oocytes [11]. Mouse oocytes in early development contain large P-body-like granules that store mRNA; however, these granules disperse as the oocytes grow and mature [43, 44]. Recent studies have shown that mRNA is primarily stored in the MARDO of fully grown oocytes [11]. MARDO is a non-membranous region in mammalian oocytes that is assembled by the mitochondrial-associated RNA-binding protein (RBP) zygote arrest 1 (ZAR1). Its primary function is to store and protect maternal mRNA, thereby supporting oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. Despite its importance in mouse oocytes, the role of ZAR1 in MARDO assembly in humans has not been investigated.

Zar1 was one of the earliest identified oocyte-specific maternal-effect genes that function during the MZT period in mice [45, 46]. ZAR1 exists in vertebrates, ranging from zebrafish to humans; however, its exact molecular function remains unclear [47–51]. Previous studies have demonstrated that in mature mouse oocytes, the storage of maternal mRNA relies on ZAR1 as an RBP and that ZAR1 also regulates mRNA translation [51, 48]. In germinal vesicle (GV)-stage oocytes, ZAR1 protein levels are elevated; these decline during meiotic maturation and fertilization, and become undetectable in two-cell embryos [45, 51]. Additionally, 80–90% of zygotes derived from Zar1−/− oocytes are arrested at the one-cell stage, with no development observed beyond the two-cell stage [45, 51]. Proteins with RNA-binding domains and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) may facilitate the formation of phase-separated liquids through collective interactions between RNA-protein and IDR-IDR [52]. ZAR1’s protein structure, which exhibits this feature, reveals its biochemical role as a core component of MARDO. According to bioinformatics predictions, ZAR1 is associated with developmental arrest in human embryos [53].

In the present study, we investigated three ZAR1 mutations identified in women with infertility, c.353T > C (p.V118A), c.1190G > A (p.R397Q), and c.362 C > A (p.S121*). These mutations specifically affect the IDRs and RNA-binding domain of ZAR1. Plasmids encoding either mutant or wild-type (WT) ZAR1 were overexpressed in mouse oocytes to assess the functional consequences of the c.353T > C (p.V118A) mutation, a homozygous recessive variant. Our results showed that the mutated ZAR1 lost mitochondrial co-localization, indicating disruption of the MARDO structure. This suggests that the c.353T > C mutation impairs ZAR1 phase separation and mitochondrial localization. In contrast, both the c.1190G > A (p.R397Q) and c.362 C > A (p.S121*) mutations disrupted RNA-binding capability and impaired MARDO formation.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

Two unrelated female patients with infertility of unknown etiology were recruited for this study. The affected individuals had normal menstrual cycles and sex hormone levels. Patient 1 was a 25-year-old with a 4.5-year history of unexplained primary infertility. Initially, IVF was performed using gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist and short GnRH agonist protocols. Only two of the 11 oocytes developed into metaphase II in the two cycles, suggesting an oocyte maturation defect. In this patient, one zygote with two pronuclei (PNs) was produced and eventually arrested at 5-cell stage (Table 1). Patient 2 was a 38-year-old woman diagnosed with unexplained primary infertility for 14 years. She underwent different IVF protocols, including short, GnRH antagonists, and mild ovarian stimulation. However, the quality of oocytes obtained in all protocols was poor, and many granulosa cells surrounded them (Fig. 1). Only 9 of the 16 ovulated oocytes formed 2PN embryos. However, these embryos were arrested between the 2-cell and 7-cell stages (Table 1). Therefore, IVF attempts produced embryos that were arrested on day 3, although fertilization was initially successful in all cases. Identification of ZAR1 pathogenic variants

Whole-exome sequencing was performed to identify pathogenic variants. After filtering for frequency (< 0.01%), oocyte expression levels, and corresponding functions, ZAR1 (GenBank: NM_175619.3) was identified as the most likely candidate gene for the disease. One homozygous and two compound heterozygous variants were selected (Fig. 2A). All variants showed low frequency in the Exome Aggregation Consortium and gnomAD exome databases (Table 2) and were confirmed using Sanger sequencing. Patient 1 carried the homozygous variant c.353T > C (p.V118A) (Fig. 2A). Patient 2 had two heterozygous variants: a nonsense variant, c.362 C > A (p.S121*) and a missense variant, c.1190G > A (p.R397Q) (Fig. 2A). The amino acids at positions p.V118, p.S121, and p.R397 were highly conserved across the species (Fig. 2C). The C-terminus of ZAR1 hosted a conserved ZF-3CxxC domain, which was responsible for RNA binding (Fig. 2D). R397 in Patient 2 was located within the ZF-3CxxC domain. Another mutation site in Patient 2, p.S121*, produced a truncated protein lacking an RNA-binding domain. The N-terminus of ZAR1 was generally less conserved than the C-terminus, but contained the IDR, which was conserved between humans and mice, particularly the amino acids adjacent to the p.V118A mutation site (Fig. 2D). This region is responsible for the formation of the MARDO structure in mice, suggesting a potential impact of the p.V118A mutation on MARDO formation in human oocytes (Fig. 2D). These results indicated a potential association between ZAR1 mutations and embryo arrest.

The c.353T > C (p.V118A) variant of ZAR1 fails to localize to the MARDO structure and impairs oocyte maturation

To evaluate the functional impact of the identified ZAR1 variants, we transfected HeLa cells (which do not endogenously express ZAR1) with plasmids expressing either WT or mutant ZAR1. Western blot results showed that the protein expression levels of the hZAR1V118A and hZAR1R397Q variants were comparable to those of hZAR1WT. However, the nonsense variant hZAR1S121* resulted in notably reduced expression of the ZAR1 protein and generated a truncated product (Fig. 3A). Similarly, the protein expression level of ZAR1V118A did not differ from that of hZAR1WT in mouse oocytes after mRNA microinjections (Fig. 3B). In addition, we developed a polyclonal antibody that specifically recognizes mouse ZAR1 (hereafter called mZAR1) but does not recognize human ZAR1 (hZAR1) (Fig. 3C). MARDO [11] is a crucial structure for mRNA storage in fully grown oocytes and is commonly found in human and mouse oocytes. This structure becomes more prominent in the fully grown oocytes, particularly those with a ‘surrounding nucleolus’ (SN) feature. MARDO formation relies on an undefined N-terminus of ZAR1 where the c.353T >C (p.V118A) mutation is located. To evaluate the effect of this mutation on MARDO formation, we injected GFP, GFP-hZAR1WT, and GFP-hZAR1V118A into mouse GV oocytes. Live-cell imaging demonstrated that GFP-hZAR1WT formed distinct cytoplasmic clusters, which colocalized with mitochondria as confirmed by Mito-Tracker staining. Whereas, GFP-hZAR1V118A exhibited a diffuse distribution pattern similar to that of GFP (Fig. 3D-E). This demonstrated that V118 is a key residue for promoting MARDO coalescence. The localization of RBP to MARDO depends on its binding to RNA. However, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) results indicated that the V118A mutation in hZAR1 did not affect its interaction with RNA (Fig. 3F).

We also investigated the effects of hZAR1V118A on mouse oocyte maturation. Transcripts encoding hZAR1WT or hZAR1V118A were microinjected into GV stage-arrested oocytes, followed by a 12-h culture in medium containing milrinone to inhibit meiotic resumption. Oocytes were subsequently released from meiotic arrest upon milrinone removal. The oocytes overexpressing hZAR1V118A normally underwent GV breakdown (GVBD) but showed a slight reduction in the polar body emission (PBE) rate compared with control oocytes (Fig. 4A–B). Abnormal spindle organization and chromosomal misalignment were observed in oocytes overexpressing hZAR1V118A (Fig. 4C-D). Immunofluorescence revealed hZAR1WT localization around the spindle; however, the hZAR1V118A variant was diffusely distributed in the ooplasm (Fig. 4C). According to a previous study, MARDO formation is predominantly regulated by ZAR1 and functions as an mRNA storage site, co-localizing with multiple RBPs, mitochondria, and mRNAs. We hypothesized that the hZAR1V118A variant disrupts MARDO function by altering its intracellular localization. Immunoprecipitation (IP) demonstrated that ZAR1 could self-interact, and the interaction between hZAR1V118A and ZAR1WT was maintained, indicating that the mutation does not alter this interaction (Fig. 4E). However, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that overexpression of hZAR1V118A, instead of hZAR1WT, disrupted the aggregation of endogenous mZAR1 in mouse oocytes (Fig. 4F). The RBPs MSY2 and LSM14B are key components of MARDO. They maintain the stability of maternal mRNAs in human and mouse oocytes [51, 54, 55]. The c.353T >C (p.V118A) mutation in hZAR1 did not disrupt its interaction with MSY2 or LSM14B (Fig. 4G-H). However, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that in more than half of the oocytes, hZAR1V118A overexpression led to LSM14B dispersion (Fig. 4I-J). These results suggested that hZAR1V118A impaired the RBP localization to MARDO.

The ZAR1 variant c.353T > C (p.V118A) impairs maternal mRNA storage

Considering that the hZAR1V118A mutation disrupts oocyte maturation, we sought to investigate whether its pathogenic mechanism involves the disruption of MARDO structural integrity and RNA storage function. Poly(A) FISH analysis showed distinct mRNA clusters in hZAR1WT, but these clusters were disrupted in hZAR1V118A (Fig. 5A). Additionally, subcellular localization analysis revealed changes in RNA mitochondrial localization, suggesting the disruption of MARDO RNA storage function (Fig. 5A–C). RT-qPCR analysis showed reduced expression of key transcription factors in mouse oocytes injected with hZAR1V118A compared with those injected with hZAR1WT (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, re-analysis of publicly available data from Zar1 knockout mice (GEO: GSE93941) corroborated these findings (Fig. 5E). Discussion

In this study, we characterized two pathogenic ZAR1 mutations: p.V118A in Patient 1 and two heterozygous variants, p.R397Q and p.S121* in Patient 2, revealing their distinct effects on MARDO. Specifically, the p.V118A mutation (located in the IDR region) disrupted the localization of ZAR1 to the MARDO structure in oocytes, resulting in mislocalization of mRNA. In contrast, the c.1190G >A (p.R397Q) mutation is located within the RNA-binding domain of ZAR1, substantially impairing its ability to interact with RNA, while the c.362 C >A (p.S121*) mutation does not involve this domain. Both the R397Q and S121* mutations impaired ZAR1’s RNA-binding capability and indirectly affected its subcellular localization and the integrity of MARDO function. These mutations disrupted mRNA storage, resulting in defects in oocyte maturation and embryo developmental arrest (Fig. 7). In biological systems, IDRs independently initiate protein phase separation [56]. In addition, interactions between RNA and RBPs may strengthen phase separation [57]. This process is modulated by RBDs, particularly in environments with varying RNA concentrations [56]. RNA may enhance the solubility of RBPs through competitive or conformational inhibition [58]. The absence or mutation [57, 59] of structural domains in RBPs can modify RNA processing, in which both RBPs and RNA act as structural scaffolds [58, 60] during phase separation. Therefore, the c.1190G >A (p. Additionally, the diverse properties of RNA play a significant role in the regulation of phase separation, including its negative charge [61], secondary structure [62], interaction dynamics [62], nucleotide composition, length, modifications [63–65], and expression levels [58]. Collectively, these factors influence protein aggregation and separation processes of proteins [58]. Due to the combined effects of charge interactions and the ability of a single RNA molecule to recruit multiple RBPs, proteins are brought into proximity to facilitate phase separation [66, 67]. In conclusion, these insights highlight the dual regulation of RNA binding protein and RNA in phase-separation mechanisms, providing valuable perspectives for interpreting this biological phenomenon.

Recent studies have shown that the impediment to embryonic progression in Zar1-deficient mouse zygotes stems primarily from the inability of Zar1–/– oocytes to undergo proper meiotic maturation [51]. Consistent with this, in our study, direct microinjection of the c.353T >C (p. V118A) mutation into mouse GV oocytes impaired MARDO formation and meiotic maturation. These results provided genetic evidence that ZAR1 is an oocyte-specific maternal-effect gene in humans. This study suggested that human ZAR1 plays a key role in maintaining maternal mRNA stability in early embryos, prior to ZGA. During MZT, maternal mRNAs are removed via two successive pathways: maternal factor-mediated decay (M-decay) and zygotic factor-dependent decay (Z-decay). The Z-decay pathway appears to play a more significant role in humans than in mice, with most maternal transcripts being degraded at the 8-cell stage in humans, compared with the observation that a large number of maternal mRNAs are removed in mouse MII oocytes and zygotes before ZGA. Therefore, a more robust mechanism is required to maintain maternal mRNA stability in humans than that in mice. Our results demonstrated that ZAR1 is a key factor in the prevention of precocious maternal mRNA decay in humans. ZAR1 may protect maternal transcripts by (1) localizing these mRNAs to MARDO by direct binding, (2) restricting the accessibility of RNA-destabilizing enzymes, such as the CCR4-NOT complex, to their substrates, and (3) recruiting other RBPs that antagonize RNA degradation activity.

This study extended the investigation of ZAR1, one of the best-known maternal-effect genes in mice, from rodents to humans. Since the initial investigation of ZAR1 function in Zar1 knockout mice more than two decades ago, this is the first report of female infertility-associated ZAR1 pathogenic mutations in humans. This finding extends the short list of human maternal-effect genes and is expected to assist in the clinical genetic diagnosis of women with oocyte maturation failure or RPEA. These results highlight the importance of the ZAR1 IDR in the assembly of human MARDO by phase separation and the importance of human MARDO in stabilizing the maternal transcriptome after fertilization. In addition, this study provides experimental evidence that the C-terminal zinc-finger domain of human ZAR1 is essential for RNA binding and indirectly affects the localization of ZAR1 within oocytes. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the expression pattern of ZAR1 during human MZT and identify additional ZAR1 mutations in human patients with female infertility and various abnormalities in oocyte maturation and preimplantation embryonic development.

Although the V118A mutation in human ZAR1 disrupted phase separation in fully grown oocytes, the corresponding V88A mutation in mouse ZAR1 did not produce a detectable phenotype (Fig S1C-D). Mouse and human ZAR1 share only approximately 59% amino acid identity, and although the N-terminal regions of both proteins promote phase separation, their sequences differ substantially. This discrepancy may reflect the species-specific structural and functional differences within the N-terminal IDR. Future studies aimed at systematically identifying and mutating candidate residues in the mZAR1 N-terminal region will be crucial for determining which elements are conserved and which are species-specific in governing ZAR1-mediated phase separation. In addition, live-cell imaging demonstrated that GFP-hZAR1WT formed distinct cytoplasmic clusters that colocalized with mitochondria, consistent with phase separation in the oocyte cytoplasm. In contrast, the hZAR1V118A failed to assemble such condensates and instead showed marked nuclear accumulation (Fig. 3). One possible explanation is that ZAR1 is capable of interacting with nuclear proteins under physiological conditions, but in the wild-type state, the formation of large cytoplasmic condensates may physically restrict its nuclear entry, maintaining the protein in a perinuclear position. Disruption of phase separation by the V118A mutation could release this spatial constraint, thereby allowing hZAR1 to translocate into the nucleus. Future studies could investigate the molecular determinants of ZAR1’s cytoplasmic retention and nuclear translocation, and assess how disruption of phase separation impacts its functional interactions within the oocyte.

Methods and materials

Clinical samples

Individuals with infertility diagnosed with oocyte maturation defects and early embryonic arrest were recruited from the Reproductive and Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya and the Center for Reproductive Medicine, Shandong University, China. All blood samples were collected after informed consent was obtained. This study involving human subjects was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Reproductive Medicine, Shandong University and the Ethics Committee of the Reproductive (2019LSZ14) and Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya (LL-SC-2021-034).

Animals

The experimental protocols involving mice were approved by the Zhejiang University Institutional Animal Care and Research Committee (ZJU20210252) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (GY2024-413). Mouse care and use were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Mouse oocyte culture

Female mice (6–8 weeks old) were injected with 5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (Ningbo Sansheng Pharmaceutical, China) and euthanized after 44 h. Oocytes at the GV stage were harvested in M2 medium (M7167; Sigma-Aldrich) and cultured in mini-drops of M16 medium (M7292; Sigma-Aldrich) covered with mineral oil (M5310; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. In some experiments, milrinone (2.5 mM) was added to the culture medium to inhibit spontaneous GVBD.

TA cloning

ZAR1 gene fragments containing the c.C362A and c.G1190A mutations were amplified from the patient’s genomic DNA using PCR with specific primers designed to flank the mutation sites. The PCR reactions were performed with Taq DNA polymerase to generate A-overhangs at the 3’ ends of the amplicons. The purified PCR products were ligated into a T/A cloning vector using T4 DNA ligase at 16 °C for 2 h. The ligation products were transformed into chemically competent DH5α cells via heat shock at 42 °C, followed by recovery in SOC medium for 1 h at 37 °C. Transformed cells were plated on LB agar containing ampicillin, X-gal, and IPTG, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. White colonies were selected and screened using PCR to confirm the presence of the inserted fragments. Positive clones were subjected to Sanger sequencing to verify sequences and identify specific mutations.

In vitro transcription and preparation of mRNAs for microinjection

Plasmids were linearized using the appropriate restriction enzymes. Then, 5-capped mRNAs were synthesized using Sp6 or T7 MESSAGE MACHINE kits (Invitrogen, AM1340 or AM1344, respectively), and poly (A) tails were added using a Poly (A) Tailing Kit (Invitrogen, AM1350). Synthesized mRNA was recovered by lithium chloride precipitation and dissolved in nuclease-free water. All microinjections were performed using an Eppendorf Transfer Man NK2 micromanipulator. Approximately 10 pL of synthetic RNA was microinjected into the ooplasm. The oocytes were harvested in M2 medium (M7167; Sigma-Aldrich) containing 2.5 mM milrinone to inhibit spontaneous GVBD.

Whole-exome sequencing and variant analysis

Human peripheral blood DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Exome capture and sequencing were performed using the Agilent Sure Select Whole Exome Capture and Illumina platforms. After sequencing, the reads were converted to FASTQ format and mapped to reference genome GRCh38 using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner. Variants were identified using the GATK pipeline and subsequently annotated using ANNOVAR, dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, gnomAD, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and MutationTaster. The variants were prioritized based on the following filtering criteria: (1) minor allele frequency < 0.01% in the ExAC and gnomAD databases, and (2) loss-of-function variants or damaging missense variants predicted by at least two software programs (SIFT, Polyphen-2, and MutationTaster). Next, the filtered candidate variants were verified through Sanger sequencing of family members.

Immunofluorescence analysis of cultured cells and oocyte

The cultured cells were seeded on coverslips and transfected with the designated plasmids. After 24 h, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS (PBST), and incubated with blocking buffer (PBST containing 5% bovine serum albumin). The cells were sequentially probed with primary antibodies, as described in the Results section, and with Alexa Fluor 594- or 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (Molecular Probes). The slides were mounted using VectaShield with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Vector Laboratories). Imaging was performed using LEICA DMi8 and Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscopes.

The oocytes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min and permeabilized in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS, oocytes were incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h and sequentially labeled with Alexa Fluor 594- or 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (Molecular Probes) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Vector Laboratories) for 30 min. Imaging was performed using LEICA DMi8 and Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscopes. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Poly (A) FISH assay and quantification

The oocytes were washed with 0.2% bovine serum albumin and transferred to 4% PFA for 25 min at room temperature. Next, the oocytes were permeabilized at room temperature for 20 min with 2% Triton X-100 and 1× RNA ribonuclease (RNase) inhibitor, and transferred to a wash buffer (preheated to 37 °C) containing 1× RNA RNase inhibitor for 15 min. Next, the oocytes were transferred to a hybridization buffer, into which a probe was added, and incubated in a thermostat incubator overnight at 37 °C in the dark. This was followed by washing the oocytes in warm wash buffer for at least 50 min at 37 °C and rinsing in 2× saline-sodium citrate for 2 min at room temperature. Then the oocytes were incubated with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies, similar to those used for immunofluorescence. Specifically, to measure the mitochondrial-localized poly(A) RNA signal relative to cytosolic levels, we performed the following steps in the Fiji software: The mitochondrial channel was processed using a band-pass filter, followed by Li auto-thresholding. Objects > 0.10 μm² were selected as mitochondrial regions of interest (ROIs). The mean poly(A) fluorescence intensity was measured within these mitochondrial ROIs. After excluding the mitochondrial ROIs from the image, the default autothresholding was applied to define the cytosolic region. The mean poly(A) intensity in the compartment was measured. The poly(A) signal ratio was determined by dividing the mitochondrial mean intensity by the cytosolic mean intensity.

Cell culture, plasmid transfection, and immunoprecipitation

HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Human ZAR1 or mouse Zar1 cDNA was ligated into pDEST eukaryotic expression vectors, and site-directed mutagenesis using Primer STAR Max DNA Polymerase (Cat #R045A; Takara) was performed to generate ZAR1 mutants, which were confirmed through sequencing. Msy2, Lsm14b, and clones were selected from the ORF library of humans and cloned into N-terminal HA- or FLAG-tagged pDest vectors. Transient plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000-015; Invitrogen). After a 48-h transfection, cells were lysed in lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 0.5% NP-40; protease inhibitors were added before use] for 20 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was subjected to immunoprecipitation using different affinity gels (Sigma-Aldrich). After incubation for 4 h at 4 °C, beads were washed three times with lysis buffer. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer was added to the beads and the eluted proteins were used for western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

Cells and oocytes were lysed in the SDS loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Total proteins were separated using SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) containing 5% defatted milk for 30 min. After probing with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, the membranes were washed with TBST, incubated with an HRP-linked secondary antibody (Abcam) for 45 min, and washed three times with TBST. The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Ribonucleoprotein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The RIP assay was modified from a previously described method [1]. Briefly, HeLa cells grown in appropriate culture medium were collected and directly lysed in polysome lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), protease inhibitor cocktail, and RNase inhibitor). A total of 10% of the cell lysate supernatant was used as the ‘input,’ while 90% was subjected to immunoprecipitation with protein A/G-coated magnetic beads conjugated with FLAG antibodies. After incubation at 4 °C for 4 h, the beads were thoroughly washed with washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 0.1% Triton X-100, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, a protease inhibitor cocktail, and an RNase inhibitor). The RNA bound to the beads was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) and reverse-transcribed using Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus (M-MLV; Invitrogen). Relative cDNA abundance was analyzed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

RNA isolation and real-time RT-PCR

Oocytes were collected and lysed in lysis buffer (0.2% TritonX-100 and 4 IU RNase inhibitor), followed by reverse transcription with primer transcript II reverse transcriptase (Takara, Kusatsu City, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time RT-PCR analysis was performed using a Light Cycler 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche) and a Roche 480 Real-Time PCR System. The respective cycle threshold (Ct) values were obtained and relative mRNA levels were calculated by normalization to control mRNA levels using Microsoft Excel. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The relative transcript levels of the samples were compared with those of the controls and fold changes were determined. For each experiment, qPCR was performed in triplicate. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Most experiments included at least three independent samples and were repeated at least three times. Experimental groups were compared using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, with statistically significant p-values (< 0.05, < 0.01, and < 0.001) indicated by asterisks (*, **, and ***, respectively). ‘n.s.’ indicates non-significant.
