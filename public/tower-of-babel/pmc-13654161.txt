Temporal leakage and calibration concerns in a pleural-reaction prediction model
Authors: Jun Jiang, Shiye Yuan, Zengping Li
Published: 2026

We read with interest the study by Feng et al. examining pleural reactions after computed tomography (CT)-guided hookwire localization of pulmonary nodules (1). The authors should be commended for systematically characterizing a potentially life-threatening but underrecognized complication and for proposing a clinically oriented severity framework.

Our main concern is that the proposed model is described as supporting preoperative risk assessment, although two of its four predictors—pneumothorax and pulmonary parenchymal hemorrhage—are detected during or after hookwire localization. Predictors should be available at the intended time of prediction; otherwise, temporal leakage converts a pre-procedural prediction model into a post-procedural recognition model (2,3). Previous studies of vasovagal or pleural reactions primarily evaluated baseline and procedural characteristics as candidate risk factors (4,5).

This distinction is not merely semantic. Pneumothorax and pulmonary hemorrhage can themselves produce pain, dyspnea, hypoxemia, anxiety, and hemodynamic deterioration, which overlap substantially with the authors’ definition of pleural reaction. Their large odds ratios may therefore reflect consequences, competing diagnoses, or components of case ascertainment rather than independent antecedent predictors. These complications may be clinically valuable warning signs after puncture, but they cannot identify high-risk patients before puncture.

The reported area under the curve (AUC) of 0.814 also represents apparent performance because the same 324-patient dataset was used for model development and evaluation. Without bootstrap-based optimism correction or cross-validation, model performance is commonly overestimated (6). No calibration plot, calibration intercept or slope, Brier score, decision-curve analysis, or external validation was presented, although contemporary prediction-model guidance emphasizes evaluation beyond discrimination alone (2,3,7).

Moreover, pleural reaction occurred in 2.24% of the source population but constituted 20.3% of the matched analytic sample. Although case-control sampling can support estimation of predictor associations under appropriate assumptions, it does not directly yield calibrated absolute probabilities or a transportable cutoff such as 0.307 without adjustment for sampling fractions and source-population prevalence (8).

A further statistical inconsistency requires clarification. For nodule-to-pleura distance, Tab. 4 and Fig. 6 report an odds ratio of 0.653 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.369–1.105, which crosses unity, despite P=0.048. The Results section instead reports a different interval of 0.428–0.996. These estimates cannot all originate from the same conventional two-sided analysis.

A stronger approach would develop a baseline-only model using the full source cohort, apply bootstrap optimism correction, report calibration and clinical utility, and perform external validation. Pneumothorax and hemorrhage could be evaluated separately in a post-procedural deterioration model. Until then, the current model should be regarded as exploratory and post-procedural rather than as a validated instrument for preoperative risk assessment.
