Ammonia in cancer: dual roles and therapeutic strategies
Authors: Jinhui Wei, Yue Chen, Quanzhang Li, Fuxin Huang, Chuanzhao Zhang, Baohua Hou, Shanzhou Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmmonia, a toxic nitrogenous metabolic byproduct, has garnered increasing attention for its pivotal role in tumor biology. The human body has developed intricate detoxification mechanisms to regulate ammonia homeostasis and maintain acid‒base equilibrium. Through their adaptation mechanisms, cancer cells can exploit ammonia to facilitate their growth and modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. As ammonia is a toxic substance, it can have a toxic effect on tumor, thereby inhibiting tumor growth. This article critically examines the sources and destinations of ammonia within the tumor microenvironment (TME), offering an innovative synthesis of its dual roles in both promoting and inhibiting tumor progression. Additionally, it explores therapeutic strategies targeting ammonia metabolism and anticipates future research trajectories, thereby providing valuable insights and a theoretical framework for ammonia-based therapies in oncology.Keywords: Ammonia, Cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Dual roles, Therapies

Introduction

Historically, ammonia has been considered merely a metabolic waste product present in the body [1, 2]. However, contemporary research has significantly altered this perspective, highlighting the novel functions and diverse impacts of ammonia, thereby reflecting an evolved understanding of its biological roles [3]. With the emergence of ammonia-induced cell death(AICD) as a mode of death [4], studies and reviews on AICD in cancer continue to emerge, leading to more attention being given to the new role of ammonia [5–9].

In humans, ammonia is generated through the catabolism of amino acids during dietary intake and tissue protein hydrolysis and is subsequently converted into urea via the urea cycle (UC) for excretion, thus maintaining ammonia homeostasis [10]. Furthermore, the kidneys play a crucial role in mediating acid‒base balance through renal ammonia metabolism and transport [11, 12].

In the context of cancer cells, ammonia is not solely a byproduct of glutamine and glutamate metabolism [13]; rather, these cells optimize nitrogen utilization by modulating genes associated with ammonia-related metabolism to support their rapid growth and proliferation [14, 15]. Through metabolic reprogramming, cancer cells diminish the conversion of ammonia into urea and increase the synthesis or activation of nutrients derived from ammonia [16, 17]. In addition, the ammonia present within the TME exerts both direct and indirect influences on various immune cells, such as T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, thereby establishing an ammonia-rich milieu that facilitates tumor progression [14, 18, 19].

However, as a toxic substance, ammonia has deleterious effects on cells, including but not limited to pH changes [20], altered metabolism [21, 22], potassium homeostasis disruption [23, 24], mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and compromised oxygen homeostasis [25–27]. Data and reviews concerning the role of ammonia in promoting tumor growth are continually emerging. Nonetheless, the existing body of evidence on the toxic effects of ammonia on cancer cells and its subsequent tumor-inhibitory effects remains relatively limited. This review begins by examining the sources and destinations of ammonia within cancer cells, with a focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms through which ammonia both promotes and inhibits tumor development. This discussion will further extend to therapeutic strategies targeting ammonia metabolism. Additionally, this review offers insights into potential future research directions regarding the role of ammonia in tumor biology. Although a recent review on the urea cycle mentioned the dose-dependent dual effects of ammonia on tumour cells [28], this review is the first to systematically and comprehensively reveal the specific mechanisms underlying this phenomenon.

The sources and destinations of ammonia in cancer cells

The main contributors to ammonia in cancer cells are glutamine, aspartate, and nucleic acid metabolism and the absorption of ammonia from the TME (Fig. 1). Cancer cells increase the expression of solute carrier(SLC) family proteins to facilitate increased uptake of glutamine and ammonia. Specifically, glutamine uptake is augmented through the upregulation of transporters such as SLC38A1, SLC38A2, SLC1A5, SLC6A14, and SLC38A5 [29–31], whereas ammonia/ammonium uptake is increased by the upregulation of SLC4A11 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) or SLC12A2 in multiple myeloma (MM) [32, 33]. Furthermore, owing to the lipid solubility of gaseous NH3, it can traverse cellular membranes via simple diffusion. In mammals, NH3 or NH4+ can also be transported across cell membranes through specific ammonia transport proteins, including Rh Family B Glycoprotein (RhBG) and RhCG [34, 35]. Cancer cells are highly dependent on glutamine metabolism, which is driven by their requirement for rapid proliferation [29, 36]. In this process, glutamine entering cells is converted into glutamate through a reaction catalysed by glutaminase (GLS), accompanied by the release of one ammonia molecule [37]. Subsequently, glutamate undergoes oxidative deamination via glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) to yield α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), with the concomitant release of an additional ammonia molecule [37]. Consequently, high expression of GLS and GDH also promotes tumour progression in HCC or oral cancer cells [38–40]. Furthermore, the metabolism of nucleic acids within cancer cells contributes to ammonia production [41], and the dysregulation of UC in these cells further exacerbates ammonia accumulation [42].

Sources include the metabolism of amino acids and nucleic acids and absorption from the TME. Destinations involve conversion into nutrients, conversion into urea and excretion back into the TME.

The mechanisms by which cancer cells take up or metabolize ammonia involve three primary destinations: first, ammonia can be synthesized into essential nutrients required for tumor growth through metabolic reprogramming; second, it can enter the UC to produce urea; and third, it can be actively expelled by cancer cells into the TME. Ammonia is not merely a waste product in cancer cells; rather, these cells actively reutilize it. In the context of amino acid synthesis, ER-positive breast cancer cells can recover ammonia by upregulating GDH to form glutamate, and pancreatic ductal carcinoma (PDAC) cells can convert ammonia back to glutamine via glutamine synthetase (GS), thereby supporting protein synthesis and metabolic adaptation [16, 43]. In addition, in the absence of glutamine, MCF7 and T47D cells with high expression of GDH2, as well as Hep3B cells with high expression of GDH, are able to convert ammonia to glutamine [40, 44]. When GLS is inhibited, gliomas with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations can still utilize branched-chain amino acids and ammonia to synthesize glutamate through the joint action of branched chain amino acid transaminase 1 (BCAT1) and BCAT2 [45]. With respect to pyrimidine synthesis, KRAS/LKB1-mutant lung cancer cells can incorporate ammonia into carbamoyl phosphate (CP) through the upregulation of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS1), thus facilitating pyrimidine biosynthesis [46]. Additionally, ammonia participates in the UC within cancer cells to synthesize urea. However, the expression levels of several enzymes associated with the urea cycle, specifically CPS1, ornithine carbamoyltransferase (OTC) and arginine succinate synthetase 1 (ASS1), tend to be downregulated in cancer cells. This downregulation appears to facilitate a reduction in nitrogen flow toward the production of urea [14], thereby promoting the synthesis of alternative growth-promoting metabolites [14, 15]. Furthermore, the endogenous arginine produced from ammonia in the urea cycle can be converted into ornithine and urea under the action of Arginase 1 (ARG1). Moreover, a portion of arginine is also converted into nitrogen-containing substances such as polyamines and nitric oxide (NO) [47]. Dysregulation of the UC, coupled with increased metabolic activity in cancer cells, may result in the intracellular accumulation of ammonia. Furthermore, the upregulation of SLC4A11 expression in β-catenin mutant HCC and FYVE-type zinc finger-containing phosphoinositide kinase (PIKFYVE) expression in DU145 prostate cancer cells increase the excretion of ammonia [48, 49].

Different subtypes of cancer cells meticulously regulate the ammonia sources and destinations of ammonia through the previously discussed different biological processes to maintain ammonia homeostasis within themselves and the TME. This regulation is crucial for meeting their metabolic demands and preventing ammonia toxicity. It should be noted that the concentrations of ammonia in tumour interstitial fluids (TIFs) varied between different tumours from 0.5 to 5 mM [3, 18].

The tumor-promoting effect of ammonia

Ammonia is a carcinogenic substance [50, 51]. Variations in the ammonia concentration within the TME actively influence several cancer characteristics, including increased proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and stem cell-like properties, alterations in autophagy, and resistance to chemotherapy and immune surveillance (Fig. 2). Ammonia facilitates the malignant characteristics of cancer cells, including proliferation, invasion, and metastasis

This facilitation primarily occurs through the increase in nutrient production or the activation of associated signaling pathways ER-positive breast cancer cells absorb free ammonia from their microenvironment via a process catalyzed by GDH, which converts α-KG into glutamate. Glutamate subsequently serves as a precursor for the synthesis of proline (Pro), aspartate (Asp), and glutamine, thereby promoting the generation of nonessential amino acids (NEAAs). NEAAs are crucial for the synthesis of various nitrogen-containing compounds and this metabolic cycle accelerates tumor proliferation and progression [16, 52]. Notably, this is the result of exposure to physiological ammonia concentrations (less than 3mM). Furthermore, these derived NEAAs stimulate the activation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), further increasing tumor growth [52–54]. This effect of ammonia (less than 4mM) was also observed in β-catenin mutant HCC with GS ablation [52]. On the contrary, ammonia (1mM) was used to synthesize Gln to promote tumour growth in PDAC cells with high expression of GS, while GS ablation inhibited its growth [43]. Other nitrogen-containing substances generated from ammonia, such as polyamines and NO, also promote the growth and proliferation of tumors [55, 56]. A 2022 study elucidated the mechanism through which ammonia, a byproduct of tumor cell metabolism, increases lipid synthesis, challenging the traditional view of ammonia solely as a nitrogen source. The study demonstrated that ammonia facilitates the dissociation of SREBP-cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) from Insig, subsequently activating sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1 (SREBP-1), which promotes lipid synthesis and tumour proliferation [17]. Notably, this is also the result of exposure to physiological ammonia concentrations (4 mM). This research offered valuable insights into the interplay between various metabolites. Furthermore, cancer cells facilitate their own proliferation by releasing ammonia, which in turn stimulates cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to secrete elevated levels of glutamine into the TME [13, 37, 57]. This process underscores the intercellular communication mediated by ammonia among different cell types.

Ammonia plays a multifaceted role in cancer biology, not only by increasing tumor proliferation and growth but also by facilitating invasion and metastasis. Hyperammonemia has been implicated in the development of liver metastases in advanced tumors [58, 59]. Cancer cells with low expression of ASS1 modulate the intracellular pH through ammonia to create an environment conducive to growth, thereby promoting disease progression and invasion [60]. Moreover, elevated ammonia levels may expedite tumour growth and metastasis by upregulating the glycolytic pathway and increasing lactate production in HCC [61]. This interaction underscores the complex interplay between ammonia and glucose metabolism, indicating that the role of ammonia in energy metabolism extends beyond merely providing a nitrogen source. Consequently, ammonia has emerged as a pivotal component in the cancer metabolic network, driving metabolic transformations that significantly favor tumor progression.

Ammonia plays a significant role in increasing the stemness of cancer cells

Elevated ammonia levels actively contribute to the enrichment and maintenance of cancer stem cells (CSCs) populations, which are pivotal in driving tumour initiation, progression and resistance to treatment [62, 63]. The influence of ammonia extends beyond mere metabolic adaptation; it directly augments the characteristics of CSCs. High concentrations of ammonia may activate stem cell-associated pathways through metabolic reprogramming, thereby selecting for CSCs populations. These selected CSCs demonstrate an increased capacity to utilize ammonia for amino acid synthesis and exhibit increased resistance to metabolic stress [41]. Furthermore, in HCC, ammonia accumulation can increase tumour cell stemness by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in CPS1-deficient cells [64], whereas SLC4A11-mediated ammonia uptake also promotes HCC stemness at 10 mM ammonia concentration [32].It should be noted that this study constructed an HCC model by inducing Myc overexpression and p53 gene knockout, and through high ammonia feeding, revealed that even under conditions of an ammonia concentration exceeding 20 mM, in vivo tumour growth was still promoted. The capacity of ammonia to elicit cellular stress responses prompts ovarian cancer stem cells to upregulate hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) as a mechanism to mitigate the activity of glutamine synthetase in glutamine metabolism [65]. In HCC, ammonia levels above physiological concentrations (10 mM) are linked to an increased presence of stem cells, as evidenced by an increased capacity for sphere formation and a greater proportion of CD133-positive cells [61]. However, the specific mechanism still requires further investigation.

These findings indicate that ammonia contributes to stemness not only through metabolic reprogramming but also by altering the physical and chemical properties of the TME, thereby fostering an environment conducive to the expression of CSC traits, even amid ammonia-induced cellular stress. Tumor stem cells may increase their survival through reprogramming, despite the cellular stress responses elicited by ammonia. Disrupting the stemness of cancer cells can provide a powerful strategy for antitumor treatment [66].

Ammonia facilitates cancer cell survival by regulating autophagy

Ammonia is intricately linked to the process of autophagy. As a byproduct of amino acid metabolism, ammonia serves as a potent inducer of autophagy [67]. Autophagy is stimulated by ammonia generated through glutamine metabolism, enabling cells to shield themselves from stressors such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, or pathogenic threats [68, 69]. For HCC, treatment with physiological ammonia concentrations (0.5 mM) has demonstrated that ammonia activates autophagy to promote the survival of cancer cells [70]. Similarly, under physiological ammonia concentration conditions (less than 5 mM), research indicates that ammonia activates autophagy via the dopamine receptor D3 (DRD3) and the mTOR signalling pathways [71]. This mechanism could elucidate how cancer cells endure stressors such as nutrient deprivation or chemotherapy without succumbing to cell death. As reported in previous research, under conditions of nutrient deprivation or ATP depletion, mTOR signaling is inhibited. However, ammonia can facilitate autophagy via the Ulk1 pathway, thereby facilitating proliferating cell survival [72, 73]. This ammonia-induced autophagy enables cancer cells to shield themselves from external stressors, including the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy drugs, thus establishing ammonia as a stress-adaptive promoter and directly associating its metabolic effects with drug resistance. For example, ammonia generated through glutamine metabolism increases autophagy, and the inhibition of this process by Sirtuin 4 (SIRT4) increases the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) [74]. This observation indirectly suggests that ammonia-induced autophagy diminishes the sensitivity of colorectal cancer to 5-FU.

Ammonia promotes the development of drug resistance in cancer cells

Cancer cells exploit ammonia not only through autophagy to increase their survival under external stress but also to facilitate drug resistance via its influence on various pathways, as evidenced in numerous tumor types. In the context of MM, ammonia (0.5 mM) produced by the intestinal microbe Citrobacter freundii is transported into MM cells via the transmembrane channel protein SLC12A2 [33]. This process stabilizes the NEK2 protein, thereby promoting chromosomal instability and resistance to the therapeutic agent bortezomib. The downregulation of SLC12A2 using furosemide sodium effectively inhibits ammonium uptake by MM cells, thereby mitigating resistance. In glioblastoma (GBM), SREBP-1 upregulates the expression of the glutamine transporter ASCT2, facilitating glutamine uptake into GBM cells. The subsequent release of ammonia from glutamine increases SREBP-1 activation, creating a feedback loop that amplifies glutamine metabolism and lipid synthesis, ultimately leading to resistance against the antipsychotic drug pimozide. Targeting ASCT2 or GLS1 in conjunction with pimozide induces significant mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress, resulting in the death of GBM cells [75]. The phenomenon was validated by treatment with 4 mM NH4OH in vitro. In liver cancer, the accumulation of ammonia leads to the production and excessive activation of reactive oxygen species in CPS1-deficient cells through the AMPK–FAO axis. Moreover, excessive activation of the AMPK–FAO–FOXM axis results in the development of sorafenib resistance in HCC patients with CPS1 deficiency [64].

Furthermore, ammonia accumulation may also interfere with anti-PDL1 therapy. Specifically, the ammonia accumulation and increased glutamine uptake caused by SLC25A15 deficiency activate the mTORC1 pathway, thereby reducing PDL1 expression in liver cancer cells and promoting their resistance to anti-PDL1 treatment. For HCC patients lacking SLC25A15, blocking glutamine metabolism in combination with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment can increase the antitumor therapeutic effect [76].

Ammonia helps tumors create an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Ammonia accumulation within the TME adversely affects the functionality of various immune cells, thereby inducing an immunosuppressive state. This state is characterized by diminished immune cell infiltration and alterations in immune cell phenotypes, which collectively facilitate tumor progression. The TME comprises a diverse array of immune cells, including T cells, NK cells, macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs). Furthermore, intricate interactions occur both among these immune cells and between immune cells and cancer cells [77].

Among them, CD8 T cells can differentiate into various cell types, such as CD8 effector T (Teff) cells, CD8 memory T (Tm) cells, and CD8 exhausted T (Tex) cells. In Teff cells, owing to the lack of CPS1, ammonia accumulates within the cells. This leads to “ammonia death” in CD8 Teff cells by increasing the pH of lysosomes and damaging mitochondrial functions, which also reveals a form of cell death directly caused by ammonia [4]. CD8 Tem cells use β-hydroxybutyrylation to upregulate CP synthetase 1, the amino acid metabolism byproduct ammonia is then fixed to form CP, and then, ammonia is cleared through the urea cycle and the ornithine cycle to promote its development [78]. In advanced CRC, OTC and Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 4 Alpha (HNF4α) are downregulated, promoting ammonia accumulation in the TME, leading to dysregulation of the T-cell trans-sulfuration pathway and ultimately increasing T-cell exhaustion and resistance to ICB treatment. However, the specific enzymatic steps that are altered in the transsulfuration pathway are not clear [79]. Additionally, CD8 T cells gradually transform into CD8 Tex cells during chronic infections. Exhausted T cells upregulate GOT1 through the malate shuttle to produce 2-ketoglutaric acid (2-KG) to detoxify ammonia, thereby enabling them to persist under chronic infection conditions [41, 80]. Recent research has demonstrated that ammonia within the TME can impair the functionality of NK cells and CAR-T cells by diminishing the secretion of mature perforin from lysosomes, consequently inhibiting the antitumor activities of these immune cells. Mechanistically, ammonia increases the pH in secretory lysosomes, which results in the dissociation of perforin from proteoglycans. Dissociated perforin is subsequently susceptible to inactivation and proteolytic degradation [18]. Investigations into the effect of ammonia on T cells have revealed that cancer cells exploit ammonia to induce apoptosis in effector T cells, leading to their death or functional attenuation. Furthermore, ammonia facilitates the conversion of these cells into noncytotoxic memory T cells, exhausted T cells or regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby sustaining them over extended periods and promoting tumor immune evasion. The inhibitory effect of ammonia on T and NK cells may be an important reason for resistance to ICB treatment in cancer.

In DCs, ammonia leads to a reduction in the cell count, phagocytic activity, and lymphocyte stimulation. The mechanisms potentially responsible for ammonia-induced dysfunction in DCs may include ammonia-induced cellular swelling, excessive production of reactive oxygen species, and mitochondrial damage [81]. While direct studies on the effects of ammonia on other immune cells within the TME are currently lacking, research on the role of ammonia as an antitumor immune metabolic regulator suggests that it may also influence the differentiation and suppression of Tregs and contribute to the immunosuppressive phenotype of macrophages [41].

Inhibitory effect of ammonia on tumors

While ammonia is predominantly recognized for its role in facilitating tumor formation, it also has the capacity to inhibit cancer cells due to its toxicity, particularly at elevated concentrations or under certain conditions. Research on these effects is still relatively limited and has focused mainly on direct inhibition of tumor proliferation and growth, disruption of autophagy and induction of apoptosis (Fig. 3). Ammonia inhibits the proliferation and growth of cancer cells

Empirical evidence indicates that ammonia impedes cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, induces genomic instability and inhibits lysosomal-mediated protein hydrolysis. The inhibition of lysosomal protein hydrolysis results in the accumulation of proteins associated with the endocytosis, autophagy and phagocytosis pathways. These findings suggest that while tumors can adapt to and exploit lower physiologically relevant concentrations of ammonia, excessive concentrations (more than 10 mM) disrupt these adaptive mechanisms, leading to genuine cytotoxicity. Furthermore, minor variations in the extracellular pH can substantially influence the cytotoxic effects of ammonia, with a lower extracellular pH—a common characteristic of the TME—potentially mitigating the inhibitory effects of ammonia [82]. High concentrations (10 mM) of ammonium chloride have been demonstrated to significantly reduce the proliferation of liver cancer cells initially, with this inhibitory effect becoming more pronounced over time. Notably, this inhibition of cell proliferation does not induce apoptosis, suggesting the presence of a distinct growth arrest mechanism [61]. Furthermore, high concentrations of ammonia may also inhibit the accumulation of the S phase in HGC27 cells, thereby inhibiting the progression of the tumor cell cycle [83]. Research on the underlying mechanism indicates that the inhibition of the urea cycle by p53 leads to the accumulation of ammonia, decreasing the activity of the rate-limiting enzyme ODC for polyamine synthesis and thus blocking polyamine synthesis, which in turn inhibits tumor proliferation and growth [42, 84]. This study demonstrated the inhibitory effect of ammonia on ODC activity under 5 or 10 mM ammonium chloride conditions. Additionally, ammonia has been shown to induce senescence in HCC. While mutations in β-catenin can trigger the transcription of the ammonia transporter SLC4A11, facilitating the expulsion of excess ammonia, the absence of SLC4A11 impedes ammonia excretion, thereby diminishing the antiaging effects in HCC and consequently inhibiting tumor growth [48]. In this study, a β-catenin-driven HCC mouse model was established through hydrodynamic tail vein injection and successfully revealed that high concentrations of ammonia (more than 6 mM) inhibited the formation of SLC4A11 knockout liver cancer in vivo. In addition, the study suggests that the ageing phenotype of HCC can be promoted under ammonia concentrations of 1–5 mM. β-Catenin mutation protects HCC cells from ageing under relatively low ammonia conditions, whereas cell ageing still occurs under high ammonia concentrations, such as 5 mM. In contrast, another HCC study suggested that even under 10 mM conditions, it can promote the enhancement of HCC stemness and thus tumour growth [32].

The observed phenomenon, in which tumors demonstrate adaptive responses at low ammonia concentrations and inhibitory responses at elevated concentrations, suggests the presence of a potential therapeutic window. By increasing ammonia concentrations beyond the tumor’s adaptive capacity, direct tumor inhibition may be attainable. This approach corresponds to a “toxic” strategy, in contrast to a “detoxification” strategy. To optimize this therapeutic method, it is crucial to elucidate the precise toxicity threshold and the underlying mechanisms of resistance to this toxicity.

Ammonia impairs tumor autophagy and induces apoptosis

Ammonia reportedly has dual effects on autophagy in vitro, with high concentrations inhibiting autophagy while low concentrations activate it [71, 85]. Previous studies have elucidated the role of ammonia in promoting tumor survival and drug resistance through the increase in autophagy. However, excessive ammonia exposure in cancer cells can disrupt autophagic lysosomes, ultimately leading to cell death. For example, in Huh7 liver cancer cells, treatment with ammonia (5 mM ammonium chloride) results in the downregulation of cMyc and its transcription factor β-catenin, thereby attenuating the regulatory effect on autophagy. This attenuation leads to the degradation of autophagic lysosomes and subsequent cell death [70]. This finding contradicts the report showing that a high ammonia concentration (10 mM) enhances the stemness of HCC and promotes tumour growth [32]. Another study on HeLa cells found that high concentrations of ammonia (10 mM) diffuse through the membrane and change the lysosomal pH to block autophagic flux [71]. The accumulation of ammonia within cells or a high ammonia concentration (25 mM) can inhibit the autophagy and proliferation of HCC cells. However, under conditions such as hypoxia, starvation, and high temperature, HCC can promote the expression of CPS1 by upregulating DNAJC24 (a heat shock protein), thereby facilitating the urea cycle to prevent ammonia accumulation and ultimately enabling it to evade the inhibition of autophagy by ammonia and help it survive [86]. In prostate cancer cells, inhibition of the PIKFYVE ammonium channel induces intracellular ammonium accumulation, causing vacuole swelling and impairing lysosomal function and autophagy [49]. Similarly, in glioma cells, increased intracellular osmotic pressure has been observed, resulting in cell swelling [87].

Elevated ammonia levels not only impair autophagy in cancer cells and induce cell death but also trigger apoptosis. In the context of bladder cancer, bladder instillation is a widely utilized therapeutic approach. Numerous studies have demonstrated that using drugs designed for the targeted delivery of urease to facilitate ammonia amplification can enhance bladder cancer treatment [88–90]. In patients with bladder cancer exhibiting TP53 mutations and upregulated CPS1 expression, the coadministration of urease and targeted small interfering RNA (siCPS1) not only catalyzes the decomposition of urea into ammonia, enabling bladder cancer cells to adapt to a high-ammonia environment but also impedes the clearance of ammonia by these cells, leading to its accumulation within the tumor. Ammonia inhibits Tom20 and elevates ROS levels, resulting in mitochondrial damage. It further inhibits MCL-1/BCL-2, leading to the downregulation of phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK), thereby inducing apoptosis in bladder cancer cells by disrupting the mitochondrial/ERK1/2 signaling pathway [89]. Furthermore, the combination of urease with glutamine synthetase inhibitors impedes the conversion of toxic ammonia into nontoxic glutamine within tumors and stromal cells, resulting in localized ammonia accumulation. This process, in conjunction with protein mobilization to increase bladder retention time, markedly suppresses the growth of in situ bladder tumors [90]. The study mentioned that obvious toxicity of ammonia on cancer cells was observed when the concentration was higher than 16 mM. Additionally, in C6 neuroglial cancer cells subjected to elevated ammonia levels (10 mM), the activation of the PKC and NFκB/NO signaling pathways induces apoptosis [91]. Research has demonstrated that inducing extracellular alkalization in tumor stem cells can alter the metabolic state of breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs), subsequently impacting cell survival and promoting apoptosis [92]. Additionally, gaseous ammonia has been identified as a potential agent for causing extracellular alkalization in cancer cells [20], thereby facilitating their apoptotic processes.

Therapeutic strategies targeting ammonia metabolism and translational challenges

As shown in Table 1, in in vitro experiments, lower ammonia concentrations (less than 5 mM) promote tumours, whereas higher ammonia concentrations (more than 5 mM) seem to inhibit tumours and even induce tumour cell death (more than 10 mM). However, under specific gene editing conditions in the body, higher concentrations of ammonia (more than 5 mM) can both promote and inhibit tumour growth, which seems to depend on the manipulation of target genes in the body.

The dual role of ammonia in cancer biology presents distinct opportunities for therapeutic intervention. These therapeutic strategies primarily include removal of ammonia from the TME, the targeted increase in the adaptability of NK and T cells to high ammonia levels, the targeted inhibition of absorption, conversion and utilization of ammonia in tumors, and the exploitation of ammonia toxicity to suppress tumor growth or induce cancer cell apoptosis (Table 2).

Removal of ammonia from the TME to disrupt the environment that supports tumor growth

Based on the aforementioned research indicating that ammonia facilitates tumor progression, it is evident that the concentration of ammonia within the TME increases. The removal of extracellular ammonia can simultaneously reduce the uptake of ammonia by cancer cells for their proliferation and modulate the immune microenvironment to activate immune cells, thereby increasing tumor cell eradication. Specifically, the elimination of ammonia can inhibit tumor growth by diminishing the nitrogen sources necessary for the synthesis of NCAAs and pyrimidine nucleotides. Additionally, it can alter the immune microenvironment by activating CD8 Teff cells and NK cells, decreasing exhausted T cells and Tregs, and reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby improving the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy. For example, in colorectal cancer (CRC), in vivo experiments have demonstrated that the ammonia-clearing agent ornithine can increase the efficacy of anti-PDL1 therapy by activating CD8 T cells [79].

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a significant complication in patients with elevated ammonia levels [93]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at ammonia clearance have demonstrated some efficacy in mitigating this complication among individuals with liver cancer and hyperammonemia. Presently, a variety of pharmacological agents are available that specifically target ammonia reduction, including sodium benzoate, glycerol phenylbutyrate, phenylacetic acid ornithine, AST-120 (a spherical carbon adsorbent) and polyethylene glycol, among others. These pharmacotherapies have shown potential in lowering blood ammonia concentrations, particularly in the context of HE [94]. Nonetheless, the extent to which these agents’ systemic ammonia reduction translates into effective ammonia reduction within the highly localized and metabolically distinct TME remains insufficiently explored. The application of engineered probiotics to metabolize excess ammonia within the TME may offer a targeted approach to specifically decrease ammonia levels in this context [95]. Further research is needed to determine whether reducing systemic ammonia can sufficiently affect ammonia levels within tumors to exert an anticancer effect or whether more targeted administration methods are needed.

Targeting key genes for ammonia processing in immune cells to increase antitumor immunity

This method seeks to mitigate the immunosuppressive effects of ammonia by modulating its processing within immune cells, thereby restoring their antitumor capabilities. The targeting of ammonia-related metabolic pathways, including reshaping of the immune microenvironment, has emerged as a promising strategy for tumor treatment. The approach of metabolic reprogramming to rejuvenate CD8 Tex cells demonstrates high specificity. The inhibition of mitochondrial GLS1, which catalyzes ammonia production, can substantially decrease ammonia levels in CD8 Teff cells, thereby increasing cell proliferation, prolonging survival and increasing the expression of effector molecules. Silencing GLS1 has been shown to inhibit tumor growth in melanoma mouse models and prolong their survival. Furthermore, GLS is recognized as a key gene associated with ammonia-induced cell death. Similarly, the enforced overexpression of CPS1 in CD8 Teff cells, which typically do not express CPS1, has been demonstrated to rescue these cells from ammonia-induced death [4]. This mechanism of ammonia clearance within immune cells presents a promising target for increasing T-cell-based cancer immunotherapy. By either targeting sources of ammonia production within T cells (such as inhibiting GLS1) or augmenting their detoxification capacity (through CPS1 overexpression), the functionality and survival of immune cells can be directly preserved. This approach can be extended to other immune cell types within the TME. By targeting critical genes involved in ammonia metabolism, increasing the detoxification capacity of immune effector cells, or diminishing the ammonia adaptation capability of immunosuppressive cells, it is possible to effectively mitigate resistance to ICB therapy caused by elevated ammonia levels in the TME, thereby improving the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy.

Targeting key genes to prevent cancer cells from absorbing, converting and utilizing ammonia

This approach involves directly disrupting the capacity of cancer cells to absorb or utilize extracellular ammonia, thereby impeding their synthetic metabolic processes. In treatments designed to prevent cancer cells from assimilating ammonia, targeting ammonia transport proteins can effectively obstruct ammonia absorption within the TME [32, 33]. For example, inhibiting SLC4A11 can prevent ammonia uptake, thereby diminishing the stemness of HCC cells, whereas inhibiting SLC12A2 can obstruct ammonium uptake in MM cells, leading to drug resistance. Furthermore, the inhibition of SLC4A11 has been shown to reduce β-catenin-driven tumor growth [48]. These findings suggest a strategy for counteracting drug resistance mechanisms mediated by oncogenes. Given that cancer cells depend on glutamine for proliferation, inhibiting glutamine transport channels to obstruct their uptake may decrease intracellular ammonia production and impede their growth. Nonetheless, cancer cells exhibit increased expression of glutamine transport channels on their surface, necessitating more precise and targeted therapeutic interventions. These findings underscore the potential of precision medicine approaches, which can tailor treatment targets on the basis of the specific genetic variations and adaptive metabolic pathways of tumors. However, several drugs have been developed to inhibit the catabolism of glutamine. This is achieved mainly by interfering with the activity of enzymes during the catabolic process, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and ammonia production. For example, GLS inhibitors include compound 968, BPTES and CB-839 [96–98]. Inhibiting the GDH pathway to prevent the conversion of ammonia, a nitrogen source for cells, into NEAAs results in reduced synthesis of protein and nucleic acid biomolecules, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [16]. This therapeutic strategy impedes the transformation of ammonia within cancer cells. Furthermore, genome-wide CRISPR knockout screening has the potential to identify novel metabolic enzymes or transport proteins essential for maintaining ammonia homeostasis, thereby revealing viable therapeutic targets for this approach [41]. However, many inhibitors currently being developed lack sufficient specificity. For example, GLS inhibitors (such as CB-839) may affect glutamine metabolism in both tumour cells and immune cells, and the function of immune cells (such as T cells) also depends on glutamine. This dual effect may lead to immune suppression, which in turn weakens the anticancer effect. Perhaps advantages such as a highly specific targeting ability and efficient killing effect can be achieved through antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), achieving the precise and efficient elimination of cancer cells [99].

One strategy involves interfering with the survival pathways mediated by ammonia, primarily by blocking ammonia-induced autophagy. For example, targeting DRD/mTOR can decrease tumor cell survival by inhibiting autophagy [71], whereas SIRT4 inhibits ammonia-induced autophagy, increasing the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to 5-FU [74]. Thus, preventing cancer cells from using ammonia to trigger autophagy could provide a theoretical foundation for addressing tumor resistance.

Utilization of intracellular and extracellular ammonia toxicity to inhibit tumor growth or kill cancer cells

This strategy seeks to transform the intrinsic toxicity of ammonia into a cytotoxic agent that specifically targets cancer cells by either promoting the intracellular accumulation of ammonia or sustaining elevated extracellular concentrations. The central premise of this approach lies in the dual functionality of ammonia as both a tumor-promoting factor and a viable target for cancer therapy.

A pivotal approach involves the inhibition of ammonia excretion. Specifically, targeting the ammonia transporter SLC4A11 or the ammonium channel PIKFYVE can effectively obstruct ammonia excretion in HCC. This results in the intracellular accumulation of ammonia, which subsequently induces cellular senescence or impairs autophagy, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and survival [48, 49]. This strategy capitalizes on the inherent toxicity of ammonia rather than attempting its removal. If cancer cells actively excrete ammonia to mitigate its toxic effects, then obstructing this excretion mechanism will sequester ammonia within the cells, leading to apoptosis or senescence, which can suppress tumor growth. This approach directly counteracts the tumor’s adaptive strategy and may prove particularly effective in tumors that are heavily dependent on ammonia excretion for survival.

Another approach involves exposing cancer cells to elevated levels of extracellular ammonia, similar to the method used to induce apoptosis in cancer cells and treat bladder cancer through ammonia delivery and amplification techniques [88–90]. The bladder, which is a relatively enclosed hollow organ, can withstand significant ammonia accumulation. However, when this method is applied to other tumors, a critical challenge arises: how to expose the tumor to such high ammonia concentrations without causing harm to the body. This necessitates a paradigm shift, focusing on identifying key genes or pathways influenced by elevated ammonia levels and targeting them, thereby indirectly inhibiting tumor growth through the effects of high ammonia concentrations.

Future research directions related to ammonia

The new understanding of the dual role of ammonia in cancer biology has profoundly highlighted its importance as a complex metabolic regulator and a promising yet challenging therapeutic target. Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating its tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing mechanisms, several key areas still warrant further investigation to translate these findings into effective clinical strategies (Fig. 4).

Schematic representation of future research directions related to ammonia. The main potential research directions include the role of ammonia in the protein diet of cancer patients and treatment involving combined ammonia clearance strategies, the involvement of ammonia in other metabolic pathways or its interaction with other metabolites, the effect of ammonia on other cells in the TME, the different distributions of ammonia in tumor tissues and its influence, and the role of ammonia in causing tumor cell death or how tumor cells manage to evade death

The role of ammonia in the protein diet of cancer patients and research on combined ammonia clearance therapy for cancer treatment

The 2021 ESPEN practical guidelines for clinical oncology nutrition recommend protein intake exceeding 1 g/kg/day, ideally reaching up to 1.5 g/kg/day (Recommendation B2-2; strength of recommendation: strong; level of evidence: moderate; strong consensus) [100]. Nonetheless, there is considerable debate surrounding the foundational research on protein diets. Some studies suggest that a high-protein diet may be linked to increased cancer mortality, whereas a low-protein diet could reduce tumor growth by affecting immune cell infiltration and macrophage polarization within the TME [101]. Conversely, a diet excessively low in protein may impair the antitumor immune response, underscoring the necessity for appropriately balanced protein intake [102]. One study has also shown that a low-carbohydrate and high-protein diet can slow tumor growth and prevent the occurrence of cancer [103]. Recent research has demonstrated that a high-protein diet activates the mTORC1 pathway in tumors and significantly inhibits the AKT pathway, thereby effectively suppressing tumor growth and increasing the efficacy of existing chemotherapy treatments [104]. Given the aforementioned findings, which indicate that low levels of ammonia promote tumor growth while high levels inhibit it, one can infer that a low-protein diet might establish an ammonia concentration conducive to tumor proliferation. Conversely, a high-protein diet could result in an excessively elevated extracellular ammonia environment within cancer cells, thereby inhibiting their growth. Alternatively, high-protein diets may provide additional ammonia sources within the TME, thereby promoting tumor growth or creating an ammonia-rich microenvironment that suppresses immune cell activity and facilitates tumor immune evasion. Consequently, the precise role of ammonia in tumor development, in the context of high- and low-protein diets, remains unclear. Further research is essential to elucidate this relationship.

Moreover, a high-protein diet may result in elevated blood ammonia levels. This is particularly concerning for cancer patients, whose UC is often compromised, making them more susceptible to hyperammonemia, which can lead to neurological damage and even HE [34]. In such instances, the implementation of combined ammonia clearance strategies appears necessary. These strategies not only more effectively mitigate the damage caused by elevated ammonia levels but also may inhibit tumor progression to some extent. However, there is a lack of research on the treatment of cancer patients with varying protein diets in conjunction with ammonia clearance agents. Considering the association between protein intake and ammonia production, exploring the impact of ammonia on tumors could provide an indirect basis for formulating protein diet recommendations for cancer patients.

Ammonia participates in other metabolic pathways or interacts with other metabolites, thereby influencing tumor progression

The aforementioned study revealed that ammonia increases lipid synthesis and glycolysis, thereby facilitating tumor progression [17, 61]. This finding underscores the necessity of exploring the interactions among metabolites. Comprehensive metabolic analyses and systematic methodological investigations are essential to elucidate the intricate relationships between ammonia and other metabolites as well as their effects on diverse cell types within the TME. Notably, ammonia may not operate in isolation; it can interact synergistically with other metabolites, such as lactate and kynurenine, influencing various cell populations, including tumor, immune, and stromal cells [41]. Future research should use multiomics approaches to delineate the metabolic network within the TME, aiming to identify not only direct effects but also synergistic or compensatory pathways. Such insights are critical for the development of rational combination therapies aimed at addressing tumor heterogeneity and metabolic plasticity.

Research on the mechanism of the effect of ammonia on other cells in the TME

Current research on the effect of ammonia within the TME predominantly investigates its inhibitory effects on CD8 T cells and NK cells. However, studies examining the direct effects of ammonia on Tregs, neutrophils, macrophages, neural cells and other cells within the TME are scarce. Investigating these cellular interactions is crucial for elucidating the role of ammonia in establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for developing tumor treatment strategies that utilize ammonia to modulate and potentially enhance the immune landscape.

The distribution of different amines in tumors affects the ecological niche of the TME

Contemporary research has focused predominantly on the static effects of ammonia on cancer cells or immune cells within the TME. Nevertheless, studies examining the distribution of ammonia in tumor tissues and its influence on the ecological niche of the TME are scarce. The application of spatial single-cell transcriptomics and metabolic imaging techniques offers a promising avenue for visualizing ammonia gradients and their spatiotemporal distribution within tumor tissues, thereby addressing this research gap [41].

Research on the forms of tumor cell death or the ability of cancer cells to evade cell death induced by ammonia

As mentioned earlier, ammonia can induce the death of cancer cells through autophagy damage and apoptosis pathways. In noncancer cells, ammonia can induce pyroptosis [105], necroptosis [106], ferroptosis [107], and programmed cell death [108]. Given the toxic effects of ammonia, further research is needed to determine whether related forms of cell death occur in cancer cells induced by high concentrations of ammonia or how cancer cells evade these types of cell death through certain mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ammonia plays a dual role in oncogenesis, functioning both as a promoter and, under high-concentration or specific conditions, as an inhibitor of tumor progression. At the same time, it should be noted that the dual role of ammonia has cancer heterogeneity. This duality offers a nuanced perspective for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies focused on ammonia metabolism. A comprehensive understanding of the intricate metabolic origins, destinations and mechanisms of ammonia—particularly its effect on tumor cell proliferation, stem cell properties, autophagy, and immune evasion—enables the formulation of precise treatment approaches. These approaches may include systemic regulation of ammonia levels, direct inhibition of ammonia utilization by cancer cells, metabolic reprogramming of immune cells, and strategic induction of ammonia accumulation within tumors. Ongoing investigations into this metabolic duality, coupled with the utilization of advanced research tools and methodologies, are anticipated to yield substantial advancements in cancer diagnosis and treatment, thereby enhancing patient outcomes.
