Pediatric home-based hospice and palliative care: a scoping review
Authors: Ellen Davis, Daniel H Grossoehme, Toluwalase Ajayi, Justin N Baker, Pamela S Hinds, Lisa Humphrey, Jill Ann Jarrell, Rachel Thienprayoon, Sarah Friebert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric palliative and/or hospice care is provided across a broad spectrum of settings, ranging from inpatient to outpatient to a child’s home. Pediatric home-based hospice and/or palliative care teams offer a specialized, interdisciplinary approach to care, allowing children to stay in the home while offering comprehensive support. This scoping review seeks to summarize what is known about pediatric home-based hospice and/or palliative care and to identify gaps in the current research. Following Arskey and O’Malley’s scoping review framework, we searched four databases for studies focused on pediatric home-based hospice and/or palliative care programs. Studies were included if they were published between 2000 and 2024, available in English, and focused on children and young adults receiving palliative or hospice care at home, including via telehealth, in the pediatric system. Of 2,552 results initially identified, 96 met our inclusion criteria and were included in this review. Common themes emerged including studies analyzing models of care, characterizing the population, end-of-life decision making, clinical outcomes of home-based hospice and/or palliative care, costs and economic impact, family experiences, quality domains, specific treatment modalities, and the use of telehealth. Overall, the available literature supported home-based hospice and/or palliative care as an effective model of care, reducing the burden on families, improving quality of life, and allowing families to stay in their preferred setting for care without sacrificing clinical outcomes. Key research gaps included small sample sizes, limited use of control groups and scarcity of randomized clinical trials, difficulties including the perspective of the ill child in research, and a need for longitudinal studies on the effects of home-based hospice and/or palliative care on children and families. Given the compelling evidence for the benefits of pediatric home-based hospice and/or palliative care, further research into disparities in care access, best models of care, and novel payment models are critical.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02146-5.Keywords: Scoping review, Pediatrics, Home-based care, Palliative, Hospice

Background

More than 21 million children globally live with life-limiting conditions and would benefit from palliative care [1, 2]. Pediatric palliative care (PPC) is specialized interdisciplinary care delivered in diverse settings including hospitals, ambulatory clinics, and the child’s home. The home is often the preferred setting for receiving PPC [2, 3]. Home-based care allows children to remain in a familiar, comforting space, preserves family routines, and provides a greater sense of normalcy. Privacy, family cohesion, and an enhanced ability to manage grief within the home setting have been cited as reasons families prefer home-based PPC [4]. Successful implementation of home-based pediatric hospice and/or palliative care programs (HBHPC-P) is more likely when specialized, interdisciplinary teams comprised of physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, and other healthcare professionals are involved [2]. Through home visits or virtual consultations, teams assist families in making informed decisions about their child’s care and address emotional, psychological, and spiritual concerns [3].

Palliative care and hospice are not only for end of life. Studies have shown the benefit of early referral to and involvement of palliative care for optimal outcomes [5–8]. In pediatric patients, hospice services may be engaged alongside disease-directed therapies as a benefit of the Affordable Care Act of 2010 [9, 10]. Thus, children and families receiving hospice and palliative care may be at varying stages of illness.

Despite the recognized benefits of HBHPC-P, significant gaps remain in research and implementation. A number of scoping reviews have examined PPC in general and in specific contexts but not home-based care in particular. We therefore set out to examine HBHPC-P’s current state and identify key trends, challenges, and areas where further research is needed. Formative questions such as equitable accessibility, effectiveness of different care models, and best practices for integrating home-based services with hospital and hospice care are a few identified specific gaps [11]; these as well as others yet to be defined are likely of interest to key stakeholders, including patients and families, healthcare teams, organizations (including community), and payers. Research on effectiveness and patient-/family-prioritized outcomes is not truly possible until key concepts can be clarified and refined. By systematically analyzing existing literature, we seek to provide insights to move toward next steps to inform future policies, improve service delivery, and enhance quality of life for children with life-limiting conditions and their families.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted using the methodological framework described by Arksey and O’Mally’, which outlines five stages: (1) identifying the research question; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting results [12]. HBHPC-P The process and search criteria for this scoping review comply with the PRISMA and the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [13, 14]. A protocol was developed prior to study initiation and followed throughout the review process, although it was not formally registered. Two academic medical librarians assisted in developing and refining the search strategy.

This review sought to map and characterize the existing literature on HBHPC-P. The study population included children and adolescents (0–18 years), as well as young adults receiving care within PPC or hospice programs. The concept of interest was the delivery of palliative care or hospice services in the home setting, including care provided by interdisciplinary PPC teams, community-based programs, and telehealth-supported services.

For the purpose of this review, “receiving care within the PPC or hospice programs” generally refers to children enrolled in structured palliative care or hospice programs led by interdisciplinary teams, which may include physicians, advanced practice providers, nurses, social workers, chaplains, and allied health professionals who facilitate symptom management, psychosocial support, and end-of-life care. This definition excludes informal caregiving without structured professional involvement and adult-only hospice services.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted between June 12 and June 22, 2024, across four electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, PsychINFO, and Web of Science. The search strategy was developed using combinations of keywords and controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms in PubMed) representing three core domains: (1) home and community-based care (e.g., home care, home-based, telehealth, telemedicine) (2), palliative and hospice care (e.g., palliative care, hospice, HBPC), and (3) pediatric populations (e.g., pediatric*, paediatric*, child*, and adolescent Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine terms within and across domains. The search strategy was adapted for each database to account for differences in indexing and syntax, while maintaining a consistent conceptual structure. Searches were limited to studies published between 2000 and 2024. The final search strategy for the PubMed database is included in Additional Materials.

This search yielded 3,096 records across all databases. HBHPC-P After removal of 544 duplicate studies, 2,552 were imported into Rayyan, a web-based software platform used to facilitate systematic and scoping review screening.

Inclusion and exclusion

Studies were included if they: were published between 2000 and 2024; were available in English; focused on children or young adults receiving pediatric palliative or hospice care; and examined care delivered in the home setting, including community-based programs or Telehealth-supported services. The search period was limited to 2000–2024 as earlier literature may not reflect current care models and advancements which are more widely studied in the past two decades. Young adults over 18 years were included when receiving care within pediatric systems, as many complex pediatric patients transition later in life or remain within pediatric care structures. Studies were excluded based on predefined criteria if they: were published prior to 2000; were unavailable in English; focused exclusively on adult palliative or hospice populations; were review articles; or were case series including fewer than five participants.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts of 2552 unique studies were screened by one reviewer using Rayyan. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied at this stage. Studies that clearly did not meet eligibility criteria were excluded, with 172 studies advancing to full-text review.

Two authors completed the full-text review. Discrepancies regarding eligibility were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer until consensus was achieved. During this more detailed process of moving from title and abstract review to the full-text review, 79 additional studies were excluded for the following reasons: not focused on pediatric populations, not describing home-based care, review articles, and insufficient sample size, in alignment with predefined criteria. The final synthesis included 93 (see Fig. 1 for study selection flow). We acknowledge that ten included studies were authored by members of our research team. To mitigate potential bias, these studies were assessed using the same objective criteria as all other included studies.

Data extraction

Data from the 93 included articles were extracted into a standardized table capturing key characteristics, including population, care setting, study design, and primary findings (see Additional Materials for extracted data on each reviewed publication). Three coauthors independently reviewed the extracted data and conducted an inductive thematic analysis to identify recurring themes and focal areas within the literature. Each reviewer initially generated provisional thematic groupings individually. The research team then met to compare preliminary categorizations, discuss areas of overlap or discrepancy, and refine theme definitions. Through frank discussions of how each reviewer arrived at their decision, clarifying definitions, and finding common understandings, consensus was reached on the final thematic framework. Themes (number of papers in each) included models of care (11), characterizing the population (14), decision making (4), clinical outcomes (18), cost and economics (5), family experience (25), provider experience (15), quality domains (6), specific treatments (4), and telehealth (10). Several papers had elements which aligned with multiple themes and were included in multiple categories when appropriate.

Results

More studies came from the United States (n = 24), followed by Germany (n = 18); only one study each came from the African continent and from the Middle East and none came from Latin America. The majority of studies were retrospective designs primarily utilizing electronic medical records and/or insurance claim data (n = 35), followed by qualitative studies (n = 24) and survey-based studies (n = 22).

Models of care

Published exploration of HBHPC-P models include domains such as scope of service, staffing, availability, and the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. Wager and colleagues described HBHPC-P’s heterogeneous nature across 33 European countries according to single “experts” in each country. Most services were offered free of charge and accessible from time of diagnosis, but otherwise the cohort had few similarities [15]. Naicker and colleagues piloted an intervention to bolster palliative care integration into home-based care for very sick children in Africa [16].

Complementary efforts in the US reveal similar heterogeneity. In a retrospective review of hospice and home health use for children who died between 2007 and 2010 in California, Lindley and colleagues examined child and family factors, revealing low uptake of both home health (24% of the cohort) and hospice services (12%) [17]. A Midwest study described provider home visits for a variety of diagnoses over a significant trajectory, highlighting important content and circumvention of barriers to care [18]. A large survey of hospice organizations conducted by Weaver and colleagues in 2023 revealed limited PPC access, especially in nonmetropolitan areas, citing barriers such as gaps in knowledge and skill, financial insecurity, low volume, and lack of overall sustainability [19]. Proposed solutions include champions, telehealth capability, and collaboration with larger organizations or community partners through a spoke-hub model [19]. Lotstein and colleagues described “referral misalignment” as a major impediment to HBHPC-P uptake from a single institution, further elucidating harms and barriers and highlighting policy reform as a larger, longer-term strategy to ensure appropriate access [20].

Several studies attempted to uncover the key elements of HBHPC-P from various stakeholders. This approach was echoed through a grounded theory analysis in Spain, extracting four themes to inform holistic PPC delivery: care, environment, patient and family, and professionals [21]. In Scotland, Malcolm and colleagues employed a realist evaluation to develop an “initial programme theory” expressed as context, mechanisms, and outcomes; this was then tested in real-life contexts and revised, resulting in five components of effective home-based end-of-life care: anticipatory approach; advance care planning; service responsiveness and flexibility; 24/7 availability; and partnerships [22]. Expanding the lens outward to systems of care incorporating HBHPC-P, Boyden and colleagues applied a multilevel ecological framework to facilitate and streamline development of effective research efforts and intervention strategies [23].

Telehealth delivery was highlighted in a Scottish study describing rapid pivoting of pediatric hospice services during the COVID-19 pandemic [24]. COVID also forced the quick transformation of PPC to a pediatric home healthcare delivery team serving a catchment population of more than 44,000 patients under age 18 in Spain, with potential plans to continue this model indefinitely [25].

Taken together, these findings paint a spotty, heterogeneous picture of home-based care delivery with two major gaps ripe for further research. First, access to pediatric HBHPC-P is shaped not only by patient and family factors, but also by referral processes and broader system-level constraints such as workforce capacity and program sustainability [19, 20]. Second, models will necessarily be varied and flexible but core elements of program delivery can be elucidates to inform policy, quality measurement, and guideline development.

Characterizing the pediatric home-based hospice and palliative care population

Three single-site studies characterized HBHPC-P programs, including patient demographic and clinical characteristics, and location of death [26–28]. Two multisite studies were identified [18, 29]. Cicozi and colleagues described patient and HBHPC-P visit data from two sites, analyzed from one year prior to the child’s initial home visit to one year after [18]. Grossoehme and colleagues described HBHPC-P at three sites, presenting patient demographic and clinical data. They noted significant deaths occurring while receiving PPC rather than hospice, suggesting the palliative care-hospice dichotomy may not be applicable in pediatrics [29, 30].

Several retrospective studies characterized specific populations. A German study of N = 70 patients with brain tumors, described clinical, symptomatic, treatment, financial aspects and between-group comparisons based on tumor location [31]. De Noriega and colleagues presented a 10-year review of children who died while being followed at home by a hospital-based PPC unit in Spain, highlighting demographic, clinical, healthcare utilization, supportive oncology treatment, and symptom prevalence data [32]. Korzeniewskva-Eksterowicz and colleagues characterized N = 53 perinatal patients receiving home-based palliative care in Poland, describing demographic and clinical characteristics, symptomatology, care duration and disposition [33]. A German study of N = 21 neonates and infants with life-limiting conditions described clinical characteristics, symptomatology, location of death, medications, and equipment use [34]. In contrast to Korzeniewska-Eksterowicz, more of these patients were discharged from PPC due to stable conditions [34, 35]. A prospective multicenter cohort study in Germany described differences between children receiving specialized palliative home care for cancer and non-cancer conditions, with distinctions in common symptoms and technological needs [36].

Two studies characterized HBHPC-P referrals. One study from Poland presented perinatal data of those enrolled 2005–2011, including diagnostic groups and medication use [33]. Lotstein and colleague characterized referrals from a hospital-based PPC team in Los Angeles in 2017 to HBHPC-P, and describe the study participants by referral type, and mortality [20]. They also presented the odds of HBHPC-P referral by hospital unit on which the index palliative care visit occurred.

As a group, these studies offer a candidate list of relevant metrics for subsequent efficacy studies, including disposition/location of death, healthcare utilization, and symptom prevalence.

Decision making

Despite the reported lack of community-based end-of-life care programs and analgesia in India, Das and colleagues reported that the majority of parents of children with incurable malignancies (70%) opted for home-based care [37]. Three German studies described aspects of advance care planning, in which decisions for Medical Orders for Life Sustaining Treatment (MOLST) occurred early in a patient’s enrollment in HBHPC-P (80% in first 100 days) and were rarely modified [38–40]. Caregivers’ decisions for MOLST were driven more by the child’s underlying disease than by caregivers’ geographic background [41]. Cicozi and colleagues characterization of two HBHPC-P programs in the United States showed that decisions to change the goals of care occurred in 44% of home visits. Discussions to allow natural death, create or modify Do Not Resuscitate orders or advance directives occurred in 26% of home visits [18]. These studies suggest that decision making, particularly related to a child’s end of life are not uncommon. Little information has been included regarding the decision-making process, who initiates it (and why) and who participates.

Clinical outcomes

Several studies analyzed clinical outcomes associated with HBHPC-P, including two qualitative studies. Schuetze and colleagues characterized the care practices of three German HBHPC-P teams [42]. Ethnographic analysis yielded five practices: familiarity with child and family; a resource-oriented attitude; empowering parents to provide care; shared decision-making; and caring for parents and their concerns. They suggest these practices differ from inpatient care due to multiple changing providers, and limited time per patient in an inpatient setting. Malcolm and colleagues described bereaved caregivers’ perspectives of effective HBHPC-P in Scotland [43]. The five qualities described were: ability to facilitate changes in preferred location of death; trusted provider-family relationships; child- and family-centered care; specialist care provided as and when needed; and quality and compassionate death and bereavement care. Parents made three recommendations: sharing learning from other families’ experiences; increased access to HBHPC-P; and promoting pediatric hospice and dispelling myths related to hospice care.

The quantitative studies utilized several control conditions, including participant as own control [44–47], nonrandom assignment to usual care or intervention [48], and persons with and without prehospital EMS callouts [47]. Two studies were prospective [47, 48]; Smith and colleagues’ participant-as-own-control design utilized retro- and prospective components relative to the child’s first home visit [46]. The most commonly outcome reported was location of death. The majority of children in studies from Germany and India died at home [37, 49]. The minority of US children died at home; especially those with public insurance [28, 50].

Healthcare utilization was a common multidimensional outcome. Three studies examined ED visits, which were not statistically different in two studies when HBHPC-P occurred (the third study lacked a comparison population) [45, 46, 51]. One Indonesian randomized controlled trial of 60 pediatric patients with malignancies found that 36.7% of children receiving palliative home care visits had fewer ED admissions after initiation of the intervention, compared to 7.7% of children in the control group [52]. Four studies included the number of admissions; in three studies which used a comparison group, two reported lower admissions when HBHPC-P occurred [44–46, 51]. Length of inpatient stays were reported in three studies, and all reported significant differences between those with and without HBHPC-P; Smith and colleagues also reported significant differences in the number of critical care days [44–46].

Two German studies of pediatric HBHPC-P reported improved child patient quality of life, though it was parent proxy data [47, 53]. Groh and colleagues used a validated instrument; Vollenbroich and colleagues a study-specific measure. Groh’s and Vollenbroich’s teams also reported improved caregiver quality of life after HBHPC-P initiation. Groh and colleagues reported improved caregiver anxiety and depressive symptoms, and decreased caregiver burden [47]. Improved caregiver confidence in managing their child’s symptoms at home was reported by Larrow and colleagues after initiating a home-based symptom management plan [51]. One randomized controlled trial analyzed the impact of a home-based palliative care visit on quality of life in children with malignancies. A statistically significant difference in quality of life and symptom intensity was observed, with favorable outcomes for the intervention group [54].

Additional studies analyzed physical symptoms impacting the child’s quality of life while receiving HBHPC-P. Das‘ study of bereaved caregivers in India reported that pain was the most commonly-reported symptom of those receiving HBHPC-P near their end of life (though the most troubling symptom was respiratory) [37]. DeNoriega’s study also reported pain as the most frequent symptom to be managed at home, followed by dyspnea and fatigue [55]. A retrospective study by Hauch and colleagues noted the prevalence of painful gastrointestinal symptoms in children receiving palliative care throughout their disease course, noting that these symptoms are often able to be controlled at home [56]. Kuhlen and colleagues’ study of pediatric patients with brain tumors receiving home-based hospice care reported relatively high symptom burden [43]. Notably, a study by Weaver and colleagues indicated that children self-reported higher physical symptom prevalence than parents and hospice nurses perceived at the time of hospice enrollment [57].

The qualitative studies noted the importance of familiarity and relationship between providers and the patient and caregiver with greater depth than may be common in an inpatient setting. The relationship may have downstream effects through creating an emotionally safe environment in which to discuss, and possibly modify, goals of care, as well as empowering caregivers (and when appropriate, patients) to provide direct care. Qualitative studies which present the patient’s voice are need to fully understand HBHPC-P’s effects on outcomes [58]. Further examination of clinical outcomes would benefit from greater consistent measurement as well as the potential of differential benefits to different populations.

Cost and economics

Gans and colleagues argue that cost to US community-based providers must be adequately quantified in order to seek adequate reimbursement for services and to ensure that children with life-limiting conditions are referred to the appropriate type of care [59]. The three studies conducted in the US based cost data on insurance claims (public or private) or on billed charges [44, 59, 60]. Studies conducted in other countries with different payor systems used healthcare institution/system cost data [61, 62]. Cost savings were primarily associated with reduced inpatient hospitalization [59, 60]. Chirico and colleagues’ examination of location of death in the United States (home versus hospital) found lower costs for children who died at home (especially those with non-malignant diagnoses and/or those on Medicaid versus Health Maintenance Organization) [60]. Postier and colleagues found reduced inpatient utilization after HBHPC-P enrollment in the US, and that total charges were lower for children with non-malignant diagnoses [44]. For those with six-months pre-enrollment HBHPC-P exposure, inpatient lengths of stay were 38 days shorter with a mean lower total hospital charge of $275,000, suggesting HBHPC-P may be not only the most advanced care delivery model but the most cost effective.

Chong and colleagues demonstrated that children in Singapore receiving HBHPC-P averaged 52 more days at home than the control group who received mostly inpatient PPC, and had fewer admissions and lower costs in the last year of life [62]. Gans and colleagues evaluated a pilot statewide PPC program providing hospice care while the child received concurrent curative care. Reduced costs were associated primarily with reduced inpatient days, which they attributed to the inclusion of a robust care coordination element to the statewide program. They note that the most complex children typically incur the largest costs and also potentially benefit from having an assigned care coordinator for their appointments and services [59]. Bradford and colleagues conducted a cost minimization analysis of estimated costs in a geographically large Australian state for PPC delivery by telehealth, ambulatory, and home-based services [61]. Telehealth service was the lowest cost and HBHPC-P was the highest; travel costs were the significant driver of differences. Clearly, in addition to developing standard comparators, accurate benchmarking of care delivery models and cost accounting will require atypical metrics, such as transportation/travel time and related costs, into any accurate financial measurement of HBHPC-P.

Family experience

Several studies described how HBHPC-P can enhance children’s and families’ quality of life by improving comfort and preserving meaningful experiences. Friedrichsdorf found improved quality of life and engagement in valued activities with the support of a HBHPC-P team [63]. Hendricks-Ferguson highlighted parental distress related to physical deterioration, while Dinkelbach described the emotional toll on healthy siblings who often report lower quality of life and well-being [41, 64]. Malcolm identified anticipatory planning, 24/7 access to support, trusting relationships with caregivers, and early communication as central to successful care delivery [22, 43]. Schuetze emphasized the importance of honest dialogue, psychosocial support, and acknowledgment of parental expertise [42]. Rico-Mena and Brenner highlighted the value of interdisciplinary care teams in coordinating complex care [65–67]. Hammer added that aligning expectations, clarifying roles, and communicating across the care trajectory were vital for supporting families and providers [68]. These findings were reinforced by Boyden and colleagues who found that parents rated physical, psychological, and emotional aspects of care as well as care coordination as highly important [69].

Families are often highly satisfied with HBHPC-P when services are responsive and comprehensive. Vollenbroich reported improved parental confidence, symptom control, and communication when families were involved with HBHPC-P teams [53, 70]. Kassam, Dangel, and Vickers all noted that the home is the preferred place of death, with increased satisfaction when that preference is honored [71–73]. Hansson reported the importance of sustaining normalcy and participation in everyday family routines to the extent possible [74]. Effendy describes parent perceived benefits including holistic provision of care [75]. Groh and colleagues surveyed family caregivers, who reported improved quality of life and decreased burdens after PPC team involvement [47]. Chocarro González showed that a caregiver training program is effective to make families feel more capable and less isolated [76].

Despite high levels of family satisfaction with HBHPC-P, parents still experience concerns with navigating these systems and face challenges. Tutelman highlighted four core parental concerns: helping children live well, navigating uncertainty, preparing for death, and caring for siblings [77]. Lazzarin and colleagues described the burdens borne by caregivers with children receiving HBHPC-P in Italy, reporting a mean time of nine hours per day of hands-on care and management of five devices [35]. Time and procedures determined the care burden rather than the child’s disease. Rud and Das noted family-perceived gaps in community services and caregiver support may leave caregivers feeling overwhelmed [37, 78]. Brouwer identified inadequate integration of spiritual care, with barriers to care including difficulties in identifying and communicating spiritual needs and a lack of spiritual care embedded into PPC systems [79]. As a whole, this theme is more heavily represented in the published literature, such that synthesis and refinement of studied components offers a coherent next step toward real-world prospective measurement of patient/family-reported impact(s).

Provider experience

One study from Spain used semi-structured interviews and focus groups to elicit data from professionals and identified two themes – the distinctive nature and team as a cornerstone of PPC [66] Another Spanish study utilized deep semi-structured interviews with home-based PPC team members to focus on emotional impact. A positive theme (changing life for the better) and its two associated subthemes (change of perspective on life and compassion satisfaction) mirrored a more negative theme (adverse effects of work) and its subthemes (feeling scarred and experiencing bad deaths); Eight additional categories expanded on the benefits and burdens, providing guidance for strategies to highlight professional wellbeing [80].

A questionnaire completed by 25 independent home nurses in France who cared for at least one dying child over a 1-year period revealed that 64% were amenable to another home PPC assignment afterward. They cited personal fulfillment and connections made with families and children as key benefits [81]. A qualitative study of a nurse-led hospice-at-home initiative in Ireland identified three key themes: communication across services, particularly regarding at-home bereavement support; consistent education and training; and the challenges of working alone. Compared with parent feedback, communication and competency emerged as shared concerns [67].

Community- and hospital-based professionals offering feedback on a specialized home PPC program for children with cancer in Denmark stated a need for systematic pathways to enhance collaboration and trustful relationships [68]. A study of 16 home-based nurses in Malaysia highlighted nuanced communication with children and families, personal suffering, systemic challenges including caseload size, and need for formal and informal debriefing [82].

Citing a number of barriers, 47% of general pediatricians in Germany, of whom the majority had experience in PPC and access to specialty PPC resources, indicated they did not want further involvement with palliative home care for children and adolescents in 2008; by report, palliative care has developed significantly in Germany since [83]. Another German study describes barriers related to a lack of legal and financial regulations for pediatric palliative home care as well as poor access to specialist services [84]. A similar study in the Netherlands demonstrated that general practitioners caring for children dying of cancer at home experienced a number of physical and/or psychological symptoms, but a majority felt positive about the circumstances surrounding the child’s death and most found support from their own family members. Recommendations reiterated here include enhanced symptom management guidance as well as more psychosocial support for families and caregivers alike [85].

Interviews with 10 rural adult community nurses providing PPC in Scotland revealed four themes in their lived experience: emotional preparedness; navigating the ‘professional road’; becoming part of the family; and “it’s everybody’s business.” Participants were grateful for aspects of the experience but also reported distress and cited multiple areas for improvement [86]. To enhance service availability across Denmark, a shared care model was developed between specialized PPC teams and locally-placed primary care nurses to formalize a coordinated approach, including an introductory meeting, a follow-up meeting in the child’s home, and ongoing involvement throughout the illness course (hub-spoke model). Results of a follow-up questionnaire indicated high satisfaction and improved preparation for and comfort in providing end-of-life care in the home, suggesting a potentially generalizable pathway toward improvement in care delivery [87].

A number of studies have addressed provider experience more tangentially. Healthcare providers in Germany who participated at least once in the care of a dying child rated their experiences with a specialized home PPC team very highly; in addition to programmatic support, they noted enhanced “assuredness” in their own practice [70]. Santana-Medina & Rodriguez-Suarez touched briefly on the need for providers to “acquire skills to increase their own capacity to control the emotions which necessarily arise in daily contact with extremely serious situations.” [21] In their pilot of a support package for home-based caregivers in Africa, acceptability was evaluated by Naicker and colleagues. The package was developed to address challenges expressed by home- and community-based caregivers. Thirteen workers from two organizations offered qualitative feedback, noting improved empowerment and self-expression [16]. A comparative study conducted in Iran emphasizes the importance of education for successful integration of pediatric palliative home care into the health system [88].

Many studies allude to or explicitly name factors negatively impacting caregivers’ experiences in HBHPC-P, including inexperience; erosion of professional boundaries; grief/loss; mental/emotional exhaustion or burnout; isolation; care discontinuity or poor transitions; insufficient time; and inappropriate remuneration for time/emotional investment. Suggestions for improvement cover multiple domains across self, colleagues, teams, organizations, communities, and healthcare policy; despite a seemingly large number of obstacles, however, thematic similarity of published provider experience across the world lends itself to implementation of a small but potentially impactful group of interventions that would likely increase engagement and thus reduce barriers to access that may stem from provider availability.

Quality domains

Across the literature, there is consensus around using the National Consensus Project (NCP) domains of palliative care as a foundation for defining quality in pediatric HBHPC-P. Thienprayoon and colleagues validated all eight NCP domains for pediatric HBHPC-P (though definitions differed between pediatric and adult care) and identified three new domains: continuity and coordination of care, highlighting the importance of communication across care settings; bereavement care as a standalone quality domain; and compassionate care, prioritized by caregivers and providers [89–91]. Other studies similarly emphasized family-centered priorities. Grossoehme et al. highlighted caregiver priorities, identifying six themes: communication, safety, relationship building, empowerment, provider insight, and shared burdens [92]. Vollenbroich and colleagues found that concordance between caregiver and provider symptom understanding correlated with family satisfaction, demonstrating that shared understanding is a key quality indicator [53]. Boyden and colleagues developed a caregiver-informed measurement tool which emphasized physical and emotional support [93]. Together, these studies suggest that high-quality HBHPC-P integrates clinical excellence with compassionate, coordinated and family centered care. Key domains and caregiver, patient, and provider defined themes provide a foundation for measuring and improving care quality.

Specific treatments

A small number of studies were identified which focused on the implementation of specific therapeutic interventions within HBHPC-P, primarily exploring their feasibility, safety, and potential effects on patients and families. One such study explored Reiki therapy, which was shown to be feasible and positively received by children and parents. Participants reported reduced pain and anxiety, as well as a lasting sense of calm after receiving treatment [94]. Similarly, De Zen, 2022 found that home-based blood transfusions were safe, highly satisfactory, and cost-effective for families as they reduced hospital disruptions and created a sense of normalcy in daily life [95]. Palliative sedation, used at the end of life for terminally ill children with refractory symptoms, was able to be safely administered at home without adverse events under the close supervision of the home hospice team [96]. Finally, home-based physical rehabilitation programs were valued by families due to their symptom relief, integration of care into the family environment, and enhanced caregiver confidence [65]. Grossoehme and colleagues demonstrated that home-based patient-controlled analgesia is both feasible and highly valued by families [97]. Collectively, these five studies indicate that a wide range of specific interventions can be meaningfully implemented into the home environment, yet their small number points to another area ripe for exploration.

Telehealth

Several studies discussed the feasibility, key functions, and limitations of telehealth implementation in PPC in the home. High priority needs, as established by Zimmerman and colleagues, included secure data transmission, access to video consultations and records, symptom questionnaires, and communication support [98]. Concerns centered around fear of diminished care quality and loss of face-to-face contact. Technological barriers were minimized by the younger demographics of users. Recommendations emphasized the importance of integrating telehealth into the medical record and allowing for customized functions to support individual needs.

Consistently in the literature, family caregivers supported telehealth for its convenience and time savings [61, 99, 100]. Telehealth allowed for the inclusion of extended family in care discussions [99]. Schmitt emphasized the importance of communication, advocacy, and navigating uncertainty during telehealth visits [100]. Weaver and Schmitt discussed the COVID-19 pandemic as a key driver for rapid telehealth uptake [100, 101]. In one study health care professionals emphasized that technology in HBHPC-P may improve information sharing and care efficiency, with the caveat that in-person interaction with the child remains crucial [102].

Rural hospice nurses’ perspectives were that telehealth allowed for timely communication and expanded program participation [103]. There were concerns that telehealth may limit emotional attunement, leading to a more superficial provider-family relationship. Similarly, Bradford reported ambivalence among clinicians, citing tradeoffs between access and relational depth, communication challenges, and logistical burdens [104]. Bento documented staff burnout in a hybrid model, indicating that the convenience of telehealth may come at a cost to providers’ well-being. Additionally, access disparities remained [105]. Williams demonstrated that non-English speakers and patients without online portals were underrepresented in telehealth delivery [106].

Despite these concerns, multiple studies confirmed that quality was able to be maintained across care domains with the use of a telehealth model. Williams found no significant differences in ED visits, ICU admissions, hospice enrollment, or end-of-life quality metrics between telehealth and in-person visits [106]. In fact, telehealth visits were found to have more conversations about goals of care and medical decision making, possibly due to increased caregiver comfort in their own home. Cost and feasibility were also favorable. Telehealth reduced travel burdens and clinician time. Weaver documented an average savings of eight hours per encounter among rural families [99]. Akard (2020) showed that telehealth is an acceptable model for providing interventions that were previously only accessible in-person [107]. Currently, research supports that benefits outweigh burdens for telehealth in HBHPC-P. As technology evolves, this area will continue to require attention to reduce barriers to access, integrate artificial intelligence (AI) platforms, prioritize team-based interactions that go beyond provider-focused visits, and provide education to facilitate best practice, especially in sensitive and uncertain conversations.

Discussion

This scoping review aims to summarize the current state of knowledge and knowledge gaps in pediatric HBHPC-P. Studies have investigated numerous facets of pediatric HBHPC-P, although most are not yet understood in sufficient depth. An important observation is that most studies originate from high income settings. Given that 98% of children who need palliative care live in low resource settings, the geographic and income level imbalance of the studies represents an important knowledge gap [108]. Additionally, this gap suggests that the current evidence base may not adequately capture the full range of implementation challenges and care models relevant to home-based palliative care worldwide. Cultural context also appears to influence decision making and preferred locations of care and death. These findings suggest that decisions regarding HBHPC-P are not determined solely by clinical factors but are also deeply embedded in cultural, social, and health system contexts.

HBHPC-P delivery appears to be feasible and acceptable across settings. A number of studies reported improved quality of life for families receiving home-based care, particularly when delivered by a coordinated interdisciplinary team. From a systems perspective, home-based care was associated with reduced hospitalizations and an overall favorable economic impact. There are also significant reductions in families’ logistical burdens, including reduced transportation needs. Emerging interventions, particularly telehealth, further enhance accessibility, especially for families in geographically underserved areas.

Despite these promising findings, evaluation of care often lacks input from a diverse set of family stakeholders. Often, the evaluation of satisfaction was limited to the perspective of a single parent–usually the mother—such that the full range of stakeholder experiences is largely absent, particularly perspectives of the ill child and their siblings. This absence is often explained due to age or neurodevelopmental limitations, but when available, the child’s voice offers unique and critical insight into care quality. The limited research available describing siblings’ experience emphasizes emotional challenges and decreased quality of life. Further research should focus on developing actionable interventions to reduce this burden.

Further, this review supports the idea that family-prioritized domains–such as emotional support, care coordination, compassionate care, and trust–are not only ideals, but practical targets. These domains can be embedded into clinical care through integration with electronic health record templates, team huddles, and care protocols. The gap between what families value and what is routinely measured presents an opportunity to guide practice changes. Future research should focus on evaluating the adoption of family-defined domains and patient-reported experience measures as a quality improvement strategy.

While these domains apply across many populations, the pediatric context introduces additional unique complexities or domains that must be addressed, such as developmental changes, continuity of education/vocation, sibling needs, and family unit dynamics. Tailoring interventions and metrics to the pediatric (and even the perinatal) setting is essential to ensure that effective, appropriate care is provided.

This review suggests that pediatric HBHPC-P provides high-quality, family-centered care with potentially substantial benefits to the experiences of children with life-limiting conditions and their families particularly in relation to care coordination, quality of life, and reduced logistical burden. The field is currently hampered by the limited number of prospective, comparative, or randomized studies, which weaken the evidence needed for systemic change. At the same time, access to pediatric-specialized HBHPC-P remains limited and inconsistent [13, 14]. The literature reviewed here suggests that these access gaps are not simply geographic variations in service delivery, but reflections of broader system-level constraints including workforce shortages, limited program sustainability, and inadequate reimbursement structures. In some settings, these limitations may leave children reliant on adult-oriented or non-specialized community systems for home-based serious illness care, raising important concerns about age-appropriate expertise and equity. Further research is needed not only to better understand disparities in access and outcomes, but also to evaluate strategies that expand pediatric-specific capacity and implement family-defined quality domains in routine practice. From a policy standpoint, the field requires sustainable payment models that reflect the longitudinal, interdisciplinary, often intensive and periodically low-volume nature of pediatric HBHPC-P. Findings such as those reported by Weaver et al. underscore that limited pediatric workforce availability, especially in nonmetropolitan regions, interacts with financial fragility and program sustainability challenges to constrain access [13]. Addressing these barriers will require investment in workforce development, support for collaborative models such as hub-and-spoke partnerships, and reimbursement approaches that account for home visits, telehealth, care coordination, bereavement support, and interdisciplinary team-based care. Integration of family-defined quality domains such as communication, trust, and care coordination into quality metrics, electronic health records, and reimbursement frameworks offers one pathway for aligning health system operations with what matters most to children and families.

Research gaps

Among the many gaps identified, three priorities appear most urgent for the field. First, improving equitable access to pediatric-specialized HBHPC-P is a critical practice and policy priority, particularly for children in rural, nonmetropolitan, and resource-limited settings. Second, the evidence base would benefit from more rigorous prospective and longitudinal designs that include child- and family-reported outcomes and better characterize which models of care work best for which populations. Third, future work should move beyond description alone to test implementation strategies, workforce models, and payment approaches that support sustainable delivery of interdisciplinary pediatric home-based care. When looking at improving equitable access to pediatric-specialized HBHPC-P, studies predate the COVID-2019 pandemic, raising questions of how congruent these populations are compared to post-pandemic environment. A limited number of prospective studies were identified, and comparison groups were not utilized or were not fully congruent with the intervention group. All studies presented clinical data; emotional and spiritual characteristics are undescribed. Except for the multisite studies, sample sizes are small, though two studies note that they describe the majority of patients in their catchment area over 7–10 year periods [33, 55]. Kuhlen and colleagues (2016) question whether PC discharge and mortality differs between countries due to PC acceptability to providers or differential availability of curative care options [34]. Decision-making research gaps include prospective, mixed-method, multisite studies of HBHPC-P teams’ effect on multiple aspects of medical decision making in the home setting. These aspects include advance care planning, prehospital care plans and decisional regret; inclusion of patient-reported outcomes; and congruence between patient/caregiver preference and actual location of death. Knowledge gaps for clinical outcomes included: including child-reported outcomes; using validated instruments to enable meta-analyses; utilizing control/comparison samples, and prospective designs. Wide geographic disparities were noted, with the United States and European countries (particularly Germany) predominating. Given that the vast majority of children who need palliative care live in low resource settings, this geographic and income level imbalance represents a critical knowledge and research gap [108].

A majority of studies were observational and retrospective, largely due to research ethics. Given the available evidence that HBHPC-P improves clinical outcomes and quality of life, making utilization of a comparable group of children not enrolled in HBHPC-P as a control group violates equipoise and is unethical [44, 59, 60]. Gans and colleagues proposed a difference in differences analyses and were unable to carry it out due to lack of a comparable group of children not enrolled in HBHPC-P [59]. An ethically viable comparison group would improve generalizability of findings and allow inferential analyses.

Even when control groups were used, questions of bias arose. Chong and colleagues included a control group formed from children with life-shortening diagnoses with a low likelihood of surviving to adulthood (excluding neonates regardless of diagnoses). They noted significant between-group differences at enrollment, age at death, ethnicity, and residency status [62]. Studies are potentially limited by a referral bias due to not knowing what differences may exist between children referred to HBHPC-P and those not referred [44].

There remains a need for longitudinal studies of HBHPC-P’s effects on children and families. For example, Gans and colleagues did not have access to participants’ pre- and post-HBHPC-P enrollment healthcare utilization data to analyze changes in the child’s health status associated with the transition to HBHPC-P [59]. Further, although the literature suggests a high satisfaction level with effectively used HBHPC-P, additional research is needed to determine whether satisfaction and parental burden vary based on the child’s diagnosis.

The gap between what matters to families and what is routinely measured offers a clear roadmap for implementation science. Integrating these domains into EHR templates, care protocols, and team huddles can create measurable practice change. Future research should evaluate domain adoption as a quality improvement strategy.

As digital health and large language models are increasingly used to extend care into the home, these domains provide a critical ethical and operational foundation for responsible innovation. Domain-aligned AI tools may help address gaps in responsiveness and access without replacing the human heart of palliative care.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations: although efforts were made to conduct a comprehensive search, it is possible that relevant studies were missed. In addition, title and abstract screening were conducted by a single reviewer, which may have increased the risk of selection bias; however, uncertainties were discussed with the research team and full text review involved multiple reviewers and consensus. One or more of the authors coauthored 12% (n = 10) of the studies reviewed, due to the limited number of researchers in this field. Heterogeneity between study designs and outcome measures limited our ability to directly compare findings across studies. As with most scoping reviews, individual study quality was not appraised, limiting conclusions that can be drawn. Studies published after June 2024 were also excluded.

Additionally, there are varying scopes of practice for both pediatric palliative care and pediatric hospice. We do not know which groups and included studies were treating patients under the nomenclature of palliative care or hospice whose patients were also receiving disease-directed therapies or which groups were providing end-of-life care. Lastly, there is likely great heterogeneity in the diagnoses and disease groups represented in these papers. Both of these latter factors may limit generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations inherent in a scoping review, important conclusions can be drawn. This review validates that commonly cited themes (e.g., quality of care, patient and family experience) are highly rated and perceived as goal concordant. It further validates that other important themes such as emotional support and care coordination) are not only theoretical ideals but actionable targets that can be, or are already, embedded into clinical care models. This reinforces the necessity of aligning clinical operations with family priorities, especially in home-based settings.

Aligning care with national guidelines that include pediatric contexts, and tailoring interventions to an expanded set of validated domains are critical components for ensuring true patient- and family-centered care. The inclusion of multiple, diverse health systems in this review supports the transferability of domain-driven care models across geography, patient demographics, and health system structures. Adherence to national guidelines that are fully informed by all key stakeholders further ensures equitability. Specifically, the needs and values most frequently cited by families (e.g., communication, trust, cultural respect) align closely with known drivers of health equity. This highlights how family-defined themes can serve as a vehicle for equity-centered care delivery—especially for populations historically underserved by traditional models. Further, this review validates the need for additional disparities research. Collaborations drawing on the expertise and experience of global partners and from communities which have previously developed this approach may be beneficial [73, 109, 110].

In addition to offering many signals for further investigation, the emerging body of literature addressing pediatric HBHPC-P as synthesized in this review provides a clear roadmap for streamlined, collaborative, cohesive program development, management, and measurement. Further, and of paramount importance, it should compel state and federal legislators and payers to work with experienced healthcare providers in this sector to develop accountability for pediatric HBHPC-P and novel payment models that are more comprehensive and equitable.
