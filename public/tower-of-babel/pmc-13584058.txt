Pharmacovigilance signals for congenital heart disease related to drug exposure: A disproportionality analysis based on the FAERS database
Authors: Chun-Lin He, Xiao-Nan Zhang, Gui-Xin Zhao, Li-Jing Ma, Rong Feng, Yan-Hong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis real-world study aimed to identify pharmacovigilance signals for congenital heart disease (CHD) associated with drugs exposure and to characterize the phenotypic spectrum of CHD adverse event reports.MethodsAdverse event reports for drug potentially associated with CHD were collected from the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) between January 2004 and September 2025. We used reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) to detect pharmacovigilance signals. The Bonferroni correction was employed to adjust for multiple comparisons and to further confirm the robustness of the signals.ResultsWe identified a total of 871 drugs with reported signals for CHD, of which 67 drugs met the criteria across all four disproportionality analysis methods. These drugs covered a wide range of categories, with nervous system agents and systemic antiinfectives being the most numerous. Further stratified analysis by therapeutic class revealed that antidepressive agents (n = 10,433), anti-epileptic agents (n = 2735), and antiemetics (n = 2364) showed a higher reported frequency. Among these groups, the spectrum of reported CHD events remained largely consistent, with atrial septal defect (ASD) and ventricular septal defect (VSD) being the most frequently reported adverse events.ConclusionThrough a comprehensive analysis of FAERS data, we identified pharmacovigilance signals for CHD associated with drug exposure and described the phenotypes of reported CHD events with suspected drug involvement. Our findings provide a valuable reference for hypothesis generation and future epidemiologic studies, and they support the ongoing pharmacovigilance of drug safety particularly during pregnancy.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, FAERS, Pharmacovigilance, Real-world study

Introduction

Congenital malformations are a leading cause of infant mortality, and congenital heart disease (CHD) constitutes a major subset of these anomalies that contribute to neonatal deaths. The global prevalence of CHD is estimated at 1.8% [1], [2]. In the United States, CHD accounts for 4.2% of all newborn death and is responsible for 30–50% of deaths attributed to congenital malformations [1], [2]. Without early diagnosis and timely treatment, up to 69% of infants with complex CHD may die from severe complications [3]. Moreover, CHD may adversely effect on the of the neurodevelopment and increase the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders and behavioral problems in infants [4]. Preventive strategies, early screening, prompt diagnosis and intervention are essential to reduce morbidity and improve prognosis of CHD [3]. Among the modifiable risk factors, drug exposure is the most readily avoidable. However, drug exposure during pregnancy remains common. Epidemiological studies indicate that the proportion of pregnant women taking medication approaches 73%, and about 4% are exposed to at least one potentially harmful drug [5], [6].

Although the CHD risks associated with certain medications has been identified, such as valproic acid, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and antihypertensives, current research on drug-related CHD risk still has significant limitations [7], [8], [9]. Most current studies consist of small-scale epidemiological investigations or case reports, and there remains notable lack of large-scale cohort studies to systematically evaluate drug-related CHD risk. Moreover, existing research mainly targets specific drug classes, offering insufficient evaluation of CHD risk across diverse drug categories in real-world clinical practice, particularly for less frequently used and newly prescribed drugs. Furthermore, no specific studies have yet focused on the pattern characteristics of drug-associated CHD, meaning that after prenatal exposure to CHD-risk medications, targeted guidance strategies for assessing offspring cardiovascular development remain unavailable [10], [11], [12].

The US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database, which contains a large body of real-world data, enables the identification of new or rare pharmacovigilance signals [13]. Therefore, this study extensively detects potential pharmacovigilance signals for CHD with a large-scale, real-world clinical setting based on the FAERS database. Meanwhile, we construct spectrum profiles of CHD across different drug classes at both the anatomical and preferred term (PT) levels, thereby translating broad pharmacovigilance signals into actionable directions for clinical risk management. This work aims to provide a reference for offspring risk management following prenatal drug exposure and to inform future research on drug safety during pregnancy. Materials and methods

Data source

The FAERS database is a spontaneous adverse event reporting system that relies on submissions from health-care professionals and consumers. The database comprises seven datasets: patient demographic and administrative information (DEMO), drug information (DRUG), adverse events (REAC), patient outcomes (OUTC), report sources (RPSR), therapy start and end dates for reported drugs (THER), and indications for drug administration (INDI). By obtaining drug information, clinical outcome information, and other relevant variables, we are able to comprehensive assess drug safety in the real-world [14]. To evaluate the epidemiological characteristics of drug-associated CHD, we conducted a pharmacovigilance analysis based on the FAERS database. Data were gathered from the first quarter of 2004 to the third quarter of 2025. Data were cleaned according to the recommendation of the Food and Drugs Administration (FDA). The PRIMARYID, CASEID and FDA_DT files selected from the DEMO table and by CASEID, FDA_DT and PRIMARYID. For reports with the same CASEID, retain the one with the latest FDA_DT. For reports with identical CASEID and FDA_DT, retain the report with the highest PRIMARYID [15], [16]. Following the deduplication, all analyses were conducted at the report level. For reports containing multiple suspected drugs, each drug-event pair was included separately in the disproportionality analysis. In the DRUG file, ROLE_COD was utilized to define the role characterization of drugs in adverse event reports. While this field includes primary suspect (PS), secondary suspect (SS), concomitant (C), and interacting (I) [17]. In our study, we strictly limited our inclusion criteria to PS in order to maximize our ability to identify CHD-risk signals while minimizing the confounding influence of concomitant medications. Reports assigned other ROLE_COD were excluded. Standardize drug names using the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/), all drugs were represented by their generic names. To maintain the integrity of the dataset, combination products were also included in the analysis and similarly represented by the generic names of their primary active ingredients. To control for confounding by indication, we manually reviewed the primary indications of all drugs meeting the signal detection criteria based on their approved prescribing information. Drugs whose primary indication was the treatment of CHD or its complications were systematically excluded, such as drugs for pulmonary arterial hypertension and adjunctive therapies used during the perioperative period for patients with CHD. Additionally, reports with missing or unidentifiable drug names, unknown ROLE_COD, or adverse events that could not be coded to the Medical Regulatory Activity Dictionary (MedDRA, 27.0) were excludes. Reports involving dietary supplements, and non-drug exposures were also removed from the dataset. The READUS-PV guidelines (A Disproportionality analysis for drUg Safety signal detection using individual case safety reports in Pharmaco Vigilance) were used to guide our study and the READUS-PV checklist was uploaded as a Supplementary material (Table S1) [18], [19].

Identification of CHD

The standardized MedDRA queries (SMQs) were employed to identify preferred terms (PTs) associated with CHD. We selected the relevant PTs from the SMQ for congenital, familial and genetic disorders (code 20000077). To further characterize the anatomical characteristics of CHD, we mapped all PTs to the International Paediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code (IPCCC). The PTs were classified into eleven categories: congenital anomaly of position or spatial relationships of thoracic-abdominal organs; congenital anomaly of an atrioventricular or ventricular-arterial connection; congenital anomaly of mediastinal vein; congenital anomaly of an atrium or atrial septum; congenital anomaly of an atrioventricular valve or atrioventricular septum; congenital anomaly of a ventricle or the ventricular septum; congenital anomaly of a ventriculo-arterial valve or adjacent regions, congenital anomaly of great arteries including arterial duct; congenital anomaly of coronary arteries, functional abnormality and unspecified (Supplementary material Table S2) [20].

Statistical analysis

The disproportionality analysis was applied to detect the pharmacovigilance signals of drug associated with CHD. Signal values were calculated using the 2 × 2 contingency table. Four disproportionality analysis methods were employed, including reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS). The calculation formulas and the detection threshold for detecting the signal were summarized in Supplementary material (Table S3) [14]. To reduce the risk of false positives arising from multiple comparisons, we applied Bonferroni correction to the disproportionally analysis to enhance the reliability of the results. A positive signal was defined only when the signal detection criteria of all four disproportionality analysis methods were simultaneously met and the adjusted p-value (p-adjust) from the Bonferroni correction was less than 0.05 [21]. Data were analyzed by R software (Version 4.5.2, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Clinical characteristics of reported CHD associated with suspected drug exposure

During the study period (Q1 2004-Q3 2025), 21426 reported cases related to CHD were identified from the FAERS database. The clinical characteristics of reported CHD cases with drug exposure were summarized in Table 1. Gender data were available for 15,334 (71.57%) submitters, of whom 37.17% (n = 7964) were female and 34.40% were male (n = 7370). Most adverse events reported in people within one-year-old, the proportion was 22.22%. The reported proportions of 1–3, 4–6, ≥ 7 years were 0.78%, 0.30%, 13.13%, respectively. However, age data were missing for more than half of the reported cases. The majority of reported cases were submitted by health-care professionals, including hospital personal, physician, other health-professional, registered nurse and pharmacist (n = 13,757, 64.21%, Fig. 1A). Congenital anomaly was the most frequently reported outcome (n = 10,606, 49.50%), followed by hospitalization (n = 4609, 21.51%), other serious (n = 2511, 11.72%), death (n = 2137, 9.97%). The results were displayed in Fig. 1B. The annual distribution of CHD-related reports is presented in Fig. 1C. The number of reported cases remained relatively stable across each year, with slight increase observed in 2015.

Drugs with pharmacovigilance signals for CHD

A total of 871 drugs with reported CHD signals were identified in the FAERS database, of which 95 drugs were consistently classified as suspect drugs by all four disproportionality analysis methods. We applied Bonferroni correction to the disproportionality analyses and excluded drugs used for the treatment of CHD to minimize confounding. Ultimately, 67 drugs with positive pharmacovigilance signals for CHD were included in our final analysis (Table 2). The top ten drugs by the number of reported cases were as follows: paroxetine (n = 2530, ROR 70.64), sertraline (n = 1230, ROR 22.33), fluoxetine (n = 898, ROR 33.61), valproic acid (n = 767, ROR 21.18), lamotrigine (n = 755, ROR 13.53), ondansetron (n = 737, ROR 55.42), venlafaxine (n = 586, ROR 12.86), citalopram (n = 528, ROR 19.16), levetiracetam (n = 484, ROR 8.15), and escitalopram (n = 423, ROR 17.23). When ranked by ROR signal intensity, the top ten drugs were: paroxetine (n = 2530, ROR 70.64), trimipramine (n = 3, ROR 57.08), ondansetron (n = 737, ROR 55.42), indometacin (n = 137, ROR 47.62), zidovudine (n = 126, ROR 41.02), nelfinavir (n = 22, ROR 34.50), fluoxetine (n = 898, ROR 33.61), lamivudine/zidovudine (n = 103, ROR 32.54), abacavir/lamivudine/zidovudine (n = 20, ROR 29.87), and clomifene (n = 24, ROR 29.87). Patterns of reported CHD cases across different drug categories

We further analyzed the differences in CHD-related reports at the PT level, restricting the analysis to drugs with more than 100 reported cases. Based on therapeutic class, the drugs with positive signals were subdivided into eight categories: antidepressive agents, antiemetics, anti-epileptic agents, anti-HIV agents, antihyperglycemic agents, antihypertensive agents, antipsychotic agents, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S4). The distribution of reported adverse events varied across with the drug classes (Fig. 3B). CHD-related reports were predominantly concentrated among antidepressive agents (n = 10,433), anti-epileptic agents (n = 2735), and antiemetics (n = 2364).

The number of reported adverse events for different drug categories. (A) Schematic diagram of drug classification according to the therapeutic class. (B) Histogram of adverse events.

To elucidate the global features of reported CHD phenotypes, we compared the distribution of adverse events across drug classes according to the IPCCC (Fig. 4). In patients treated with antidepressive agents, the most common adverse events were congenital anomaly of an atrium or atrial septum (n = 2878, ROR 36.69) and congenital anomaly of a ventricle or the ventricular septum (n = 1961, ROR 32.87). Congenital anomaly of a ventriculo-arterial valve or adjacent regions showed the strongest signal (ROR 59.48). Similar patterns were observed among the anti-epileptic agents group, anti-HIV agents group, antihypertensive agents group, and antipsychotic agents group; in which more than half of the reported cases involved congenital anomaly of an atrium or atrial septum and congenital anomaly of a ventricle or the ventricular septum. Congenital anomaly of great arteries including arterial duct, were the most common adverse event in patients treated with NSAIDs (n = 397, ROR 14.32). However, in the antiemetics group, the most common frequently reported category was “unspecified” (ROR 457, 69.23) according to the IPCCC classification, making it difficult to characterize the phenotypic pattern. In the antihyperglycemic agents group, in addition to congenital anomaly of an atrium or atrial septum (n = 82, ROR 2.52), congenital anomaly of a ventriculo-arterial valve or adjacent regions (n = 45 ROR 6.74) also accounted for a substantial proportion.

To further elucidate the detailed features of drug-related CHD, we analyzed the adverse event reports at the PT level. The spectrum of reported adverse events varied across different drug classes (Fig. 5). For antidepressive agents, 10470 PTs were extracted from the FAERS database, of which 10,433 were defined as positive signals by all four disproportionality analysis methods. The top three PTs were atrial septal defect (ASD) (n = 2839), ventricular septal defect (VSD) (n = 1852), and heart disease congenital (n = 1022). For anti-epileptic agents, 2822 PTs were identified, of which 2735 were defined as positive signals. The top three PTs were ASD (n = 885), VSD (n = 590), and heart disease congenital (n = 321). For anti-HIV agents, 869 target PTs were retrieved form the database, and 817 PTs met the signal detection criteria. The top three PTs were VSD (n = 262), ASD (n = 194), and heart disease congenital (n = 71). For antihypertensive agents, 377 PTs were identified, and 288 met specified criterion. The most common PTs were ASD (n = 155), VSD (n = 69), and fallot's tetralogy (n = 27). For NSAIDs, 452 targeted PTs were identified by all four disproportionality analysis methods. Ductus arteriosus stenosis foetal (n = 226) and ductus arteriosus premature closure (n = 169) accounted for the highest proportion. For antiemetics, 2364 PTs were extracted from the FAERS database, of which 2351 were defined as positive signals by all four disproportionality analysis methods. The top three PTs were heart disease congenital (n = 421), ASD (n = 401), VSD (n = 290). For antihyperglycemic agents, 205 CHD-related PTs were identified. ASD (n = 82) had the largest number of reported cases. For antipsychotic agents, 218 PTs were included. The most frequently reported PT was ASD (n = 131).

Reported associations between medications and CHD

To better understand the relationship between drug exposure and CHD, we reviewed pharmaceutical package inserts and the literature. Drugs with more than 100 reported cases were categorized into three groups: established association (adverse events recorded in package inserts), possible association (adverse events reported in the literature), and unconfirmed signal (pharmacovigilance signals identified in the present study). We constructed a diagram of drug-associated CHD (Fig. 6). Currently, five antidepressive agents (paroxetine, sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram, and escitalopram), two anti-epileptic agents (valproic acid and carbamazepine), and three NSAIDs (diclofenac, ibuprofen, and indomethacin) were considered to be associated with CHD. For some drugs, the association remains controversial, with adverse events documented only in the literature; these include two antidepressive agents (venlafaxine and bupropion), one antiemetic (ondansetron), and two anti-HIV agents (zidovudine, and lamivudine/zidovudine). In addition, our analysis identified ten drugs that have not been previously reported to be associated with CHD. Detailed information on the associations between specific drugs and CHD is provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Discussion

An intriguing signal for CHD was observed with metformin in our study (n = 214, ROR 2.70) [26], [27]. A broad range of CHD subtypes were reported, including ASD, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, and bicuspid aortic valve. However, interpreting these signals requires careful consideration of multiple potential confounders. First, maternal diabetes mellitus is a well-established risk factor for CHD [28], [29]. A nationwide cohort study reported that the risk of CHD in offspring is four times higher among mothers with pre-gestational diabetes mellitus (PGDM) than those without diabetes [28]. Although metformin is commonly used for glycemic control during pregnancy, it can cross the placenta and may not provide adequate glycemic control [30]. However, through the FAERS database, we are unable to obtain maternal glycemic control status; thus, it remains uncertain whether the observed risk of CHD should be attributed to metformin itself or to underlying maternal diabetes. Recent studies have shown that metformin can improve endothelial function, inhibit cardiac fibrosis, and modulate the JNK pathway, conferring potential cardiovascular protective effects in patients with diabetes [31], [32], [33]. However, whether these effects influence fetal cardiovascular development and lead to abnormalities remains unknown. These highlights need to systematically evaluate the potential impact of metformin on the fetal cardiovascular system from a developmental biology perspective. Furthermore, previous literature has indicated that VSD, PDA, and ASD were the most common CHD phenotypes in offspring of diabetic mothers [34]. In contrast, our study found that arterial valve abnormalities and atrioventricular or ventricular-arterial connection anomalies were also common CHD subtypes in metformin exposed pregnancies. This finding suggests that metformin treatment during pregnancy may increase the risk of specific CHD subtypes. Nevertheless, owing to the inherent limitations of the FAERS database, these results alone do not support restricting the use of metformin during pregnancy and future systematic studies are needed to comprehensively evaluate the safety of metformin exposure during pregnancy.

In this study, ondansetron exhibited a strong pharmacovigilance signal for CHD. A range of CHD phenotypes were identified, including ASD, VSD, and left-to-right cardiac shunt, among others. Certain PTs demonstrated exceptionally strong signal values, including laevocardia, left-to-right cardiac shunt, and bicuspid pulmonary valve. The relationship between ondansetron exposure during pregnancy and CHD remains the subject of ongoing debate. Previous systematic reviews and retrospective studies have suggested that prenatal ondansetron exposure may be associated with a slightly increased risk of CHD, particularly septal defects [35], [36]. However, other studies have not produced consistent evidence linking ondansetron to CHD [37], [38]. Our large-scale study contributes to this discourse by demonstrating not only the presence of a signal, but also the specific subtype features with a broad anatomical framework. We acknowledge that these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Potential confounders may influence the results, including indications for use, maternal complications, and concomitant medications [37]. Given that ondansetron is a second-line therapy for severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP), it would be unwise to prohibit its use during pregnancy based solely on potentially biased result [39]. The distinct phenotypes observed in our study provided a hypothesis for future prospective studies and underscore the importance of continued pharmacovigilance for this widely used antiemetic.

Highly active antiretroviral therapy has markedly altered the course of HIV disease and reduced the incidence of maternal-fetal transmission. However, the incidence of cardiac events has also increased [40], [41]. The impact of in utero exposure to antiretroviral drugs on offspring the cardiovascular function has drawn increasing concern. Assessment of cardiovascular status in HIV-exposed but uninfected children who were exposed to antiretroviral drugs in utero has revealed that these drugs are associated with an increased risk of left ventricular dysfunction [42], [43]. In utero zidovudine exposure has been associated with CHD, with VSD and ASD as the most common subtypes [44], [45]. In the present study, disproportionate CHD reporting signals among anti-HIV agents were detected based on the FAERS database. Zidovudine, lamivudine/zidovudine, ritonavir, nevirapine, and emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil each had more than 100 reported cases. We also observed notable signals for zidovudine (ROR 41.02), nelfinavir (ROR 34.50), and lamivudine/zidovudine (ROR 32.54), with VSD and ASD as the predominant reported phenotypes. Ritonavir, nevirapine and emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil was defined as potential signals. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as HIV infection itself is also a known risk factors for cardiovascular disease [46], [47]. In pharmacovigilance signal analyses based on the FAERS database, we are unable to determine whether the offspring were infected with HIV, or can we determine maternal complications or drug combination. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, the above results still suggest that attention should be paid to the cardiovascular health of offspring following in utero exposure to antiretroviral drugs.

Consistent with previous epidemiological studies, multiple antidepressive agents were identified as being associated with CHD, including five SSRIs (paroxetine, sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram, and escitalopram), one SNRI (venlafaxine), and bupropion [8], [10], [22], [23], [24]. Among these, paroxetine was the most frequently reported drug in our real-world analysis, with ASD and VSD being the predominant adverse events. Similarly, valproic acid and carbamazepine were also found to be associated with CHD in our study, with ASD, VSD, coarctation of the aorta, tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), and hypoplastic left heart syndrome as the most frequently reported adverse events-finding that are broadly consistent with previous research [7], [12], [25]. These findings suggest that the disproportionality methods used in this study can effectively reproduce well-established drug-CHD associations reported in the literature, thereby supporting the stability and reliability of our results.

However, we must acknowledge a limitation inherent to all pharmacovigilance analyses of pregnancy exposures, confounding by indication. Maternal underlying conditions such as epilepsy, diabetes, depression, and HIV are well-established risk factors for CHD [28], [29], [46], [47]. Relying solely on data from spontaneous reporting systems makes it difficult to determine whether the observed CHD risk is attributable to drug exposure, maternal disease, or genetic factors. Only through rigorous adjustment for confounding variables (indications) and meticulous stratified analysis may help isolate the impact of maternal diseases to directly assess drug-exposure risks. However, drug use during pregnancy almost always requires a clear clinical indication. Consequently, it is virtually impossible to fully exclude the influence of the underlying maternal disease at this stage. Nevertheless, our study also has some reference value. First, our results demonstrated differences in the number of reported CHD cases and signal strengths among drugs used for the same indication, for example, paroxetine (ROR 70.64) and sertraline (ROR 22.33). If maternal disease were the sole drive of CHD risk in offspring, within the same therapeutic class would be expected to exhibit similar signal profiles. Moreover, CHD subtype patterns associated with certain maternal diseases have been reported, for instance, diabetes increase the risk of VSD, PDA, and ASD in offspring. In contrast, our study found that risks of arterial valve abnormalities and atrioventricular or ventricular-arterial connection anomalies were present in the metformin treatment group, suggesting the possibility of drug-specific CHD pathogenic mechanisms. Gestational age, timing of exposure, and genetic factors are all critical elements in assessing teratogenicity. Such information is currently unavailable from the FAERS database, and our findings may therefore be biased due to the influence of these unmeasured confounders. However, as a complex disease that poses significant threat to the health and survival of newborns, CHD risk assessment following drug exposure is a critical component of medication risk evaluation during pregnancy [48]. There is urgent need to establish a targeted guidance framework. Current approaches to studying drug safety during pregnancy are extremely limited, and safety data remain severely inadequate, particularly for newly prescriptions drugs. Our study utilizes large-scale data from the FAERS database to extensively explore CHD risk signals and to attempt to establish associations between drug categories and CHD subtypes. This structured characterization of CHD subtypes by drug class not only provides a reference for CHD risk assessment of similar drugs but also offers a basis for future comparative analyses of CHD pattern differences across various drug classes. This approach enhances the translational value of the FAERS database data for clinical applications. Notably, due to the limitations of the database and current pregnancy pharmacovigilance methodologies, these results must be interpreted with caution. This study cannot establish a definitive association between any specific drug and CHD risk, nor can we exclude such association. Our findings should not be used as clinical guidance for prescribing decisions during pregnancy. The pharmacovigilance signals identified here serve purely as hypothesis-generating clues to guide future systematic, prospective studies.

Conclusion

In this real-world pharmacovigilance study, we thoroughly analyzed pharmacovigilance signals of CHD associated with drug exposure. A total of 67 drugs exhibited disproportionate signals for CHD using data from the FAERS database. These drugs were classified into 11 ATC categories, with the pharmacovigilance signals concentrated in nervous system agents and antiinfective for systemic use. A descriptive map of the reported CHD phenotypic patterns across therapeutic classes was developed. Our work contributes to understanding the reporting characteristics of CHD-related signals and address the current lack of drug safety studies during pregnancy. The findings also provide valuable information for future epidemiologic studies and for medication safety monitoring in pregnant women with drug exposure and their offspring.
