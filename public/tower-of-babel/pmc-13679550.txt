Green Nanoparticles in Precision Agriculture: A Critical Review of Smart Crop Management, Nano-Enabled Delivery Systems, and Sustainable Farming
Authors: Abisha Meji Marshal Williams, Milon Selvam Dennison, Sarad Pawar Naik Bukke, Bosco Christin Maria Arputham Ashwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable agricultural production requires approaches that improve resource-use efficiency while limiting environmental burdens. This critical review examines green-synthesized Nanoparticles (NPs) in precision agriculture, covering sustainable synthesis, smart crop management, nano-enabled delivery systems, nano-biosensors, plant-nanoparticle interactions, environmental risks, circular agriculture, and digital technologies. The NPs derived from Plant, microbial, biomolecule, and waste shows potential for targeted nutrient delivery, crop protection, stress management, and improved monitoring. However, reported performance varies with NP size, surface properties, concentration, formulation, crop type, soil, and environmental conditions. The review therefore critically considers both benefits and risks, including phytotoxicity, effects on beneficial microorganisms, persistence, bioaccumulation, and long-term ecosystem impacts. Waste-derived NPs and circular nanomanufacturing offer opportunities for resource recovery and agricultural waste valorization, but their scalability and economic feasibility remain uncertain. The integration with Artificial Intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoTs), Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), remote sensing, and smart decision-support systems may improve site-specific management. The review also examines technology readiness, commercialization pathways, manufacturing consistency, cost, and regulatory requirements. The green NPs shows promise for sustainable precision agriculture, but wider adoption requires stronger field validation, standardized characterization, environmental risk assessment, reproducible manufacturing, economic evaluation, and responsible regulatory development.Keywords: circular agriculture, green nanoparticles, nano-biosensors, nano-enabled delivery systems, nano-fertilizers, nano-pesticides, plant-nanoparticle interactions, precision agriculture, smart crop management, waste-derived nanoparticles

Introduction

Modern agriculture must increase food production to meet the growing demand for food while reducing environmental impacts and using limited land and water resources efficiently.1 Climate variability, soil degradation, and growing pressure on natural resources make this increasingly difficult.2 In many conventional farming systems, fertilisers, water, and pesticides are applied evenly across whole fields even though soil and crop conditions are not the same everywhere. This can lead to nutrient loss through leaching and runoff, unnecessary use of agrochemicals, higher greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and poor use of resources.3,4 Future farming therefore needs better ways to use water, nutrients, and crop-protection products while maintaining crop production.5

Precision agriculture (PA) has developed to address these problems and it uses technologies such as remote sensing, Global Positioning System (GPS), Variable Rate Technology (VRT), and Internet of Things (IoT) systems to apply farm inputs according to actual field conditions.4 Satellites and Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) provide information on crop health, soil moisture, nutrient status, and pest occurrence. GIS can then be used to map this information and guide field operations.3,6 GPS-guided equipment improves the accuracy of sowing, fertiliser application, and harvesting, while VRT allows water, nutrients, and pesticides to be applied at different rates within the same field.4 Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and big-data analysis are now also being used to support decisions on irrigation, fertilisation, pest control, and harvesting.3,7 Reported studies indicate water savings of 20–50% and fertiliser reductions of 15–30% while maintaining or increasing yields.8 However, high costs, technical difficulties, and limited digital infrastructure still restrict wider use of these technologies.3,7 More importantly, PA can show where a problem exists, but the required input must still be delivered accurately and at the right time.

Green-synthesised nanoparticles (NPs) may help to address the above stated problem. Nanoparticles are generally smaller than 100 nm and have properties that differ from larger particles because of their small size and large surface area.9 Conventional methods for producing NPs include chemical precipitation, sol-gel, solvothermal, and hydrothermal processes. These methods may require high energy input and hazardous chemicals.9,10 Green synthesis uses plant extracts, microorganisms, or biomolecules to form and stabilise NPs.9,11 Plant materials contain compounds such as flavonoids, proteins, polysaccharides, and alkaloids that can help in NP formation. Alternatively, bacteria, fungi, algae, and other biological materials can also be used.11,12 Agricultural wastes, such as fallen leaves, can also serve as raw materials, linking NP production with waste use and circular agriculture.10 These methods can reduce the need for hazardous chemicals, but the final properties and performance of the NPs still depend on the source material and the conditions used during synthesis.5,13

Green NPs are being studied for several agricultural uses. In nutrient management, nano-fertilisers and nano-carriers can provide nutrients slowly and in a more controlled manner, which may improve nutrient-use efficiency.12,13 Green-synthesised silver (Ag), Copper Oxide (CuO), Titanium dioxide (TiO2), and Zinc Oxide (ZnO) NPs have shown antifungal, antibacterial, and nematicidal effects.12,14 Silica nanoparticle-coated biochar has also been reported to reduce Fusarium oxysporum infection in crops and to activate plant defence responses.15 Nano-enabled delivery systems can release active substances in a controlled and targeted way. Nanoparticles have also been studied for improving plant responses to drought, salinity, metal stress, and other environmental stresses.15,16 At the same time, nano-biosensors are being developed to detect plant stress, pathogens, pesticide residues, and nutrient status at an early stage. Some of these sensors are being combined with portable devices and AI-based systems for field use.17–19

The reported benefits of green NPs are not the same in every situation. Their effects depend on particle size, surface properties, charge, concentration, formulation, crop species, soil type, and environmental conditions.15,16,20 The dose is also important, for example, nano-Fe-modified biochar reduced cadmium toxicity at 0.2–0.4% application rates but increased cadmium toxicity in leaves at 0.8–1.6%.21 This shows that a higher dose does not always give a better result. There are also concerns about environmental safety, the Ag NPs may affect useful soil microorganisms such as Rhizobium and Glomus species. Nanoparticle accumulation in plants has also been linked with oxidative stress, reduced photosynthesis, poor root and shoot growth, delayed germination, and genetic damage.22,23 Their movement and persistence in soil can also change with particle size, surface coating, and environmental ageing and the long-term field information is still limited.20 In addition, problems related to scale-up, batch consistency, production cost, characterisation cost, and regulation continue to limit wider use.16,24–26

The main problem is that the available evidence is scattered across different topics. Some studies focus on NP synthesis, others on nutrient delivery, disease control, stress management, or sensing. Many are carried out in laboratories or greenhouses and under a limited range of crop and soil conditions. There is still limited field-scale evidence, limited information on nanoparticle interactions with soil, microbes, and plants under real farming conditions, and limited agreement on methods for nanoparticle characterisation and testing. Information on life-cycle impacts and what happens to nano-enabled inputs after use is also limited.16,20,27

This review examines how green-synthesised NPs can be used in PA and what is needed for their safe and practical use. It covers green synthesis using plants, microorganisms, biomolecules, and agricultural wastes; nutrient management; crop protection; stress management; nano-enabled delivery systems; nano-biosensors; plant–nanoparticle interactions; environmental effects; circular agriculture; AI and digital farming; technology readiness; manufacturing; cost; commercialisation; and regulation.

The review addresses five main questions: (1) How do green synthesis methods and nanoparticle properties influence agricultural performance? (2) What benefits, limitations, and mechanisms have been reported for the major agricultural applications of green nanoparticles? (3) What are the main environmental and biological concerns related to nanoparticle toxicity, environmental fate, accumulation, and effects on plants and ecosystems? (4) How can green nanoparticles be integrated with precision agriculture, digital technologies, and circular agriculture? (5) What scientific, technical, economic, regulatory, field-validation, and scale-up challenges must be addressed for the practical and responsible use of green nanoparticles in agriculture?

The main contribution of this review is its focus on green-synthesised NPs within the broader precision-agriculture framework, linking their synthesis and agricultural functions with smart crop management, nano-enabled delivery, nano-biosensors, plant–nanoparticle interactions, environmental safety, digital agriculture, and circular resource use. The review further examines the evidence needed to move these technologies from laboratory and controlled studies towards field application by considering technology readiness, scale-up, economic feasibility, manufacturing consistency, commercialisation, and regulatory requirements. Rather than presenting these areas separately, the review examines their connections and identifies the key scientific, technical, environmental, and practical gaps that must be addressed for responsible agricultural application.

Inclusion, Exclusion Criteria and Study Selection

The literature was selected based on its relevance to green-synthesised NPs and their agricultural applications. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed articles, reviews, book chapters, and conference proceedings addressing nanoparticle synthesis, characterisation, agricultural applications, environmental effects, or sustainability. Studies unrelated to agriculture, focused solely on industrial applications, non-English publications, duplicates, and records with insufficient technical information were excluded. Retrieved records were managed in Zotero reference manager, where duplicates were removed before title, abstract, and full-text screening. Studies were retained when they addressed green-synthesised NPs and at least one defined agricultural outcome within the review scope. Additional studies were identified through backward and forward reference tracking, and the selected literature was grouped according to the major review themes. As this is a narrative critical review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, the findings were critically synthesised rather than statistically pooled. The main limitations include differences in nanoparticle properties, experimental conditions, crop and soil systems, and the limited field evidence available for some applications.

Evidence Synthesis and Critical Appraisal

The selected literature was critically synthesized according to the major themes of the review, including green synthesis, nutrient management, crop protection, stress management, nano-enabled delivery systems, nano-biosensors, plant-nanoparticle interactions, environmental impacts, circular agriculture, and technology readiness. For each theme, findings were compared according to nanoparticle type, synthesis route, particle characteristics, concentration, application method, crop or biological system, experimental conditions, and reported outcomes. Greater emphasis was given to studies providing clear experimental methods, nanoparticle characterization, appropriate controls, quantitative results, and evidence from field or semi-field conditions. Findings were interpreted by identifying consistent results, contrasting observations, and important limitations across studies. Conclusions were then developed from the overall pattern of evidence rather than from individual studies, with particular attention to the strength of evidence, uncertainties, and the extent to which laboratory findings had been validated under realistic agricultural conditions.

Green Synthesis of Nanoparticles for Agricultural Applications

Plant-Mediated Synthesis

Plant-mediated synthesis of NPs using phytochemicals and agricultural biomass offers a sustainable and reduced chemical use alternative to conventional chemical and physical methods, which often involve harsh conditions and toxic chemicals. This green synthesis approach leverages the natural reducing and stabilizing properties of plant extracts, which contain phytochemicals such as phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and proteins, to produce NPs with enhanced stability and uniformity.28,29 The use of agricultural biomass and plant extracts not only reduces the environmental burden but also utilizes renewable resources, making the process cost-effective and sustainable.30 This method has been successfully applied to synthesize various NPs, including Copper (Cu), Silver (Ag), and Zinc (Zn), which have significant applications in agriculture, such as enhancing plant growth, improving disease resistance, and acting as nano-fertilizers.28,31,32 For example, Cu NPs have been shown to increase root and shoot growth in mung beans and enhance grain yield and stress tolerance in wheat.28 Similarly, Ag NPs synthesized using plant extracts have demonstrated antimicrobial properties against phytopathogens, offering a green solution for managing agricultural diseases.33 The integration of these NPs into precision farming systems can optimize nutrient delivery and pest control, thereby improving crop yield and quality while minimizing environmental impact.34,35 Moreover, the use of endophytic microbiomes and other biological synthesis pathways enhances the sustainability of nanoparticle production, contributing to more resilient agricultural systems.36 The plant-mediated synthesis of NPs aligns with the principles of PA by providing site-specific, need-based solutions that enhance agricultural productivity and sustainability.32

Microbial and Biomolecule-Assisted Synthesis

Microbial and biomolecule-assisted nanoparticle synthesis approaches are increasingly recognized for their eco-friendly characteristics, controlled synthesis capabilities, and relevance to sustainable agricultural applications. These methods leverage the natural metabolic processes of microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, algae, and even plant-derived biomolecules to produce NPs in a manner that is both environmentally benign and cost-effective. Unlike traditional chemical and physical synthesis methods, which often involve toxic reagents and high energy consumption, biological synthesis routes are sustainable and reduce environmental toxicity.37–39 Microbial synthesis, in particular, offers advantages such as scalability and the ability to produce NPs with tunable size, shape, and surface properties through enzymatic and metabolic processes.40 This approach is not only eco-friendly but also allows for precise control over NP characteristics, which is crucial for their application in agriculture.41,42 In sustainable agriculture, these biologically synthesized NPs can be used as nano-fertilizers, nano-pesticides, and plant growth stimulators, enhancing plant growth and resistance to diseases while minimizing the use of harmful agrochemicals.36,43 For example, Trichoderma-mediated NPs have been applied as nano-fungicides and plant growth stimulators, demonstrating their potential in agri-food applications.37 Moreover, the integration of nanotechnology in agriculture through these green synthesis methods can lead to the development of smart delivery systems for nutrients and pesticides, thereby improving crop productivity and sustainability.44 The use of endophytic microbiomes for NP synthesis is another promising approach, offering reduced chemical use alternatives that enhance plant growth and disease resistance.36 The microbial and biomolecule-assisted NP synthesis not only aligns with the principles of green chemistry but also holds significant promise for advancing sustainable agricultural practices by improving crop yields and reducing environmental impact.45

Sustainability-Relevant Characteristics of Green Nanoparticles

Green NPs, synthesized through eco-friendly methods, exhibit several sustainability-related properties that make them highly suitable for precision agricultural applications. These NPs are characterized by their biodegradability and biocompatibility, which are crucial for minimizing environmental impact and ensuring safety for both plants and soil ecosystems. The use of natural polymers and biological resources in their synthesis, such as polysaccharides and plant extracts, enhances their biodegradability and reduces toxicity, making them a safer alternative to conventional agrochemicals.46,47 Green NPs also demonstrate reduced toxicity due to the absence of harmful chemicals in their synthesis process, which aligns with the principles of green chemistry and minimizes the risk of environmental contamination.47,48 Their environmental compatibility is further underscored by their ability to degrade into non-toxic by-products, such as bioavailable monosilicic acid in the case of Silica (Si) NPs, which enriches soil health and supports beneficial microbial communities.49 In PA, these NPs offer significant advantages, including enhanced nutrient delivery systems, improved stress tolerance, and reduced dependency on synthetic inputs. For example, ZnO NPs and Si NPs have been shown to improve nutrient use efficiency and bolster plant resilience against abiotic and biotic stresses, thereby supporting sustainable agricultural practices.32,49 Additionally, the encapsulation of natural compounds within polysaccharide-based NPs allows for controlled release and precise dosage, reducing the risk of treatment resistance and minimizing the negative effects of chemical fertilizers and pesticides.46 The integration of green NPs in agriculture not only promotes environmental sustainability but also enhances crop productivity and resilience, aligning with global sustainability goals.48,49

Although green synthesis can reduce the use of hazardous reagents and make use of renewable biological resources, these advantages do not automatically establish lower environmental impact or better agricultural performance. The properties of the resulting NPs depend strongly on the biological source, synthesis conditions, particle size, surface chemistry, and post-synthesis processing. Consequently, NPs produced through the same general green route may show different stability, biological activity, and toxicity. The literature therefore supports viewing green synthesis as a potentially lower-impact production approach rather than as evidence of inherent environmental safety or sustainability. Green Nanoparticles in Smart Crop Management

Precision Nutrient Delivery Systems

Green NPs have emerged as a promising approach in precision nutrient delivery systems, offering controlled nutrient release, enhanced nutrient-use efficiency, reduced fertilizer loss, and sustainable crop productivity. The application of nano-fertilizers, such as nano-DAP, has demonstrated significant improvements in nutrient use efficiency and crop yield. For example, a study on rainfed maize showed that using nano-DAP alongside conventional fertilizers could reduce nitrogen and phosphorus inputs by 25% without compromising yield or profitability, while also reducing greenhouse gas emissions and energy consumption.55 Si NPs further enhance nutrient delivery by providing controlled and sustained release mechanisms, which minimize nutrient losses and ensure consistent crop uptake. SiNPs also improve plant resilience against abiotic stresses and reduce dependency on conventional agrochemicals, aligning with sustainability goals.49 The development of Nano-Biochar-Coated Slow-Release Fertilizers (NBC-SRF) exemplifies the potential of NPs in extending nutrient availability and improving water retention, thereby enhancing nutrient use efficiency and environmental sustainability.56 Similarly, chitosan xerogels embedded with cerium oxide NPs offer controlled release of nutrients, improving plant growth and reducing environmental impact.57 Nanophotonic fertilizers, which integrate light conversion and nutrient delivery, have shown to significantly enhance photosynthetic performance and biomass, demonstrating the multifunctional capabilities of NPs in agriculture.58 Nanohybrid fertilizers, which incorporate nanotechnology into traditional fertilizer matrices, enable precise control over nutrient release, reducing nutrient runoff and environmental pollution while improving crop yields.59 Bio-nano-fertilizers, combining nanoscale materials with beneficial microorganisms, further enhance nutrient uptake and soil health, offering a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers.60 Collectively, these advancements in green nanoparticle applications underscore their important role in advancing PA, promoting sustainable crop productivity, and mitigating the environmental impacts of traditional fertilization practices.

Smart Pest and Disease Management

Nano-enabled pesticide and antimicrobial delivery systems utilizing green NPs present a promising approach for targeted pest and disease control, significantly reducing the need for excessive agrochemical application and minimizing environmental contamination. These systems leverage the unique properties of NPs, such as increased surface area and enhanced chemical effectiveness, to improve the delivery and efficacy of active ingredients in pesticides and antimicrobials.61 The development of multifunctional NPs and nano-pesticides has shown to enhance absorption and conduction by plants, improve efficacy, reduce dosage, and delay resistance, while also protecting beneficial insects and reducing residues.62 These advancements are crucial in addressing the adverse effects of conventional pesticides, which include environmental pollution, health risks, and soil microbiota disruption.63 Green synthesis of nano-agrochemicals, which emphasizes eco-friendly and sustainable practices, further enhances the potential of these systems to transform agriculture by increasing crop yields and reducing environmental contamination.64 Controlled Release Systems (CRSs) based on nanocarriers offer an eco-efficient strategy for the precise delivery of agrochemicals, responding to specific environmental stimuli and reducing premature loss of active ingredients.65 Moreover, the integration of nanotechnology in agriculture facilitates the development of smart delivery systems that can target specific pathogens and pests, thereby improving biosafety and reducing collateral damage to non-target organisms.66,67 Despite these promising developments, the commercial application of nano-enabled systems in agriculture remains limited, necessitating further research to assess ecological impacts and optimize delivery strategies.61 The strategic use of green NPs in pesticide and antimicrobial delivery systems holds significant potential for sustainable agriculture, enhancing food security while safeguarding environmental and human health.68,69

Stress Management and Climate-Resilient Agriculture

Green NPs play a pivotal role in enhancing plant tolerance to various environmental stresses, such as drought, salinity, and oxidative stress, which are critical for developing climate-resilient agricultural systems. These NPs, synthesized through reduced chemical use methods, possess unique physicochemical properties that enable them to penetrate plant tissues, thereby enhancing nutrient uptake and stress resistance.70,71 They mitigate the adverse effects of abiotic stressors by optimizing physiological and biochemical processes, such as photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, and water absorption, which are often disrupted under stress conditions.72 Specifically, NPs enhance the antioxidant defense systems in plants, thereby reducing oxidative stress caused by Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and maintaining redox homeostasis.71,73 This is crucial as oxidative stress can impair plant growth and development. Moreover, NPs improve photosynthetic efficiency and protect cellular membranes, which are vital for maintaining plant health under drought and salinity stress.74,75 They also regulate gene expression and boost the accumulation of osmolytes, hormones, and phenolics, contributing to enhanced stress tolerance.74 The application of NPs in agriculture not only improves plant resilience but also promotes sustainable farming practices by reducing the need for conventional agrochemicals, thus minimizing environmental pollution.76,77 However, while the benefits of NPs are significant, there are concerns regarding their environmental and health impacts, necessitating further research to develop standardized application protocols and assess long-term effects.22,70 The integration of green NPs into agricultural systems offers a promising approach to enhance crop resilience and ensure food security in the face of climate change.72,78

Crop Growth Enhancement and Yield Improvement

Green NPs have emerged as a promising approach in precision farming systems, significantly influencing seed germination, nutrient uptake, photosynthesis, metabolic activity, and overall crop growth and productivity. Nanopriming, a technique involving the use of NPs, enhances seed germination by stimulating aquaporin expression and increasing amylase production, which facilitates starch degradation and water uptake, thereby improving seed vigor and stress tolerance throughout the plant’s lifecycle.79,80 The unique physicochemical properties of NPs, such as high surface-area-to-volume ratios, enable them to penetrate biological membranes effectively, allowing for targeted nutrient delivery and enhanced stress tolerance. This capability is crucial in mitigating abiotic stresses like drought, salinity, and heavy metal toxicity, which are major contributors to global yield losses.22,72 Nanoparticles also enhance photosynthetic efficiency and enzymatic activity, which are vital for plant growth and resilience under stress conditions.72,81 The application of nano-fertilizers, such as silica-based and carbon-based variants, improves nutrient retention, reduces runoff, and enhances nitrogen use efficiency, contributing to sustainable crop production.72,82 Moreover, NPs can modulate biochemical pathways and signalling mechanisms, promoting stress resistance and reducing the need for conventional pesticides and fertilizers.83 Despite these benefits, the long-term environmental impact and potential toxicity of NPs remain areas of concern, necessitating further research to develop standardized application protocols and ensure their safe integration into agricultural practices.22,81 The strategic use of green NPs in precision farming holds immense promise for enhancing crop productivity and sustainability, addressing the challenges posed by climate change and resource depletion.84,85

The reported agronomic benefits should, however, be interpreted with caution because nanoparticle responses are not uniform across crops, materials, concentrations, and growing conditions. Some studies report improvements in nutrient uptake, photosynthesis, stress tolerance, and yield, whereas excessive nanoparticle concentrations can produce phytotoxic effects and impair plant physiological processes. The evidence therefore suggests a concentration and context-dependent response rather than a universally positive effect. Differences in crop species, nanoparticle properties, application method, exposure duration, and environmental conditions also make direct comparison between studies difficult. These variations indicate that promising results obtained under controlled conditions cannot automatically be considered representative of field performance.

Nano-Enabled Delivery Systems in Precision Agriculture

Controlled and Targeted Agrochemical Delivery

The development of nano-enabled delivery systems for fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides represents a promising development in agricultural technology, focusing on controlled release, target specificity, reduced chemical losses, and improved agricultural efficiency. These systems utilize nanocarriers such as synthetic and natural polymers, nano-emulsions, lipid NPs, and nanofibers to create Controlled Release Systems (CRSs) that are eco-friendly and reduce environmental pollution and health risks associated with conventional agrochemicals.65 Nano-fertilizers, for example, are engineered to enhance nutrient uptake efficiency by delivering macro- and micronutrients directly to the plant rhizosphere in a regulated manner, thus improving nutrient management and reducing environmental impact.82,92 Similarly, nano-pesticides and nano-herbicides are designed to provide targeted delivery and controlled release, which significantly enhances their efficacy while minimizing non-target effects and environmental contamination.68,93 The integration of smart, stimuli-responsive release mechanisms allows these nano-based pesticides to respond to specific environmental triggers, thereby optimizing their application and reducing the indiscriminate use of chemicals.94 Moreover, innovative systems like the Pesticide-Fertilizer All-In-One Combination (PFAC) strategy employ deep learning and advanced materials such as hollow mesoporous silica and polydopamine coatings to achieve precision delivery and synergistic effects in pest control and nutrient supply.95 The use of nanocomposites also facilitates the sustained release and targeted delivery of agrochemicals, enhancing plant production and protection while addressing biosafety and environmental concerns.96 Despite these advancements, the field of nano-enabled agriculture is still in its infancy, with ongoing research needed to develop biodegradable, cost-effective, and safe nanomaterials, as well as to conduct systematic studies on nanotoxicity and public awareness.69 The nano-enabled delivery systems hold great promise for creating a more sustainable and efficient agricultural system, but further research and development are essential to fully realize their potential.97

Smart Delivery Mechanisms and Release Dynamics

Stimuli-responsive nano-delivery systems are innovative approaches in PA, designed to enhance the efficiency and environmental safety of agrichemicals by responding to specific environmental triggers. These systems include pH-responsive, temperature-responsive, moisture-responsive, and enzyme-triggered release mechanisms. pH-responsive systems, such as those utilizing bio-stimulant-based nanocarriers, release their payload in response to the acidic or basic conditions of the target environment, optimizing the delivery of fungicides like tebuconazole and prothioconazole for effective control of Fusarium Head Blight in wheat.98 Temperature-responsive systems, exemplified by poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-based nanogels, adjust the release rate of pesticides like lambda-cyhalothrin in response to temperature changes, aligning pesticide release with pest population dynamics and improving pest control efficacy while reducing environmental residues.99 Moisture-responsive systems, although not explicitly detailed in the provided contexts, generally rely on the presence of water to trigger the release of active ingredients, ensuring that agrichemicals are only released when conditions are conducive to plant uptake. Enzyme-triggered systems leverage the presence of specific enzymes secreted by pests or pathogens to degrade the carrier material and release the active ingredient, providing targeted action against pests and pathogens while minimizing non-target effects.100 These systems are often constructed using biopolymer-based core-shell nanostructures, which can be tailored for multi-stimuli responsiveness, enhancing the precision of agrichemical delivery and improving plant health metrics such as photosynthesis efficacy and nutrient uptake.101 The integration of these stimuli-responsive mechanisms into nano-delivery systems represents a promising development in sustainable agriculture, offering a means to reduce the indiscriminate use of pesticides and fertilizers, thereby mitigating their environmental and health impacts.65,94 As research progresses, the development of hybrid and multi-stimuli-responsive formulations continues to hold promise for further enhancing the stability, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of agrichemicals, paving the way for more efficient and eco-friendly agricultural practices.102

Sustainability and Efficiency of Nano-Delivery Systems

Nano-enabled delivery systems have significantly contributed to reducing agrochemical overuse, minimizing environmental losses, improving resource-use efficiency, and supporting sustainable farming practices. These systems, which include nano-fertilizers, nano-pesticides, and nano-herbicides, offer enhanced efficiency and targeted delivery of agrochemicals, thereby reducing the required dosages and minimizing environmental contamination.72,103 For example, nano-fertilizers provide controlled and sustained nutrient release, which reduces leaching and pollution, while enhancing nutrient uptake and plant health.49,72 Similarly, nano-pesticides and herbicides enable precise application, reducing waste and contamination, and improving crop protection.104,105 The use of nano-biosensors further supports sustainable agriculture by enabling real-time monitoring of plant health and environmental conditions, thus optimizing resource usage and enhancing precision farming practices.106,107 Additionally, Si NPs have been shown to improve plant resilience against abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity, further supporting sustainable crop production.49 The integration of nanotechnology into agriculture not only enhances the efficiency of agrochemical use but also aligns with global sustainability goals by reducing the ecological footprint of farming practices.104,108 Despite these advancements, challenges remain, including the need for further research on the long-term environmental impacts and the development of biodegradable and cost-effective nanomaterials.69,109 Overall, nano-enabled delivery systems represent a promising avenue for achieving sustainable agriculture by enhancing productivity while minimizing environmental impacts.

The sustainability benefits of nano-enabled delivery systems also depend on the formulation and conditions of application. Controlled release may reduce input losses and improve delivery efficiency, but this does not necessarily mean that the complete delivery system has a lower environmental burden. Carrier materials, synthesis requirements, formulation stability, degradation products, and end-of-life (EoL) behaviour may introduce additional impacts that are not always considered in short-term agronomic studies. Moreover, the limited commercial application of many nano-enabled systems indicates that laboratory demonstrations of improved delivery efficiency have not yet consistently translated into field-scale benefits. Nano-Biosensors for Soil and Crop Monitoring

Nano-biosensors have emerged as a promising approach in precision farming systems, offering promising developments in monitoring soil nutrients, crop health, pathogens, environmental conditions, and agricultural stress factors. These sensors leverage nanotechnology to provide highly sensitive, accurate, and real-time data, which is crucial for optimizing agricultural practices and ensuring sustainable farming. For example, nano-sensors can be seamlessly integrated into plant systems to detect stress signals and environmental conditions, thereby enabling early intervention and precise management of crop health.115,116 In soil nutrient monitoring, nano-biosensors facilitate the efficient use of natural resources by providing detailed insights into soil conditions, which helps in optimizing nutrient application and reducing environmental impacts.117,118 Moreover, these sensors are instrumental in detecting pathogens and managing crop diseases, offering a proactive approach to pest and pathogen control, which is essential for maintaining crop productivity and quality.118,119 The integration of nanotechnology with sensor networks also enhances the detection of environmental pollutants and stress factors, contributing to improved plant growth and stress resistance.120,121 Additionally, the development of plant wearables and wireless sensor networks has expanded the capabilities of PA, allowing for continuous monitoring and data-driven decision-making.116 Despite these advancements, challenges such as biosafety, regulatory issues, and scalability remain, necessitating further research and development to fully harness the potential of nano-biosensors in agriculture.103,121 The application of nano-biosensors in precision farming represents a significant step towards achieving sustainable agriculture by enhancing resource-use efficiency, reducing agrochemical inputs, and supporting environmental conservation.114,122

Integration with Precision Farming Technologies

The integration of green nanoparticle-based sensing systems with IoT technologies, drones, remote sensing platforms, and real-time agricultural monitoring systems represents a promising development in PA, enhancing efficiency and sustainability. Plant-Nanoparticle Interactions and Uptake Mechanisms

Uptake and Translocation Pathways

The uptake, transport, translocation, and accumulation pathways of green NPs in plants involve complex interactions across root, foliar, and vascular systems. Root uptake is a primary pathway where NPs are absorbed through root hairs and transported via the xylem to aboveground tissues. Studies have shown that NPs such as nano-biochar and polystyrene nanoplastics are readily absorbed by plant roots and translocated to shoots and leaves, with significant accumulation in root cortical and vascular tissues.139,140 The efficiency of this process can vary significantly among plant species, influenced by anatomical and physiological traits.140 Foliar uptake occurs primarily through stomatal and cuticular routes, where NPs penetrate the leaf surface and are transported via apoplastic and symplastic pathways.141,142 The translocation of foliar-applied NPs is facilitated by the phloem, enabling bidirectional movement to roots and other plant parts, although the extent of this movement can be limited by factors such as nanoparticle size, surface charge, and plant species.143,144 Vascular transport involves the movement of NPs through the xylem and phloem, with studies on CeO2 NPs in cucumber plants demonstrating their distribution and transformation within plant tissues.145 The transport and accumulation of NPs are further influenced by environmental conditions and the physical-chemical properties of the NPs, such as size, zeta potential, and affinity to organic phases.141,146 These pathways highlight the potential for NPs to enhance nutrient delivery and crop protection, but also raise concerns about their entry into the food chain and associated health risks, necessitating further research into their long-term implications and safe application in agriculture.139,147

Physiological and Biochemical Responses

The exposure of plants to green NPs elicits a range of physiological, biochemical, and metabolic responses that significantly impact photosynthesis, enzymatic activity, nutrient assimilation, and stress responses. Green NPs, such as Fe3O4, TiO2, and AgNPs, have been shown to enhance photosynthetic efficiency by increasing chlorophyll content and improving the photosynthetic apparatus, which is crucial for carbon assimilation and overall plant growth.148–150 For example, Fe3O4 NPs have been reported to increase sugar content and photosynthesis in Dendrobium huoshanense, while TiO2 NPs enhance photosynthesis efficiency in Pisum sativum by improving CO2 assimilation and gas exchange parameters.149,150 These NPs also influence enzymatic activities, particularly those involved in antioxidant defense mechanisms. The application of NPs can boost the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxidase (POD), thereby enhancing the plant’s ability to mitigate oxidative stress induced by environmental factors.73,151 This is particularly evident under drought conditions, where NPs have been shown to reduce oxidative stress markers like malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).151 Moreover, green NPs can alter nutrient assimilation by affecting the uptake and accumulation of essential elements such as Fe, Mn, Co, and Zn, which are vital for various metabolic processes.149,150 However, the impact of NPs is dose-dependent, as excessive concentrations can lead to phytotoxicity, disrupting photosynthesis and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, as observed with copper oxide NPs in lettuce.152 Despite these challenges, the strategic use of NPs, particularly at optimal concentrations, can enhance plant resilience to stress by modulating stress-responsive pathways and improving overall plant health and productivity.153,154 Thus, while green NPs offer promising benefits for plant growth and stress tolerance, careful consideration of their concentration and type is essential to maximize their positive effects while minimizing potential adverse impacts.

Interaction with Soil Microbiome and Rhizosphere

Green NPs (NPs) have a multifaceted impact on soil microorganisms, rhizosphere interactions, microbial diversity, and soil ecological balance within sustainable agricultural systems. The effects of NPs on soil microorganisms are highly variable, depending on factors such as NP type, concentration, and soil properties. For example, metal and metal oxide NPs, like Ag and ZnO, can have both stimulatory and inhibitory effects on soil microbiota, with toxicity often increasing at higher concentrations.155–157 These NPs can disrupt key microbial processes such as mineralization and nitrogen fixation, which are crucial for plant growth and soil health.156 However, at lower concentrations, ZnO NPs have been shown to enhance plant growth and microbial activity, indicating a potential for beneficial applications in agriculture.156 The interactions between NPs and soil microorganisms also extend to the rhizosphere, where they can alter microbial community structures and functions. For example, TiO2 NPs have been observed to shift the wheat rhizobiome towards chemoheterotrophy, promoting beneficial plant-associated bacteria.158 Moreover, plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) can facilitate the green synthesis of NPs, which can be used for soil remediation and nutrient delivery, thus supporting sustainable agriculture.159 Despite these potential benefits, the introduction of NPs into soil ecosystems poses risks to microbial diversity and ecological balance. Meta-analyses have shown that NPs can significantly reduce microbial diversity, biomass, and activity, with metal NPs having more pronounced negative effects than carbon-based NPs.155,160 These adverse effects underscore the need for careful assessment and management of NP applications in agriculture to avoid disrupting soil ecological balance.160 Moreover, the integration of nanotechnology with biological processes, such as rhizoremediation, offers promising strategies for enhancing soil health and mitigating pollution, although the ecological risks associated with NP use must be addressed.161,162 While green NPs hold potential for advancing sustainable agricultural practices, their application requires a nuanced understanding of their interactions with soil microorganisms and ecosystems to ensure environmental safety and efficacy.163

Taken together, these findings indicate that the biological effects of NPs cannot be interpreted solely in terms of whether a material is classified as green or non-green. The same nanoparticle may produce beneficial responses at one concentration or under one set of environmental conditions and adverse responses at another. Particle characteristics, exposure conditions, plant species, soil properties, and microbial communities therefore need to be considered when interpreting reported agricultural outcomes. This variability is an important reason why results from individual laboratory studies should not be generalized directly to field-scale agricultural systems. As NPs accumulate in roots, stems, leaves, reproductive organs, and cellular compartments, they interact with physiological and biochemical processes that regulate nutrient assimilation, photosynthetic activity, water-use efficiency, and stress tolerance. These interactions can stimulate antioxidant defense systems, modulate reactive oxygen species homeostasis, activate stress-responsive pathways, and influence metabolic and hormonal regulation. Environmental and Ecotoxicological Implications

Soil and Water Environmental Impacts

The environmental fate, mobility, persistence, and accumulation of green NPs in soil and water systems, particularly in agricultural applications, are complex and multifaceted issues. Green NPs, such as cerium dioxide (CeO2-NPs), copper-based nanomaterials (Cu-based NMs), and magnetic iron-based nanoparticles (MINPs), are increasingly utilized in agriculture due to their potential to enhance crop productivity and resilience against stressors like drought and heavy metal contamination.169,170 However, their release into the environment raises concerns about their long-term ecological impacts. In soil systems, NPs can undergo various transformation processes, including agglomeration, dissolution, and redox reactions, which influence their mobility and bioavailability.171 These transformations can affect the soil’s physico-chemical properties and microbial communities, potentially disrupting plant-microbe interactions essential for plant nutrition and soil health.170,172 NPs can also act as carriers for other pollutants, facilitating the spread of contaminants to adjacent water bodies or groundwater.171 The persistence of NPs in the environment is influenced by their nanoscale size, which complicates their detection and risk assessment.173,174 Studies using models like nanoFate have shown that even soluble metal oxide NPs can accumulate in the environment at concentrations that may exceed toxic thresholds, particularly in high-production scenarios.175 The uptake and translocation of NPs in plants occur through various pathways, including root and foliar adsorption, and are influenced by factors such as soil characteristics and the presence of other ions.176 Despite their potential benefits, the accumulation of NPs in soil and plant systems poses risks to crop production and food security, necessitating further research to understand their environmental behavior and develop guidelines for their safe use in agriculture.172,177 While green NPs offer promising applications in agriculture, their environmental fate and impacts require careful consideration to ensure sustainable agricultural practices.177,178

Ecotoxicological Risks and Bioaccumulation

The ecotoxicological impacts of green NPs on non-target organisms, aquatic systems, beneficial soil biota, and food-chain bioaccumulation are multifaceted and complex. Zinc oxide NPs have been shown to affect various aquatic organisms, with freshwater invertebrates being particularly sensitive, followed by bacteria, algae, and vertebrates. The primary mechanisms of toxicity include the release of Zn2+ ions and the generation of ROS.179 Similarly, Ag NPs exhibit toxicity in aquatic environments, with bioaccumulation and transformation processes affecting aquatic flora and fauna. The toxicity of biologically synthesized AgNPs is comparable to chemically synthesized ones, impacting aquatic microcosms through various exposure routes.180 The fate of NPs in aquatic and terrestrial environments is influenced by their direct and indirect introduction through industrial discharges and runoff, affecting both water and soil ecosystems.178 The ecotoxicological effects of nanomaterials (NMs) are significant across different environments, with a need for further research to address knowledge gaps and achieve sustainable development goals.181 Studies have shown that crustaceans, algae, and fish are particularly sensitive to Ag, CuO, and ZnO NPs, with potential threats to non-target organisms due to their release from consumer products.182 Metallic nanomaterials (MNMs) can accumulate and transform within aquatic food webs, posing risks of bioaccumulation and biomagnification, although current studies often show biomagnification factors (BMF) of less than 1, indicating limited biomagnification.183 Graphene oxide contamination in aquatic food webs can disrupt digestion and physiological processes, leading to bioaccumulation and potential biomagnification, particularly affecting top-level consumers and human populations reliant on seafood.184 The unique properties of manufactured NPs complicate risk assessments, as their bioavailability and toxicity can be influenced by environmental factors such as water chemistry.185 Engineered NPs like AgNPs and AuNPs induce oxidative stress in aquatic organisms, with environmental factors affecting their aggregation and toxicity.186 Lastly, titanium dioxide (TiO2) NPs have been shown to reduce microbial abundance and plant growth in terrestrial ecosystems, with potential genotoxic effects on aquatic organisms, highlighting the risks of trophic transfer and toxicity in both terrestrial and aquatic environments.187 The ecotoxicological impacts of green NPs are significant, necessitating careful consideration of their environmental release and interactions to mitigate adverse effects on ecosystems and human health.

An important contradiction in the literature is that biological synthesis does not necessarily eliminate the environmental hazards associated with nanoparticle composition. For example, biologically synthesized AgNPs have been reported to show toxicity comparable to chemically synthesized AgNPs under aquatic exposure conditions. This suggests that the environmental behaviour of a nanoparticle is governed not only by the synthesis route but also by its composition, particle characteristics, dose, transformation, and exposure environment. Therefore, claims of improved environmental safety should be supported by nanoparticle-specific ecotoxicological evidence rather than inferred from the use of a green synthesis method alone.

Long-Term Sustainability Concerns

Long-term sustainability concerns associated with continuous nanoparticle exposure, ecosystem imbalance, environmental persistence, and unintended ecological consequences are multifaceted and complex. Nanoparticles (NPs), due to their unique properties and widespread use, persist in various environmental matrices, leading to bioaccumulation and potential ecological disruptions. For example, Au NPs have been shown to persist in terrestrial environments, affecting soil organisms like Enchytraeus crypticus by reducing reproduction rates over extended exposure periods, highlighting the increased hazards of low concentrations over time.188 Similarly, Ag NPs have been observed to alter community structures in aquatic ecosystems, reducing zooplankton density and increasing phytoplankton biomass, which in turn affects ecosystem respiration and function.189 The persistence and bioaccumulation of NPs in ecosystems pose significant challenges, as their nanoscale size complicates understanding their environmental behavior and toxicity, necessitating comprehensive risk management strategies.174 Moreover, the interaction of NPs with photosynthetic organisms, such as plants and algae, can disrupt growth and health, impacting ecosystem stability and function.190 The chronic exposure to NPs, such as CuO NPs, can lead to population collapse in pest species due to oxidative stress and impaired reproduction, underscoring the need for multigenerational toxicity assessments.191 Additionally, the effects of boron and vanadium NPs on soil organisms reveal varying toxicological impacts depending on exposure duration and concentration, emphasizing the importance of long-term studies for accurate risk assessment.192 The ecological implications of NPs are further complicated by their potential to alter microbial community structures and functions, as seen with AgNPs affecting microbial decomposer communities in streams, which are crucial for nutrient cycling and ecosystem health.193 The unintended ecological consequences of NPs necessitate a holistic understanding of their environmental impacts, advocating for a safer-by-design approach and the integration of long-term and multigenerational studies into risk assessment frameworks to ensure sustainable NP use.194,195

Environmental Implications of Green Nanoparticles

Circular Agriculture and Waste-Derived Green Nanoparticles

Agro-Waste and Biomass-Derived Nanoparticles

The utilization of agricultural residues, food waste, and biomass resources for green nanoparticle synthesis represents a promising development in waste valorization and sustainable resource utilization. Green synthesis methods leverage natural reagents such as plant extracts, microorganisms, and agricultural waste, offering an reduced chemical use alternative to conventional chemical and physical nanoparticle synthesis methods, which are often energy-intensive and environmentally harmful.35,201 Agricultural residues, such as lignocellulosic biomass, are particularly promising due to their abundance and potential to be converted into high-value nanomaterials like nanocellulose and nanolignin, which have applications in packaging, medicine, and energy.202 For example, lignin NPs derived from agricultural residues can be transformed into multifunctional catalytic systems, such as lignin-based magnetic nanocomposites, which are effective in dye removal from wastewater.203 Similarly, fruit and vegetable wastes, such as Citrus limetta peels and potato peels, have been used to synthesize iron oxide and silver NPs, respectively, demonstrating excellent catalytic and antimicrobial properties.204,205 These NPs not only offer potential applications in environmental remediation and medicine but also contribute to sustainable waste management by converting waste into valuable products.205 Moreover, the synthesis of silica NPs from agricultural waste, particularly from silica-rich residues like cereal crops, highlights the potential for agro-waste management and valorization, with applications in biomedicine and environmental remediation.206,207 The green synthesis of NPs from biomass not only reduces the carbon footprint and energy consumption but also enhances the economic viability of nanoparticle production by utilizing low-cost, renewable resources.30,45 The integration of green synthesis methods in nanoparticle production underscores the importance of sustainable practices in advancing nanotechnology and addressing global environmental challenges.30,35

Resource Recovery and Circular Farming Systems

Green NPs play a pivotal role in supporting circular agriculture by enhancing nutrient recycling, resource recovery, waste minimization, and closed-loop farming systems. These NPs, synthesized through eco-friendly methods, offer a sustainable alternative to traditional agricultural inputs like fertilizers and pesticides, thereby reducing environmental impact and promoting resource efficiency.48,208 Green nanomaterials, such as nanofertilizers and nanopesticides, are designed to improve soil health and plant productivity by enhancing nutrient uptake and stress resistance, which are crucial for nutrient cycling and soil microbial diversity.209,210 The use of biological resources in the synthesis of these NPs minimizes the production of hazardous substances and energy input, aligning with the principles of circular agriculture.45 Moreover, nanotechnology facilitates the valorization of agricultural waste into high-value products, such as biofuels and bio-based compounds, thus supporting resource recovery and waste minimization.211 The integration of nanotechnology in precision farming allows for targeted delivery of nutrients and pesticides, optimizing their use and reducing environmental pollution.103,163 Additionally, the development of graphene from agricultural waste exemplifies how nanotechnology can transform waste into valuable materials, further closing the loop in agricultural systems.212 Despite these advancements, there are concerns regarding the potential toxicity and environmental impact of NPs, necessitating robust risk assessment and management strategies to ensure their safe and effective use in agriculture.208,213 The green NPs offer promising solutions for advancing circular agriculture by enhancing sustainability and efficiency across various agricultural processes.

Waste-derived nanoparticle production also involves important trade-offs. Although the use of agricultural residues can reduce waste disposal and provide low-cost feedstocks, the overall sustainability of the process depends on feedstock collection, transportation, pre-treatment, extraction efficiency, synthesis yield, energy requirements, and the management of residual materials. Variability in waste composition may also affect nanoparticle properties and reproducibility. Therefore, waste valorization should not automatically be equated with circularity; its environmental and economic benefits need to be demonstrated across the complete production chain.

Sustainable Nanomanufacturing for Agriculture

Sustainable and scalable nanomanufacturing approaches in agriculture emphasize eco-friendly production, circular resource utilization, and environmentally responsible practices. Nanotechnology offers transformative solutions for sustainable agriculture by enhancing crop productivity, improving soil health, and ensuring efficient resource utilization. For example, nanoscale delivery systems, such as polymer-based NPs and metal oxide particles, enable precise application of fertilizers and pesticides, reducing environmental impact and optimizing nutrient use.43,103 The integration of nanotechnology with digital agriculture platforms facilitates real-time monitoring and adaptive management, further supporting sustainable practices.103 Biogenic synthesis of NPs, using plant extracts and microorganisms, presents an potentially lower-impact alternative to conventional methods, offering benefits such as cost-effectiveness, scalability, and biocompatibility.214 This green synthesis aligns with the principles of the circular economy by promoting waste valorization and resource efficiency, as seen in the conversion of organic waste into biofuels using nanocatalysts.211 Additionally, nanotechnology aids in pollution detection and stress resistance, enhancing plant growth and resilience while minimizing agrochemical use.121 However, challenges such as nanopollution, nanotoxicity, and regulatory issues must be addressed to ensure responsible deployment.215,216 The development of biodegradable nanomaterials and standardized guidelines for evaluating nanomaterial toxicity are crucial for mitigating potential risks.103,208 Collaborative efforts among researchers, policymakers, and stakeholders are essential to harness the full potential of nanotechnology for sustainable agricultural growth, ensuring food security and environmental stewardship for future generations.119,211

Challenges and Limitations

Technical and Field-Level Challenges

The practical implementation of green NPs in PA faces several technical and field-level challenges, as highlighted across the provided papers. One significant challenge is the reproducibility of green synthesis methods, which, although eco-friendly and cost-effective, often suffer from variability in nanoparticle size and properties due to the inherent complexity of biological systems used in synthesis.214 Scalability of nanoparticle production is another hurdle, as many green synthesis methods are not yet optimized for large-scale production, limiting their practical application in agriculture.251 Field conditions introduce variability that affects the consistency of nanoparticle performance; factors such as soil type, climate, and crop species can influence the efficacy of NPs, complicating the development of standardized application protocols.22 Nanoparticle stability and shelf life are also critical concerns, as many green NPs may degrade or lose functionality over time, impacting their effectiveness in field applications.109 Delivery efficiency is another challenge, with the need for advanced delivery systems that ensure targeted and controlled release of NPs to maximize their benefits while minimizing environmental impact.103 Plant-nanoparticle interactions are complex and not fully understood, with potential risks of toxicity and unintended effects on plant physiology and soil microbiota.70 Finally, achieving consistent agronomic performance under diverse environmental and cropping systems remains elusive, as NPs may perform well under controlled conditions but fail to deliver the same results in variable field environments.72 Addressing these challenges requires interdisciplinary research, improved synthesis techniques, and robust regulatory frameworks to ensure the safe and effective use of green NPs in agriculture.32,33

Economic Feasibility and Commercialization

The economic feasibility and commercialization potential of green nanoparticle-based agricultural technologies are influenced by several factors, including production costs, availability of biomass-derived feedstocks, and scalability of green synthesis processes. The use of lignocellulosic biomass for nanoparticle production is promising due to its biodegradability and broad applicability, but challenges such as high energy requirements and low product stability persist, impacting economic viability.202,252 The availability of biomass-derived feedstocks is abundant, with agro-industrial waste offering a sustainable source for cellulose and lignin extraction, although the efficiency of these processes is contingent on the feedstock composition and pre-treatment strategies.253 Green synthesis methods, such as those using plant extracts and microbes, offer reduced chemical use alternatives to conventional nanoparticle production, but scalability remains a challenge due to variability in raw materials and process reproducibility.254 In terms of cost-benefit considerations for precision farming applications, NPs like Ag/AgCl derived from ginger straw waste have shown effective antimicrobial properties against soil-borne pathogens, suggesting potential for reducing agrochemical use and enhancing crop protection.255 However, farmer adoption barriers include the high initial costs and the need for technical knowledge to integrate these technologies into existing farming practices.256 Market readiness is advancing, with bio-based fertilizers and other green technologies projected to grow significantly, driven by regulatory support and circular economy policies.257 Moreover, challenges such as biosafety concerns, regulatory gaps, and the need for standardized testing protocols hinder large-scale industrial deployment.256,258 While green nanoparticle-based agricultural technologies hold significant promise for sustainable agriculture, their commercialization will require overcoming technical, economic, and regulatory hurdles through integrated approaches and industry-academia partnerships.256,259

Regulatory and Safety Considerations

The agricultural use of green NPs presents several regulatory and safety challenges that necessitate comprehensive frameworks for risk assessment and management. Although international guidance and testing approaches for nanomaterial characterization, environmental fate and risk assessment are available, important gaps remain in their harmonized application to agricultural nanomaterials, particularly under realistic field conditions.249,260 The complexity of nanoparticle interactions in agricultural settings, such as their mobility, transformation, and interaction with soil components, further complicates the assessment of their ecological risks.260 Toxicity testing protocols must be refined to account for the unique properties of NPs, including their size, surface chemistry, and potential for bioaccumulation, which can influence their toxicity and environmental persistence.261,262 Additionally, the integration of NPs into PA raises food safety concerns, as their transfer from soils to edible plant tissues could pose risks to human health.263 Public acceptance of nano-enabled technologies is another critical factor, as societal concerns about the safety and ethical implications of nanotechnology can influence regulatory decisions and market adoption.264 To address these challenges, there is a pressing need for regulatory harmonization and the development of internationally recognized safety standards that support innovation while ensuring environmental and human health.265 Policy measures should focus on fostering collaboration between researchers, industry stakeholders, and regulatory bodies to align scientific advancements with regulatory frameworks.266 This includes the establishment of platforms like MIND, which aim to integrate fragmented initiatives and promote knowledge sharing across disciplines.266 Ultimately, a balanced approach that considers both the benefits and potential risks of NPs is essential for their safe and sustainable integration into agriculture, ensuring that technological advancements do not compromise ecological integrity or public health.267,268

Current regulatory approaches to agricultural nanotechnology are largely implemented through existing chemical, pesticide, fertiliser, food and environmental legislation rather than through a single regulatory system specifically dedicated to agricultural nanomaterials.269,270 In the European Union, nanomaterials are covered by nano-specific provisions under the REACH framework, while nano-enabled plant protection products are addressed within the existing plant protection product regulatory framework, although important gaps remain in relation to nanoforms.270,271 For nanofertilisers, recent evidence indicates that the European Union still lacks a fully fit-for-purpose regulatory pathway specifically adapted to their development and assessment.272 Food and feed applications involving NPs fall within the scope of EFSA safety assessment, with requirements addressing physicochemical characterisation, dietary exposure and, where relevant, nano-specific hazard assessment.273 At the international level, OECD test approaches and guidance have been developed for nanomaterial characterisation and environmental fate, including dissolution, aggregation and transformation processes.274 However, applying these frameworks to agricultural NPs remains challenging because particle size, surface properties, dissolution, transformation, aggregation and environmental conditions can influence their mobility, bioavailability and toxicity.271,274 Greater harmonisation of testing requirements, environmental monitoring and regulatory criteria is therefore needed to support consistent risk assessment and responsible deployment of nano-enabled agricultural products.270–272

Technology Readiness Levels, Commercialization Pathways and Industrial Scale-Up

Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) provide a useful framework for distinguishing laboratory research from technologies that have progressed toward agricultural use. The framework consists of nine levels, with TRL 1–3 covering discovery and proof of concept, TRL 4–6 covering testing, validation and prototype development, TRL 7–8 covering demonstration, and TRL 9 representing commercial deployment.275,276 For green NPs in PA, most of the literature remains at the discovery stage. Plant-mediated ZnO, Ag and TiO2 NPs are being investigated for agricultural applications, but inconsistent control of particle size, shape and product quality remains a major barrier to commercial translation.10,14,277 During development, evidence becomes stronger through controlled and semi-realistic validation. For example, 25 nm ZnO NPs produced 85% higher zinc uptake efficiency in tomato than zinc sulfate, while nano-fertilizer studies reported improvements in crop yield and nutrient-use efficiency.22,241 Nano-biosensors for detecting plant stress, pathogens and pesticide residues are also mainly in development, although field trials, farmer education, portability and cost-effectiveness remain important requirements.18 Field demonstration is less common but increasing. A multi-location nano urea study across 21 locations in India reported grain-yield increases of 7.6–9.1% across four zones, with a potential 25% reduction in nitrogen use and an estimated annual reduction of 5.06 billion kg CO2-equivalent emissions.278 In lowland rice, biogenic urea NPs allowed at least 25% of conventional urea to be substituted without harming soil bacterial and fungal species.279 Crop-protection technologies are also progressing toward field validation. Nanoencapsulated lufenuron achieved more than 76% control in semi-field trials and released the active compound up to eight times more slowly than free lufenuron.280 A protein-gated mesoporous silica-λ-cyhalothrin formulation was scaled up for field efficacy testing, while a unimolecular nanopesticide system of approximately 3 nm demonstrated field-scale efficacy against multiple pests.281,282 Commercial deployment remains narrow and is concentrated mainly in nutrient delivery. Nanofertilizers have already been adopted in India, while nano-nitrogen, nano-zinc and nano-copper are under initial testing.283 However, commercialization is not equivalent to widespread adoption because awareness, health concerns, climatic influences, technological limitations and product-quality issues can affect uptake of nano urea.284 Nano-biosensors and many nano-formulated pesticides remain mainly at laboratory or pilot scale.285 Two commercialization pathways are identified: incumbent-led integration through established fertilizer or agrochemical manufacturers and research-led translation through research institutes, spin-outs and consortia.245,281,283 Industrial scale-up remains difficult because biological feedstocks vary with species, geographical origin, growing conditions, season and extraction method, affecting particle size, morphology and surface charge.277,286,287 Differences in precursor concentration, pH, temperature and reaction time can further alter nanoparticle properties, reducing batch-to-batch reproducibility and manufacturing consistency.5,288 High synthesis and characterization costs, together with raw-material and processing costs, further constrain commercial production.16,289 The evidence indicates a strong discovery and development base, a smaller but growing field-demonstration stage, and a narrow commercial segment. Progress toward the sustainable PA objectives of this review will depend on improved manufacturing consistency, field validation, regulatory alignment, economic feasibility and responsible translation from research to agricultural practice.

Future Perspectives

The integration of green NPs with AI and other advanced technologies presents significant opportunities for enhancing PA, making it more intelligent, scalable, and sustainable. Green NPs, such as those synthesized using plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), can be utilized for soil remediation and targeted nutrient delivery, thereby improving soil health and resilience against environmental stressors like drought and salinity.159 When combined with AI, these NPs can enhance decision-making processes in PA by providing real-time data on soil conditions and crop health, which can be further optimized using machine learning algorithms and IoT technologies.290 The use of AI in PA, particularly through agentic AI and federated learning, allows for the integration of distributed sensing devices and intelligent agents, enabling real-time monitoring and decision support at the farm level.291 This integration can be further enhanced by incorporating UAVs and satellite remote sensing, which provide high-resolution data for crop health monitoring and landscape analysis, thereby improving resource use efficiency and reducing environmental impact.130 Additionally, the use of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) in agriculture offers potential for pollutant remediation and controlled agrochemical release, which can be optimized through AI-assisted design and multifunctional integration.258 The development of biosensing technologies for pathogen detection, coupled with AI and IoT, facilitates predictive diagnostics and precision crop management, reducing chemical dependency and enhancing climate resilience.292 Moreover, the adoption of bio-based fertilizers derived from waste streams supports circular agricultural practices by improving soil properties and reducing environmental impacts, which can be integrated with AI-driven optimization for enhanced nutrient recovery and climate-adaptive formulations.257 The convergence of these technologies offers a pathway to sustainable PA, addressing challenges such as climate change, resource scarcity, and food security while promoting a circular economy and resilient farming systems.

Recent advances are extending the role of AI from farm management to the design and application of agricultural nanomaterials. Machine learning, deep learning and reinforcement learning can support the design and optimisation of nanocarriers, nano-fertilisers, nano-pesticides and nano-sensing platforms by linking material properties with biological and delivery performance. At the farm level, the integration of nano-biosensors, IoT networks, UAVs, satellite and other remote-sensing systems can provide continuous information on soil nutrients, moisture, crop health, pathogens and environmental conditions. These data can be processed through AI models and smart decision-support systems to improve irrigation scheduling, nutrient application, disease detection and site-specific crop management. Such integration represents a shift towards a continuous sense-analyse-respond agricultural system, although reliable datasets, sensor performance, system interoperability, model transparency and validation under diverse field conditions remain important challenges.130,293,294

Critical Analysis and Discussion

The evidence reviewed in this study shows that the performance of green NPs depends on several factors and cannot be explained by the type of nanoparticle alone. Dose, particle size, surface properties, method of application, crop type, soil condition and environmental factors can all affect the final outcome. The same nanoparticle may therefore produce a useful response at one concentration but cause harmful effects at a higher concentration. For this reason, the use of green NPs in agriculture should be based on their intended function and application conditions rather than on the general assumption that they are safe because they are produced by a green method.

Metal NPs, particularly silver and copper NPs, show clear potential for disease and pathogen control. However, their effects can change greatly with dose. For example, silver NPs at 100 ppm produced a stimulatory response, whereas 200 ppm reduced maize growth, chlorophyll content and the uptake of phosphorus, magnesium and potassium. Exposure of soil bacteria to 1–10 mg/kg of silver NPs also reduced bacterial diversity by 15–40% within 30 days. These findings highlight an important trade-off. Strong antimicrobial activity may help control harmful microorganisms, but the same property may also affect beneficial microorganisms that are important for soil health and nutrient cycling. Therefore, the effective dose for crop protection may not always be the safest dose for the wider soil environment. More work is needed to identify concentration ranges that provide the required agricultural benefit while limiting damage to non-target organisms.

Metal oxide NPs show an even wider range of responses. Some can provide nutrients, support plant growth, reduce stress or help immobilize contaminants in soil, while others may negatively affect nutrient cycling or plant nutrient uptake. NiFe2O4 at 50 mg/kg increased 1000-seed weight by 12.3% and total yield by 15.6%, showing that some metal oxide systems can provide measurable agronomic benefits. In contrast, CuO reduced nitrification by 25–50%, while TiO2 reduced nutrient uptake efficiency by 10–30% in reported studies. These differences are important because they show that metal oxide NPs cannot be considered as one uniform group. Their environmental effects are strongly influenced by their composition, size, surface characteristics, concentration and interaction with soil and plants. Future studies should therefore evaluate individual materials rather than making broad conclusions about all metal oxide NPs.

Silica NPs appear particularly useful where controlled delivery is required. Their high surface area and tunable structure allow active ingredients to be carried and released over time. A mesoporous silica-chlorfenapyr formulation, for example, provided sustained release for two weeks and produced 57.2% mortality compared with 42.1% for a commercial suspension. This suggests that nanoscale delivery can improve the efficiency of some agricultural chemicals. However, better delivery does not automatically mean better overall performance. A practical formulation must also remain stable during storage, be suitable for large-scale production, release the active ingredient at the required rate and behave predictably after application to soil or plants. The long-term environmental fate of the carrier should also be considered before large-scale use.

Polymeric and biopolymer NPs show considerable promise because they can be used for controlled delivery and are generally designed to improve the stability and handling of agricultural inputs. Reported results include increases of 40.6% in antioxidant enzyme activity, 77.9% in defense enzyme activity, 103.2% in defense-gene expression, 28.1% in biomass and 15.7% in yield. An alginate/chitosan system also achieved more than 50% encapsulation, while PLA-based systems showed high encapsulation efficiency. These results indicate that polymer-based systems can support both crop protection and stress management. At the same time, much of the available evidence is still based on laboratory or controlled experiments. Performance under open-field conditions may be different because temperature, rainfall, soil properties, microbial activity and other environmental factors can change release behaviour and effectiveness. Production cost, raw material consistency, storage stability and regulatory requirements also need further study.

Selenium NPs have a different role from many of the other systems reviewed. Their main value appears to be related to stress tolerance, plant defense and biofortification. They have been associated with the activation of stress-responsive pathways and improved plant responses under adverse conditions. However, the available evidence does not provide a numerical effect size that can be directly compared with the quantitative results reported for several other nanoparticle systems. This is an important evidence gap. In addition, selenium has a relatively narrow range between an effective concentration and an excessive concentration. Future research should therefore focus on identifying suitable dose ranges for different crops and on determining how repeated applications influence selenium accumulation in soil and edible plant tissues.

Carbon-based nanomaterials also show promising results, but their effects are strongly material- and dose-dependent. At 100 µg/mL, graphene oxide increased crop height by 41%, compared with 33% for CNTs and 40% for functionalized CNTs. Carbon dots at 240 mg/L reduced tobacco gray mould by 44%. These results demonstrate the potential of carbon-based materials for plant growth and disease management. However, the reported values should not be treated as a direct comparison of overall performance because the studies used different materials, crops, concentrations and experimental conditions. The possible persistence of some carbon materials, dose-dependent toxicity, production cost and difficulties in large-scale manufacturing also remain concerns.

A major issue across the literature is the gap between promising experimental results and practical field application. Many studies report positive effects under controlled conditions, but fewer studies examine long-term use across different soils, climates, crops and farming systems. There is also limited information on repeated application, accumulation, movement through soil and water, effects on beneficial microorganisms, transfer through the food chain and impacts on non-target organisms. These issues are particularly important for agricultural use because NPs may remain in the production environment after the initial treatment.

Economic feasibility and technology readiness are factors that should receive greater attention. A nanoparticle may perform well in the laboratory but have limited agricultural value if it is expensive to produce, store, transport or scale. TRL assessment helps distinguish laboratory research from field-validated and commercial technologies. Green synthesis can reduce chemical use and allow waste-derived materials, but this does not automatically make the final product inexpensive. Feedstock availability, process consistency, purification, energy requirements, formulation and quality control influence cost. Commercialization may involve manufacturers or research-led translation through institutes, spin-outs and consortia. Future studies should report production cost and scale-up requirements alongside biological effectiveness.

The literature supports green NPs as promising tools for PA, but the evidence does not support treating them as automatically safe, economical or ready for widespread field use. The key need is to move technologies from discovery and development towards field demonstration and commercial deployment through well-designed trials examining benefits and risks. Researchers should report particle size, surface properties, concentration, formulation and application method and evaluate crop performance together with environmental safety, stability, cost, scalability and manufacturing reproducibility. Regulatory requirements should also be considered. This approach would provide a stronger basis for determining which technologies can progress from laboratory research to agricultural practice and where further evidence remains necessary.
