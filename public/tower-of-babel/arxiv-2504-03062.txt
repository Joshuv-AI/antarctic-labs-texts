OPTIMAL TRANSPORT ON THE SUB-LORENTZIAN HEISENBERG GROUP
Samuël Borza, Wilhelm Klingenberg, Patrick Wood

Abstract
We investigate the synthetic metric spacetime structure of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group and we study the optimal transport problem in this space. The sub-Lorentzian version of Brenier’s theorem is established in this setting. Finally, we provide examples of optimal transport maps and derive a sub-Lorentzian Monge-Ampère equation.

1
Introduction

Sub-Lorentzian geometry is to Lorentzian geometry what sub-Riemannian geometry is to Riemannian geometry. A sub-Lorentzian space is a smooth manifold equipped with a Lorentzian metric defined at every point on a subspace of the tangent space, known as the horizontal space of admissible directions. There is an extensive body of literature and active research in sub-Riemannian geometry; see comprehensive references such as
[
Mon02
,
ABB20
]
. However, relatively little research has been conducted on sub-Lorentzian geometry. The idea of such a theory was envisaged in Strichartz’s seminal paper on sub-Riemannian geometry
[
Str86
]
, but it was really initiated through Grochowski’s work, whose first publication on the topic seems to be
[
Gro02
]
.

The Heisenberg group is the prototypical and simplest example of a geometric space with non-holonomic constraints. It provides significant insight into the theory and has been studied extensively when equipped with a sub-Riemannian metric—see again
[
ABB20
]
, as well as
[
CDPT07
]
. It is therefore natural to consider the Heisenberg group equipped with a sub-Lorentzian metric, and important contributions have been made in
[
Gro04
,
Gro06
,
SS23
]
.

In the past few decades, the study of length spaces and the analysis of metric measure spaces have been very successful in developing an abstract, synthetic, and non-smooth theory encompassing (sub)-Riemannian and (sub)-Finslerian theories, as well as their limits in the sense of Gromov-Hausdorff. Recently, a theory of Lorentzian length spaces and metric spacetimes was introduced in
[
KS18
]
with the goal of developing an analogous synthetic setting for Lorentzian geometry. While in metric geometry the central object is the distance function, it is the time-separation, i.e. the maximum (proper) time as measured by a clock along any line between two given points, that is taken as the embodiment of the fundamental structural feature of a spacetime. In addition to being interesting from a mathematical point of view, this approach is also motivated in physics by the need to handle spacetimes with very low regularity, both in general relativity and in quantum gravity.

Sub-Lorentzian geometry is also pertinent from a physical point of view, but it has not yet been studied within the non-smooth framework of Lorentzian length spaces. One of the contributions of the present work is to show (see
Section

2.3
) that the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group is indeed a Lorentzian metric spacetime in the sense of
[
KS18
]
, with regularity properties and its place within the “causal ladder” identified in
Proposition

18
. This is achieved after reviewing, in
Section

2.1
, the sub-Lorentzian structure of the Heisenberg group “from the Hamiltonian point of view”, according its the optimal control formulation of
[
SS23
]
.

An important turning point in the recent development of metric geometry was the discovery of the central role that optimal transport can play, see
[
Vil09
]
and
[
San15
]
for a detailed overview and futher references. From curvature-bound conditions to isoperimetric problems, and even interpolation inequalities, optimal transport has become a key tool in modern geometric analysis. It is therefore not surprising that the foundation for a Lorentzian version of optimal transport theory, compatible with the framework from
[
KS18
]
, was soon laid down in
[
MS23
,
CM24
,
CM22
]
.

In modern language, Monge’s original problem, which he introduced and studied in 1781, can be formulated as follows. Given
p\geq 1
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that are assumed, for simplicity, to be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and with compact support, find a map that minimises the functional

M(T)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,T(x))^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

among all the measurable maps
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that transport the measure
\mu
to
\nu
, i.e. satisfying
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
. Monge was motivated by an engineering problem where an amount of material extracted from the earth or a mine, the “déblai” modelled by
\mu
, is to be placed into a new construction, the “remblai” modelled by
\nu
, in the most efficient way. This problem with
p=1
is the one that he originally considered and roughly corresponds to minimising the total distance that each particle of material will travel. When
p=2
, it is more of an energy minimisation that is at play. Because of convexity issues, it turned out that the case
p=1
is actually much trickier than for
p>1
. The latter case was resolved by Brenier in his celebrated paper
[
Bre91
]
, and the result is now known as Brenier’s theorem.
For another clear explanation, see also
[
San15
, Theorem 1.17]
, and we refer the reader to
[
San15
, Chapter 3]
for the former case
p=1
, which we will not address in the present work. From there, Brenier’s theorem was generalised to other background spaces: in Riemannian geometry in
[
McC01
]
, in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group in
[
AR04
]
, and for large classes of sub-Riemannian manifolds in
[
FR10
]
. In any such result, the optimal transport map
T
is proven to be unique, and expressed as the geodesic exponential map of the differential of a convex function, where "convexity" is understood in a suitable sense dependent on the cost
d^{p}/p
.

We briefly explain the Lorentzian version of Monge’s optimal transport problem in order to state our result. Given
p\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
, a Lorentzian length space
X
with a time-separation function
\tau
, and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, the (forward) Lorentz-Monge problem consists in maximising the functional

M^{+}(T)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{X}\tau(x,T(x))^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

among all the measurable maps
T:X\to X
such that
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
, and such that
\tau(x,T(x))>0
for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
. A map maximising this functional is called a (forward) optimal transport map from
\mu
to
\nu
. This latter condition is necessary to ensure that transport occurs forward in time and in a chronological manner, i.e.
T(x)
lies in the future of
x
, and the journey from
x
to
T(x)
can be completed at a speed lower than that of light. The cosmological interpretation here is that we wish to transport a spacetime gas with configuration
\mu
chronologically to a future new configuration
\nu
in the most optimal way, that is to say, while maximising proper time. Analogously, we can study the Lorentz-Monge transport problem that is backward in time, i.e. maximise the function

M^{-}(T)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{X}\tau(T(x),x)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

among all the measurable maps
T:X\to X
such that
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
, and such that
\tau(T(x),x)>0
for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
.

In this paper, the Lorentzian length space is the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group, which we denote by
\mathds{H}
, and we assume that
p\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
. In the following statement, the Hamiltonian
H
is given in
Equation

6
, while the notion of a
c_{p}
-concave map will be explored in detail in
Section

3
, with the notation
c_{p}:=\tau^{p}/p
. For now, it suffices to view a
c_{p}
-concave map
\varphi:A_{1}\subseteq\mathds{H}\to\mathbb{R}
as a locally semiconcave function, which is, in particular, differentiable almost everywhere. We denote by
\mathds{H}_{\ll}^{2}
the set of pairs of points
(q,q^{\prime})\in\mathds{H}^{2}
such that
\tau(q,q^{\prime})>0
. The exponential map
\mathrm{exp}_{q}
represents the sub-Lorentzian geodesic flow, and we will discuss it in detail in
Section

2.1
.

Theorem 1

(Brenier’s theorem in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group)
.

Let
p\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{)}
,
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
with compact support,
\mu,\nu\ll\mathcal{L}^{3}
, and
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\ll}^{2}
. Then, the following holds.

(i)

There is a unique forward optimal transport map
T^{\mu\to\nu}
from
\mu
to
\nu
and it is given by

T^{\mu\to\nu}(q)=\mathrm{exp}_{q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{q}\varphi}\bigg/{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{2H(q,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{q}\varphi)}}}\right)^{\tfrac{p-2}{p-1}}}}}\right),

(1)

where
\varphi:A_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is any map that is
c_{p}
-concave relative to a pair of sets
(A_{1},A_{2})
, with
\mu(A_{1})=\nu(A_{2})=1
,
A_{1}
being open,
A_{2}
bounded,
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq A_{1}
, and
A_{1}\times A_{2}\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\ll}^{2}
.

(ii)

Conversely, if
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are non-empty subsets of
\mathds{H}
such that
A_{1}\times A_{2}\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\ll}^{2}
, if
A_{1}
is open, and if
\varphi:A_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
c_{p}
-concave map relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
such that
\varphi
is differentiable
\mu
-almost everywhere, then the map
T
given by
Equation

1
is a forward optimal transport map from
\mu
to
\nu
.

(iii)

Denoting by
T^{\nu\to\mu}
the unique backward optimal transport map from
\nu
to
\mu
, we get that

T^{\mu\to\nu}\circ T^{\nu\to\mu}=\mathrm{Id}\ \ \ \text{ $\nu$-a.e. in $\mathds{H}$, }\ \ \ \ \ T^{\nu\to\mu}\circ T^{\mu\to\nu}=\mathrm{Id}\ \ \ \text{ $\mu$-a.e. in $\mathds{H}$}.

We prove this theorem through a series of results in
Section

3.2
, removing the assumptions of absolute continuity and compactness of support whenever possible.

There are Lorentzian versions of Brenier’s theorem in the literature, specifically in Lorentzian manifolds
[
McC20
]
and in Finsler spacetimes
[
BO24
]
, and our result should be the first in the sub-Lorentzian setting. As will be made clear from the proof, a key fact used to establish the theorem is the regularity of the time-separation function in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group. This was achieved in
[
SS23
, Theorem 6]
and we recall this result in
Theorem

11
.
The elements of the argument specific to the sub-Lorentzian setting are inspired by
[
AR04
]
and
[
FR10
]
: we formulate a sub-Lorentzian Lagrange multiplier rule for timelike covectors in
Section

2.2
and use it to derive the key geometric
Lemma

15
and
Lemma

16
, which are well known in sub-Riemannian geometry. Finally, we deduce in
Section

3.3
a Monge-Ampère type equation for the optimal transport map which is a classical consequence of Brenier’s theorem, and we show some examples of optimal transport maps, including a study of the optimality of the right-translations.

We conclude this introduction with some open questions. We have not followed the approach in
[
AR04
, Section 6]
regarding the convergence of transport maps in the Lorentzian approximation of
\mathds{H}
to their sub-Lorentzian counterparts. This could be interesting and useful. The ideas presented in this paper are quite standard, but what prevents us from proving a Brenier’s theorem for general sub-Lorentzian manifolds beyond the Heisenberg group is that we do not have enough knowledge on the regularity properties of the time-separation function. In sub-Riemannian geometry, the regularity properties of the distance function and the relationship with abnormal minimisers are well understood, as discussed in
[
ABB20
, Chapter 11]
. A sub-Lorentzian version of these results is still lacking. As already mentioned, our result does not cover the case
p=1
, for which different techniques should be employed, see
[
San15
, Chapter 3]
to have an idea in the Euclidean setting. A study of the so-called timelike curvature bounds
\mathsf{TCD}
and
\mathsf{TMCP}
will appear in a subsequent work.

2
The sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group

2.1
Spacetime geometry of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group

The Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
is the connected and simply connected Lie group whose Lie algebra is graded, nilpotent, satisfying
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{2}
and such that
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is two-dimensional and generates
\mathfrak{h}
. In particular,
\mathds{H}
is three-dimensional and fixing a basis
X,Y,
and
Z=[X,Y]
of
\mathfrak{h}
induces global coordinates on
\mathds{H}
, called
exponential coordinates
, through the map
(x,y,z)\mapsto\mathrm{exp}(xX+yY+zZ)
. By the Campbell-Hausdorff formula, the law group in these coordinates writes as

(x,y,z)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)}}\right).

Using the left-translation
L_{q}:\mathds{H}\to\mathds{H}:q^{\prime}\mapsto q\cdot q^{\prime}
, we can define, with a slight abuse of notation, the left-invariant vector fields on
\mathds{H}
whose values at the identity are
X,Y
and
Z
. These will also be denoted by
X,Y
and
Z
respectively. Their expression in exponential coordinates is

X=\partial_{x}-\frac{y}{2}\partial_{z},\ Y=\partial_{y}+\frac{x}{2}\partial_{z},\ \text{ and
}\ Z=\partial_{z}.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
spanned by
X
and
Y
is left-invariant and bracket generating, that is to say,
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathcal{D}_{q})=\T_{q}(\mathds{H})
for all
q\in\mathds{H}
since the only non-zero Lie bracket is
[X,Y]=Z
.

The Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
has been studied extensively when equipped with its natural sub-Riemannian structure, i.e. with a scalar product on
\mathcal{D}
that turns
X
and
Y
into an orthonormal basis.
In this work, we propose to further the study of the Heisenberg group endowed with a
sub-Lorentzian
structure, in line with the previous works
[
SS23
,
HY12
,
Gro06
,
Gro04
]
.

Before addressing the Lorentzian optimal transport problem on this structure, we review its sub-Lorentzian geometry. The
sub-Lorentzian metric
of
\mathds{H}
is the Lorentzian metric

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathcal{D}
, i.e. a non-degenerate, smooth, symmetric metric tensor on
\mathcal{D}
that has index 1, defined by

\langle u,v\rangle_{p}:=u_{2}v_{2}-u_{1}v_{1},

for all
u=u_{1}X(q)+u_{2}Y(q)
and
v=v_{1}X(q)+v_{2}Y(q)
in
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, and all
q\in\mathds{H}
. In other words, it is the Lorentzian metric on
\mathcal{D}
satisfying
\langle X,X\rangle=-1
and
\langle Y,Y\rangle=1
. For a given
q\in\mathds{H}
, a vector
v\in\T_{q}(\mathds{H})
is
horizontal
if
v\in\mathcal{D}_{q}
. A horizontal vector
v\in\mathcal{D}_{q}
is said to be timelike if
\langle v,v\rangle_{q}<0
, null or lightlike if
\langle v,v\rangle_{q}=0
and
v\neq 0
, spacelike if
\langle v,v\rangle_{q}>0
or
v=0
, and causal (or non-spacelike) if
\langle v,v\rangle_{q}\leq 0
and
v\neq 0
.

The vector field
X
is chosen as the
time orientation
: for
q\in\mathds{H}
, a horizontal vector
v\in\mathcal{D}_{q}
is future-directed if
\langle v,X(p)\rangle_{q}<0
and past-directed if
\langle v,X(p)\rangle_{q}>0
, adopting the “mostly plus” metric sign convention.

A horizontal curve is an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
such that
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\mathcal{D}_{\gamma(t)}
for almost every
t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
, i.e. if there exists a control
u=(u_{1},u_{2}):\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that

\dot{\gamma}(t)=u_{1}(t)X(\gamma(t))+u_{2}(t)Y(\gamma(t)),\ \ \text{for a.e. }t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}.

(2)

We will denote by
P_{u}^{t_{0},t_{1}}(q_{0})
the associated flow, i.e.
P_{u}^{t_{0},t_{1}}(q_{0}):=\gamma(t_{1})
where
\gamma
solves
Equation

2
with initial condition
\gamma(t_{0})=q_{0}
.
A horizontal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
is timelike (resp. spacelike, lightlike, causal, future-directed, past-directed) if
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is a timelike (resp. spacelike, lightlike, causal, future-directed, past-directed) horizontal vector for almost every
t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
. The length of a causal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
controlled by
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined by

\mathrm{L}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{T}|\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle_{\gamma(t)}|^{1/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{u_{1}(t)^{2}-u_{2}(t)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

For points
q_{0},q\in\mathds{H}
, the set
\Omega_{q_{0}q}
denotes the set of future-directed causal curves
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
joining
q_{0}
to
q
. A timelike curve
\gamma
is parametrised by constant speed
v>0
if
\mathrm{L}(\gamma|_{\mathopen{[}s,t\mathclose{]}})=v|s-t|
for all
t,s\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
, i.e. when
u_{1}(t)^{2}-u_{2}(t)^{2}=v^{2}
for almost every
t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
. Any future-directed timelike curve can be reparametrised to have constant speed. By convention, we will also say that a lightlike curve has constant speed
v>0
if
u_{1}(t)=v
for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2
.

The time-separation function of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
is the map
\tau:\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H}\to\mathopen{[}0,\infty\mathclose{]}
defined as

\tau(q_{0},q):=\begin{cases}\ell(q_{0},q):=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{L}(\gamma)\mid\gamma\in\Omega_{q_{0}q}}}\right\}&\text{ if }\Omega_{q_{0}q}\neq\emptyset\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

(4)

The causal future (resp. the chronological future) of a point
q_{0}\in\mathds{H}
is the set
J^{+}(q_{0})
(resp.
I^{+}(q_{0})
) of points
q\in\mathds{H}
for which there exists a future-directed causal (resp. a future-directed timelike) curve joining
q_{0}
to
q
. The causal past
J^{-}(q_{0})
and chronological past
I^{-}(q_{0})
of a point
p\in\mathds{H}
are defined similarly with past-directed curves. We also set
\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}:=\{(q_{0},q)\in\mathds{H}^{2}\mid\ell(q_{0},q)\geq 0\}
and
\mathds{H}_{\ll}^{2}:=\{(q_{0},q)\in\mathds{H}^{2}\mid\ell(q_{0},q)>0)\}
.

A maximising geodesic is a future-directed causal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
joining
q_{0}
to
q
in
\mathds{H}
such that
\mathrm{L}(\gamma)=\tau(q_{0},q)
. The search for a maximising geodesic
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
joining
q_{0}\in\mathds{H}
to
q\in J^{+}(q_{0})
can be stated as the following optimal control problem:

\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma}(t)=u_{1}(t)X(\gamma(t))+u_{2}(t)Y(\gamma(t))\\
u(t)\in U:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid u_{1}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{2}}}\right\rvert}}\right\}\text{ for a.e. }t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\\
\mathrm{L}(\gamma)\to\max\\
\gamma(0)=q_{0},\ \gamma(1)=q\end{cases}.

(C)

Since the sub-Lorentzian structure of
\mathds{H}
is induced by left-invariant vector fields, the concepts defined above are invariant under left translations too. For instance, we have
\tau(q_{0},q)=\tau(L_{q^{\prime}}(q_{0}),L_{q^{\prime}}(q))
,
J^{\pm}(q_{0})=J^{\pm}(L_{q}(q_{0}))
, etc.

For
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the sub-Lorentzian (controlled) Hamiltonian is the map
\mathscr{H}:U\times\mathrm{T}^{*}(\mathbb{H})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda):=u_{1}h_{X}(\lambda)+u_{2}h_{Y}(\lambda)-\nu\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}},\ \text{ for }u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in U\text{ and }\lambda\in\mathrm{T}^{*}(\mathbb{H}),

where for a vector field
V
on
\mathds{H}
, we have denoted by
h_{V}
the function on
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
defined by
h_{V}(\lambda):=\langle\lambda,V(\pi(\lambda))\rangle
. Note that
h_{X}
,
h_{Y}
, and
h_{Z}
form a system of coordinates on each of the fibers of
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
.

For a fixed
u\in U
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathscr{H}(u,\lambda,\nu)
is smooth and the symplectic gradient, or Hamiltonian vector field,
\overrightarrow{\mathscr{H}}^{\nu}(\cdot,u):\T^{*}(\mathds{H})\to\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
is then the unique vector field on
\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
satisfying

\sigma_{\lambda}(\cdot,\overrightarrow{\mathscr{H}}^{\nu}(\lambda,u))=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{\lambda}\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(\cdot,u),\text{ for all }\lambda\in\T(\T^{*}(\mathds{H})),

where
\sigma
is the symplectic form of
\mathrm{T}^{*}(\mathbb{H})
.

We now state Pontryagin’s maximum principle below (following
[
AS04
, Chapter 12]
), a first order necessary condition for solutions to the optimal control problem
Equation

C
.

Theorem 3

(Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle)
.

Assume that the curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathbb{H}
controlled by
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},U)
is optimal for the control problem given in
Equation

C
. Then, there exists a Lipschitz curve
\lambda:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathrm{T}^{*}(\mathbb{H})
, and a number
\nu\in\{-1,0\}
such that
\pi(\lambda(t))=\gamma(t)
for all
t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
and

(i)

\dot{\lambda}(t)=\overrightarrow{\mathscr{H}}^{\nu}(u(t),\lambda(t))
;

(ii)

\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u(t),\lambda(t))=\max_{u\in\overline{U}}\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda(t))=\mathrm{const}
;

(iii)

If
\nu=0
, then
\lambda(t)\neq 0
for all
t\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
.

A Pontryagin extremal, or simply extremal, is a Lipschitz curve
\lambda:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
such that there exists
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},U)
for which (i), (ii) and (iii) of
Theorem

3
are satisfied. An extremal is said to be a normal (resp. abnormal) extremal if it satisfies those conditions with
\nu=-1
(resp. with
\nu=0
). The characterisation of the extremals of the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group was carried out in
[
SS23
]
, and we repeat here some of details for the sake of clarity.

Proposition 4
.

Given
\lambda\in\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
, if the maximum of
u\mapsto\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda)
is attained for some
u\in U
(resp.
u\in\mathrm{int}(U)
), then
\max_{u\in U}\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda)=0
(resp.
\max_{u\in\mathrm{int}(U)}\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda)=0
) and
h_{X}(\lambda)\leq-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert
(resp.
h_{X}(\lambda)<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert
). More specifically, the maximum is attained at

(i)

(u_{1},u_{2})=(0,0)
if
h_{X}(\lambda)<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert
;

(ii)

(u,\mathrm{sgn}(h_{Y}(\lambda))u)
for any
u\geq 0
if

(iii)

any

(iv)

In this case, there are unique

such that

,
h_{Y}(\lambda)=\sinh(\psi)
, and
u=(A\cosh(\psi),A\sinh(\psi))

Proof.

If the maximum of

is attained on the set
\{(u_{1},u_{2})\mid u_{1}=u_{2}\geq 0\}
, then in this case we have

\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda)=u_{1}(h_{X}(\lambda)+h_{Y}(\lambda)),

and a maximum exists if and only if
h_{X}(\lambda)\leq-h_{Y}(\lambda)
. In this case, the maximum on this set is zero and is attained at
(u_{1},u_{2})=(0,0)
if
h_{X}(\lambda)<-h_{Y}(\lambda)
and at any value
u_{1}=u_{2}\geq 0
if
h_{X}(\lambda)=-h_{Y}(\lambda)
. Similarly, if the maximum is attained on the set
\{(u_{1},u_{2})\mid u_{1}=-u_{2}\geq 0\}
, then we have

\mathscr{H}^{\nu}(u,\lambda)=u_{1}(h_{X}(\lambda)-h_{Y}(\lambda)),

and a maximum exists if and only if
h_{X}(\lambda)\leq h_{Y}(\lambda)
. In this case, the maximum on this set is zero and is attained at
(u_{1},u_{2})=(0,0)
if
h_{X}(\lambda)<h_{Y}(\lambda)
, and at any value
u_{1}=-u_{2}\geq 0
if
h_{X}(\lambda)=h_{Y}(\lambda)
. In summary, the maximum on
\partial U
exists if and only if
h_{X}(\lambda)\leq-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert
, and when that is the case, the maximum is zero and is attained at
(u_{1},u_{2})=(0,0)
if
h_{X}(\lambda)<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert
, at
(u,u)
for any
u\geq 0
if
h_{X}(\lambda)=-h_{Y}(\lambda)
, and at
(u,-u)
for any
u\geq 0
if
h_{X}(\lambda)=h_{Y}(\lambda)
.

Now, if a maximum is attained at
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathrm{int}(U)
, the first order condition implies that

h_{X}(\lambda)=\frac{\nu u_{1}}{\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}}},\text{ and }h_{Y}(\lambda)=-\frac{\nu u_{2}}{\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}}}.

If
\nu=0
, then we must have that
h_{X}(\lambda)=h_{Y}(\lambda)=0
, and the maximum on
\mathrm{int}(U)
is zero and attained for any

. If
\nu=-1
, then the maximum is also zero and writing

and
u_{2}=A\sinh(\psi)
for the unique corresponding parameters

and

yields

h_{X}(\lambda)=-\cosh(\psi),\text{ and }h_{Y}(\lambda)=\sinh(\psi),

which has a (unique) solution

if and only if
h_{X}(\lambda)^{2}-h_{Y}(\lambda)^{2}=1
and
h_{X}(\lambda)\leq-1
. Note that
h_{X}(\lambda)<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h_{Y}(\lambda)}}\right\rvert

∎

Proposition 6

\lambda(t)
onto
\mathds{H}

\begin{cases}\dot{h}_{X}=-h_{Y}h_{Z},\\

Remark 8
.

Let
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathbb{H}
be a curve parametrised by constant speed
\sqrt{2H}
such that it is the projection of a strictly normal extremal
\lambda:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\T^{*}(\mathbb{H})

(q,\lambda_{0})\in\T^{*}(\mathds{H})
such that
\gamma(t)=\mathrm{exp}_{q}(t\lambda_{0})

\langle e^{t\overrightarrow{H}}(q_{0},\lambda_{0}),V(\gamma(t))\rangle=\sqrt{2H}\ h_{V}(t),\text{ for }V=X,Y,\text{ and }Z.

This is evident when comparing
Equation

8

includes both past- and future-directed curves, and it rescales the vertical component of the Pontryagin extremals from

[

define their exponential map by restricting its domain of definition to covectors that generate arclength curves, which avoids this rescaling. In contrast, we choose to define the sub-Lorentzian exponential map on the entire cotangent space, as this allows us to use the homogeneity of the Hamiltonian
H
to simplify certain expressions.

, which we reproduce here:

\tau(e,(x,y,z))=\sqrt{x^{2}-y^{2}}\frac{\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{z}{x^{2}-y^{2}}}}\right)}{\sinh\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{z}{x^{2}-y^{2}}}}\right)}.

where
\beta:\mathopen{(}-1/4,1/4\mathclose{)}\to\mathbb{R}
is the inverse function to the diffeomorphism

\alpha(t):=\frac{\sinh(2t)-2t}{8\sinh^{2}(t)}.

In particular, it holds that

\tau((x_{0},y_{0},z),(x_{1},y_{1},z))=\sqrt{(x_{0}-x_{1})^{2}-(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}},

(16)

and that

\tau((x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1}))\leq\sqrt{(x_{0}-x_{1})^{2}-(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}},

(17)

since
0\leq x/\sinh(x)\leq 1
for all

.

2.2
The Lagrange multiplier rule

As in sub-Riemannian geometry, Pontryagin extremals can be characterised via Lagrange’s multipliers rule, which will be useful later on. We start by defining the usual end-point map.

Definition 12
.

The end-point map relative to the pair of times
(t_{0},t_{1})\in\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}^{2}
, and to the point
q\in\mathds{H}
is the map

E^{t_{0},t_{1}}_{q}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathds{H}:u\mapsto\gamma_{u}(t_{1})

where
\gamma_{u}:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
is the unique solution to the differential equation
\dot{\gamma}=u_{1}X+u_{2}Y
with initial condition
\gamma(t_{0})=q
.

The end-point map is smooth and its differential is well-known.

Proposition 13

(
[
ABB20
, Proposition 8.5]
)
.

The end-point map
E_{q}^{t_{0},t_{1}}
is smooth, for every
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, its differential
D_{u}E_{q}^{t_{0},t_{1}}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\T_{\gamma_{u}(t_{1})}(\mathds{H})
satisfies

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{u}E_{q}^{t_{0},t_{1}}(v)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(P_{u}^{t,t_{1}})_{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v_{1}(t)X+v_{2}(t)Y}}\right](\gamma_{u}(t_{1}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(18)

The sub-Lorentzian optimisation problem can reformulated as the problem of finding
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\overline{U})
that maximises the length
\mathrm{L}
among all the controls
u\in(E^{T}_{q_{0}})^{-1}(q)
. The Lagrange multiplier rule, see
[
ABB20
, Theorem 8.7]
, asserts that if
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},U)
is such a maximiser, then there exists
\lambda_{1}\in\T^{*}_{q}(\mathds{H})
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{u}E_{q_{0}}^{T}+\nu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{u}L=0
. A small computation shows that when
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},U)
describes a curve parametrised by arclength, then

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{u}\mathrm{L}(v)={}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{L}(u+sv)\Big|_{s=0}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{(u_{1}(t)+sv_{1}(t))^{2}-(u_{2}(t)+sv_{3}(t))^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big|_{s=0}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{u_{1}(v)v_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)v_{2}(t)}{\sqrt{u_{1}(t)^{2}-u_{2}(t)^{2}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{1}(t)v_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)v_{2}(t)}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

In light of this, we set

(u,v)_{\mathrm{sL}}:=\int_{0}^{T}(u_{2}(t)v_{2}(t)-u_{1}(t)v_{2}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

for all
u,v\in\mathrm{L}^{+\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The Lagrange Multiplier rule is known to be related to Pontryagin’s maximum principle. The following result is the analogue of
[

, Proposition 8.9]
for sub-Lorentzian strictly normal extremals.

In the next result, we fix

Lemma 15
.

Let

2.3
The Lorentzian metric spacetime structure

In the last part of this section, we place the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group within the broader context of non-smooth Lorentzian geometry, following the general framework outlined in
[
KS18
,
CM22
,
CM24
]
. In order to have a suitable metric topology at hand, we will consider the sub-Riemannian distance
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}
on
\mathds{H}
. The sub-Riemannian structure of the Heisenberg group is given by a Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{sR}}
on the subbundle
\mathcal{D}=\mathrm{span}\{X,Y\}
of
\T(\mathds{H})
such that the family
\{X,Y\}
forms an orthonormal basis. The sub-Riemannian length of a horizontal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\mapsto\mathds{H}
controlled by
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is given by

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{sR}}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{u_{1}(t)^{2}+u_{2}(t)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The sub-Riemannian distance is then given by

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(q_{0},q):=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{sR}}(\gamma)\mid\gamma:\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}\text{ is horizontal, }\gamma(0)=q_{0}\text{, and }\gamma(T)=q}}\right\}.

It can be shown, by the Chow–Rashevskii theorem (see
[
ABB20
, Theorem 3.31]
), that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}
is indeed a distance function and induces a metric topology equivalent to the manifold topology. The metric space
(\mathds{H},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d})
is actually a length space, as noted in
[
ABB20
, Section 3.7]
, and

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{sR}}(\gamma)=\mathrm{L}_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}(\gamma):=\sup\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\gamma(t_{i}),\gamma(t_{i+1})),

where the infinimum is taken over all
N\in\mathds{N}
and all finite partitions
(t_{i})_{i=0,\dots,N}
of
\mathopen{[}0,T\mathclose{]}
satisfying
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
. We aim to keep the use of sub-Riemannian geometry to a minimum and refer interested readers to
[
ABB20
]
for further details.

Definition 17
.

The two relations
\leq
and
\ll
on the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group
\mathds{H}
are defined as follows: for
q_{0},q\in\mathds{H}
, we set

(i)

q_{0}\leq q
if and only if there exists a future-directed causal curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}t_{0},T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
with
\gamma(t_{0})=q_{0},\gamma(T)=q
.

(ii)

q_{0}\ll q
if and only if there exists a future-directed timelike curve
\gamma:\mathopen{[}t_{0},T\mathclose{]}\to\mathds{H}
with
\gamma(t_{0})=q_{0},\gamma(T)=q
.

It is said that
q_{0}
and
q
are
causally
(resp.
timelike
) related if
q_{0}\ll q
(resp.
q_{0}\leq q
).

Given any subset
A\subseteq\mathds{H}
, we also set

A_{\leq}^{2}:=\{(q_{0},q)\in A^{2}\mid q_{0}\leq q\},\ \ A_{\ll}^{2}:=\{(q_{0},q)\in A^{2}\mid q_{0}\ll q\}.

It is clear that
\ll
is transitive and contained in
\leq
, while
\leq
is transitive and reflexive. This turns
(\mathds{H},\ll,\leq)
into a
causal space
, following
[
CM22
, Definition 1.1]
. The
causal future
and the
chronological future
of
A
as defined previously in
Section

2.1
can be written as

J^{+}(A):=\{q\in\mathds{H}\mid q_{0}\leq q\text{ for some }q_{0}\in A\},\ \ I^{+}(A):=\{q\in\mathds{H}\mid q_{0}\ll q\text{ for some }q_{0}\in A\}.

The same can be done for
J^{-}(A),I^{-}(A)
, and recall that
J^{\pm}(q_{0}):=J^{\pm}(\{q_{0}\})
and that
I^{\pm}(q_{0}):=I^{\pm}(\{q_{0}\})
. It is clear that if
q_{0}\leq q_{1}\ll q_{2}
or
q_{0}\ll q_{1}\leq q_{2}
, then
q_{0}\ll q_{2}
, for all
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathds{H}
.

The time-separation function introduced in
Definition

2
enriches the structure of the causal space
(\mathds{H},\ll,\leq)
. It is not difficult to see from the work done in the previous sections that the map
\tau:\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H}\to\mathopen{[}0,+\infty\mathclose{)}
is lower semicontinuous (actually continuous, see
Theorem

11
), and that for every
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathds{H}
, it holds

(i)

\tau(q_{0},q_{1})=0
if
q_{0}\nleq q

(ii)

\tau(q_{0},q_{1})>0
if and only if
q_{0}\ll q_{1}
, and

(iii)

if
q_{0}\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}
, then we have the
reverse triangle inequality

\tau(q_{0},q_{2})\geq\tau(q_{0},q_{1})+\tau(q_{1},q_{2}).

These properties of
\tau
imply that
\ll
is an open relation, i.e. the set
\{(q_{0},q)\in\mathds{H}^{2}\mid\tau(q_{0},q)>0\}
is open in
\mathds{H}^{2}
, and that the sets
I^{+}(A)
and
I^{-}(A)
are open for every
A\subseteq\mathds{H}
. By construction,
\tau(q,q)=0
and
\tau(q,q_{0})=0
if
\tau(q_{0},q)>0
, for all
q_{0},q\in\mathds{H}
. The structure
(\mathds{H},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d},\ll,\leq,\tau)
is then said to be a
Lorentzian pre-length space
, in the sense of
[
CM22
, Definition 1.2]
.

We start by showing that the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group enjoys some important regularity properties.

Proposition 18
.

The sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group is

(i)

strongly causal
, i.e. the so-called Alexandrov topology obtained by taking
\{I^{+}(q_{0})\cap I^{-}(q)\mid q_{0},q\in\mathds{H}\}
as a subbase coincides with the metric topology;

(ii)

causally closed
, i.e.
\mathds{H}^{2}_{\leq}
is a closed set (
\leq
is a closed relation in the product topology);

(iii)

compatible with the sub-Riemannian distance
, i.e. for every
q\in\mathds{H}
, there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of
q
and a constant
C>0
such that
\mathrm{L}_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}(\gamma)\leq C
for all causal curves
\gamma
with values in
\mathcal{U}
;

(iv)

non-totally imprisoning
, i.e. for all compact subsets
K
of
\mathds{H}
, there is a uniform bound
C>0
for the sub-Riemannian lengths of causal curves
\gamma
with values in
K
;

(v)

globally hyperbolic
, i.e. it is non-totally imprisoning and also the sets
J^{+}(q_{0})\cap J^{-}(q_{1})
are compact for all
q_{0},q_{1}\in\mathds{H}
;

(vi)

\mathcal{K}
-
globally hyperbolic
, i.e. it is non-totally imprisoning and also the sets
J^{+}(K_{1})\cap J^{-}(K_{2})
are compact for all compact subsets
K_{1},K_{2}\subseteq\mathds{H}
.

Proof.

Strongly causal.
We have already noted that
I^{+}(q_{0})
,
I^{-}(q)
, and thus
I^{+}(q_{0})\cap I^{-}(q)
, are open for all
q_{0},q\in\mathds{H}
. Actually, the metric topology is always finer than the Alexandrov topology in a Lorentzian pre-length space by
[
KS18
, Section 2.4]
. It remains to show that for all
q_{0}\in\mathds{H}
, for all
\delta>0
, there exists
A
in the Alexandrov subbase such that
q_{0}\in A
and
A\subseteq B_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}(q_{0},\delta)
. By left translation and since the metric topology coincides with the manifold topology, it is enough to consider
q_{0}=(0,0,0)
and argue that for all
\delta>0
, there is
q=(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})\in\mathds{H}
such that
I^{+}(q_{0})\cap I^{-}(q)
is contained in an Euclidean ball of radius
\delta
. We note that
I^{-}(q)=L_{q}(I^{-}(q_{0}))
and a small computation then shows that

I^{-}(q)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathds{H}\mid-(x-x_{1})^{2}+(y-y_{1})^{2}+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z-z_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}(xy_{1}-x_{1}y)}}\right|<0,x<x_{1}}}\right\}.

,

-x_{1}<y<x_{1}
, and
-x_{1}^{2}/4<z<x_{1}^{2}/2.
In other words, we have the inclusion

(22)

Causally closed.
Consider

for all

and

as

. Then, we have that
q_{0}:=(0,0,0)=p_{n}^{-1}\cdot p_{n}\leq p_{n}^{-1}\cdot q_{n}
for all

by left-invariance. Since
J^{+}(q_{0})
is closed by
Equation

If

Suppose that

We follow some of the ideas from the proofs of

. Consider the partition
\sigma_{n}:=(t_{i}^{n})_{i=1,\dots,n}

the curve defined by the concatenation of length-maximisers parametrised on

For almost every

\dot{\gamma}_{n}(t)=u_{1}^{n}(t)X(\gamma_{n}(t))+u_{2}^{n}(t)Y(\gamma(t)),

where for all

Thus, we have that

for almost every

V_{q}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f_{u}(q):=u_{1}X(q)+u_{2}Y(q)\ |\ u_{1}\geq|u_{2}|\text{ (resp. $u_{1}>|u_{2}|$)}}}\right\}.

(24)

Note that

(25)

Continuing in local coordinates, by uniform convergence, we have that for

Furthermore, since

Recalling

3
Optimal Transport in the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group

3.1
Lorentzian optimal transport in the Heisenberg group

In this section, we introduce the Lorentzian optimal transport problem in the Heisenberg group, together with details on Kantorovich duality. The results presented here are formulated for the sub-Lorentzian Heisenberg group, although they also hold in the more general setting of Lorentzian metric spaces. Further details and proofs can be found in
[
McC20
,
CM22
,
CM24
]
. We will assume that
p\in\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
, unless stated otherwise.

Given two Borel probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
, the (forward) Lorentz-Monge problem consists of maximising the functional

M^{+}(T):=\int_{\mathds{H}}c_{p}(x,T(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(M
+
)

among all the
(forward) transport maps
from
\mu
to
\nu
, i.e. the measurable map
T:\mathds{H}\to\mathds{H}
such that
T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu
and
T(q)\in J^{+}(q)
for
\mu
-almost every
q\in\mathds{H}
. A map that realises the maximum of
Equation

M+
is called an
(forward) optimal transport map
from
\mu
to
\nu
. Similarly, the backward Lorentz-Monge problem consists of maximising the functional

M^{-}(T):=\int_{\mathds{H}}c_{p}(T(x),x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

(M
-
)

among all the
(backward) transport maps
from
\nu
to
\mu
, i.e. the measurable map
T:\mathds{H}\to\mathds{H}
such that
T_{\sharp}\nu=\mu
and
T(q)\in J^{-}(q)
for
\nu
-almost every
q\in\mathds{H}
.

This optimisation problem, whose Euclidean version was originally introduced by the French Mathematician Gaspard Monge in 1781, is not easy to solve directly with the usual methods of Calculus of Variations because the constraints are not closed with respect to the weak topology. Instead, it is easier to first analyse a relaxation, which was initially studied by Kantorovich in 1942. The idea is to define the set of
transport plans
between
\mu
and
\nu
as

\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H})\mid(P_{1})_{\#}\pi=\mu,(P_{2})_{\#}\pi=\nu}}\right\},

where
P_{1},P_{2}:\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H}\to\mathds{H}
are the projections onto the respective coordinate, i.e.
P_{1}(q_{0},q_{1}):=q_{0}
and
P_{2}(q_{0},q_{1}):=q_{1}
, for all
q_{0},q_{1}\in\mathds{H}
.
We also introduce the set of causal (respectively timelike) transport plans from
\mu
to
\nu
as

\text{ and }\ \Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\mid\pi(\mathds{H}^{2}_{\leq})=1}}\right\},\ \ \Pi_{\ll}(\mu,\nu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\mid\pi(\mathds{H}^{2}_{\ll})=1}}\right\}.

The Lorentz-Kantorovich problem then consists of maximising the functional

K(\pi):=\int_{\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H}}c_{p}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)

(K)

among all
\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
. A plan
\pi
realising the maximum in
Equation

K
is called an
optimal transport plan
from
\mu
to
\nu
. Note that if
T
is an optimal transport map for
Equation

M+
(resp. for
Equation

M-
), then
\pi:=(\mathrm{Id}\times T)_{\sharp}\mu
(resp.
\pi:=(T\times\mathrm{Id})_{\sharp}\nu
) is optimal for
Equation

K
.

The advantage of this relaxation is that the constraint
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
is compact for the weak topology, and thus a proof for the existence of a maximiser follows from a standard argument. The next statement follows immediately from
[
CM22
, Proposition 1.5]
, since we have shown in
Proposition

18
that
\mathds{H}
is a globally hyperbolic Lorentzian geodesic space.

Proposition 21
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
and suppose that

(i)

\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
,

(ii)

there exist measurable functions
a,b:\mathds{H}\to\mathbb{R}
with
a\oplus b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
and
\tau(q,q^{\prime})^{p}\leq a(q)+b(q^{\prime})
for all
(q,q^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
, where
a\oplus b
denotes the function defined by
a\oplus b(q,q^{\prime})=a(q)+b(q^{\prime})
.

Then the supremum in
Equation

K
is attained and finite.

A general assumption that is reasonable to assume on
\mu
and
\nu
, other than just assuming
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\varnothing
, is that

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}.

(I)

Indeed, if that is the case, then
\mu\otimes\nu\in\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}
. Note that by the reverse triangle inequality of
\tau
, we have

\tau(q,q^{\prime})^{p}\leq(\tau(q,q^{\prime\prime})-\tau(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime}))^{p}\leq 2^{p}(\tau(q,q^{\prime\prime})^{p}+\tau(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime})^{p})

and

\tau(q,q^{\prime})^{p}\leq(\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime})-\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q))^{p}\leq 2^{p}(\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime})^{p}+\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q)^{p}).

Thus if
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
are such that there exists
q^{\prime\prime}\in\mathds{H}
with

\begin{split}\min\Big(\int_{\mathds{H}}\tau(q,q^{\prime\prime})^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(q)+&\int_{\mathds{H}}\tau(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime})^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q^{\prime}),\\
&\int_{\mathds{H}}\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(q)+\int_{\mathds{H}}\tau(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime})^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q^{\prime})\Big)<+\infty,\end{split}

(II)

then (ii) of
Proposition

21
would be satisfied. Since
\tau
is continuous, this is the case if both
\mu
and
\nu
have compact support.

The relaxation
Equation

K
also defines a Lorentzian metric on the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
.

Definition 22
.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
, the
p
-Lorentzian cost is given by
c_{p}(x,y)=\tau(x,y)^{p}/p
. The
p
-Lorentz-Wasserstein distance on
\mathds{H}
, associated with the cost
c_{p}
, is then defined as

\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathds{H}\times\mathds{H}}c_{p}(x,y)\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)}}\right)^{1/p}\quad\text{ if }\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)\neq\emptyset.

(26)

By convention, we set
\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)=-\infty
if
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)=\emptyset
.

Analogously to the well-known Kantorovich-Rubinstein-Wasserstein distances in classical optimal transport theory, the Lorentz-Wasserstein distance

satisfies the reverse triangle inequality; see
[
CM24
, Proposition 2.5]
. Specifically, for all
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
, we have

\ell_{p}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})+\ell_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq\ell_{p}(\mu_{0},\mu_{2}),

where by convention we set
\infty-\infty=-\infty
.
The space
\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
of probability measures on
\mathds{H}
can thus be endowed with a Lorentzian structure induced from
\ell_{p}
, and we call this structure the Lorentz-Wasserstein space. Details on the geometry of the Lorentz-Wasserstein space can be found in
[
CM22
,
CM24
]
. We say that
(\mu_{t})_{t\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})
is an
\ell_{p}
-geodesic if

\ell_{p}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=(t-s)\ell_{p}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),\qquad\text{ for all }t,s\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}\text{ with }s\leq t.

(27)

The standard concept of cyclical monotonicity can also be introduced in this context, following
[
CM24
, Section 2.2]
.

Definition 23
.

A set

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{p}(x_{i},y_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{p}(x_{i+1},y_{i}),

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{N},y_{N})\in\Gamma

such that
x_{N+1}=x_{1}
. A coupling

-cyclically monotone set, i.e. if there is a
c_{p}
-cyclically monotone set

\pi(\Gamma)=1
.

Note that since

is continuous by

is

-cyclically monotone if and only if

is

-cyclically monotone. Kantorovich found out that cyclical monotonicity was actually closely related to optimality. The same can be said in the Lorentzian setting, see
[

\mu
to
\nu
.

Our next goal is to introduce the concept of duality, a fundamental result in optimal transport theory. The dual problem consists in minimising the functional

D(u,v):=\int_{\mathds{H}}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathds{H}}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu

(D)

among the pair of functions
u,v:\mathds{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{+\infty}}\right\}
with
u\oplus v\geq c_{p}
on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
and
u\oplus v\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)
. When
\sup K(\pi)=\inf D(u,v)
, we say that Kantorovich duality holds for the pair of measures
(\mu,\nu)
. When
\max K(\pi)=\min D(u,v)
, we say that strong Kantorovich duality holds for the pair of measures
(\mu,\nu)
.

It is therefore natural to ask whether the infimum in
Equation

D
is attained for some functions
u
and
v
. The following definition is key to addressing this question.

Definition 25

(
c_{p}
-transform,
c_{p}
-concavity,
c_{p}
-subdifferential)
.

Let
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
be non-empty subsets of
\mathds{H}
, and
\varphi:A_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
a function.
The function

\varphi^{(c_{p})}:A_{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}:y\mapsto\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\varphi(x)+c_{p}(x,y)\mid x\in A_{1}}}\right\}

is the
c_{p}
-transform of
\varphi
(relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
)
. The function
\varphi
is
c_{p}
-concave (relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
) if there is a function
\psi:A_{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
with

\varphi(x)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\psi(y)-c_{p}(x,y)\mid y\in A_{2}}}\right\},\text{ for all }x\in A_{1}.

(28)

The
c_{p}
-subdifferential
of the function
\varphi
is the set
\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi
defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi:={}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,y)\in(A_{1}\times A_{2})\cap\mathds{H}^{2}_{\leq}\mid\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-\varphi(x)=c_{p}(x,y)}}\right\}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,y)\in(A_{1}\times A_{2})\cap\mathds{H}^{2}_{\leq}\mid\forall z\in A_{1},\ \varphi(x)+c_{p}(x,y)\leq\varphi(z)+c_{p}(x,z)}}\right\}.

We say that a pair of functions
(u,v)
are
c_{p}
-conjugate (relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
)
if
u:A_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
v:A_{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
v=u^{(c_{p})}
.

Let us prove some useful properties about
c_{p}
-concave functions.

Lemma 26
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be non-empty subsets of
\mathds{H}
. For any
\varphi:A_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)}}\right)\geq\varphi(x),\ \text{ for all }x\in A_{1},

(29)

and
Equation

29
is an equality if and only if
\varphi
is
c_{p}
-concave relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
. The
c_{p}
-transform
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
of
\varphi
is bounded from below.

Proof.

For
x\in A_{1}
, we can write that

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)}}\right)={}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in A_{1}}\Big(\varphi(x^{\prime})+c_{p}(x^{\prime},y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\Big(\varphi(x)+\tau(x,y)^{p}-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)=\varphi(x),

where the inequality is obtained by taking
x^{\prime}=x
in the supremum.

On the one hand, when
Equation

29
is an equality for all
x\in A_{1}
, the function
\varphi
is clearly
c_{p}
-concave, simply by taking
\psi=\varphi^{c_{p}}
in
Definition

25
. One the other hand, if
\varphi
is
c_{p}
-concave, then

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)}}\right)={}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in A_{1}}\Big(\varphi(x^{\prime})+c_{p}(x^{\prime},y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in A_{1}}\inf_{y^{\prime}\in A_{2}}\Big(\psi(y^{\prime})-c_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+c_{p}(x^{\prime},y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in A_{1}}\Big(\psi(y)-c_{p}(x^{\prime},y)+c_{p}(x^{\prime},y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\Big(\psi(y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)=\varphi(x),

where the inequality is obtained by taking
y^{\prime}=y
in the infimum.

Finally, suppose that
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is not bounded from below, i.e. there is a sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A_{2}
with
\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})\neq+\infty
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})=-\infty
. Then, we have that

\varphi(x)\leq{}\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)}}\right)\leq\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})-c_{p}(x,y_{n})\leq\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n}),

(30)

and thus, by taking the limit
n\to+\infty
, we obtain
\varphi(x)\leq-\infty
, which is impossible since
\varphi
is real-valued.
∎

Remark 27
.

The
c_{p}
-transform of a
c_{p}
-concave function
\varphi
cannot be identically
+\infty
. Indeed, if that were the case, we would have
\varphi(x)=+\infty
for all
x\in A_{1}
because of the equality in
Equation

29
but, by definition, a
c_{p}
-concave function is real-valued.

When the sets
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
are bounded, a
c_{p}
-concave function
\varphi
and its
c_{p}
-transform enjoy even more regularity properties.

Lemma 28
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be non-empty subsets of
\mathds{H}
and
(\varphi,\varphi^{(c_{p})})
be a pair of
c_{p}
-conjugate functions relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
.

(i)

If
A_{2}
is bounded, then
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is bounded from above, and
\varphi
is continuous.

(ii)

If
A_{1}
is bounded, then
\varphi
is bounded from above,
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is real-valued and continuous.

Proof.

Let us start by proving (i). Assume that
A_{2}
is bounded and suppose, for a contradiction, that there is a sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A_{2}
with
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})=+\infty
. For any
x\in A_{1}
, it holds that

\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})\leq\varphi(x)+c_{p}(x,y_{n}).

Up to taking a subsequence, we can assume that
y_{n}\to y\in\mathrm{cl}(A_{2})
when
n\to+\infty
. By taking the limit above and recalling that
\tau
is continuous by
Theorem

11
, we obtain
\varphi(x)=+\infty
for all
x\in A_{1}
. This is impossible because
\varphi
is real-valued.

We now prove that
\varphi
is continuous. Let
q\in A_{1}
and
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A_{1}
be a sequence converging to
q
.
Remark

27
clarified the fact that
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is bounded from below and so the infimum property in
Equation

28
implies that for all
n
sufficiently large, there exists
y_{n}\in A_{2}
with

\varphi(q_{n})\geq\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},y_{n})-\frac{1}{n}.

(31)

By compactness of
\mathrm{cl}(A_{2})
, we can extract a subsequence of
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which we can assume without loss of generality to be
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that converges to some
y\in\mathrm{cl}(A_{2})
. We then have that for all

\displaystyle\varphi(q)\leq{}

\displaystyle\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})-c_{p}(q,y_{n})=\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})-c_{p}(q,y_{n})+c_{p}(q_{n},y_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},y_{n})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})-c_{p}(q,y_{n})+c_{p}(q_{n},y_{n})+\varphi(q_{n})-\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{n})+\frac{1}{n},

the first inequality being justified by the infimum in
Equation

28
and the second by
Equation

31
. Simplifying and rearranging gives

\varphi(q_{n})\geq\varphi(q)+c_{p}(q,y_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},y_{n})-\frac{1}{n}.

(32)

Similarly, for all
n
sufficiently large, there exists
z_{n}\in A_{2}
such that

\varphi(q)\geq\varphi^{(c_{p})}(z_{n})-c_{p}(q,z_{n})-\frac{1}{n}.

(33)

By compactness of
\mathrm{cl}(A_{2})
again, we can extract a subsequence of
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which we can assume without loss of generality to be
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that converges to some

. We then have that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\varphi(q_{n})\leq{}

\displaystyle\varphi^{(c_{p})}(z_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},z_{n})=\varphi^{(c_{p})}(z_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},z_{n})+c_{p}(q,z_{n})-c_{p}(q,z_{n})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\varphi^{(c_{p})}(z_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},z_{n})+c_{p}(q,z_{n})+\varphi(q)-\varphi^{(c_{p})}(z_{n})+\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\varphi(q)+c_{p}(q,z_{n})-c_{p}(q_{n},z_{n})+\frac{1}{n},

the first inequality being justified by the infimum in
Equation

28
and the second by
Equation

33
. Since
\tau
is continuous in both variables by
Theorem

11
, we deduce that
\varphi(q_{n})
tends to
\varphi(q)
by letting

in the inequality just above and in
Equation

32
.

In the proof of (ii), the arguments showing that
\varphi
is bounded from above and
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is continuous are similar to that of (i). The map
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
is real-valued because

\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)=\sup_{x\in A_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi(x)+c_{p}(x,y)}}\right)\leq\sup_{x\in A_{1}}\varphi(x)+\sup_{x\in A_{1}}c_{p}(x,y)<+\infty,

for all

.
∎

and

(34)

We also define

\varphi_{R}(x):=\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\Big(\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big),\ \text{ for }x\in A_{1}^{R},

(35)

and

\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{R}(y):=\sup_{x\in A_{1}}\Big(\varphi(x)+c_{p}(x,y)\Big),\ \text{ for }y\in A_{2}^{R}.

(36)

Lemma 29
.

Let
R>0
,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be non-empty subsets of
\mathds{H}
, and
(\varphi,\varphi^{(c_{p})})
be a pair of
c_{p}
-conjugate functions relative to
(A_{1},A_{2})
.

(i)

If
A_{2}
is bounded, then
\varphi_{R}
is real-valued and
(\varphi_{R},\varphi^{(c_{p})})
is a pair of
c_{p}
-conjugate functions relative to
(A_{1}^{R},A_{2})
.

(ii)

If
A_{1}
is bounded, then
\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{R}
is real-valued and
(\varphi,\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{R})
is a pair of
c_{p}
-conjugate functions relative to
(A_{1},A_{2}^{R})
.

(iii)

If
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are bounded, then both
\varphi_{R}
and
\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{R}
are real-valued and
(\varphi_{R},\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{R})
is a pair of

When

A similar argument shows that

\varphi(x)=\inf_{y\in A_{2}}\Big(\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{r}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)\geq\inf_{y\in A_{2}^{R}}\Big(\varphi^{(c_{p})}_{r}(y)-c_{p}(x,y)\Big)

We end this discussion by relating
c_{p}
-cyclical monotonicity and Kantorovich duality.

Theorem 30
.

A Borel set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}
is
c_{p}
-cyclically monotone if and only if there exists a measurable map
\varphi:P_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
that is
c_{p}
-concave relative to
(P_{1}(\Gamma),P_{2}(\Gamma))
and such that
\Gamma\subseteq\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi
.

Proof.

The necessary part of the statement is the difficult one, and it is shown in the proof of
[
CM24
, Theorem 2.26]
. The converse, however, is proven straightforwardly by directly
verifying that
Definition

23
is satisfied. Indeed, if a Borel set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}
is contained in the
c_{p}
-subdifferential
\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi
of some measurable
c_{p}
-concave map
\varphi
, then it must be
c_{p}
-cyclically monotone because

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{p}(x_{i},y_{i})={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{i})-\varphi(x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y_{i})-\varphi(x_{i+1})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{p}(x_{i+1},y_{i}),

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{N},y_{N})\in\Gamma

such that
x_{N+1}=x_{1}
. The first equality holds because
\Gamma\subseteq\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi
. The second equality is verified just because the sum if finite and we can freely rearrange the terms. The inequality is valid because
\varphi^{c_{p}}(y_{i})\geq\varphi(x_{i+1})+c_{p}(x_{i+1},y_{i})
by definition of the
c_{p}
-transform.
∎

With
Theorem

30
at hand, finding a pair of functions
(u,v)
that attains the maximum in

Equation

D
is not difficult, as shown in the last part of the proof of
[
CM24
, Theorem 2.26]
which we reproduce in our context for the sake of clarity.

Theorem 31
.

Let
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H}^{2})
such that
\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)<+\infty
. If a plan
\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
is
c_{p}
-cyclically monotone, then it is an optimal transport plan from
\mu
to
\nu
and

\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)^{p}=\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(37)

where
\varphi:P_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
is any
c_{p}
-concave map relative to
(P_{1}(\Gamma),P_{2}(\Gamma))
, provided by
Theorem

30
, corresponding to a Borel
c_{p}
-cyclically monotone set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathds{H}_{\leq}^{2}
such that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
. Furthermore, strong Kantorovich duality holds for
(\mu,\nu)
.

Proof.

Note that, since
\pi(\Gamma)=1
and
\pi
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, it follows that
\mu(P_{1}(\Gamma))=1
and
\nu(P_{2}(\Gamma))=1
.
A
c_{p}
-concave map
\varphi:P_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
from
Theorem

30
verifies that
\Gamma\subseteq\partial_{c_{p}}\varphi
. Thus,
c_{p}(x,y)=\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-\varphi(x)
for all
(x,y)\in\Gamma
, and we have

\displaystyle K(\pi)={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}c_{p}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)=\int_{P_{2}(\Gamma)}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{P_{1}(\Gamma)}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)}K(\pi^{\prime})=:\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)^{p}.

The functions
\varphi
and
\varphi^{(c_{p})}
are in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mu)
and
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\nu)
respectively, from the assumption
\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)<+\infty
.
By definition of the
c_{p}
-transform, we also have
\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)-\varphi(x)\geq c_{p}(x,y)
for
(\mu\otimes\nu)
-every
(x,y)\in\mathds{H}
and integrating this inequality with respect to any
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu,\nu)
yields

\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)^{p}\leq\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Since we always have

\ell_{p}(\mu,\nu)^{p}\leq\inf D(u,v)\text{, and }\inf D(u,v)\leq\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi^{(c_{p})}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{\mathds{H}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

we deduce that strong Kantorovich duality holds.
∎

A measurable
c_{p}
-concave map satisfying
Equation

37
is called a
c_{p}
-Kantorovich potential.

induced from the metric

\langle(u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},v_{2})\rangle:=u_{2}v_{2}-u_{1}v_{1}.

It is well-known that the time-separation function
\tilde{\tau}
of the Minkowski plane is given by

\qquad\tilde{\tau}(u,v)=\sqrt{-\langle u-v,u-v\rangle},

for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\langle u,v\rangle\leq 0
and
\tilde{\tau}=0
otherwise. Note that the causal and chronological future/past of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are well-known to be given by

\tilde{J}^{\pm}(x,y):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathds{H}|-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}\leq 0,\pm x\geq 0}}\right\}

(47)

and

\tilde{I}^{\pm}(x,y):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathds{H}|-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}<0,\pm x>0}}\right\}.

(48)

One may consider the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem in this setting with the cost
\tilde{c}_{p}:=\tilde{\tau}^{p}/p
. The Minkowski analogue to the functional appearing in
Equation

M+
will be written as
\tilde{M}^{+}
.

Proposition 45
.

Let
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathds{H})

\tilde{\mu}_{0},\tilde{\mu}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\mathsf{p}_{\sharp}\mu_{0}=\tilde{\mu}_{0}
and assume that
\tilde{T}=(\tilde{T}_{1},\tilde{T}_{2}):\mathbb{R}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{2}
is a (forward) transport map from
\tilde{\mu}_{0}
to
\tilde{\mu}_{1}
optimal for the Monge problem in the Minkowski plane equipped with the Lorentzian cost
\tilde{c}_{p}
. Then, the map
T:\mathds{H}\to\mathds{H}

T(x,y,z):=(x,y,z)\cdot\mathrm{exp}_{e}(-(\tilde{T}_{1}(x,y)-x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+(\tilde{T}_{2}(x,y)-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)

is an optimal (forward) transport map from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
for the Monge problem in
\mathds{H}
with cost

Proof.

Note that the assumption that
\tilde{T}
is a (forward) transport map means that

, i.e.
(\tilde{T}_{1}(x,y)-x)^{2}-(\tilde{T}_{2}(x,y)-y)^{2}\geq 0

\tilde{T}_{1}(x,y)\geq 0

\tilde{\mu}_{0}

\displaystyle\tau((x,y,z),T(x,y,z))={}
