Evaluation of Delphi high-resolution computed tomography phenotype classification for treatment-naïve patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis
Authors: Ce Wang, Sijia Guo, Shi Shu, Feiyan Chang, Hongliang Sun, Yanhong Ren, Huaping Dai, Tongxi Liu, Sheng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe recently developed Delphi consensus provides a simplified high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) phenotype classification system for pulmonary sarcoidosis. This study aimed to investigate the clinical value of HRCT phenotype classification from the Delphi consensus study on pulmonary sarcoidosis in treatment‑naïve patients and to identify potential refinements for classifying early‑stage sarcoidosis.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 278 treatment‑naïve patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis. HRCT signs were scored, and clinical data were recorded for all patients. Patients were followed up to observe the treatment effect, recurrence, and onset of pulmonary fibrosis. Associations between HRCT findings, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), early corticosteroid response, recurrence, and fibrosis progression were analyzed. The imaging data were then classified into seven Delphi-defined HRCT phenotypes to evaluate their clinical and prognostic relevance.ResultsHRCT signs, including nodules, consolidation, and ground-glass opacity (GGO), were significantly associated with decreased PFT parameters. The presence of central consolidation and nodules was predictive of a favorable early treatment response, while the presence of coarse granular GGOs was associated with a significantly higher risk of recurrence. In terms of Delphi HRCT phenotype, there were 196 patients with the nonfibrotic phenotype (70.5%) and 28 patients with likely-to-be fibrotic phenotypes (10.1%) in our cohort. However, there were unclassifiable cases, including those with isolated lymphadenopathy (12.2%), GGO (5.4%), and pleural effusion‑dominant (1.8%) patterns. Likely-to-be fibrotic phenotypes were associated with the greatest impairment of pulmonary function and the highest risk of fibrosis. The consolidation-dominant type was also associated with an increased risk of fibrosis. According to Cox proportional hazards regression, the isolated lymphadenopathy type was associated with a lower risk of recurrence.ConclusionsThe Delphi HRCT phenotype classification is clinically useful but may not be sufficient to fully characterize early-stage pulmonary sarcoidosis. The isolated lymphadenopathy and GGO-dominant types should be considered in the future framework due to their distinct functional and prognostic relevance.Keywords: Sarcoidosis, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), imaging phenotypes, pulmonary fibrosis

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem granulomatous disorder, with pulmonary involvement occurring in approximately 90% of patients who have this condition (1,2). Pulmonary involvement may include the airways, lung parenchyma, pleura, and mediastinal lymph nodes, with certain imaging patterns correlating with clinical outcome (3,4). Lymphadenopathy and pulmonary parenchymal involvement are characteristic radiologic features of sarcoidosis. However, the heterogeneity of morphologic manifestations on imaging poses significant challenges to the accurate diagnosis of atypical cases. More than 50% of patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis require therapeutic intervention, and a delayed diagnosis may incur unnecessary costs. In addition, approximately 10–30% of these patients progress to chronic, nonremitting, and severe forms of the disease, including progressive pulmonary fibrosis and recurrent episodes, leading to respiratory failure and mortality (5,6). The underlying mechanisms and the factors responsible for the unfavorable outcomes among these patients remain unclear.

The chest X-ray staging system proposed by Scadding in 1958 provides some prognostic value but does not delineate phenotypic subgroups or facilitate early diagnosis (7). Crucially, it applies only to confirmed cases and lacks diagnostic precision. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) has demonstrably superior diagnostic accuracy compared to chest radiography and provides valuable insights into disease activity and prognosis (8). Although several studies have introduced HRCT-based scoring systems, the complexity and limited clinical utility of semiquantitative methods have restricted their adoption in practice (9,10). In 2024, an expert consensus for the simplified classification of pulmonary sarcoidosis phenotypes was published in The Lancet (11). This system distinguishes nonfibrotic from likely-to-be-fibrotic disease and classifies imaging manifestations into seven distinct phenotypes. Evidence from an independent study demonstrated a relatively high reproducibility of this classification system, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.69 (12). However, no study has evaluated the utility of this classification in relation to pulmonary function impairment or its association with long-term outcomes.

In our study, we assessed the clinical applicability of the Delphi classification for pulmonary sarcoidosis, especially in treatment‑naïve patients. We further examined the associations between Delphi-defined phenotypes, disease course, and therapeutic outcomes. By integrating HRCT patterns with clinical and functional data, we sought to determine the classification’s value in prognostic stratification and imaging interpretation and thus to develop individualized treatment strategies for patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0866/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective single-center study included 278 consecutive treatment-naïve patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis who were admitted to China-Japan Friendship Hospital, National Center for Respiratory Medicine, a tertiary referral center, between June 2016 and June 2025 for diagnostic evaluation and baseline clinical assessment. The patients met the current clinical and histopathological diagnostic criteria for pulmonary sarcoidosis. The inclusion criteria included the following: (I) endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration or transbronchial lung biopsy indicating nonnecrotizing granulomatous inflammation but no evidence of tuberculosis or other granulomatous diseases; (II) laboratory tests excluding active tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria; (III) availability of HRCT imaging at the time of hospitalization; and (IV) definitive diagnosis of sarcoidosis from a multidisciplinary team. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) previous corticosteroid therapy or other treatments for sarcoidosis before initial diagnosis; (II) coexisting infectious diseases, severe systemic diseases, or cancer; and (III) poor-quality imaging with significant motion artifacts. All patients who satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period were included. No formal sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective observational design. Demographic and clinical data were collected from medical records. Patients were followed up to assess treatment response, recurrence, and fibrosis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (No. 2021-125-K83; date of approval: August 31, 2021). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. A flowchart of the study is presented in Figure 1.

Analysis and classification of imaging features

Baseline HRCT scans were acquired at the time of initial diagnosis and before initiation of any therapy. To reduce observer bias, all images were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists, one with more than 5 years and the other with more than 15 years of experience in thoracic imaging. Both reviewers were blinded to all clinical information. Disagreements were resolved via discussion or adjudication by a senior thoracic radiologist. Imaging features were assessed according to a predefined scoring system (Table 1 and Figure 2), adapted from the framework proposed by van den Heuvel et al. (13). Central consolidation was defined as a hilar-centered area of consolidation, typically exhibiting an air bronchogram. Peripheral consolidation was defined as consolidation abutting the pleura, with a broad base directly continuous with the pleural surface. The extent of parenchymal involvement was graded separately for each pulmonary lobe as follows: 0, absent; 1, 1–25%; 2, 26–50%; 3, 51–75%; and 4, 76–100%. Micronodular (<3 mm) burden in terms of lobar involvement was categorized according to the following scheme: 0, minimal (0–2 micronodules); 1, mild (3–10 scattered micronodules); 2, moderate (11–50 micronodules); and 3, severe (>50 micronodules with a tendency to coalesce). The lingula was regarded as a separate lobe; therefore, the total lobar score for both lungs ranged from 0 to 24. Nodules larger than 3 mm were quantified via artificial intelligence (AI)-based pulmonary nodule detection software (uAI; United Imaging Intelligence, Shanghai, China). All AI-detected nodules were reviewed by the radiologists, and false-positive detections were excluded.

The CT acquisition parameters were as follows: a tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation, detector collimation with an acquired slice thickness of 0.5 mm or 0.625 mm, images reconstructed with a slice thickness/increment of 1.0 mm/1.0 mm or 1.25 mm/1.25 mm, and a matrix size of 512×512. Iterative reconstruction was applied with a high-spatial-frequency lung kernel (FC86 or “Lung”). The field of view was automatically adjusted according to each patient’s body size.

Clinical assessment and outcomes of patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis

Comprehensive laboratory evaluations included measurement of serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (sACE) levels. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) assessed forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), the FEV1/FVC ratio, and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), all of which are expressed as percentages of predicted values. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected, and lymphocyte counts were conventionally analyzed.

For patients with clinical indications for corticosteroid therapy, treatment was generally initiated shortly after diagnostic confirmation and completion of baseline clinical assessment during the initial management period. Early treatment response was assessed using HRCT performed 1 month after corticosteroid initiation. According to the HRCT signs, treatment effects were classified into improved (>30% radiographic resolution) and persistent disease (radiographic resolution <30%, lack of regression of existing lesions, or emergence of new lesions). Patients were followed up at regular intervals according to standard clinical protocols. Patients were monitored for a minimum of 6 months to assess recurrence, which was defined as the development of new lesions or enlargement of pre-existing lesions on follow-up HRCT. To determine which baseline imaging features are more likely to progress to fibrosis, we evaluated new-onset fibrosis on serial CT scans in patients with a minimum follow-up of 1 year. New-onset fibrosis was defined as the appearance of traction bronchiectasis or architectural distortion, either focal or extensive.

Validation of Delphi consensus HRCT phenotypes for pulmonary sarcoidosis

According to the Delphi consensus, we allocated the patients’ HRCT imaging into seven distinct phenotypes (Figure 3). The seven phenotypes included nonfibrotic and likely-to-be-fibrotic phenotypes. The nonfibrotic subtypes included: (I) multiple peribronchovascular, perifissural, or subpleural micronodules (Type 1); (II) multiple larger peribronchovascular nodules (Type 2); (III) scattered larger nodules (Type 3); and (IV) consolidation as the predominant or sole abnormality (Type 4). The likely-to-be-fibrotic subtypes included: (I) bronchocentric reticulation with or without dense parenchymal opacification and without cavitation (Type 5); (II) bronchocentric reticulation and dense parenchymal opacification with cavitation (Type 6); and (III) large bronchocentric masses (Type 7).

Statistical analysis

HRCT signs were systematically analyzed, and interobserver agreement was assessed via the ICC. Multivariate linear regression was applied to examine correlations between HRCT findings and PFT results. Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify imaging predictors of new-onset fibrosis, recurrence, and treatment response.

After patients were allocated into subtype groups, intergroup differences in laboratory indices and pulmonary function parameters were assessed. Categorical variables were compared via the chi‑squared or Fisher exact test, while continuous variables were compared via one‑way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Treatment response was assessed via logistic regression, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze recurrence and new‑onset fibrosis, with adjustments made for clinical covariates. Time-to-event outcomes were calculated from the date of diagnosis to the occurrence of recurrence or new-onset fibrosis, with the last available follow-up defined as the censoring point. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with available data, and denominators are reported for variables with missing data. No imputation was performed.

Results

A total of 278 patients were included in the study (85 males and 193 females; mean age 50.0 years; age range 24–79 years). Among these patients, 57 (20.5%) had a smoking history, and 42 (15.1%) reported environmental exposure. PFT data were available for 242 patients, with 114 (47.1%) and 16 (6.6%) of these patients exhibiting impaired DLCO (<80% predicted) and obstructive defects (FEV1/FVC <70% postbronchodilator), respectively. Elevated sACE levels were found in 148 of 249 (59.4%) patients. Among patients with data from BALF analysis (n=205), 111 (54.1%) had a CD4+/CD8+ ratio >3.5.

Corticosteroid therapy was applied in 200 of 278 patients (71.9%). Early treatment response, assessed using HRCT performed 1 month after corticosteroid initiation, was evaluated in 167 patients, of whom 130 (77.8%) achieved improvement. During follow-up, recurrence occurred in 45 of 163 patients (27.6%), with a median time to event of 23.1 months (range, 4.7–73.3 months). Signs of new-onset fibrosis were observed in 75 of 159 patients (47.2%), with a median time to event of 22.2 months (range, 1.26–90.9 months). HRCT findings

Mediastinal lymphadenopathy was the most common abnormality, appearing in 259 of 278 (93.2%) patients. Micronodules were observed in 209 patients (75.2%) and nodules in 143 (51.4%). Peripheral and central consolidations were present in 134 (48.2%) and 95 patients (34.2%), respectively. Ground-glass opacity (GGO) was uncommon (n=36, 12.9%) and included coarse granular and diffuse fine patterns. Additional findings included bronchovascular bundle thickening (20.9%), pleural effusion (10.4%), mosaic sign (6.8%), and cavitation (3.6%). ICCs ranged from 0.48 to 0.95, indicating moderate-to-excellent inter-observer agreement. The detailed quantitative results are provided in Table 3.

Associations of HRCT findings with pulmonary function and clinical outcomes

Scores of micronodules, central consolidation, peripheral consolidation, and pleural effusion independently predicted a reduced DLCO (P<0.05). In addition, the presence of cavities and GGOs was a strong negative predictor of DLCO (P<0.001). For FEV1, significant negative predictors included central consolidation (P<0.01), peripheral consolidation (P<0.01), and cavities (P<0.05). A decline in FVC was associated with nodules (P<0.05), central consolidation (P<0.05), peripheral consolidation (P<0.001), and cavities (P<0.01). HRCT phenotypes of sarcoidosis according to the Delphi consensus

The cohort of 278 patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis was classified by two independent reviewers into seven imaging subtypes according to the Delphi consensus. In cases of disagreement, the final classification was adjudicated by a senior thoracic radiologist with more than 20 years of experience. Among untreated patients, the most common phenotype was Type 1 (n=94, 33.8%), followed by Type 4 (n=46, 16.5%), and Type 3 (n=45, 16.2%). The least frequent phenotype was Type 6 (n=2, 0.7%). Among patients in the “unable to classify” category (n=54, 19.4%), 34 (12.2%) exhibited isolated lymph node enlargement without pulmonary parenchymal involvement. Moreover, 5 (1.8%) patients had lymphadenopathy with concurrent pleural effusion, and 15 (5.4%) presented with lymphadenopathy accompanied by GGOs. Utility of the HRCT subtypes in clinical practice

Due to the small number of cases in each likely-to-be-fibrotic phenotype, Types 5, 6, and 7 were merged into a single likely-to-be-fibrotic group for statistical analysis. In addition, cases that could not be classified according to the Delphi system were also included in the clinical analyses.

Pulmonary function indices demonstrated significant variation across the imaging subtypes of pulmonary sarcoidosis. The likely-to-be-fibrotic subtypes exhibited the greatest impairment in FVC, FEV1, and DLCO compared with the other phenotypes (P<0.05). The GGO-dominant type also demonstrated reduced DLCO (P<0.05). In contrast, the isolated lymphadenopathy type and Type 1 exhibited preserved lung function. The FEV1/FVC ratio did not differ significantly among the HRCT phenotypes (P=0.204). The likely-to-be-fibrotic type had the highest level of sACE, and the sACE level of Type 1 was higher than that of Type 3. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical utility and prognostic value of the recently established Delphi consensus classification for pulmonary sarcoidosis in our cohort. Interstitial nodule burden and parenchymal consolidation burden were both found to correlate with restrictive ventilation and impaired diffusive pulmonary function and are also risk factors for the development of pulmonary fibrosis. Patients that were defined as having the likely-to-be-fibrotic subtypes accounted for only a small proportion of the cohort, but showed the highest risk of new-onset fibrosis. Meanwhile, Type 4 was also associated with a significantly increased risk of fibrosis. In addition, the GGO-dominant pattern, which is not defined as a distinctive type in the Delphi consensus classification, was significantly associated with a reduced diffusive capacity and a higher risk of recurrence.

Regression analysis indicated that micronodule burden was associated with impaired DLCO. This finding can be explained by the distribution of granulomas along bronchovascular bundles and within the interstitium, where diffuse involvement may restrict gas exchange and reduce DLCO (6,7,14). Coarse GGOs were also associated with a decline in DLCO, possibly reflecting fine granulomatous accumulation and subtle fibrosis (15). Meanwhile, central and peripheral parenchymal consolidations were associated with a lower FVC and FEV1, likely reflecting airway involvement and consequent ventilatory impairment (4,7,16). Peripheral consolidations were also associated with reduced DLCO and obstructive ventilatory defects, possibly due to the compression of the peripheral small airways by granulomatous infiltration (17). Cavitary lesions were strongly associated with pulmonary function impairment, likely resulting from granulomatous airway narrowing and a ball-valve mechanism that led to air trapping and parenchymal destruction (18). It was further found that pleural effusion was also associated with impaired DLCO. This association may be attributable to concomitant parenchymal involvement and the reduced effective lung volume caused by pleural effusion.

In our study, early treatment response was assessed at 1 month. This time point was chosen because systemic prednisolone is typically initiated at moderate doses for approximately 4 weeks, after which tapering is performed according to disease response (19). Since there are no standardized radiological criteria for evaluating treatment response in patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis, we adapted the principles of the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) to objectively define radiologic improvement in our cohort (20). With respect to early treatment response, central consolidations and nodules were associated with more favorable outcomes following oral glucocorticoid therapy. Murdoch et al. reported that nodular lesions are the most likely type to regress after treatment (21), and other studies have confirmed that consolidations may also completely resolve following therapy (7). Nonetheless, recurrence occurred in a proportion of patients, although the reasons for this remain unclear. Extensive parenchymal involvement, particularly coarse granular GGO, was linked to an increased risk of recurrence. Moreover, progression to fibrosis was independently associated with higher scoring for micronodules, central consolidations, and peripheral consolidations. In a previous study of 74 patients with sarcoidosis, patients with nodules and consolidations on HRCT had elevated serum cancer antigen 15-3 (CA15-3) levels, which may be a biomarker for progressive fibrosis (22,23).

In our cohort of treatment‑naïve patients, the likely-to-be-fibrotic subtype accounted for only 10.1% of cases. As expected, patients with this subtype exhibited ventilation impairment and reduced diffusive capacity. These patients also had a high risk of fibrosis progression. Our results support the clinical and prognostic relevance of the Delphi classification. However, patients with the likely-to-be-fibrotic subtype accounted for a small portion of our cohort, and this may reflect a late stage of sarcoidosis. Nonfibrotic patterns predominated at the first visit but had variable clinical relevance. Interestingly, while patients with Type 1 had relatively preserved FVC and FEV1, Type 4 within the nonfibrotic group was associated with pulmonary function decline. These findings suggest that pulmonary function, particularly diffusive capacity, deteriorates as radiologic severity increases. Furthermore, sACE levels in patients with Type 1 disease were significantly elevated compared to those in patients with Type 3, which is likely due to the greater severity of active granulomatous inflammation and a higher total granulomatous burden during the earlier stages of the disease (24,25). In terms of treatment response, patients with a predominant scattered nodular pattern exhibited relatively good outcomes with corticosteroid therapy, consistent with earlier reports (21). However, no significant differences were observed for treatment response between the other phenotypes, suggesting that the Delphi classification may have limited predictive value for short-term clinical outcomes. Overall, the Delphi classification demonstrated good model fit for the progression of fibrosis. This highlights the potential value of the Delphi system in identifying patients at increased risk for fibrosis and supports its prognostic relevance for longitudinal monitoring. Notably, although Type 4 is categorized as a nonfibrotic phenotype in the Delphi system, our findings suggest it may be associated with a high risk of fibrosis, warranting closer monitoring. A substantial proportion of patients (19.4%) could not be classified within the existing Delphi framework, yet many of these cases exhibited imaging features with clear prognostic relevance. These included isolated lymphadenopathy without parenchymal involvement, corresponding to Scadding Stage I. In our cohort, this phenotype was associated with a lower risk of recurrence. Another subset presented with diffuse or patchy GGO as the predominant abnormality. Although the 70% threshold for consensus classification in the Delphi study was not reached for GGOs, agreement was 69% after two survey rounds, suggesting substantial reproducibility. In our cohort, the GGO-dominant type correlated with significantly reduced DLCO, supporting its consideration as a clinically relevant imaging phenotype. Additionally, a small number of patients presented with mediastinal lymphadenopathy accompanied by pleural effusion, a pattern not included in the current consensus categories. However, previous studies suggest that pleural effusion may indicate pleural involvement and adverse outcomes (3,17). Given their clinical impact, these unclassified imaging patterns warrant consideration for inclusion in future refinements of phenotypic schemes.

Limitations

This study involved several limitations that should be noted. First, as we employed a single-center design with a relatively small sample size, the risk of errors in subgroup comparisons was high. Second, the retrospective nature of the study resulted in variations in follow-up intervals, potentially hampering the accurate assessment of clinical endpoints. Third, because all patients were recruited from an inpatient cohort at a tertiary referral center, there was potential selection bias, and patients with milder disease managed exclusively in outpatient settings may be underrepresented. Finally, bronchoscopic mucosal findings were not systematically incorporated into the phenotyping analysis, although such findings may reflect airway involvement not fully captured by HRCT. The exclusion of these factors may undermine the reliability of the conclusions. Despite these limitations, our cohort was derived from the National Center for Respiratory Medicine in China and, as a highly enriched sample, may represent a broad regional population. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized imaging acquisition and longitudinal follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and refine the Delphi classification. Such an expanded framework would transform the Delphi consensus from a valuable classification system into a more robust clinical tool capable of guiding individualized risk stratification and management for all patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis.

Conclusions

This study validates the clinical utility of the Delphi HRCT phenotypic classification for pulmonary sarcoidosis and proposes a broader framework for future precision subtyping.

The recognition of these imaging phenotypes is critical for enhancing diagnostic precision, enabling individualized prognostication, and guiding targeted follow-up strategies. Future multicenter prospective studies should be conducted to validate and refine the classification system, ensuring broader applicability across a greater diversity patient populations, especially for patients with early-stage pulmonary sarcoidosis.
