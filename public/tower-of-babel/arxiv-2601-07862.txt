1.
Introduction.

For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, we can recursively define
a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},n\geq 1
using the following rule

r_{0}=\xi\,\ a_{0}=\lfloor\xi\rfloor,\ r_{k+1}=\frac{1}{r_{k}-a_{k}},\ a_{k}=\lfloor r_{k}\rfloor.

For rational
\xi
this process eventually terminates, and the following expression is called a continued fraction for
\xi

\xi=\quad a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}}}=\left[a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right].

For an irrational real number
\xi
we write

\xi=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\ldots}}}=\left[a_{0},a_{1},\ldots\right].

The integers
a_{i}
are called the coefficients of the continued fraction, and the convergents to
\xi
i.e. the truncations

\frac{h_{n}}{k_{n}}=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}}}=[a_{0},\cdots,a_{n}],

may be computed using the recursions

h_{-2}=0,k_{-2}=1,h_{-1}=1,k_{-1}=0

h_{n}=a_{n}h_{n-1}+h_{n-2},

k_{n}=a_{n}k_{n-1}+k_{n-2},

It is a classical fact that
\frac{h_{n}}{k_{n}}\to\xi
as
n\to\infty
which justifies the above equality sign between
\xi
and the infinite continued fraction. If the coefficients of the continued fraction are such that for all
n>n_{0}
,
a_{n+N}=a_{n}
, then we say that the continued fraction of
\xi
is eventually periodic, denoted by

\xi=[a_{0},\cdots,a_{n_{0}},\overline{a_{n_{0}+1},\cdots,a_{n_{0}+N}}].

This type of continued fractions is characterized by the following theorem

Theorem 1

(Lagrange)
.

A real number
\xi
has an eventually periodic continued fraction if and only if
\xi
is the root of a quadratic polynomial with rational coefficients.

Such
\xi
is called a quadratic irrationality. If
a_{n+N}=a_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
, then we will say that the continued fraction is purely periodic, that is,
\xi=[\overline{a_{0},\cdots a_{N-1}}]
. It follows from Theorem
1
that
\xi=\frac{a+b\sqrt{c}}{d}
for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
, so we define the conjugate of
\xi
by
\bar{\xi}=\frac{a-b\sqrt{c}}{d}
. Using conjugates simplifies the characterization of the set of purely periodic continued fractions.

Theorem 2

(Galois)
.

Quadratic irrationality
\xi
is purely periodic if and only if
\xi>1
and
-1<\bar{\xi}<0
.

In
[
12
,
13
]
, Kalinin and Shkolnikov provide a generalization of Archimedes’ exhaustion method (motivated by the study of sandpiles in convex domains), which, when applied to the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,\xi)
yields the following identity for an irrational number
\xi
:

(1)

\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}(h_{n}-\xi k_{n})^{2}=\xi.

The terms
h_{n}-\xi k_{n}
in (
1
) are known in the literature as the errors in the approximation of the continued fraction of
\xi
.

The study of error terms arising from continued fractions has a long history,
motivated by the remarkable quality of Diophantine approximations provided by
convergents. Given a real number
\xi
with convergents
h_{n}/k_{n}
, the error

\varepsilon_{n}=h_{n}-\xi k_{n}
measures the local quality of the approximation and
often displays a rich algebraic structure when
\xi
possesses additional
arithmetic properties, such as periodicity or algebraicity.

The first systematic analysis of error–sum functions appear in

[
15
]
,
where the series

\sum_{n\geq 0}|k_{n}\xi-h_{n}|

was investigated for general
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
Subsequent developments focused particularly on the case when
\xi
is a
quadratic irrational. In this setting, the continued fraction expansion is
eventually periodic. Explicit evaluations of error sums were obtained
in
[
8
]
, where closed forms are
derived for
\sqrt{d}
and expressed in terms of Lucas and Pell numbers for
\sqrt{5}
and
\sqrt{2}
.
Later, analytic refinements and weighted variants were developed in
[
7
]

where generating functions
of the form

\sum_{n\geq 0}\varepsilon_{n}x^{n}

were studied and shown to admit algebraic structure for a quadratic irrationality
\xi
. An interesting identity

\sum_{n\geq 0}|k_{n}e-h_{n}|=e\left(-1+10\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)!(2n^{2}+7n+3)}\right)

for
e
were obtained in
[
3
]
.
Generalizations with “split denominators” and more flexible summation
structures were introduced in
[
4
]
,
offering a broader framework in which convergent–based expansions arise
naturally and reveal new identities.

More recent work has also explored fractal behavior and coprime–restricted
summation in error functions, most notably
[
2
]
,
showing that such expressions can encode geometric and measure–theoretic
information when
\xi
varies.
These results illustrate the diversity and continued activity of research in
this area.

In this work, we introduce and analyze the weighted error series

f_{\xi}(s)=\sum_{n\geq-1}a_{n+1}\,|h_{n}-\xi k_{n}|^{s},\qquad s\geq 1,

for
\xi=[\overline{a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{N-1}}]
purely periodic.
We show that the error sequence splits into
N
geometric subsequences

\varepsilon_{mN+r}=\varepsilon_{r}\,\rho^{m},\qquad 0\leq r<N,

a simple and nice result which we could not find in the literature. This leads to explicit closed formulae for
f_{\xi}(s)
in terms of the
initial segment and the ratio
\rho
.
In particular, for integer
s\geq 1
, each subseries admits a simple rational
description in
\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
,
thereby providing a unified generalization of earlier identities for
s=1
.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we establish the geometric decomposition of the
error terms for purely periodic quadratic irrationals and derive explicit closed
formulae for the weighted sums
f_{\xi}(s)
. We present
three independent proofs of this decomposition: via matrix actions on convergent
pairs, via algebraic conjugation in
\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
, and via complete quotients of the
continued fraction.

In Section
3
we interpret the common ratio in terms
of algebraic units and relate it to the fundamental unit of the underlying
quadratic field.
Finally, in
Section
4
we extend the argument to
generalized Euler–type continued fractions with numerators
(b_{n})
, leading to
similar identities for error sums. Using these error sums we obtain
formulae for
\pi
and
\ln 2
.

2.
The main theorem and its three proofs

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\xi=[\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{N-1}}]
is a purely periodic quadratic irrationality. Then the sequence of approximation errors
\varepsilon_{n}=h_{n}-\xi k_{n}
for
n\geq-1
can be decomposed into
N
geometric subsequences. Namely, there exists a real number
\rho\in\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
such that for any residue class
r\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and any integer
m\geq 0
we have

\varepsilon_{mN+r}=\varepsilon_{r}\cdot\rho^{m}.

We generalize identity (
1
) by defining the series for
s\geq 1

(2)

f_{\xi}(s)=\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}|h_{n}-\xi k_{n}|^{s}.

Definition 1
.

Let
\xi
be purely periodic with period
N
. For each residue class
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
, we define the sub-series

\beta_{k}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq-1\\
j\equiv k\pmod{N}\end{subarray}}|h_{j}-\xi k_{j}|^{s}.

Using this definition, we can rearrange (
2
) as

f_{\xi}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}a_{i+1}\beta_{i}(s).

Theorem 4
.

Let
\xi
be purely periodic and let
s\geq 1
be an integer.
For each
0\leq m\leq N-1
, the series
\beta_{m}(s)
is geometric with
common ratio
|\rho|^{s}
. Explicitly,

\beta_{m}(s)=\frac{|h_{m}-\xi k_{m}|^{s}}{1-|\rho|^{s}},\beta_{N-1}(s)=\frac{|h_{-1}-\xi k_{-1}|^{s}}{1-|\rho|^{s}}.

Corollary 1
.

For every integer
s\geq 1
we have
f_{\xi}(s)\in\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
.

In this article, we will give multiple proofs of Theorem
3
using linear algebra and classical number theory.

The three proofs below illustrate complementary viewpoints:
matrix dynamics, quadratic conjugation, and complete quotients.
Each framework reveals a bit different mechanism behind the geometric decay of errors.

2.1.
First proof: matrix approach

We associate the purely periodic continued fraction

\xi=[\overline{a_{0},\dots,a_{N-1}}]

with the period matrix

M_{N}=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&1\\[2.0pt]
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\varepsilon_{n}=h_{n}-\xi k_{n}
. From the recurrence relations for
h_{n}
and
k_{n}
, the error terms satisfy the linear relation

\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{n}\\
\varepsilon_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{n-1}\\
\varepsilon_{n-2}\end{pmatrix}.

Iterating this over the period
N
implies that for any
n\geq 0
:

\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{n+N}\\
\varepsilon_{n+N-1}\end{pmatrix}=M_{N}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{n}\\
\varepsilon_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
M_{N}
are determined by its action on the basis vector
(\xi,1)^{T}
. Since
\xi
is the fixed point of the transformation
x\mapsto\frac{Ax+B}{Cx+D}
, we have:

M_{N}\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A\xi+B\\
C\xi+D\end{pmatrix}=(C\xi+D)\begin{pmatrix}\frac{A\xi+B}{C\xi+D}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=(C\xi+D)\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
\lambda_{1}=C\xi+D
is an eigenvalue with eigenvector
v_{1}=(\xi,1)^{T}
. Since
\xi
is purely periodic, we have
\xi>1
and
a_{n}\geq 1
. Furthermore, the matrix entries satisfy
C=k_{N-1}\geq 1
and
D=k_{N-2}\geq 0
. It follows that
\lambda_{1}=C\xi+D>1
.

Since
\det(M_{N})=(-1)^{N}
, the second eigenvalue is

\lambda_{2}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{\lambda_{1}},

which satisfies
|\lambda_{2}|<1
.

The error vector
V_{k}=(\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon_{k-1})^{T}
can be written as a linear combination of the eigenvectors of
M_{N}
. Since
\varepsilon_{nN+k}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the component corresponding to the expanding eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
must vanish. Therefore,
V_{k}
lies in the eigenspace of
\lambda_{2}
, and the sequence follows the geometric progression:

\varepsilon_{nN+k}=\varepsilon_{k}\lambda_{2}^{n}.

Thus,
\rho=\lambda_{2}
.

2.2.
Second Proof. Without Matrices.

Recall that since
\xi
is purely periodic with period
N
, we have the identity
[
14
]
:

\xi=\frac{h_{N-1}\xi+h_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}}.

This is equivalent to the quadratic equation

k_{N-1}\xi^{2}+(k_{N-2}-h_{N-1})\xi-h_{N-2}=0.

Using Vieta’s formulas, the product and the sum of the roots
\xi
and its conjugate
\xi^{\prime}
are:

\xi\bar{\xi}=\frac{-h_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}}\,\ \xi+\bar{\xi}=\frac{h_{N-1}-k_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}}.

Lemma 1
.

The quantity
u=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}
and its conjugate
\bar{u}
satisfy
u\bar{u}=(-1)^{N}
. Furthermore,
|u|>1
and
|\bar{u}|<1
.

Proof.

First, we establish the bound for
u
. Since
\xi
is purely periodic, we have
\xi>1
. The period
N\geq 1
implies
k_{N-1}\geq 1
and
k_{N-2}\geq 0
. Then,
u=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}>1
, that is,
|u|>1
. We take the product
u\bar{u}

u\bar{u}=(k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2})(k_{N-1}\bar{\xi}+k_{N-2})=k_{N-1}^{2}(\xi\bar{\xi})+k_{N-1}k_{N-2}(\xi+\bar{\xi})+k_{N-2}^{2}.

Substituting the Vieta relations, the term
k_{N-2}^{2}
cancels, leaving:

u\bar{u}=k_{N-1}(-h_{N-2})+k_{N-2}(h_{N-1})=h_{N-1}k_{N-2}-h_{N-2}k_{N-1}.

By the determinant identity for convergents, this equals
(-1)^{N}
. Finally, since
|u|>1
and
|u\bar{u}|=1
, it follows immediately that
|\bar{u}|<1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

The general continued fraction identity is:

[a_{0},\dots,a_{N-1},x]=\frac{h_{N-1}x+h_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}x+k_{N-2}}.

Substituting
x=h_{n}/k_{n}
, we obtain:

\frac{h_{n+N}}{k_{n+N}}=\frac{h_{N-1}h_{n}+h_{N-2}k_{n}}{k_{N-1}h_{n}+k_{N-2}k_{n}}.

Multiplying both sides by the denominator and regrouping terms gives

(3)

h_{n+N}-\xi k_{n+N}=(h_{N-1}-\xi k_{N-1})h_{n}+(h_{N-2}-\xi k_{N-2})k_{n}.

Next, we determine the coefficients. Using the defining relation
h_{N-1}\xi+h_{N-2}=\xi u
, we find

h_{N-2}-\xi k_{N-2}=(\xi u-h_{N-1}\xi)-\xi k_{N-2}=\xi(u-k_{N-2})-h_{N-1}\xi.

Since
u-k_{N-2}=k_{N-1}\xi
, this simplifies to

h_{N-2}-\xi k_{N-2}=\xi(k_{N-1}\xi)-h_{N-1}\xi=-\xi(h_{N-1}-\xi k_{N-1}).

On the other hand, the determinant identity
h_{N-1}k_{N-2}-h_{N-2}k_{N-1}=(-1)^{N}
divided by
u
implies

h_{N-1}-\xi k_{N-1}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u}.

Substituting these into (
3
), we obtain

h_{n+N}-\xi k_{n+N}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u}h_{n}-\xi\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u}k_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u}(h_{n}-\xi k_{n}).

Let
\rho=(-1)^{N}/u=\bar{u}
. Iterating this relationship
m
times for a fixed residue class
r\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
yields:

h_{r+mN}-\xi k_{r+mN}=\rho^{m}(h_{r}-\xi k_{r}).

Since
|\bar{u}|<1
from Lemma
1
, we have
|\rho|<1
. Thus, for each
r
, the subsequence of errors
\varepsilon_{r+mN}
forms a geometric progression with common ratio
\rho
.

2.3.
Third Proof. Using Complete Quotients

We define the sequence of complete quotients
\xi_{n}
by the recursion:

\xi_{0}=\xi,\qquad\xi_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\xi_{n}-a_{n}},\qquad n\geq 0,

where
a_{n}=\lfloor\xi_{n}\rfloor
.
Recall the fundamental identity:

\xi=\frac{\xi_{n+1}h_{n}+h_{n-1}}{\xi_{n+1}k_{n}+k_{n-1}}.

Using the determinant relation
h_{n}k_{n-1}-h_{n-1}k_{n}=(-1)^{n-1}
, we can solve for the error term exactly:

\varepsilon_{n}=h_{n}-\xi k_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{\xi_{n+1}k_{n}+k_{n-1}}.

To find the decay rate over one period, we consider the ratio of errors separated by
N
steps:

\frac{\varepsilon_{n+N}}{\varepsilon_{n}}=\frac{(-1)^{n+N-1}}{\xi_{n+N+1}k_{n+N}+k_{n+N-1}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{n+1}k_{n}+k_{n-1}}{(-1)^{n-1}}.

Using the periodicity
\xi_{n+N+1}=\xi_{n+1}
and the linear recurrence for convergents, the denominator scales by the factor
u=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}
. Specifically, one finds that
(\xi_{n+1}k_{n+N}+k_{n+N-1})=u(\xi_{n+1}k_{n}+k_{n-1}).

Substituting this back into the ratio, we obtain

\frac{\varepsilon_{n+N}}{\varepsilon_{n}}=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u}=\rho.

By applying this recurrence
n
times, we obtain:

\varepsilon_{nN+k}=\varepsilon_{k}\cdot\rho^{n}.

Since
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, it must be that
|\rho|<1
. This is consistent with the fact that
\xi_{n}>1
for all purely periodic continued fractions. This argument also proves the following

Corollary 2
.

For
\xi=[\overline{a_{0},\cdots,a_{N-1}}]
purely periodic, Theorem
3
gives

\rho=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{u},
with
u=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}
.

Remark 1
.

The converse of Theorem
3
is also true. Suppose that for any irrational
\xi
their sequences of errors satisfies
\varepsilon_{mN+r}=\varepsilon_{r}\cdot\rho^{m}
, then for
n=-1
we get
\varepsilon_{N-1}=\varepsilon_{-1}\cdot\rho
that is equivalent to relation
\rho=h_{N-1}-\xi k_{N-1}
, since

\rho=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}},

we can substitute the expression obtained for
\rho
in the previous equation. After some elementary algebraic manipulations, we can obtain the equation

k_{N-1}\xi^{2}-(h_{N-1}-k_{N-2})\xi-h_{N-2}=0,

is exactly the characteristic identity of periodic continued fractions.

\xi=\frac{h_{N-1}\xi+h_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}},

therefore
\xi
is purely periodic. Then the Theorem
3
gives a characterization for the purely periodic quadratic irrationalities.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Proof.

By Theorem 3, for each
0\leq r\leq N-1
, the terms of the sub-series satisfy
|\varepsilon_{mN+r}|=|\varepsilon_{r}||\rho|^{m}
.
Therefore,

\beta_{r}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|h_{mN+r}-\xi k_{mN+r}|^{s}=|\varepsilon_{r}|^{s}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|\rho|^{sm}.

Since
|\rho|<1
, this is a convergent geometric series with sum
\frac{|\varepsilon_{r}|^{s}}{1-|\rho|^{s}}
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Since
\varepsilon_{k}
and
\rho
lie in the field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
,
the sums
\beta_{k}(s)
also belong to
\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
for every integer
s\geq 1
.
For non–integral values of
s
the arithmetic nature of
f_{\xi}(s)
is much
more delicate; one expects transcendental values in many cases, but we do not
pursue this question here.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\xi
be a

and put
K=\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
.
Assume moreover that
\xi>1
, and let
h_{n}/k_{n}
be the convergents of the
(regular) continued fraction of
\xi
.

If there exists
N\geq 1
such that

u:=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\quad\text{(equivalently }N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(u)=\pm 1\text{)},

then
\xi
is purely periodic.
Conversely, if
\xi
is purely periodic, then for
N
equal to its (primitive) period
the element
u=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}
is a unit.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume that
\xi
is purely periodic of (primitive) period
N
.
Then the standard identity

\xi=\frac{h_{N-1}\xi+h_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}}

holds. As in Lemma

u\bar{u}=(k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2})(k_{N-1}\bar{\xi}+k_{N-2})=(-1)^{N},

hence

.

(
\Leftarrow
) Now assume
\xi
is real quadratic,
\xi>1
, and that for some
N\geq 1

the element
u:=k_{N-1}\xi+k_{N-2}
is a unit. Then

\bar{u}:=k_{N-1}\bar{\xi}+k_{N-2}=\frac{N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(u)}{u},

so, since
\xi>1
and
k_{N-1}\geq 1
,
k_{N-2}\geq 0
, we have
u>1
, hence

|\bar{u}|=\frac{1}{|u|}<1.

Solving the displayed relation for
\bar{\xi}
gives

\bar{\xi}=\frac{\bar{u}-k_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}}.

Because
k_{N-1}>k_{N-2}\geq 0
for convergent denominators, and because
-1<\bar{u}<1
,
we obtain the bounds

\bar{\xi}<\frac{1-k_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}}\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\xi}>\frac{-1-k_{N-2}}{k_{N-1}}>\frac{-1-(k_{N-1}-1)}{k_{N-1}}=-1.

Thus
-1<\bar{\xi}<0
. Since we also assumed
\xi>1
, Galois’ criterion (Theorem
2
)
implies that
\xi
is purely periodic.
∎

4.
Some identities for Euler continued fractions

As mentioned in the introduction, the identities

\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}\,(h_{n}-\xi k_{n})^{2}=\xi,\qquad\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}\,|h_{n}-\xi k_{n}|=\xi+1,

for irrational
\xi
, derived in
[
12
,
13
]
, were the original
motivation for this work. In this section we consider generalized continued
fractions of the form

\xi=a_{0}+\cfrac{b_{1}}{a_{1}+\cfrac{b_{2}}{a_{2}+\cfrac{b_{3}}{\ddots}}}

with positive integers
a_{n},b_{n}
, and show how analogous identities persist
in this setting.

The numerators and denominators of the
n
-th convergent to
\xi
are defined by

h_{-2}=0,\ h_{-1}=1,\ h_{0}=a_{0},\ h_{n+1}=a_{n+1}h_{n}+b_{n+1}h_{n-1},

k_{-2}=1,\ k_{-1}=0,\ k_{0}=1,\ k_{n+1}=a_{n+1}k_{n}+b_{n+1}k_{n-1}.

We define
B_{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}
for
n\geq 1
, with
B_{0}=1
.

Theorem 7
.

If
\xi
is an irrational number represented by the generalized continued fraction above, then

\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}\frac{1}{B_{n+1}}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=\xi.

Note that when
b_{n}=1
for all
n
we recover the identities mentioned at the beginning of this section.

Proof.

For convenience, we put
c_{n}=\frac{1}{B_{n}}
. And remember that
\varepsilon_{n}=h_{n}-\xi k_{n}
, the argument of our proof relies on telescoping these
\varepsilon_{n}
as done in
[
13
]
. So, note that for recurrences for
h_{n},k_{n}
we have

\varepsilon_{n+1}=h_{n+1}-\xi k_{n+1}=a_{n+1}(h_{n}-\xi k_{n})+b_{n+1}(h_{n-1}-\xi k_{n-1}),

\varepsilon_{n+1}=a_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}+b_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n-1}.

Then, if we multiply the previous expression by
c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}
, we get

a_{n+1}c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}(\varepsilon_{n+1}-b_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n-1})=c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}\varepsilon_{n+1}-b_{n+1}c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}\varepsilon_{n-1},

note that by definition of
c_{n}
,
b_{n+1}c_{n+1}=c_{n}
therefore

a_{n+1}c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}\varepsilon_{n+1}-c_{n}\varepsilon_{n-1}\varepsilon_{n}.

Summing it from
n=-1
to
N

\sum_{n=-1}^{N}a_{n+1}c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=c_{N+1}\varepsilon_{N}\varepsilon_{N+1}-c_{0}\varepsilon_{-2}\varepsilon_{-1}.

Since
\varepsilon_{N}\to 0
we conclude

\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}c_{n+1}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=-c_{0}\varepsilon_{-2}\varepsilon_{-1}=\xi.

∎

Remark 3
.

Note that if all
b_{n}=1
, then
B_{n+1}=1
and we recover the original identities. Furthermore, the proof of Theorem
7
relies purely on algebraic telescoping. Thus, the identity holds for any numbers (real or complex) or functions, provided that the limit terms
c_{N}\varepsilon_{N+1}
tends to zero as
N\to\infty
.

The formulas in Theorem
7
specifically the first formula allow us to obtain several identities when considering certain continued fractions. Furthermore, we can think of
\xi
as a function represented as a continued fraction, so these identities can also be extended to functions. We will present some of these identities based on some representations as continued fractions known in the literature.

4.1.
An identity involving
\pi

Remark 4
.

From
[
9
]

\arctan x=\cfrac{x}{1+\cfrac{x^{2}}{3-x^{2}+\cfrac{(3x)^{2}}{5-3x^{2}+\ddots}}}

Setting
x=1
and multiplying by
4
yields the generalized continued fraction for
\pi

\pi=\cfrac{4}{1+\cfrac{1^{2}}{2+\cfrac{3^{2}}{2+\ddots}}}

The coefficients are
a_{0}=0,a_{1}=1
, and
a_{n+1}=2
for
n\geq 1
. The numerators are
b_{1}=4
and
b_{n}=(2n-3)^{2}
for
n\geq 2
. Consequently, for
n\geq 1
, we have

B_{n+1}=\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}b_{j}=4\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{n+1}(2j-3)^{2}=4[(2n-1)!!]^{2}.

Lemma 2
.

For
n\geq 1
, the denominators satisfy
k_{n}=(2n-1)!!
and the convergents are given by the partial sums of the Leibniz series,
h_{n}/k_{n}=4L_{n}:=4\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}
.

Proof.

The recurrence
k_{n}=2k_{n-1}+(2n-3)^{2}k_{n-2}
with base cases
k_{1}=1,k_{2}=3
satisfies
k_{n}=(2n-1)!!
by simple induction. Similarly, the relation
h_{n}=4k_{n}L_{n}
is verified by substituting the recurrence relations into the difference
4k_{n}L_{n}-h_{n}
, which vanishes for all
n
. Applying Theorem
7
, we get

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{4[(2n-1)!!]^{2}}(h_{n}-\pi k_{n})^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}=\pi.

Rearranging terms and substituting
h_{n}-\pi k_{n}=k_{n}(4L_{n}-\pi)
, where
S_{n}-\pi
is the negative of the series tail, we find

\pi-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{k_{n}}-\pi\right)^{2}=8\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}\right)^{2}.

∎

Remark 5
.

We can express the tail
\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}
using the digamma function
\psi(z)
. This relies on the standard series representation for the difference of digamma values, found in classical references such as
[
1
]
. We start from the Weierstrass product for the Gamma function:

\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}=ze^{\gamma z}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z}{k}\right)e^{-z/k}.

Taking logarithms and differentiating yields the logarithmic derivative

\psi(z)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}=-\gamma+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+z}\right).

Subtracting
\psi(a)
from
\psi(b)
gives the identity valid for
a,b\notin\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}

\psi(b)-\psi(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+a}-\frac{1}{k+b}\right).

By rearranging the alternating sum,

\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+\frac{2n+1}{4}}-\frac{1}{k+\frac{2n+3}{4}}\right).

Applying the identity above with
a=\frac{2n+1}{4}
and
b=\frac{2n+3}{4}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+1}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{4}\left[\psi\left(\frac{2n+3}{4}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{2n+1}{4}\right)\right].

Thus, the main identity can be written in terms of special functions as

\pi-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\psi\left(\frac{2n+3}{4}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{2n+1}{4}\right)\right]^{2}.

4.2.
An Identity Involving
\ln 2
.

Let us use Euler’s continued fraction
[
9
]
for
\ln(1+x)
at
x=1
:

\ln 2=\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1^{2}}{1+\cfrac{2^{2}}{1+\ddots}}}

Here,
a_{n+1}=1
and
b_{n}=(n-1)^{2}
for
n\geq 2
,
b_{1}=1
. The product term is
B_{n}=((n-1)!)^{2},n\geq 1
,
B_{0}:=1
.
A straightforward induction shows that
k_{n}=n!
and that the convergents equal the partial sums of the alternating harmonic series,
h_{n}/k_{n}=S_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}
.

Since
a_{0}=0
, applying Theorem
7
yields:

\ln 2=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}(h_{n}-k_{n}\ln 2)^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(S_{n}-\ln 2\right)^{2}.

Since
S_{n}-\ln 2=-\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}
, we derive the identity:

\ln 2=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}\right)^{2}.

We can derive an equivalent version in terms of the digamma function by analyzing the tail term
\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}
. Grouping terms in pairs allows us to rewrite the sum:

\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+\frac{n+1}{2}}-\frac{1}{k+\frac{n+2}{2}}\right).

Applying the difference identity derived in Remark
5
with
a=\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}
and
b=\frac{n}{2}+1
yields

\ln 2=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}.

5.
Further directions.

5.1.
Possible generalizations of the main theorem

A natural direction is to provide multidimensional analogues for the formulas

\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}\,(h_{n}-\xi k_{n})^{2}=\xi,\qquad\sum_{n=-1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}\,|h_{n}-\xi k_{n}|=\xi+1,

replacing the classic algorithm for continued fractions with, for example, the Jacobi-Perron algorithm
[
11
,
1
]
. The first proof for the pair of identities
[
12
]
shows that they are geometric in nature, the first related to the area and the second to the perimeter, both of the triangle of vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,\xi)
. Therefore, a possible
d
-dimensional generalization would consist of a family of identities for the perimeter, area, volume, and so on, up to the
d
-volume of a
d
-simplex. Following this, we would like to be able to give an analogue to Theorem
3
for purely periodic irrationals of degree
d
, that is, roots of rational polynomials of degree
d
whose expansion as a multidimensional continued fraction is purely periodic.

This generalization is not trivial. For algorithms such as Jacobi-Perron, Bernstein
[
5
]

[
6
]
gives examples of families of cubic irrationalities whose expansion in the Jacobi-Perron algorithm is non-periodic and also when is periodic. So we have to deal with Hermite’s problem, finding an algorithm where we can always guarantee periodicity as in the quadratic case and with these we can be able to give multidimensional analogues for Theorems
1
and
2
. The second problem is then to find a correct generalization of the sequence of errors
\varepsilon_{n}
for the multidimensional case. This is necessary for the generalization of identities such as the main theorem.

As an example of what we are looking for we can consider the following, let us work with the Jacobi-Perron algorithm for those irrationalities of arbitrary degree
\eta
whose expansion is purely periodic. We can take the basis vector of the algorithm as
\mathcal{B}=(1,\eta,\cdots,\eta^{d-1})^{T}
. The
n-
step of the algorithm can be represented by a matrix
M_{n}\in GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
, we define the vectors
C_{n}
as the last column of
M_{n}
. Following this we can consider the generalization of the error sequences
\varepsilon_{n}
as the sequence of scalar products
\vartheta_{n}=\langle C_{n},\mathcal{B}\rangle
. Therefore in this sense the generalization to the main theorem is the following

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that the JPA expansion of
\eta
is purely periodic with period matrix
M_{N}=M_{0}\cdots M_{N-1}
, and suppose that
\mathcal{B}=(1,\eta,\cdots,\eta^{d-1})^{T}
is a left eigenvector of
M_{N}
, that is
M_{N}^{T}\mathcal{B}=\mu\mathcal{B}
for some eigenvalue
|\mu|<1
. Then for any
m\geq 0
and residue
r
we have

\vartheta_{mN+r}=\mu^{m}\vartheta_{r}.

Proof.

From the definition of the algorithm being purely periodic with period
N
, the convergent vector at step
mN+r
is

C_{mN+r}=M_{N}^{m}C_{r}.

Then by definition we have

L_{mN+r}=\langle M_{N}^{m}C_{r},\mathcal{B}\rangle.

By the following identity
\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^{T}y\rangle
we get

L_{mN+r}=\langle C_{r},(M_{N}^{T})^{m}\mathcal{B}\rangle,

and by hypothesis we have
(M_{N}^{T})^{m}\mathcal{B}=\mu^{m}\mathcal{B}
, therefore

L_{mN+r}=\langle C_{r},(M_{N}^{T})^{m}\mathcal{B}\rangle=\mu^{m}\langle C_{r},\mathcal{B}\rangle=\mu^{m}L_{r}.

∎

The above theorem depends entirely on hypothesis
\mathcal{B}
is a left eigenvalue of
M
, which, unlike the quadratic case, cannot always be guaranteed.
