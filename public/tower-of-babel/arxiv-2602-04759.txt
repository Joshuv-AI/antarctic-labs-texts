Abstract
. The last decade has witnessed a rapid advancement of generative AI technology that significantly scaled the accessibility of AI-generated non-consensual intimate images (AIG-NCII), a form of image-based sexual abuse that disproportionately harms and silences women and girls. There is a patchwork of commendable efforts across industry, policy, academia, and civil society to address AIG-NCII. However, these efforts lack a shared, consistent mental model that clearly situates the technologies they target within the context of a large, interconnected, and ever-evolving technological ecosystem. As a result, interventions remain siloed and are difficult to evaluate and compare, leading to a reactive cycle of whack-a-mole. In this paper, we contribute the first comprehensive AIG-NCII technological ecosystem that maps and taxonomizes 11 categories of technologies facilitating the creation, distribution, proliferation and discovery, infrastructural support, and monetization of AIG-NCII. First, we build and visualize the ecosystem through a synthesis of over a hundred primary sources from researchers, journalists, advocates, policymakers, and technologists. Next, we demonstrate how stakeholders can use the ecosystem as a tool to 1) understand new incidents of harm via a case study of Grok and 2) evaluate existing interventions via three more case studies. We conclude with three actionable recommendations, namely that stakeholders should 1) use the ecosystem to map out state, federal, and international laws to produce a clearer policy landscape, 2) collectively develop a centralized database that dynamically tracks the 11 technologies in the ecosystem to better evaluate interventions, and 3) adopt a relational approach to researching AIG-NCII that studies the “edges” of the ecosystem map to better understand how the ecosystem technologies interact. Our goal is to produce an AIG-NCII technological ecosystem that provides a clear, shared terminology and framework for stakeholders to move into the future of AIG-NCII prevention with clarity and foresight.

Methodology.
Guided by these tenets, we iteratively analyzed and synthesized the heterogeneous sources described above into higher-level categories representing key technologies grouped by their role in supporting AIG-NCII. As we uncovered gaps in the emerging ecosystem (e.g., the role of developer platforms in hosting models and datasets), we actively sought out additional sources to fill in these gaps (e.g., technical artifacts, terms of service, and documentation pages of Github and Civitai). When necessary, we also fact-checked claims by creating test accounts on related platforms (e.g., we created a new X account to study Grok’s models, interfaces, and payment methods – see section
4.1
). To produce a robust and evidence-based map, our focus was on technologies for which there are multiple concrete sources we could triangulate to characterize their role in the AIG-NCII ecosystem, rather relying on single sources or speculating about the possible roles of different technologies. Throughout our iterative mapping process, we aimed to achieve a level of granularity that was specific enough to differentiate distinct technical roles (e.g., splitting infrastructural technologies into those that support discovery vs. those that support monetization) and support the design of targeted interventions. At the same time, we also aimed to maintain a level of abstraction that could support a rapidly-changing ecosystem and interventions that recognize and target broader categories of technologies rather than individual models or apps. goes into further detail about sources used for each of the 11 technologies in the ecosystem.

10. Critical Service Providers) provide critical infrastructural support that allows technologies on the creation and distribution levels to function.

9. Developer Platforms
are platforms for developers to create, store, manage, and share code, including models and datasets. Developer platforms are central to the development of open-source models and datasets that are used in the creation of deepfakes. Activists and researchers documented Github’s role in hosting early face-swap models like DeepFaceLab, DeepNude, and Unstable Diffusion that were used to produce AIG-NCII
(
111
;
77
;
49
)
. AI-specific developer platforms like Hugging Face and Civitai are used to host open-weight models, such as pre-trained base models Stable Diffusion and Flux, as well as almost 35,000 fine-tuned “deepfake” variants, a majority of them sexual and signaling intent to produce non-consensual intimate images
(
48
)
. An audit of Civitai also found that it was predominantly used to host “not-safe-for-work” (NSFW) models and datasets
(
106
)
.

10. Critical Service Providers
, including cloud service providers, domain name services (DNS), and authentication services, are support software, enabling the existence of multiple pieces of the technological ecosystem, including nudifier apps and deepfake websites. An Indicator audit of 85 AI nudifier websites
(
6
)
found that “Amazon and Cloudflare provide hosting or content delivery services for 62 of the 85 nudifiers” and “Google enabled simple sign-on for 53 out of 85.” In section
4.2
, we provide a case study demonstrating how Mr.DeepFakes shut down due to a critical service provider
(
4
)
terminating service, demonstrating how this piece of the ecosystem can be used to influence other technologies.

4.
How to Use the AIG-NCII Ecosystem

4.2.
Use Case #2. Analyzing and Evaluating Existing Interventions

The AIG-NCII ecosystem also allows stakeholders to critically evaluate interventions to mitigate AIG-NCII by understanding and analyzing which pieces of the ecosystem interventions target (and which they do not) and how. Stakeholders can use the ecosystem as a tool to highlight where interventions are likely to succeed, how they may be bypassed, and ways in which they could be modified or expanded to achieve different effects. In the following section, we evaluate three case studies of interventions. For each, we (1) use the map to identify the technologies involved and make the underlying intervention strategy explicit; and (2) analyze the intervention, drawing out implications for its effectiveness and lessons for future interventions.

4.2.1.
The TAKE IT DOWN Act

Context.
The TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed into law in May 2025 (and on effect in May 2026), is the first U.S. federal law to tackle the non-consensual distribution of intimate images (NDII), both traditional and AI-generated
(
92
;
10
)
.
In the context of the ecosystem map, TAKE IT DOWN targets
distribution channels
, specifically public platforms. This law requires them to remove reported non-consensually posted “intimate visual depictions” within 48 hours, to be enforced by the federal trade commission.

Analysis.
Public platforms play the role of
Distribution
within the AIG-NCII ecosystem, meaning the TAKE IT DOWN Act only affects AIG-NCII by removing existing non-consensually distributed images. The Cyber Civil Rights Initiative has critiqued the TAKE IT DOWN Act for being “unconstitutionally vague, unconstitutionally overbroad, and lacking adequate safeguards against misuse”
(
59
)
. Beyond implementation limitations, the AIG-NCII map demonstrates how TAKE IT DOWN’s sole target of distribution channels means that current U.S. federal protections only includes
removing
AIG-NCII but does not protect people from the
creation
of new AIG-NCII
(
29
)
. This means victim-survivors bear the burden of going through the emotional and physical labor of identifying and removing abusive content only to see more images appear again in the future. The gendered chilling effect of image-based sexual abuse occurs as a result of vulnerable communities retreating from online spaces due to fear of harassment
(
39
;
66
;
56
;
58
)
. The lack of federal protections on the
creation
level means that as long as creation technologies proliferate, the risk of image-based sexual abuse exists.

4.2.2.
San Franciscio Lawsuit Against 16 AI Nudifier Apps

Context.
In August 2024, the San Francisco city attorney conducted a landmark lawsuit against 16 AI nudifier websites with over 200 million visits
(
24
;
101
)
. 10 of the websites have now shut down. This lawsuit addresses
generative AI interfaces
, namely AI nudifier apps that allow users to easily create AIG-NCII.

Analysis.
AI nudifier apps belong under the
Creation
category. This is the first example in the U.S. of using litigation to target upstream creation technologies rather than simply taking down non-consensually posted images. By targeting the AI nudifier apps, this litigation approach prevents the future creation of new images and sets the precedence for new lawsuits, such as the one recently against the ClothesOff AI nudifier app in New Jersey
(
7
)
.

4.2.3.
Mr.DeepFakes Shut Down

Context.
May 6, 2025, Mr.DeepFakes, the world’s most notorious “deepfake pornography” site with over 650,000 users shut down because an unknown “critical service provider terminated service permanently” and “data loss has made it impossible to continue operation”
(
4
)
. The site contained AIG-NCII depicting public figures including celebrities, activists, journalists, politicians, as well as private citizens
(
36
)
. Mr.DeepFakes was both a
distribution channel
where users would post “deepfake” videos and images as well as a
deepfake creation community
where users could assist each other in the creation of new content
(
47
;
102
)
. The shut down was connected to the actions of an unknown
critical service provider
.

Analysis.
This case study shows how a critical service provider (a form of
infrastructural support
for creation and distribution technologies) is able to intervene in the AIG-NCII ecosystem by refusing to support the existence of Mr.DeepFakes and thus 1) eliminating a key
distribution channel
for AIG-NCII, 2) removing existing posted images and videos, and 3) eliminating a deepfake creation community that supports the future creation of new images). This intervention can also be replicated with similar effectiveness to shut down the smaller AI nudifier applications which rely on critical service providers like cloud service providers, domain name services, and authentication services
(
6
)
.

5.
How to Stop Playing Whack-a-Mole: Recommendations for Stakeholders

Preventing AIG-NCII is so often a game of whack-a-mole. When deepfake creation community “r/deepfakes” shut down, “Mr.DeepFakes” emerged
(
102
)
. When the generative AI interface “DeepNude” shut down after public pressure, the open source repository for the app appeared on Github
(
49
)
. And most recently, as we saw with Grok (see section
4.1
), @Grok was generating thousands of images per hour
(
20
)
. Simply removing threads, apps, or images in response to new instances of harm is not enough. In building the AIG-NCII ecosystem, we hope to provide a guiding landscape that connects the dots and provides the clarity, terminology, and shared mental model for stakeholders to move into the future of AIG-NCII prevention with foresight. In this section, we provide 3 recommendations for policymakers, civil society organizations, technologists, advocates, and researchers in this direction.

5.1.
Recommendation #1. Stakeholders should use the AIG-NCII technological ecosystem to map out state, federal,
and international laws to produce a clearer policy landscape.

Policymakers, technologists, advocates, and researchers, and civil society organizations and practitioners should use the AIG-NCII technological ecosystem as a lens to take a group of existing laws (e.g., from multiple U.S states or multiple countries) to develop a policy landscape that clearly maps laws to the technologies they target. We use an example of U.S. state law to demonstrate this approach.

According to a legislative tracker by Public Citizen, nearly all 50 U.S. states have some form of law addressing AIG-NCII
(
80
)
. However, these laws use inconsistent terminology, definitions, and involve varying levels of protections for victim-survivors
(
55
)
. The AIG-NCII ecosystem can be used as a tool to compare and contrast multiple state laws and understand which technologies are targeted. We can compare, for example, Arkansas and California. Arkansas HB1529
(
94
)
(enacted in 2025) states that “the Attorney General may institute a civil action on behalf of the state against a provider or developer of image generation technology that was used to create deepfake visual material in violation if 1) “the deepfake visual material that was created…was generated substantially or in its entirety by a prompt based image generation technology” and 2) “the provider or developer of the image generation technology did not have reasonable safeguards in place to protect against the generation of deepfake visual material.”
(
94
)
. This law targets providers and developers of “prompt-based image generation technology,” falling under the category of
generative AI models
and potentially
generative AI interfaces
. In contrast, California’s SB-981
(
18
)
(enacted in 2024) would “require a social media platform to provide a mechanism that is reasonably accessible to a reporting user who is a California resident who has an account with the social media platform to report sexually explicit digital identity theft to the social media platform,” i.e., targeting
distribution channels
in a similar fashion to U.S. federal law (see a case study of the TAKE IT DOWN Act in section
4.2.1
).

Mapping policies against the AIG-NCII ecosystem allows stakeholders to clearly characterize and compare different state laws. As a result, industry players can improve product compliance; advocacy organizations can develop localized victim-survivor resources; lawyers have a larger toolkit for litigation; and state and local policymakers can situate their laws within a larger landscape. This recommendation goes beyond the example of U.S. state law. Although this paper is limited to discussions of U.S. federal and state policy, AIG-NCII is a global issue affecting women and girls internationally. Governments and AI Safety Institutes globally have recognized AIG-NCII of adults and children as a key synthetic content harm to urgently address
(
75
;
45
;
46
)
. In response to Grok (see case study in section
4.1
), regulators from the European Commission, United Kingdom, France, Ireland, India, Malaysia, Indonesia, Brazil, Australia, Canada, and many others have initiated investigations
(
27
)
. The AIG-NCII ecosystem can be used as a toolkit to understand how an international patchwork of laws can be used collectively to design more comprehensive interventions that go beyond the whack-a-mole strategy of responding to individual incidents of harm when they occur.

5.2.
Recommendation #2. Stakeholders should collectively develop a centralized database that dynamically tracks the 11 technologies in the ecosystem to better evaluate interventions.

The AIG-NCII ecosystem is an extremely dynamic ecosystem. Deepfake creation communities are constantly migrating. New generative AI models are released every day. Dynamically tracking different pieces of the ecosystem over time will allow us to quantitatively measure effectiveness of interventions and their ripple effects. Individual researchers have built databases tracking individual pieces of the ecosystem like hundreds of nudifier apps, distribution sites, and “deepfake creators”
(
41
)
and over five hundred incidents of users publicly requesting Grok AI to generate AIG-NCII
(
67
)
. There are also technical reports that capture numbers at a certain point in time
(
49
;
6
;
48
)
. However, there does not yet exist one centralized database that tracks all 11 technologies. Such a database could play a crucial role in understanding the effectiveness of interventions at the ecosystem level.

For example, in April 2025, developer platform Civitai (which has hosted thousands of NSFW models and datasets
(
106
)
) updated their Terms of Service to explicitly prohibit “content that uses, reproduces, or is based on the likeness of real people - living or deceased - including public figures, celebrities, influencers, and private individuals, in any context”
(
26
)
. 404 Media has reported that Civitai’s new policy “deals [a] major blow to the nonconsensual AI porn ecosystem”
(
33
)
. Using the AIG-NCII ecosystem map, we can see Civitai’s policy inducing a ripple effect as follows: Civitai is a
developer platform
which provides supporting infrastructure for
training datasets
and
generative AI models
. Researchers can hypothesize that, by targeting technologies that support the creation of AIG-NCII, Civitai’s new policy will reduce, e.g., the number of NSFW
generative AI models
available on Civitai, or even further downstream, the number of
AI nudifier apps
available on
app stores
. However, without a shared database tracking each piece over time, it is difficult to understand if and how these kinds of developer platform policies are effective.

5.3.
Recommendation #3. Stakeholders should adopt a relational approach to researching AIG-NCII that studies the “edges” of the ecosystem map to better understand how the ecosystem technologies interact.

In addition to tracking individual pieces of the ecosystem (see section
5.2
), researchers should also move towards a relational approach of studying AIG-NCII that examines the “edges” of the ecosystem to better understand how different technologies influence each other and the potential ripple effects that interventions may have. There has been promising initial work in this direction. For example, Gibson et al.’s
(
42
)
2024 study of 20 AI nudifier applications and their underlying monetization infrastructure characterizes the relationship between
generative AI interfaces
and
payment processors
. Hawkins et al.’s
(
48
)
2025 study on the number of Stable Diffusion and Flux “deepfake” and “sexual” variants on Hugging Face and Civitai surfaces how
generative AI models
and
developer platforms
are interconnected.

There are many “edges” of the ecosystem that have not yet been studied. For example, to our knowledge, there has yet to be a study on the relationship between
generative AI models
and
generative AI interfaces
(specifically AI nudifier applications). Current AI nudifier apps or “undressing” sites state that they use generative AI technology, but do not specify the underlying model being used, reflecting a gap in tech accountability upstream: beyond the identified generative AI interface, we do not know exactly which generative AI models and training datasets are responsible for the resulting AIG-NCII. Further research on along this edge would assist existing interventions (e.g., lawsuits against the creators of these technologies
(
7
)
) and shed light on future directions for AI governance (e.g., designing AI safety techniques to mitigate downstream harm
(
54
)
).

The three recommendations in this section all intersect and should be pursued in tandem.
For example, an improved relational understanding of the technologies will clarify the policy landscape by identifying the scope of targeted technologies when laws use vague terms like “creator”; a centralized database transparently tracking and documenting the ecosystem players will aid in the research of “edges” and vice versa; and a clearer policy landscape will motivate improved documentation of individual actors.

6.
Conclusion

Image-based sexual abuse is a longstanding issue that predates generative AI. However, generative AI technologies and the vast technological ecosystem surrounding them enable abuse at scale, eliminating the barriers to abuse and reinforcing the hypersexualization, dehumanization, and silencing of women and girls around the world. Taking down images, apps, websites, models, or datasets in an endless game of whack-a-mole is not enough to protect vulnerable populations within a rapidly evolving technological, advocacy, and policy landscape. In this paper, we contribute the first comprehensive ecosystem of 11 technologies that facilitate the creation, distribution, proliferation and discovery, infrastructural support, and monetization of AIG-NCII through a synthesis of over a hundred sources from researchers, technologists, policymakers, journalists, trust and safety professionals, advocates, and civil society organizations (section
2
). We provide a taxonomy (section
3
) and visualization (Figure
1
) of the ecosystem and use four case studies to demonstrate how stakeholders can use the ecosystem to 1) understand new incidents of harm and 2) evaluate existing interventions (section
4
). We conclude with three actionable recommendations (section
5
) for stakeholders that enable the redirection of AIG-NCII prevention efforts away from recurring games of “whack-a-mole” and towards informed and collaborative efforts to eliminate this rapidly growing form of abuse.
