Clinical Performance of International and Korean Genetic Testing Criteria for Hereditary Breast Cancer: A Large-Scale Cohort Analysis of 2,188 Individuals
Authors: Bom-Yi Lee, Hyun Jeong Lee, Jiyoung Kim, Dongwon Kim, Jiwon Jeong, Kyungah Yoh, Eunyoung Kang, Sung-Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeInternational guidelines, including the American Society of Breast Surgeons, American Society of Clinical Oncology-Society of Surgical Oncology (ASCO-SSO), and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), have broadened the eligibility for germline testing in breast cancer (BC), whereas the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (K-HIRA) criteria remain more restrictive. This study evaluated the prevalence of pathogenic variants (PVs) and compared the diagnostic yield of Korean versus international criteria in a high-risk hereditary BC cohort from a single-institution.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 2,188 individuals referred for genetic counseling between January 2015 and June 2025. Germline testing included BRCA1/2 sequencing, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, and multigene panels. Eligibility was determined according to the ASCO-SSO (Recommendation 1.1), NCCN (v3.2025), and K-HIRA. The prevalence, sensitivity, and number of missed cases of PVs were compared.ResultsPVs were identified in 178 (8.1%) patients. Among patients with BC (n = 1,796), 159 carried PVs (8.9%), 131 (82.4%) carried BRCA1/2, and 28 (17.6%) carried non-BRCA genes. Across the frameworks, the prevalence of PV among eligible patients was comparable (ASCO-SSO, 8.7%; NCCN, 9.5%; K-HIRA, 10.2%). The sensitivity differed (NCCN, 96.9%; ASCO-SSO, 92.5%; K-HIRA, 89.3%): the sensitivity of NCCN was significantly higher than that of K-HIRA (p = 0.001), whereas the differences were not significant for NCCN vs. ASCO-SSO (p = 0.071) and ASCO-SSO vs. K-HIRA (p = 0.336). Missed carriers were more frequent under K-HIRA (17/159, 10.7%) than under ASCO-SSO (12/159, 7.5%) and NCCN (5/159, 3.1%), and those missed by K-HIRA often had non-specific family histories (58.8%) or no family history (23.5%).ConclusionIn this cohort, K-HIRA criteria showed lower sensitivity than the international guidelines, leading to missed opportunities for prevention and treatment. Broader eligibility criteria and the integration of multigene testing may improve the detection of hereditary cancer and optimize clinical management in Korea.

INTRODUCTION

Hereditary breast cancer (BC) represents a clinically important subset of all BCs, with germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (PVs) in BRCA1 and BRCA2 accounting for a substantial proportion of hereditary risks [1]. In addition to BRCA1/2, high- and moderate-penetrance genes such as TP53, PTEN, PALB2, CDH1, STK11, CHEK2, and ATM also contribute to hereditary BC risk [123]. Identifying PV carriers enables individualized risk assessment, guides preventive strategies for relatives, and increasingly influences therapy, including the use of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and other targeted agents; however, radiotherapy may be contraindicated [456789]. With the wider use of multigene panels, a substantial fraction of clinically actionable variants are now being detected outside BRCA1/2, emphasizing the need to re-evaluate testing criteria in light of evolving therapeutic strategies and diverse populations.

Professional societies have proposed clinical criteria for germline testing to optimize detection. The American Society of Breast Surgeons (ASBrS) recommends universal testing for all patients with BC [10], while the American Society of Clinical Oncology-Society of Surgical Oncology (ASCO-SSO) prioritizes testing for patients with BC diagnosed at ≤ 65 years [8]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) has progressively broadened eligibility by expanding age thresholds, family history definitions, and clinically relevant genes [11]. Recently, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) expanded the indications for germline testing and recommended tumor sequencing as part of routine clinical practice [7912]. In its 2025 guidelines, the ESMO has highlighted six genes for routine testing: BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51C, RAD51D, and TP53 (the latter specifically for early-onset BC) [12]. These genes broadly overlap, but are not identical to the core set emphasized by the NCCN and ASCO-SSO (BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, PALB2, CDH1, PTEN, and STK11) [78912]. However, prior studies have reported that selective approaches fail to detect between one-fourth and one-half of the carriers [1013141516]. Moreover, the shortage of professional genetic counselors or clinical genetics specialists continues to limit the consistent and appropriate use of genetic testing, even as multigene panels are rapidly adopted worldwide [1718].

In Korea, eligibility is determined by the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (K-HIRA). Although revised in July 2020, the criteria remain restrictive compared to international guidelines, and their performance in capturing PV carriers has not been clearly evaluated. Against this background, we conducted a single-institution study of 2,188 individuals comprising a high-risk hereditary BC cohort who were referred for genetic counseling over a 10-year period to evaluate PV prevalence and compare the diagnostic yield of the ASCO-SSO, NCCN, and K-HIRA criteria relative to that of universal testing.

METHODS

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed individuals referred for cancer genetic counseling at a single tertiary referral center in Korea between January 2015 and June 2025. A total of 2,583 individuals underwent genetic counseling during the study period, and germline testing data were collected for all individuals, either from tests performed at our institution or from external results provided by the patients (n = 44). After excluding those who declined genetic testing or cascade testing of relatives of an index case (n = 395), 2,188 individuals were included in the final analysis. Among them, 1,796 patients had a personal history of BC (BC group), while 392 had no personal history of BC (non-BC group) (Figure 1). Clinical information, including age at diagnosis, tumor subtype, family history of cancer, and history of multiple primary tumors, was obtained from medical records and pedigree assessment during genetic counseling sessions conducted by a genetic counselor specializing in clinical genetics. Family history was defined as having at least one first-, second-, or third-degree relative with breast, ovarian, prostate, or pancreatic cancer (BOPP), or another documented cancer type.

Genetic testing

All individuals underwent germline testing with BRCA1/2 direct sequencing, BRCA1/2 multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) or a 69-gene multigene panel test depending on the clinical indications. BRCA1/2 direct sequencing was performed according to the K-HIRA criteria (see the definition of the guideline criteria below). BRCA1/2 MLPA and/or multigene panel testing were performed either concurrently with BRCA1/2 direct sequencing or sequentially, depending on patient preference and physician judgment, with written informed consent.

All germline tests were performed in certified laboratories according to validated protocols. The peripheral blood samples were processed in a clinical genomics laboratory accredited by the Korean Institute of Genetic Testing Evaluation. The testing included BRCA1/2 MLPA using P002-D1 and P045-C1 Probemixes for BRCA1 and BRCA2, respectively. A targeted multigene panel next-generation sequencing (NGS) test was also conducted, which included 69 cancer predisposition genes (AIP, ALK, APC, ATM, AXIN2, BAP1, BARD1, BLM, BMPR1A, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDH1, CDK4, CDKN2A, CHEK2, DICER1, EPCAM, FANCC, FH, FLCN, GALNT12, GREM1, HOXB13, MAX, MEN1, MET, MITF, MLH1, MRE11A, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, NBN, NF1, NF2, NTHL1, PALB2, PHOX2B, PMS2, POLD1, POLE, POT1, PRKAR1A, PTCH1, PTEN, RAD50, RAD51C, RAD51D, RB1, RECQL, RET, SCG5, SDHA, SDHAF2, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SMAD4, SMARCA4, SMARCB1, STK11, SUFU, TMEM127, TP53, TSC1, TSC2, VHL, and WT1). Variant interpretation followed the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines [19], and only PVs or likely PVs were included in the analysis.

Definition of guideline criteria

The eligibility of each patient for testing was retrospectively assessed according to three guidelines: ASCO-SSO [8], NCCN (v3.2025) [11], and K-HIRA. ASCO-SSO Recommendation 1.1 was applied, specifying germline testing for all patients with BC diagnosed at ≤ 65 years, including BRCA1/2 testing at the time of a new diagnosis of stage I–III or de novo stage IV/metastatic disease. Recommendations 1.2 (risk-based expansion for selected patients older than 65 years) and Recommendations 1.3 (personal or family history-based expansion to non-BRCA predisposition genes) were excluded from the sensitivity and prevalence analyses to ensure consistency with prior evidence, which demonstrated that extending eligibility to ≤ 65 years yielded a sensitivity > 90% for nine predisposition genes (ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, CHEK2, NF1, PALB2, PTEN, and TP53) and 98% for BRCA1/2 [14]. NCCN criteria (v3.2025) included patients with a personal history of BC diagnosed at ≤ 50 years, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), multiple primary BC (synchronous or metachronous), or male BC. Eligibility also extended to those with a family history of BC in a close blood relative at ≤ 50 years, male BC, ovarian cancer (OC), pancreatic cancer (PnC), or prostate cancer (PrC) that was metastatic or classified as high- or very-high-risk. In addition, patients with ≥ 3 diagnoses of BC and/or PrC (any grade) on the same side of the family, including the patient, were considered eligible. The K-HIRA criteria specify the eligibility for insurance-covered BRCA1/2 sequencing. Eligible cases include BC diagnosed at age ≤ 40 years, TNBC diagnosed at ≤ 60 years, bilateral BC, OC, male BC, BC with OC or PnC, and BC with a family history of BOPP cancer in a first-, second-, or third-degree relative. Patients with OC and mucinous histology were excluded, and a family history of metastatic or high-risk PrC was determined based on patient self-reports. Eligibility was coded dichotomously (1 = eligible; 0 = not eligible) for each guideline framework. The total number of patients fulfilling each criterion was determined and compared with the actual distribution of PVs.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were the prevalence and sensitivity of PV detection for each guideline evaluated relative to the entire BC cohort (n = 1,796). Secondary outcomes included pairwise comparisons of prevalence and sensitivity across the three guidelines, and the number and proportion of missed PV carriers.

Statistical analysis

Prevalence was defined as the proportion of PV carriers among eligible patients and sensitivity as the proportion of all PV carriers in the captured cohort. The prevalence of each guideline and the entire cohort were compared using two-sample proportion z-tests with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated using the Wilson score method. Sensitivity relative to the reference was compared using McNemar’s test, which was restricted to patients with PVs. For each pair of criteria, PV carriers were cross-classified into four groups: (i) eligible by both guidelines, (ii) eligible only by criterion A, (iii) eligible only by criterion B, and (iv) ineligible by either guideline. McNemar’s test evaluated discordant pairs, that is, PV carriers identified by one criterion but missed by another. For example, when comparing the NCCN and K-HIRA, we tested whether the number of carriers eligible under the NCCN, but not the K-HIRA, differed from those eligible under the K-HIRA but not the NCCN. Pairwise comparisons among the three guidelines were performed using two-sample proportion tests (prevalence) and McNemar’s tests (sensitivity). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. For the bilateral BC subgroups (synchronous, metachronous, and ipsilateral recurrence), the overall group differences were tested using chi-square and pairwise comparisons with Fisher’s exact test and Bonferroni adjustment. All analyses were performed using jamovi (The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia) version 2.6.44 (interface to R 4.4).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Public Institutional Review Board (Institutional Review Board [IRB] No. P01-202308-06-001). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the minimal risk to the participants, the IRB waived the requirement for written informed consent.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Of the 2,583 individuals referred for hereditary cancer genetic counseling, 2,053 (79.5%) had a personal history of cancer. Among them, BC was the most common, affecting 1,796 patients (69.5% of the total cohort). In contrast, 530 individuals (20.5%) had no personal history of cancer, but were referred for family history. A total of 232 families with PVs were enrolled in this study. Genetic testing was performed on 2,392 individuals, corresponding to 92.6% of the entire cohort (n = 2,583) (Table 1).

Distribution of PVs

PVs were identified in 178 of the 2,188 individuals (8.1%), with a prevalence of 8.9% (159/1,796) in patients with BC and 4.8% (19/392) in those without BC (Figure 2A). Among all the PVs, 37.1% were BRCA1 (n = 66), 46.1% were BRCA2 (n = 82), 15.2% were non-BRCA (n = 27), and 1.7% were double heterozygotes (DHs) (n = 3) (Figure 2A). Non-BRCA PVs contained 15 genes, most frequently ATM (n = 4), CHEK2 (n = 3) and PALB2 (n = 3) (Figure 2B). In the BC group, 82.4% of PVs occurred in BRCA1/2 (n = 131) and 17.6% in non-BRCA genes and DHs (n = 28) (Figure 2A and B). The PV yields were 7.4% (127/1,712) with BRCA1/2 sequencing, 0.8% (4/479) with MLPA, and 4.5% (30/664) with multigene panel testing, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1 (excluding DHs). Notably, two MLPA-detected variants were identified in patients who previously tested negative by BRCA1/2 sequencing. Among all BRCA1/2 PVs, 2.2% (3/134) were detected by MLPA, corresponding to 5.1% (3/59) of BRCA1 PVs (Supplementary Table 2).

From the perspective of the K-HIRA criteria, 77.5% (n = 1,392) of the patients with BC met the eligibility criteria. The most common qualifying sub-criteria were a family history of BOPP cancer (n = 923), age ≤ 40 years at diagnosis (n = 437), TNBC at ≤ 60 years (n = 235), and bilateral BC (n = 167) (Figure 2C, Table 2). Relative PV prevalence was highest in male BC (33.3%), followed by those with a family history of OC (32.6%), family history of PnC (22.2%), TNBC ≤ 60 years (17.0%), bilateral BC (10.8%), and age ≤ 40 years (10.8%) (Table 2). Several subgroups not covered by the K-HIRA criteria also showed a relatively high PV prevalence despite the small sample sizes. These included ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) (22.2%), TNBC diagnosed at > 60 years of age (16.1%), and BC with another primary malignancy (12.7%) (Table 2). Patients with BC and a family history of other cancers also had a PV prevalence of 9.5%, which was comparable to that of PnC (9.9%). In addition, PV prevalence significantly differed among patients with multiple BCs (χ2 = 9.50, df = 2, p = 0.009). Synchronous bilateral BC had the lowest prevalence (5.7%), which was significantly lower than that of metachronous bilateral BC (19.4%; Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.027) and IBTR (22.2%; adjusted p = 0.049), whereas no difference was observed between the latter two groups (adjusted p = 1.000) (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3).

Diagnostic yield of guideline criteria

We considered the entire BC cohort (n = 1,796)—corresponding to the ASBrS universal testing as the reference group in this study [10]. Against this background, we evaluated three selective testing criteria, ASCO-SSO (restricted to BC diagnosed at ≤ 65 years; Recommendation 1.1), NCCN (v3.2025), and K-HIRA, for their ability to capture PV carriers (Table 3) [811]. Among eligible patients, PV counts and prevalence were 147/1,686 (8.7%, 95% CI, 7.4–10.2) for ASCO-SSO, 154/1,617 (9.5%, 95% CI, 8.2–11.1) for NCCN, and 142/1,392 (10.2%, 95% CI, 8.7–11.9) for K-HIRA, compared with 159/1,796 (8.9%, 95% CI, 7.6–10.3) in the entire cohort. The prevalence did not differ from the reference for any criterion (ASCO-SSO, p = 0.889; NCCN, p = 0.498; K-HIRA, p = 0.197). The sensitivities relative to the reference were 92.5% for ASCO-SSO (p < 0.001), 96.9% for NCCN (p = 0.062), and 89.3% for K-HIRA (p < 0.001), corresponding to 12 (7.5%), 5 (3.1%), and 17 (10.7%) missed PVs, respectively. In the pairwise tests, the prevalence did not differ among the guidelines (ASCO-SSO vs. NCCN, p = 0.422; ASCO-SSO vs. K-HIRA, p = 0.161; NCCN vs. K-HIRA, p = 0.534). McNemar’s test of discordant PV carriers showed that the NCCN performed significantly better than the K-HIRA (p = 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed between the ASCO-SSO and NCCN (p = 0.071) or between the ASCO-SSO and K-HIRA (p = 0.336). Figure 2D illustrates that the relative distributions of BRCA1/2, non-BRCA, and DH PVs were broadly similar across the three criteria, although each guideline missed a small but distinct subset.

Characteristics of missed PVs

We analyzed patients whose PVs were not captured according to the guideline criteria (Table 4). In total, 34 missed instances were observed: 12 in ASCO-SSO, 5 in NCCN, and 17 in K-HIRA, acknowledging that some carriers were missed in more than one guideline. Under the ASCO-SSO criteria, most missed carriers were older (66–88 years) with a family history of BOPP cancers (75.0%) or TNBC diagnosed after the age of 60 years (16.7%). Notably, these cases (n = 11) would have been detected if the ASCO-SSO recommendation 1.2 had been applied. NCCN missed carriers primarily because BOPP family history was restricted to relatives diagnosed at ≤ 50 years, including one carrier whose affected relative was diagnosed after this age. In addition, 40.0% of missed carriers of NCCN had no family history. The K-HIRA missed the largest proportion of carriers, most commonly those with a family history of other (non-BOPP) cancers (58.8%), while 23.5% had no family history. Among the carriers missed due to family history, the age at diagnosis ranged from 41 to 67 years, with 71.4% (10/14) diagnosed at 41–50 years and 92.9% (13/14) at 41–65 years. Non-BRCA variants were disproportionately missed, and across all three guidelines, they were observed only for family history-based indications. All the three non-BRCA PVs missed by the ASCO-SSO criteria (ATM, CHEK2, MUTYH), met the BOPP family history criteria.

DISCUSSION

This study directly compared the ASCO-SSO (Recommendation 1.1), NCCN, and K-HIRA criteria with universal testing in a large Korean cohort reflecting a decade of single-institution experience. Although PV prevalence was similar across guidelines (8.7%–10.2%), sensitivity varied; NCCN performed best (96.9%), whereas ASCO-SSO (92.5%) and K-HIRA (89.3%) were less sensitive. The ASCO-SSO primarily failed to identify older patients (> 65 years) with TNBC or those with a family history of BOPP cancer. However, if Recommendation 1.2 were fully applied, only one PV carrier in our cohort would have been missed, suggesting that ASCO-SSO may be the most effective selective criterion. NCCN missed carriers mainly because family history was restricted to relatives diagnosed at ≤ 50 years, and 40% of missed carriers had no family history. The K-HIRA missed the largest number of carriers, including those with a family history of non-BOPP cancers (58.8%), and a notable fraction without a family history (23.5%). Two carriers with ‘no family history’ were later reclassified after developing OC or reporting a new BOPP history, highlighting the evolving nature of hereditary cancer risk assessment, which depends on changes in personal and family histories over time. This includes a case in our cohort where a carrier was initially missed due to a VUS classification and an incomplete family history, but was subsequently reclassified as carrying an LPV following a variant update and the discovery of a new family history of BC.

Our findings align with international evidence that selective criteria miss clinically relevant subsets. Large-scale studies have shown that one-quarter to one-half of carriers fall outside the guideline-based eligibility, particularly when their clinical or family history is incomplete [71013141516]. For example, earlier evidence that motivated the expansion of ASCO-SSO eligibility showed that when the testing threshold was broadened to age ≤ 65 years, the sensitivity exceeded 90% for nine BC predisposition genes (ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, CHEK2, NF1, PALB2, PTEN, and TP53) and 98% for BRCA1/2 [14]. However, in the same study, the sensitivity of the NCCN criteria was limited to 86.9% for BRCA1/2, 79.1% for six high-risk genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, PALB2, PTEN, and TP53), and 70.1% for nine predisposition genes. These findings underscore both the progress and limitations of the NCCN, which has been updated (v3.2025), to improve its inclusiveness. Based on these data, guidelines have shifted toward broader or universal testing. The ASBrS now recommends testing for all patients with BC [10]; ASCO-SSO recommends testing, primarily for those diagnosed at ≤ 65 years [8]; ESMO supports tumor sequencing and expanded germline testing, encompassing all high-risk patients including those with early-stage BC [7920]; and NCCN has progressively broadened its criteria over successive updates [11], extending eligibility to clinically relevant genes beyond BRCA1/2.

Contrastingly, in Korea, the reimbursement criteria set by the HIRA were revised on July 1, 2020, representing an expansion compared with the prior policy. Nonetheless, the system remains restrictive, with eligibility defined by several specific criteria, particularly younger age ≤ 40 years, TNBC at ≤ 60 years, or a family history of BOPP cancer. In our cohort, 17 PV carriers were missed by K-HIRA. Their age at diagnosis ranged from 41 to 67 years, with 12 (70.6%) diagnosed at ≤ 50 years, suggesting that an expanded age threshold would improve detection and substantially cover cases not captured by family history-based criteria (Table 4).

In addition, several subgroups excluded under the K-HIRA, including IBTR and TNBC diagnosed after the age of 60 years, had a PV prevalence well above the overall cohort rate of 8.9%. Patients with IBTR showed a prevalence of 22.2%, which was significantly higher than that of synchronous bilateral BC (5.7%) (Table 2), consistent with prior reports that BRCA carriers face markedly higher risks of recurrence and contralateral BC [2122]. Taken together, these findings suggest that refinements to the K-HIRA could include extending the eligibility to early-onset BC diagnosed at age ≤ 50 years, IBTR, and TNBC at all ages. When combining these three subgroups (IBTR, TNBC > 60 years, and BC diagnosed at 41–50 years not covered by K-HIRA), the overall prevalence was 5.5% (95% CI, 3.8–7.9), corresponding to a number needed to test of 18 (95% CI, 13–27) to detect one additional carrier. These estimates were calculated without adjusting for potential overlap between subgroups and should therefore be interpreted with caution. From the perspective of policymaking and cost-effectiveness, the negative predictive values (NPVs) observed in our cohort were 95.8% for the K-HIRA, 89.1% for the ASCO-SSO, and 97.2% for the NCCN. Although the K-HIRA criteria demonstrate a relatively high NPV, suggesting their efficiency in identifying low-risk individuals, this is offset by their lower sensitivity, which leaves a significant number of PV carriers undetected. Interestingly, the lower NPV of the ASCO-SSO criteria (89.1%) compared to that of others is attributable to its broad inclusiveness, which substantially reduces the size of the ineligible group and makes the NPV more sensitive to even a few missed cases. Ultimately, the NCCN criteria achieved the highest NPV (97.2%) and lowest false omission rate (2.8%), reinforcing that expanding testing eligibility can enhance the clinical safety and reliability of negative results without necessarily compromising overall cost-effectiveness.

The detection of non-BRCA PVs remains challenging when strategies primarily focus on BRCA1/2. In our supplementary analysis, many non-BRCA carriers exhibited BRCA-like features (early-onset BC, bilateral BC, male BC, or TNBC ≤ 60 years) (Supplementary Table 1). Beyond breast and OC, associated tumors include gastric, lung, colorectal, liver, thyroid, and hematologic malignancies, underscoring the need for counseling and surveillance beyond classical hereditary breast and OC syndromes (Supplementary Figure 1) [23]. Furthermore, consistent with ESMO guidance [7], our data show that family history-based criteria miss a substantial proportion of carriers (e.g., non-BOPP history and no family history), supporting broader eligibility frameworks. Therefore, first-tier testing should be considered for multigene panels covering hereditary BC genes.

Furthermore, MLPA should be used to detect large genomic rearrangements, particularly in patients with TNBC. An additional 0.8% of PVs were detected by MLPA, with all BRCA1 rearrangements in patients with TNBC accounting for 2.2% (3/134) of all BRCA1/2 PVs and 5.1% (3/59) of BRCA1 PVs, reinforcing their relevance in this context [242526]. These findings highlighted the importance of incorporating MLPA into routine testing.

In summary, the restrictive K-HIRA framework could be improved by five key modifications: expanding i) the age threshold to ≤ 50 years, ii) incorporating IBTR, iii) recognizing TNBC at any age, iv) adopting multigene panel testing beyond BRCA1/2, and v) including MLPA to detect large genomic rearrangements (Table 5). These refinements are particularly warranted given the heterogeneous and often atypical presentation of hereditary BC, which necessitates a broader genetic approach. In line with the Korean Breast Cancer Society clinical practice guidelines for hereditary BC, the recommended multigene panel should encompass ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDH1, CHEK2, NF1, PALB2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11, TP53, and Lynch syndrome genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM) [27]. However, the final composition of the multigene panel may require further expert consensus and ongoing updates to reflect the emerging evidence. Furthermore, when revising the family history-based criteria, the previous restriction on metastatic PrC was removed, reflecting the practical limitation that metastatic status is usually derived from verbal family history, which is often incomplete or imprecise.

TP53 testing should be recommended for patients with BC diagnosed at ≤ 30 years, as suggested in international guidelines (Table 5) [11162829]. In line with the recommendations of a comprehensive multigene panel, our results highlight a specific, clinically important subgroup of very young patients with BC. We found that both TP53 carriers in our cohort met the international testing guidelines. In our cohort, both TP53 carriers, excluding those with DH, met this criterion and were initially diagnosed at 19 and 28 years of age (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the patient diagnosed at 19 years of age underwent breast-conserving surgery and subsequently developed IBTR at 29 years of age. These cases underscore the importance of TP53 testing in very young patients with BC, not only for genetic risk assessment but also for surgical planning, as carriers are generally contraindicated for radiotherapy owing to their heightened sensitivity to radiation-induced malignancies [71130].

Collectively, these changes would better align Korean practice with international guidelines and enhance the detection of clinically meaningful subsets. Expanding eligibility does not necessarily increase the overall prevalence of PVs, as confirmed by previous studies and our own findings [31]. However, broader frameworks improve the sensitivity by capturing clinically meaningful subsets that are otherwise missed under restrictive criteria. For example, Naaseh et al. [32] found that 5.4% of PVs would have been overlooked using the NCCN criteria despite a similar overall prevalence. Ultimately, in line with international guidelines, the inclusion of patients with advanced or metastatic BC who are eligible for PARP inhibitors should also be considered. In this context, the application of NGS has become increasingly important in clinical practice as it enables the identification of a wider spectrum of clinically actionable genes beyond BRCA1/2. The integration of NGS into clinical care requires expertise and infrastructure. In Korea, additional challenges include the limited availability of genetic counselors and regulatory restrictions that limit NGS ordering to certified institutions, which may influence patient access to targeted therapies [3334].

Broader testing also has therapeutic implications: PARP inhibitor indications now extend beyond germline BRCA1/2 to somatic BRCA1/2 and germline PALB2 [93536], and BRCA1/2 status may influence outcomes with CDK4/6 inhibitors [37383940]. Beyond treatment considerations, genetic testing benefits patients and at-risk relatives through cascade testing and underscores the importance of including clinically actionable genes, such as those highlighted in the ACMG Secondary Findings List [41].

This study had some limitations. Although the cohort was large, it comprised a high-risk population referred for genetic counseling at a single institution, and therefore may not fully represent the broader BC population. Additionally, several clinically relevant subgroups included relatively small sample sizes, and the findings from these subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and should not be directly translated into guideline revisions without further validation. Furthermore, germline testing strategies have not been uniformly applied across cohorts. The use of BRCA1/2 sequencing, MLPA, and multigene panel testing varies according to the clinical judgment, patient preference, evolving guidelines, and reimbursement policies. This heterogeneity may have led to an underestimation of non-BRCA PVs and should be considered when interpreting the differences in guideline performance. Nevertheless, these limitations reflect the real-world clinical practice over a decade of rapidly changing genetic testing paradigms. Future large multicenter studies with standardized testing strategies are required to validate and extend our findings.

In conclusion, most PV carriers were identified using the ASCO-SSO, NCCN, and K-HIRA criteria; however, important subsets were not captured, particularly under K-HIRA, thereby limiting opportunities for targeted treatment and indicating the need to refine current testing guidelines and support broader insurance coverage for multigene testing in Korea.
