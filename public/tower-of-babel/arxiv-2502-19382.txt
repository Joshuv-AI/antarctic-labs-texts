Functional central limit theorems for non-local branching Markov processes
Christopher B. C. Dean, Emma Horton *

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to study the fluctuations of a general class of supercritical branching Markov processes with non-local branching mechanisms. We establish functional central limit theorems and show that the limiting behaviour falls into three regimes, determined by the size of the spectral gap associated with the first-moment semigroup of the branching process. The main novelty is to develop a unified functional fluctuation theory for spatial branching Markov processes with non-local reproduction, allowing a general finite-dimensional spectral structure for the first-moment semigroup, including non-simple leading eigenvalues and nilpotent Jordan-type components. In doing so, we extend the classical small, critical and large fluctuation trichotomy beyond the finite-type and local spatial settings, and obtain limiting processes that capture the covariance structure induced by non-local offspring displacement. Keywords : Branching process, fluctuations, central limit theorem, non-local branching. MSC: 60J80, 60F05.

The aim of this article is to provide a unified characterisation of the fluctuations of a general class of supercritical branching Markov processes with non-local branching mechanisms via functional central limit theorems. We show that there exist three regimes corresponding to the size of the spectral gap of the process, such that each regime results in different limiting behaviour. Results of this kind have been studied in a range of settings, including multi-type branching processes

, spatial branching Markov processes

, developed independently and in parallel to our own, which proves central limit theorems for non-local branching processes under weaker stability assumptions on the first-moment semigroup, but in a framework restricted to a simple leading eigenvalue and to convergence at fixed times in the small and critical regimes. The results presented in this article extend this existing literature in several directions.

•

We treat branching Markov processes in which particles move in a general spatial state space and, at branching times, may give birth to offspring at locations different from that of the parent. This simultaneously incorporates spatial motion and genuinely non-local reproduction. In this respect, our results go beyond the multi-type setting of
[
17
]
, where the state space is finite, and beyond the spatial branching frameworks of
[
22
,
23
]
, where branching is local in the spatial variable. The non-local mechanism is not a purely notational extension: it changes the structure of the second moment formulae and hence the covariance terms appearing in the fluctuation limits. In particular, even when the first moment semigroup admits a spectral description analogous to the local case, the second moment and covariance formulae contain new correlation terms arising from the joint law of the offspring locations; these terms disappear or collapse to simpler moments in the local setting.

•

We allow the leading spectral structure of the first-moment semigroup to be finite-dimensional, rather than assuming a simple Perron eigenvalue. In particular, our framework permits several leading eigenfunctions and, more generally, nilpotent Jordan-type components. This leads to polynomial corrections in the law of large numbers and in the fluctuation scalings, and is not covered by the simple-eigenvalue assumptions in
[
7
,
17
,
22
,
23
]
.

•

We obtain functional convergence, rather than only at a fixed time. More precisely, in the small and critical regimes (defined below) we prove convergence of finite-dimensional distributions for bounded test functions and functional convergence in the Skorokhod topology for a suitable regularity class of test functions. This strengthens the one-time central limit theorems available in
[
7
,
22
,
23
]
. The functional formulation is important because it captures temporal correlations in the limiting fluctuations; in particular, in the small regime it reveals the dependence between the martingale-limit component and the remaining fluctuation component, while in the critical regime it makes visible oscillatory effects arising from complex eigenvalues that are hidden at a single time point.

Thus, the novelty of the paper lies not only in each of these extensions individually, but in treating them simultaneously within a single fluctuation theory covering the small, critical and large regimes.

We consider a measure-valued stochastic process

One way to characterise the behaviour of

(Ha)

\sup_{x\in E,f\in B_{1}(E)}\big|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}t}\varphi_{1}(x)^{-1}\psi_{t}[f](x)-\tilde{\varphi}_{1}[f]\big|\to 0,\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

\frac{{\rm e}^{-\lambda_{1}t}X_{t}[\varphi_{1}]}{\varphi_{1}(x)},\quad t\geq 0,\,x\in E.

Moreover,

{\sup_{x\in E,f\in B_{1}(E)}t\bigg|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}t}\psi_{t}[f](x)-\mathrm{e}^{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})t}\tilde{\varphi}_{1}[f]\varphi_{1}(x)-\tilde{\varphi}_{2}[f]\varphi_{2}(x)\bigg|\to 0,\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.}

\psi

(Large Regime (simplified))

Suppose that

. Then, for any initial position

\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{x}}

\widetilde{M}_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}t}X_{t}[\varphi_{2}],\quad t\geq 0.

(1.7)

Theorem

(Small Regime (simplified))
.

Suppose that
2\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}
and let
f\in C_{\psi}(E)
. Then, for any initial position
x\in E

n\to\infty

\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(n+t)}\left(X_{n+t}[f]-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}(n+t)}\tilde{\varphi}_{1}[f]\varphi_{1}(x)W_{\infty}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mathrm{d}}}}{{\rightarrow}}(\varphi_{1}(x)W_{\infty})^{1/2}Z_{S}(t),\quad in\quad\mathbb{D}[0,\infty),

(1.8)

Theorem

(Critical Regime (simplified))
.

Suppose that

n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda_{1}nt}{2}}(X_{nt}[f]-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}nt}\tilde{\varphi}_{1}[f]\varphi_{1}(x)W_{\infty})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mathrm{d}}}}{{\rightarrow}}(\varphi_{1}(x)W_{\infty})^{1/2}Z_{C}(t),\quad in\quad\mathbb{D}[0,\infty),

where

is a scaled Brownian motion that is independent of

.

The classification into these three regimes can be stated in terms of the spectral gap,
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
. Indeed, the large (resp. critical, small) regime corresponds to the spectral gap being less than (resp. equal to, greater than)
\lambda_{1}/2
. Now, regardless of the regime, the leading order behaviour of the system will always be described by
(\lambda_{1},\varphi_{1},\tilde{\varphi}_{1})
, as in
(Ha)
. However, the size of the spectral gap dictates how quickly this convergence happens, and hence how the system fluctuates around this first order behaviour.

In the small regime, the spectral gap is greater than
\lambda_{1}/2
, and so the convergence seen in
(Ha)
happens quickly. This gives too little time for second-order fluctuations to manifest. Thus, in the small regime,
we see a limit over a small time scale that is stationary
. In the critical regime, the speed of convergence,
\lambda_{1}/2
, is such that the “pull” towards the stationary distribution in
(Ha)
matches the “push” away arising from the second-order fluctuations, thus resulting in Brownian fluctuations over a longer timescale. Finally, if the spectral gap is smaller than
\lambda_{1}/2
, then the speed of convergence in
(Ha)
is slow enough to allow the growth of second-order fluctuations. This dominates the stationary behaviour seen in the small and critical regimes, yielding fluctuations that mimic the behaviour of (
1.3
). As previously noted, we will present these results in a more general framework that removes the need for simple eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. However, this heuristic remains true in the more general setting.

Section

Spectral assumption

Before we state our main assumption, we require some preliminary definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we say that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\psi
with finite-dimensional generalised eigenspace
A_{\lambda}\subset B(E)
if there exists a bounded projection

\Pi_{\lambda}:B(E)\to A_{\lambda}

such that, writing
K_{\lambda}:=\ker\Pi_{\lambda}
, we have

B(E)=A_{\lambda}\oplus K_{\lambda},

(2.1)

both
A_{\lambda}
and
K_{\lambda}
are invariant under
\psi_{t}
for every
t\geq 0
, and

\psi_{t}|_{A_{\lambda}}=e^{(\lambda+\mathcal{N}_{\lambda})t},

(2.2)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}:A_{\lambda}\to A_{\lambda}
is nilpotent, ie. there exists
p>0
such that
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}^{p}=0
.

The above definition entails the existence of a basis
\varphi_{j}^{(k)}
,
1\leq j\leq p
,
1\leq k\leq k_{j}
, of
A_{\lambda}
, such that

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}\varphi_{1}^{(k)}=0,\quad 1\leq k\leq k_{1},\quad\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}\varphi_{j}^{(k)}=\varphi_{j-1}^{(k)},\quad 2\leq j\leq p,\quad 1\leq k\leq k_{j}.

One can see this as the Jordan basis of the generator of
\psi_{t}|_{A_{\lambda}}
; indeed, we shall refer to
\varphi_{j}^{(k)}
as an eigenfunction of rank
j
. Moreover, since
\Pi_{\lambda}
is bounded, there exist bounded (with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
) linear coordinate maps
\tilde{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)}:B(E)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, such that

\tilde{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)}[\varphi_{j}^{(k)}]=1,\qquad\tilde{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)}[\varphi_{\ell}^{(q)}]=0,\,(j,k)\neq(\ell,q),\qquad\tilde{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)}[f]=0,\,f\in K_{\lambda}.

(2.3)

Note that for
f\in B(E)
,
x\in E
,
\overline{\psi_{t}[f](x)}=\psi_{t}[\bar{f}](x)
. Thus, if
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, by taking the complex conjugate in Definition
2.1
we see that there exists a conjugate eigenspace
A_{\bar{\lambda}}=\mathrm{span}\{\bar{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)},1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq k_{j}\}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\bar{\lambda}
and nilpotent operator
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{\lambda}}
satisfying

\mathcal{N}_{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{\varphi}_{1}^{(k)}=0,\;1\leq k\leq k_{1},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{\varphi}_{j}^{(k)}=\bar{\varphi}_{j-1}^{(k)},\;2\leq j\leq p,\,1\leq k\leq k_{j}.

(2.4)

The operator
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
should be understood as the nilpotent part of the
semigroup restricted to the generalised eigenspace
A_{\lambda}
. Since
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
is nilpotent,
e^{t\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}}
is a finite polynomial in

t
. The operator
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
therefore records the Jordan block structure
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
: if
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}=0
, then

A_{\lambda}
is spanned by genuine eigenfunctions, while if

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}\neq 0
, then
A_{\lambda}
also contains generalised
eigenfunctions, and polynomial prefactors appear in the asymptotics.

For notational clarity, when several eigenvalues,
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
, are considered at once, we
write the Jordan basis of
A_{\lambda_{i}}
as

\varphi^{(k)}_{i,j},\qquad 1\leq j\leq p_{i},\quad 1\leq k\leq k_{i,j},

where
i
indexes the eigenvalue,
j
indexes the rank in the Jordan chain, and
k
indexes multiplicity within that rank. Thus

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda_{i}}\varphi^{(k)}_{i,1}=0,\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\lambda_{i}}\varphi^{(k)}_{i,j}=\varphi^{(k)}_{i,j-1},\quad j\geq 2.

We also have
\tilde{\varphi}_{i,j}^{(k)}[\varphi_{\ell,n}^{(q)}]=0
for each
(i,j,k)\neq(\ell,n,q)
.

Moreover, when several eigenspaces are considered simultaneously, we write
\mathcal{N}
for the
operator obtained by applying the corresponding nilpotent part on each
generalised eigenspace and killing the remaining component. More precisely, if

f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{\lambda_{i}}+f_{0},\qquad f_{\lambda_{i}}\in A_{\lambda_{i}},\quad f_{0}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}K_{\lambda_{i}},

then we define

\mathcal{N}f:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{N}_{\lambda_{i}}f_{\lambda_{i}}.

Thus
\mathcal{N}
is block diagonal across the generalised eigenspaces

A_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,A_{\lambda_{m}}
, with block
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda_{i}}
on

A_{\lambda_{i}}
, and
\mathcal{N}f_{0}=0
. In particular,

\psi_{t}[\varphi_{i,j}^{(k)}](x)={\rm e}^{(\lambda_{i}+\mathcal{N})t}\varphi_{i,j}^{(k)}(x),\quad\qquad\tilde{\varphi}_{i,j}^{(k)}[\psi_{t}[f]]={\rm e}^{\lambda_{i}t}\tilde{\varphi}_{i,j}^{(k)}[{\rm e}^{\mathcal{N}t}f],\quad f\in B(E).

(2.5)

.

(H1)

There exists an integer

1

1

Discussion

The purpose of this section is threefold. We first discuss assumption

in further detail and provide sufficient conditions for it to hold. Next we discuss the restriction

and explain why this condition is natural for functional convergence. Finally, we present several examples that demonstrate different behaviours covered by the theory in the previous section.

Assumption
(H1)

In practice, showing Assumption

To this end, let us introduce the aforementioned many-to-one formula. By

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the above proposition does not imply that

Let us now compare the assumptions given in Proposition

to those made in the literature. First note that in the case that

is finite, Proposition
3.1
holds with
\mu
as the counting measure. Thus, we extend the classical results of
[
17
]
to the case where
\lambda_{1}
is non-simple.

Next, consider the setting of
[
23
]
, where the authors consider (local) branching Markov processes. Here the authors assume conditions that allow them to derive a spectral decomposition of
\psi
in
L^{2}(E,\mu)
. In particular, their assumptions satisfy those of Proposition

except for the assumptions that
\mu
is finite and (

) holds, where

is taken to be the space of continuous compactly supported functions. Indeed,
[
23
]
only assumes

is in

. In our setting, these two additional assumptions are used to transfer the spectral decomposition of

in

to a spectral decomposition in
(B(E),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
. At least heuristically, an assumption of the form (

) seems necessary to obtain this stronger version of the spectral decomposition. Indeed, in
(H1)
, we have that the asymptotic behaviour of
\psi_{t}
is characterised by pairs of the form
(\varphi_{i,j}^{(k)},\tilde{\varphi}_{i,j}^{(k)})
, where
\tilde{\varphi}_{i,j}^{(k)}
does not depend on

. If (
3.3
) did not hold, then this would suggest that

, and hence
(H1)
would not hold.

We see two main benefits of Proposition
3.1
compared to the setting of
[
23
]
. Firstly, having a spectral decomposition of the form given in
(H1)
is a natural extension of the assumptions typically made for non-local branching processes in recent literature; see
[
14
]
and references therein. In particular,
(H1)
allows us to exploit recent results on the asymptotic behaviour of the moments of non-local BMPs, originally developed in
[
10
]
and extended in
[
8
]
, which are crucial to proving the functional CLT results given in Section
2
. These results are formulated in the
L^{\infty}
norm, and this is natural for the fluctuation theory developed here. Indeed, although spectral decompositions are often most naturally obtained in an
L^{2}(E,\mu)
setting, the branching process may be initiated from a point mass
\delta_{x}
, and the quantities of interest are the random variables
X_{t}[f]
, their martingale limits and their covariances, all of which depend pointwise on the initial state
x
. The moment estimates used below are therefore uniform in
x\in E
and
f\in B_{1}(E)
, rather than merely
L^{2}(E,\mu)
estimates. Moreover, since
X_{t}
is a random finite measure,

is defined for bounded measurable test functions independently of any reference measure, whereas an
L^{2}(E,\mu)
formulation identifies functions only up to
\mu
-null sets and does not directly control
\psi_{t}[f](x)
uniformly in
x
.

This distinction is also relevant from the point of view of simulation and numerical approximation. An
L^{2}(E,\mu)
spectral expansion controls the approximation error only after averaging over the reference measure
\mu
, while Monte Carlo approximations of branching systems are typically built from particles started at specified locations and observed along realised trajectories. Regions of small
\mu
-measure may nevertheless contribute significantly to the growth or variance of such estimators, for example through high reproductive weight, rare but influential offspring displacements, or boundary effects. The
B(E)
-formulation of
(H1)
gives precisely the uniform control of
\psi_{t}[f](x)
, martingale limits and covariance terms needed for such point-mass and simulation-based interpretations.

Secondly, Proposition
3.1
only asks for
\hat{P}_{t}
to have an
L^{2}(E,\mu)
density for
t
sufficiently large. This is particularly useful in the non-local setting, since it allows the underlying Markov motion to be singular, provided that the non-local branching mechanism produces sufficient smoothing at positive times. A typical example of such dynamics is the neutron branching process,
[
12
,
14
,
13
]
. Thus, our approach does not preclude the use of
L^{2}
spectral theory; rather, Proposition
3.1
shows how
L^{2}
methods can be used to verify the
L^{\infty}
-type assumptions required for the branching fluctuation theory in concrete examples.

Restriction of
f
to
C_{\psi}(E)

As briefly discussed in the previous section, the restriction to
C_{\psi}(E)
can be thought of as a regularity assumption on the branching process, since it is a condition on the Markov process,
\xi
, the state space,
E
, and the function,

. We start by discussing this class in specific settings and then show that the regularity imposed by this restriction transfers to similar regularity on the branching process.

Since (M
4
) is required for the vast majority of our results, we consider

under the minimal assumption (M
4)
. Let

be a metric on

. Then

contains all bounded Lipchitz functions with respect to

whenever there exists a constant

, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in E,0\leq t\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1}}t^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{x}[d_{E}(\xi_{t},x)^{2}]\leq C,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in E,0\leq t\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1}}t^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{x}[d_{E}(\xi_{t},x)^{4}]\leq C.

(3.5)

Taking

to be the standard Euclidean norm, (

) holds for piecewise deterministic Markov processes with uniformly bounded velocities and uniformly bounded jump rates, as well as multitype branching processes, see Examples
3.4
and

below. Other examples include Lévy processes in bounded domains with absorbing boundary conditions and whose Lévy measure has finite fourth moments. Similarly, Itô diffusions (in such domains) which take the form

with

Examples

In this section we discuss several examples where
(H1)
holds. The examples are chosen to illustrate different aspects of the theory including a non-simple leading eigenvalue, Jordan blocks and polynomial corrections arising from the existence of generalised eigenfunctions and a non-local spatial model where the many-to-one process regularises the otherwise singular motion. We will assume throughout that
(H2)
and (M4) are in place.

Example 3.3
.

Our first example demonstrates a case when the leading eigenvalue is not simple by introducing an immigration mechanism into the branching process.

Let

Example 3.4
.

While the previous example provides a relevant setting where the leading eigenvalue is non-simple, the leading eigenvalue only has eigenfunctions of rank 1. This example provides a setting where the leading eigenvalue has eigenfunctions of rank 2.

Here we consider the setting of multi-type branching processes. In this case, take
E=\{1,\dots,d\}
for some

. It is well-known (see
[
17
]
) that, for any

,

is the

\boldsymbol{f}=(f(1),\dots,f(d))
, and

1
) and
(H2)

An example of

with non-simple

Example 3.5
.

Our final example is the aforementioned neutron branching process. This is an important model in nuclear physics as it is used to model the evolution of neutrons in fissile material
[
14
]
. Notably, this is an example that intrinsically contains non-local branching due to how neutrons interact with matter. Furthermore, since the underlying Markov motion is singular, this example demonstrates the need for an
L^{\infty}
fluctuation theory. We shall subsequently see that the density of the many-to-one semigroup only exists for sufficiently large times as a consequence of the singular motion and non-local branching, highlighting the importance of Proposition

.

The neutron branching process is a stochastic representation of the evolution of neutrons undergoing nuclear fission. In this setting, the state space
E
is a six dimensional space

, where

is a open and bounded, representing the set of spatial locations a neutron can take, and

\xi
, is a piecewise deterministic Markov process that, when at

, changes velocity at rate
\sigma_{s}(r,\upsilon)
, continuing its motion but now with velocity

with probability
\pi_{s}(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime}){\rm d}\upsilon^{\prime}
. The branching rate is denoted
\sigma_{f}
, which is also a function of position and velocity. Finally, the offspring distribution is supported on
\{0,\dots,N_{max}\}
, for some
N_{max}\geq 1
and satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{(r,v)}[\mathcal{Z}[g]]=\int_{V}g(r,\upsilon^{\prime})\pi_{f}(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime}){\rm d}\upsilon^{\prime},

for some kernel
\pi_{f}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):D\times V\times V\to[0,\infty)
. Thus, the branching mechanism is local in the spatial variable,

, but non-local in the velocity variable,

.

In practice, the so-called cross-sections,

,
\sigma_{f}
,

and
\pi_{f}

is also a piecewise deterministic Markov process with jump rate

, and jump kernel
\pi(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime})=(\sigma_{s}(r,\upsilon)\pi_{s}(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime})+\sigma_{f}(r,\upsilon)\pi_{f}(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime}))/\alpha(r,\upsilon)
. Under the additional assumptions

•

\sup_{r,\in D,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime}\in V}\alpha(r,\upsilon)\pi(r,\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime})>0
,

•

there exists

such that

is non-empty and connected,

•

,there exists

and for all

and

it was shown in

that

holds. Moreover, a consequence of the steps in proving this result show that

has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure for sufficiently large

) by a constant,

say, and hence (

The next result is an extension of the Berry-Esseen theorem. In the case where all the random variables have constant variance, then we do indeed recover the classical result.

Next, we state and prove a result that gives an appropriate bound on moments of sums of independent mean-zero random variables.

Lemma A.5
.

For
k,n\geq 1
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent mean-zero random variables in
\mathbb{C}
with finite
k
-th moment. Then, there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
that depends only on
k
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|^{k}\right]\leq C_{k}n^{k/2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{k}].

(A.13)

Proof.

We first treat the case of real random variables, then the complex result immediately follows by splitting into real and imaginary parts. By the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{k}\right]\leq C_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{2}\right)^{k/2}\right]=C_{k}n^{k/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{2}\right)^{k/2}\right],

where
C_{k}
is a positive constant that depends only on
k
. By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{2}\right)^{k/2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{k}]\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{k}].

Putting these two inequalities together yields the result.
∎

\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{1}\geq\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{m}\geq K,
