Rescue Diagnosis of Upper‐Lobar Station 12U Lymph Nodes Using the Thin Convex‐Probe EBUS Bronchoscope (BF‐UCP190F): A Report of Two Cases
Authors: Toki Kitano, Yuki Takigawa, Ken Sato, Mayu Goda, Hitoshi Hara, Tomoya Osedo, Yuto Sasano, Keisuke Shiraha, Suzuka Matsuoka, Jun Nishimura, Hiromi Watanabe, Kenichiro Kudo, Keiichi Fujiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBF‐UCP190F (Olympus) is a newly developed thin convex‐probe endobronchial ultrasound (TCP‐EBUS) bronchoscope, improving access to distal bronchi. We report two cases in which TCP‐EBUS enabled successful endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) of the upper‐lobar station 12U lymph node (#12U LN) via B3 after conventional bronchoscopic forceps sampling failed to provide a definitive diagnosis. In the first case, forceps biopsy of a left upper lobe tumour was non‐diagnostic; TCP‐EBUS‐guided EBUS‐TBNA of the left #12U LN established lung adenocarcinoma with N1 involvement. In the second case, the initial bronchoscopy yielded only cytological evidence of carcinoma without histological confirmation; TCP‐EBUS‐guided sampling of the right #12U LN revealed metastatic small cell carcinoma. In both cases, the pathological diagnosis of EBUS‐TBNA guided subsequent treatment. These cases suggest that BF‐UCP190F expands the diagnostic range of bronchoscopy and may serve as a valuable rescue technique for sampling upper lobar hilar lymph nodes in clinical practice.

Introduction

The BF‐UCP190F (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) is a thin convex probe endobronchial ultrasound (TCP‐EBUS) bronchoscope that was introduced in 2025 to improve access to deeper bronchial regions. Its main specifications include a 5.9‐mm distal outer diameter, 170° upward angulation, a 1.7‐mm working channel, and compatibility with a 25‐gauge EBUS‐TBNA needle. Unlike conventional balloon‐based convex probe EBUS bronchoscopes, the BF‐UCP190F does not require a saline‐filled balloon for ultrasound coupling.

Although EBUS‐TBNA is widely used for lung cancer diagnosis and nodal staging, conventional convex‐probe EBUS bronchoscopes can be difficult to advance peripherally into the narrow bronchi, particularly in the upper lobes. In a cadaveric model, Takashima et al. demonstrated that a TCP‐EBUS bronchoscope improved accessibility and aspiration ability in the segmental and subsegmental bronchial areas compared with a conventional CP‐EBUS bronchoscope [1].

These characteristics may be particularly relevant for the upper lobar station 12 lymph node (#12U LN), which is anatomically close to the segmental bronchi but can be difficult to sample using conventional convex‐probe EBUS bronchoscopes. Herein, we report two cases in which BF‐UCP190F enabled real‐time EBUS‐TBNA of #12U LN via B3 and provided pathological diagnoses that directly informed subsequent treatment.

Case Report

Case 1

A 75‐year‐old man was referred to our hospital for the evaluation of a 12 × 42 mm left upper lobe pulmonary lesion detected on chest imaging (Figure 1A). Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a left upper lobe nodule and an enlarged left #12U LN. Under conscious sedation with midazolam and fentanyl, a thin bronchoscope (BF‐P290; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced towards the suspected primary lesion in the left upper lobe (S3). Although close to the lesion, radial EBUS demonstrated within‐lesion findings, and prominent adjacent vessels limited safe and adequate transbronchial biopsy (Figure 1B). Rapid on‐site touch‐imprint cytology (ROSE‐TIC) of conventional forceps biopsy was negative for malignant cells. Therefore, the diagnostic target was changed to the left #12U LN (short axis 10 mm), and the BF‐UCP190F was advanced to the left B3 (the diameter of the B3 bronchus was 5.9‐mm). Referring to preprocedural virtual bronchoscopic navigation (VBN) images generated using SYNAPSE VINCENT (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) (Figure 1C), the bronchoscope (Figure 1D) was rotated to the 3 o'clock position in B3, enabling EBUS visualization of the left #12U LN. Fluoroscopy confirmed that the needle sheath had emerged from the bronchoscope tip (Figure 1E). EBUS‐TBNA was performed using a 25‐gauge needle (ViziShot2 25G, NA‐U401SX‐4025N; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under real‐time ultrasonography guidance (Figure 1F). Two passes were performed with 30 needle strokes per pass. ROSE of the specimen obtained by EBUS‐TBNA revealed malignant cells, and the final pathological diagnosis was adenocarcinoma (Figure 1G).

The tumour stage was finally classified as cT2bN1M0, corresponding to stage IIB lung adenocarcinoma (PD‐L1: 30%, driver mutation‐negative for lung cancer compact panel assay). After a multidisciplinary assessment, neoadjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin, pemetrexed and durvalumab was administered, followed by video‐assisted thoracoscopic left upper lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection.

Case 2

A 75‐year‐old man with interstitial lung disease was referred to our hospital with a 20‐mm nodule in the right upper lobe. Initial bronchoscopy with radial EBUS using a guide sheath yielded a cytological diagnosis of carcinoma; however, the histological specimens contained only atypical cells and did not permit definitive histological subtyping. Because the previous bronchoscopy did not provide sufficient specimens for definitive subtyping, additional bronchoscopic re‐biopsy was planned. An enlarged right #12U LN (short axis 18 mm) identified on contrast‐enhanced CT was selected as a next target for diagnosis and clinical staging (Figure 2A).

(F) Histopathological examination of the EBUS‐TBNA specimen revealed small cell carcinoma.

Under conscious sedation with midazolam and fentanyl, the BF‐UCP190F was advanced to right B3 (the diameter of the B3 bronchus was 6.0 mm). Referring to the preprocedural VBN images (Figure 2B), the bronchoscope was rotated towards the 9 o'clock position in B3, enabling EBUS visualization of the right #12U LN (Figure 2C). Under fluoroscopy guidance, the physician confirmed the orientation of the TCP‐EBUS bronchoscope (Figure 2D). EBUS‐TBNA was performed using a 25‐gauge needle (ViziShot2 25G, NA‐U401SX‐4025N; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under real‐time ultrasound guidance (Figure 2E). Seven passes were performed with 30 needle strokes, and adequate specimens were obtained. ROSE was positive for malignant cells. Pathological examination revealed small cell carcinoma (Figure 2F).

The patient was diagnosed with metastatic lung cancer and small cell carcinoma, and systemic chemotherapy with carboplatin and etoposide was initiated.

Discussion

We report two cases in which the BF‐UCP190F bronchoscope enabled EBUS‐TBNA of #12U LN, leading to definitive diagnoses after a non‐diagnostic forceps biopsy. Nguyen proposed that TCP‐EBUS extends real‐time US‐guided bronchoscopy to the middle third of the lungs, supporting its role as an additional bronchoscopic sampling modality [2]. TCP‐EBUS should not be regarded as a replacement for conventional convex‐probe EBUS but rather as a complementary device for lesions located beyond the reach of conventional EBUS bronchoscopes [1].

In both cases, conventional bronchoscopic forceps biopsy performed either during the same procedure or a previous bronchoscopy failed to provide sufficient pathological information for treatment planning. As a rescue diagnostic approach, BF‐UCP190F‐guided EBUS‐TBNA of #12U LN successfully established the final diagnosis, revealing lung adenocarcinoma with N1 involvement in Case 1 and metastatic small cell lung carcinoma in Case 2. These pathological diagnoses directly influenced subsequent therapeutic decision‐making. Our experience suggests that BF‐UCP190F expands the diagnostic capabilities of bronchoscopy by enabling EBUS‐TBNA of selected upper‐lobar lymph nodes that are difficult to access using conventional convex‐probe bronchoscopes, thereby providing a valuable rescue option when specimens obtained by forceps biopsy are non‐diagnostic.

Although fluoroscopic guidance is not routinely required for conventional EBUS‐TBNA, it may be useful when performing EBUS‐TBNA with the TCP‐EBUS in peripheral. In conventional EBUS‐TBNA, the needle sheath can often be advanced beyond the bronchoscope tip and fixed under direct visualization before positioning adjacent to the target. However, pre‐positioning and fixing the sheath may potentially limit access to targets that require substantial angulation. Therefore, in the present cases, the TCP‐EBUS was first advanced peripherally into the target B3 and positioned adjacent to the station #12U LN before the needle sheath was fixed. Because the diameter of the bronchus was approximately 6.0‐mm, close to the 5.9‐mm distal outer diameter of the TCP‐EBUS bronchoscope, direct visualization of the sheath emerging from the bronchoscope tip was limited at this peripheral position. Minimal fluoroscopic guidance was therefore used to confirm appropriate positioning of the TCP‐EBUS and safe emergence of the needle sheath from the bronchoscope tip before aspiration under real‐time EBUS guidance. In addition, preprocedural VBN was useful for identifying the target bronchus and the direction of the target, thereby facilitating appropriate orientation for EBUS‐TBNA.

Recent advances in balloon‐less convex probe EBUS devices have broadened the range of lesions that can be approached under real‐time ultrasound guidance. We previously reported the usefulness of a novel balloon‐less convex‐probe EBUS bronchoscope (EB‐710US: Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) with a 6.4‐mm distal outer diameter for lung cancer diagnosis and staging using EBUS‐TBNA and EUS‐B‐FNA [3].

To the best of our knowledge, published clinical experience with the TCP‐EBUS (using BF‐UCP190F) bronchoscope remains limited. However, emerging reports suggest that the indications for TCP‐EBUS are expanding. Torii et al. described a case of malignant lymphoma diagnosed by TCP‐EBUS‐guided TBNA of a peripheral pulmonary lesion in the lower lobe [4]. Recently, Nakai et al. demonstrated the feasibility of TCP‐EBUS‐guided cryobiopsy for peripheral pulmonary lesions, highlighting the expanding role of TCP‐EBUS in real‐time ultrasound‐guided bronchoscopic diagnosis [5]. Our case adds to the limited clinical experience with TCP‐EBUS by demonstrating its feasibility for real‐time sampling of the #LN 12U for rescue bronchoscopy diagnosis. Further accumulation of clinical experience with TCP‐EBUS is essential to better define its optimal indications and procedural techniques, and to contribute to improving bronchoscopic diagnosis.

This study has several limitations. It included only two cases, and the accessibility and diagnostic performance of TCP‐EBUS were not directly compared with those of a conventional CP‐EBUS bronchoscope. Therefore, TCP‐EBUS should not be regarded as a replacement for conventional CP‐EBUS but rather as a complementary device that may facilitate access to selected anatomically challenging targets [6].

In conclusion, EBUS‐TBNA using TCP‐EBUS (BF‐UCP190F) may represent a useful bronchoscopic technique for obtaining a pathological diagnosis when samples of conventional forceps biopsy are non‐diagnostic for pulmonary nodules.
