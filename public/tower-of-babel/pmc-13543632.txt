Eucommia ulmoides leaves improving growth by regulating the rumen microbiome in sheep
Authors: Yehao Hu, Lijuan Pan, Boshuai Liu, Chuanyou Su, Liyang Zhang, Wenjuan Li, Tong Fu, Tengyun Gao, Hongxia Lian, Linfeng Wang, Yangdong Zhang, Kaizhen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRuminants have the ability to convert agricultural byproducts into valuable resources. However, the influence of functional roughage with medicinal value on production performance and rumen function in ruminants remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the role of Eucommia ulmoides leaves (EUL) in the growth performance and rumen microbiome of sheep.ResultsTwenty-one healthy female sheep were randomly divided into three groups, in which the EUL feeding amounts were 0% (CON), 5% (Diet 1), and 10% (Diet 2) on a dry matter basis. The results revealed that body weight gain, dry matter intake, and feed conversion efficiency were significantly greater in the Diet 1 group than in the CON and Diet 2 group. The metatranscriptome annotation results indicated that feeding EUL to sheep altered the rumen microbial community. Compared with those in the other two groups, the abundances of butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Coprobacillus, significantly increased in the Diet 1 group. Functional enrichment analysis of the metatranscriptome indicated higher metabolic activity in the carbohydrate and amino acid pathways in the Diet 1 group than in the control group, which might be due to improved nutrient utilization in the Diet 1 group.ConclusionsIn conclusion, feeding 5% EUL to sheep enhanced production performance, feed conversion efficiency, and microbial metabolism. These findings suggest that EUL plays a critical role in sustainable livestock farming as a promising functional roughage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05089-2.

Background

Global agriculture must increase output to satisfy the escalating demands of an expanding global population. In the context of mutton sheep farming, particularly in the context of the rapidly expanding global population and the consequent increase in food demand, as well as the stringent requirements for intensive production systems, mutton sheep farming practices should not involve the use of high-concentrate feed to expedite the process of fattening. However, such a diet often elicits a series of inflammatory reactions, resulting in involuntary culling [1–3]. The feeding of roughage with functional ingredients may provide valuable insight into the sustainable development of livestock farming. Eucommia ulmoides Oliv. is an endemic plant in China that has been used in traditional medicine for nearly 2000 years. Over twenty-four distinct natural compounds, such as flavonoids, phenols, and terpenoids, have been isolated from Eucommia ulmoides leave (EUL) [4–7]. According to the latest record of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia on the homology of medicine and food, EUL can be directly used as a functional food or raw feed material [8].

As a traditional Chinese medicine, EUL contains a variety of bioactive compounds that have driven its development as a food additive for humans and livestock [9]. In the context of animal nutrition, the secondary metabolites in EUL — such as chlorogenic acid and flavonoids — have attracted attention for their potential antioxidant, antibacterial, and metabolic-modulating properties [10–18]. These functional components are highly relevant for developing sustainable roughage alternatives that support ruminant health and feed efficiency. The anti-inflammatory molecular processes of Eucommia ulmoides polysaccharide extracts have been reported. Research has shown that Eucommia ulmoides polysaccharide enhances rumen epithelial immune barrier function by activating the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and reducing the release of inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [19]

Although many studies have focused on the analysis of extracts derived from Eucommia bark, few studies have directly examined its potential as a roughage. The application of functional roughage to the diet has the potential to improve feeding efficiency by influencing the rumen microbial response and associated metabolism. Therefore, the use of adapted roughage combinations to promote livestock health and efficiency is paramount.

Microbes that colonize the rumen are essential for the digestion and fermentation of fibrous components in the diet. Furthermore, host genetics have been shown to significantly influence the ruminal microbiota [20, 21]. A growing body of evidence indicates a strong correlation between the ruminal microbiome and productive phenotypes, including feed efficiency, nitrogen digestibility, and methane emissions [22–24].

Although the health benefits of Eucommia ulmoides bark have been well documented, bioactive compounds are predominantly encapsulated within the cell wall, limiting their release into extraction solutions [25, 26]. Feeding ruminants with functional roughage reduces energy expenditure during extraction and enhances the functional properties of the roughage in the diet. Although fiber is essential for ruminants, the incorporation of greater quantities of roughage combined with beneficial ingredients, accompanied by an accurate balance of dietary fiber content, can yield superior production outcomes. From a sustainable development perspective, this approach not only enhances industrial efficiency, but also reduces emissions, thereby contributing to a more environmentally conscious approach to industrial production. An optimized dietary structure that incorporates these replacements is aligned with the future prospects of the breeding industry. Compared to high-risk fattening diets, natural plant components are more aligned with animal welfare requirements and ultimately improve production efficiency [27].

A substantial body of evidence underscores the pressing necessity for the fundamental restructuring of dietary patterns in the animal husbandry industry to effectively mitigate the prevalence of morbidity. The incorporation of EUL has been shown to confer benefits with regard to resistance to oxidative inflammation and the enhancement of animal immunity, as evidenced in the context of animal husbandry, and Eucommia ulmoides leaf extract (ELE) has demonstrated efficacy in eliminating oxidative stress markers, such as DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) [28, 29], However, although EUL cannot directly replace roughage in large quantities, its addition to ruminants may yield favorable effects because of their unique digestive systems. Therefore, it is imperative to develop and promote the functional byproducts of EUL if the animal husbandry industry is to achieve healthy, efficient, and sustainable development.

The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the effects of adding 5% and 10% EUL on the growth performance and rumen fermentation characteristics of sheep. This study subsequently sought to characterize the rumen active microbial community and its functional differences through metatranscriptomics. A more in-depth exploration of the core microbial groups and key metabolic pathways closely related to production performance is necessary to provide a theoretical basis and mechanism analysis for the application of EUL as a functional roughage.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets, and experimental design

This trial was performed at Longxupo Farming and Animal Husbandry Company (Luoyang, Henan, China). The animal experimental procedure was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences No:IAS − 2018 − 17. Throughout the experimental period, animal welfare and health status were monitored daily by trained veterinarians, ensuring prompt intervention if any signs of distress or illness occurred. Twenty-one nonpregnant healthy female Hu sheep (body weight: 38.35 ± 2.77 kg; age: 5–6 months) were selected for the experiment. After a 14-day adaptation period, all sheep were housed in individual pens and randomly assigned to three groups (seven in each group) and fed three different diets that differed in NDF content (CON 45.17% DM, Diet 1 43.20% DM, and Diet 2 41.24% DM) on dry matter basis. The total mixed rations (TMR) were composed primarily of corn silage, peanut vine, EUL, corn, soybean meal, and wheat brans. In this study, only the leaves of Eucommia ulmoides (EUL) were utilized, which were harvested during the autumn season to ensure optimal accumulation of secondary metabolites. Prior to the feeding trial, chemical analysis was conducted on the EUL batch to quantify the concentrations of its primary active organic compounds. The gradients of NDF content were formed by varying amounts of peanut vine and EUL, which are locally accessible resources (Table S1). The three diets were formulated to be isoenergetic and isonitrogenous (metabolizable energy: 8.8 MJ/kg and crude protein: 13.0%). To control for the varying NDF content, the diets were strictly balanced utilizing the Nutrient Requirements of Small Ruminants: Sheep, Goats, Cervids, and New World Camelids [30]. Net Energy for gain (NEg) system. Additionally, the actual daily feed intake was empirically measured for all individual animals.

Sample collection and determination

Five sheep were randomly selected from each group and rumen content samples (100 mL each) were collected via an oral stomach tube (esophageal route). To minimize animal stress and maintain welfare, no surgical procedures for rumen fistulas or cannulas were performed, nor were any trocar punctures utilized. To eliminate the potential confounding influence of saliva, the initial 5 ml of rumen fluid was discarded each time [31]. The collected rumen contents were separately filtered through four-layer cheesecloth, and rumen liquid were aliquoted into 5 mL as described in Wang et al.[22].The samples were used for the determination of the metatranscriptome, and a 5 mL aliquot was acidified with 25% (w/v) sulfuric acid (1:4, v/v) and centrifuged (10,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min). The supernatants were stored at −20 °C for volatile fatty acid (VFA) analysis via gas chromatography (Agilent 7890 A, Agilent Inc., Palo Alto, CA) via a DB-FFAP column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm). Carrier gas: N2 at 1.0 mL/min; injector: split 20:1, 1 μL; oven program: 100 °C (1 min) — 180 °C @10°C/min (2 min) — 220 °C @20 °C/min (3 min); FID: 250 °C. VFAs (C2—C6) were quantified via external standard curves (0.1—20 mmol/L; recovery 95—105%), with each sample run in triplicate.

Metatranscriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 2 mL of rumen fluid via the RNeasy PowerMicrobiome Kit (QIAGEN) and assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN > 7.0). Ribosomal RNAs were depleted with Ribo-Zero Plus (Illumina). Libraries were prepared via a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp paired-end).

Taxonomic and functional profiles were generated using the bioBakery meta’omics workflow [32]. The raw metatranscriptome data were subjected to quality control with FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Trimmomatic v0.39 [33] (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, MINLEN:50) for trimming; KneadData v0.3 against the Ovis aries genome to remove host reads; rRNA reads were identified and removed via SortMeRNA [34]. The sequencing reads were processed with the KneadData (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/kneaddata) 0.3 quality control pipeline to filter out low-quality bases and reads of human origin.

To profile transcript expression levels, reads were aligned to predicted ORFs in each genome via Bowtie2 [35], retaining only those with a MAPQ score ≥ 35. The expression levels were calculated with CoverM (–min-read-percent-identity 0.97, –min-covered-fraction 0.95, https://github.com/wwood/CoverM), which included only genes covered by at least 5 reads. Differential expression analysis was conducted via DESeq2 v1.36.0, which utilizes empirical Bayes shrinkage for dispersion estimation to ensure robust results in small sample sizes. Significance was evaluated using the Wald test with Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (adjusted P < 0.05) strictly applied to minimize false positives [36].

Taxonomic profiling was performed with MetaPhlAn4 4.0.6 [37], which classifies the metagenomic reads to taxonomies and yields relative abundances of taxa identified in the sample based on approximately 1 million clade-specific marker genes derived from 17,000 microbial genomes (corresponding to > 13,500 bacterial and archaeal). To strictly control for false positives and reduce the multiple-testing burden, we excluded microbial species that did not surpass a minimum prevalence (present in > 50% of samples within at least one dietary group) and a relative abundance threshold of 0.01%. This approach reflects the actively expressed microbial community rather than total DNA‐based diversity. Metatranscriptomes were functionally profiled via HUMAnN3 [38]. Briefly, for each sample, taxonomic profiling identified detectable organisms, and reads were recruited to sample-specific pangenomes containing all gene families from detected microorganisms via Bowtie2 [39]. Unmapped reads were aligned against UniRef90 (e-value ≤ 1e-5) [40] via DIAMOND translated search. Additionally, the mapping of proteins to enzyme functions utilized the SWISS-PROT database [33, 41], and regrouped into carbohydrate-active enzymes categories [42].

Network analysis

WGCNA is a systemic biological technique for analyzing the correlation patterns among genes across a group of special samples [43]. When the pairwise correlation matrix was calculated, the adjacency matrix was calculated by increasing the correlation matrix. The dendrogram branches​(gene modules) was constructed according to average hierarchical linkage clustering. Finally, the gene module was classified statistically, and these highly similar modules were merged. The correlations of the gene modules with the rumen fermentation indices and bacteria were determined via WGCNA.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA v1.71) was conducted in R; the soft threshold β = 6 (scale-free R2 ≥ 0.85), the minimum module size = 30, and the merged cut height = 0.25. Module eigengenes were correlated (biweight midcorrelation) with fermentation parameters and microbial abundances, with significance at p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

All phenotypic data were tested for normality (Shapiro‒Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) in SPSS v19.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD was used for comparisons; the results are presented as the means ± SD. Significance thresholds: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**). Complete ANOVA tables and post hoc results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Results

In this study, two levels of Eucommia ulmoides leaf (EUL) were incorporated into isoenergetic and isonitrogenous diets as functional roughage. Following a two-month feeding trial, rumen fermentation parameters, production performance, and metatranscriptomic sequencing of the rumen fluid were measured. The results of this study demonstrate that the incorporation of 5% EUL into the diet of ruminants enhances rumen fermentation and microbial crude protein synthesis. While total volatile fatty acid (VFA) production did not significantly increase, the mean VFA concentration was higher than that in the other groups. Metatranscriptomic analysis identified a core set of actively expressed microbial taxa that were enriched by EUL supplementation. These findings provide a novel theoretical basis for the targeted modulation of rumen microecology and the mechanism of functional roughage.

Microbial differences at multiple taxonomic levels frequently reflect specific responses to dietary treatment. These findings indicate that the genus Coprobacillus exhibited a notable response to EUL in the diet, especially in the Diet 1 group, where the abundance and activity were elevated at both the genus and species levels. This finding suggests that EUL stimulates the activity of the genus Coprobacillus.

Effects of EUL on the functional of rumen microorganisms

The present study demonstrated that rumen microbes exhibited significant functional responses to varying levels of dietary EUL supplementation. Metatranscriptomic analysis revealed clear differences in microbial composition and metabolic activity between the experimental groups. These alterations were most discernible in pivotal metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism pathways. Diet 1 resulted in a significant increase in microbial function. Although there was a decrease in the activity of carbohydrate enzymes, carbohydrate metabolic pathways were more active.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of different ratios of EUL supplementation on the functional expression of the rumen microbiota. To achieve this goal, we conducted a differential expression analysis across the three groups. Subsequent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis via KAAS (Fig. 2A) revealed similar pathway enrichment across the groups. The secondary classification pathways revealed that the effects of EUL addition were primarily concentrated in protein families associated with the following functional categories: genetic information processing, transport and catabolism, signal transduction, translation and carbohydrate metabolism. Notably, two unique pathways, namely, metabolism of other amino acids and aging, were found to be enriched in the comparison between CON and Diet 2. Furthermore, two additional pathways, cell motility and the immune system, were enriched in the DEGs between the Diet 1 and Diet 2 groups.

KEGG enrichment results across different treatments indicated that the carbohydrate metabolism pathway was most significantly affected by the addition of EUL. Moreover, the enrichment of the immune system pathway between Diet 1 and Diet 2 indicated that the proportion of EUL may influence the host immune response.

Furthermore, the COG (eggNOG, non-supervised orthologous groups) database was used for functional prediction, which revealed notable discrepancies in functional expression between the CON and treatment groups. According to COG functional annotation, the most active eggNOG functional category in the Diet 1 group was coenzyme transport and metabolism, whereas in the Diet 2 group, the most active category was intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport.

In addition to the aforementioned findings, there were notable discrepancies in the microbial protein concentrations between the experimental groups. Microbial crude protein (MCP) was 32.7% higher in Diet 1 (4.46 ± 0.51 g/L) than in CON (3.36 ± 0.55 g/L; P = 0.015), whereas Diet 2 (3.98 ± 0.16 g/L) showed no significant difference (Supplementary Table S2).. Furthermore, a comparison of the GH5_22 and GH13_24 samples from the Diet2 group with those from the control group revealed a statistically significant difference (174.75 ± 76.05 vs. 59.57 ± 41.42, P < 0.05; Fig. 3C) and an extremely significant difference (31.23 ± 14.11 vs. 4.40 ± 3.03, P < 0.01; Fig. 3C). Compared with that in the Diet1 group, the level of GH13_24 was significantly greater (31.23 ± 14.11 vs. 10.57 ± 9.32, P < 0.05; Fig. 3D).

In general, EUL addition resulted in significant functional alterations in the rumen microbiota, which played a crucial role in enhancing host metabolic efficiency and optimizing production performance. However, excessive addition may disrupt this balance and negatively impact productivity, highlighting the importance of determining the optimal addition ratio for practical applications.

This analysis allowed the identification of genes that were most closely associated with rumen metabolic parameters. We selected the top 100 key genes in the gene network from the red module, sorted the top 10 microorganisms by expression, and used the degree method to select nine hub microorganisms (Fig. 4E; two genes were annotated repeatedly). We then selected the top five microorganisms in terms of both centrality and expression (Fig. 4F). The WGCNA results revealed that these microorganisms are involved in propionic acid production, fiber decomposition, and nitrogen metabolism. Feeding EUL resulted in different expression patterns of these genes among the three groups, with the highest expression observed in the Diet 1 group, followed by the Diet 2 group, whereas the CON group presented the lowest expression (Fig. 4B, C). Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways (Fig. 4D) such as carbon fixation in prokaryotes, propanoate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and pyruvate metabolism.

This study used WGCNA to identify modules and genes that strongly correlation with host phenotypes. The genes in question demonstrated substantial functional enrichment in pivotal metabolic pathways, including carbon fixation, propanoate metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and amino acid metabolism. These pathways are strongly associated with host energy metabolism, the immune response, and lipid metabolism, providing further insight into the impact of feeding EUL on host production traits.

In conclusion, the incorporation of EUL into the diet altered the expression patterns of rumen microbes, which in turn affected host metabolic functions. An optimal addition level, such as 5% in Diet 1, promotes a synergistic effect between microbial and host metabolism, thereby improving production performance. Nevertheless, excess addition may disrupt this equilibrium, resulting in a reduction in production efficiency.

Discussion

The current study demonstrated that production performance was significantly influenced by the amount of 5% EUL in the diet compared with that in the CON group. Rumen microbes play a critical role in host metabolism [44]. The application of functional roughage may exert a beneficial synergistic effect on both microbial and host metabolism. The increase in beneficial bacterial activity in the rumen can increase fiber conversion, increase the energy available for host metabolism and ultimately influence the production performance of host [45]. Metatranscriptomics offers a more comprehensive perspective by identifying transcriptionally active microbial populations and their associated metabolic pathways for researchers, which extends the scope of genetic information available through metagenomic analyses [46]. As a powerful tool, metatranscriptomics elucidate the subtle but clinically significant differences often emerge between different active bacterial populations in individuals [47, 48].

Most studies reported that the increase in VFAs is dominated by variations in microbial communities, and similar results were obtained in this study [49]. Compared with those in the other two groups, the abundance of Coprobacillus in the Diet 1 group was notably greater, Coprobacillus and Coprobacillus sp. CAG:826 were among the top 25 most abundant bacteria, showing a higher degree of enrichment in the Diet 1 group than in the other two groups. There were few reports on the function of Coprobacillus.. In the intestine, Coprobacillus can ferment glucose to produce lactic acid [50]. Moreover, As a key butyrate producer, Coprobacillus engages in cross-feeding with Anaerostipes, Roseburia, and Bifidobacterium to maintain colonic butyrate levels [51]. This genus ​participates in butyrogenesis and complex lipid metabolism within microbial ecosystems [52, 53]. The inclusion of EUL at all tested levels resulted in a numerical increase in total VFA concentrations compared with those in the CON group, although the differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.290). In the Diet 1 group, the concurrent enrichment of Coprobacillus and Clostridium was potentially associated with the numerical trend of increased ruminal butyrate, although butyrate concentrations did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.121). Notably, while the abundance of Coprobacillus varied between the Diet 1 and Diet 2 groups, the Diet 2 group presented the highest butyrate concentration among the three groups. Therefore, the extensive metabolic involvement of Coprobacillus may accelerate host metabolic processes, which in turn underlies the superior production performance of Diet 1 relative to the other treatments. Despite the reduction in dietary fiber content caused by EUL addition, total and individual VFA syntheses were maintained and even showed a numerical increase; however, this increase was ultimately constrained by the overall fiber level and the relative abundance of Coprobacillus, providing a plausible explanation for the plateau in VFA concentration observed in the Diet 2 group.

Low-abundance microbes may exert a limited influence, and impact of high-abundance microbes is typically more pronounced [54]. Low-abundance microbes are frequently considered byproducts of the symbiotic effects associated with the core high-expressing microbiota. Accordingly, the present study focused on the distinctions between highly expressed microbial communities in the context of experimental treatments. In line with the metatranscriptomic annotation results of the present study, we identified DEGs across the three groups following the addition of EUL. After feeding EUL, we found that metabolic functions were particularly active in carbohydrate metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, and nucleotide metabolism. Differential gene pathway enrichment between CON and Diet 1 revealed that nucleotide metabolism was directly linked to cellular homeostasis. It was essential for a number of physiological processes, including carbohydrate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, nucleotide biosynthesis, and signal transduction. Enhanced purine and pyrimidine metabolism facilitates the synthesis of thiamine pyrophosphate within cells and the oxidation of pyruvate in mitochondria. The results of the enrichment analysis indicated that feeding EUL enhanced the metabolic pathways of carbohydrates, nucleotides, and amino acids. Pyruvate is a pivotal intermediate in bacterial carbohydrate metabolism, is derived from a vast array of six-, five-, and four-carbon compounds, and is intricately involved in metabolic or synthetic reactions. Glucose serves as the primary carbohydrate source for bacterial carbon metabolism, and is converted into pyruvate through four distinct pathways. However, the precise mechanisms through which EUL affects metabolism remain to be elucidated.

The combination of functional annotations from the COG database revealed that the most active EggNOG functional category for Diet 1 was coenzyme transport and metabolism, whereas for Diet 2, the most active eggNOG functional category was intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport. A substantial body of evidence indicated that the tricarboxylic acid cycle, nutrient oxidation, histone acetylation, and the synthesis of lipids, polysaccharides, and heme all require the cofactor coenzyme A (CoA) [55]. The results from the functional enrichment analysis revealed that the functional modules associated with coenzyme transport and metabolism were increased in the Diet 1 treatment group compared with those in the other two groups. The enrichment of functional modules in the Diet 2 group suggested that high doses of EUL may enhance the efficiency of immune responses. Therefore, maintaining a balance in the amount of addition might be a better approach for sheep. This was accompanied by alterations in microbial communities, which in turn led to changes in the production performance of the system. This trend is also evident in carbohydrate enzyme activity, which is closely related to diet composition. Feeding EUL in diet resulted in a slight reduction in the NDF content of the diet, which led to a decline in carbohydrate enzyme activity in the Diet 1 group. However, in the Diet 2 group, the expression of carbohydrate enzymes increased in comparison with that in the Diet 1 group, which indicated that NDF content was not the unique factor limiting carbohydrate enzyme activity. A follow-up study on human dietary habits demonstrated that a high-fiber diet can influence carbohydrate enzyme activity [56].

Short-chain fatty acids are the primary end products of gut microbial fermentation and serve as proxies for microbial metabolic activity [57]. Additionally, differences in genomic content at the species and strain levels, along with other dietary components, can also impact microbial abundance in the rumen. The introduction of new substances may alter the microbiome and the associated CAZyme composition over different time scales [55].

Cellobiohydrolase modules (CBMs) have been demonstrated to increase catalytic efficiency by strengthening enzyme–substrate interactions. In particular, GH43_17—a subtype that targets hemicellulose-to-xylose conversion—likely acts synergistically with CBM38 to promote hemicellulose degradation. This elevated CAZyme activity corresponds with the numerical trend of higher volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentrations observed in the Diet 1 group, suggesting that low-dose EUL supplementation facilitates fiber breakdown and optimizes host energy metabolism.

Conversely, Diet2 exhibited a distinct enrichment of GH13_24, a constituent of the GH13 family that is predominantly responsible for starch hydrolysis and remodeling. This finding indicates that high-dose EUL incorporation modifies microbial carbohydrate metabolism, favoring starch polysaccharide utilization. This observation is consistent with the butyrate-dominant VFA profile measured in Diet2 and reflects a divergent host metabolic response to EUL dosage.

Furthermore, lower levels of feeding EUL improve the roughage matrix by introducing plant acids, flavonoids, and other antioxidants, which may potentially influence lipid metabolism [58], thereby providing additional energy substrates for peripheral tissues [59].

Network analysis was utilized to identify microorganisms associated with rumen fermentation parameters, which may serve as potential markers for efficient rumen fermentation and enhanced host metabolism. Hub were identified on the basis of degree of enrichment, and functional enrichment analysis was conducted through WGCNA [43]. The significantly enriched pathways included carbon fixation in prokaryotes, propanoate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, pyruvate metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, and amino acid-related enzymes. These pathways are closely related to metabolism by overlapping expression levels and network centrality. We identified Bacteroidales bacterium 36–12, Porphyromonas sp. COT-052 OH4946, Paludibacter propionicigene, Porphyromonadaceae bacterium H1, and Paludibacter sp. 47–17 as core species. These microorganisms were highly expressed not only within the association module but also within the microbial network. Thus, we propose that these five populations play critical roles in host metabolism, influencing VFA concentrations and metabolic functions, ultimately enhancing host production performance.

These findings demonstrated that the addition of EUL was closely linked to microbial expression. This study highlighted the importance of incorporating functional roughage into a balanced diet with appropriate levels of NDF and it hsowed a potential relationship between rumen microbial expression and host metabolism. Five key microorganisms at the species level were identified as potential marker microorganisms. Identifying microorganisms closely associated with host production performance is essential for advancing sustainable and efficient animal husbandry. The intricate relationship between the host and its microbiome has remained the central focus of microbiological research. While strict statistical corrections (FDR), such as the cross-validation of WGCNA hubs (Supplementary Table S3) and stringent prevalence filters were applied in this study to control for false positives, the relatively small sample size (n = 5 per group) in our metatranscriptomic and WGCNA analyses remains a limitation. Future large-scale, multifactorial in vivo studies are needed to independently validate these expression profiles and further unravel how microorganisms influence host metabolism. Such insights would pave the way for strategies that regulate microbial communities to directly enhance host performance and achieve greater efficiency in animal production systems.

Conclusions

This study indicated that the inclusion of EUL in diet positively influenced feed intake, with moderate supplementation enhancing feed efficiency and reducing costs. By investigating the impact of EUL on microbial expression, we observed that the expression of specific microorganisms, such as Bacteroidales bacterium 36–12, Porphyromonas sp. COT-052 OH4946, Paludibacter propionicigene, Porphyromonadaceae bacterium H1, and Paludibacter sp. 47–17, was increased, which influenced the host's glucose, amino acid, and lipid metabolism and facilitated complex crosstalk between the rumen microbiome and the host. The identification of five key microorganisms at the species level provides valuable insight for further exploration and potential applications in the utilization of EUL as a functional roughage.
