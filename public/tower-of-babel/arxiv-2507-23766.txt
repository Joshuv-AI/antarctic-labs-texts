Twisted embeddings of tori have small extrinsic systole
Sahana Vasudevan

Abstract
. We prove a type of systolic inequality for embeddings of T 2 T^{2} in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . In particular, a highly twisted T 2 T^{2} embedded in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} must contain a non-contractible loop of small ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} -diameter.



1.
Introduction

Let
T
be a
2
-dimensional torus equipped with a Riemannian metric. Loewner’s systolic inequality answers the question: what conditions on the geometry of the metric does the topology of the torus force? It states:

Theorem 1.1

(Loewner, 1949)
.

There exists a non-contractible closed curve
\gamma
on
T
such that

\length(\gamma)\leq 2^{1/2}3^{-1/4}\Area(T)^{1/2}.

The systole of
T
is the length of the smallest non-contractible closed curve on
T
. Alternatively, it is the diameter of the smallest ball in
T
that contains a non-contractible curve.
Theorem

1.1
gives an upper bound on the systole.

In this paper, we consider a
2
-dimensional torus
T
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Again, we may ask the question: what geometric conditions does the topology of the embedding force? This question is connected to knot distortion, another invariant in metric geometry. Our main result is an upper bound on the diameter of the smallest ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that contains a non-contractible curve on
T
. The upper bound depends only on the metric on
T
and the topology of the embedding, and in some cases, is much smaller than the systole of
T
.

In order to state our main result, let us describe the topology of the embedding in more detail. The complement of the torus,
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
, has two connected components. Let
u\in H_{1}(T,\mathbb{Z})
be a nonzero primitive homology class that is trivial in one of the connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
. Let
v\in H_{1}(T,\mathbb{Z})
be a nonzero primitive homology class that is trivial in the other connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
.

Definition 1.2
.

The homological systole with respect to
u
is

\textstyle\sys_{u}(T)=\displaystyle\inf_{[\gamma]=u}\length(\gamma),

where
\gamma
ranges over closed curves on
T
representing
u
.

Our main result is the following inequality:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a closed curve
\gamma
on
T
representing
v
such that

\textstyle\diam_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\gamma\lesssim\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}\Area(T)^{2/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1}\Area(T),

with constant independent of
T
.

Note that the inequality is invariant under scaling of
T
. Furthermore,
T
need not be unknottedly embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In any case, there are always two homology classes on
T
that are trivial in the two components of the complement. Note also that the hypotheses regarding
u
and
v
are symmetric.

Theorem

1.3
is most interesting when
\sys_{u}(T)
is large compared to
\Area(T)
. In this case, the conclusion of the theorem is that a small ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
contains a non-contractible curve on
T
, even if the systole of
T
is not necessarily small. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 1.4
.

The extrinsic systole of
T
, denoted
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)
, is the diameter of the smallest ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that contains a non-contractible curve on
T
.

Then
Theorem

1.3
gives an upper bound on
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)
in terms of intrinsic geometric quantities on
T
. As a special case, we have:

Example 1.5
.

Let
T=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be the flat torus equipped with the Euclidean metric. Embed
T
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
unknottedly and
C^{1}
-isometrically, but in the following twisted way. Let
u
and
v
be homology classes in
H_{1}(T)
trivial in the homology of the two components of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
, respectively. Note that closed curves on
T
are given by points on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We embed
T
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
so that under this identification,

\begin{cases}(1,0)=u+nv\\
(0,1)=v,\end{cases}

for a positive integer
n
. Geometrically, such an embedding may be constructed by taking a standard cylinder of height
1
and circumference
1
, and twisting one end
n
times before gluing it to the other end, as shown in Figure 1. By
Theorem

1.3
,
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)\lesssim n^{-1/3}
.

Figure 1
.

The inequality in
Theorem

1.3
is asymptotically sharp as
\sys_{u}(T)\to\infty
. We include a sharp example for it in
Section

A.1
. On the other hand, it is not clear if the bound
n^{-1/3}
in
Example

1.5
is asymptotically sharp, over all
n
-twisted embeddings of the flat torus. The flat torus
T=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
admits an
n
-twisted
1
-Lipschitz map to a standard torus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
scaled by
\sim n^{-1}
. By the Nash isometric embedding theorem, we may perturb this map in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of the standard torus so that it is a
C^{1}
-isometry. The resulting embedding of
T
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has extrinsic systole
\sim n^{-1}
. It is unknown if there is another embedding of
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with the prescribed topology whose extrinsic systole is larger. (In
Section

A.3
, we describe a generalization of this question.)

1.1.
Connection to knot distortion

In
[
Gro83
]
, Gromov introduced the notion of distortion
\dist(K)
associated to a knot
K
. Distortion is an invariant of
K
that measures the infimum of the bi-Lipschitz constant of embeddings of
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, over all such embeddings. It is difficult to prove lower bounds for distortion, even for specific knots. In
[
Par11
]
, Pardon proved that torus knots
T_{p,q}
satisfy that
\dist(T_{p,q})
is at least
\min\{p,q\}
, asymptotically, leaving open whether
\dist(T_{2,q})\to\infty
as
q\to\infty
.

Pardon’s method of proof was to actually study a torus that
T_{p,q}
lies on. A slight modification of the proof would give an extrinsic systolic inequality for any such torus. This result would say that if
\sys_{u}(T)
and
\sys_{v}(T)
are both large compared to
\Area(T)
, then
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)
is small. (The precise statement and proof are included in
Section

A.2
.) Given this background, if we expect
\dist(T_{2,q})\to\infty
as
q\to\infty
, it is natural to ask whether just
\sys_{u}(T)
being large compared to
\Area(T)
implies that
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)
is small, which is the conclusion of
Theorem

1.3
.

1.2.
Ideas in the proof of
Theorem

1.3

Since the inequality is invariant under scaling of
T
, we may assume
\Area(T)=1
. The bulk of the theorem is the case wherein
\sys_{u}(T)
is large. In this case, we want to find a curve on
T
representing
v
with small
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-diameter. We will prove the contrapositive: if no such curve exists, then
\sys_{u}(T)
has an upper bound.

The first step is to state a variant of
Theorem

1.3
involving the intersection of
T
and the CW complex associated to a cubical lattice. This variant makes it easier to track topologically if
T
contains a curve representing
v
with small
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-diameter. Roughly speaking, the variant states that if
T
intersects the skeleta of the cubical lattice in a bounded way, and
T
has no curve representing
v
contained in a cube, then
\sys_{u}(T)
also has an upper bound. In
Section

2
, we state this variant and prove
Theorem

1.3
assuming it. To understand the intersection between
T
and the cubical lattice, we prove a type of isoperimetric inequality on the
2
-skeleton of the lattice in
Section

3
. Finally, we prove the variant: the assumption that
T
has no curve representing
v
in any cube means that
T
does have a curve representing some
au+bv
(with
a\neq 0
), contained in the
2
-skeleton of the cubical lattice. The isoperimetric inequality allows us to extract a representative of
u
with bounded length. We work out the details of this argument in
Section

4
.

1.3.
Comments and references

Other variants of the systole and generalizations of
Theorem

1.1
are the subject of study in systolic geometry. Gromov’s systolic inequality in
[
Gro83
]
generalizes
Theorem

1.1
to higher dimensional essential manifolds (in particular, higher dimension tori). Finer results on the systolic inequality for surfaces are found in
[
Gro96
]
,
[
BS10
]
and
[
BPS12
]
. See
[
Gut11
]
for a survey and more references.

2.
A variant of
Theorem

1.3

In this section, we state a variant of
Theorem

1.3
, and prove that the theorem follows as a consequence of the variant.

Let
X
be the infinite CW
3
-complex given by a cubical lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
be the
1
-skeleton and
2
-skeleton of
X
, respectively. All homology will be with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T\subset X\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an embedded torus, in general position with the CW structure of
X
. Let
u
and
v
be primitive homology classes in
H_{1}(T)
that are trivial in the two components of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
, respectively. Suppose

\textstyle\sys_{u}(T)\geq 20\cdot(|X^{1}\cap T|+1)\cdot\length(X^{2}\cap T).

Then there exists a
3
-cell of
X
containing a representative of
v
.

2.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.3

Perturb
T
infinitesimally so that it is smooth. Scale
T
so that
\Area(T)=1
, noting that the inequality in
Theorem

1.3
is invariant under scaling. We wish to show that there exists
\gamma
, representing the homology class
v
, such that

\textstyle\diam_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\gamma\lesssim\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1}.

Let

m=\frac{(\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1})^{-1}}{1000}.

Let
X
be the
m^{-1}
-side length cubical lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a translation of
T
, denoted
T+s
, in general position with the CW structure on
X
, satisfying

\length(X^{2}\cap(T+s))\leq 9m

and

|X^{1}\cap(T+s)|\leq 9m^{2}.

Proof.

Note that
X^{2}\cap(T+s)
and
X^{1}\cap(T+s)
only depend on the image of
s
under the map

\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}/m^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

We claim,

(1)

\int_{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}}\length(X^{2}\cap(T+s))ds\leq 3m^{-2}.

To see this, note that
X^{2}
is the union of parallel planes in three directions. Denote by
X^{2}_{x}
,
X^{2}_{y}
and
X^{2}_{z}
the union of the planes in
X
perpendicular to the
x
direction,
y
direction and
z
direction, respectively. Since
\Area(T)=1
,

\int_{s=(0,s_{y},s_{z})}^{(m^{-1},s_{y},s_{z})}\length{(X^{2}_{x}\cap(T+s))}ds_{x}\leq 1.

As
T
is translated in the
y
or
z
direction,
X^{2}_{x}\cap(T+s)
does not change. Hence

\int_{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}}\length(X^{2}_{x}\cap(T+s))ds\leq m^{-2}.

Similar equalities hold for the
y
and
z
directions. Adding these equalities,
Eq.

1
follows.

Next, we claim,

(2)

\int_{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}}|X^{1}\cap(T+s)|ds\leq 3m^{-1}.

To see this, note that
X^{1}
is the union of parallel lines in three directions. Denote by
X^{1}_{x}
,
X^{1}_{y}
and
X^{1}_{z}
the union of the lines of
X^{1}
in the
x
direction,
y
direction and
z
direction, respectively. Then,

\int_{s=(s_{x},0,0)}^{(s_{x},m^{-1},m^{-1})}|X^{1}_{x}\cap(T+s)|ds_{y}ds_{z}\leq 1.

As
T
is translated in the
x
direction,
X^{1}_{x}\cap(T+s)
does not change. Hence

\int_{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}}|X^{1}_{x}\cap(T+s)|ds\leq m^{-1}.

Similar equalities hold for the
y
and
z
directions. Adding these equalities,
Eq.

2
follows.

By
Eq.

1
,

\Vol(\{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}|\length(X^{2}\cap(T+s))\leq 9m\})\geq 2m^{-3}/3.

By
Eq.

2
,

\Vol(\{s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}||X^{1}\cap(T+s)|\leq 9m^{2}\})\geq 2m^{-3}/3.

Therefore, some
s\in[0,m^{-1}]^{3}
satisfies the conditions of the lemma.
∎

We return to the proof of
Theorem

1.3
assuming
Theorem

2.1
. By
Lemma

2.2
, after an appropriate translation of
T
, we may assume that

\length(X\cap T)\leq 9m

and

|X_{1}\cap T|\leq 9m^{2}.

Note that the translation does not change any of the conditions in
Theorem

1.3
.

Suppose that there is no representative
\gamma
of
v
on
T
with

\textstyle\diam_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\gamma\leq 3^{1/2}m^{-1}.

Then no
3
-cell of
X
contains a representative of
v
. By
Theorem

2.1
,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sys_{u}(T)

\displaystyle\leq 20\cdot(|X_{1}\cap T|+1)\cdot\length(X_{2}\cap T)

\displaystyle\leq 1620m^{3}+180m

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1})^{-3}}{617283}+\frac{(\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1})^{-1}}{4}

\displaystyle<\textstyle\sys_{u}(T)

which is a contradiction. Therefore, there is a representative
\gamma
of
v
on
T
with

\displaystyle\textstyle\diam_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\gamma

\displaystyle\leq 3^{1/2}m^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq 3^{1/2}\cdot 1000\cdot\textstyle(\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}+\sys_{u}(T)^{-1}),

completing the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

2.2.
Roadmap to prove
Theorem

2.1

Assume the contrary, that no
3
-cell of
X
contains a representative of
v
. We first prove that this implies that the
2
-skeleton
X^{2}
contains a representative of
u
. While this representative may not be embedded, if
|X^{1}\cap T|
is small, we prove that its multiplicity is bounded. So if
\length(T\cap X^{2})
is also small, then
\sys_{u}(T)
must be bounded above, which gives a contradiction.

Let us describe the first step of the proof in slightly more detail. We will prove that if no
3
-cell of
X
contains a representative of
v
, then
X^{2}
contains an embedded representative of some
au+bv
with
a
nonzero. Since
H_{1}(X^{2})=0
, this curve may be filled by a
2
-chain on
X^{2}
. Because
u
and
v
are the only homology classes which are trivial in the complement of
T
, the
2
-chain must actually contain a representative of
u
. To bound the multiplicity of the resulting representative, we bound the multiplicity of the
2
-chain. To this end, in
Section

3
, we prove a type of isoperimetric inequality on
X^{2}
. Then in
Section

4
, we prove
Theorem

2.1
by implementing the strategy above.

3.
An isoperimetric inequality on
X^{2}

The goal of this section is: given an embedded curve on
X^{2}
, we find a
2
-chain on
X^{2}
of bounded multiplicity filling the curve. To define the notion of multiplicity precisely, we work with cellular chains.

3.1.
Cellular chains associated to arcs and curves

Let
Y
be a CW complex. A cellular
1
-chain is a linear combination of
1
-cells. We associate a cellular chain to an arc or curve on the
1
-skeleton of
Y
. In the following,
Y
may be an infinite CW complex, but arcs and curves on
Y
are assumed to be finite.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a
1
-cell of
Y
. The quotient map

q_{\sigma}:Y^{1}\to Y^{1}/(Y^{1}-\sigma)\simeq S^{1}

is formed by collapsing
Y^{1}-\sigma
to a point.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to Y^{1}
be an arc (or
\gamma:S^{1}\to Y^{1}
a closed curve) in the
1
-skeleton of
Y
with
\partial\gamma\subset Y^{0}
. (If
\gamma
is a closed curve, we consider it as an arc with both endpoints the same
0
-cell.) For each
1
-cell
\sigma
,
q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma
is a curve on
Y^{1}/(Y^{1}-\sigma)\simeq S^{1}
with endpoints at the same point. We denote by
\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)
the element of
\pi_{1}(S^{1})\simeq\mathbb{Z}
determined by
q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma
. When
\gamma
is a closed curve,

\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)=\deg(q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma).

In this case,
\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)
is independent of choice of basepoint on
\gamma
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\gamma
be an arc or closed curve on
Y^{1}
. The cellular
1
-chain associated to
\gamma
is

\ch(\gamma)=\sum_{\sigma\text{ is a }1\text{-cell in }Y^{1}}\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)\sigma.

3.2.
Basic properties of associated chains

Associated chains add when arcs concatenate.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\gamma
be a concatenation of arcs
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in
Y^{1}
with boundary in
Y^{0}
. Then

\ch(\gamma)=\ch(\gamma_{1})+\ch(\gamma_{2}).

Proof.

It suffices to show that for all
1
-cells
\sigma
,
\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)=\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma_{1})+\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma_{2})
. This is true since
q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma
is the product of
q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma_{1}
and
q_{\sigma}\circ\gamma_{2}
in
\pi_{1}(Y^{1}/(Y^{1}-\sigma))
.
∎

The space of cellular
1
-chains on
Y
is identified with
H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0})
. Under this identification, associated chains are simply the pushforward of a fundamental class.

Lemma 3.5
.

For an arc
\gamma
, the associated chain
\ch(\gamma)
is the pushforward of the fundamental class of
[0,1]
under the map

\gamma_{*}:H_{1}([0,1],\partial[0,1])\to H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0}).

For a closed curve
\gamma
, the associated chain
\ch(\gamma)
is the pushforward of the fundamental class of
S^{1}
under the composition

H_{1}(S^{1})\overset{\gamma_{*}}{\to}H_{1}(Y^{1})\to H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0}).

Proof.

There is an identification

H_{1}([0,1],\partial[0,1])\simeq H_{1}(S^{1})

and

H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0})\simeq H_{1}(Y^{1}/Y^{0})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{|\text{1-cells of Y}|}.

The space
Y^{1}/Y^{0}
is a wedge of circles. Let

q:(Y^{1},Y^{0})\to(Y^{1}/Y^{0},\{\text{pt}\})

be the quotient map. An curve
\gamma
on
Y^{1}
, or an arc
\gamma
on
Y^{1}
with
\partial\gamma\subset Y^{0}
, is sent to a closed curve
q(\gamma)
on
Y^{1}/Y^{0}
.

In either case of whether
\gamma
is an arc or a closed curve,
q(\gamma)
is a closed curve on
Y^{1}/Y^{0}
. Viewing
q(\gamma):S^{1}\to Y^{1}/Y^{0}
as a map, the pushforward of the relevant fundamental class in the lemma statement is the pushforward of the fundamental class of
S^{1}
with respect to map induced by
q(\gamma)
on homology. This gives a element
H_{1}(Y^{1}/Y^{0})
.

Given a
1
-cell
\sigma
of
Y
, the
\sigma
-coefficient of this element may be obtained by further quotienting to
Y^{1}/(Y^{1}-\sigma)
and taking its image in homology. This recovers
\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)
from
Definition

3.2
.
∎

Associated chains are also natural with respect to cellular maps.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
f:Y\to Z
is a cellular map and
\gamma:S^{1}\to Y^{1}
is a closed curve, then

f_{*}\ch(\gamma)=\ch(f(\gamma)).

Proof.

Since
f
is cellular,
f:(Y_{1},Y_{0})\to(Z_{1},Z_{0})
. So we have
f_{*}:H_{1}(Y_{1},Y_{0})\to H_{1}(Z_{1},Z_{0})
. The lemma follows from
Lemma

3.5
and functoriality of homology.
∎

The homology class of a cellular chain associated to a closed curve
\gamma
also agrees with the singular homology class
[\gamma]
the curve determines.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\gamma
be a closed curve in
Y^{1}
. The cellular chain
\ch(\gamma)
is a
1
-cycle, and the corresponding cellular homology class
[\ch(\gamma)]
is identified with
[\gamma]
under the isomorphism between cellular homology and singular homology.

Proof.

Inside
H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0})
, the space of cycles is canonically isomorphic to
H_{1}(Y^{1})
. This isomorphism is induced by the inclusion map
j_{*}:H_{1}(Y^{1})\to H_{1}(Y^{1},Y^{0})
(see
[
Hat02
, Theorem 2.35]
). By
Lemma

3.5
,
\ch(\gamma)=j_{*}([\gamma])
(in particular, it lies in
H_{1}(Y^{1})
).

To build the isomorphism

H_{1}(Y)\simeq H_{1}^{\cell}(Y),

we have a long exact sequence

H_{2}(Y,Y^{1})\overset{\delta}{\to}H_{1}(Y^{1})\to H_{1}(Y)\to H_{1}(Y,Y^{1})\simeq 0

which identifies
H_{1}(Y)\simeq H_{1}(Y^{1})/\im(\delta)
. Because
\im(\delta)
is identified with the boundaries in
H_{1}(Y^{1})
, we obtain an isomorphism between
H_{1}(Y)
and
H_{1}^{\cell}(Y)
. This isomorphism identifies the singular chain
[\gamma]
with the cellular chain
j_{*}([\gamma])
.
∎

3.3.
Norms of cellular chains

In this section, we define the
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
norms on spaces of cellular chains.

Definition 3.8
.

Let

\alpha=\sum_{\sigma\text{ is a }1\text{-cell in }Y}c_{\sigma}\sigma

be a cellular
2
-chain. The
L_{1}
-norm of
\alpha
is

\|\alpha\|_{1}=\sum_{\sigma\text{ is a }1\text{-cell in }Y}|c_{\sigma}|.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\alpha
be a cellular
1
-cycle on
Y
. There exist embedded curves

\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{k}:S^{1}\to Y^{1}

such that

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ch(\gamma_{i})

and

\|\alpha\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|\ch(\gamma_{i})\|_{1}.

Proof.

We do induction on
\|\alpha\|_{1}
. Let

\alpha=\sum_{\sigma\text{ is a }1\text{-cell in }Y}c_{\sigma}\sigma.

View
Y^{1}
as a graph with the
0
-cells as vertices and
1
-cells as edges. Let
\sigma_{1}
be a
1
-cell in
Y
with
c_{\sigma_{1}}\neq 0
. Suppose
c_{\sigma_{1}}>0
. If
\partial\sigma_{1}=0
, then
\sigma_{1}
is a closed curve. Reducing
|c_{\sigma_{1}}|
by
1
, we have decreased
\|\alpha\|_{1}
. So assume
\partial\sigma_{1}=x_{1}-x_{0}
. Since
\partial\alpha=0
, there exists a
1
-cell
\sigma_{2}
such that

\partial\sign(c_{\sigma_{2}})\sigma_{2}=x_{2}-x_{1}

for some vertex
x_{2}
. If
x_{2}=x_{0}
, the edges
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
produce a closed curve, and we again eliminate the curve and reduce
\|\alpha\|_{1}
. Otherwise, since
\partial\alpha=0
, there exists a
1
-cell
\sigma_{3}
such that

\partial\sign(c_{\sigma_{3}})\sigma_{3}=x_{3}-x_{2}

for some vertex
x_{3}
. Continuing this process, we produce a string of
1
-cells whose endpoint vertex is a vertex already reached. This produces a closed curve
\gamma
we may eliminate to reduce
\|\alpha\|_{1}
. By construction,

\|\alpha\|_{1}=\|\alpha-\ch(\gamma)\|_{1}+\|\ch(\gamma)\|_{1}.

A.1.
A sharp example for
Theorem

1.3

In this section, we describe an example due to Berdnikov, which shows that
Theorem

1.3
is asymptotically sharp as
\sys_{u}(T)\to\infty
.

Let
\beta
be a torus knot
T_{n,n-1}
of length
\sim n
lying on a standard torus

\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-2)^{2}+z^{2}=1\}

\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
U
be the unit normal disk bundle of the torus restricted to
\beta
, so that
U
is a knotted annulus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Push

infinitesimally along its normal bundle, keeping
\partial U
fixed, to get an isotopic copy

. The interior of
U^{\prime}
does not intersect
U
, and
\partial U^{\prime}=\partial U
. Let

, so that

is a knotted torus embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
be the two connected components of
\partial U
. They are isotopic curves on
T
.

Let the interior of
T
(denoted
\interior(T)
) be the bounded connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
. Let
v
be the primitive homology class that is trivial in
\interior(T)
. Then
v
is represented by a curve
\gamma
lying in an infinitesimal neighborhood of a unit normal vector in

. In particular,
\diam_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\gamma)\sim 1
.

Let the exterior of
T
(denoted
\exterior(T)
) be the unbounded connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
. Let
u
be the primitive homology class of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
that is trivial in
\exterior(T)
. We construct

explicitly as follows. Let
w
be the homology class in
H_{1}(T)
represented by
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
. Note that
v
and
w
are linearly independent. Now, we express
u
in terms of
v
and
w
. Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersed disk with
\partial D=\zeta_{1}
. Isotope
D
so that it intersects
T
and
\zeta_{2}
transversely. Then
D\cap\exterior(T)
and
D\cap\interior(T)
are immersed submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The boundary of

represents a homology class

, while the boundary of

represents a homology class

. By construction,

. Moreover,

is a multiple of

since it must be trivial in the homology of

. The multiplicity of

is the linking number of
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
, which is

. So

. By construction,

. Furthermore,

Now,

, while
\sys_{v}(T)\sim 1
. Hence,
\sys_{u}(T)\sim n(n-1)
.

On the other hand,
\Area(T)\sim\length(\beta)\sim n
. Therefore, in this example,

\textstyle\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)\sim\sys_{u}(T)^{-1/3}\Area(T)^{2/3}.

whose intersection with
T
is bounded. Without loss of generality, assume
z
is the long dimension of
Q^{\prime}
. Let
P_{s}
be the intersection of the plane
z=s
with the
Q^{\prime}
. Again by our area normalization,

\int_{-r_{0}/10}^{r_{0}/10}\length(P_{s}\cap T)ds\leq 1.

Therefore, for some
s\in[-r_{0}/10,r_{0}/10]
,

|P_{s}\cap\beta|\leq 5r_{0}^{-1}.

As always, we choose
P_{s}
so that it intersects
T
transversely.

The plane
P_{s}
divides
Q^{\prime}
into two boxes, which we label
Q^{\prime}_{1}
and
Q^{\prime}_{2}
. Apriori,
Q^{\prime}_{1}
and
Q^{\prime}_{2}
may not be boxes of any scale, as they may have the wrong ratio of dimensions. However, both
Q^{\prime}_{1}
and
Q^{\prime}_{2}
are contained in boxes of scale
(2/3)r_{0}
. Since
Q^{\prime}
contains
Q
, it contains representatives of both
u
and
v
in
H_{1}(T)
. Thus,

\genus(Q^{\prime}\cap T)=1.

Since
Q
was assumed to be the smallest box containing representatives of
u
and
v
,

\genus(Q^{\prime}_{1}\cap T)=\genus(Q^{\prime}_{2}\cap T)=0.

Let
S_{t}
be the
1
-parameter family of spheres, starting from
S_{0}=Q^{\prime}
and doing surgery along
P_{s}
, so that
S_{1}
is a disjoint union of spheres. Since

\length((Q^{\prime}\cup P_{s})\cap T)\leq 10r_{0}^{-1},

we may assume that

\length(S_{t}\cap T)\leq 20r_{0}^{-1}

for all
0\leq t\leq 1
. Since

r_{0}^{-1}<\frac{\textstyle\min\{\sys_{u}(T),\sys_{v}(T)\}}{20},

S_{t}
only intersects
T
in inessential curves. (If it intersected
T
in an essential curve, we could find an embedded representative of
u
or
v
, which would give a contradiction to the length bound on
S_{t}\cap T
.) So we have a
1
-parameter family of spheres
S_{t}
, each intersecting
T
only in inessential curves, such that

\genus(S_{0}\cap T)=\genus(Q^{\prime}\cap T)=1

and

\genus(S_{1}\cap T)=\genus(Q^{\prime}_{1}\cap T)+\genus(Q^{\prime}_{2}\cap T)=0.

This is a contradiction by
[
Par11
, Lemma 2.3]
.
∎

A.3.
Twisted embeddings of flat tori

In this section, we describe the following question of Guth, which is a generalization of
Example

1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be the flat torus equipped with the Euclidean metric. Let

f:\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

be an unknotted
C^{1}
-isometric embedding. There is an induced map on homology given by

f_{*}:H_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to H_{1}(T).

There is a canonical identification
H_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, as follows. A point in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
determines a line to the origin, which in turn gives a closed curve and homology class on the torus. Let
u
and
v
in
H_{1}(T)
be homology classes trivial in the homology of the two components of
\mathbb{R}^{3}-T
, respectively. Then
u
and
v
also give an identification
H_{1}(T)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Under these identifications,
f_{*}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a matrix that encodes the topology of the embedding of the flat torus.

Question A.2
.

What is the best upper bound for
\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)
in terms of the matrix
f_{*}
?

To study this question, it helps to consider the matrix entries

f_{*}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

This means,

\begin{cases}f_{*}^{-1}u=(d,-b)\\
f_{*}^{-1}v=(-c,a).\end{cases}

Hence,
\sys_{u}(T)=(b^{2}+d^{2})^{1/2}
and
\sys_{v}(T)=(a^{2}+c^{2})^{1/2}
.
Theorem

1.3
implies

\textstyle\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)\lesssim\min\{(b^{2}+d^{2})^{-1/6},(a^{2}+c^{2})^{-1/6}\}

while
Theorem

A.1
implies

\textstyle\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)\lesssim\max\{(b^{2}+d^{2})^{-1/2},(a^{2}+c^{2})^{-1/2}\}.

If
b^{2}+d^{2}\lesssim(a^{2}+c^{2})^{1/3}
(or
a^{2}+c^{2}\lesssim(b^{2}+d^{2})^{1/3}
), then
Theorem

1.3
gives a better bound. Otherwise,
Theorem

A.1
gives a better bound.

Examples can be constructed using the Nash isometric embedding theorem. There is a
1
-Lipschitz map from
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to the standard torus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
scaled by
\min\{(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1/2},(c^{2}+d^{2})^{-1/2}\}
, whose topology is given by
f_{*}
. By the Nash isometric embedding theorem, we may perturb the embedding locally to make it a
C^{1}
-isometry to a torus
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so that

\textstyle\sys_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T)\sim\min\{(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1/2},(c^{2}+d^{2})^{-1/2}\}.

When
b^{2}+d^{2}\sim a^{2}+c^{2}
, the upper bound given by
Theorem

A.1
matches the lower bound above asymptotically. For other ranges of
a
,
b
,
c
and
d
, there is a gap between the upper and lower bounds.
