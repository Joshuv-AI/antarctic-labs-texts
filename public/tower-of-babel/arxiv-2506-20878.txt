Exact vacuum solution with Hopf structure in general relativity
Junpei Harada

Abstract
An exact solution to the vacuum Einstein equations is presented, whose structure is based on the Hopf fibration. The solution employs a geodesic null vector field that defines a twisting congruence and appears in the metric in Kerr–Schild form. This solution is of Petrov type D and involves two parameters. Remarkably, the resulting spacetime is regular, with no curvature singularities. Both the Kretschmann scalar and the Chern–Pontryagin scalar are nonzero and remain finite throughout the spacetime. In addition, the Newman–Penrose Weyl scalar Ψ 2 \displaystyle\Psi_{2} possesses both nonzero real and imaginary parts, reflecting the topologically nontrivial nature of the gravitational field. The spacetime also admits two Killing vector fields and a Killing–Yano tensor, which induces an associated Killing tensor, revealing its hidden symmetry. The derivation is simple and self-contained, offering a transparent and geometrically guided approach to finding new exact solutions in general relativity.



I
Introduction

The Hopf fibration
Hopf 1931
, mapping the 3-sphere
\displaystyle S^{3}
onto
\displaystyle S^{2}
with linked circular fibers, exhibits an elegant topological structure, whose influence spans diverse areas of physics
Urbantke 2003
. In electromagnetism, for instance, field configurations with linked and knotted lines, known as hopfions, arise as exact solutions to the vacuum Maxwell equations
Rañada 1989
;
Kedia et al. 2013
. These configurations have also been observed experimentally in materials. In gravity, these structures have been constructed in both linearized gravity and electromagnetism using a unified formalism
Smołka and Jezierski 2018
, and more recently, anti-self-dual spacetimes have been shown to be related to hopfions
Sabharwal and Dalhuisen 2019
.
Such developments naturally raise the question: can general relativity, despite its inherent nonlinearity, admit exact solutions that reflect the topological richness of the Hopf fibration?

This work presents an exact solution to the vacuum Einstein equations, derived using the Kerr–Schild metric with a null vector field, built from the Hopf fibration. The resulting metric is of Petrov type D and includes two real parameters. It is free of curvature singularities, and both the Kretschmann and Chern–Pontryagin scalars are finite and nonzero throughout the spacetime. The solution also admits two Killing vectors and a nontrivial Killing–Yano tensor, which generates a corresponding Killing tensor and reveals the presence of hidden symmetries.

The construction is simple and self-contained, suggesting that topologically nontrivial geometries may be more accessible in general relativity than previously expected. The method employed here may offer a practical route to discovering further exact solutions in general relativity.

II
Kerr–Schild metric

The Kerr–Schild metric
Kerr and Schild 2009
takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}=(\eta_{\mu\nu}+Hk_{\mu}k_{\nu})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu},

(1)

where
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}
is the Minkowski metric,
\displaystyle H
is a real function, and
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
is a null vector with respect to both
\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}
and
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle k^{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}.

(2)

The inverse of the metric tensor depends linearly on
\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}-Hk^{\mu}k^{\nu},

(3)

which is an exact equation. The determinant of the Kerr–Schild metric satisfies
\displaystyle\det(g_{\mu\nu})=\det(\eta_{\mu\nu})
. Thus, the Kerr–Schild metric is uniquely determined once the null vector
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
and a real function
\displaystyle H
are specified.

Several exact solutions to the vacuum Einstein equations can be expressed in the Kerr–Schild form.
In coordinates
\displaystyle(t,x,y,z)
with
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}=\text{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
,
the Schwarzschild metric takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}=\quantity( 1, \frac{x}{r}, \frac{y}{r}, \frac{z}{r}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle H=\frac{2M}{r},

(4)

and the Kerr metric
Kerr 1963
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}=\quantity( 1, \frac{rx+ay}{r^2+a^2}, \frac{ry-ax}{r^2+a^2}, \frac{z}{r}),\,\,H=\frac{2Mr^{3}}{r^{4}+a^{2}z^{2}},

(5)

where the parameter
\displaystyle r
is not a coordinate but is implicitly determined by the null condition
\displaystyle k_{\mu}k^{\mu}=0
in both the Schwarzschild and the Kerr metrics.

In light-cone coordinates,
\displaystyle x^{0}=v=(t+z)/\sqrt{2}
,
\displaystyle x^{1}=u=(t-z)/\sqrt{2}
,
\displaystyle x^{2}=x
,
\displaystyle x^{3}=y
with
\displaystyle\eta_{01}=\eta_{10}=-1
and
\displaystyle\eta_{22}=\eta_{33}=1
, the pp-wave spacetime takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}=\quantity(0, 1, 0, 0),

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{,xx}+H_{,yy}=0,

(6)

where
\displaystyle H=H(u,x,y)
and
\displaystyle H_{,xx}=\partial^{2}H/\partial x^{2}
.

In all these examples—Schwarzschild, Kerr, and pp-wave—the null vector
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
is (affinely parametrized) geodesic with respect to both
\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}
and
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}k_{\mu}=k^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}k_{\mu}=0,

(7)

where
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}
is a covariant derivative.

III
Hopf-based null vector

The Hopf fibration
Hopf 1931
is used to construct the null vector
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
as follows. Two complex numbers
\displaystyle z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\displaystyle|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}=1
can be parametrized as

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{1}=\frac{2(x+iy)}{r^{2}-t^{2}+1+2it},

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{2}=\frac{r^{2}-t^{2}-1-2iz}{r^{2}-t^{2}+1+2it},

(8)

where
\displaystyle r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}
. A pair of complex numbers
\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2})
defines coordinates on the 3-sphere
\displaystyle S^{3}
. At each point on
\displaystyle S^{3}
, consider the following map:

\displaystyle\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2})\rightarrow\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}}=\frac{2(x+iy)}{r^{2}-t^{2}-1-2iz}=:\phi(t,x,y,z),

(9)

where
\displaystyle\phi
is a complex scalar. Since
\displaystyle z_{1}/z_{2}=e^{i\alpha}z_{1}/(e^{i\alpha}z_{2})
for
\displaystyle\alpha=[0,2\pi)
, every point on the circle
\displaystyle e^{i\alpha}(z_{1},z_{2})
in
\displaystyle S^{3}
maps to a single point
\displaystyle z_{1}/z_{2}=\phi
. Therefore, the inverse map from
\displaystyle z_{1}/z_{2}
to the points on
\displaystyle S^{3}
forms an
\displaystyle S^{1}
fiber
Hopf 1931
.

A tangent vector
\displaystyle k^{\mu}
to this
\displaystyle S^{1}
fiber can be defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}k^{\nu}=0,

(10)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}:=\frac{1}{i}\quantity(\partial_\mu\phi^* \partial_\nu\phi- \partial_\nu\phi^* \partial_\mu\phi).

(11)

An imaginary unit
\displaystyle i
is introduced so that
\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}
is real. The vector
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
is also required to satisfy both the null condition
\displaystyle\eta^{\mu\nu}k_{\mu}k_{\nu}=0
and the geodesic condition
\displaystyle k^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}k_{\mu}=0
. One finds that the following
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
satisfies these conditions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\quantity(1+(t-z)^2)}\begin{pmatrix}1+(t-z)^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}\\
-2(t-z)x-2y\\
-2(t-z)y+2x\\
1+(t-z)^{2}-x^{2}-y^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(12)

where
\displaystyle t-z
direction has been chosen rather than
\displaystyle t+z
, and a factor
\displaystyle 1/\sqrt{2}
is introduced for later convenience in light-cone coordinates. The one-form
\displaystyle k_{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}
is invariant under rotations in the
\displaystyle xy
-plane.

Since
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
depends on
\displaystyle t-z
, it is convenient to adopt light-cone coordinates. For
\displaystyle x^{0}=v=(t+z)/\sqrt{2}
,
\displaystyle x^{1}=u=(t-z)/\sqrt{2}
,
\displaystyle x^{2}=x
,
\displaystyle x^{3}=y
, the null vector
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}=\quantity(1, \frac{x^2+y^2}{2 u^2 + 1}, - \frac{2 u x + \sqrt{2} y}{2 u^2 + 1}, - \frac{2 u y - \sqrt{2}x}{2 u^2 + 1}).

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{k}=(k_{u},k_{x},k_{y})
with the constant vector
\displaystyle\bm{k}=(1,0,0)
representing a pp-wave. In the figure, the vertical axis corresponds to the
\displaystyle u
-axis. It is evident that the null vector in Eq. (

) exhibits a twisted structure that is qualitatively distinct from that of a pp-wave.

IV
Regular Petrov D spacetime

The
\displaystyle k_{\mu}
from Eq. (
13
) is used in the Kerr–Schild metric

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-2\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v+\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\mathrm{d}y^{2}+Hk_{\mu}k_{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}.

(14)

If
\displaystyle H
is a function of
\displaystyle u
,
\displaystyle H=H(u)
, then two components of Ricci tensor contain only first-order derivatives

\displaystyle\displaystyle R^{2}{}_{2}=R^{3}{}_{3}=\frac{2}{2u^{2}+1}\quantity(u H^\prime(u) + \frac{2 u^2 -1}{2 u^2 + 1}H(u)),

(15)

where
\displaystyle H^{\prime}(u)=\mathrm{d}H(u)/\mathrm{d}u
. Solving
\displaystyle R^{2}{}_{2}=0
, one obtains

\displaystyle\displaystyle H(u)=\frac{Nu}{2u^{2}+1}

(16)

where
\displaystyle N
is an integration constant. If
\displaystyle H(u)
is given by this function, all components of the Ricci tensor vanish. Therefore, the metric in Eq. (
14
), together with Eqs. (
13
) and (
16
), represents an exact solution to the vacuum Einstein equations.

By restoring a dimensional parameter, one obtains

\displaystyle\displaystyle k_{\mu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\quantity(1, \frac{x^2+y^2}{2\qty(u^2 + b^2)}, - \frac{u x + b y}{u^2 + b^2}, - \frac{u y - b x}{u^2 + b^2}),

(17)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle H(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{Nu}{u^{2}+b^{2}},

(18)

where
\displaystyle b>0
is a parameter with the dimension of length. Note that
\displaystyle b<0
can be absorbed by redefining
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
, so
\displaystyle b>0
is assumed without loss of generality, and
\displaystyle b=0
is excluded by construction. The integration constant
\displaystyle N
is redefined accordingly. It is also worth noting that the gauge field
\displaystyle A_{\mu}:=H(u)k_{\mu}
satisfies the vacuum Maxwell equations in flat spacetime,
\displaystyle\eta^{\mu\rho}\partial_{\rho}(\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu})=0
.

The final form of the solution is thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\,-2\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v+\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\mathrm{d}y^{2}+\frac{Nu}{u^{2}+b^{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\quantity[\mathrm{d} v+\frac{x^2+y^2}{2\qty(u^2 + b^2)} \, \mathrm{d} u - \frac{u x + b y}{u^2 + b^2} \, \mathrm{d} x - \frac{u y - b x}{u^2 + b^2} \, \mathrm{d} y]^{2}.

(19)

In the limit
\displaystyle N\rightarrow 0
or
\displaystyle u\rightarrow\pm\infty
, the solution asymptotically approaches the Minkowski metric.

This solution admits two Killing vector fields
\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}
, given in coordinates
\displaystyle(v,u,x,y)
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}=\quantity(1, 0, 0, 0),\,\,\quantity(0, 0, y, -x)

(20)

and an antisymmetric Killing–Yano tensor
\displaystyle f_{\mu\nu}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&b&0&0\\
-b&0&y&-x\\
0&-y&0&u\\
0&x&-u&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

(21)

which satisfies
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}f_{\nu\rho}+\nabla_{\nu}f_{\mu\rho}=0
. From this Killing–Yano tensor, one can construct the Killing tensor
\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}:=f_{\mu\rho}f_{\nu\sigma}g^{\rho\sigma}.

(22)

This tensor
\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}
satisfies
\displaystyle\nabla_{\rho}K_{\mu\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\rho}+\nabla_{\nu}K_{\rho\mu}=0
,
and it is irreducible, in the sense that it cannot be written as a linear combination of
\displaystyle\xi_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}
and the metric
\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}
.

Since
\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}=0
, the Kretschmann scalar
\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
equals the square of the Weyl tensor,

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{12N^{2}(u^{6}-15b^{2}u^{4}+15b^{4}u^{2}-b^{6})}{(u^{2}+b^{2})^{6}},

(23)

and the Chern–Pontryagin scalar
\displaystyle{{}^{\star}{}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle{{}^{\star}{}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle={{}^{\star}{}C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{24N^{2}bu(3u^{4}-10b^{2}u^{2}+3b^{4})}{(u^{2}+b^{2})^{6}},

(24)

where
\displaystyle{{}^{\star}{}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}}=(1/2)\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}R^{\alpha\beta}{}_{\rho\sigma}
.

The Newman–Penrose Weyl scalars are

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Psi_{2}=-\frac{N}{2(u+ib)^{3}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Psi_{0}=\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{3}=\Psi_{4}=0.

(25)

This implies that the solution is of Petrov type D, as in the Schwarzschild and Kerr metrics. Both the real and imaginary parts of
\displaystyle\Psi_{2}
are nonzero, as in the Kerr case.

Using the Newman–Penrose scalars, the curvature invariants can be expressed in particularly simple forms

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=+48\real\Psi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=+\real\frac{12N^{2}}{(u+ib)^{6}},

(26)

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle={{}^{\star}{}C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-48\imaginary\Psi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\imaginary\frac{12N^{2}}{(u+ib)^{6}},

(27)

which follow from the identity
Cherubini et al. 2002

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}-iI_{2}=16\quantity(3 \Psi_2^2 + \Psi_0 \Psi_4 - 4 \Psi_0 \Psi_3).

(28)

The magnitude
\displaystyle\quantity| I_1 - i I_2|
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{(I_{1})^{2}+(I_{2})^{2}}=48|\Psi_{2}|^{2}=\frac{12N^{2}}{(u^{2}+b^{2})^{3}}.

(29)

This expression is finite and nonzero everywhere, with no curvature singularities since
\displaystyle b>0
. It depends only on
\displaystyle u
and is independent of
\displaystyle v,x,y
, indicating a wave propagating along the
\displaystyle+z
-direction at the speed of light with a planar wavefront. Due to the
\displaystyle u^{-6}
dependence, the curvature is concentrated near the origin of the
\displaystyle u
-axis and falls off rapidly away from it.

V
Conclusions

This work has presented an exact vacuum solution to the Einstein field equations in Kerr–Schild form, constructed using a geodesic null vector field derived from the Hopf fibration. The vector field defines a twisting null congruence with nontrivial topology. The solution involves two parameters and is of Petrov type D, with a nonvanishing Weyl scalar
\displaystyle\Psi_{2}
that possesses both real and imaginary components. The resulting spacetime is regular everywhere, with no curvature singularities. Both the Kretschmann scalar and the Chern–Pontryagin scalar are finite and nonzero, indicating the presence of a genuinely nonlinear and topologically nontrivial gravitational field. The spacetime also admits two Killing vector fields and a nontrivial Killing–Yano tensor, indicating hidden geometric symmetries.

To the best of current knowledge, this appears to be the first explicit example of a singularity-free, vacuum, exact solution of Petrov type D constructed from a Hopf-structured null vector field. Notably, this metric is not listed in the standard reference
Stephani et al. 2003
, and its derivation presented here is remarkably simple and fully self-contained. The construction demonstrates that geometrically motivated null structures—particularly those with nontrivial topology, such as the Hopf fibration—can serve as powerful tools for discovering exact solutions in general relativity. This suggests that further exploration of such structures may reveal new classes of physically relevant spacetimes beyond the known catalog.
