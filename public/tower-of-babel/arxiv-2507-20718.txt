Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution
Sungjun Lim, Kangjun Noh, Youngjun Choi, Heeyoung Lee, Kyungwoo Song

Abstract
Text embeddings are essential components in modern NLP pipelines. While numerous embedding models have been proposed, their performance varies across domains, and no single model consistently excels across all tasks. This variability motivates the use of ensemble techniques to combine complementary strengths. However, most existing ensemble methods operate on deterministic embeddings and fail to account for model-specific uncertainty, limiting their robustness and reliability in downstream applications. To address these limitations, we propose Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution (UEC) . UEC first transforms deterministic embeddings into probabilistic ones in a post-hoc manner. It then computes adaptive ensemble weights based on embedding uncertainty, grounded in a Bayes-optimal solution under a surrogate loss. Additionally, UEC introduces an uncertainty-aware similarity function that directly incorporates uncertainty into similarity scoring. Extensive experiments on retrieval, classification, and semantic similarity benchmarks demonstrate that UEC consistently improves both performance and robustness by leveraging principled uncertainty modeling. 1 1 1 Code is available at: https://github.com/MLAI-Yonsei/UEC

Introduction

Embeddings are core building blocks in modern NLP, capturing semantic meaning for words, sentences, and documents. They support tasks like similarity
[
1
]
, retrieval
[
2
]
, QA
[
3
]
, and classification

]
. Numerous embedding models
[

]
have emerged with diverse architectures and training objectives, but their performance varies across tasks and domains. No single model excels universally; instead, models offer complementary strengths. This motivates combining multiple embeddings to leverage their diverse capabilities.

In this example, both candidate embeddings contribute equally, resulting in an incorrect retrieval. In contrast, the proposed UEC (right) adjusts weights based on the uncertainty, assigning higher importance to the more reliable candidate and successfully retrieving the correct answer.

One intuitive strategy is to ensemble multiple embedding models directly in the representation space. Model merging techniques
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
assume shared architectures and initialization. However, embeddings are typically used in a decoupled manner: fixed representations are extracted in advance and stored for downstream tasks such as retrieval or classification
[
10
,
11
]
. Following this, combining embeddings at the representation level is more flexible and widely applicable. However, most existing ensemble approaches rely on deterministic aggregation methods such as uniform averaging, which fail to consider the reliability or uncertainty of individual embeddings
[
12
,
13
]
. Without modeling uncertainty, these methods treat all embeddings as equally reliable, which can lead to suboptimal or unstable performance, especially when some models are poorly calibrated or mismatched to the target task (Figure
1
).

To address these limitations, we propose
Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution (UEC)
, a framework for combining embeddings in a principled, uncertainty-aware manner. UEC consists of three key components. First, it converts pre-trained deterministic embeddings into probabilistic embeddings in a post-hoc fashion, allowing each embedding to represent both its mean and uncertainty. Second, it computes adaptive ensemble weights based on estimated uncertainty, down-weighting less reliable embeddings. This weighting strategy is grounded in a Bayes-optimal solution under a surrogate loss. Third, UEC introduces an uncertainty-aware similarity function that incorporates both distance and variance into the similarity score, enhancing robustness in similarity-driven tasks.

In summary, our key contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a post-hoc mechanism to transform pre-trained deterministic embeddings into probabilistic representations, thereby enabling inherent uncertainty quantification for diverse existing embedding models.

•

We introduce the UEC framework, which adaptively combines multiple embeddings by weighting their contributions based on estimated uncertainties, a method theoretically grounded as a Bayes-optimal solution under surrogate loss minimization.

•

We develop an uncertainty-aware similarity function that explicitly incorporates embedding variance into the similarity scoring process, enhancing robustness and reliability in various similarity-driven downstream tasks.

Related Works

2.1
Embedding Ensemble

Embedding ensemble methods aim to improve performance and robustness by combining multiple pretrained models. Shuang et al.
[
14
]
propose CDWE, which handles polysemy by generating multiple word prototypes through deconvolution. Fang et al.
[
13

[

[

distill multiple encoders into one by matching their averaged outputs. Khan et al.
[
17

explore fusion strategies for domain-specific BERTs, yielding minor improvements. Despite these advances, most approaches rely on deterministic embeddings, limiting their ability to model uncertainty. In contrast, our method ensembles probabilistic embeddings, capturing both distributional information and model diversity through principled uncertainty estimation.

3
Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution

We propose
Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution (UEC)
, a principled framework for combining multiple embedding models by modeling predictive uncertainty. UEC consists of three stages (Figure
2
): (1) post-hoc conversion of deterministic embeddings into probabilistic ones via Laplace approximation (LA) (Section
3.1
), (2) Gaussian convolution with uncertainty-aware weights (Section
3.2
), and (3) uncertainty-aware similarity scoring (Section
3.3
). This enables robust and adaptive ensemble without manual tuning.

Figure 2
:
Overview of the UEC framework: UEC first transforms deterministic embeddings from multiple encoder models into probabilistic representations using Laplace approximation. These probabilistic embeddings are then adaptively combined by computing uncertainty-driven ensemble coefficients based on per-dimension variances. Finally, similarity is measured using a uncertainty-aware metric that accounts for both the mean and uncertainty of the ensembled embedding.

3.1
Post-hoc Probabilistic Embedding Model

We first transform deterministic embeddings into probabilistic embeddings using LA
[
27
,
28
]
. This allows each embedding to express both mean and uncertainty, which we later exploit for adaptive model combination and similarity estimation.

Laplace Approximation

LA approximates the posterior distribution of model parameters
w
as a Gaussian centered at the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate. We denote the training dataset as
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y)\}
, where
x
and
y
are the input and corresponding output, respectively. The posterior is given by Bayes’ theorem:

p(w|\mathcal{D})=\frac{p(\mathcal{D}|w)p(w)}{p(\mathcal{D})},

where
p(\mathcal{D}|w)
is the likelihood,
p(w)
is the prior, and
p(\mathcal{D})
is the evidence. Since this posterior is generally intractable, LA approximates
p(w|\mathcal{D})
with a Gaussian centered at
\hat{w}
(the MAP) and precision matrix given by the Hessian
H_{\hat{w}}
:

\displaystyle-\log p(w|\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\approx-\log p(\hat{w}|\mathcal{D})+\frac{1}{2}(w-\hat{w})^{\top}H_{\hat{w}}(w-\hat{w}),

\displaystyle H_{\hat{w}}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}[-\log p(\mathcal{D}|w)-\log p(w)]\Big|_{w=\hat{w}}.

This approximates posterior
p(w|\mathcal{D})\approx\mathcal{N}(\hat{w},H^{-1}_{\hat{w}})
.

Gaussian Embedding Generation

Since computing the full Hessian is often impractical for large models, recent work shows that applying diagonal LA to only the last layer yields sufficiently accurate uncertainty estimates with lower computational overhead
[
29
]
. Following this line of work, we apply LA with a diagonal covariance approximation only to the final layer of the embedding model. Let
f(x)
be an embedding model with
L
layers, and let
w^{(L)}
and
h^{(L-1)}
denote the parameters and output of the penultimate layer, respectively. In the deterministic setting, the embedding is computed as
z=w^{(L)\top}h^{(L-1)}
.

Applying LA to
w^{(L)}
, we obtain posterior of last layer parameter
p(w^{(L)}|\mathcal{D})\approx\mathcal{N}(\hat{w}^{(L)},H^{-1}_{\hat{w}^{(L)}})
, which implies that the output embedding
z
becomes a random variable:

z(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu(x),\Sigma(x))=\mathcal{N}(\hat{w}^{(L)\top}h^{(L-1)},h^{(L-1)\top}H^{-1}_{\hat{w}^{(L)}}h^{(L-1)}).

3.2
Bayes-optimal Weights for Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution

Gaussian Convolution

Suppose we have
K
independent embedding models, each producing a probabilistic embedding
z_{k}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x),\Sigma_{k}(x))
. To integrate these into a single representation, we perform a
Gaussian convolution
in the embedding space. Formally, we define the convolutional embedding
z(x)
as the result of a weighted combination of independent Gaussian variables:

z(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\cdot z_{k}(x),

where
\pi_{k}(x)
denotes the weight (convolution coefficient) assigned to model
k
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)=1
.

Since each
z_{k}(x)
is Gaussian and the coefficients
\pi_{k}(x)
are deterministic, the resulting embedding
z(x)
follows a Gaussian distribution as well, according to the properties of linear combinations of independent Gaussians
[
30
]
:

z(x)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\mu_{k}(x),\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{2}(x)\Sigma_{k}(x)\right).

(1)

Gaussian convolution aggregates the centers (means) and spreads (covariances) of the input Gaussians into a single distribution. This process inherently allows for the propagation of uncertainty in a closed form, enabling the ensemble to capture and represent epistemic uncertainty arising from disagreements or variations across different models.

Bayes-optimal Coefficients

To determine the optimal coefficients for Gaussian convolution, we define an uncertainty-aware surrogate loss that reflects how closely the embeddings of positive pairs are expected to align. We then derive a convex optimization problem that identifies the coefficients minimizing this surrogate loss.

Text embeddings are often trained with contrastive losses like InfoNCE
[
31
]
. For
\ell_{2}
-normalized features, the squared Euclidean distance relates directly to cosine similarity:
\|u-v\|^{2}=2(1-u^{\top}v)

[
32
]
. These losses also encourage alignment of positive pairs and uniformity on the unit hypersphere. Leveraging this connection, we adopt the squared loss as a principled surrogate for contrastive objectives.

Let
(x,x^{\prime})
be a positive pair. For each model
k
we observe
z_{k}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x),\Sigma_{k}(x))

and
z_{k}(x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x^{\prime}),\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))
.
We define the uncertainty-aware surrogate loss, a multi-task objective that aggregates model-specific embedding errors weighted by uncertainty-aware task coefficients, as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur}}(\pi;x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\,\mathbb{E}_{z_{k}(x)}\left[\|z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\left(\underbrace{\|\mu_{k}(x)-\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}}_{\text{Fidelity}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))}_{\text{Uncertainty}}\right).

(2)

This surrogate loss decomposes into fidelity and epistemic uncertainty. While
\mu_{k}(x)
and
\Sigma_{k}(x)
are directly computable from the input
x
, the corresponding terms
\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})
and
\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime})
require evaluating the embedding model at the reference point
x^{\prime}
. However, in our retrieval setting—aligned with common practice—document embeddings must be precomputed and stored in advance, independent of the incoming query
x^{\prime}
. This practical constraint makes it infeasible to rely on any query-dependent terms during document embedding construction. As such, we approximate the surrogate loss by retaining only the components that depend on
x
, leading to the following approximation:

\min_{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\left(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}\right)\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)=1,\;\pi_{k}(x)\geq 0.

(3)

This is a convex optimization problem over the probability simplex, where each model is penalized based on both its uncertainty and the magnitude of its embedding. Its unique closed-form solution is the inverse-variance weighting:

\pi_{k}^{\star}(x)\approx\frac{1/\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))}{1/\sum_{j=1}^{K}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{j}(x))}.

(4)

Eq. (
4
) generalized inverse-weighting scheme accounts for both epistemic uncertainty (via
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))
and representation fidelity (via
\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}
) allowing the ensemble to favor embeddings that are simultaneously confident and well-aligned with the input. This optimal coefficient
\pi^{*}(x)
minimizes the uncertainty-aware surrogate loss in Eq. (
3
), maintaining consistency with the original objective. Full derivation appears in App.
A.1
.

3.3
Uncertainty-aware Similarity Estimation

When embeddings are modeled as probability distributions, the similarity between two inputs
x
and
x^{\prime}
must be redefined to incorporate uncertainty. A natural candidate for distributional similarity is the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD)
[
33
]
, which measures divergence between two distributions. Although KLD admits a closed-form expression between Gaussian distributions, computing it involves matrix inverses and log-determinants of covariance matrices, making it prohibitively expensive in high-dimensional and large-scale pairwise comparison settings. To preserve the efficiency of cosine similarity, we propose an uncertainty-aware extension of cosine similarity with minimal computational overhead
[
25
]
.

Let the ensembled embeddings for
x
and
x^{\prime}
be
q=z(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{q},\Sigma_{q})
and
c=z(x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{c},\Sigma_{c})
, respectively. As in prior work
[
34
,
35
]
, we apply
\ell_{2}
normalization to both
q
and
c
, reducing cosine similarity to the standard dot product. We model the dot product
q^{\top}c
as a Gaussian random variable via moment matching
[
36
,
37
]
:

s=q^{\top}c\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{s},\sigma_{s}^{2}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{s}=\mu_{q}^{\top}\mu_{c},\quad\sigma_{s}^{2}=\mu_{q}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}\mu_{q}+\mu_{c}^{\top}\Sigma_{q}\mu_{c}+\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{q}\Sigma_{c}).

(5)

To incorporate uncertainty within similarity score, we simply apply a probit approximation
[
38
,
39
]
:

\hat{s}\approx\frac{\mu_{s}}{\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{8}\sigma_{s}^{2}}}.

(6)

This formulation preserves the computational simplicity of cosine similarity without additional sampling, while incorporating predictive uncertainty in a principled and lightweight manner. Full derivation is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

5
Conclusion and Discussion

We proposed the Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution (UEC) framework, which converts pre-trained deterministic embeddings into probabilistic representations and adaptively ensembles them based on estimated uncertainty. UEC also introduces an uncertainty-aware similarity function that incorporates variance into scoring. Experiments on retrieval, classification, and semantic similarity tasks show that UEC consistently outperforms strong baselines by effectively leveraging complementary embedding strengths.

Limitation and Future Work

This work focuses on text embeddings, with limited validation on other modalities. While we employ diagonal LA for uncertainty estimation, more advanced or efficient alternatives may further enhance performance. A limitation of our approach is the requirement that all embeddings share the same dimensionality, which may constrain its applicability to heterogeneous model ensembles. Future work includes extending UEC to multimodal settings and exploring scalable uncertainty estimation for real-world deployment.

Impact Statement

UEC can improve the robustness and reliability of NLP systems, particularly in high-stakes domains like healthcare, finance, and law. However, misapplication on biased or incomplete data may lead to misleading confidence estimates. Responsible deployment requires bias mitigation, transparent uncertainty communication, and continuous monitoring.

Appendix A
Derivation and Proof

A.1
Derivation of Bayes-optimal Weights for Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of the optimal ensemble coefficients used in the uncertainty-driven embedding convolution described in Section
3.2
. Specifically, we aim to derive a closed-form solution for the optimal coefficients
\pi_{k}(x)
that minimize an uncertainty-aware surrogate loss reflecting the expected distance between positive pairs.

Problem Setting

Let there be
K
independent embedding models. Each model
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
produces a probabilistic embedding
z_{k}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x),\Sigma_{k}(x))
for input
x
, where
\mu_{k}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the mean and
\Sigma_{k}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the covariance matrix. Similarly, we define
z_{k}(x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x^{\prime}),\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))
for a positive example
x^{\prime}
.

We define the ensembled embedding as a weighted combination of model-specific embeddings:

z(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\cdot z_{k}(x),\quad\text{with }\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)=1,\quad\pi_{k}(x)\geq 0.

Surrogate Loss

Text embeddings are often trained with contrastive losses such as InfoNCE
[
31
]
, which aim to bring semantically similar pairs closer while pushing dissimilar pairs apart. In the case of
\ell_{2}
-normalized features, the squared Euclidean distance is closely related to cosine similarity via the identity:
\|u-v\|^{2}=2(1-u^{\top}v)

[
32
]
. This connection reveals that minimizing squared distance effectively encourages high cosine similarity, aligning well with the objectives of contrastive learning.

Furthermore, recent theoretical analyses
[
32
]
show that contrastive objectives optimize two essential properties: (1)
alignment
, by pulling positive pairs together, and (2)
uniformity
, by encouraging embeddings to spread uniformly on the hypersphere. These principles are naturally supported by using the squared loss in the normalized feature space.

Leveraging this insight, we adopt the squared Euclidean loss as a principled surrogate for contrastive objectives. Unlike InfoNCE, which requires negative sampling and temperature scaling, the squared loss admits a simpler and deterministic formulation while still capturing the key inductive biases of alignment and uniformity. For a positive pair
(x,x^{\prime})
, and for each embedding model
k
, we define the surrogate loss as the expected squared distance between probabilistic embeddings:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sur}}(\pi;x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{z_{k}(x)}\left[\|z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}\right].

Expected Distance Between Gaussians

To compute the surrogate loss, we evaluate the expected squared distance between two independent Gaussian embeddings:

\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}],

where
z_{k}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x),\Sigma_{k}(x))
and
z_{k}(x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}(x^{\prime}),\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))
.

Using the identity
\|a-b\|^{2}=\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}-2a^{\top}b
, the expectation becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k}(x)\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}[z_{k}(x)^{\top}z_{k}(x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}+\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))+\|\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}-2\mu_{k}(x)^{\top}\mu_{k}(x^{\prime}),

where we used two standard results for Gaussian vectors:

•

\mathbb{E}[\|z\|^{2}]=\text{tr}(\Sigma)+\|\mu\|^{2}
, and

•

\mathbb{E}[z^{\top}z^{\prime}]=\mu^{\top}\mu^{\prime}
for independent
z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),\ z^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
.

Combining these yields:

\mathbb{E}[\|z_{k}(x)-z_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}]=\|\mu_{k}(x)-\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}+\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime})).

This decomposition reveals two key components:

•

The
fidelity term

\|\mu_{k}(x)-\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}
, which measures semantic alignment between the means of the embeddings.

•

The
uncertainty term

\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\text{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))
, which reflects how uncertain the model is about each input.

This result plays a central role in our surrogate loss formulation, as it enables UEC to quantify both how close embeddings are (fidelity) and how reliable they are (uncertainty), using closed-form computations under the Gaussian assumption.

Tractable Approximation

Directly evaluating the full surrogate loss requires computing both the fidelity term
\|\mu_{k}(x)-\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}
and the uncertainty terms
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))
and
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime}))
for every positive pair
(x,x^{\prime})
. However, in many practical settings, this becomes computationally prohibitive, as document embeddings (corresponding to
x^{\prime}
) are typically precomputed and stored in advance. This design choice is essential for scalable retrieval systems, which need to index millions of documents offline and later perform fast matching against online queries
x
at inference time.

Consequently, the embedding
\mu_{k}(x^{\prime})
and its associated uncertainty
\Sigma_{k}(x^{\prime})
are fixed and query-independent at runtime. Moreover, the query
x
is not known during document encoding, so it is infeasible to compute any query-dependent quantities during offline document embedding. To ensure consistency with this retrieval pipeline, we simplify the surrogate loss by retaining only the components that are computable solely from the input
x
(i.e., the query):

\min_{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)\left(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}\right)\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(x)=1,\;\pi_{k}(x)\geq 0.

(7)

This formulation preserves the uncertainty-awareness of our surrogate loss while adhering to the architectural constraint of precomputed document embeddings. It also allows for efficient, query-time optimization of the ensemble coefficients
\pi_{k}(x)
based only on the statistics of the query embedding.

Closed-form Solution

The optimization problem defined above (Eq. (
7
)) is convex over the probability simplex and has a linear objective. The objective is minimized when more weight is assigned to components with lower values of the cost term
c_{k}:=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}
.

We derive the solution using the method of Lagrange multipliers. The Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}c_{k}-\lambda\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}-1\right),

where
c_{k}:=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}
and
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier. Taking the derivative with respect to
\pi_{k}
and setting to zero gives:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi_{k}}=c_{k}-\lambda=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=c_{k}\quad\forall k\ \text{ s.t. }\ \pi_{k}>0.

This condition implies that the minimum is achieved when
\pi_{k}
is inversely proportional to
c_{k}
. More formally, the optimal solution is:

\pi_{k}^{\star}(x)=\frac{c_{k}^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}^{-1}}=\frac{\left(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))+\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{j}(x))+\|\mu_{j}(x)\|^{2}\right)^{-1}}.

(8)

In practice, the embeddings
\mu_{k}(x)
are typically
\ell_{2}
-normalized, which implies that
\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}\approx 1
for all
k
. Therefore, we approximate Eq (
8
) as:

\pi_{k}^{\star}(x)\approx\frac{1/\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k}(x))}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}1/\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{j}(x))},

by treating the constant
\|\mu_{k}(x)\|^{2}
term as uniform across all models. This simplification preserves the desirable behavior of assigning higher weights to models with lower predictive uncertainty, while significantly reducing computational overhead.

Interpretation

This optimal solution is Bayes-optimal under the surrogate loss, as it minimizes the expected discrepancy between the ensembled embedding and its positive reference under Gaussian uncertainty. The form of the solution resembles that of precision-weighted model averaging, often seen in Bayesian linear models, where less uncertain models contribute more to the final prediction.

A.2
Derivation of Uncertainty-aware Similarity Estimation

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of the uncertainty-aware similarity estimation method introduced in Section
3.3
. When embeddings are modeled as multivariate Gaussian distributions, the similarity between them becomes a random variable. We aim to derive the distribution of this similarity score and propose a calibrated approximation that accounts for predictive uncertainty.

Problem Setup

Let
q=z(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{q},\Sigma_{q})
and
c=z(x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{c},\Sigma_{c})
denote two ensembled probabilistic embeddings generated by the proposed UEC method. Each embedding is represented as a Gaussian distribution with a mean vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

We define the cosine similarity between
q
and
c
as:

s:=\cos(q,c)=\frac{q^{\top}c}{\|q\|\cdot\|c\|},

which itself is a random variable, since both
q
and
c
are random. In our implementation, both
q
and
c
are
\ell_{2}
-normalized in expectation (i.e.,
\|\mu_{q}\|\approx\|\mu_{c}\|\approx 1
), and their variances are relatively small. Therefore, we simplify cosine similarity to the inner product:

s\approx q^{\top}c,

as is common in prior work
[
37
,
36
]
.

Distribution of the Inner Product via Moment Matching

To characterize the distribution of
s=q^{\top}c
, we approximate it as a Gaussian random variable using moment-matching
[
36
]
. Since
q
and
c
are independent Gaussian vectors, their inner product
s
follows an approximately Gaussian distribution whose mean and variance can be computed in closed form.

Mean.
By linearity of expectation:

\mu_{s}:=\mathbb{E}[q^{\top}c]=\mathbb{E}[q]^{\top}\mathbb{E}[c]=\mu_{q}^{\top}\mu_{c}.

(9)

Variance.
Using the law of total variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(q^{\top}c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\mathrm{Var}(q^{\top}c\mid q)\right]+\mathrm{Var}_{q}\left(\mathbb{E}[q^{\top}c\mid q]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}[q^{\top}\Sigma_{c}q]+\mathrm{Var}_{q}(q^{\top}\mu_{c}),

where assume
q
and
c
are independent.

We evaluate each term as follows. For the first term, using the identity
\mathbb{E}[x^{\top}Ax]=\mathrm{Tr}(A\Sigma)+\mu^{\top}A\mu
for
x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)

[
30
]
:

\mathbb{E}_{q}[q^{\top}\Sigma_{c}q]=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{c}\Sigma_{q})+\mu_{q}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}\mu_{q}.

For the second term, using the variance of a linear form in a Gaussian:

\mathrm{Var}_{q}(q^{\top}\mu_{c})=\mu_{c}^{\top}\Sigma_{q}\mu_{c}.

Combining both, we obtain:

\sigma_{s}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(q^{\top}c)=\mu_{q}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}\mu_{q}+\mu_{c}^{\top}\Sigma_{q}\mu_{c}+\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{q}\Sigma_{c}).

Therefore, under moment-matching, we approximate the distribution of similarity as:

s=q^{\top}c\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{s},\sigma_{s}^{2}).

Probit-based Calibration of Similarity Scores

The similarity distribution provides a way to quantify uncertainty, but we still need a scalar value to rank candidates. To account for this uncertainty in a principled yet computationally efficient way, we apply a probit-based calibration to the mean similarity score:

\hat{s}\approx\frac{\mu_{s}}{\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{8}\sigma_{s}^{2}}}.

(10)

This formula arises from approximating the expectation of a sigmoid over a Gaussian random variable
[
39
,
38
]
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(z)]\approx\sigma\left(\mu/\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{8}\sigma^{2}}\right)
when
z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the logistic sigmoid function.

Interpretation and Robustness

The numerator
\mu_{s}
captures alignment between the means, while the denominator serves as a soft penalty for high uncertainty. As
\sigma_{s}^{2}
increases, the similarity score
\hat{s}
is downscaled, reflecting reduced confidence in the similarity estimate. This makes the scoring robust in the presence of model disagreement, variance, or noise, particularly useful in retrieval settings.

This uncertainty-aware formulation preserves the computational simplicity of cosine similarity while incorporating predictive variance in a principled manner, enabling better performance under high uncertainty.

Fitting Laplace Approximation

To quantify uncertainty for each embedding model, we apply a diagonal Laplace approximation (LA) to the final layer using a small, labeled dataset. In this experiment, we fit the approximation using the MIRACL Hard Negative

laplace-redux
library
2
2

2

https://aleximmer.com/Laplace/
.
The posterior mean corresponds to the MAP estimate of the final layer’s weights, and the posterior variance is derived from the inverse of the diagonal Hessian.

B.1.2
Dataset

For the MIRACL subset evaluation, we constructed a test set by sampling 25 queries each from four languages (English, Russian, Chinese, and Arabic) from the multilingual MIRACL Hard Negative dataset
[
44

. This selection aimed to cover diverse language families and scripts, ensuring a balanced multilingual evaluation.

B.1.3
Metrics

We employed three commonly used retrieval metrics: nDCG@10, Recall@10, and AUC@10.

•

NDCG@10
(Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) measures the ranking quality based on the positions of relevant passages among the top 10 retrieved items. It assigns higher weights to relevant items appearing earlier in the list, penalizing misranked relevant results logarithmically.

•

RECALL@10
evaluates whether at least one relevant passage is retrieved within the top 10. It is computed as the fraction of queries for which a relevant document appears among the top-10 predictions.

•

AUC@10
(normalized Area Under the Curve) measures how well a model’s confidence scores align with its actual retrieval performance when abstention is allowed. Specifically, the model is allowed to abstain on queries with low confidence scores, and AUC@10 tracks how the average performance improves as more uncertain queries are excluded. We compute AUC@10 by plotting NDCG@10 as a function of the abstention rate—i.e., the proportion of low-confidence queries discarded—and measuring the area under this curve. To enable fair comparison across models, we normalize this area between two bounds: (1) a
baseline curve
where abstention does not improve performance, and (2) an
oracle curve
where the worst-performing queries are perfectly abstained first. The normalized AUC is then computed as:

\text{nAUC}=\frac{\text{AUC}_{\text{Actual}}-\text{AUC}_{\text{Baseline}}}{\text{AUC}_{\text{Oracle}}-\text{AUC}_{\text{Baseline}}}.

Intuitively, higher nAUC values indicate that the model’s confidence scores are well-calibrated: as the model abstains on low-confidence queries, its average performance improves meaningfully. Conversely, a negative nAUC implies that the model’s confidence scores are misleading—i.e., low-confidence queries were actually high-performing, or high-confidence queries were often wrong.

, ensuring the weights sum to 1.

•

Task Arithmetic
: We implemented task arithmetic following
Ilharco et al. 2022
, where the merged embedding is computed by linearly combining base model embeddings with the direction vector between two other models. Formally, given three models
A,B,C
, the merged embedding is:

\text{Embed}_{\text{merged}}=\text{Embed}_{A}+\alpha\cdot(\text{Embed}_{B}-\text{Embed}_{C}),

where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter controlling the direction scaling. We performed grid search over
\alpha\in\{0.0001,0.001,0.01,0.1,1.0\}
.

•

Weight Ensemble
: We used grid search over weight combinations including
[0.1,0.1,0.8]
,
[0.1,0.2,0.7]
,
\ldots
,
[0.8,0.1,0.1]
, ensuring the weights sum to 1.

•

UEC (Ours)
: The temperature was fixed at
1.8
throughout the experiments. We search
\beta
, scaling parameter for
\sigma_{s}^{2}
, in range of
[0.0001,0.001,0.01,0.1]
.
