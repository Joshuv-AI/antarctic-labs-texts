Social networks, messaging apps, addictive use, and academic performance in adolescents: findings from the EHDLA study
Authors: Andrea Aquino-Blanco, Fiorella Quiroz-Cárdenas, José Adrián Montenegro-Espinosa, Nerea Martín-Calvo, Rodrigo Yañéz-Sepúlveda, Héctor Gutiérrez-Espinoza, Jorge Olivares-Arancibia, Masoud Rahmati, Brendon Stubbs, Lee Smith, José Francisco López-Gil, Estela Jiménez-López
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic performance in adolescence is a key predictor of future educational, occupational, and health outcomes. While social networks (SNs) and messaging apps are increasingly integrated into adolescents’ daily lives, their impact on academic achievement remains debated. This study aimed to evaluate associations between the use of SNs, messaging apps, addictive behaviors, and academic performance in Spanish adolescents.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the Eating Healthy and Daily Life Activities (EHDLA) study, which included 583 adolescents aged 12–17 years from three secondary schools in Valle de Ricote, Spain, during the 2021–2022 academic year. SNs and messaging app use were assessed via a self-report scale. Addictive behaviors were measured using the Short Social Networks Addiction Scale-6 Symptoms (SNAddS-6 S). Academic performance was evaluated using grade point average (GPA) and subject-specific grades obtained from school records. Associations were analyzed using generalized linear models adjusted for important covariates.ResultsThe median age of participants was 14 years (interquartile range: 13 to 15), with 57% female. TikTok and WhatsApp were the most frequently used platforms. No statistically significant association was found between the overall use of SNs and GPA. However, higher levels of addictive behaviors related to SNs use were significantly associated with lower academic performance (B = − 0.15, 95% CI: − 0.26 to − 0.03, p = 0.014).ConclusionsThere was no significant association between overall SNs use and GPA. However, a significant negative association was observed for addictive behaviors related to SNs use. These findings highlight the need for educational interventions that address problematic or addictive patterns of SN use, fostering self-regulation and healthy digital habits while preserving the benefits of online connectivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02180-8.Keywords: Psychological well-being, Adolescent behavior, Academic performance, Social networks, Addiction, Youth

Introduction

Academic performance is the evaluation of a student accomplishments based on factors such as grade point average (GPA), standardized test results, and educational goals [1]. Academic performance broadly involves acquiring knowledge, developing skills and competencies, attaining high grades and other academic milestones, striving for a successful career, and exhibiting dedication and persistence in education [2]. Studies performed in England and Slovakia on adolescent educational process have identified key factors influencing academic performance. These factors include motivation, stress levels and coping mechanisms, anxiety, self-esteem and self-efficacy, time management, and the methods and strategies employed to acquire academic knowledge [2, 3]. Academic performance is a critical indicator of adolescent success and achievement. It plays a vital role in child development, as skills such as reading and mathematics impact a wide range of outcomes, including educational attainment, career performance and income, physical and mental health, and overall life expectancy [4].

Social networks (SNs) refer to online platforms and digital tools that facilitate user interactions by allowing individuals to share information, express opinions, and engage with shared interests [5]. Instagram, Telegram, Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp, TikTok, Snapchat, and YouTube are among the most popular SNs and messaging applications. This also includes gaming and live-streaming platforms such as Twitch [6, 7]. Adolescents and young adults are increasingly spending time on SNs activities [8], as a result, engaging with SNs requires the dedication of various resources, including time, cost, and effort, which can significantly impact daily life in both positive and negative ways [9]. According to a report by Carat [10] on SNs usage among young people aged 8–17 years, 91% access SNs regularly. Usage is intensive, with more than 3 h per day from Monday to Friday and more than 5 h on weekends. Children typically have a smartphone at the age of 11 years, which is the primary device used for accessing SNs and it has been associated with more problematic patterns of use [11]. According to the report by Carat, Instagram is their preferred platform, followed by the growth of TikTok and gaming platforms. While SNs offer new ways of communication, evidence suggests connections with issues related to body image, isolation, depression, addictive behaviors, and bullying [10].

Researchers have explored whether SNs have a positive or negative relationship with academic performance [12, 13]. SNs usage among students can play a role in collaborative learning by helping teachers address educational challenges and increasing student engagement [12]. Platforms such as YouTube and Twitter could enhance interactive learning by supporting knowledge transfer through visual and auditory means while fostering engagement and collaboration among students, teachers, and peers, respectively [14, 15]. Additionally, SNs help students share ideas and research globally, supporting academic problem solving [16]. In contrast, some studies suggest that SNs have a negative effect on academic performance, especially when they are overused [17–19]. In this line, studies have shown that students experience distractions and lack focus due to excessive SNs use. Liu et al. conducted a meta-analysis of studies examining the correlation between SNs use and academic achievement, finding a small but significant negative association with GPA highlighting that excessive SNs use relates to lower academic outcomes, although the magnitude varied depending on factors such as type of SNs activity [20]. Contrarily, Huang et al. synthesized evidence across secondary school populations and found both positive and negative associations between SNs use and academic achievement. They emphasized that while SNs can support learning through engagement and information sharing, inappropriate or excessive use impacts academic results [21]. Additionally, studies have identified that adolescents abusing SNs use report less time to study, submitting assignments late, and struggling with poor spelling and grammar as a result of heavy SNs consumption [22, 23].

Studies have shown that overconsumption of SNs is linked to higher levels of procrastination and decreased productivity, impacting overall academic performance [24]. Adolescents and young adults are often the most vulnerable to these patterns of use, with some individuals developing addictive behaviors characterized by a persistent and uncontrollable urge to engage with social networking platforms. This addictive use of SNs represents the more severe end of a spectrum known as problematic media use, which encompasses behaviors ranging from occasional overuse to compulsive engagement that disrupts daily functioning. Unlike casual or even frequent use, addictive use is distinguished by symptoms such as loss of control, withdrawal-like experiences when access is restricted, and prioritization of online interactions over offline responsibilities and relationships [25]. This widespread use of SNs and the design features commonly used, while fostering connectivity and engagement with online communities, also has the potential to manipulate and influence young people, ultimately affecting their well-being in negative ways. As such, the impact of SNs on students’ academic and personal lives is complex, requiring a balanced approach to harness its benefits while mitigating its risks [16]. Consequently, concerns regarding possible addiction to SNs have emerged, being especially worrisome among the younger generations, who spend a significant part of their daily lives on these platforms [26].

In this context, the present study aims to evaluate the link between SNs and academic performance in Spanish adolescents. Specifically, we hypothesize that higher frequency of SNs use will be associated with lower academic performance, and that adolescents exhibiting addictive or compulsive patterns of SNs use will show even greater risk of academic difficulties. While SNs can enhance learning and communication, excessive use may lead to distractions, procrastination, and diminished academic outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a cross-sectional analysis using data from the Eating Healthy and Daily Life Activities (EHDLA) study. Participants were recruited during the 2021–2022 academic year from three secondary schools located in the Valle de Ricote (Region of Murcia, Spain). The selected schools (Cooperativa de Enseñanza (CE) El Ope, Instituto de Educación Secundaria (IES) Vicente Medina, and IES Pedro Guillén) collaborated to provide access to adolescents aged 12 to 17 years who were registered or residing in the region. Recruitment involved informing eligible students and their parents or legal guardians about the study objectives and procedures, followed by obtaining written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participants completed physical assessments and questionnaires during their Physical Education classes. The detailed methodology of the EHDLA study is described elsewhere [27].

The inclusion criteria established in the EDHLA study specified that participants had to meet the following conditions: [a] be between 12 and 17 years of age, and [b] be registered and/or reside in the Valle de Ricote. With respect to exclusion criteria, adolescents were not enrolled if they: [a] were exempt from the Physical Education subject at school, as both the tests and the completion of the questionnaires were carried out during these lessons [b], presented any pathology or medical condition that contraindicated the safe practice of physical activity or required special medical attention that prevented their full participation in the assessments [c], were undergoing any type of pharmacological treatment [d], did not have authorization from parents or legal guardians to participate in the research project, or [e] did not agree to take part in the study [27].

Of the 1378 adolescents initially assessed for eligibility, 115 (8.3%) were excluded due to missing information on GPA, leaving 1263 participants. A further 588 (42.7%) had incomplete data on SNs use, resulting in 675 participants. Of these, 36 (2.6%) were excluded due to missing body mass index (BMI) values, and an additional 23 (1.7%) were excluded for missing data on physical activity (YAP-S). Finally, 33 participants (2.4%) lacked valid information on energy intake. The final analytical sample therefore consisted of 583 adolescents (42.3% of the initial sample).

Variables

Social networks and messaging applications

The use of SNs (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, WhatsApp) was evaluated by a single-item scale asking adolescents what type of SNs they used, on the basis of five possible responses: (a) I never or rarely use it (b) I am a low consumer, (c) I am a medium consumer, (d) I am a fairly high consumer, or (e) I am a very high consumer. Each response was assigned a score from 0 (never or rarely) to 4 (very high consumer). The total SNs use score was calculated by summing the scores across all six platforms, resulting in a composite measure that reflects the overall frequency of SNs use for each participant. The instrument used to assess SNs use in this study is not a validated tool, but it has previously been employed in adolescent populations in Spain, notably in a survey of television and media preferences among adolescents in Barcelona conducted by Casas and Madorell (2007) [28–30]. In our sample, it showed Cronbach’s α = 0.60 and McDonald’s ω total = 0.70 (polychoric), which is expected given the heterogeneity across platforms. We therefore interpret this as a formative exposure index rather than a reflective latent construct.

Addictive behaviors toward social networks

The Short Social Networks Addiction Scale-6 Symptoms (SNAddS-6 S) was used to assess SNs addiction [31]. The SNAddS-6 S is a validated instrument specifically adapted for Spanish-speaking populations, including adolescents, designed to measure the degree of SNs addiction in individuals. It consists of six items that evaluate key aspects of SNs addiction, focusing on behaviors and psychological symptoms associated with excessive use. These aspects include: salience (social networking use becomes the individual’s primary concern and motivation), tolerance (the need to spend increasing amounts of time on SNs), mood modification (using SNs to alter mood, either by excitement or relaxation), withdrawal (experiencing psychological or physical symptoms when unable to access SNs), conflict (SNs use interfering with other social or daily activities), and relapse (returning to excessive use after attempts to control or reduce it). For each item, participants respond using dichotomous (yes/no) options, indicating whether they have experienced each symptom during the past year. The total score is calculated by summing the number of “yes” responses, with higher scores reflecting a greater degree of addictive SNs use. This scale has been shown to have a unidimensional factor structure with good internal consistency and construct validity among Spanish adolescents, making it appropriate for monitoring problematic and potentially addictive SNs use in this population [31]. Internal consistency was evaluated using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), a reliability coefficient equivalent to Cronbach’s α for dichotomous items. In our sample (n = 583), the SNAddS-6 S showed KR-20 = 0.65 and McDonald’s ω total = 0.80 based on a tetrachoric correlation matrix, indicating acceptable reliability for research use with dichotomous items.

Academic performance

School records were provided by each high school at the end of the academic year. Academic performance was assessed in two ways: first, the GPA was calculated as the mean of all academic subject scores for each student; second, individual evaluations were conducted in language, mathematics, and English as a foreign language [32]. In the Spanish education system, all grades are awarded on a scale from 0 to 10, and a higher grade means greater academic performance. Using official academic records ensures objectivity and accuracy in measuring educational outcomes. These standardized academic metrics are widely recognized and reliably capture variations in students’ achievements across different content areas.

Covariates

The relationship between academic performance and SNs in adolescents could be influenced by various factors that may mediate or moderate the effects of SNs, such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, physical activity, BMI, sleep duration, energy intake, and sedentary behavior [33, 34]. The adolescents self-reported their age and sex. Socioeconomic status was assessed via the Family Affluence Scale (FAS-III) [35], which evaluates six items: whether adolescents have their own bedroom, family ownership of a car and a dishwasher, the number of bathrooms in the home, the number of vacations taken outside Spain in the past year, and the number of household computers. The FAS-III score ranges from 0 to 13 points. Data on sedentary behavior and physical activity were collected via the Spanish-Youth Activity Profile (YAP-S) [36], in which scores are calculated by summing points from each section. Anthropometric measurements, such as height and body weight, were taken via a portable stadiometer (Leicester Tanita HR 001, Tokyo, Japan) and an electronic scale (Tanita BC-545, Tokyo, Japan), respectively. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Total sleep duration was assessed by asking participants the same questions for weekdays and weekends separately: “What time do you go to bed?” and “What time do you typically wake up?”. The average daily sleep duration for each participant was calculated in the following manner: [(average nocturnal sleep duration on weekdays × 5) + (average nocturnal sleep duration on weekends × 2)]/7 [27]. Energy intake was assessed via a self-reported dietary habits questionnaire that has been validated for the Spanish population [37].

Statistical analysis

To assess whether the variables followed a normal distribution, visual methods such as density plots and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were utilized, along with the Shapiro–Wilk test. These analyses indicated that none of the continuous variables, including GPA, followed a normal distribution. Therefore, all continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), whereas qualitative variables are reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), both for the overall sample and those classified by SNs use. Since no significant interaction was found between sex and SNs or messaging apps with addictive behaviors in relation to academic performance (p > 0.05 in all cases), all subsequent analyses were carried out including the total sample. All models were based on complete-case analyses. To evaluate the potential impact of missing data, we provided a descriptive comparability table contrasting the analytic sample with the full EHDLA dataset, allowing visual inspection of distributions across sociodemographic, lifestyle, exposure, and outcome variables. No major differences were observed, reducing concern about systematic bias due to missingness. A priori power calculations were performed to determine whether the sample size was sufficient. Based on the assumption of a moderate effect size (R2 = 0.13), a significance level of α = 0.05, and 80% statistical power, at least 114 participants would have been required to detect relevant associations in a generalized linear model (GLM) including nine covariates. As the final analytical sample comprised 583 adolescents, our study notably surpasses this requirement, ensuring adequate power to detect the hypothesized associations. Robust generalized linear models with Gaussian family were applied to examine the association between SNs use and GPA among adolescents as a continuous variable, adjusting for relevant covariates (sex, age, socioeconomic status, BMI, physical activity, sedentary behaviors, sleep duration, and energy intake). This approach does not rely on the strict normality of residuals, as robust estimation methods down-weight the influence of extreme observations, thereby providing valid inference under moderate deviations from normality. Additionally, unstandardized beta coefficients (B) were estimated to quantify the relationship, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) indicating the precision of the estimates. Standardization was performed by rescaling each predictor by its standard deviation relative to that of the outcome. For binary predictors, the observed standard deviation (√p(1 − p)) was used, providing directly interpretable standardized effect sizes. Standardized beta coefficients (β) were interpreted using conventional benchmarks (|β| ≈ 0.10 small, ≈ 0.30 moderate, ≈ 0.50 large) to facilitate the assessment of the practical significance of associations. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) using RStudio (version 2023.12.1 + 402; Posit, Boston, USA).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Approval for the EHDLA project was granted by the Bioethics Committee of the Universidad de Murcia (ID 2218/2018, approved on 18 February 2019) and the Ethics Committee of the Complejo Hospitalario Universitario de Albacete and the Gestión Asistencial Integrada de Albacete (ID 2021–86, approved on 23 November 2021). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki by the World Medical Association. With respect to participation in this research project, parents or legal guardians of the adolescents received a written informed consent form to sign in advance. In addition, both the parents or guardians and the participants were given an information sheet outlining the objectives of the study, along with details about the tests and questionnaires that would be administered. Adolescents were also asked about their willingness to take part in the study [27].

Results

With respect to the independent variables, the sample presented a median SNs use of 14.0 points (IQR 11.0 to 16.0), and the applications most used were TikTok, WhatsApp, and Instagram, all of which presented median scores of 4.0 points (IQR for TikTok 2.0 to 5.0; IQR for WhatsApp 3.0 to 4.0; IQR for Instagram 3.0 to 5.0). Discussion

Our study provides evidence that the use of SNs, specifically, when they become addictive, may be associated with lower academic performance among adolescents. The findings reveal a significant negative relationship between addictive behaviors related to SNs use and GPA, suggesting that higher levels of SNs addiction are linked to lower academic performance, independent of sociodemographic, anthropometic and lifestyle factors. However, further research is warranted to explore the causal pathways and potential moderating variables influencing this relationship. Among the platforms studied, Instagram, Snapchat, and Facebook showed a statistically significant association with lower GPA and the use of these SNs. In contrast, Twitter, TikTok, and WhatsApp did not show a significant association. Addictive behaviors, such as mood modification and relapse, showed the strongest associations with low GPA.

Although the exact reasons why SNs use and addictive behaviors toward it may lead to lower academic performance have not been exposed, there are some hypotheses that could explain these findings. First, research has identified a link between SNs multitasking and academic performance. A study conducted by Lau [12] revealed that SNs multitasking and nonacademic use of SNs negatively predict academic performance among university students. This study also highlighted that those students who frequently engaged in SNs multitasking, such as checking SNs while studying or completing academic tasks, experienced a decline in their GPA. This is likely due to the cognitive overload caused by dividing attention between multiple tasks, which reduces the ability of the brain to process and retain information effectively [38]. Similarly, a study conducted with Spanish adolescents revealed that those who engaged in SNs multitasking experienced a negative impact on their executive function and academic performance. Additionally, adolescents who frequently multitasked with media while doing homework reported higher levels of executive function difficulties [39].

Second, another factor that may explain the potential link between SNs abuse and low GPA may be attributed to decreased self-esteem and increased stress. A study conducted by Landa-Blanco et al. [24] revealed that SNs addiction could undermine academic engagement by reducing self-esteem and increasing depressive symptoms. In addition, stress plays a central role in the negative impact of SNs addiction on academic performance. SNs use has been shown to elevate stress through mechanisms such as constant connectivity, social comparison, and pressure to maintain an idealized online image. Additionally, social pressure to obtain more followers and likes increases stress levels, anxiety, and addictive behaviors [40]. Conversely, it is also possible that students who are already experiencing high levels of academic stress may turn to SNs as an avoidant coping strategy or as a means of mood regulation. This perspective is supported by the prominent role of mood modification observed in our findings, suggesting that students may use SNs to temporarily escape academic pressures or negative emotions. In this way, SNs use may serve as a short-term relief from stress, but over time, such coping behaviors could reinforce compulsive use patterns and further exacerbate academic difficulties. Recognizing this bidirectional relationship is important for understanding the complex relationship between SNs use, stress, and academic performance [41]. This prolonged stress can interfere with cognitive functions like attention and memory, ultimately impairing academic performance [42]. Thus, the combination of SNs addiction and elevated stress can create a harmful cycle that undermines student´s ability to succeed academically [40].

In addition, procrastination may be another potential link between SNs addiction and academic performance. Landa-Blanco et al. [24], also revealed that addiction to SNs negatively affects mental health and productivity by increasing procrastination. Another study conducted by Caratiquit and Caratiquit [43], found that among secondary school students, higher levels of SNs addiction were strongly associated with increased academic procrastination. This procrastination, in turn, had a significant negative impact on academic achievement, indicating that procrastination may mediate the relationship between SNs addiction and academic performance. The relationship between internet or SNs addiction and procrastination is further supported by studies focusing on university students. For example, research conducted by Chavez-Yacola et al. [44] found that academic self-efficacy may explain the relationship between internet addiction and academic procrastination. Students with higher levels of self-efficacy were less likely to develop internet addiction and, consequently, less prone to procrastinate academically. These behaviors reflect a loss of control over SNs use, leading to less time devoted to academic activities and an increased tendency to procrastinate.

From a psychodynamic and neurobehavioral perspective, addictive SNs use may involve dysregulation within the brain’s reward circuitry and executive control networks, particularly within dopaminergic pathways associated with reinforcement learning and self-control, according to the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model, individual vulnerabilities like, impulsivity and affective instability, interact with situational cues (e.g., social feedback, online rewards) to promote compulsive engagement [45]. For example, previous research has explored the role of dual-process theories in explaining persuasion and engagement on platforms such as Facebook. In this line, it has been examined how both peripheral (impulsive) and central (reflective) routes of processing affect user interaction with SNs content and highlights the cognitive mechanisms that drive online behavior and potentially addictive patterns [46]. In the context of SNs use and addiction, the dual-process framework suggests that excessive engagement arises when the impulsive system dominates, leading individuals to seek immediate gratification through notifications, likes, and novel content. The imbalance between heightened reward sensitivity and reduced inhibitory control results in compulsive patterns of SNs use, often persisting despite negative consequences [47]. Integrating these frameworks provides a more comprehensive understanding of the motivational and emotional processes that underlie problematic digital behavior.

Finally, our study also highlights that not all SNs have the same potential influence on academic performance. This finding is consistent with research by Junco [48], who reported that certain SNs platforms, particularly those designed for rapid content consumption and frequent updates (e.g., Instagram and Facebook), are more likely to distract students and reduce their academic engagement. This aligns with our observation that platforms such as Instagram, Snapchat, and Facebook, which constantly draw users back to check for new content, interfere with their study time and concentration, are more strongly associated with lower GPAs than other platforms, such as Twitter or messaging apps like WhatsApp. With regard to TikTok, the absence of a significant association with academic performance may be explained by its predominant use as a passive entertainment platform, which likely entails less social pressure and fewer opportunities for social comparison than the demands of online self-presentation on Instagram or Facebook. It is important to highlight that our results also support the growing evidence that the use of SNs itself is not inherently associated with lower academic performance. The study conducted by Lau [12] identified that functional use of SNs does not necessarily interfere with student´s academic responsibilities and, in some cases, may even provide benefits such as access to educational resources, enhanced digital literacy, and improved social connection. Furthermore, another study by Valkenburg et al. [49] found that the frequency or duration of SNs use alone does not predict lower grades or academic disengagement, as some adolescents are able to balance their academic obligations with recreational or communicative use of these platforms.

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. As our analysis is cross-sectional, causal inferences regarding the relationship between addictive SNs use and academic performance should not be made. It remains unclear whether problematic use leads to poorer performance or whether students struggling academically are more likely to seek online engagement as a coping strategy. Longitudinal and experimental research will be necessary to establish temporal directionality and identify mediating mechanisms such as stress regulation, procrastination, or cognitive overload. The instrument used to measure SNs use is a composite frequency index that is not validated and may reflect only an approximate measure of overall SNs use. This introduces potential measurement errors and limits construct validity. Any such non-differential error would most likely attenuate associations toward the null, making our estimates conservative. A notable limitation of our study concerns the relatively high attrition rate, as approximately 60% of the initial cohort was excluded due to incomplete data. Although our comparability analyses showed no major sociodemographic or behavioral differences between included and excluded participants, some residual bias cannot be fully ruled out, particularly in relation to socioeconomic or regional disparities in digital access. Although our complete-case analyses reduced the sample size, we present a descriptive comparability table (Table S1) contrasting the full cohort with GPA data and the analytic sample. A visual inspection of these distributions suggests broadly similar profiles across sociodemographic, lifestyle, and academic variables, with no major differences observed. This reduces concern about selection bias, although some residual bias cannot be entirely excluded if missingness was not completely at random. Some covariates (e.g., sleep, sedentary behavior) were self-reported and may be prone to recall bias. Finally, the sample comes from three schools in one region, which may limit generalizability. Future longitudinal designs should incorporate strategies to minimize missing data and ensure representativeness across diverse socioeconomic contexts. Our study also has important strengths that must be considered. We included in the analyses many covariates, such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, BMI, physical activity, sedentary behavior, sleep duration, and energy intake, enhancing the validity of the statistical results. Additionally, we also analyzed various SNs separately to assess their distinct effects, providing key insights for addictive behaviors. This study offers evidence on the relationship between addictive behaviors toward SNs use and academic performance by distinguishing between frequent use and addictive behavior. The findings indicate that only patterns of addictive SNs use, rather than general frequency of use, are associated with lower GPA. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to clarify the directionality and underlying mechanisms of these associations. Developing educational interventions focused on mindful technology use may be crucial for encouraging healthy habits. The results presented here provide evidence for advancing the study of academic well-being in today’s digital era.

Conclusions

There was no significant association between overall SNs use and GPA. However, a significant negative association was observed for addictive behaviors related to SNs use. These findings highlight the need for educational interventions that address problematic or addictive patterns of SN use, fostering self-regulation and healthy digital habits while preserving the benefits of online connectivity. However, prospective cohort or experimental studies are necessary to unravel these temporal relationships and to identify causal pathways, mediators, and moderators underlying observed associations. Future initiatives should provide students with strategies and raise awareness about the potential academic costs of uncontrolled SNs consumption. Preventive strategies should move beyond educational awareness alone and include interventions aimed at strengthening self-regulation, impulse control, and stress management. Integrating such approaches into school-based mental health programs could mitigate the potential academic and psychological harms of excessive SNs use while preserving the social and communicative benefits of the digital era.
