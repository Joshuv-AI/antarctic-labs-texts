Cellular adhesion-dependent 3D morphogenesis indicating brain tumor aggressiveness and chemosensitivity in spherical cavity culture
Authors: Thuy Huynh, Jieun Kim, Sarnai Amartumur, Inhee Choi, Ji Yeoun Lee, Luke P. Lee, Chaejeong Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malignancies of the nervous system remain a critical challenge in oncology, contributing substantially to cancer-related mortality in children and adults. Their aggressive behavior is influenced by adhesion heterogeneity of the tumor cells, as distinct patterns of cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix interactions shape tumor organization, invasion, and resistance to conventional treatments. Here, we investigate tumor adhesion dynamics in a 3D spherical cavity culture platform that enables the rapid and high-throughput formation of uniform spheroids for parallel morphogenesis, adhesion profiling, and drug screening. The morphogenetic patterns of human neuroblastoma and patient-derived glioblastoma observed in 3D cavity culture closely correlated with tumor aggressiveness, ranging from adhesive layers in benign tumor subtype to compact spheroids in malignant cells and irregular aggregates in highly invasive, cancer stem-like counterparts. These morphologies corresponded to adhesion profiles: aggressive cells displayed elevated N-cadherin with variable integrin, while compact spheroids maintained a balance of both molecules. Blocking N-cadherin with ADH-1 disrupted spheroid integrity, and combining ADH-1 with DOX yielded synergistic cytotoxic effects in malignant phenotypes, highlighting N-cadherin upregulation in tumor cells with higher aggressiveness in 3D culture. Overall, our dynamic 3D spherical cavity culture enables reproducible 3D adhesion phenotyping, providing a simple method for malignancy assessment and supporting adhesion-targeted therapeutic strategies for personalized medicine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00785-1.

To the editor

Malignancies of the nervous system, including neuroblastoma (NB) and glioblastoma (GBM), remain among the most aggressive cancers [1]. The variable expression and function of adhesion molecules is a critical contributor to both tumors’ clinical heterogeneity, with NB cells exhibiting adhesion heterogeneity among its subtypes, while adhesion molecules in GBM play a central role in invasion [2, 3]. Key molecules – such as N-cadherin and integrins – play pivotal roles in maintaining 3D tumor architecture, influencing cell invasion, and ultimately therapy resistance. Since efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents including doxorubicin (DOX) is limited by adhesion-mediated resistance mechanisms, disruption of intercellular cohesion may enhance therapeutic sensitivity [4]. Previous studies have highlighted the critical roles of adhesion molecules in spheroids derived from ovarian and colon cancer cells [5, 6]. However, the application of three-dimensional (3D) models to study adhesion mechanisms in brain tumors is still lacking, leaving a significant gap in understanding their heterogenous behaviors [7].

Various 3D culture systems, including hanging-drop spheroids, hydrogel encapsulations, and tumor organoids, have been employed to study adhesion-dependent behavior in brain tumors [7–9]. These platforms provide valuable insights into cell-matrix interactions and tumor heterogeneity but often incorporate exogenous extracellular matrix (ECM) components. As a result, intrinsic adhesion-dependent behaviors can be difficult to isolate. Here, we employed a spherical cavity culture platform designed to minimize substrate attachment and impose defined geometric confinement, allowing cell-cell adhesion driven morphogenesis to emerge under reduced matrix variability.

The cavity features a constricted upper opening and bulb-shaped lower chamber, which promotes centralized aggregation compared with conventional round-bottom wells (Figure S1-4) [10]. In this environment, NB subtypes displayed distinct morphogenetic patterns that corresponded to their aggressiveness (Fig. 1a-b). Cultures were maintained on a shaker at 10 rpm for the first 48 h, which improved single spheroid formation efficiency of SH-SY5Y spheroids and BE-(2)C aggregates, but not SH-EP monolayers (Fig. 1c, S5-7).

Using Tuj1 and vimentin, we confirmed the correct identification of NB subtypes (Fig. 1d). Quantitative analysis of spheroid circularity further verified the compactness of SH-SY5Y spheroids vs. the aggregates formed by BE(2)-C cells (Fig. 1e). Meanwhile, SH-EP cells adhered and spread across the PDMS cavity surface over time (Fig. 1f). Notably, 48 h represents a critical time point at which all three NB subtypes can be reliably distinguished by their morphogenetic behaviors.

We also assessed the platform’s applicability for GBM primary cells from two patients, SNUH-NG-27 and SNUH-NG-65, who demonstrated markedly different invasion potentials (Fig. 1g). These patient-derived GBM cells (NG-27 and NG-65) were previously characterized, with corresponding tumoroids preserving tumor features and invasion phenotypes consistent with their patient origins [8]. Additionally, another localized GBM (SNU-5399 cells) and a diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG50 cells) were also investigated. Distinct differences in dynamics and morphology between four patient-derived cells were observed when culture in spherical cavities. NG-27 and SNU-5399 formed compact spheroids, exhibiting complete structural maturation over time. In contrast, NG-65 and DIPG50 formed irregular aggregates over the growth period (Fig. 1h-i, S8a).

Immunofluorescence investigation of adhesion molecular mechanisms of NB cultures revealed the localization of integrin and N-cadherin (Fig. 2a), corroborated by Western blot quantification (Figs. 2b-c, S9a). A schematic summarizes 3D adhesion modes: SH-EP rely on integrin to spread, SH-SY5Y balance N-cadherin and integrin to form compact spheroids, and BE(2)-C overexpress N-cadherin but lack integrin, resulting in loose aggregates (Fig. 2d). Patient-derived cells followed similar tendency (Fig. 2e-g, S8b, S9b). NG-27 and SNU-5399, derived from less invasive tumors, downregulated integrin while upregulating N-cadherin in 3D. In contrast, NG-65 and DIPG50, derived from highly invasive tumors, mainly elevated N-cadherin in 3D.

Since 3D cultures within the cavity rely heavily on N-cadherin, we assessed whether disrupting N-cadherin alters therapeutic response using ADH-1, a well-characterized N-cadherin antagonist that has undergone clinical trials in solid tumors [11]. When malignant cells were cultured in 3D, their sensitivity to ADH-1 increased significantly (Fig. 2h; Figure S10). Co-treatment further enhanced cytotoxic effects compared with DOX alone. DOX IC50 lowered from ~ 35 µg/mL to ~ 15 µg/mL in SH-SY5Y spheroids and from ~ 52 µg/mL to ~ 18 µg/mL in BE-2 C aggregates (Fig. 2i). A similar shift was observed in GBM, where the IC₅₀ of DOX decreased from ~ 120 µg/mL to ~ 50 µg/mL in NG-27 spheroids and from ~ 160 µg/mL to ~ 95 µg/mL in NG-65 aggregates (Fig. 2j). Notably, the DOX IC₅₀ in NG-65 aggregates remained substantially elevated even after ADH-1 co-treatment may indicate incomplete sensitization due to their persistence of dual adhesion signaling [12].

ADH-1 blocks N-cadherin interactions resulting in spheroid destabilization and enhanced DOX efficacy (Fig. 2k). These findings underscore N-cadherin as a critical regulator for drug resistance in neural malignancies and demonstrate that 3D adhesion phenotyping captures clinically relevant adhesion-dependent vulnerabilities that are not detectable in 2D culture. Given the limited number of patient-derived GBM samples, however, these observations remain preliminary and will require validation in larger cohorts. Expanded cohorts of both NB and GBM patient-derived cells as well as targeting dual cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesion pathways, since this platform does not reproduce the full biochemical complexity of the tumor microenvironment, will be beneficial in future works.

In conclusion, our 3D spherical cavity platform provides a simple readout of brain tumor aggressiveness by linking morphogenesis to the dynamics of different adhesion molecules during aggregation of tumor cells. These results highlight 3D adhesion phenotyping as a translational approach for malignancy assessment and therapeutic development.
