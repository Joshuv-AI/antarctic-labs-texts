Towards Copyright Protection for Knowledge Bases of Retrieval-augmented Language Models via Reasoning
Junfeng Guo, Yiming Li, Ruibo Chen, Yihan Wu, Chenxi Liu, Yanshuo Chen, Heng Huang

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into real-world personalized applications through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mechanisms to supplement their responses with domain-specific knowledge. However, the valuable and often proprietary nature of the knowledge bases used in RAG introduces the risk of unauthorized usage by adversaries. Existing methods that can be generalized as watermarking techniques to protect these knowledge bases typically involve poisoning or backdoor attacks. However, these methods require altering the LLM’s results of verification samples ( e . g . e.g. , generating incorrect outputs), inevitably making these watermarks susceptible to anomaly detection and even introducing new security risks. To address these challenges, we propose RAG \raisebox{-.9pt} {C}⃝ {}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}} for ‘harmless’ copyright protection of knowledge bases. Instead of manipulating LLM’s final output, RAG \raisebox{-.9pt} {C}⃝ {}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}} implants distinct yet benign verification behaviors in the space of chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, maintaining the correctness of the final answer. Our method has three main stages: (1) Generating CoTs : For each verification question, we generate two ‘innocent’ CoTs, including a target CoT for building watermark behaviors; (2) Optimizing Watermark Phrases and Target CoTs : Inspired by our theoretical analysis, we optimize them to minimize retrieval errors under the black-box and text-only setting of suspicious LLM, ensuring that only watermarked verification queries can retrieve their correspondingly target CoTs contained in the knowledge base; (3) Ownership Verification : We exploit a pairwise Wilcoxon test to verify whether a suspicious LLM is augmented with the protected knowledge base by comparing its responses to watermarked and benign verification queries. Our experiments on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that RAG \raisebox{-.9pt} {C}⃝ {}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}} effectively protects knowledge bases and its resistance to adaptive attacks.

Background and Related Work

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).
RAG is a technique designed to enhance the capabilities of LLMs by integrating external knowledge sources (
i.e.
, knowledge bases)

27
]

Unlike traditional LLMs, which generate responses solely based on the knowledge encoded during pre-training, RAG combines both retrieval and generation mechanisms to produce more accurate, contextually relevant, and up-to-date outputs. Currently, existing RAG systems implemented dual encoders to map queries and texts within the knowledge base into the embedding space and retrieve candidate texts that produce high similarity values with the given query. Recent works were proposed to improve the effectiveness of retrieval models by implementing different encoder architectures

Numerous real-world sectors are deploying RAG systems to enhance the LLM service, where the external knowledge bases contain private and proprietary data. Recently, several companies (

Methodology

3.1
Preliminaries and Threat Model

The Main Pipeline of Retrieval-augmented LLMs (RA-LLMs).
In this paper, we discuss LLMs built with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mechanism under a knowledge base
\mathcal{D}
based on the prompt corpus. Specifically, the knowledge base
\mathcal{D}
contains a set of in-context query-solution examples
\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}}
, where
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
represent the query and its corresponding solution within the retrial knowledge base
\mathcal{D}
, respectively. In RAG, for each given query
\bm{x}
, the retrieval model uses an encoder
E_{q}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{q})
parameterized by
\bm{\theta}_{q}
to map it into the embedding space via
E_{q}(\bm{x};\theta_{q})
and seeks the most relevant samples within
\mathcal{D}
based on their similarity (
i.e.
, cosine similarity). Technically, RAG finds
k
nearest examples within
\mathcal{D}
of
\bm{x}
(dubbed
\varepsilon_{k}(\bm{x},\mathcal{D})
) in the embedding space through KNN search
[
7
]
. After retrieving
\varepsilon_{k}(\bm{x},\mathcal{D})
, RAG arranges these instances and
\bm{x}
into an augmented input text
\bm{x}_{r}
using a specifically designed template. Finally, the (pre-trained) LLM
f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{l})
takes
\bm{x}_{r}
as input to perform in-context learning and output the generated text
f(\bm{x}_{r};\bm{\theta}_{l})
.

Threat Model.
Following previous works in data copyright protection, we consider two main parties, including the defender (
i.e.
, external knowledge base’s owner) and the adversary (
i.e.
, who owns the LLM but unauthorized uses the protected external knowledge base), in our threat model. Specifically, the adversaries intend to ‘steal’ and misuse the protected knowledge base released by the defender to improve their developed LLM via RAG to design RA-LLM without authorization. In contrast, the defender aims to protect the copyright of their valuable knowledge base by verifying whether a given suspicious model is augmented with it. In particular, we consider the most practical and stringent defender’s settings,
i.e.
,
black-box
and
text-only
setting, where the defender can only query the suspicious LLM with prompts and get its correspondingly generated content through its API.

{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
requires an advanced LLM to generate two distinctive yet innocent CoTs for each defender-specified verification question corresponding to the correct answer. In the second stage, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
further optimizes the watermark phrase and its corresponding target CoTs, leading the watermarked question and target CoT to be far away from the other knowledge base’s instances related to the question in the embedding space of the target retrieval model. After that, the optimized target CoT and its non-target version for each question would be injected into the protected knowledge base, which will be retrieved by the retriever, thus affecting the CoT process for answering the corresponding question (via the in-context learning). In the third stage, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
verifies the copyright by examining whether a given suspicious LLM’s answers on verification questions contain the information of their corresponding target CoTs.

The Overview of RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}

Definition 3.1

((Empirical) Harmfulness Degree of Ownership Verification for Knowledge Base)
.

Let
\mathcal{\hat{D}}=\{(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
indicates the pairs of questions and results for ownership verification of a RAG system with the LLM
f

\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}

H\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\{\bm{y}_{i}\not\in f(\hat{\bm{x}}_{i})\}

where

is the indicator function.

In general, the aforementioned (empirical) harmfulness degree is measured by the abnormal/incorrect degree of the RA-LLM in its response to the verification questions. This is the approximation/surrogate of the ‘strict’ harmfulness that measures whether the watermarking process is completely undetectable or maliciously exploited by adversaries. Arguably, it is almost impossible to provide a formal definition of strict harmfulness degree, due to the complexity of DNNs.

According to

), including optimization-based and LLM-based ones. However, we find that using optimization-based method for Eq.(

) is highly or even unbearably costly as the target text can be much longer than that of the watermark phase. Therefore, we hereby directly exploit the LLM-based method to solve it. As we will analyze in our ablation study, this approach is still highly effective. Notably, the optimization of the watermarked phrase and the target CoT are entangled. In this paper, we optimize the watermark phases first and then the target CoTs. Besides, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
with optimization-based watermark generation is dubbed as ‘RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
-O’ while RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
with LLM-based watermark generation is dubbed as ‘RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
-L’.

After obtaining the optimized target CoT
\bm{t}
and the watermark phrase
\bm{\delta}
, we will inject the watermarked target CoT (
i.e.
,
\bm{t\oplus\delta}
) along with its non-target versions for each pre-defined question into the protected knowledge base. We expect that when the watermarked verification question
\bm{x\oplus\delta}
presents, the retriever
E_{q}(\cdot;\bm{\theta_{q}})
will retrieve the (watermarked) target CoT as the context to help answer it (via in-context learning). As such,
\bm{t\oplus\delta}
within the context will affect the reasoning process (revealed by our designed query template in
Appendix

B
) for giving the correct answer.

3.4
Stage 3: Ownership Verification in RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}

RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
hereby identifies whether a given suspicious LLM is augmented with our protected knowledge base by querying it with the original and watermarked verification questions. Specifically, we query the suspicious LLM
f
with any verification question
\bm{x}
and its watermarked version
\bm{x}\oplus\bm{\delta}
to determine whether their answers contain the information of their corresponding target CoT (
i.e.
,
\bm{t}\in f(\bm{x}\oplus\bm{\delta})
and
\bm{t}\notin f(\bm{x})
). Given the complexity and diversity of natural languages, we leverage the power of advanced LLMs (
e.g.
, GPT-4) to judge it. We put the designed template used by GPT-4 in
Section

B.4
. In particular, to reduce the side effects of randomness in selecting verification questions, we design a hypothesis-test-guided method for ownership verification, as follows.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
X
,
X^{\prime}
,
T
denote the variable of verification question, its watermarked version, and its target CoT, respectively. For a suspicious large language model
f
, suppose
C
is the judgment function,
i.e.
,
C(X^{\prime})\triangleq 2\cdot\mathbb{I}\{T\in f(X^{\prime})\}-1
and
C(X)\triangleq 2\cdot\mathbb{I}\{T\in f(X)\}-1
. Given the null hypothesis
H_{0}
:
\mathcal{C}(X^{\prime})+\mathcal{C}(X)=0
(
H_{1}
:
\mathcal{C}(X^{\prime})+\mathcal{C}(X)>0
), we claim that it is built with the protected knowledge base if and only if
H_{0}
is rejected.

In practice, we randomly select
m
(
i.e.
, 100) verification questions (as well as their watermarked versions and target CoTs) for the ownership verification. Specifically, we hereby use the pairwise Wilcoxon test
[
40
]
since the results of the judgment function
C
are discrete. The null hypothesis
H_{0}
is rejected if and only the p-value is smaller than the significance level
\alpha
(
e.g.
, 0.01).

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
to protect the copyright of knowledge bases used in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) of large language models (LLMs). By leveraging chain-of-thought reasoning instead of manipulating final outputs, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
offers a ‘harmless’ watermarking method for ownership verification that maintains the correctness of the generated answers of LLMs augmented with the protected knowledge base. RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
leveraged optimized watermark phrases and verification questions to detect potential misuse through hypothesis test-guided ownership verification. We also provided the theoretical insights for the design of our RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
. Extensive experiments on benchmark datasets verified the effectiveness of RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
and its resistance to potential adaptive attacks.

G.1
The Detailed Comparison of Copyright-related Works for RA-LLMs

CPR
[
12
]
proposed copyright-protected RAG to provide copyright protection guarantees in a mixed-private setting for diffusion models. CPR focused on addressing privacy leakage issues in the generation procedure of diffusion models. However, the copyright protection technique for the knowledge base of RAG remains blank. SILO
[
34
]
aims to investigate how RAG can be used to improve results without training with private data, while we focus on how to protect the copyright of the RAG’s knowledge bases. Arguably, we have completely different settings, but equally important.
[
22
]
requires embedding watermark patterns into LLMs instead of modifying the knowledge base, whose threat model is different from ours as
RAG^{C}
is not assumed to modify LLM but can only embed the watermark into the corresponding knowledge base.

Another recent set of works
[
57
,
21
,
38
,
39
]
focused on exploring or mitigating the privacy vulnerabilities for RAG, which has a quite different goal from ours.
For example,
[
57
]
proposes to mitigate privacy issues for RAG by replacing real private data with synthetic data, which considers a different scenario from ours and will hinder the utility and information of the knowledge base. We notice that some of these works
[
21
,
38
,
39
]
can maliciously extract private information within the knowledge base for the victim RAG. However, such extraction of private knowledge doesn’t exhibit distinguishable signals or information to verify/prove the ownership of these knowledge bases. Therefore, they are not capable of ownership verification approaches for RAG.

G.2
The Comparison to Membership Inference Attacks

In the previous main manuscript, we argue that (watermarking)-based dataset ownership verification is currently the only feasible method to protect the copyright of public datasets including the knowledge bases used for augmenting the LLMs as discussed in this paper. However, we also notice that some people may argue that we can also use membership inference attacks (MIAs)
[
42
,
18
,
17
]
as fingerprints of the dataset to design ownership verification. Specifically, the dataset owner can verify whether (most of) the samples in the victim dataset are members of the suspicious third-party model. If so, we can assume that dataset stealing has occurred. In this section, we compare our method (as the representative of watermarking-based methods) to MIA-based methods and explain why we believe these methods may not be suitable for protecting the copyright of knowledge bases.

In general, MIA-based methods tend to lead to a high false-positive rate
[
10
]
,
i.e.
, mistakenly treat an innocent model as an infringing model. This is mostly because MIA-based methods leverage only the inherent features in the dataset
[
30
,
31
,
10
]
and treat semantically similar samples (
e.g.
, paraphrased sentences) also as members. Accordingly, it usually misjudges especially when two (independent) datasets share similar features. This problem becomes more serious in protecting the knowledge base of augmented LLMs since samples used for pre-training and fine-tuning instead of solely samples in the knowledge base can also be regarded as members based on the generation of augmented LLMs. In contrast, watermarking-based methods (like our RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
) can implant external features that will not included in datasets other than the protected one, therefore, avoiding this problem.

Appendix H
Potential Limitations and Future Directions

Firstly, as outlined in our threat model, the goal of our defense is consistent with previous work on dataset ownership verification (DOV)
[
29
,
15
]
that we aim to trace the utilization of the protected knowledge base. Our approach can not prevent the protected knowledge base from being misused or stolen in a proactive manner. In the future, we will explore a new approach that can prevent the knowledge base from being misused a in a proactive manner.

Secondly, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
requires conducting optimization on the watermark phrase for each verification question and corresponding target CoTs, requiring certain computational resources. In the future, we will explore how to further improve our efficiency.

Thirdly, RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
primarily focuses on the pure language models and can not directly be applied to the multimodal setting, such as the Vision Language Model. We will further explore a more generalized approach that can perform effectively across different tasks and architectures of models in the future.

Appendix I
Discussions on Adopted Data

In this paper, we only use open-source datasets for evaluation. Our research strictly obeys the open-source licenses of these datasets and does not lead to any privacy issues. These datasets may contain some personal information, although we don’t know whether it’s true or not. Nevertheless, our work treats all instances equally and does not intentionally manipulate these elements. The injected watermark phases also do not contain any malicious semantics. As such, our work complies with the requirements of these datasets and should not be construed as a violation of personal privacy.

Appendix J
Social Impact

Unauthorized knowledge base misuse and stealing have posed a serious threat to the intellectual property rights (IPRs) of the knowledge base’s owners. In general, the knowledge base plays a critical role in the effectiveness of the RAG, containing valuable and often proprietary content. As such, they are valuable intellectual property of their owners. Arguably, ownership verification via watermarking knowledge bases is a promising solution to detect whether a suspicious LLM is (unauthorizedly) augmented by the protected knowledge base. In this paper, we propose a new paradigm of harmless knowledge base ownership verification, named RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
. Our RAG
{}^{\scriptsize\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\raisebox{-.9pt} {C}}}}
is purely defensive and harmless, which does not introduce new threats. Moreover, our work only exploits the open-source benchmark and does not infringe on the privacy of any individual or real-world commercial systems. As such, this work does not raise any ethical issues and negative societal impacts in general.
