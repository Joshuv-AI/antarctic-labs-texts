Research on cuttings and flower yield improvement of marigold cv. Siracole for sustainable production through Jeevamrit application
Authors: Nitesh Kaushal, Bharati Kashyap, Balbir Singh Dilta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A sustainable approach of farming involves using desi-cow waste products helps to improve soil biology. Jeevamrit, a traditional biofertilizer, enhances soil health and organic carbon and also reduces the need for farmyard manure, hence minimizing carbon footprint and supports eco-friendly agriculture. The present study investigated the effectiveness of Jeevamrit compared to chemical fertilizers and growth regulators for maintaining a healthy mother block of marigold. Jeevamrit was applied at a rate of 2 L per square meter every 15 days in the mother block. A comprehensive evaluation of seven nutritional and growth regulator modules was conducted across five harvesting flushes (H1, H2, H3, H4, and H5), with subsequent planting occurring in both summer and winter, and vegetative parameters compared to other treatments. Comparing cuttings from various treatments, Jeevamrit-treated plants had higher diameter (3.64 mm), rooting percentage (77.62 %), roots per cutting (35.46), survival rate (81.59 %), faster rooting (14.90 days), the microbial analysis shows a higher microbial population i.e. bacterial (18.27 × 105 cfu g−1 Soil), fungal (3.64 × 103 cfu g−1 Soil), and actinomycetes (3.12 × 103 cfu g−1 Soil) in soil and a higher uptake of N (17.73 kg ha −1), P (9.48 kg ha −1), and K (15.03 kg ha −1) by plants resulting into better growth and development. Yet, the highest cutting yield per plant (92.92) and m−2 (826.64) was observed with foliar application of benzyl adenine @ 100 ppm combined with N-P-K @ 30:20:20 g/m2. Among the harvesting flushes, H4 yielded the highest number of cuttings per plant (109.39) and per square meter (973.34). In contrast, H1 achieved the best results for rooting percentage (81.90 %), number of roots per cutting (29.78), survival percentage (78.07 %), and the shortest rooting time (14.49 days). Additionally, H3 showed the highest microbial activity, with viable bacterial counts at 14.38 × 105 cfu g−1, fungal counts at 3.56 × 103 cfu g−1, actinomycetes at 2.84 × 103 cfu g−1, and organic carbon content at 0.97 %. Under the second experiment the plants grown under natural farming produce more number (0.76 %) and yield of marketable flowers per plant (3.77 %) over recommended dose of fertilizers. The study concludes that while Jeevamrit significantly enhances plant growth and soil health, the combination of benzyl adenine with a balanced N-P-K fertilizer results in higher cutting yield. Moreover, Jeevamrit has been identified as an effective alternative to chemical fertilizers for the cultivation of marigold, specifically in the production of cuttings and flowers.

Introduction

Tagetes erecta L. belongs to the Asteraceae family and is commonly found in mountainous regions and tropical climates, mainly in South America. The genus Tagetes consists of over 50 species, with T. erecta, T. tenuifolia, T. lunata, and T. patula being the most widely cultivated worldwide [1]. In Brazil, T. erecta is commonly referred to as marigold, while in Mexico, Central America, and other parts of South America, it is known by various names such as cempasúchil, amarillo, yellow carnation, and flor de muerto [1]. Due to the intense yellow to orange coloration, the flowers are used in various industries, due to the high concentration of the carotenoid lutein [[2], [3], [4]]. The flowers of marigold are also used as an ingredient in salads and as a natural food colorant, since it is one of the most popular edible flowers all over the world [5]. Flowers are utilized in various applications, including pharmaceuticals, processed foods, confectionery, and the poultry industry. Notably, one of the most significant benefits of these plants is their role as a valuable intercrop for the control of plant-parasitic nematodes and their insecticidal properties [6].

The cultivar ‘Siracole’ (commonly called as ‘Laddu Gainda’ in India), which was identified in Eastern India [7]. The main specialty of this cultivar is uniform size of flowers and bushy growth of foliage. Due to uniform size of flower and higher yield, this variety is in great demand and fetched higher prices in the market. Marigold cultivar ‘Siracole’ is mainly propagated by terminal stem cuttings for which a mother block is raised from which cuttings for rooting are taken. Maintenance of mother block of marigold is of utmost importance as only a healthy mother block can supply healthy cuttings, for getting higher yield.

The marigold cultivar ‘Siracole’ is primarily propagated through cuttings rather than seeds due to several advantages that enhance its cultivation efficiency and reliability. Cuttings ensure genetic consistency, allowing new plants to be genetically identical to the parent, thereby preserving desirable traits such as flower color, size, and disease resistance [8]. This method facilitates rapid establishment with faster root development and earlier flowering, which is especially beneficial for commercial growers seeking a timely return on investment [9]. Additionally, plants propagated from cuttings demonstrate greater uniformity in growth patterns and flowering times, crucial for landscape design and aesthetic consistency [10]. Moreover, certain traits that confer disease resistance in ‘Siracole’ can be more reliably transferred through cuttings, as opposed to the genetic variability encountered with seeds. Furthermore, the exclusivity of some cultivars under patent protection necessitates the use of cuttings to maintain control over production. Finally, the cuttings method is typically less resource-intensive, reducing production costs and minimizing environmental impact. Overall, these factors make cutting propagation the preferred choice for both commercial growers and researchers working with ‘Siracole’.

There is only a scanty literature available on maintenance of mother block, therefore under the present studies the effect of different doses of nutrients and growth regulators viz. Jeevamrit, NPK, BA and GA3 were assessed for cutting production in marigold. The application of Gibberellic Acid (GA3) on marigold (Tagetes spp.) hold significant importance in influencing shoot morphology, including parameters such as shoot length and shoot weight. Gibberellic acid (GA3) is a crucial plant hormone that regulates multiple facets of plant growth and development. In the context of plant responses to heavy metal contamination, GA3 has been shown to have a significant impact on plant physiology and biochemistry. The application of GA3 significantly enhances plant length, fresh and dries mass, leaf chlorophyll contents, ionic contents, and yield [11,12]. Gibberellic acid promotes the growth of roots, shoots, and leaf number by modifying the mechanisms of cell division and cell elongation [13]. GA3 application also enhances total chlorophyll content in plant [14].

Benzyladenine (BA), a cytokinin classified as a plant growth regulator, plays a pivotal role in promoting basal shoot proliferation. Its effects include the stimulation of cell division, cell elongation, and shoot initiation, which collectively enhance plant growth. Additionally, BA facilitates nutrient mobilization and delays senescence, while also modulating apical dominance, thereby promoting lateral shoot growth [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the application of benzylaminopurine (BA), a synthetic plant hormone, can induce multiple branching and control flowering time in Veronica dahurica Steven, as reported by Kim et al. [16].

Jeevamrit, an economically viable and ecologically friendly fermented organic formulation, is prepared from cow-derived substances, namely dung, urine, as well as additional components such as gram flour and jaggery. Cow urine, containing antifungal properties and serving as a valuable reservoir of plant nutrients, forms a key constituent of Jeevamrit. This preparation serves as an effective enhancer of plant growth with versatile application methods [17]. The utilization of various fermented liquid organic formulations further underscores their efficacy in bolstering soil fertility through the amplification of soil microorganism and fauna activities. This formulation substantially catalyzes biological processes within the soil, demonstrated by its capacity to foster an elevated abundance of beneficial microorganisms [18].

Sustainable agriculture practices minimize environmental impact are crucial for maintaining soil health and also promote plant growth. One such approach involves the utilization of desi-cow waste products, i.e. Jeevamrit, a traditional biofertilizer that has been shown to enhance soil biology, and reduce the need for farmyard manure [19]. This not only supports eco-friendly agriculture but also provides the essential nutrients required for growth and development of plant, which rely on compounds like Benzyladenine, gibberellic acid, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium [20].

The farmers of Himachal Pradesh currently purchase cuttings of marigold from West Bengal, India, highlighting the need to standardize nutrient requirements and produce local planting material. To address this issue, our study investigates the efficacy of Jeevamrit compared to conventional chemical fertilizers and growth regulators in maintaining a healthy mother block of marigold. Furthermore, we examine the comparative effect of these two approaches on the flower yield of marigold. By comparing the effect of Jeevamrit and chemical fertilizers, we aim to provide insights into the potential of this natural fertilizer to promote healthy plant growth and optimize flower production.

Material and methods

The investigation was carried out in the Department of Floriculture and Landscape Architecture at the Experimental Research Farm of Dr. YS Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry, Nauni, Solan, Himachal Pradesh, India during the summer and winter seasons of 2022-23 and 2023-24. The Experimental farm of the department is located in the hilly regions of Western Himalayas at an altitude of 1276 m above mean sea level having latitude of 30° 52′02″ N and longitude 70°11′30″E. Approximately 75 % of the regional average annual rainfall, totaling 1115 mm, is received during the monsoon season, which extends from mid-June to mid-September. Harvesting methodology for cuttings

Timing of harvesting

The initial harvesting phase begins precisely 45 days post-planting of the mother block. This waiting period allows adequate time for the plants to mature and produce the desired yield. Following the first harvest, subsequent flushes are harvested at an interval of seven days. This approach maximizes the yield from each mother block while ensuring the quality and freshness of the cuttings. Prior to harvesting, the mother block is irrigated to maintain the turgidity of the cuttings, with harvesting conducted during cooler hours of the day, preferably in the morning or evening.

Preparation for harvesting

Prior to commencement of the harvest, it is essential to evaluate the crops' readiness based on visual indicators such as length and diameter. All necessary tools and equipment must be prepared and sanitized to reduce the risk of contamination and damage to the crops. Given the delicate nature of marigold cuttings, scissors are employed for both harvesting and preparation.

Harvesting process

Harvesting begins with the initial set of crops that demonstrate readiness for collection. Cuttings are meticulously excised from their respective stems to prevent damage to adjacent branches, and cuts are specifically made below the second node of the stem. The rationale for cutting below the second node of cuttings is twofold. First, this location contains a higher concentration of vascular tissue and meristematic cells, which promotes robust root development. Second, it preserves the cuttings' ability to generate new growth, enhancing their viability and success in propagation. Thus, this method optimizes the health and productivity of subsequent crop yields.

Following the initial round of harvesting, records of the volume and quality of the cuttings are meticulously documented. This data is crucial for evaluating yield and planning future harvesting activities. Harvesting is conducted at seven day intervals, during which the growth and readiness of the crops are closely monitored for the next flush.

Post-harvest handling

Once harvested, the crops are transported immediately to the processing area. It is essential that they are cleaned, sorted, and prepared for rooting. Any signs of spoilage or damage should be carefully examined during the sorting process, with substandard crops being discarded according to quality standards. Prior to planting in a growth medium composed of cocopeat, perlite, and sand (in a 1:1:1 ratio), the cuttings are treated with fungicides, specifically Dithane M 45 and Bavistin, each at a concentration of 0.2 % for a duration of 15 min. Subsequently, the cuttings are treated with a solution of NAA at a concentration of 500 ppm for 5 s before being placed in the propagation chamber for rooting.

This methodology ensures that the harvesting of cuttings from the mother block is conducted efficiently and effectively, ultimately leading to improved yields and quality of crops in future planting cycles.

Maintenance of mother block

Establishment of Mother Block: After the cuttings had successfully rooted, they were transplanted into well-prepared beds in the research field. The experimental beds were consistently maintained at a height of 20 cm, with each bed measuring one square meter in area. Fertilizers and Jeevamrit were applied according to the established technical program (Table 1), ensuring that the plants received the necessary nutrients for optimal growth and development. A spacing of approximately 30 × 30 cm between plants was maintained to facilitate air circulation and sunlight penetration.

Disbudding Practice: Regular disbudding was implemented to prevent flowering and focus the plant's energy on vegetative growth, thereby producing healthy and viable cuttings. The disbudding practice commenced 30 days after the establishment of the mother block.

Frequency: Disbudding was performed daily, where developing flower buds were carefully removed from the plants. This process was crucial to ensure that all resources were diverted towards the growth of foliage and root systems, enhancing the overall health of the mother plants.

Technique: Disbudding was performed manually to minimize the risk of injury to the plants and reduce the likelihood of infection. Careful attention was given to ensure that only the flower buds were removed, while the surrounding leaves and the integrity of the stems were preserved.

This systematic approach ensured that the Marigold cultivar Siracole maintained optimal growth conditions, facilitating the production of high-quality cuttings for further propagation.

In this experiment, seven distinct treatments were implemented during both summer and winter seasons to assess their effects on quality cutting production. Farmyard manure (FYM) @ 5 kg m−2 was used in both treatments at the time of field preparation. In this study, we incorporated RDF (Recommended Dosage of Fertilizers) comprising urea, single super phosphate (SSP), and muriate of potash (MOP) into the soil during the field preparation phase across individual plots. A split application of urea was employed, where the first half was applied at the time of field preparation. The remaining half was administered in two subsequent splits: the first application occurred one month after transplanting, followed by the final application one month after the first, coinciding with the late vegetative stage to early flowering period.

In addition, a foliar application of gibberellic acid (GA3) at 100 ppm and benzyladenine (BA) at 100 ppm was carried out, and drench applications of nitrogen at concentrations of 1000 ppm and 1500 ppm every 15 days. This was conducted three times, commencing 20 days after the planting of the mother block. Jeevamrit, a traditional organic amendment, was also applied as a drench at a rate of 2 L m−2 at 15-day intervals during the entire growing season.

In this experiment, two distinct treatments were implemented during both summer and winter seasons to assess their effects on plant growth and yield. The first treatment involved the application of Jeevamrit at a rate of 2 L per square meter, administered as a drench every 15 days over a span of three applications, beginning 20 days after planting the rooted cuttings. This organic approach aimed to enhance soil health and improve plant performance through natural inputs. In contrast, the second treatment utilized a recommended dose of fertilizers (RDF) that included urea, single super phosphate (SSP), and muriate of potash (MOP). These fertilizers were incorporated into the soil during the field preparation phase for their respective plots. A split dosage of urea was employed, with the first half applied at the time of field preparation and the second half divided into two applications: one after one month of transplanting and the final application after another month, coinciding with the late vegetative stage to early flowering. Together, these treatments provided a comprehensive approach to nutrient management, allowing for a comparison between natural and conventional fertilization methods in optimizing plant growth and productivity.

Preparation of Jeevamrit

Jeevamrit was prepared by combining 10 kg of cow dung, 10 L of cow urine, 2 kg of black jaggery, 2 kg of flour made from black chickpeas, and a handful of soil (approximately 100 g) to serve as microbial inoculum. The total volume of the mixture was then adjusted to 200 L by adding water. The mixture was stirred twice daily in a clockwise direction. On the fifth day, the solution was filtered, and the resulting filtrate was utilized for soil drenching. Jeevamrit was applied to the soil at a rate of 2 L per square meter at intervals of 15 days. The first application was applied 20 days after the planting of the cuttings, with the final two applications made 15 days apart following the initial treatment. Sample collection and measurement of soil chemical properties

Soil samples were collected from each plot to a depth of 15 cm using a soil auger, both prior to planting and after harvesting the crop. To create a composite sample, soil was collected from multiple locations within each plot. The excess soil was then quartered, and 500 g was retained for analysis. The remaining two quarters were mixed again, and this quartering process was repeated until only 500 g of soil remained. For chemical analysis, the soil samples were ground and sieved through a 2 mm mesh screen, and subsequently placed in cotton bags to air dry. Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were measured using a conductivity meter.

Soil parameters

Organic carbon content was estimated using the Walkley-Black method [22].To obtain actual percentage of organic carbon by Walkley and Black method, organic carbon should be multiplied by a factor 100/77 i.e. 1.3 (77 % being the average recovery factor). In Experiment I, soil sampling was conducted after each harvesting flush during both growing seasons.

Microbiological properties of soil

The microbiological properties of the soil were analyzed following the completion of the trial for each treatment. The quantification of viable microbes was carried out using the nutrient agar (NA) for bacterial count, potato dextrose agar medium (PDA) for fungal count, and Kenknight and Munaier's medium (KNM) for actinomycetes count, using the standard spread plate technique with serial dilutions [26]. Statistical analysis

Field experiments were conducted with three replications, consisting of nine plants per replication, and were designed using a Randomized Complete Block Design (RBD).

For Experiment-I, a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the interactions among two growing seasons, five harvesting flushes, and seven treatments. Comparisons of the interactions were made at a significance level of 0.05. The data collected during this experiment were statistically analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2010 and SPSS Version 16.0.

In Experiment Two, a similar approach was employed, featuring two growing seasons, two treatments, and three harvesting flushes. The analysis was again conducted using a three-way ANOVA to assess the interactions among the different factors, with significance tested at the same level of 0.05. Statistical analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel 2010 and SPSS Version 16.0.

Effect of season on viable microbial count

The soil sampling was conducted after each harvesting flush and throughout each season. The microbial count was significantly higher during summer compared to the winter. This difference is likely due to the favorable climatic conditions during the summer, which stimulate the growth and multiplication of soil microbes. The viable microbial diversity i.e. bacteria (16.15 × 10⁵ cfu g−1), fungi (3.33 × 10³ cfu g−1), and actinomycetes (2.68 × 10³ cfu g−1) were observed higher during summer. Conversely, organic carbon (0.97 %) was observed at higher levels during the winter. This is likely because colder temperatures during the winter reduce the microbial population, leading to slower decomposition rates and greater organic carbon accumulation in the soil.

Effect of nutritional modules

The soil collected from the plots where Jeevamrit was applied were observed to have more viable bacteria (18.27 × 105 cfu g−1), fungi (3.64 × 103 cfu g−1) and actinomycetes (3.12 × 103 cfu g−1) but the organic carbon was recorded more under T7 (1.14 %).

Pearson correlation analysis of yield and quality of cuttings of marigold with soil and plant analysis

The number of cuttings per plant, rooting percentage and number of roots per cutting exhibited a positive correlation with the soil microbial count and nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium by the plant; whereas, the correlation is dependent upon the presence of a certain level of microbial activity in the soil, as an increase in microbial population can lead to a decrease in organic carbon. In other words, as microbial populations grow, organic carbon levels tend to decline, suggesting a delicate balance between these factors in the soil ecosystem.

The data revealed a strong correlation between viable bacterial population and potassium uptake by plants (Fig. 3), with a coefficient of determination (r) 0.989. In contrast, the population of fungi exhibited a significant correlation with potassium uptake (r = 0.833), indicating that these microorganisms play a critical role in plant nutrition. Notably, actinomycetes count showed an extremely high correlation with fungi (r = 0.995), suggesting a close relationship between these microbial groups. The yield of cuttings per plant (r = 0.694) and rooting percentage (r = 0.771) showed positive correlations with phosphorus uptake by plants, highlighting the importance of phosphorus in plant growth and development.Fig. 3Correlation with yield and quality of cuttings with soil and plant properties.Fig. 3

Correlation with yield and quality of cuttings with soil and plant properties.

On the other hand, organic carbon in soil exhibited negative correlations with different parameters, i.e. bacteria (r = −0.290), fungi (r = −0.756), actinomycetes (r = −0.690), nitrogen (N) (r = −0.109), phosphorus (P) (r = −0.232), potassium (K) (r = −0.326), rooting percentage (r = −0.398), survival percentage (r = −0.580) and number of cuttings per plant (r = −0.558). The results propose that the microbial population and N, P, and K uptake by plants are inversely proportional to organic carbon; as the microbial activity helps to break down organic matter, thereby reducing the organic carbon content in the soil.

Discussion

Experiment-I

The foliar application of gibberellic acid (GA3) in conjunction with a nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium (N-P-K) ratio of 30:20:20 g/m2 significantly increased cutting length (Table 5). Plants treated with GA3 exhibited enhanced shoot length, likely due to the exogenous application of GA3 facilitating improved endogenous accumulation, which in turn promotes overall plant growth [28]. The GA3 is believed to play a role of a hormone that is significant for elongation of cells; hence, a better shoot length under GA3 treated mother block of marigold in the present study (Table 5, Table 6) could possibly be as a result of higher cell and stem elongation [11,31]. The application of GA3 may also increase the plant growth because of improved carbohydrate metabolism [31]. A foliar application of GA3 resulting into more biomass accumulation in and prevent heat stress in plant. The present study got support from the findings of Prodhan et al. [32] on cauliflower they found that Gibberellins are weakly acidic growth hormones having a gibbane ring structure which causes cell elongation of intact plants in the general and increased internodal length of genetically dwarfed plants. Our findings align with those of Chen et al. [33], who reported that the application of gibberellic acid (GA3) enhances biomass in Vigna radiata. Additionally, the exogenous application of GA3 in Arabidopsis has been shown to mitigate the detrimental effects of salt, heat, and oxidative stress [34]. According to Khan et al. [35], exogenous GA3 was more effective in reducing temperature stress in date palms by increasing plant height and fresh biomass.

Still in Table 4 the present study was carried out to assess the efficacy of Jeevamrit, a traditional bio-fertilizer, in comparison to chemical fertilizers and plant growth regulators for maintaining a healthy mother block of marigold and production of quality cuttings. Jeevamrit was applied at 2 L m−2 to the mother block, and the performance was evaluated in both summer and winter seasons. Our findings suggested that the application of Jeevamrit improved the diameter of un-rooted cuttings because Jeevamrit application enhanced soil organic carbon, improved microbial load (Table 9, Table 10), and nutrient uptake by plant (Table 11) resulting into better growth and development of plants. Organic amendments enhance overall plant growth through either promotion of root growth, or through stimulation of microbial activities in the soil and improvement of the water holding capacity of the soil, thus improving plant stress, disease tolerance and reduce abiotic stresses [36]. Our findings are consistent with the findings of [37,38].

Present findings also show that the application of Benzyladenine in combination with RDF increased the number of cuttings per plant and per square meter (Table 5, Table 6) due to the fact that it plays a significant role in suppressing apical dominance and induce the growth of lateral shoots. Jabbar and Saleh [39] reported an increase in vegetative traits of strawberry plants following foliar application of benzyladenine (BA), a cytokinin. This enhancement is attributed to the pivotal role of cytokinins in the regulation of apical dominance, which typically suppresses the growth of lateral shoots. By applying cytokinin, the effects of apical dominance are mitigated, as it inhibits the growth of dominant shoots. Consequently, this suppression promotes the lateral growth of the plant, leading to improved vegetative characteristics. Moreover, the use of cytokinins spray minimize the inhibitory effect of the auxins naturally present in lateral buds and this would promote the growth of lateral buds and increase vegetative growth [40,41].

Continuous harvesting of cuttings (i.e. from H1 to H5) in combination with nutritional and growth regulator modules increased the average lateral shoot length. Similar result of pinching the plants resulting into greater number of lateral shoots was noted by Dunn et al. [42] and Warner, [43].

In the context of natural farming practices, the application of Jeevamrit, a traditional microbial inoculant, has proven effective in maintaining a healthy mother block. By enhancing soil health and stimulating microbial activity, Jeevamrit creates a nutrient-rich environment that supports the vitality of the mother plants. This results in stronger, healthier cuttings with improved rooting potential and resilience, ultimately contributing to more successful propagation and a sustainable farming system. The use of Jeevamrit not only enhances the immediate quality of the cuttings but also fosters the long-term health and productivity of the mother block. More specifically, it reduces the time required for rooting, increases the rooting percentage, and enhances the number of roots developed (as illustrated in Table 7, Table 8). Moreover, the survival rate of the cuttings in the field was much better. The results presented in Table 9, Table 10 showed that the microbial diversity in the soil had risen, which, in turn, was reflected in the ability of the plants to uptake the nutrients (Table 11). Higher nutrient uptake and accumulation in the vegetative parts is due to the enhanced cellular metabolism, translocation of nutrients in the root zone resulting into higher vegetative growth [44]. Organic fertilizers increase nutrient solubility and accessibility to the plant roots and the rest of the plant system, hence better nutrient status in the root zone and overall plant system. Higher N, P and K concentrations in plants and improved vigor suggest that organic ammendments raised the uptake of N, P and K [44,45].

Microorganisms can enhance plant growth and yield by increasing the nutrient uptake [46]. Consistently, higher doses of organic fertilizers increased phosphate and potassium uptake. Present study got support from the findings of He et al. [47] and Mishra et al. [48].

Experiment-II

Data presented in Table 13, Table 14 shows the Jeevamrit application under natural farming exhibited higher number of marketable flowers and yield of marketable flowers per plant. Summer planting improved the yield of plant, this could be as a result of enhanced vigor in plant which is likely to be contributed by enhanced photosynthesis and carbon assimilation in summer planted crops due to favorable climate [49]. More temperature during summer is one of the major exogenous factors influence the growth and development of plants [50]. Temperature affects plant growth through both direct and indirect mechanisms. These influences can significantly alter physiological processes, developmental stages, and overall plant health. The direct impact has some effect on the leaf and air temperature while indirect one has an effect on the absorption and transportation of water and fertilizer from soil [51,52]. Therefore, the optimal temperature is critical for rapid growth of plants in addition to the deposition of organic matter.

The present study pointed out that the Jeevamrit brought a considerable improvement in yield of marigold in both growing seasons (Table 13). This study is in line with a prior research stating that bio-fertilizers have a part to play in the carbon equation and metabolisms of nitrogen and therefore, increase the content of amino acids [53]. The enhanced N, P and K uptake observed in the study, evidenced by the reduction of soil N, P, and K levels post-experiment (Table 15), is likely attributed to the stimulation of phytohormone synthesis by organic amendments, which promoted root growth and increased nutrient absorption. The reduced nitrogen uptake during winter periods is likely due to the inhibitory effects of low temperature on plant metabolism. At temperatures below optimal levels, plant growth and metabolism are significantly slowed, resulting in decreased nitrogen uptake and reduced plant growth and yield. This temperature-mediated reduction in nitrogen uptake is consistent with previous studies of Kaushal et al. [45] that have demonstrated the negative impact of low temperature on plant nutrient uptake and overall growth. Thus, the application of Jeevamrit may have the potential to enhance the plant yield through enhancing the photosynthesis and the nutrient assimilation. In the current study, natural farming system stimulate the hormones including ABA, GA and amylase, as they lead to intensified organic manure in relation to the chemical fertilizers. These results align with the findings of previous research [43,54]. Jeevamrit enhances microbial activity by introducing a diverse range of beneficial microorganisms, which improves soil fertility through the breakdown of organic matter and the mineralization of nutrients. Notably, the bacteria within Jeevamrit facilitate nitrogen fixation, increasing nitrogen availability and reducing reliance on synthetic fertilizers, while phosphate-solubilizing bacteria convert insoluble phosphorus into accessible forms, crucial for plant development [45]. Additionally, Jeevamrit improves soil structure and aeration, promoting healthy root systems that can access a larger volume of soil, thus enhancing nutrient and water uptake. By stimulating mycorrhizal fungi, it fosters symbiotic relationships that improve absorption of nutrients, particularly phosphorus and micronutrients [Saharan et al., 2023]. Furthermore, Jeevamrit aids in pH regulation and increases soil organic matter, which enhances cation-exchange capacity (CEC) for better nutrient retention [40]. The beneficial microbes also suppress harmful pathogens, leading to healthier plants that can efficiently utilize nutrients. Ultimately, the combined effects of increased nutrient availability, improved soil health, and enhanced microbial diversity lead to superior plant growth and yield, establishing Jeevamrit as a powerful tool for promoting sustainable and productive farming systems.

However, when growing marigolds, one might take cuttings several times from the same mother block which reduces the vigour and vitality of the plant with time resulting into reduced length, diameter, number of roots, rooting percentage and ultimately survival of rooted cuttings (Table 5, Table 6, Table 7, Table 8). This is so because the mother plant loses strength and size with each harvest and hence the growth and yield of plants raised from third and subsequent flush of cutting is relatively low and may not be as good in quality (Table 13). The number of harvests should also be taken into consideration by growers when planning for their cultivation practices in order to achieve the best growth and quality of the flowers. The results got support from the findings of Druege, [43,55].

The increased rate of decomposition of organic matter in soil, a process which is mainly driven by microorganisms, is probably due to the negative correlation between microbial diversity and organic carbon in soil (Fig. 3). This inverse relationship is supposed to be so due to the fact that microorganisms are involved in the process of breaking down organic matters into simpler compounds and make them available to plants. Hence, as microbial loads rise, their metabolic rates rise and this enhances the rate of decomposition of organic matter and a decline of organic carbon. The present investigation got support from the findings of [56].

Conclusion

This study highlights the positive impact of natural farming practices, particularly the use of Jeevamrit, on marigold growth and yield for both cuttings and flower production. Summer emerged as a favorable season for cultivation, likely due to enhanced nutrient uptake by plants. Jeevamrit application demonstrably increased yields, potentially by stimulating microbial diversity and nutrient absorption. This effect may be linked to the synthesis of plant hormones, leading to higher-quality cuttings with increased diameter, rooting percentage, and ultimately, improved survival rates. Similar beneficial outcomes were noted in flower yield. This study illuminates the potential of Jeevamrit as a viable alternative to chemical fertilizers in marigold cultivation as well as production, highlighting its value for sustainable agriculture. To further study its efficacy, future research should focus on expanding these findings to encompass a wider range of crops and geographical regions. Investigating the optimal formulation and application methods of Jeevamrit will be crucial in maximizing its benefits for both crop yields and environmental sustainability. Such research endeavors will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of Jeevamrit's potential and its role in shaping a more sustainable agricultural practice.
