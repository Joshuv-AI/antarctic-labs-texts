Regional variations in serum IL-35 levels and association with systemic lupus erythematosus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nontaphat Leerach, Kanchana Ngaosuwan, Wiriya Mahikul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin (IL)-35 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that regulates autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). However, the association between the cytokine and disease may vary by geographical region. This study performed a meta-analysis to quantitatively assess the correlation between the serum IL-35 levels in SLE patients and sub-group analyses were conducted. Four main electronic databases—Scopus, Embase, Science Direct, PubMed—were searched for relevant studies. After a database search, Endnote software was used to find and remove duplicate studies. Random-effects models were used to estimate standard mean differences in serum/plasma IL-35 levels by Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots, and risk of bias was assessed according to the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Sixteen studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in a qualitative review; data from 15 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Total IL-35 levels (pg/mL) did not differ among patients with active SLE and healthy controls (Hedges’s g: 0.22, 95% CI − 0.51, 0.95, p = 0.55). Sub-group analysis revealed that IL-35 levels in patients with active SLE were lower than in healthy controls in Chinese studies (Hedges’s g: − 3.11, 95% CI − 5.72, − 0.51), but not in non-Chinese studies (Hedges’s g: 1.63, 95% CI − 0.31, 3.57). This regional difference was statistically significant (p < 0.01). The analysis comparing patients with inactive SLE and healthy controls showed a similar trend. This study suggests that serum IL-35 levels are lower in patients with SLE in studies from China, but not other regions. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease involving a wide range of tissues that can cause serious organ damage and failure. This autoimmune disease is more prevalent in women than men1,2. The exact etiology of SLE remains unknown, but several factors—including genetics, drugs, hormones, and immunological derangement—can contribute to this disease1,2. The immunopathogenesis of SLE includes the overproduction of organ-specific antibodies, immune complex formation and deposition, and complement activation, leading to immune cell activation3. In addition, an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines can promote and accelerate SLE progression4.

Disproportionate numbers of regulatory T cells (Tregs)/T helper (Th17), regulatory B cell (Bregs), and activated B cells contribute to the development and progression of autoimmune diseases5–7. Interleukin (IL)-35, an IL-12 family member, is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that plays a crucial role in autoimmune diseases8–10. IL-35, comprising subunits p35 and EBI3, is mainly produced by regulatory immune cells, particularly CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs and CD19+CD24highCD27+ Bregs8–10. IL-35 signals through homo- and heterodimeric receptors that are expressed on both B and T cells. Coupling of IL-35 with its receptor on B cells promotes differentiation into IL-35–producing Bregs (i35-Bregs) and IL-10–producing B cells. On the other hand, IL-35 receptor engagement on T cells suppresses T cell proliferation11. IL-35 can suppress pro-inflammatory macrophages and inhibit the maturation and differentiation of dendritic cells11. Therefore, IL-35 has a role in diminishing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and upregulating other anti-inflammatory cytokines, like IL-10. Accordingly, IL-35 plays an essential role in ameliorating lupus-associated symptoms, including flare and renal failure, through its immunosuppressive function10–12.

Previous studies reported an association of IL-35 gene single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with genetic susceptibility to SLE and rheumatoid arthritis in a Chinese cohort13,14. In addition, EBI3 subunit polymorphisms—particularly rs4740, which affects both genotype frequency and allele frequency—were found to be associated with renal disorder in a Chinese SLE cohort 13. However, this rs4740 polymorphism was not associated with SLE susceptibility in an Egyptian population15. Thus, the rs4740 SNP may be the essential factor that accelerates SLE progression selectively in the Chinese, but not Egyptian, population.

In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis to ascertain the relationship between serum IL-35 levels in patients with SLE compared with healthy controls, and the association of IL-35 with SLE in patients in China compared with other geographical regions.

Methods

Reporting guidelines and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA)16. The protocol of this systematic review is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023433594).

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic search of electronic online databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and ScienceDirect, was performed from inception to 10th June 2024, to retrieve studies reporting serum IL-35 levels in patients with SLE. The search strategy combined key words with Boolean operators (AND, OR) as follows: (Lupus erythematosus OR Systemic lupus erythematosus OR SLE OR Lupus OR Lupus nephritis OR Libman-Sacks disease OR Cutaneous lupus OR Discoid lupus OR Systemic connective tissue disorders) AND (Interleukin-35 levels OR Interleukin-35 OR Serum IL-35 OR Plasma IL-35 OR Serum Interleukin-35 OR Plasma Interleukin-35 OR IL-35 OR Serum anti-inflammatory cytokines OR Plasma anti-inflammatory cytokines). We used “OR” to connect wordings within the concept. However, “AND” was used to connect wordings between concepts. The description of search strategy was explained in Supplementary Table 1. There was no limitation regarding language or region of study in the literature search.

Study selection

Two reviewers (NL and KN) applied the eligibility criteria and selected studies for inclusion in the systematic review. Disagreements were resolved by a third opinion (WM). Duplication of articles was detected using the Endnote desktop software application and by manual comparison.

Data extraction and quality assessment

For each study, two reviewers extracted the first author, publication year, country, sample size of patients with active/inactive SLE and matched controls, mean age, percentage of female individuals in the sample population, mean and standard deviation (SD) of the IL-35 levels reported in pg/mL, SLE diagnosis criteria, and SLE disease activity. To standardize the data for the meta-analysis, we transformed the interquartile range into SD17. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with the third reviewer.

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS). The quality assessment method encompassed selection criteria, comparability of cases and controls on the basis of the study design or analysis, and ascertainment of exposure/outcome of case and control. The risk of bias graph and summary were generated by robvis18.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analyses were conducted with the STATA 16 software package (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). In this study, the levels of IL-35 in patients with SLE and healthy controls were used to calculate standardized mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a random-effects model. The random-effects model was applied when the heterogeneity I2 statistic was > 50% and statistically significant when the p-value was < 0.10 using a chi-squared test (Cochran Q test). A subgroup analysis was performed for all studies to further investigate the study heterogeneity. In addition, publication bias was assessed by means of Begg’s funnel plots and Egger’s statistical test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sub-group analysis

In the main analysis, we estimated the standardized mean difference in IL-35 levels between the overall SLE patient group and healthy controls. Subsequent analyses were conducted as follows: active SLE group versus healthy controls, inactive SLE group versus healthy controls, and active versus inactive SLE groups. We also present subgroup analyses in which the standardized mean differences in IL-35 between these groups are stratified by nationality (Chinese versus non-Chinese). Additionally, we analyzed IL-35 levels across three groups: medication-untreated patients, treated patients, and those with unreported treatment status. To explore the sources of heterogeneity in the overall pooled results, we conducted further subgroup analyses based on publication year, definition of disease activity, NOS score, treatment status, and region of study.

Systematic review findings

The majority of the studies were conducted in China, with others carried out in diverse regions including the Middle East (such as Egypt, Iraq, and Iran) and Europe. Participants in these studies were adults, and the healthy controls were matched by sex and age. The criteria for SLE diagnosis were based on the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) revised SLE criteria and the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)6,8,9,21,23–25,27–30, as well as the European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) criteria20. However, some studies did not clearly report the diagnostic criteria12,19,22,26. SLE disease activity was assessed using both the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)6,8,9,21–23,26,28–30 and the SLEDAI-2K20,24,25,27. Among these studies, nine reported the treatment regimens for SLE patients, including immunosuppressants such as steroids, hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, methotrexate, leflunomide, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus6,8,9,20,24,27–29. The mean ± SD serum IL-35 levels ranged from 29.40 ± 6.60 to 722.73 ± 227.16 pg/mL in SLE patients, and from 4.11 ± 2.15 to 235.00 ± 234.58 pg/mL in healthy controls. IL-35 levels varied by country, with European and Middle Eastern studies showing a trend toward increased IL-35 levels, while Chinese studies reported lower levels. Additionally, IL-35 production was measured using alternative methods such as qPCR and flow cytometry, as detailed in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2.

Quality evaluation of the studies included in the meta-analysis

The risks of bias for the studies included in the meta-analysis are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1. The NOS scores for the included studies ranged from six to nine, indicating moderate to high quality, making them suitable for pooled analysis. More than 50% had a low risk of bias. Most of the included studies used clearly defined SLE diagnostic criteria for the cases and the sites of the samples obtained. Only one study was unclear for participant selection21. All studies explicitly defined controls, such as age-matched, gender-matched healthy individuals. Four studies showed unclear ascertainment of exposure/outcome (detection bias)12,21,24,25.

Meta-analysis comparing serum IL-35 levels in SLE patients and healthy controls

A comprehensive analysis of 15 studies6,8,12,19–30 examined IL-35 levels in SLE patients, both in active and inactive states (n = 688), compared to healthy controls (n = 458). The quantitative data from these studies were utilized for meta-analysis. Among active SLE patients, over 90% were female, and all participants were adults. Due to significant heterogeneity (I2 = 96.59%, p < 0.01), a random-effects model was employed for the pooled analysis. The findings revealed no significant difference in serum IL-35 levels between overall patients with SLE and healthy controls (Hedges’s g: 0.22, 95% CI − 0.51, 0.95, p = 0.55) (Fig. 3A). Given that half of the included studies were conducted in China, subgroup analyses based on study location (China vs. non-China) were performed. The results indicated that serum IL-35 levels appeared to be lower in Chinese SLE patients (Hedges’s g: − 0.40, 95% CI − 1.53, 0.73, p = 0.49) and higher in non-Chinese SLE patients (Hedges’s g: 0.70, 95% CI − 0.35, 1.74, p = 0.19), though these differences were not statistically significant. Significant heterogeneity was present in both Chinese (I2 = 97.00%; p < 0.00) and non-Chinese (I2 = 96.68%; p = 0.00) populations (Fig. 3B). To account for the impact of treatment on IL-35 levels, we conducted an analysis across three groups: medication-treated patients, untreated patients, and those with unreported treatment status. The results revealed no significant differences in IL-35 levels among these groups (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Meta-analysis comparing serum IL-35 levels in patients with active SLE and healthy controls

Seven studies compared serum IL-35 levels between patients with active SLE (n = 211) and healthy controls (n = 195)6,8,9,20–22,24,28. The results indicated that serum IL-35 levels did not differ between patients with active SLE and healthy controls (Hedges’s g: 0.28, 95% CI − 1.52, 2.09, p = 0.76). The significant heterogeneity was presented (I2 = 98.04%, p < 0.01). (Fig. 4A). We followed this analysis with sub-group analysis between Chinese and Non-Chinese. We found that, among the Chinese population, serum IL-35 levels in active SLE were significantly lower than in healthy controls (Hedges’s g: − 3.11, 95% CI − 5.72, − 0.51). In contrast, among non-Chinese populations, IL-35 levels in SLE did not differ from those in controls (Hedges’s g: 1.82, 95% CI − 0.87, 4.51). The p-value for the difference between Chinese and non-Chinese groups was lower than 0.01. However, in the sub-group analysis, significant heterogeneity was found among both Chinese (I2 = 94.78%; p < 0.00) and non-Chinese (I2 = 98.34%; p = 0.00) populations (Fig. 4B). This result supports the findings of previous studies that Chinese populations with lower IL-35 levels are prone to the development of SLE6,12,25,28.

Meta-analysis comparing serum IL-35 levels in patients with inactive SLE and healthy controls

Serum IL-35 levels between patients with inactive SLE (n = 135) and healthy controls (n = 159) were compared in six studies6,8,12,20,23,28. The results indicated that serum IL-35 levels did not differ between patients with inactive SLE and healthy controls (Hedges’s g: 0.48, 95% CI − 0.54, 1.50, p < 0.36). The significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 93.91%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 5A). According to Chinese and non-Chinese sub-group analysis, the results showed that among the Chinese populations expressed lower level of IL-35 in patients with inactive SLE than in healthy controls (Hedges’s g: −0.61, 95% CI − 1.16, − 0.05). On the other hand, studies in Egypt and Spain showed that patients with inactive SLE had higher levels of IL-35 than controls (Hedges’s g: 1.59, 95% CI 1.02, 2.17). The p-value for the difference between the Chinese and non-Chinese groups was < 0.01. In addition, heterogeneity significantly decreased in this sub-group analysis for Chinese studies (I2 = 63.45%; p = 0.06) while heterogeneity for the populations in the non-Chinese studies was not significant (I2 = 53.26%; p = 0.14) (Fig. 5B).

Meta-analysis comparing serum IL-35 levels in patients with active SLE and inactive SLE

Five studies compared serum IL-35 levels between patients with active and inactive SLE6,8,20,25,28. The pooled results from the meta-analysis indicated no significant difference in serum IL-35 levels between active and inactive SLE patients (Hedges’s g: − 0.71, 95% CI − 2.29, 0.87, p = 0.38). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 97.18%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6A). Subgroup meta-analysis revealed that IL-35 levels were significantly lower in Chinese patients with active SLE compared to those with inactive SLE (Hedges’s g: − 2.06, 95% CI − 3.07, − 1.04), while IL-35 levels were significantly higher in non-Chinese patients with active SLE compared to those with inactive SLE (Hedges’s g: 1.33, 95% CI − 0.76, 3.42). The p-value for the differences between these groups was < 0.01. However, significant heterogeneity was also found in both the Chinese (I2 = 87.02%, p < 0.00) and non-Chinese (I2 = 95.57%, p = 0.00) subgroups (Fig. 6B).

Subgroup analysis

To investigate the source of heterogeneity in the overall pooled results, we conducted subgroup analyses based on publication year, definition of disease activity, NOS score, treatment status, and region (continent versus China). The results showed no differences in IL-35 levels between active SLE patients and healthy controls (Table 3), nor between inactive SLE patients and healthy controls (Table 4), across subgroups defined by publication year, disease activity definition, NOS score, and treatment status. However, significant heterogeneity persisted in these subgroup analyses (Tables 3 and 4), indicating that these factors might not be responsible for the observed heterogeneity. Notably, the subgroup analysis based on region (continent versus China) produced inconsistent results: studies from the Middle East and Europe showed significantly elevated IL-35 levels, whereas studies from China reported a reduction in this interleukin. Additionally, there was no statistically significant heterogeneity in the region-based subgroup among active SLE patients and healthy controls (Table 3). These findings suggest that the geographic area of the study might be the source of heterogeneity. In addition, these results confirmed that Chinese populations expressed lower level of IL-35 both in active and inactive phase of disease.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially removing individual studies to identify potential sources of heterogeneity, as illustrated in Fig. 7. When examining the differences in IL-35 levels between SLE patients and healthy controls (Fig. 7A), active SLE patients and healthy controls (Fig. 7B), and inactive SLE patients and healthy controls (Fig. 7C), the overall pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) remained stable. However, in the comparison between active and inactive SLE patients, the study by Bassiouny et al.8 emerged as an outlier. Excluding this study significantly altered the meta-analysis results (P = 0.024, Hedges’s g: − 1.47, 95% CI − 2.75 to − 0.19; Fig. 7D).

Publication bias

Egger’s and Begg’s tests were utilized to evaluate publication bias. As depicted in Fig. 8, the analysis visually highlights the skewness in the observed effect sizes. Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was significant for comparisons between active SLE patients and healthy controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 8A), inactive SLE patients and healthy controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 8B), and active SLE patients and inactive SLE patients (p < 0.001; Fig. 8D), indicating notable publication bias. Conversely, Egger’s regression test did not show significant publication bias for the comparison between inactive SLE patients and healthy controls (p = 0.4685; Fig. 8C).

Discussion

This meta-analysis showed the first time that patients with SLE, including active disease and SLE in remission (inactive), in China had significantly lower levels of IL-35 than healthy controls. However, in other countries, including Egypt, Iraq, Iran, and Spain, IL-35 levels in patients with SLE were not lower than those in healthy controls. In addition, Chinese patients with active SLE exhibited a lower level of IL-35 than those in remission. However, the pooled standardized mean difference of IL-35 levels in all countries did not statistically differ between individuals with active SLE and healthy controls. In addition, the medication did not show any impact on circulating IL-35 levels in the overall SLE patient group in the analysis.

SLE is a complex autoimmune disease that contributes to organ failure32, and early detection of this condition is required to manage and prevent complications33. Although the uncontrolled activation of autoantibody-producing B cells and immune complex formation play crucial roles in the development of SLE, the precise disease etiology remains largely unknown3. An imbalance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines is also an indispensable factor that accelerates autoimmune disease progression5–7. Elevated levels of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, have been found in the serum of patients with SLE, and positively corelated with disease severity34. On the other hand, lower serum levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, like IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35, were found to be associated with autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE35.

In this study, we found that IL-35 levels in patients with active SLE were lower than in healthy controls or individuals with inactive SLE in China, whereas this phenomenon was not observed in studies from other countries. The Chinese patients with active SLE showed significantly lower levels of IL-35 than those in comparable patients in Iraq, Iran, and Egypt. Previous studies reported that the serum level of IL-35 is related to IL-35 gene mutations that are associated with the risk of SLE in the Chinese Han Population. In addition, mutation of IL-35, particularly the EBI3 polymorphism rs4740, is significantly associated with renal and hematological disorder, a clinical feature of active SLE, in Chinese patients with SLE13. Moreover, IL-35 gene polymorphisms are associated with other immune-related diseases, such as Graves’ disease36 and rheumatoid disease14, uveitis37. However, in Egypt, there was no association between SNP rs4740 and the occurrence of or susceptibility to SLE15. We also found that IL-35 levels associated with remission were lower than levels in healthy controls in China, but this phenomenon was not observed for other countries. We further performed sub-group analysis between active SLE and inactive SLE, and found that patients with active SLE in China had lower IL-35 levels than those in Egypt. Thus, this result supported the findings of the previous study that IL-35 levels may be affected by IL-35 polymorphisms that influence SLE disease activity in the Chinese population. This result indicated that the potential for IL-35 production may be impaired in the Chinese population with SLE. This paradigm might be associated with genetic variation in IL-35 (rs4740), which was prevalent among Chinese individuals with SLE13 but not among Egyptian individuals with SLE15.

Moreover, several factors significantly influence the development of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Beyond single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) mutations, a myriad of genetic and environmental factors, such as proximity to agricultural areas, passive smoking, exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons38, consumption of water with high nitrogen levels39, and sunlight exposure40, have been implicated in its pathogenesis. Additionally, geographical and climatic variables—including temperature, atmospheric pressure, mean humidity, wind speed, and precipitation, as well as geographical attributes such as latitude, longitude, and altitude—are closely linked to ultraviolet radiation (UVR) and have been examined for their role in SLE susceptibility40. These elements likely trigger the secretion of inflammatory mediators, disrupt apoptotic pathways and autophagy in keratinocytes, and modify epigenetic factors and gene-environment interactions41. A potential factor involved in SLE susceptibility is gut microbiome dysbiosis, although the precise mechanism by which diet influences SLE remains unclear. Dietary patterns vary among populations, leading to differences in gut microbiome profiles based on ethnicity42. Previous studies have found that the genera Rhodococcus, Eggerthella, Klebsiella, Prevotella, Eubacterium, Flavonifractor, and Incertae sedis are significantly enriched, while Dialister and Pseudobutyrivibrio are significantly depleted in SLE patients from China43. In contrast, Spanish SLE individuals exhibit significantly lower levels of Firmicutes and higher levels of Bacteroidetes44. Additionally, Wei et al. (2019) reported an increase in Proteobacteria and a decrease in Ruminococcaceae in SLE patients from various regions45. Consequently, ethnicity, geographical location, and dietary patterns also contribute to the development of SLE.

Our findings suggest that serum IL-35 levels in patients with active SLE in relation to healthy individuals differed between Chinese and non-Chinese populations. Lower levels of IL-35 were observed in individuals in the Chinese population with active SLE, and the difference was associated with SLE activity. These results suggest that IL-35 levels could be useful for early detection and monitoring of active SLE. The variation in clinical phenotype and IL-35 levels can significantly influence treatment responses. This heterogeneity underscores the potential for developing tailored treatment strategies, such as personalized medicine, which poses a significant challenge for managing SLE patients across different countries46.

There were several limitations to our study. The pooled analysis displayed significant heterogeneity, warranting cautious interpretation of the findings. Autoimmune diseases themselves exhibit considerable heterogeneity, with elusive etiologies and fluctuating cytokine variations. Although ELISA was employed to measure this cytokine, factors such as sampling time, disease duration, and stress could contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Medications, including corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, may also influence cytokine levels, and some studies did not report whether patients were receiving any medications at the time of IL-35 measurement. Additionally, different ethnicities among the populations might be associated with baseline variations in cytokine levels, necessitating careful interpretation of the results. While all included studies used ELISA to assess IL-35 levels, different ELISA manufacturers provided varying lower detection limits. We conducted an additional analysis to investigate whether variations in SLEDAI scores might be associated with differences in IL-35 levels. However, it’s important to acknowledge that the use of different SLE criteria (SLEDAI and SLEDAI-2k), variations in cut-off values across studies, and the limited number of studies reporting these scores may have influenced the results. Consequently, our findings showed no linear relationship between SLEDAI differences and mean IL-35 level differences (regression coefficient = 0.07, P = 0.76). Additionally, it is important to note that there is currently no standardized method for measuring IL-35 levels. Further research that includes a larger number of studies conducted across various countries may be necessary to investigate additional factors contributing to this heterogeneity, such as genetic variations, environmental influences, and methodological differences between studies.

Conclusion and recommendation

SLE is an autoimmune disease characterized by significant phenotypic heterogeneity across individuals. A reduction in anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-35 may exacerbate disease progression. Our current study revealed that individuals with SLE in China exhibited lower levels of IL-35, whereas patients in other regions showed higher levels. This variation could be influenced by several factors, particularly genetic mutations such as SNPs. Consequently, treatment with immunomodulators should be closely monitored in patients with lower IL-35 levels. Specific interventions tailored to these patients should be considered in the development of future studies and clinical practices. In addition, IL-35-producing regulatory cells included T and B cells population should be explored in SLE patients.
