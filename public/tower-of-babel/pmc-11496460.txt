Multi-modal EEG NEO-FFI with Trained Attention Layer (MENTAL) for mental disorder prediction
Authors: Garrett Greiner, Yu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection and accurate diagnosis of mental disorders is difficult due to the complexity of the diagnostic process, resulting in conditions being left undiagnosed or misdiagnosed. Previous studies have demonstrated that features of Electroencephalogram (EEG) data, such as Power Spectral Density (PSD), are useful biomarkers for indicating the onset of various mental disorders. Existing models using EEG data are typically trained to distinguish between healthy and afflicted individuals, and they are unable to distinguish between individuals with different disorders. We propose MENTAL (Multi-modal EEG NEO-FFI with Trained Attention Layer) to predict an individual’s mental state using both EEG and Neo-Five Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) personality data. We include an attention layer that captures the interactions between personality traits and PSD features, and emphasizes the important PSD features. MENTAL features a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) to model the temporal nature of EEG data. We train our model with the Two Decades Brainclinics Research Archive for Insights in Neuroscience (TDBRAIN) dataset, which consists of 1274 healthy and psychiatric individuals including over 30 different diagnoses. MENTAL is able to achieve 93.3% accuracy when trained to classify between healthy individuals and those with ADHD. When trained to identify individuals with ADHD from among 33 disorder classes, MENTAL improves accuracy from 70.5 to 81.7%. MENTAL also demonstrates over 20% improvement in predictive accuracy when trained for MDD prediction. For the multi-class classification task of three classes, MENTAL improves accuracy by 4%, and for five classes, by nearly 8%. MENTAL is the first multi-modal model that utilizes both EEG and NEO-FFI data for the task of mental disorder prediction. We are one of the first groups to utilize TDBRAIN for automated disorder classification. MENTAL is accessible and cost-effective, as EEG is more affordable than other neuroimaging methods such as MRI, and the NEO-FFI is a self- reported survey. Our model can lead to acceptance and support for individuals living with mental health challenges and improve quality of life in our society.

Introduction

Mental health is undeniably an important issue in today’s world, as in 2019, the WHO found that over 970 million people were living with a mental disorder.1 Mental disorders have devastating impacts on individuals, severely impairing important functions of the brain, such as emotion regulation [1–3], behavior [4], and general cognition [4]. Poor mental health has serious consequences, the worst of them being suicide. In the United States, suicide is the second leading cause of death for youths aged 10–14, and the third leading cause for youths aged 15–24.2 Further, only 46% of individuals who die by suicide were diagnosed with a mental condition [2]. Therefore, creating effective strategies for early and accurate detection of mental disorders is a task of the utmost importance, as it will help alleviate mental health struggles for all, especially for the younger population.

Although the low rate of diagnosis is partly due to individuals not seeking help because of the stigma surrounding disorders or a lack of access to proper care, the complexity of mental disorders is a more important factor to consider. Medical diagnosis and the ability to differentiate between various disorders is the foundation of clinical practice. It is important that we are able to accurately diagnose individuals in order to provide them the proper treatment. For this reason, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) was created in order to propose a standard to aid professionals in their diagnosis of mental disorders [4]. It is currently in its 5th edition, and much has changed since the first edition that was published in 1952. Diagnostic criteria is always evolving, and this can lead to difficulties in understanding each disorder. Throughout its entire existence, the DSM has always been criticized for its various issues. For example, recently, there have been complaints that criteria do not differentiate disorders accurately enough [5, 6]. This issue of common criteria makes the diagnostic process hard for professionals, and this demonstrates that the average person will not be able to identify if someone around them is affected by a mental disorder. There is evidence for this, as a study in 2020 conducted by Al Salem et al, found that non-psychiatric physicians were only able to accurately diagnose depression 40% of the time [7]. The low diagnostic rate found in this study indicates that the typical person does not know what mental disorders look like, and will not be able to recognize when someone around them needs to be seen by a professional.

Another layer of complexity is added to the diagnostic process, as misdiagnosis can also occur due to the presence of multiple disorders. The presence of multiple disorders in an individual can complicate the diagnostic process, as one disorder can suppress or magnify the symptoms of another. This is seen in the case of Multiple Sclerosis (MS) and Functional Neurological Disorder (FND), for example [8, 9]. MS and FND are two different diagnostically challenging conditions, which share some neurological symptoms. Misdiagnosis of either of these conditions causes harm to patients, due to the delay of diagnosis, and consequently, of proper treatment as well. Another study has shown comorbidy between ADHD, autism (ASD), and eating disorders (ED) such as anorexia (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and binge eating disorder (BED) [10–12]. There is much overlap between ADHD and ASD, and a dual-diagnosis can be made according to the DSM-5. The diagnosis of ASD can be delayed due to the initial diagnosis of ADHD [10], which can lead to delayed treatment. Futher, there is symptom overlap between ASD and AN (most commonly), and between ADHD and BN [10].

Since mental disorders are hard to diagnosed, we want to utilize the predictive models to reduce the complexity. One of the main problems with creating predictive models is that mental illnesses lack biological indicators that can be tested for, unlike physical conditions. We would like to be able to find biomarkers that are reliable and consistent indications of various mental disorders. The discovery of such biomarkers could lead to an increase in diagnostic success. There has recently been a push to find biomarkers in EEG data for mental conditions [13–16]. Therefore, it is essential that we try to create models that can predict various disorders, and discover invaluable biomarkers, allowing us to diagnose individuals properly and give them the treatment they need.

Although various studies have utilized EEG data in the task of mental disorder prediction, a recent study conducted by Shusharina et al found that a problem with current openly available EEG datasets is that they often include a limited number of participants (the average sample size of patients with depressive disorder is around 30 people) [17]. This is partly due to the fact that it is harder to release data related to individual’s mental health because of medical data regulations. Therefore, there are not many public datasets, so most studies have developed their own datasets for training purposes. We found five studies whose datasets had n<100 individuals [18–22], and six studies whose datasets had 100<n<200 individuals [23–28]. There were three studies we found that had datasets of size n>300, with sizes 430 [29], 328 [30], and 945 [31]. These previous studies utilize small datasets for training, which could hinder the generalizability of the results. In 2022, van Dijk et al published the the two decades brainclinics research archive for insights in neurophysiology (TDBRAIN) EEG database [32]. This database contains 1247 individuals, and is open to the public. We are one of the first groups to utilize this dataset, and we hope to create a model with this new data it provides.

Researchers have created models that use EEG data to predict individual’s personality trait scores as recorded by the Neo Five Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) questionnaire. The NEO-FFI is a 60-item questionnaire which measures the five personality domains, as according to the Five Factor Model (FFM): Openness, Concientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism. These traits captured by the FFM have gained the most widespread recognition among researchers [18, 29]. Zhao et al were able to predict the traits of extraversion, neuroticism, and openness with roughly 64% accuracy [18], and conscientiousness and agreeableness traits with rougly 72% accuracy. Jach et al were able to predict agreeableness and neuroticism scores using EEG power in the alpha and lower beta bands (8-12Hz), and EEG power in the lower theta band (3-6Hz), respectively [23]. In addition, studies have shown that personality traits measured by the NEO-FFI questionairre, such as neuroticism, are similarly strong indicators of psychological dysfunction [33]. For example, there is a strong association between neuroticism and depression in particular [34, 35]. Similarly, higher levels of neuroticism were associated with subjective memory complaints (SMC) [28]. Another study found that individuals who previously attempted suicide had higher levels of neuroticism and lower levels of openness, as compared to non-suicidal individuals [36]. There is evidence that personality traits as measured by the NEO-FFI are correlated with both EEG data and various mental disorders. Including both EEG and personality data in a model could increase predictive accuracy, and we aim to asses this claim in this paper.

We propose a multi-modal model, Multi-modal EEG NEO-FFI with Trained Attention Layer (MENTAL), that utilizes both EEG and personality data to predict mental disorders. We include an attention layer to capture the interactions between the modes of data, since they have been shown to be correlated. MENTAL is one of the first models to utilize both EEG and NEO-FFI data. Through using both modes of data, we hope to be able to identify more useful biomarkers that could be used for future predictive models. Further, we hope these biomarkers can distinguish between disorders that typically have overlapping symptoms. In addition, it accounts for multiple different parts of the diagnostic process, including both subjective (personality) data, and objective (EEG) data. Our main contributions are as follows.

Methods

We propose the MENTAL (Multi-modal EEG NEO-FFI with Trained Attention Layer) architecture for mental disorder prediction in Fig. 1. MENTAL is a multi-modal model that uses EEG, NEO-FFI, and demographic data in order to predict mental disorders. We developed an attention layer that selects important PSD features based on the NEO-FFI personality data. MENTAL uses a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), specifically the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architecture, to capture the temporal features of the EEG data. In order to account for the missing data in TDBRAIN, we utilize a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to impute missing values.

Algorithm

MENTAL model train

Algorithm 1 details the training process of MENTAL. First, we extract the PSD values from each 2-second window in the EEG data. For any missing values among the demographic or NEO-FFI data in the sample, we use a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to perform imputation. Using the missing mask, o, we keep the recorded variables, and gather the imputed values for the missing variables. Then, for each window, we perform Scaled Dot Product Attention on the PSD values using the NEO-FFI personality data. Once we have the attention guided PSD values, we use them as the input to the GRU. The GRU network keeps the temporal order of the updated states. We repeat this for the entire sequence, and at the end, return a disorder classification for the individual.

Pre-processing

We adopted the pre-processing method introduced by van Dijk et al. [32]. First, using a method proposed by Gratton et al. [37], bipolar EOG (electrooculogram) was computed and removed from the EEG-signal. Afterwards, a bandpass-filter was applied to the data, keeping frequencies between 0.5 to 100Hz. In addition the notch-frequency of 50Hz was removed. Further, they detected various artifacts, such as: EMG (electromyography), sharp channel-jumps, kurtosis, extreme voltage swing, residual eyeblinks, electrode bridging, and extreme correlations [32]. If more than 66% of a channel’s recorded signal contained artifacts, they would take the “Euclidian distance weighted average of at least 3 neighboring channels” to repair the signal [32].

Feature extraction

After the EEG data has been pre-processed, we can now perform feature extraction. We used the power spectral density (PSD) values of the EEG data. PSD values represent the distribution of power in a frequency range of a signal. First, we split the 2 min EEG recording into 2 s intervals (60 total intervals). For each interval, PSD features were extracted from each of the 26 channels over the five frequency bands: delta (0–4Hz), theta (4–8Hz), alpha (8–12Hz), beta (16–32Hz), and gamma (32–64Hz). For each 2-s window, we extract PSD values from each channel for these specified bands, resulting in 130 features (26 channels × 5 frequency bands). This totals to 7800 PSD features (60 window × 130 PSD/window). We used the MNE-Python package to extract these PSD features [38]. We use these PSD features to train MENTAL.

Imputation

The TDBRAIN dataset [32] has missing data. For example, 324 individuals have missing data for their NEO-FFI questionnaire responses. Therefore, in order to be able to use these individuals’ data, we perform imputation. We use a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to do so. An autoencoder consists of two components: the encoder, and the decoder. The encoder maps a given incomplete input to some point in the latent space, which is a lower-dimensional representation of the input. Then, the decoder maps from the latent space to produce a complete sample. What makes a VAE unique is that rather than mapping to a single point in the latent space, it maps to a distribution, which gives the latent space better properties. Then, to get the complete output, we sample from the distribution, and pass it through the decoder in order to get the complete data entry.

Attention layer

A unique aspect of MENTAL is its use of NEO-FFI data alongside the EEG data. Since we are one of the first models to use this multi-modal data, there is not much research done into fusing NEO-FFI and EEG data. We propose a feature-fusion approach, using an attention mechanism to select important EEG features based on the NEO-FFI results. We employ a co-attention layer in order to highlight the relations between the NEO-FFI data and the EEG data. Attention mechanisms are able to capture the correlations between data. Previous studies have had difficulty with differentiating one disorder from another. The addition of an attention mechanism would hopefully enable to model to better differentiate between disorders that share some similar tendencies.

Gated recurrent unit (GRU)

In our model, we use a RNN, specifically the GRU architecture. Since EEG data is time-series data, using a GRU allows us to capture the temporal aspects of our data. In addition, this allows us to extract data from each interval, and enables us to place more importance on small differences in the data. Further, other studies in the past have shown that RNNs have promise in the task of mental disorder prediction using EEG data [40, 41]. In addition, attention mechanisms are often used in conjunction with RNNs [39]. An analysis of the PSD values extracted from the EEG data is presented to illustrate the similarities and differences seen in the PSD values of subjects. Training

For each task, we used both EEG and NEO-FFI data to train MENTAL. Specifically, we performed tests with three different types of EEG data: EC, EO, and EC+EO. We compared MENTAL to a unimodal model that we trained on EEG data (EC, EO, and EC+EO) alone. We also compared MENTAL to refs. [42, 43] in multi-class classification.

The first task we train MENTAL for is One Disorder vs Healthy. In this case, we perform binary prediction, where the two classes are one disorder, and healthy controls. For this task, we train MENTAL to perform prediction between MDD and healthy controls, and ADHD and healthy controls. MDD (N = 360) and ADHD (N = 234) are the two most common disorders in the TDBRAIN dataset [32].

However, because the size of the healthy controls is small (n = 47), we attempted to perform another binary classification task. This task is called One Disorder vs All Disorders. In this case, we perform binary classification, where the first class consists of individuals of one disorder, and the other contains the remaining individuals without that disorder, including healthy controls. We train this task to distinguish between MDD and the rest, and ADHD and the rest.

To compare MENTAL to refs. [42, 43], we also train MENTAL to perform multi-class classification. That is, the model classifies individual samples with a label among multiple classes. We train MENTAL to perform two such multi-class classifications. First, we train MENTAL to perform multi-class classification on the top three most common disorders in TDBRAIN: MDD (N = 360), ADHD(N = 234), SMC (N = 119). We call this task Three Disorder. We also train MENTAL to perform this type of classification among the top 4 disorders MDD (N = 360), ADHD(N = 234), SMC (N = 119), OCD (N = 57), and healthy controls. We call this task Four Disorder + Healthy.

In order to gain a better understanding of the data and the relationships between the disorders present in the TDBRAIN dataset, we analyze the PSD values observed for individuals based on their disorder label. As we can see in Fig. 2, there is a notable difference between the PSD values of individuals with and without ADHD. In both Eyes Open and Eyes Closed data, individuals with ADHD show greater PSD values in both the Delta and Theta frequency bands (Fig. 2a−d). In the alpha frequency band, individuals with ADHD still show higher values of PSD than others, however, the gap is much smaller (Fig. 2e, f). Further, the values of those with ADHD are more similar to that of healthy individuals in the alpha frequency band (Fig. 2e, f). Therefore, we can conclude that in comparison to other disorders, those with ADHD are more distinguishable from others by EEG data alone.

Notice that in each of the plots of Fig. 2, the rest of the disorders exhibit similar PSD values in these frequency bands, for both EO and EC. That is, MDD, SMC, OCD, Tinnitus, and Insomnia all exhibit similar values for the Delta, Theta, and Alpha frequency bands. This indicates that it is difficult to distinguish between these disorders, as on average, they have similar PSD values. Therefore, we can conclude that distinguishing between individuals with two different disorders based on EEG data alone will be a difficult task, which calls for the use of MENTAL.

Fig. 3 shows that in the case of both MDD and ADHD prediction, MENTAL performs better than unimodal model in every case of EC, EO, and EC+EO. In the case of MDD prediction, MENTAL increases performance by up to 20% when trained on EO data (see Fig. 3d). In the case of ADHD prediction, MENTAL increases performance by up to 10% when trained on EC+EO data (see Fig. 3e). Therefore, MENTAL is effective in predicting presence of mental disorders, as compared to simply using EEG data. The large increase in performance can be best explained due to the inclusion of the attention layer. As noted in the previous section, individuals with ADHD have very distinct PSD values when compared to those with other disorders. This can explain why the model that uses only EEG data to predict ADHD is able to achieve a decent accuracy of ∼70%, while the model trained for MDD identification is able to only achieve ∼60% accuracy. Although ADHD is already distinguishable from other individuals from EEG data alone, MENTAL is still able to increase predictive accuracy in the case of ADHD prediction with the inclusion of the attention layer. However, the increase in performance in the case of MDD (∼20%) is greater than the increase we see in the case of ADHD (∼10%). This can be explained by the complexity of the task of MDD identification. Since the PSD values of MDD are similar to many other disorders, the task of MDD identification is more difficult when using EEG data alone. However, with the inclusion of the attention layer, MENTAL is able to distinguish between individuals with similar PSD values, allowing for it to find more success in the task of MDD classification.

In Fig. 4, we found that when MENTAL is trained for MDD prediction, it obtains greater ROC and AUC results than that of ADHD. This could indicate that MENTAL is more sensitive in distinguishing MDD from the other disorders. This behavior could also be explained due to the class imbalance in the dataset. There are 360 individuals with MDD, as compared to only 234 with ADHD. This imbalance may cause MENTAL trained for MDD to have lower accuracy than for ADHD but larger AUC value.

MENTAL is even more effective in the task of binary classification between ADHD/MDD and healthy controls. MENTAL is roughly ∼30% more accurate than using EEG data alone in the case of ADHD classification, as seen in Fig. 5a, c, e. In addition, it is also ∼30% more accurate than a model trained on EEG data alone for the task of MDD prediction, as seen in Fig. 5d, f. When performing MDD prediction using EC data, it seems that MENTAL only provides ∼10% accuracy increase when compared to an EEG only model (Fig. 5b). MENTAL reaches over 90% accuracy in the case of ADHD prediction when trained on EC or EO data (Fig. 5a, c), however, it only reaches approximately 80% accuracy when trained on EC+EO data (Fig. 5e). In the case of MDD prediction, MENTAL achieves ∼80% accuracy when trained on EC, EO, and EC+EO data. The low results for the EEG only models trained for this binary classification task could be due to the small sample size (47 Healthy, 47 disorder, 94 total) that the model is trained on (as a result of undersampling). The smaller amount of data points used could explain why the training curves for this task converge quickly.

In Fig. 6, we found that when MENTAL is trained for MDD prediction, it obtains greater ROC and AUC results than that of ADHD. This was the case for the previous task (Fig. 4) as well, further indicating that MENTAL is more sensitive in distinguishing MDD from the other disorders. The more discrete nature of these curves could be attributed to the fact that we have a small testing set used due to the undersampling.

Multi-class classification

After an examination of literature, only two studies were found that have used TDBRAIN for mental disorder prediction [42, 43]. Gour et al. [42] is a unimodal model that utilized raw EEG data only (vs. that MENTAL extracted the PSD data). Therefore we named [42] by Unimodal-Raw-EEG. The method proposed in Basheer et al. [43] is named SCAM-Learning. The Unimodal-Raw-EEG and SCAM-Learning models were both trained for two multi-class classification tasks: prediction among the four most common disorders in TDBRAIN and healthy controls (MDD, ADHD, SMC, OCD, and Healthy), and prediction among the three most common disorders (MDD, ADHD, SMC). In order to compare our models to theirs, we also trained MENTAL for these tasks.

To evaluate the models, we used four metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score. MENTAL outperforms the Unimodal-Raw-EEG model and SCAM-Learning (with the exception of Three Disorder EO) due to three reasons. First, MENTAL is a multimodal model that generates an attention layer across three types of data (the EEG data, the NEO-FFI survey, and the demographic data). This ability enhances MENTAL to better understand the content across different modalities. Further, we extract PSD features from the data, gaining more useful information as compared to simply using raw EEG data as the Unimodal-Raw-EEG model does. Last, MENTAL utilizes a RNN, which enables it to capture the temporal nature of the EEG data. In comparison, the Unimodal-Raw-EEG model splits the recording into intervals, makes a classification for each window, and then decides the label for the individual based on majority vote. Their approach does not allow for the model to capture temporal aspects, which limits their model’s ability.

Discussion

The major limiting factor in determining the success of models such as MENTAL is the lack of data availability. TDBRAIN is the only known large multi-modal dataset containing EEG, NEO-FFI, and demographic data, therefore limiting the space over which architectures such as MENTAL can be trained. In addition, some characteristics of the TDBRAIN dataset have certain implications for the results obtained with MENTAL that are important to consider.

First, TDBRAIN has a small number of healthy samples in its dataset (n = 47 out of 1274). Although MENTAL performs classification between healthy controls and afflicted individuals with high accuracy, since there are so few healthy individuals, it can lead to overfitting. In order to account for this, it would be optimal to develop methods that provide solutions to the imbalanced classes in TDBRAIN, to more accurately reflect the population in the real world.

The results of the experiments seen in Fig. 5 are from a MENTAL model trained on a portion of TDBRAIN that was balanced using undersampling. Since we had very few healthy controls (n = 47), we randomly removed samples from the disorder classes (MDD or ADHD) until we acheived a balanced dataset. MENTAL achieved high accuracy when trained on these balanced datasets, further demonstrating the utility of undersampling. However, other sampling methods such as oversampling or GAN (Generative Adversarial Networks) could be explored to create balanced datasets to train MENTAL.

In addition, since there is a large portion of missing data for the NEO-FFI (n = 264 out of 1301), we implemented imputation methods using Variational Autoencoders (VAE) to fill in these pieces of data. Using imputation, we were able to gain access to these samples that would have otherwise been discarded, but at the expense of precision of the predictions.

An analysis of the extracted PSD features indicated that ADHD is easily distinguished from the other disorders and healthy controls. This explains why the EEG only model is able to obtain a decent accuracy for the task of ADHD prediction, and not for MDD prediction. The other various disorders are harder to distinguish from one another, leading to the classification task being more complex. MENTAL utilizes the NEO-FFI data and the demographic data by including an attention layer to select the important PSD features, enabling the model to obtain a classification accuracy of ∼80% even when trained for these more complex tasks.

Conclusion

MENTAL ability to distinguish individuals with different disorders shows promise. MENTAL outperforms other models trained to perform the same task. We trained MENTAL to perform both binary and multi-class classification, and it performed well in both tasks. However, there could be some improvements to the 5 class multi-class classification task. In the future, there are various ways in which MENTAL could be further tested and developed. First, MENTAL should be trained to predict various other disorders, and its performance should be assessed. Although ADHD and MDD are two of the most common disorders, it is important to create a model that is flexible, one that can identify any disorder. Further studies could be carried out to determine the aptitude of MENTAL for predicting other disorders.

In addition, extending MENTAL to perform multi-label (a sample has multiple disorders) classification could be fruitful. TDBRAIN has a small number of individuals with multiple labels for disorders (N = 62 two disorders, N = 6 three disorders, no more than three disorders). A version of MENTAL that aims to perform such classification could be trained on TDBRAIN. As discussed earlier, a common reason that misdiagnosis occurs is the presence of multiple disorders, or comorbidity, complicates the diagnostic process. This is because one disorder can suppress or magnify the symptoms of another, leading to a delay in diagnosis. Therefore, training MENTAL to perform multi-label classification would enable it to combat the issues seen in late diagnosis due to comorbidity.

There are a variety of different ways to approach the imbalanced and missing data problems seen in TDBRAIN. One option is to gather more data, but this option is not very feasible, mainly because there is a lack of multi-modal datasets such as TDBRAIN. Instead, we can use various sampling methods in order to balance the datasets. Two common methods are oversampling and undersampling [44]. Oversampling methods involve duplicating the data from minority classes, while undersampling methods simply remove data from the majority classes until equality is reached. Using undersampling can result in a great loss of information in the majority class, while using oversampling can result in overfitting since samples from the minority class are duplicated many times. An alternative to these methods is to use generative methods such as GAN (Generative Adversarial Networks), which could be used to generate artificial data for the minority class. We will compare the three methods to determine the optimal data balancing technique for this task.
