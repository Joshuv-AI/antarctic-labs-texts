Do all colorectal mucinous adenocarcinomas arise via the serrated neoplasia pathway?
Authors: Anne‐Marie Vos, Peter H van Zwam, Natasja Rutgers, Shannon van Vliet, Elisa Vink‐Börger, Rachel S van der Post, Femke Simmer, Iris D Nagtegaal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal mucinous adenocarcinomas (MACs) and serrated adenocarcinomas (SACs) demonstrate considerable overlap in morphological and molecular features, suggesting a spectrum rather than two distinct tumour types. This study investigates the presence of serrated morphological features, precursors, and molecular features in a large cohort of MAC. The study includes 698 MAC collected via the Dutch nationwide pathology databank. Histological review and mismatch repair evaluation were performed on all cases. Next generation sequencing was performed on 396 cases. Multiplex nested methylation‐specific PCR (MSP) was performed on 260 microsatellite stable (MSS) cases. The majority of tumours were right‐sided (62.6%). Precursor lesions were identified in 9.6% of cases, 88% of which were serrated. Serrated morphology was present in 71% of MAC. Of all cases, 33.8% were microsatellite unstable (MSI). Half of the evaluated cases were CpG island methylator phenotype high. Tumours were divided into six molecular subcategories BRAF/MSS (9.8%), BRAF/MSI (24.7%), KRAS/MSS (38.6%), KRAS/MSI (4.3%), double wild‐type/MSS (16.7%), and double wild‐type/MSI (5.8%). Based on the presence of a serrated precursor, and/or serrated morphology, and/or a serrated molecular profile, 83.1% of MAC in our cohort could be classified as serrated‐MAC. Serrated‐MAC developed in older patients, was more often right‐sided, showed less perineural invasion, and fewer nodal metastases, when compared to MAC‐NOS. Survival analysis showed no significant difference between serrated‐MAC and MAC‐NOS. We have confirmed that a large majority of colorectal MAC have similar characteristics to SAC.Keywords: colorectal cancer, mucinous adenocarcinoma, serrated adenocarcinoma, serrated neoplasia pathway

Introduction

Colorectal mucinous adenocarcinoma (MAC) is defined by extracellular mucin containing overt malignant cells covering >50% of the total tumour surface [1]. Depending on geographical variation, its prevalence varies between less than 5% in Asian cohorts and over 20% in Mediterranean and Australian cohorts [2]. MAC has specific clinicopathological features when compared to conventional adenocarcinoma, not otherwise specified (NOS) and is particularly present in older female patients with right‐sided tumours presenting at a more advanced stage [3]. Molecularly, MAC shows higher percentages of microsatellite instability (MSI) [4], BRAF and KRAS mutations [2], and CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) than non‐mucinous tumours [5]. The prognostic impact of MAC has been under debate. No difference in survival was observed in a systematic review including all stages [6]. However, the prognosis might be worse in the metastatic setting [7, 8].

There is considerable overlap between features present in MAC and the more recently acknowledged serrated adenocarcinoma of the colorectum (SAC). SAC arises via the molecular serrated neoplasia pathway [9], which is thought to be the cause of about 35% of all colorectal carcinomas (CRC) in Western populations [10], with sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) and traditional serrated adenomas (TSAs) as precursors [11]. The molecular serrated neoplasia pathway starts with the acquisition of a mutation in a gene important to the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, usually in the form of BRAF or KRAS [12, 13]. The next step for lesions with BRAF mutations is widespread CIMP, which is characterised by silencing many (tumour suppressor) genes via the hypermethylation of promoters, often resulting in MSI [14]. This sequence of molecular events is also observed in the majority of MAC.

MAC shares morphological features with SAC. Large portions of SAC (20–41%) show extensive mucinous components [15, 16]. Specific histological criteria for SAC include epithelial serration (often slit‐like), abundant clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm, mucin production (often towards the invasive front) with cell balls and/or papillary rods, vesicular nuclei, and absence or less than 10% necrosis of the total tumour surface area [15, 17].

A defining feature of SAC is their serrated precursor, in particular SSL and TSA. Remnants of these can be identified in up to 17–50% of SAC [18]. Precursors of MAC are not well described. However, it is acknowledged that detection of precursors in retrospective cohorts is challenging, due to sampling error and complete overgrowth by the invasive tumour [19].

The molecular and morphological overlap of MAC and SAC suggests that these might be part of a spectrum rather than two distinct tumour types. To investigate this, we systematically analysed a unique large cohort of MAC for features of SAC, by examining (1) presence of precursor lesions, (2) morphology, and (3) molecular features.

Materials and methods

Cohort selection

Patients included in this study were identified by searching the Dutch nationwide pathology databank (PALGA), registered under LZV2016‐64. The initial selection was based on resection specimens from patients diagnosed with colorectal mucinous carcinomas between January 2000 and December 2010 in the following hospitals: Maxima Medisch Centrum, Catharina ziekenhuis, St. Anna ziekenhuis, Jeroen Bosch ziekenhuis, Bernhoven ziekenhuis, TweeSteden‐Elisabeth ziekenhuis, Elkerliek ziekenhuis, VieCuri ziekenhuis, and Amphia ziekenhuis. All rectal, anal, and appendiceal cases were excluded, as well as all signet ring cell carcinomas. All cases were reviewed and formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) material and H&E‐stained slides were available for all 698 eligible cases. This study was performed in accordance with research policies approved by the Radboud University Medical Centre Institutional Review Board (2021‐8200).

Clinicopathologic characteristics

The 698 cases were randomly divided into a test cohort of 396 cases and a validation cohort of 302 cases. Both cohorts were reviewed and all cases were scored by pathologists PZ and AMV for standard parameters based on criteria described in the 5th edition WHO of Digestive System Tumours [1] as well as for the presence of (lympho)vascular/perineural invasion, the amount of extracellular mucin deposition, the presence of signet ring cells, goblet cells, serrated growth pattern (defined as the presence of invasive glands with serrated slit‐like lumens), fixed and/or floating patterns (where fixed is defined as glands lined by a columnar mucus‐secreting epithelium together with interstitial mucin; and floating is defined as chains or irregular clusters of cells surrounded by mucus) [20], a pushing border at the invasive front, an identifiable precursor lesion adjacent to the invasive carcinoma, and the presence of appendiceal lesions. In case of discordance with previous diagnostic reports or any discrepancies otherwise, these cases were discussed in consensus meetings with a third pathologist, after which the majority vote was used.

Tissue microarrays

FFPE tumour blocks were available for 680 MACs and used to construct tissue microarrays (TMAs). Four core punches of 2 mm in two selected mucinous areas and, if applicable, two core punches of 2 mm in a selected non‐mucinous area were obtained from each FFPE block using the Tissue‐Tek Quick‐Ray System (Sakura Finetek USA, Inc., Torrance, CA, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections of 4 μm were cut from the constructed TMAs, mounted, and subsequently dried at 56 °C (for at least 20 min).

To establish the mismatch repair (MMR) status, immunohistochemical staining of MSH2 (clone: FE11, GA085, Dako Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), MSH6 (clone: EP49, GA086, Dako Agilent), MLH1 (clone: ES05, GA079, Dako Agilent) and PMS2 (clone EP51, GA087, Dako Agilent), was performed using the Dako EnVision FLEX method (GV800, Dako Agilent) and was carried out automatically by the Dako Omnis platform. Cases were considered MMR proficient (pMMR) when nuclear staining in tumour cells was present. Loss of nuclear staining in tumour cells with positive internal and external controls was considered as MMR deficient (dMMR).

DNA isolation and precipitation

To collect DNA samples from the 396 test cohort cases, depending on the size of the area of interest, 6–10 μm thick whole‐slide sections of FFPE material were cut. On these sections manual macrodissection was performed, according to marked areas of tumour tissue on corresponding H&E slides. Based on the amount of tissue, 50–200 μl of 5% Chelex‐100 (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA) in TET lysis buffer (10 mM Tris/HCL, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween20) was added. Samples were incubated at 95 °C and 350 rpm for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, 10% v/v Proteinase K (20 mg/mL, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was added. Samples were then incubated at 56 °C and 350 rpm for 16 h, followed by inactivation at 95 °C and 350 rpm for 10 min. After centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 rpm, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The supernatant was cooled on ice for 30 min. A 1/10th volume of 3 M Sodium Acetate (pH 5.2) and double the total volume of 100% Ethanol were added. The samples were then centrifugated for 15 min at 4 °C and 14,000 rpm. The supernatant was discarded and the pellets were washed with cold 70% ethanol and with 100% ethanol. After centrifugating for 2 min at 4 °C and 14,000 rpm, again the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were air‐dried and resolved in 50 μl TE buffer. DNA concentration was measured using Qubit dsDNA HS kit (Thermofisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Single‐molecule molecular inversion probes (SmMIP) sequencing and sequence data analysis

The same panel, library preparation, sequencing and variant calling were used and performed as described previously [21], including the following actionable target genes: BRAF, EGFR, ERBB2, GNAS, KRAS, MET, NRAS, PIK3CA, PTEN, and TP53. The determination of pathogenicity for variants found was done as described previously [22].

Bisulfite conversion of DNA

To perform the CT conversion of DNA, the EZ DNA Methylation Direct kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) was used, according to manufacturer instructions. We used a DNA input of 200 ng; if less than 200 ng of DNA was available, we used all the available DNA instead.

Multiplex nested methylation specific PCR (MSP)

The methylation status of the regions of CACNA1G, IGF2, NEUROG1, RUNX3, and SOCS1 was investigated using a multiplex nested MSP method. The outside primer sets were developed to bind the flanking regions upstream and downstream of the target. The inside primer sets are intended to amplify the target within the outside PCR products and detect the methylation status of the targeted CpG sites. The sequences of the primers used for the outside and inside PCR are shown in supplementary material, Table S1.

The outside (multiplex) PCR was performed in a reaction mixture with a final volume of 25 μl, containing five outside primer pairs (0.2 μM of each primer); dGTP, dCTP, dATP, and dTTP (0.3125 mM of each); 1× Magic buffer (16.6 mM (NH4)2SO4, 67 mM TRIS pH 8.8, 6.7 mM MgCl2 · 6H2O, 10 mM β‐mercapto‐ethanol); 0.5 U Immolase (BIO‐21047, GC Biotech BV, Waddinxveen, The Netherlands); and 4 μl of bisulfite‐treated DNA, or control DNA. The PCR conditions included a single cycle at 95 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s, followed by a final extension of 4 min at 72 °C.

For the inside PCR, the methylated and unmethylated reactions for each sample were performed separately. The final volume of each reaction mixture was 25 μl, containing one inside primer pair (0.4 μM of each), dGTP, dCTP, dATP, and dTTP (62.5 μM of each); 1× Magic buffer; 0.5 U Immolase; 1% Glycerol; and 4 μl of 1:1,000 in TE buffer diluted outside PCR product. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min; X cycles (for the values of X, see supplementary material, Table S1) of 95 °C for 30 s, X°C (for the values of X, see supplementary material, Table S1) for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; and 4 min at 72 °C. Depending on the inside primer pairs, annealing temperature and the numbers of cycles varied (see supplementary material, Table S1).

The methylation status of each sample was then analysed on a 2% agarose gel by loading 10 μl of the PCR products. The gene was interpreted as methylated when the result of the methylated PCR product was as strong or stronger than the result of the unmethylated PCR product on gel. A tumour was classified as CIMP‐H when at least three of the five tested genes were methylated.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical calculations, SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used. The different parameters were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Correction for multiple comparisons was done using the Bonferroni method. The interval between the date of diagnosis and the date of death or the last follow‐up, was used for the overall survival in the survival analyses. Patients alive at the end of their follow‐up were censored. Kaplan–Meier was used to generate the survival curves and log‐rank testing was used to compare the distributions. The p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The entire cohort consisted of 698 patients (Table 1), of whom 82.6% were older than 60 years, 51.7% were female, and 62.6% of tumours were located in the right colon. The distribution according to the UICC (8th) stages was as follows: 5.0% stage I, 49.7% stage II, 27.9% stage III, and 17.3% stage IV. In 302 patients, the classification serrated‐MAC or MAC‐NOS was only based on morphology and/or precursor presence. In 396 patients we added the molecular classification. These showed similar age, sex, location, and stage distribution (Table 1 and supplementary material, File S1).

Most precursors show serrated morphology

All cases were examined for precursor lesions. In 94 cases, no slides with adjacent normal colon tissue were available. In the remaining 604 cases, precursor lesions were found in 58 tumours (9.6%). These included 33 TSAs, 18 SSLs, of which 13 with dysplasia, four tubulovillous adenomas, and three tubular adenomas (Figure 1).

Growth patterns and precursor lesions of MAC. (A) Serrated morphology with elongated glands with serrated lumens. (B) Floating growth pattern. (C) Fixed growth pattern. (D) Lined with green is a sessile serrated lesion directly adjacent to the invasive MAC. (E) Lined with green is a traditional serrated lesion with low‐grade dysplasia directly adjacent to the invasive MAC.

Predominance of serrated morphology in MAC

Most of the 698 cases (71%) showed serrated morphology of the invasive tumour. The majority of tumours showed a fixed (42.3%) or mixed (42.0%) growth pattern (Figure 1). Signet ring cells and goblet cells were present in 23.5% and 80.2% of cases, respectively. Predictors of poor outcome were present relatively often: lymph vessel invasion (LVI) (29.9%), extramural venous invasion (EMVI) (19.1%) and perineural invasion (PNI) (10.7%) (Table 1).

Molecular subtypes of MAC

Using targeted next generation sequencing, an average of 2.49 pathogenic variants were found per tumour, ranging from 0 to 6. The most commonly mutated genes were KRAS (42.7%), APC (37.1%), BRAF (34.1%), TP53 (33.3%), TGFBR2 (26.5%), RNF43 (25.5%), PIK3CA (11.9%), and FBXW7 (10.6%) (Table 2). Of the 396 test cases, 33.8% were classified as MSI, using immunohistochemistry. Of the 260 MSS cases, only 121 cases showed unequivocal results due to degradation of the DNA and/or too low tumour cell percentages, with 60 cases (49.6%) being classified as CIMP‐L and 61 cases (50.4%) as CIMP‐H.

Subclassification of MAC

We applied three methods to classify a tumour as serrated: (1) Presence of a serrated precursor, (2) Serrated morphology, (3) Serrated molecular profile. The presence of a BRAF mutation (irrespective of the microsatellite stability status) was considered indicative of a serrated molecular profile, as was the presence of a KRAS mutation in combination with MSS and CIMP‐H. The tumours were classified as MAC‐NOS when lack of an adjacent serrated precursor and serrated morphology in combination with wild‐type BRAF and KRAS, or KRAS mutation in combination with MSI was present. Tumours lacking a serrated precursor and serrated morphology, but with a KRAS mutation in combination with MSS without CIMP‐H, were considered unclassifiable. Of all MAC, 83.1% was classified as serrated‐MAC, 9.8% as MAC‐NOS, and the remaining 7.1% as unclassifiable. There was considerable overlap between the serrated features, showing that tumours with serrated morphology often also show presence of a serrated precursor and/or presence of the described serrated molecular features (Figure 3).

Overlap of serrated features in MAC. Significant overlap of serrated precursors, serrated morphology, and/or serrated molecular features is shown. A small group of tumours show no serrated characteristics, and an even smaller group of unclassifiable tumours (KRAS mutated MSS tumours with no serrated precursor or serrated morphology).

Comparison of serrated‐MAC versus MAC‐NOS

When comparing clinicopathological characteristics, serrated‐MAC was present in significantly older patients [mean 70.5 years (SD 11.5) versus 63.2 (SD 10.4), p = <0.001], and showed more right‐sidedness (68.9% versus 45.9%, p = 0.005), less PNI (7.6% versus 23.1%, p = 0.002), more fixed growth pattern (75.7% versus 50.0%, p = 0.005), and fewer signet ring cells (20.0% versus 50.0%, p = 0.030). Serrated‐MAC showed lymph node metastases less often (38.3% versus 61.6%, p < 0.012) (Table 1).

By definition, serrated‐MAC showed significantly more mutations in BRAF (40.7% versus 0%, p < 0.001) and KRAS (41.6% versus 10.3%, p < 0.001). Discussion

Based on morphology we were able to determine that the majority of MAC show SAC specific characteristics, albeit with >50% extracellular mucin. Acknowledgement of cancer subtypes in the WHO Classification is restricted to those tumours that are recognised as being different in at least one dimension from the most common tumour type (clinical, histological, or genetic), preferably resulting in a different treatment or outcome [23]. While both MAC and SAC are distinct from the most common tumour type (adenocarcinoma NOS) in two or three dimensions, their overlap is challenging for optimal classification. Additionally, there was no difference in overall survival in serrated‐MAC. This potentially underscores the idea of a spectrum rather than two distinct tumour types.

Ideally, a cancer subtype implies a different treatment or outcome. MAC does present with distinct clinical behaviour, because MAC amongst others (e.g., signet ring cell carcinoma) show a different metastatic pattern [24] and less response to neoadjuvant therapy and palliative systemic therapy compared to adenocarcinoma NOS [7, 25]. It is hypothesised that the mucinous lakes are responsible by ‘shielding’ tumour cells in established metastases [25]. This implies that the morphology of MAC is directly linked to the clinical behaviour. Less is known about the clinical features of SAC, partly due to lack of specific registration data.

Currently the diagnosis is based on morphology alone [1] and SAC can present either as MAC (as shown in the current study) or without a dominant mucinous component [16]. Morphological classification is often associated with considerable interobserver variability [18]. Therefore, we explored the value of additional molecular features as well as the determination of precursor lesions.

We could examine these precursors in 9.6% of cases, which is in line with other studies that show precursors in 10% of CRC [26, 27]. However, these are based on registrations and lack adequate information on serrated polyps as well as on tumour types. While the presence of a serrated precursor is highly suggestive of involvement of the serrated pathway and might indicate SAC, there are some caveats. In particular, SSL can present with a variation of dysplastic patterns, including conventional dysplasia [19]. Whether this type of dysplasia is a precursor for conventional adenocarcinoma is unknown. Moreover, selective overgrowth of this type of dysplasia might lead to an incorrect diagnosis of conventional adenoma. The majority of our cases with a serrated precursor also showed molecular features of the serrated pathway (81.2%).

The serrated neoplasia pathway refers mainly to the development of (right‐sided) CRC from serrated precursors, in particular SSL. Molecularly, these tumours are characterised by BRAF mutations, CIMP, and MSI due to hypermethylation of MLH1 [9, 13]. In contrast, the alternative serrated pathway starts with TSA, resulting in the development of (left‐sided) CRC, characterised by KRAS mutations and MSS [13, 28]. However, these molecular features show significant overlap with the conventional adenoma‐carcinoma sequence [29] and are non‐informative. The molecular background of SAC is under discussion: while early papers suggest BRAF‐mutation/CIMP/MSI [12], more recent papers acknowledge the role of KRAS‐mutation in tumours with morphology fitting with SAC [28, 30].

Another more recently described event in serrated tumorigenesis is upregulation of the WNT pathway due to inactivating mutations affecting the RNF43‐ZNRF3 complex [31, 32, 33] or downregulation of RNF43 as a result of fusions in the RSPO gene family [34]. This is in contrast to upregulation of the WNT pathway in conventional CRC which generally happens through APC mutation [31, 35]. These truncating mutations of RNF43 and APC are thought to be mutually exclusive [36]. Our cohort of MAC shows a high rate of inactivating RNF43 mutations (31.6%), and indeed only 2.2% of cases show inactivating mutations in both RNF43 and APC (Figure 2). The downregulation of RNF43 via fusions in the RSPO1‐4 genes is said to occur in 30% of TSAs [13], with a preference for KRAS mutated TSAs. Unfortunately, we were not able to perform fusion gene analyses and are therefore unaware of the presence of RSPO1‐4 fusion genes in this MAC cohort. We consider this a limitation of our study, since there is a possibility that there are cases, now labelled as unclassifiable MAC, that could have been classified as MAC‐SAC due to the presence of a RSPO1‐4 gene fusion; specifically, because it is known that mutations in RNF43 are inversely associated with KRAS mutations [32].

In conclusion, we show that the large majority of colorectal MAC have similar features to SAC, with serrated precursor lesions, serrated morphology in the invasive tumour, and/or the presence of the specific molecular traits attributed to the serrated neoplasia pathway.
