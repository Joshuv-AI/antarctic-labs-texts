The TP53-LGALS4 axis modulates the tumor immune microenvironment and synergizes with anti PD-L1 therapy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Fan Zhang, Minli Yang, Xiaoqing Peng, Qing Qin, Yulan Huang, Renchao Deng, Dapeng Zhang, Lili Zhang, Houjie Liang, Jianjun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of galectin-4 (LGALS4) in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is poorly understood.MethodsThrough analysis of single-cell profiling data from various colon tissues, this work identifies tumor suppressor genes critically involved in CRC development. The impact of LGALS4 on CT26 cell growth was evaluated through a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments. The potential mechanisms regulating LGALS4 expression and LGALS4 regulatory biological functions were investigated using different analytical and detection assays. In vivo experiments evaluated the synergistic anti-tumor effect of LGALS4 overexpression and PDL1 neutralizing antibody.ResultsThis study revealed that the expression of LGALS4 was significantly reduced in clinical CRC tissue samples. Multimodal analysis combined with experimental verification suggested that TP53 mutations might mediate the downregulation of LGALS4 expression. Functional studies indicated that overexpression of LGALS4 did not affect the biological functions of CT26 cells, such as proliferation, apoptosis, and migration, but significantly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous transplanted tumor. Mechanistically, LGALS4 exerts its effect by improving the TIME. Specifically, it activates the CCL4/CCR5 axis through the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting the recruitment of various immune cells, including macrophages. Moreover, overexpression of LGALS4 can synergistically enhance anti-tumor effects with PD-L1 neutralizing antibodies.ConclusionCollectively, these findings demonstrate a novel role for TP53-mediated LGALS4 in regulating tumor progression. This work identifies LGALS4 as a promising therapeutic target and provides a foundation for the development of combined immunotherapy based on galectin for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07598-6.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), TP53, LGALS4, Macrophages, PD-L1

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths, whose initiation and progression are closely associated with genetic and environmental factors as well as dysregulated gene regulation [1–5]. Aberrant regulation of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes represents one of the critical mechanisms underlying tumor initiation and progression [6]. Among these, tumor suppressor genes regulate cell proliferation to prevent malignant transformation and are involved in the development and differentiation of normal cells. Notably, tumor suppressor genes have unique advantages in cancer treatment, as they can directly induce the apoptosis of cancer cells, enhance the efficacy of conventional therapies, and have minimal toxic effects on normal cells [7].

Mutations-driven aberrant activation of molecular signaling pathways and genes plays pivotal roles in the initiation and progression of CRC [8]. As one of the genes with a high mutation rate in CRC, TP53 significantly impacts the prognosis of CRC patients [8]. The encoded p53 protein coordinates multiple cellular responses, including DNA repair and cell cycle arrest [9]. Furthermore, p53 functions as a sequence-specific transcription factor that binds to specific DNA sequences within the genome (p53 response elements or p53-binding sites), subsequently activating the transcription of adjacent downstream genes [10–12]. Owing to the lack of structural features typical of druggable targets in p53 as a nuclear transcription factor, along with the diversity of TP53 mutations and dynamic conformational changes, p53 has long been regarded as an “undruggable” target. Thus, there is an urgent need to actively explore potential therapeutic strategies based on the p53 pathway.

Galectins contain one or two carbohydrate recognition domains (CRDs) and are classified into three types: prototype galectins (e.g., LGALS1, LGALS2), chimeric galectins (exemplified by LGALS3), and tandem-repeat galectins (including LGALS8 and LGALS9). Notably, galectins function both intracellularly and extracellularly following secretion, where they regulate apoptosis and maintain immune homeostasis [13–18]. Dysregulation of their expression is associated with the progression and metastasis of various tumors, rendering them promising therapeutic targets [18–21]. Research has shown that LGALS9 is downregulated in CRC and can regulate the chemotaxis of natural killer (NK) cells by affecting the polarity of F-actin [22–24]. LGALS4 is classified as a tandem-repeat galectin. Studies indicate that low expression of LGALS4 in multiple cancer types is correlated with poor prognosis, although the precise mechanism remains unclear [18–26].

Immune checkpoint blockade therapy (ICI) has achieved remarkable results in the treatment of various cancers, including CRC, lung cancer, and breast cancer [27–29]. For example, targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or its ligand (PD-L1) has demonstrated durable antitumor effects in the treatment of metastatic CRC patients with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), providing more clinical benefits to patients [30–32]. However, ICIs usually have limited efficacy in most CRC patients with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) and microsatellite stable (MSS) or instability-low (MSI-L) [33, 34]. In addition, the complexity and heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) can lead to the development of resistance to ICI therapy [27]. Therefore, further exploration of combined treatment strategies based on ICIs may help overcome therapy resistance [35, 36].

In this study, database analysis and clinical sample validation revealed that LGALS4 is significantly reduced in CRC patients, and the downregulation was attributed to the transcriptional expression mediated by TP53. LGALS4 overexpression improved the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by promoting the recruitment of CCL4/CCR5-dependent macrophages and inhibiting tumor growth. Furthermore, LGALS4 overexpression synergistically enhanced the antitumor effect of PD-L1 neutralizing antibodies. Taken together, these findings elucidate a novel role for LGALS4 in regulating tumor progression, providing a foundation for galectin-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data analysis

The CRC RNA sequencing datasets (GSE10714, GSE35279, and GSE8671) originated from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the CRC scRNA-seq datasets (SCP259, GSE132465, EMTAB8107, GSE132465 and GSE166555) originated from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Omics Discovery Index (OmicsDI) (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/studies/E-MTAB-8107). The data of CRC patients were obtained from the TCGA database (portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). After the data were downloaded, the R statistical environment (v4.3.1) deployed on the Xiyou Cloud platform (https://www.xiyoucloud.net/) was used for analysis. The remaining data were analyzed via online databases, including GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) and UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/).

Interaction information was obtained from the STRING (https://string-db.org/) database, and a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed via Cytoscape software to search for Hub genes. The treatment response prediction ability of the TCGA-COAD data was evaluated via tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE, http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/login/).

The RNA sequencing analysis was conducted by Shanghai Ouyi Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Each test sample group had three independent samples. The expression levels of genes in the shLGALS4-OE group and the NC group were analyzed via DESeq2 software. A P value less than 0.05 and an absolute value of |log2FC| greater than 1.5 were considered to indicate significant differential expression. For functional enrichment analysis, the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1) was used to identify significantly enriched GO pathways.

The prediction of binding sites for transcription factors and promoter sequences was obtained by using the JASPAR database(https://jaspar.elixir.no/).

Clinical samples

Human CRC tissues and corresponding adjacent normal tissues were collected (12 pairs for RNA level detection, 6 pairs for protein level detection, 3 samples for immunohistochemical staining, and 3 samples for immunofluorescence staining). These samples were collected and diagnosed at Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). Each patient signed a written informed consent form, and these scientific studies were approved by the patients and the ethics committee.

Mice

Male BALB/c wild-type mice (6–8 weeks old, body weight 16–20 g) and male athymic nude mice (6–8 weeks old, body weight 16–20 g) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Mouse studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University) (approval number AMUWEC20232985) and were carried out according to the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the US National Institutes of Health (publication no. 85–23, revised 1996). All the mice were housed in a pathogen-free facility with a 12-h light, 12-h dark cycle and were provided with food and purified water ad libitum.

Cell culture

In this study, a mouse colon adenocarcinoma cell line (CT26) preserved in our laboratory was used. It was verified via short tandem repeat (STR) analysis and confirmed to be free of harmful mycoplasma contamination via PCR detection. The cells (including primary macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; #C3113-0500, VivaCell) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #C04001-500, VivaCell) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin solution (#C0222, Beyotime) and placed in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂.

In a 6-well plate, the CT26 cells were divided into the LGALS4-NC group, the LGALS4-OE group and the WT group. When the cells covered 40%-60% of the bottom area of the 6-well plate, infection was carried out according to the instructions of the lentivirus particle kit (Corues Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). After puromycin screening (#ST551-10 mg, Beyotime), stably transfected LGALS4-overexpressing cell lines were successfully obtained. These cells were subsequently inoculated into T-25 cell culture flasks for expansion and cultivation for use in subsequent cell experiments and animal experiments. BAY11-7802 (#S2913, Selleck) was used in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted via TRIzol reagent (#R0016, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the RNA concentration was determined, 1 µg of RNA was immediately reverse transcribed into cDNA via the PrimeScript RT Kit (#RR036A, TaKaRa). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed in a 20 µL total volume on the Bio-Rad CFX Connect system via the TB Green Premix Kit (#RR820A, TaKaRa). GAPDH and β-actin were used as housekeeping genes for the quantification of relative gene expression, which was calculated via the 2(−ΔΔCt) method.

Western blot

Upon harvesting cells or tissues, total protein extraction was performed via RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (#P1045, #PP0013K, Beyotime). The protein concentration was quantified via BCA assay (#P10012S, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were loaded for SDS‒PAGE: electrophoresis was initiated at 50 V for 30 min and then adjusted to 120 V until the tracking dye reached the gel bottom. Protein transfer was conducted via wet transfer method at 90 V for 120 min onto a PVDF membrane (#IPVH00010, Merck Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight on a shaker (antibody dilution followed manufacturers’ instructions). After washing, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. After washing, chemiluminescence detection was performed, and images were captured via a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensity analysis was conducted with ImageJ software.

CCK8 proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was quantified via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Product No. #BS350B, Biosharp). The cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well. After incubation periods of 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h, 200 µL of complete medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO₂ for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently measured via a microplate spectrophotometer. Prior to data analysis, the background absorbance value (from blank wells containing medium and CCK-8 but no cells) was subtracted from all experimental measurements.

EdU proliferation assay

Tumor cells in the logarithmic growth phase were removed and digested with trypsin to generate a single-cell suspension. After the cells were counted, the density was adjusted to 1 × 104 cells per well. After being resuspended in 1 mL of culture medium, the cells were inoculated into 24-well plates with preplaced coverslips. The plates were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment. The next day, upon observing a cell density of 40%-50%, the medium was discarded. Then, 500 µL of EdU working solution prepared according to the instructions (#C0078L, Beyotime) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for another 1 h. The cells were fixed with 0.4% paraformaldehyde (#BL539A, Biosharp) for 20 min, washed twice with PBS, and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. After being washed twice again with PBS, EdU staining was performed according to the instructions (#C0078L, Beyotime). Anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (#P0131-25 mL, Beyotime) was added for mounting. The samples were dried in the dark and observed under a high-resolution fluorescence microscope.

Colony formation assay

Tumor cells in the logarithmic growth phase were obtained and digested with trypsin to form a single-cell suspension. After cell counting, the cell density was adjusted to 500 cells per well. The cells were resuspended in 2 mL of culture medium and then inoculated into 6-well plates. The cells were gently mixed, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 10‒14 days. The medium was replaced in a timely manner during this period. Once the colonies were visible to the naked eye, the medium was discarded, the cells were rinsed with PBS, and the cells were then fixed with 0.4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. The cells were subsequently washed twice with PBS and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (#C0121-100 mL, Beyotime). The excess dye was washed away, the plates were allowed to dry, and then, the colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted under a microscope to analyze the colony-forming ability of the cells.

Annexin V-PI apoptosis assay

Tumor cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected. These cells were resuspended in 2 mL of culture medium at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and then seeded into 6-well plates. The 6-well plates were incubated in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Trypsin (#C0201-100 mL, Beyotime) was subsequently used to digest the cells, and a cell suspension with a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL was obtained. Next, the cells were gently washed 2–3 times with PBS to remove residual impurities. Thereafter, the cells were processed strictly according to the instructions of the Annexin V/7ADD staining kit (#E-CK-A212, Elabscience). Finally, flow cytometry (Instrument model: BD FACSVerse C6, USA) and FlowJo 10.8.1 software were used to analyze the processed cells for the detection of apoptosis.

Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining

After the mouse lung tissue was frozen, it was processed according to the instructions of the Hematoxylin‒Eosin (HE) staining kit (#BL2714B, Biosharp). The samples were observed and photographed under a microscope and then analyzed.

Immunohistochemistry

All the tissue sections used for immunohistochemistry were first fixed with formalin and then embedded in paraffin. After dewaxing and rehydration, the slides were placed in 1× citrate buffer (ZLI-9065; Beijing Zhong Shan Golden Bridge Biological Technology) for antigen retrieval at 95 °C for 20 min. After natural cooling, permeabilization was performed with 0.5% Triton X-100 (#1139ML100, Germany Biofroxx), followed by quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H₂O₂. The slides were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-LGALS4 antibody (#27552-1-AP, Proteintech). The next day, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (#KIT-9922, MXB Biotechnology) for 30 min. After incubation, the slides were visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (#ZLI-9018, Beijing Zhong Shan Golden Bridge Biological Technology) and then counterstained with hematoxylin (#AR005, BOSTER Biological Technology). Finally, the degree of immunohistochemical staining was evaluated via the immunoreactive score system (IRS).

Immunofluorescence

Clinical colorectal cancer tissue samples were obtained and subjected to cryosectioning (HM525NX, Thermo). The resulting sections were then fixed onto glass slides. The slides were rinsed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) (#BL602A, Biosharp), followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100. After permeabilization, the slides were rinsed again with TBS to remove residual reagents. Subsequently, blocking solution (#ZLI-9311, Beijing Zhong Shan Golden Bridge Biological Technology) was added dropwise onto the sections, and the slides were incubated for a certain period to block nonspecific binding sites. After blocking, specific primary antibodies (#27552-1-AP, Proteintech) were added, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the slides were washed with TBS. Then, the corresponding secondary antibodies were added (#711165152, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and the slides were incubated at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, the slides were rinsed once more with TBS. Anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI (#P0131-25 mL, Beyotime) was subsequently added dropwise for nuclear staining. Finally, the mounting step was completed, observations were made, and images were captured via a fluorescence microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates. When the cell confluence reached approximately 60%-70%, the plasmid mixture containing LGALS5-promoter, TP53 overexpression plasmid, and Renilla control plasmid (PRL-TK) was co-transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (#L3000015, Invitrogen). After 48 h, the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay was carried out following the standard protocol provided by the Double-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (#E1910, Promega). The experimental results were calculated after normalization.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

The spleen was gently crushed through a 75-µm filter (#BS-70-CS, Biosharp) and then dissolved in culture medium. After centrifugation, the red blood cells were lysed with red blood cell lysate (#R1010, Solarbio) for 2 min. The lysis reaction was terminated by adding an equal volume of PBS containing fetal bovine serum (FBS). The obtained cells were resuspended in PBS and filtered through a 45-µm (#BS-40-CS, Biosharp) filter again, resulting in a single-cell suspension.

The tumor tissue was first carefully minced into approximately 3-mm³ pieces via ophthalmic scissors. The minced tumor tissue was subsequently placed in a digestive solution (prepared by dissolving 1 mg/mL type IV collagenase, 0.1 mg/mL DNase, and 0.1 mL/mL hyaluronidase). The tissue was digested at 37 °C for 45 min. After digestion, the digestion mixture was filtered and centrifuged. Like spleen cells, red blood cells were lysed to obtain a single-cell suspension of the tumor tissue.

In the cell sorting experiment, after the spleen was processed in the aforementioned manner, MDSC staining was performed via flow cytometric antibodies. After being stained with the cell viability dye (DAPI), the MDSCs were immediately sorted via a flow cytometer. Attention was given to maintaining a sterile and low-temperature environment to ensure the survival rate of the cells for subsequent experiments.

Cell hypoxia induction experiment

After placing the cells on the plate and allowing them to stabilize for 24 h, expose them to hypoxia using the Whitley H35 hypoxystation (Don Whitley Scientific Limited) for 24 h and 48 h. The specific conditions are as follows: The conditions were 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2.

Mouse models of subcutaneous tumors, peritoneal implantable metastasis and lung metastasis

The mice were randomly assigned to groups via a random number table (n = 4 per group). The tumor cells were resuspended in precooled PBS at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL. A 100 µL aliquot of the corresponding cell suspension was subcutaneously injected into the inguinal region of the left lower limb of each mouse, followed by a 14-day feeding period. The mice were anesthetized via CO₂ inhalation and humanely euthanized. The tumor tissues were excised, measured, and documented. The tumor volume was calculated via the following formula: volume= (length×width²)/2. When the intraperitoneal metastasis model was constructed, 100 µl of the tumor suspension was injected into the abdominal cavity of the right lower abdomen. To establish the lung metastasis mouse model, 100 µl of the tumor suspension was injected into the tail vein of each mouse.

Mouse treatments

In the mouse treatment experiment, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups. On day 3 after inoculation, when the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm³, 100 µL of the drug and the control treatment were administered via intraperitoneal injection. On days 5, 7, 9, and 11, 100 µL of the drug or the control treatment was subsequently given. For the survival experiment, the mice were also randomly divided into groups. On the 3rd day after inoculation, once the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm³, 100 µL of the drug was intraperitoneally injected every other day for approximately 3 weeks until the end of the experiment. The drugs utilized included TAK-779 (#HY-13406, MedChemExpre), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (#BL165B, Biosharp), PDL1 (B7-H1) (#BE0101-1MG, InVivoMab), or a mouse IgG2b isotype control antibody (#BE0090-1MG, InVivoMab).

Isolation of peritoneal macrophages

The mice were administered an intraperitoneal injection of 2 mL of 3% thioglycollate on day 1 (#T9032, Sigma‒Aldrich). On day 3, the mice were subsequently humanely euthanized via exposure to carbon dioxide (CO2). Peritoneal cells were then harvested through lavage using 5 mL of DMEM supplemented with a 1% penicillin‒streptomycin solution. Following centrifugation, the pelleted peritoneal cells were resuspended and transferred to cell culture dishes for incubation. After a one-hour incubation period, all nonadherent (floating) cells were carefully aspirated, and the adherent cells were designated peritoneal macrophages (PMs). The above procedures were carried out in a biosafety cabinet to ensure that the cells were not contaminated. The cells were subsequently harvested via accutase cell detachment solution (#423201, BioLegend) and recounted for the next experiment. TAK-779 (#HY-13406, MedChemExpre) was used in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Migration and invasion assays

Cell migration was evaluated via an 8-µm pore size Transwell insert (#353097, Falcon). A cell invasion chamber with an 8-micron aperture (#354480, Corning) was used to assess the invasion ability of the cells. The cells were suspended in 500 µL of serum-free medium at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. This cell suspension was then placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. In the lower chamber, 700 µL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added as a chemoattractant. The insert was incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the migrated cells on the insert were fixed with 0.4% formaldehyde solution and stained with 0.1% iodine crystals. The non-migrated cells were subsequently gently removed from the surface of the upper membrane via a cotton swab. Finally, the cells in three randomly selected microscopic fields on each membrane were counted.

The migration of PMs and MDSCs was evaluated via 8-micron pore diameter Transwell chambers (#353097, Frank Company). Five hundred microliters of complete medium was resuspended with 1 × 105 PMs or MDSCs and placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell chamber [37]. A total of 700 µl of complete medium containing 1 × 105 tumor cells was added to the lower chamber. The remaining steps were the same as above.

Graphic plotting

The relevant graphs were all constructed via BioGDP.com and IBS 2.0 [38, 39].

Statistical analyses

When appropriate, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis, log-rank test, and Student’s t test were performed. The data are presented as the means ± s.d.s, and these means are derived from at least three independent studies. Statistical analysis was conducted via GraphPad Prism version 9 software (GraphPad Software). Differences were considered statistically significant when *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 and nonsignificant when n.s.

Results

Low expression of LGALS4 in human CRC tissues

To investigate changes in gene expression patterns during the transformation from normal intestinal tissue to CRC, we obtained human colorectal single-cell (scRNA-seq) data from four stages—normal, preinflammatory, inflammatory, and cancerous—from SCP259 and GSE132465 (Fig. 1A). Through clustering analysis of gene expression in intestinal epithelial cells that give rise to CRC, six distinct clusters of gene expression trends were identified across the four stages (Fig. 1B). We focused on cluster 1, where gene expression continued to decline, suggesting that it might be a tumor suppressor gene. We subsequently conducted a Cox regression analysis on cluster 1 and found that LGALS4 was closely related to CRC progression (Fig. 1C). LGALS4 (Gal-4, galectin-4) is a tandem-repeat galectin. Previous studies reported that LGALS4 plays a tumor-suppressing role in CRC [18, 25]. In addition, transcriptomic profiling of LGALS genes revealed consistent downregulation of LGALS4 in both normal and neoplastic tissues across three independent cohorts. (Fig. 1D). The data obtained from the TCGA-COAD and UALCAN databases also indicate that the RNA and protein levels of LGALS4 are downregulated in CRC tissues. (Fig. 1E-F). Although the RNA and protein expression of LGALS4 did not significantly correlate with the stage of CRC (Fig. 1G-H), the low expression of LGALS4 in CRC was associated with a poor prognosis (Fig. 1I-J).

To validate our findings, we analyzed paired human CRC tissues (T) and adjacent paracarcinoma tissues (N). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), Western blotting, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses consistently revealed that LGALS4 expression in CRC was decreased (Fig. 1K‒Q).

LGALS4 does not affect the proliferation, apoptosis or migration of CRC cells in vitro

To further explore the role of LGALS4 in CRC, the CRC scRNA-seq datasets were scored for 14 representative tumor gene sets (e.g., proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis) based on the expression level of LGALS4. The results indicated that the different expressions of LGALS4 did not have significant impact on various cell biological activities of CRC (Fig. 2A). To validate the findings of our analysis, we established CT26 cell lines with stable LGALS4 overexpression (Fig. 2B-C). Compared with the control group (LGALS4-NC), the overexpression of LGALS4 (LGALS4-OE) had no significant effect on the proliferation of CRC cells according to the results of the CCK-8 (Fig. 2D), EDU (Fig. 2E-F), and colony formation (Fig. 2G-H) assays. In addition, flow cytometric analysis revealed no significant changes in the apoptosis of LGALS4-OE cells (Fig. 2I-J). Transwell assays confirmed that LGALS4-OE did not influence cell migration or invasion (Fig. 2K-L). Taken together, these results indicate that LGALS4 does not affect CRC cell biological behavior.

LGALS4-OE inhibits the subcutaneous growth of CRC in mice

To further evaluate the function of LGALS4 in vivo, we constructed a series of mouse CRC models, including subcutaneous tumor, peritoneal carcinomatosis, and lung metastasis models. We found that LGALS4-OE did not influence tumor progression in nude mice (Fig. 3A-H). Previous studies have demonstrated that the galectin family may influence tumor progression by regulating the immune system [40–43]. To explore whether LGALS4 influences tumor growth via the immune system, we established the abovementioned transplanted tumor model in immunocompetent BALB/c mice. No significant changes were observed in either the peritoneal carcinomatosis model or the lung metastasis model (Fig. 3I-M). However, LGALS4-OE significantly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous tumors (Fig. 3N-P). These results indicate that LGALS4 may suppress the growth of subcutaneously transplanted tumors in mice through tumor immunity.

The mutation of TP53 leads to the low expression of LGALS4

Given the low expression of LGALS4 and its regulatory role in tumors, we further investigated the specific mechanism driving the decreased expression of LGALS4 in CRC. Oncogenic mutations and the TME are key drivers of altered gene expression in tumor cells [44–47]. Therefore, we assessed the correlation between LGALS4 and oncogenic mutations via public databases. The mutation landscape plot revealed that the frequency of TP53 mutations was relatively higher in the low LGALS4 group (Fig. 4A-B). Subsequent analysis of the TCGA-COAD cohort further confirmed that the expression of LGALS4 was lower in the TP53 mutation group (Fig. 4C). Based on the prediction from the JASPAR database, TP53, functioning as a transcription factor [48], may bind to the promoter region of LGALS4 and potentially regulate its transcriptional expression (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, dual-luciferase reporter gene assay confirmed that the transcriptional activity of LGALS4 was enhanced after the overexpression of TP53 (P53OE) (Fig. 4E). After mutating the LGALS4 promoter sequence of the predicted binding site, the P53 protein could not promote the expression of LGALS4 (Fig. 4F-G). Additionally, we further investigated the relationship between LGALS4 expression and TME. Correlation analyses showed a significant negative correlation between LGALS4 and hypoxic metabolism, and a positive correlation with lactate metabolism and glucose metabolism (Fig. 4F-I). However, the qPCR assays revealed that hypoxia treatment actually stimulated the upregulation of LGALS4 (Fig. 4J). Furthermore, the high lactate profile within the TME is inconsistent with the downward trend of LGALS4 expression [49]. Members of the galectin family, including LGALS4, exhibit a close association with cellular glucose metabolism [50]. Therefore, the nutrient-depleted TME may also contribute to the regulation of LGALS4 expression. Collectively, these findings indicate that TP53 mutations might be a key factor in regulating the expression of LGALS4 in CRC.

LGALS4-OE obviously regulates the tumor immune microenvironment

To further explore how LGALS4 regulates tumor immunity, the group with high expression of LGALS4 in the CRC, an increase in the infiltration of myeloid cells was revealed by single-cell transcriptomics research (Fig. 5A-B). To further validate our analysis results, flow cytometry was employed to analyze the immune microenvironment of mouse tumors (Fig. S1A). The results revealed that the proportion of CD45-positive immune cells significantly increased in the LGALS4-OE group (Fig. 5C). Therefore, we further tested the changes in T cells, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Compared with the control, LGALS4-OE obviously promoted the infiltration of total T cells and CD8⁺ T cells while reducing the proportion of CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 5D and E). Moreover, the proportion of macrophages in the TME also significantly increased, accompanied by a decrease in the number of M2-type macrophages (Fig. 5F and G). Furthermore, LGALS4-OE significantly increased the number of MDSCs and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) but did not affect the proportion of monocyte-derived MDSCs (M-MDSCs) (Fig. 5H and I).

Given that systemic immunity is also extremely important for the progression of tumors, changes in immune cells in the mouse spleen were further examined. However, we did not observe any significant changes in CD45⁺ immune cells (Fig. S1B). There were also no significant differences in the proportions of total T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, or CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. S1C and Fig. S1D). The overall proportion of macrophages in the spleen remained unchanged, but the proportion of M1-type macrophages increased, whereas the proportion of M2-type macrophages decreased (Fig. S1E and Fig. S1F). Furthermore, LGALS4-OE had no effect on the number of MDSCs, M-MDSCs, or PMN-MDSCs in the spleen (Fig. S1G and Fig. S1H).

LGALS4 mobilizes antitumor immunity via CCL4/CCR5-dependent macrophage recruitment

To elucidate how LGALS4 regulates the TIME, we analyzed the RNA sequencing data and found that chemotaxis-related pathways and immune cell recruitment were significantly enriched (Fig. 6A). Therefore, we performed transwell assays and found that LGALS4 could significantly increase the migration ability of macrophages while having a relatively minor effect on the migration of MDSCs (Fig. 6B-C). To further clarify the mechanism by which LGALS4 enhances macrophage recruitment, the genes of the three significant chemotaxis-related pathways identified through Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis were subjected to differential expression analysis. The results revealed that the upregulation of the chemokine C-C motif ligand 4 (CCL4, macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1β) was significant in the LGALS4-OE group (Fig. 6D). Previous studies have confirmed that tumor cells and immune cells can secrete CCL4 to recruit cytotoxic lymphocytes and macrophages to exert antitumor effects [51–54]. To elucidate the role of LGALS4 in CCL4-dependent macrophage recruitment, migration assays showed that after pretreatment with an inhibitor (TAK − 779) specific to the CCL4 receptor, a type of C-C chemokine receptor (CCR5), the increase in macrophage migration caused by overexpression of LGALS4 disappeared (Fig. 6E-F). This result indicated that LGALS4 may recruit macrophages through CCL4. To further investigate signaling mechanisms by which LGALS4 regulates CCL4, we analyzed RNA sequencing data and identified significant changes in NF-κB signaling (Fig. 6G). Subsequent Western blot experiments confirmed that LGALS4 could promote the phosphorylation of P65, and the effect of LGALS4 on CCL4 expression disappeared after treating with a P65 phosphorylation inhibitor. (Fig. 6H). Notably, TAK-779 treatment also eliminated the inhibitory effect of LGALS4-OE on tumor growth (Fig. 6I-K). Collectively, these data demonstrate that LGALS4 may activate the CCL4/CCR5 axis through NF-κB signaling to drive macrophage infiltration, thereby suppressing CRC growth.

LGALS4-OE synergizes with PD-L1 Blockade to inhibit CRC progression

ICIs, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 blocking antibodies, constitute cornerstone therapies in modern oncology [55, 56]. PD-1/PD-L1 blockade exerts multipronged antitumor effects, such as restoring the function of exhausted T cells and remodeling tumor-associated macrophages [57–59]. Given that LGALS4-OE significantly enhances the infiltration abundance of immune cells in the TME, we speculate that LGALS4 might affect the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy. Thus, we analyzed the correlation between LGALS4 expression levels and sensitivity to immunotherapy and found that high LGALS4 expression predicts a more sensitive response to immunotherapy (Fig. 7A-B). To further investigate whether LGALS4-OE could increase the efficacy of PD-L1 immunotherapy, we combined LGALS4-OE therapy with anti PD-L1 neutralizing antibody (anti PD-L1) treatment (Fig. 7C). The results revealed that the antitumor effect of the combined therapy was significantly greater than that of any single therapy, indicating that there is a synergistic effect between LGALS4-OE and anti PD-L1 blockade therapy (Fig. 7D-G).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that low expression of LGALS4 caused by TP53 mutation in CRC tissues can reduce the infiltration of immune cells into the TME and promote tumor growth, primarily via NF-κB signaling to CCL4/CCR5-mediated macrophage recruitment. Additionally, LGALS4-OE and PD-L1 neutralizing antibody therapy can act synergistically to exert antitumor effects. These findings further elucidate the role of LGALS4 in regulating the immune microenvironment of CRC and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

Analysis results show that LGALS4 expression progressively decreases from normal tissues through inflammatory stages to tumor progression. This study has further confirmed that TP53 mutation is the core driver of LGALS4 downregulation in CRC. However, there are still many key questions regarding the molecular mechanism by which TP53 regulates the transcription of LGALS4 that require in-depth exploration. For instance, there is currently a lack of analysis of the TP53 mutation subtypes that regulate LGALS4, which prevents us from clearly identifying the specific roles of different mutation subtypes in the regulation of LGALS4 expression. And it is not clear whether mutant TP53 abnormally recruits auxiliary inhibitory factors during the downregulation of LGALS4, or loses the ability to bind with auxiliary activating factors. Moreover, due to the absence of human immunotherapy experiments and the limited number of in-vivo samples, there is uncertainty when translating our research conclusions into clinical immunotherapy applications. This uncertainty is reflected in the inability to determine whether the LGALS4-related mechanisms are still effective in the human immunotherapy environment. Therefore, in-depth studies on the TP53-LGALS4 regulatory network, the implementation of human immune therapy experiments, and the expansion of the sample size in vivo are of crucial importance for promoting the clinical application of LGALS4. This will provide solid theoretical support for the “prevention - treatment - reversal of drug resistance” strategy for CRC.

In this study, the overexpression of LGALS4 had a relatively weak effect on the proliferation of CRC cells in vitro, but it could significantly inhibit tumor growth in vivo. The underlying reason for this phenomenon is that LGALS4 promoted immune cell infiltration within the TME, particularly through CCL4/CCR5-dependent macrophage recruitment, consequently suppressing CRC growth. We also found that blocking the NFκB signal can reverse the regulatory effect of LGALS4 on CCL4. Notably, LGALS4 synergizes with PD-L1 neutralizing antibodies to enhance antitumor responses. Further elucidating the precise mechanisms by which the LGALS4/CCL4 axis modulates the TIME will be crucial for the rational design of novel combination treatment strategies based on LGALS4.

In conclusion, this study revealed that TP53 upregulated the expression of LGALS4, and LGALS4 exerts antitumor effects by recruiting macrophages via the CCL4/CCR5 axis. Moreover, LGALS4 can synergize with PD-L1 neutralizing antibodies, thereby prolonging the survival of mice. These findings provide new insights into the regulatory mechanisms and functional roles of LGALS4, particularly regarding its immunomodulatory functions, and offer a theoretical basis for combination treatment strategies involving PD-L1 inhibition.
