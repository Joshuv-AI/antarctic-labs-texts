SynthPET: A 3D generative AI approach for FDG‐PET image synthesis from T1‐weighted MRI and ASL CBF in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Xavier Beltran‐Urbano, Katie R Jobson, Ilya M Nasrallah, Ajay Kumar, Manuel Taso, Sandhitsu R Das, Christopher A Brown, Sipei Li, Long Xie, Corey T McMillan, Paul A Yushkevich, Dave A Wolk, Greg Zaharchuk, Sudipto Dolui, John A Detre, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONFluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) is widely used for detecting metabolic changes associated with neurodegeneration in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other dementias but is costly and involves ionizing radiation. Here we developed SynthPET, a multimodal deep learning framework that synthesizes FDG PET images from T1‐weighted (T1w) and arterial spin labeling (ASL) perfusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data.METHODSTwo conditional generative models were trained on paired MRI and FDG PET acquisitions: one using T1w alone (n = 1170) and one integrating T1w with ASL via cross‐modality transfer learning (n = 220). We evaluated synthetic FDG PET through voxel‐wise fidelity metrics, regional correlation and receiver operating characteristic analyses, blinded reader studies, and differential diagnosis across independent external cohorts.RESULTSBoth models achieved high fidelity with real FDG PET (structural similarity > 93%) and strong diagnostic performance in AD‐specific regions, with ASL providing modest improvements. Results generalized to an independent AD external cohort.DISCUSSIONSynthPET generates clinically plausible FDG PET from routinely acquired MRI, potentially expanding access to metabolic neuroimaging where PET is unavailable.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, arterial spin labeling, cerebral blood flow, cross‐modality synthesis, deep learning, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, generative adversarial network, neurodegeneration, synthetic positron emission tomography, T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging, transfer learning

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, characterized by cognitive and behavioral impairments that eventually disrupt daily activities. 1 , 2 A biological definition of the AD continuum uses the ATN framework, which consists of the presence of three core biomarkers: amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques (A), tau tangles (T), and neurodegeneration (N) 3 , 4 . Within this framework, [18F]‐fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) has been widely used to assess patterns of regional glucose metabolism that are associated with neurodegeneration 5 , 6 and to support the differential diagnosis of dementia by identifying disease‐specific patterns of hypometabolism across neurodegenerative disorders. 6 , 7 However, PET imaging is more costly and less available than other neuroimaging modalities, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries. 8 In addition, PET requires the administration of a radioactive tracer, which exposes patients to ionizing radiation and limits its suitability for repeated use. 9

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers a widely available, non‐invasive alternative for characterizing neurodegeneration. T1‐weighted (T1w) MRI enables detailed characterization of regional brain volume 10 , 11 , 12 and cortical thickness, both of which are established markers of neuronal loss 13 , 14 , 15 and hence neurodegeneration. 10 , 12 However, unlike FDG PET, structural MRI does not quantify metabolism and therefore is potentially less sensitive to early neurodegeneration, as metabolic changes detected by FDG PET can precede detectable atrophy on MRI. 16 Arterial spin labeling (ASL) perfusion MRI enables non‐invasive measurements of regional cerebral blood flow (CBF), a key biomarker of neurodegeneration that is closely coupled to glucose metabolism. 17 , 18 ASL can now be acquired as part of a routine MRI protocol and has shown good correspondence with FDG PET. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Nevertheless, FDG PET remains the reference standard for detecting early metabolic changes linked to neurodegeneration. 22

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence have enabled cross‐modality image synthesis, 23 , 24 , 25 wherein one imaging modality is generated from another. In neuroimaging, MRI‐to‐FDG PET synthesis has been explored using various deep learning approaches, demonstrating promising results in reproducing PET‐like patterns. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 However, most prior studies rely on unimodal inputs, typically T1w MRI, and given that FDG PET reflects metabolic activity, such structural MRI‐based models may not fully capture functional changes associated with neurodegeneration. Incorporating information from other MRI modalities that reflect physiological rather than structural changes, such as ASL, may improve the biological relevance of synthetic FDG PET by integrating complementary information on regional CBF.

We recently reported an approach that used ASL CBF maps and T1w MRI to predict FDG PET of temporal lobe epilepsy patients, 30 wherein we demonstrated comparable diagnostic performance of synthetic FDG PET compared to actual FDG PET in lateralizing interictal temporal lobe hypometabolism. That approach was subsequently extended to data from a small cohort of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients. 31 Prior research has also shown the benefits of using ASL data to synthesize FDG PET scans in patients with brain neoplasia. 32 Building on this prior work, in the present study we propose a multimodal framework for synthesizing FDG PET images from T1w MRI and ASL CBF maps in individuals along the AD continuum, using data acquired across multiple sites and scanner vendors. We hypothesized that combining these modalities may improve FDG PET synthesis, as T1w MRI and ASL capture complementary structural and perfusion‐related consequences of neurodegeneration, respectively. To address the scarcity of datasets containing both T1w MRI and ASL along with FDG PET, we developed two conditional 3D generative adversarial network (GAN) models: one using T1w MRI alone and another integrating both T1w MRI and ASL. We introduced a cross‐modality transfer learning strategy, in which a model pretrained on T1w MRI‐to‐PET synthesis is adapted to incorporate ASL information. We evaluated the contribution of each modality by comparing unimodal and multimodal approaches through global similarity metrics as well as regional and diagnostic analyses. We also assessed generalizability on an independent external cohort. Clinical feasibility was further examined through two pilot blinded reader studies, in which neuroradiologists evaluated synthetic FDG PET alongside real scans. Finally, we explored the potential of this approach for differential diagnosis across neurodegenerative disorders.

For this project, we used a multi‐site, multi‐vendor dataset (Figure 1A) comprising paired, single timepoint T1w MRI and FDG PET scans from a total of 1209 participants. The majority of data were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) study, including ADNI 2 (n = 893) and ADNI 3 (n = 262), with an additional 54 scans from the Penn Memory Center (PMC). 19 All T1w MRI and FDG PET scans were visually inspected for quality, and participants with suboptimal scans were excluded. Among the included participants, a subset of 259 participants additionally had ASL CBF maps of acceptable quality. ASL quality was determined using QEI‐Net, an automatic ASL quality evaluation tool that outputs a score with range [0,1], with higher values indicating better quality. 33 A quality evaluation index (QEI) threshold of 0.45 was empirically chosen to balance higher image quality with the preservation of a reasonable sample size. From this subset, ≈ 15% (n = 39) were reserved as a held‐out test set for model evaluation, along with n = 9 additional participants from the Consortium for Clarity in ADRD Research Through Imaging (CLARiTI) 34 study, yielding a total held‐out test set of n = 48 (Table 1). All held‐out test set participants had paired T1w MRI, ASL CBF maps, and FDG PET, and were entirely excluded from all training procedures. After the exclusion of these participants, two training datasets were defined (Table 1). Dataset1 (n = 1170) included all remaining participants with paired T1w MRI and FDG PET (ADNI: n = 1129; PMC: n = 41) and was used to train the unimodal model (modelT1w). Dataset2 (n = 220), a subset of Dataset1, consisted of those participants who additionally had ASL CBF maps (ADNI: n = 179; PMC: n = 41) of acceptable quality (QEI ≥ 0.45) and was used to train the multimodal model (modelT1w+ASL).

To further evaluate the generalizability of the proposed methods, we used an independent external cohort from the Penn Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (Penn ADRC; n = 221; Table 1). We additionally included a second independent external cohort from the Penn Frontotemporal Dementia Center (Penn FTD Center; n = 55; Table 1) for an exploratory analysis of whether the feature representations could distinguish AD dementia from dementia due to other neurodegenerative disorders (see section 2.8). For both cohorts, inclusion criteria required available T1w MRI, ASL CBF maps with acceptable image quality (QEI ≥ 0.45), and clinical diagnosis. For the Penn ADRC cohort, amyloid status was additionally required.

Across all datasets included in this study, amyloid status was determined from amyloid PET with either [18F]‐florbetapir or [18F]‐florbetaben using cerebellum‐normalized standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) and tracer‐specific thresholds (1.11 and 1.08, respectively) or through clinical reads. Because amyloid status was not required for model training, all participants of Dataset1 and Dataset2 with missing amyloid information were included to train our models. Participant demographics are summarized in Table 1, and additional details on image acquisition protocols are provided in the Supplementary Materials in supporting information.

Preprocessing

We used a standardized preprocessing pipeline for T1w MRI, ASL CBF, and FDG PET data. T1w MRI preprocessing included skull stripping using FreeSurfer SynthStrip, 37 , 38 bias‐field correction with N4ITK, 39 whole‐brain segmentation using FreeSurfer SynthSeg, 40 , 41 and registration to the MNI152 template at 2 mm isotropic resolution using ANTs, 42 with the resulting transforms propagated to all other modalities. FDG PET images were co‐registered to the corresponding T1w MRI using ANTs, harmonized to an 8 mm isotropic full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) resolution using scanner‐specific 3D Gaussian kernels, and converted to SUVR images by normalizing intensities to the cerebellar cortex. ASL CBF maps were likewise co‐registered to T1w MRI using ANTs, and relative CBF (rCBF) maps were generated by normalizing CBF values calculated using a standard single‐compartment kinetic model 43 to the bilateral putamen as the reference region. Prior to network input, all volumes were zero padded to a uniform size of 128 × 128 × 128. Moreover, the intensity values of the T1w volumes were normalized using percentile‐based min–max scaling, for which the 2nd and 98th percentiles (excluding background voxels) were used as the lower and upper bounds, respectively. To preserve perfusion and metabolic information while scaling intensities to the [0, 1] range, rCBF maps were clipped to [0, 2.5], and both the rCBF and SUVR images were then divided by 2.5.

For regional analyses, we applied FreeSurfer's recon‐all pipeline 44 , 45 to T1w MRI from the held‐out test set to obtain cortical and subcortical parcellations based on the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) atlas. 46 These parcellations were used to extract standardized regions of interest (ROIs). In addition to the DKT‐defined regions, we included a composite ROI (hereafter named Meta ROI), constructed based on regional FDG PET sensitivity to cognitive decline in AD and MCI. 47

Network architecture

Figure 1B shows the architecture of the proposed conditional 3D GAN approach. The input of the network consists of the T1w image, either alone or in combination with the ASL rCBF map. Each modality is passed through its own encoder to extract meaningful features from each image. Because our dataset only contains ASL volumes for a limited number of subjects, we trained both encoders under different settings. We first trained a modelT1w using Dataset1 to predict the FDG PET SUVR volume from T1w scans. We refer to this output as FDG PET sSUVR (T1w), for which sSUVR denotes synthetic SUVR. Then, using Dataset2, we trained ModelT1w+ASL, which incorporates ASL to predict the FDG PET SUVR volume from both modalities. We refer to this output as FDG PET sSUVR (T1w+ASL). To implement ModelT1w+ASL, we added a new encoder to extract features from ASL rCBF maps and we transferred the weights of the T1w encoder from ModelT1w to exploit feature representations learned on the larger T1w MRI‐only cohort and mitigate ASL‐data scarcity. During ModelT1w+ASL training, the T1w encoder was kept frozen throughout training. Additionally, the discriminator weights from ModelT1w were also transferred to ModelT1w+ASL. Figure 1B (right) shows the workflow of the proposed transfer learning strategy.

Network implementation

The proposed network, implemented in the PyTorch framework, consists of four main components: dual encoders, a bottleneck, a shared decoder, and a discriminator. The dual encoders independently extract modality‐specific features, which are fused in the bottleneck. Each encoder is composed of three hierarchical blocks, each containing 3D convolutional layers (kernel size 3 × 3 × 3), instance normalization, sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activations, and residual connections. In the bottleneck, self‐attention modules are embedded within the residual blocks. These are implemented using the memory‐efficient attention mechanism from the xformers library. 48 Finally, the decoder reconstructs the output by mirroring the encoder's hierarchical structure, using up‐sampling blocks and skip connections to integrate features from both encoders.

All training and evaluation of this study were conducted on one NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40GB of memory.

Hyperparameter tuning

To conduct hyperparameter tuning and evaluate model performance, we used a stratified 5‐fold cross‐validation procedure based on diagnostic categories (cognitively unimpaired [CU], MCI, AD), ensuring each fold maintained a proportionate distribution of clinical conditions. Due to the large sample size and high computational demands, tuning was initially conducted on a representative subset (one single fold) before being refined across all five folds. Based on cross‐validation results, we identified the optimal hyperparameters for both ModelT1w and ModelT1w+ASL. The final models were retrained on the full training dataset and evaluated on the held‐out test set. ModelT1w was trained for 100 epochs using a customized loss function (defined in the next section), batch size of six, and the Adam optimizer with learning rates of 1e−4 for the generator and 1e−5 for the discriminator. A Reduce‐on‐Plateau scheduler was used to enable finer learning rate adjustments during training. ModelT1w+ASL was trained with the same settings, except for a reduced batch size of four. For both models, data augmentation strategies, including random flips and rotations of ± 5°, were applied to improve generalizability.

Pilot reader studies

As a preliminary assessment of clinical feasibility, we piloted two small, blinded reader studies with two physicians dual board‐certified in nuclear medicine and neuroradiology. In both, to avoid bias from prior viewing of data from the same participant, readers were blinded to participant identity and modality name, including whether an FDG PET scan was synthetic or real, and all scans were presented one at a time in randomized order. Diagnostic accuracy, confidence, and inter‐rater agreement, the latter quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient 51 (ICC), were evaluated.

In the first study, each physician independently rated 20 randomly selected participants from the held out test set (8 CU, 12 MCI/AD), chosen solely based on cognitive status, across five modalities: T1w MRI, ASL rCBF map, FDG PET sSUVR (T1w), FDG PET sSUVR (T1w+ASL), and real FDG PET SUVR, on a scale from −3 to +3 (−3: definitely patient; +3: definitely control).

In the second study, informed by the results and physician feedback from the first, participant selection was refined. To better capture the AD biological spectrum, participants were restricted to 10 CU Aβ− and 10 MCI/AD Aβ+, with the 10 highest‐quality ASL scans selected per group from the held‐out test set to minimize artifact‐related noise. Moreover, to simulate a clinically realistic scenario in which PET imaging is unavailable, each physician reviewed cases under two conditions: (1) T1w MRI + ASL rCBF + age, and (2) T1w MRI + ASL rCBF + age + synthetic FDG PET sSUVR (T1w+ASL), allowing direct assessment of the diagnostic added value of the synthetic PET. Ratings were given on a broader scale from −5 to +5 (−5: definitely impaired; +5: definitely control).

Exploratory analysis: differentiating AD dementia, non‐AD dementia, and healthy controls

To explore the ability of the proposed approach to differentiate among dementia subtypes, we analyzed the latent representations learned by the SynthPET encoders. Bottleneck features were extracted separately from the T1w MRI and ASL encoder branches, as well as their combination. These features were standardized, and dimensionality reduced to 30 principal components (PCs) 52 before being projected into two dimensions using t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE). 53 The stability of the t‐SNE embeddings was assessed across five random seeds and five perplexity values (5, 15, 30, 45, 50), quantified as the trustworthiness of each embedding relative to the 30 PCs, 54 which captures preservation of local neighborhood structure under dimensionality reduction. The analysis included healthy controls (CU Aβ−; n = 99) and dementia due to AD (n = 46) participants from the Penn ADRC cohort, together with all participants from the Penn FTD Center cohort (Table 1, behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia: n = 12; primary progressive aphasia: n = 25; corticobasal syndrome: n = 10; progressive supranuclear palsy: n = 8). For this analysis, participants were grouped into three categories: healthy controls, dementia due to AD, and non‐AD dementia. We additionally trained a basic random forest classifier on the same 30 principal components to distinguish among these three categories, with performance assessed using a leave‐one‐out cross‐validation strategy and balanced accuracy as the main metric.

Model evaluation and statistical analysis

To evaluate the proposed methods, we performed two complementary sets of analyses. First, we computed quantitative image quality metrics to assess the similarity between synthetic and original FDG PET SUVR images, including the SSIM, the normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), 55 and the peak signal‐to‐noise ratio (PSNR), 56 all computed within the brain mask (excluding background voxels).

Second, we performed statistical analyses to disentangle the contributions of T1w MRI and ASL to the synthetic FDG PET signal across clinically relevant brain regions. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.3 (scipy.stats, scikit‐learn, and statsmodels libraries) and R version 4.5.1. For all hypothesis testing in this study, statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05). Analyses were focused on three a priori canonical AD‐associated regions: an FDG PET–based Meta ROI, a combined ROI of the bilateral posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus (PCC+Precuneus) reflecting the region where ASL MRI shows the greatest sensitivity to AD, 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 and bilateral hippocampus. Regional correlations between different ROIs were assessed using Spearman correlation, and the two‐sided Steiger test was applied to compare the strength of correlations sharing a common variable.

To explore discrimination power between healthy controls (CU Aβ−) and patients (MCI/AD Aβ+) in the held‐out test set, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted using the mean value within each ROI as the discriminating feature. For each ROI, separate ROC curves were generated for ASL rCBF, synthetic FDG PET from modelT1w, synthetic FDG PET from modelT1w+ASL, and real FDG PET SUVR. Area under the curve (AUC) values were then derived for each ROC curve. To assess statistical reliability, 95% confidence intervals for AUC values were computed using bootstrap resampling (n = 10,000 bootstrap samples) with a fixed random seed for reproducibility, and pairwise comparisons between modalities were performed using a DeLong test for correlated ROC curves. The same analyses were performed on an external test cohort (Penn ADRC) to evaluate the generalizability of the presented methods. Within this external cohort, group‐wise comparisons across diagnostic categories were additionally performed using a linear mixed model (LMM), with mean regional values as the dependent variable. Diagnostic group, modality, and brain region served as fixed effects, with sex and age as covariates, and subject as the random intercept. We also included a three‐way interaction among group, modality, and region (Mean_Value ∼ Study_Group × Modality × Region + Sex + Age). Post hoc pairwise contrasts between diagnostic groups were computed within each modality × region cell using estimated marginal means, and interaction contrasts (difference‐of‐differences) were computed within each region to test whether one modality separated two diagnostic groups more strongly than another.

Finally, to assess the ability of the encoder latent space to separate diagnostic groups in the differential diagnosis setting (see section 2.8), cluster separation was quantified on the 30 PCs embeddings using PERMANOVA (10,000 permutations, Euclidean distance), with post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted for all group pairs.

RESULTS

Fidelity of FDG PET sSUVR images

We evaluated the voxel‐wise fidelity of the FDG PET sSUVR images by comparing them to ground‐truth PET scans from the held‐out test set (n = 48). Performance was assessed separately for CU (n = 17) and MCI/AD (n = 31) using NRMSE, PSNR, and SSIM. ROI‐based analyses

To evaluate the biological plausibility of the synthetic FDG PET SUVR maps, we conducted ROI‐based analyses using the Meta ROI and 34 bilateral regions defined by the DKT atlas, comprising the 31 bilateral cortical regions together with three subcortical regions with established relevance in AD: the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus. 61

Regional correlations with ground‐truth SUVR

We first examined on the held‐out test set whether regional perfusion measures obtained from ASL rCBF maps corresponded to glucose metabolism measured by ground‐truth FDG PET SUVR (Figure 3A). Across 34 bilateral ROIs, 13 showed significant correlations (Table S1 in supporting information). In AD‐relevant regions, the hippocampus did not show a significant association (r = 0.24, p = 0.187), whereas significant correlations were observed in the PCC+Precuneus (r = 0.49, p = 0.003) and the Meta ROI (r = 0.63, p < 0.001). We then investigated the regional correspondence between synthetic and real FDG PET SUVR values. Synthetic FDG PET SUVR maps derived from ModelT1w significantly correlated with ground‐truth values in 33 out of 34 bilateral regions (Figure 3B; Table S1), with moderate correlations in the hippocampus (r = 0.43, p = 0.003) and stronger correlations in the PCC+Precuneus (r = 0.58, p < 0.001) and the Meta ROI (r = 0.65, p < 0.001). Synthetic FDG PET SUVR maps produced by ModelT1w+ASL exhibited similar performance, with significant correlations in all 34 regions (Figure 3C, Table S1. Although correlations for ModelT1w+ASL were higher than for ModelT1w in the hippocampus (r = 0.44, p = 0.002), PCC+Precuneus (r = 0.64, p < 0.001), and the Meta ROI (r = 0.70, p < 0.001), these differences were not statistically significant.

Diagnostic accuracy analysis

We next used ROC analyses to assess the diagnostic utility of the synthetic FDG PET images on the held‐out test set. Regional evaluations comparing healthy controls (CU Aβ−; n = 15) to patient (MCI and AD Aβ+; n = 29) groups revealed that synthetic FDG PET maps consistently outperformed both ASL rCBF maps and ground truth FDG PET, achieving higher AUC values in all regions (Figure 4A, Table S2 in supporting information). Within the hippocampus, ModelT1w reached an AUC of 0.92 (0.83–0.98) and ModelT1w+ASL reached 0.87 (0.75–0.96), while ASL rCBF achieved 0.60 (0.40–0.80) and ground‐truth FDG PET SUVR achieved 0.67 (0.51–0.82; Figure 4B, Table S2). In the PCC+Precuneus, ModelT1w achieved AUC of 0.83 (0.69–0.95), and ModelT1w+ASL achieved 0.83 (0.68–0.94), outperforming ASL rCBF at 0.68 (0.51–0.84), and ground‐truth FDG PET at 0.71 (0.53–0.86; Figure 4C, Table S2). Finally, in the Meta ROI, ModelT1w achieved AUC of 0.87 (0.75–0.96), ModelT1w+ASL at 0.90 (0.79–0.98), ASL rCBF at 0.77 (0.62–0.90), and ground‐truth FDG PET at 0.76 (0.60–0.90; Figure 4D, Table S2). Although synthetic FDG PET maps consistently achieved higher AUC values across these regions, most pairwise comparisons based on the DeLong test (Table S3 in supporting information) did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction. Only two comparisons remained significant (p = 0.049 and p = 0.049 for ASL rCBF vs. FDG PET sSUVR [T1w] and FDG PET sSUVR [T1w] vs. FDG PET SUVR, respectively), likely due to the limited statistical power in this sample.

Diagnostic accuracy analysis on an external test set

On the Penn ADRC external cohort, we compared ASL rCBF and FDG PET sSUVR generated from ModelT1w and ModelT1w+ASL within AD‐specific ROIs (hippocampus, PCC+Precuneus, and Meta ROI) across four groups: CU Aβ– (n = 99), CU Aβ+ (n = 25), MCI Aβ+ (n = 34), and AD Aβ+ (n = 46). Group differences were evaluated using post hoc pairwise contrasts derived from the LMM, and interaction contrasts tested whether one modality separated a given group pair more strongly than another (see section 2.9). Across all regions, FDG PET sSUVR showed the expected monotonic decline from CU Aβ– to AD Aβ+, whereas ASL rCBF showed a broadly similar but less pronounced pattern. Within the Meta ROI (Figure 5A), all three modalities performed comparably overall. In post hoc testing (Table S4 and S5 in supporting information), the synthetic FDG PET modalities separated the late‐disease comparison (MCI Aβ+ vs. AD Aβ+) significantly better than ASL rCBF (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 for ASL rCBF vs. FDG PET sSUVR [T1w] and ASL rCBF vs. FDG PET sSUVR [T1w+ASL], respectively), whereas ASL rCBF tended to separate early‐disease comparisons (CU Aβ− vs. MCI Aβ+) more strongly than the synthetic FDG PET modalities, though these differences did not survive FDR correction in this region (p = 0.05 and p = 0.06 for ASL rCBF vs. FDG PET sSUVR [T1w] and ASL rCBF vs. FDG PET sSUVR [T1w+ASL], respectively). The two synthetic FDG PET modalities did not differ significantly from each other for any group comparison. A similar pattern of better early‐disease discrimination with ASL rCBF and late‐disease discrimination with synthetic PET was also observed in the hippocampus and PCC+Precuneus (Table S4 and S5; Figures S1 and S2 in supporting information), with ASL showing highly significant modality effects for early disease in the PCC+Precuneus (p < 0.0001 for ASL rCBF vs. FDG‐PET sSUVR [T1w] and FDG PET sSUVR [T1w+ASL]). These results suggest that perfusion may be more sensitive to earlier disease changes, while synthetic FDG PET better captures later stage neurodegeneration.

Pilot reader studies

In the first reader study (Tables S7 and S8 in supporting information), diagnostic accuracy varied across readers and modalities, ranging from 55.0% to 85.0%. No single modality achieved uniformly high performance across both readers. Notably, real FDG PET itself showed limited specificity (25.0% for both readers), underscoring the difficulty of the classification task. Reader confidence, measured as the mean absolute score relative to the rating scale (± 3), varied across modalities, with one reader consistently showing lower confidence (mean |score| range: 1.15–2.15) than the other (range: 1.55–2.70). Inter‐rater agreement was generally low across all modalities, with synthetic FDG PET from ModelT1w and ModelT1w+ASL showing ICCs of 0.43 (p = 0.025) and 0.35 (p = 0.063), respectively, compared to real FDG PET SUVR (ICC = 0.29, p = 0.103).

In the second reader study (Tables S9 and S10 in supporting information), the addition of synthetic FDG PET to T1w MRI, ASL rCBF, and age had variable effects on individual reader accuracy, improving performance for one reader (50% to 60%) while slightly decreasing it for the other (80% to 70%). Reader confidence, now measured on a broader ± 5 scale, was moderate to high across both conditions, with one reader consistently showing lower confidence (mean |score| range: 2.95–3.45) than the other (range: 3.55–3.95). Inter‐rater agreement improved notably from ICC = 0.47 (p = 0.015) without synthetic PET to ICC = 0.75 (p < 0.001) with it, suggesting that the synthetic images provided a more standardized basis for interpretation. However, this improvement did not reach statistical significance (Steiger test), likely due to the limited sample size of this pilot study.

Dementia type discrimination

To explore the capability of the presented approach for differential diagnosis, we examined the latent representations of the SynthPET encoders across dementia subtypes. The t‐SNE plots revealed that combined ASL+T1w features (Figure 5E) achieved the clearest separation among diagnostic groups, with healthy controls, dementia due to AD, and non‐AD dementia forming distinct clusters. ASL features (Figure 5F) showed good separation between dementia due to AD and the remaining groups, but poorer separability between healthy controls and non‐AD dementia. T1w features alone (Figure 5G) showed good separation between healthy controls and non‐AD dementia but poorer discrimination of dementia due to AD. Trustworthiness was high and stable across all seeds and perplexity values for each of the three encoder configurations (Figure S3 in supporting information), indicating that the observed group structure was not an artifact of a single random seed or perplexity setting (Figure S3). PERMANOVA computed on the 30 PCs (Table S11 in supporting information) confirmed significant differences between all supergroup pairs across all feature spaces (p < 0.001). Random forest classification further showed that the combined and ASL‐derived features effectively distinguish the three groups (balanced accuracy 0.86 and 0.85, respectively), whereas the T1w‐only features are considerably weaker (balanced accuracy 0.66), suggesting that perfusion‐derived features carry substantial signal for distinguishing dementia subtypes (Figure S4 in supporting information). These exploratory results suggest that, although trained exclusively on AD‐spectrum data, the features encoded by SynthPET retain information that distinguishes the different groups, supporting future studies of this approach for differential diagnosis of neurodegenerative disorders.

Ablation studies

A series of ablation experiments were conducted using Dataset2 (n = 220) to disentangle the contributions of input modality, ASL image quality, training sample size, and transfer learning to FDG PET SUVR synthesis. All models used the same architecture and hyperparameters to ensure comparability. We evaluated seven configurations including a T1w‐only model (n = 220), two ASL‐only models trained with QEI thresholds > 0.45 (n = 220) and > 0.5 (n = 154), and four multimodal T1w+ASL models trained with QEI thresholds > 0.45 (n = 220) or > 0.5 (n = 154), either with transfer learning from the T1w‐only model or trained from scratch. As in section 3.1, quantitative performance was assessed using NRMSE, PSNR, and SSIM metrics across CU and MCI/AD groups.

Table 3 summarizes the ablation results. The multimodal T1w+ASL model with transfer learning achieved the best performance across all metrics for CU and MCI/AD participants. However, its gains over the corresponding model trained without transfer learning were modest, likely reflecting the limited size of the source dataset used for initialization, which may constrain the observable benefit of transfer learning. Applying a stricter ASL quality threshold (QEI > 0.5), with or without transfer learning, did not improve performance and in fact tended to degrade results, possibly related to the reduced effective training sample size. In single‐modality experiments, T1w MRI alone produced strong synthetic images and outperformed ASL alone, indicating that ASL is insufficient by itself, likely due to greater variability in image quality and susceptibility to gross artifacts. Consistent with this interpretation, ASL‐only performance further declined when including only QEI > 0.5 cases, again reflecting the impact of reduced data sample size in that condition.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed and validated a new multimodal 3D GAN framework for synthesizing FDG PET images from T1w MRI and ASL rCBF maps in participants across the AD continuum. Good quality synthetic PET (SynthPET) scans were generated under both unimodal (T1w MRI) and multimodal (T1w MRI + ASL) input configurations, achieving high voxel‐wise similarity and strong regional correlation with real FDG PET in AD‐specific ROIs. Our findings indicate that most of the information required for FDG PET synthesis can be derived from neurodegeneration features encoded in T1w MRI, including regional atrophy patterns 10 , 11 , 12 and cortical thinning. 13 , 14 , 15 These structural markers are well‐established indicators of neurodegeneration and show strong correspondence with the hypometabolic patterns observed in FDG PET imaging 62 , 63 (Figures S5 and S6 and Table S12 in supporting information). Although perfusion information from ASL was incorporated in the multimodal framework, its added value was limited, as improvements over the unimodal T1w‐only model were modest and often did not reach statistical significance. These results probably do not reflect a lack of physiologically relevant information in ASL, as tight coupling between regional perfusion and glucose metabolism has been repeatedly demonstrated. 17 , 18 , 21 Rather, it likely arises from the higher variability of ASL image quality, including its lower signal‐to‐noise ratio, resolution, and susceptibility to artifacts. This challenge is further accentuated by the limited sample size of matched ASL and FDG PET data, much of which originated from early ADNI acquisitions consisting of pulsed ASL (PASL) without background suppression. Together, these factors make ASL a particularly challenging input for network training. However, its contribution is likely to increase as higher quality data become more widely available.

The role of structural MRI in FDG PET synthesis is overestimated in global metrics because FDG PET reflects metabolism superimposed upon structure throughout the brain, including partial volume effects in regions of atrophy due to neurodegeneration, whereas diagnostic value relies on regional patterns. Regional analysis therefore provides further insight into the biological plausibility and modality‐specific strengths of the proposed approaches. Both models demonstrated strong correlations with ground‐truth FDG PET across canonical AD‐affected regions, with particularly high correlations in the PCC+Precuneus and Meta ROI, regions known to exhibit metabolic changes in the AD continuum. 47 , 64 We also observed complementary regional strengths aligned with the input modality used for synthesis. ModelT1w performed best in the hippocampus, likely reflecting its reliance on structural atrophy patterns visible on T1w MRI, whereas ModelT1w+ASL achieved superior performance in the FDG PET–defined Meta ROI, where ASL‐derived perfusion likely contributes complementary information correlated with metabolism. These results are consistent with prior work 65 , 66 and reflect the regional heterogeneity of AD, in which structural and functional alterations emerge with different spatial and temporal patterns. Extending these findings to diagnostic performance, ROC analyses showed that synthetic PET maps consistently achieved higher AUC values than both ASL and ground‐truth FDG PET for discriminating healthy controls from patients across the hippocampus, PCC+Precuneus, and Meta ROI. Although pairwise comparisons did not remain significant after FDR correction, synthetic PET consistently yielded higher diagnostic AUCs than real FDG PET across different regions in this sample. This probably does not reflect metabolic superiority of synthetic FDG PET versus real FDG PET per se, particularly as the generative models were trained to reproduce real FDG PET and are thus not expected to exceed real FDG PET in true metabolic content. Rather, the synthetic FDG PET images are strongly driven by atrophy features encoded in T1w MRI, to which real FDG PET is itself only partially sensitive through partial volume effects. Accordingly, this pattern is most apparent in atrophy‐prone regions such as the hippocampus (section 3.2.2). In addition, the denoising and regularization inherent to generative synthesis may reduce acquisition noise present in single real FDG PET scans, which could increase group separability, particularly in a small test set in which the real FDG PET estimates are noisy. Similar region‐ and modality‐specific patterns were observed in the external Penn ADRC cohort, which differed from the training data in acquisition protocols, scanner hardware, and participant demographics. Despite this heterogeneity, synthetic FDG PET preserved good discriminative performance in the same ROIs, mirroring the internal test set findings and providing initial evidence of robustness within the AD spectrum, though further validation across a wider range of independent sites, acquisition protocols, and clinical populations will be required to establish broader generalizability.

The ablation studies provided important insights into the impact of our methodological choices, particularly the transfer learning strategy, which was designed to address a fundamental challenge in medical image synthesis: the limited availability of paired multimodal data. Models leveraging transfer learning consistently outperformed those trained from scratch, supporting its efficacy under data scarcity. However, the observed gains were modest, likely reflecting the substantial differences between T1w MRI and ASL, as T1w MRI primarily captures structural atrophy whereas ASL encodes more complex and variable perfusion patterns. The scale of pretraining may also play a role, as our approach relied on just over one thousand subjects, compared to the millions of images typically used in ImageNet‐based approaches, 67 which can constrain the learned representations and their ability to generalize across modalities such as ASL. We also examined the trade‐off between ASL image quality and training sample size by restricting analyses to higher quality ASL data. The failure to improve model performance with a smaller but higher quality dataset reflects a common challenge in deep learning, highlighting the critical role of sample size in achieving robust performance. Finally, the ablation experiments confirmed that ASL alone is insufficient for FDG PET synthesis, as ASL‐only models consistently yielded the poorest performance, likely reflecting the ASL image quality challenges described above.

The pilot reader studies revealed modest and variable diagnostic accuracy across readers and modalities. The addition of synthetic FDG PET notably improved inter‐rater agreement versus interpretation of T1w+ASL images alone, suggesting that synthetic images may provide a more standardized basis for clinical interpretation, as diagnostic consistency is a critical attribute for any imaging biomarker. Of note, real FDG PET itself showed limited specificity and poor inter‐rater agreement in this setting, underscoring the inherent difficulty of the diagnostic task under these conditions. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility rather than clinical readiness, given the small sample sizes (n = 20 per study) and limited number of readers (n = 2). Larger, adequately powered reader studies are needed to determine whether synthetic FDG PET can meaningfully improve diagnostic accuracy and consistency in clinical practice.

In addition to AD classification, the exploratory analysis for differentiating AD dementia, non‐AD dementia, and healthy controls demonstrated that the latent representations learned by the SynthPET encoders captured meaningful distinctions between AD and non‐AD dementias, despite being trained exclusively on AD‐spectrum data. This suggests that the multimodal framework encodes disease‐relevant features that generalize beyond its training scope. These findings are preliminary, however, as the model was not trained on non‐AD dementia data, and validation in larger and more diverse cohorts will be needed before any differential‐diagnostic utility can be established.

Limitations and future directions

Some limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our results and guiding future directions. First, although the multimodal approach yielded consistent improvements over the unimodal model, these gains were modest, suggesting that the model may not fully capture the complex relationship between cerebral perfusion as quantified by ASL MRI and glucose metabolism as quantified by FDG PET. This is likely driven by the variable and suboptimal quality of ASL data in our training datasets, as well as by the relatively small number of subjects with paired multimodal imaging. Assembling a dataset with complete multimodal imaging was one of the major limitations of this study, and this limitation was further compounded when high‐quality ASL data are required. Future work should therefore focus on leveraging emerging multimodal datasets with state‐of‐the‐art ASL sequences, such as those from the CLARiTI study and newer ADNI phases, to expand training data and improve model performance. Second, the proposed framework was trained and evaluated exclusively in AD datasets, and its generalizability to other neurodegenerative disorders with distinct metabolic and perfusion patterns remains unknown. This limitation is particularly relevant for conditions with known mismatches between metabolic dysfunction and structural neurodegeneration such as Lewy body disease, 68 because the synthesized FDG PET images are largely driven by atrophy features encoded in T1w MRI. Future work should evaluate SynthPET across diverse clinical populations, and assess whether recent FDG PET–based differential diagnosis approaches 69 can be successfully applied to MRI‐based synthetic FDG PET images. Third, FDG PET measurements were not explicitly corrected for partial volume effects, which are known to influence signal estimates in regions with significant atrophy, such as the hippocampus. Because partial volume effects can make ground‐truth FDG PET signal partially dependent on underlying structural atrophy, some of the observed correspondence between synthetic and real FDG PET, as well as the higher discriminative performance of synthetic FDG PET in atrophy‐prone regions, may reflect shared sensitivity to atrophy rather than purely metabolic changes. Future studies incorporating partial volume correction may help disentangle these effects. Fourth, although MRI‐based synthetic FDG PET potentially provides the opportunity to longitudinally track disease progression without repeated cost and exposure to ionizing radiation of FDG PET, it has not been validated for that task and therefore, its performance remains unknown. Fifth, the blinded reader studies were pilot assessments with only two readers and small sample sizes (n = 20 per study). The variable effects on individual reader accuracy and the lack of statistical significance in the ICC comparison underscore the need for larger, adequately powered studies with more readers and diverse clinical expertise. Future reader studies should therefore include larger, more diverse cohorts and expert panels to robustly establish the clinical utility of synthetic FDG PET.

Beyond methodological considerations, synthetic PET approaches such as the one proposed here have the potential to improve access to PET‐like image contrast using less costly and more widely available imaging infrastructure. By leveraging routinely acquired MRI sequences, such as T1w MRI and ASL, the proposed approach could begin to be evaluated for screening studies in patients with cognitive complaints and to extend access to FDG PET–like diagnostic capabilities in locations that lack the infrastructure to support a PET scanner.
