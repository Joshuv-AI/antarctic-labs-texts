Effects of chicken-liver-hydrolysate paste addition on structure-function relationships in emulsion-type sausages☆☆☆
Authors: Yu-Jui Chen, Jr-Wei Chen, Yi-Chen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the possible application on intergradation of chicken-liver-hydrolysate pastes (CLHPs) with meat products, which could enrich the utilization of CLHPs to meet the agrocycle principle. CLHPs rich in polyunsaturated and ω-3 fatty-acid contents, were evaluated as sustainable raw-meat replacers in emulsion-type chicken sausages. Chicken breast was partially substituted with 0∼20% CLHPs. Increasing CLHP incorporation reduced viscoelasticity in raw meat batter as indicated by a decrease in storage modulus (G′), and altered water distribution in cooked meat batters, with LF-NMR T₂ relaxometry showing increased bound water and decreased free water. In cooked sausages, excessive CLHP addition (20%) destabilized the emulsion system, resulting in reduced cooking yield and water-holding capacity, together with elevated lipid oxidation (P < 0.05). Textural attributes were concurrently weakened at high substitution levels. CLHP inclusion altered color characteristics by decreasing lightness and increasing redness. Microstructural observations confirmed structural disruption and oil droplet coalescence at high inclusion levels. Overall, CLHP substitution should be limited to 15% to preserve emulsion stability and product quality. This research demonstrated that CLHPs are a novel value-added ingredient derived from broiler by-products. Their appropriate incorporation improved physicochemical properties in emulsified meat systems and provides practical guidance for partial meat replacement. These findings support the development of sustainable meat processing strategies and contribute to the valorization of poultry by-products within a circular food economy.Keywords: Chicken-liver-hydrolysate paste, Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance, Texture profile analysis, Colour

Introduction

Animal byproducts are non-primary materials from livestock production. Among them, slaughter residues including viscera, bone, hair, blood, and other inedible fractions attract the greatest concern due to their large volume, limited utilization, and potential environmental burden. Woodgate (2023) summarized that there are 40, 45, 30, and 32% for animal byproducts not intended for human consumption in cattle, sheep, pig, and poultry. Consequently, developing effective strategies to utilize these materials has become an urgent priority for advancing agricultural sustainability across governmental, industrial, and scientific sectors. In Taiwan, the total slaughter volume of broilers in 2024 researched approximately 435,000 metric tons where livers were approximately 11,000 metric tons weight (Ministry of Agriculture, 2025). Lu et al. (2021a & b) reported that chicken-liver protein could exhibit promising technological and nutritional properties as a functional ingredient in emulsified meat systems. The partial replacement of pork back fat with succinylated chicken-liver protein and pre-emulsified sunflower oil in emulsified sausages improved lipid utilization and contributed to favorable physicochemical characteristics. These findings provided a foundation for understanding the emulsifying potential of native and modified chicken-liver proteins prior to enzymatic hydrolysis. Meanwhile, due to concerns about odor and health risk, consumer preference for chicken livers as table food has sharply declined. To address this, our team aims to align with governmental agricultural-recycling policies for efficient utilization of chicken livers. For example, the chicken-liver hydrolysates (CLHs) with multiple bio-functionalities, i.e. antioxidant, lipid-lowering, hepatoprotective, and glycemic-control effects have been developed, proven, and patented (Chen et al., 2017, 2018; Wu et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2025). Moreover, the remaining material obtained from the CLHs was referred to as chicken-liver hydrolysate pastes (CLHPs), which accounts for approximately 50% of the total raw material weight. Regarding the edible animal by-products, the meat processing industry has increasingly sought to transform them into valuable raw materials or novel products, thereby improving nutritional value and product textural properties (Lima et al., 2021; Toldrá et al., 2021). Therefore, an incorporation of these edible CLHPs into processed foods represents a possible strategy for the sustainable and efficient utilization of broiler livers.

Meat products are foods derived from animal edible tissues that have been processed through methods such as cooking, salting, smoking, emulsification, or the addition of seasonings and spices. As we know, the purpose of processing not only enhances the flavor and texture of meat products but also extends the shelf life. Among these processing techniques, emulsification is a fundamental process in meat products, where salt-soluble myofibrillar proteins act as natural surfactants to reduce interfacial tension and stabilize the dispersion of fat and water into a uniform emulsion (Zhang et al., 2025a). Among such products, sausages represent one of the most widely produced and consumed categories worldwide, largely due to their convenience, affordability, and versatility (Horita et al., 2014). As typical oil-in-water systems, emulsion-type sausages are manufactured from minced or chopped meat mixed with water, seasonings, and functional additives such as phosphates or maize starch, which contribute to improved stability, texture, and water holding capacity (WHC) (Molina et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2025) indicated that the physicochemical and structural characteristics of meat products are critical determinants of their overall texture quality, sensory perception, and consumer acceptance. Meat rheological behavior reflects protein functionality in gelation and textural development, where cross-linked aggregates form a well-spanned gel network that maintains product integrity and controls water retention (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, water distribution is closely associated to cooking loss and WHC and can be effectively assayed by using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) which differentiates bound, immobilized, and free water (Han et al., 2019). Structural properties also affect mechanical properties, such as texture profile analysis and microstructure, both of which directly influence consumer satisfaction and marketability (Chen et al., 2025). Therefore, an optimization of structural and textural properties is essential for producing high-quality, consistent, and appealing meat products.

Chicken liver has attracted an increasing attention as a sustainable protein source due to its nutritional value and functional properties; however, limited studies have explored the application of CLHPs as partial meat replacers in emulsified meat products. In addition, excessive hydrolysis may weaken protein-protein interactions and destabilize the emulsion matrix, potentially resulting in inferior physicochemical and textural properties. Based on the current literature, we hypothesized that the incorporation of CLHPs into emulsion-type chicken sausages would modify protein-lipid-water interactions within the meat matrix, thereby affecting the rheological behavior, water distribution, microstructure, and physicochemical properties of the final products. This study aimed to evaluate the potential application of CLHPs for use in emulsion-type chicken sausages. The fatty-acid profile of CLHPs was first characterized, followed by an assessment of physicochemical properties including rheological properties and water distribution in chicken-sausage batters as well as WHC, texture profile analysis, lipid oxidation, colour attributes, and microstructure of cooked chicken sausages. In addition, the optimal incorporation level of CLHPs was also determined with the dual objectives of maximizing the utilization of broiler livers and following the regulation of agricultural-sustainability principle.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The manufacture of the chicken-liver hydrolysates (CLHs) was based on description of a patent from Chen et al. (2018). Briefly, frozen chicken livers were mixed with distilled water, homogenized, and heated at 95°C for 10 min to inactivate endogenous enzymes. Pepsin (Sigma Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) was applied for hydrolyzation. The reaction was terminated by heating at 95°C for 15 min, followed by centrifugation (Model 7000, Kubota Co., Tokyo, Japan). The supernatant was collected for functional CLH production, whereas the remaining material obtained from the CLHs was referred to as chicken-liver hydrolysate pastes (CLHPs). The emulsion-type chicken sausages with different levels of CLHPs were then prepared following the procedures referenced by Lima et al. (2021) with slight modifications. Broiler breast and pork back fat were purchased from the local market (Nanmen Market, Taipei City, Taiwan). Five formulations of chicken sausages were processed (Table 1) in 3 different batches. The broiler breast and pork back were ground using a meat grinder (TSA-200, Shengyuan, Taichung, Taiwan) equipped with a 3 mm plate before sequent processing. After grinding and weighing breasts, fat, and additives were transferred to a mixer machine (PY-790, Felsted, China). Then the meat batter was cured under storage in a refrigerator (SY-70, Glory Foods Processing Machinery Ltd, Taiwan) at 4°C for approximately 20 min. The cured meat batter was vacuum-packed and stored at 4°C for further analyses. Part of the cured meat batter was stuffed and filled the sausage with cellulose casing approximately 22 mm in diameter; using sausage filler (90512000-A, Dick Corp, Farmingdale, USA). The emulsion-type chicken sausages were dried at 40°C for 30 min and then cooked in the water bath at 85°C until its internal temperature reaches 72°C. Finally, the sausages were chilled in iced water for 15 sec, vacuum-packed, and stored at −20°C for further analyses.

Fatty-acid profile in CLHPs and emulsion-type chicken sausages

The major fatty-acid profile in CLHPs and emulsion-type chicken sausage, i.e., saturated fatty acids (SFAs), unsaturated fatty acids (USFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), was analyzed at Experiment Station Laboratories of National Animal Industry Foundation, Pingtung, Taiwan. Total lipids were extracted from the samples using the chloroform-methanol (2:1, v/v) method described by Wang et al. (2016). Briefly, 4 mL of methanolic H2SO₄ (4%, w/v; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to the lipid fraction, and the mixture was heated in a water bath at 90°C for 60 min to obtain fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). The reaction mixture was then passed through a glass pasteur pipette containing anhydrous Na2SO₄ (Sigma-Aldrich) to remove moisture, followed by drying under a stream of nitrogen in a 60°C water bath for 60 min. The dried FAMEs were re-dissolved in filtered isooctane and analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC 6890 N; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and a highly polar fused silica capillary column (SP-2560, 100 m × 0.2 mm i.d., 0.20 µm film thickness; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 250°C and 300°C, respectively. The oven temperature program started at 170°C, increased to 200°C at a rate of 3°C min⁻¹, and was held for 40 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 0.75 mL min⁻¹, with a split ratio of 40:1. Individual FAMEs were identified by comparing their retention times with those of known standards (Sigma-Aldrich), and the relative percentage of each fatty acid was calculated from the integrated peak areas using Star GC Workstation Version 6.0 software (Varian Analytical Instruments, Walnut Creek, CA, USA).

Chicken-sausage batters

Rheological properties. The rheological properties of the meat batter samples were determined using a rheometer (HR-2, TA Instruments Inc., New Castle, DE, USA), according to the method of Lin and Barbut (2024), with minor modifications. The rheological behaviors of meat batters during thermal gelation were determined by a rheometer equipped with a parallel plate (40 mm in diameter), maintaining 1 mm measurement gap. Gels were prepared by heating from 5 to 100°C with a heating rate of 2°C/min and continuously shearing in an oscillatory mode with a fixed frequency (0.1 Hz) and strain (1%). The storage modulus G′ (elasticity), loss modulus G″ (viscosity), and phase angle δ (the ratio of G″ to G′, general viscoelasticity) were recorded to characterize the rheological properties of samples.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR). The water distribution of the cooked meat batter samples (heating temperature: 95°C; internal temperature: 72∼75°C) was determined using a low-field NMR analyzer (NMI20-040H-I, Niumag, Soochow, China), according to the method of Hsieh et al. (2025) with minor modifications. The temperature of the permanent magnet in the NMR analyzer was maintained at 32°C, and the proton resonance frequency used was 21 MHz. Cool samples (room temperature, approximate 25°C) were gently dried by KimwipesTM (Kimberly-Clark Worldwide, Inc., Taipei, Taiwan) first and then cut into parallelepipeds (1 × 1 × 3 mm3) and then placed in NMR tubes. Subsequently, NMR tubes equipped with plugs were put into the magnet cavity of LF-NMR instrument for acquiring T2 relaxation characteristics which measured by Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence. The 90° and 180° pulse durations were 16.52 and 32.48 μs. A total of 7000 echoes and 4 scans was collected. Water populations were classified as bound water (T2 < 1 ms), immobilized water (T2 = 10 - 100 ms), and free water (≥ 1000 ms), with their relative proportions determined from the peak areas of each T2 component.

Texture profile analysis (TPA). The TPA of the sausage samples was determined using a texture analyzer (TA.XT plus, Stable micro systems Ltd., Godalming, Surrey, UK). Before testing, the sausage samples were cut into cubes (1 × 1 × 1 mm3). The conditions for texture analysis were as follows: probe test speed 5.0 mm/s, probe post-test speed 5.0 mm/s, strain 60%, a compression distance of 5 mm, with the trigger set to auto and a trigger force of 5 g. Hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience were determined followed by the description of Wang et al. (2016). Five duplicates per sample batch were tested.

Lipid oxidation. The lipid oxidation of the sausage samples was evaluated by using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay (Wu et al., 2021). The minced sausage samples were mixed with distilled deionized water (ddH2O, 1:9, w/v), homogenized, and then centrifuged at 800 x g for 15 min. Subsequently, 60 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 90 μL TBA solution (Sigma Sigma-Aldrich Co.) and 510 μL TCA-HCl (Sigma Sigma-Aldrich Co.) reagent. The samples were heated in a water bath for 30 min at 95°C, followed by cooling and centrifugation at 9,000 x g for 3 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 535 nm. The TBARS value of the cooked samples was calculated by taking the extinction coefficient of malondialdehyde (MDA) to be 156,000 M-1 cm-1 and expressed as mg malondialdehyde (MDA) eq./kg meat.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The microstructures of the sausage samples were imaged using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-6510LV, Jeol, Japan), according to the method of Chen et al. (2025) with minor modifications. Sausage samples were cut into cubes (5 × 8 × 3 mm3) and fixed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde for at least 24 h at 25°C. After that, the fixed samples were rinsed 3 times using 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.3), and then post-fixed for 1 h in 0.1 M phosphate buffer containing 1% osmium tetroxide and rinsed. Then, all the samples were dehydrated in a series of ethanol (35, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 90, 95, 100, 100 and 100%) for 10 min at each concentration. Finally, all the samples were dried in the critical point dryer (Samdri-PVT-3B system, Tousimis Co., Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) and coated with gold. The prepared samples were visualized with the scanning electron microscope at 50, 500, and 1000 X magnification.

Statistical analysis

All analytical parameters were determined in 3 independent batches (replications, n = 3). The data were shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The experiment was conducted using a completely random design and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A significant difference was used at 0.05 probability level. Differences between treatments were tested using the least significant difference (LSD) test. All of the experimental data were analyzed by SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results and discussion

Fatty-acid profile in chicken-liver-hydrolysate pastes (CLHPs)

The fatty-acid profile of CLHPs is presented in Table 2. The total saturated fatty acid (SFA) content of CLHPs was 3.59 g/100 g, consisting primarily of palmitic acid (C16:0, 1.96 ± 0.14 g/100 g) and stearic acid (C18:0, 1.53 ± 0.12 g/100 g). Caproic acid (C6:0), myristic acid (C14:0), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0), tricosanoic acid (C23:0), and lignoceric acid (C24:0) were present only in trace amounts (≤ 0.03 g/100 g). Capric acid (C10:0), lauric acid (C12:0), arachidic acid (C20:0), and behenic acid (C22:0) were non-detectable (N.D.). In comparison, chicken breast contained substantially lower amounts of SFA, with palmitic acid (0.421 g/100 g), stearic acid (0.123 g/100 g), and myristic acid (0.014 g/100 g) representing the predominant components. The total monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) content of CLHPs was 2.39 g/100 g, dominated by 9-cis-oleic acid (C18:1, 2.08 ± 0.14 g/100 g). Palmitoleic acid (C16:1) and 11-cis-vaccenic acid (C18:1) were present at lower levels (0.11 and 0.13 g/100 g, respectively). In chicken breast, 9-cis-oleic acid (C18:1, 0.697 g/100 g) was likewise the major MUFA. The total polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content of CLHPs was 3.35 g/100 g, dominated by 9,12-cis-linoleic acid (LA, C18:2, 2.27 ± 0.30 g/100 g). Moreover, the contents of 5,8,11,14,17-cis-eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, C20:5) and 4,7,10,13,16,19-cis-docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6) in CLHPs were approximately 10 times higher than those in chicken breast (EPA: 0.03 vs. 0.003 g/100 g; DHA: 0.08 vs. 0.008 g/100 g). Overall, the CLHPs exhibited higher levels across all fatty-acid classes compared with chicken breast. The total SFA content of CLHPs was approximately 6 times higher than that of chicken breast, indicating a substantially greater proportion of saturated lipids (3.59 vs. 0.568 g/100 g). Similarly, the total unsaturated fatty acid (USFA) content of CLHPs was more than 4 times higher than that of chicken breast (5.74 vs. 1.332 g/100 g) while the SFA/USFA ratio (0.63) still remained less than 1, confirming that USFA still predominated. Within the unsaturated fraction, MUFAs in CLHPs were nearly 3 times higher than those in chicken breast (2.39 vs. 0.797 g/100 g), while PUFAs were more than 6 times higher than those of chicken breast (3.35 vs. 0.535 g/100 g). CLHPs contained approximately 5 times higher total ω−3 fatty acids (0.30 vs. 0.060 g/100 g), while ω−6 fatty acids (3.05 vs. 0.475 g/100 g) were elevated by more than 6 times compared with chicken breast. Irawan et al. (2022) reported that greater dietary intake of ω−3 fatty acids may produce fewer inflammatory-provoking mediators and alleviation of oxidative stress, leading to cardiovascular protection. Although EPA and DHA are recognized as the most physiologically significant ω−3 fatty acids in human nutrition, α-linolenic acid (ALA, C18:3, 0.06 g/100 g CLHPs) was also characterized as anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects (Wang et al., 2016). In comparison with chicken breast, CLHPs contains higher levels of ω−3 fatty acids (ALA, EPA, DPA, and DHA), suggesting their strong potential for use as health-promoting ingredient in emulsion-type sausages. In addition, it was also demonstrated that chicken-liver protein possesses promising emulsifying and nutritional properties, supporting its application as a functional ingredient in emulsified meat products (Lu et al., 2021a, Lu et al., 2021b). Therefore, due to both their nutritional value and structure-forming properties, native or modified chicken-liver proteins may effectively contribute to emulsion stability, physicochemical quality, and health-promoting properties in meat systems.

Dynamic rheology of chicken-sausage batters prepared with various levels of CLHPs

The storage modulus (G′) of the chicken-sausage batters revealed that, upon heating from 5 to 100°C, the G′ gradually declined when an incorporation levels of CLHPs were increasing (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 1). This trend suggested a weakened gel network structure and reduced elastic properties of the meat batters. This behavior can be attributed to the partial substitution of intact myofibrillar proteins with hydrolyzed protein fractions, which contain shorter peptide chains and exhibit a reduced capacity to form strong protein-protein interactions and develop a continuous three-dimensional gel network. Typically, myosin begins to denature at approximately 50°C, which generally leads to a sharp rise in G′ due to protein aggregation and network formation. However, in the 20CLHP group, this increase was not apparent, implying a less stable gel structure and inferior rheological performance compared with the other formulation ones. As the temperature increased from 65 to 100°C, the G′ of the chicken-sausage batters decreased, indicating the disruption and subsequent reformation of the gel structure. The loss modulus (G″) remained relatively low across all treatments, and no noticeable differences among all group were observed during heating (Fig. 1B) indicating that the viscous dissipation behavior and molecular mobility of the continuous phase were largely unaffected by CLHP addition. This suggests that CLHP primarily interferes with protein-protein interactions responsible for elastic network formation, rather than altering the viscosity of the continuous phase. As a result, the reduction in G′ may be attributed to a weakened or less interconnected protein network, while the stable G″ reflects preserved frictional and hydration-related energy dissipation within the system. Throughout the heating cycle, G′ consistently exceeded G″, as reflected in the tan δ (G″/G′) curves (Fig. 1C), indicating that all batters behaved as predominantly elastic gel systems. Correspondingly, each treatment batter exhibited an increased phase angle (δ) values between 35 and 45°C, coinciding with the primary viscoelastic transition associated with myosin denaturation.

The G′ and G″ are critical determinants of texture and overall quality in processed meat product, representing its elasticity and viscosity (Hsieh et al., 2025). As the temperature reaches approximately 40-50°C, a pronounced elevation in rheological property was observed, corresponding to the thermally induced unfolding and aggregation of myosin subunits, especially heavy meromyosin, which triggers the initial development of a protein gel network (Wang et al., 2023). At temperatures above 70°C, both G′ and G″ tend to decrease as proteins undergo further denaturation and form more compact, stable disulfide-linked gel matrices (Yang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). Lin and Barbut (2024) observed that the inclusion of plant-derived by-products reduced the G′ values compared with the control group, which consisted of a standard meat batter formulation without any these by-products, with a progressive decline as the substitution ratio increased. Based on the rheological results of the different chicken-sausage batter groups (Fig. 1), substitution levels of CLHPs below 15% still appear to form an elastic, compact, and stable structure for further chicken sausage production.

Water distribution of chicken-sausage batters prepared with various levels of CLHPs

According to the low-field NMR (LF-NMR) analysis, the T2 relaxation time distributions in cooked chicken-sausage batter prepared with various levels of CLHPs are illustrated in Figure 2. The three peaks are considered to three different water components in the system (Fig. 2A). Specifically, T21 (0.1-10 ms), T22 (10-150 ms), and T23 (100-1000 ms) represent bound water tightly associated with macromolecules, immobilized water confined within the gels, and free water locating outside the protein gel network, respectively. The peak area percentages of the fitted LF-NMR relaxation time were PT21 (proportion of bound water), PT22 (proportion of immobilized water), and PT23 (proportion of free water), respectively (Fig. 2B). The PT21 of cooked chicken-sausage batter in the CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups were 1.83, 1.90, 2.52, 3.10, and 3.34%, respectively. The PT22 of cooked chicken-sausage batter in the CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups were 95.79, 95.69, 95.44, 95.32, and 95.57%, respectively. The PT23 of cooked chicken-sausage batter in the CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups were 2.38, 2.41, 2.04, 1.58, and 1.09%, respectively. These results indicated that increasing levels of CLHPs increased the proportion of bound water while decreasing the proportion of free water within the cooked chicken-sausage batter, suggesting reduced water mobility and redistribution of water populations during heat-induced gelation.

Specifically, the increased proportion of bound water suggests enhanced water-protein interactions, whereby water molecules become more tightly associated with macromolecular structures. In contrast, the reduction in free water reflects decreased water mobility within the gel network. These changes imply that CLHP incorporation promoted water immobilization within the protein matrix rather than reducing overall water retention capacity. The altered water distribution may be associated with changes in protein-water interactions caused by partial substitution of myofibrillar proteins with CLHPs. As temperature increases, the native tertiary structure of proteins, which is initially stabilized by interactions such as hydrophobic forces, is altered as buried hydrophobic residues become exposed (Soleymani et al., 2022). According to the LF-NMR assay procedure, heating at 95°C induced protein denaturation and structural rearrangement in the chicken-sausage batters, which exposed additional hydrophobic groups and promoted exudate release prior to measurement. It is speculated that a greater proportion of water became more tightly associated with the protein matrix in CLHPs incorporated chicken-sausage batters, leading to an increase in PT21 and a decrease in PT23. Similar observations were reported by Han et al. (2019), who demonstrated that heat-induced protein denaturation and crosslinking significantly affected water distribution within meat batters during thermal processing. Zhang et al. (2025b) reported that the majority of water in muscle is retained within the structure of the muscle-fiber network and muscle cells. Bound water refers to water molecules closely associated with non-aqueous constituents, such as proteins, exhibiting reduced mobility and thus not readily migrating to other compartments. This fraction is highly resistant to freezing and to removal by conventional heating (Cheng et al., 2020). Another fraction, termed entrapped (or immobilized) water, is found within muscle tissues and meat, where the water molecules are retained either by steric constraints or through attraction to the bound water (Xu et al., 2025). In contrast, free water represents the portion of water whose movement from the tissue is largely unimpeded and is held mainly by weak surface forces (Cheng et al., 2020). The LF-NMR results were consistent with the rheological observations, indicating that CLHP incorporation modified the heat-induced gel network of chicken-sausage batters (Figs. 1& 2). Although rheological analysis suggested a reduction in gel elasticity at higher CLHP levels, LF-NMR data revealed a decrease in free water and a concomitant increase in bound water, indicating enhanced water immobilization within a structurally reorganized protein matrix. This suggests that changes in gel viscoelasticity do not necessarily correspond to reduced water-protein interactions. Overall, incorporation of CLHPs altered the spatial distribution and binding state of water within the gel matrix during thermal processing, thereby modulating the physicochemical properties of cooked chicken-sausage batter.

Potential application of CLHPs on physicochemical properties of emulsion-type chicken sausages

Yield, water holding capacity (WHC), lipid oxidation, and fatty-acid composition

The emulsion-type chicken sausages were prepared by replacing 0% (CON), 5% (5CLHP), 10% (10CLHP), 15% (15CLHP), and 20% (20CLHP) of the chicken breast with CLHPs in the standard formulation (Table 1). Increasing the proportion of CLHPs in emulsion-type chicken sausages led to a gradual decline in both yield and WHC, accompanied by a progressive increase in lipid oxidation. The yields of emulsion-type chicken sausages in the CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups were 79.69, 79.06, 78.61, 78.02, and 76.27%, respectively (Table 2). The results indicate that when the inclusion level of CLHPs exceeds 15% (as in the 20CLHP group), a significantly decreased (P < 0.05) yield was obtained compared with the CON and other CLHP substituted groups. The WHC of emulsion-type chicken sausages in the CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups was 94.37, 94.17, 93.91, 92.97, and 88.95%, respectively (Table 2). The results indicated that the inclusion of CLHPs at 15 and 20% significantly decreased (P < 0.05) WHC compared with the other 3 groups, with the 20CLHP group exhibiting lower WHC than 15CLHP group. Additionally, LF-NMR analysis revealed that increasing CLHPs content decreased the proportion of free water, likely due to greater water loss upon heating (Fig. 2). Rheological observations corroborated this trend, with G′ values declining as CLHPs increased, indicating a weakened internal network. Consequently, structural weakening of the protein matrix reduced water retention, leading to lower WHC and decreased yield. Regarding the product quality, TBARS values of emulsion-type chicken sausages of CON, 5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups were 1.64, 2.02, 2.99, 3.37, and 3.75 mg MDA eq./kg, respectively (Table 2). The results indicated that when the inclusion level of CLHPs exceeded 5% (as in the 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups), TBARS values were higher (P < 0.05) compared with CON groups. According to the fatty-acid profile analysis of final emulsion-type sausages (Supplementary Table 2), the 15CLHP and 20CLHP treatments exhibited higher levels of total ω−3 fatty acids, primarily ALA, than the CON group, indicating that the ω−3 fatty acids present in CLHPs (Table 1) were effectively retained after processing and cooking. However, the increased proportions of USFAs, including MUFAs, PUFAs, total ω−3 fatty acids (mainly ALA), and total ω−6 fatty acids (mainly LA), in CLHP-formulated sausages may also enhance susceptibility to lipid oxidation and rancidity. Therefore, the observed physicochemical characteristics can be mechanistically attributed to both the altered protein matrix structure (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) and the modified fatty-acid composition of CLHPs (Table 2) and the corresponding CLHP-formulated sausages (Supplementary Table 2).

A well-organized and compact myofibrillar protein network enhances water retention, reduces water migration, and minimizes cooking loss during thermal processing (Glorieux et al., 2017). Previous studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of alternative protein sources to partially replace meat can negatively affect water retention and cooking yield (Tahmasebi et al., 2016). Broucke et al. (2022) also reported that the addition of extruded pea protein weakened network formation of a stable protein network, resulting in a less cohesive structure and increased cooking loss. WHC has been reported to be strongly associated with the structural quality of the gel network. A higher WHC indicates a stronger cross-linking ability between myofibrillar protein and water, leading to a more homogeneous and denser network structure in the meat emulsion (Yang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). Similarly, Wang et al. (2022) observed that partial replacement of meat with mushroom led to significantly a lower (P < 0.05) cooking yield compared with the group without mushroom replacement, indicating a WHC reduction of the meat product. Consistent findings were also reported when pork-spleen hydrolysate protein was applied as a meat replacer in emulsion-type sausages, where increasing its incorporation level was associated with a progressive decline in cooking yield (Toldrá et al., 2021). In animal by-products such as edible offal, susceptibility to lipid oxidation is inherently high due to their substantial levels of unsaturated fatty acids, heme pigments, and pro-oxidative enzymes (Zhang et al., 2025c). Hence, the presence of residual heme iron in CLHPs which is known to potentially promote lipid oxidation (Lima et al., 2021) and the hydrolysis procedure and condition, i.e. period, temperature change, also contributed pro-oxidation. The progression of lipid oxidation generates secondary byproducts such as malondialdehyde (MDA), which not only contribute to undesirable flavor and odor but also diminish the overall quality and acceptability of meat products (Chen et al., 2025). Beyond sensory deterioration, lipid-derived aldehydes like MDA are potent protein-reactive species capable of inducing protein oxidation. These aldehydes readily interact with amino-acid side chains, promoting carbonylation, thiol oxidation, and disulfide bond formation that accelerate protein unfolding, aggregation, and cross-linking. Such oxidative modifications disrupt the formation of a continuous and cohesive protein matrix during thermal gelation and impair key functional attributes, such as WHC and gel strength, which are essential for maintaining the integrity of emulsified meat systems (Liu et al., 2024). Taken together, an excessive CLHP addition weakened the gel network and impaired protein-water interactions, resulting in reduced WHC and yield. Therefore, a CLHP substitution level below 15% is considered the optimal level for maintaining the structural integrity, water retention, and oxidative stability of emulsion-type chicken sausages. Although chicken-liver hydrolysates (CLHs) have been reported to exhibit certain antioxidant properties (Chou et al., 2014), lipid oxidation in CLHP-containing treatments may be influenced not only by fatty-acid composition but also by hydrolysis-derived oxidative peptides and residual heme iron. Hence, the initial oxidative status of CLHPs should be considered prior to incorporation into meat systems.

Textural properties. The texture profile analysis (TPA) of emulsion-type sausages plays a pivotal role in determining consumer acceptability, as it provides insights into how the incorporation of CLHPs influences the structural integrity and mechanical properties, thereby helping to identify the optimal level of CLHP addition. In our chicken-sausage samples, increasing the incorporation level of CLHPs resulted in a gradual deterioration (P < 0.05) of texture parameters, indicating a weakened gel matrix and reduced structural stability. In particular, the 20CLHP group showed the lowest (P < 0.05) values of hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience compared with the other 4 CLHPs substituted groups (Table 2), indicating that substitution with CLHPs at a 20% level significantly deteriorate the structural integrity. The texture profile of meat products is determined by the structural integrity of the protein matrix, the WHC and emulsifying properties of proteins, as well as the interactions among protein molecules (Barbut, 2023). Álvarez et al. (2018) reported that incorporating animal by-products as partial meat replacers in sausages significantly altered the textural and functional characteristics of the products, depending on the substitution level and the nature of the by-product used. Consistently, Toldrá et al. (2021) demonstrated that porcine spleen could be successfully utilized as a meat replacer at levels up to 15% without inducing any detrimental effects on the physicochemical or techno-functional properties, including WHC and instrumental texture. These findings suggest that certain animal by-products are capable of maintaining a sufficiently stable protein-gel backbone, allowing the overall structural integrity of the sausage matrix to be preserved despite partial meat replacement. These textural declines at higher substitution levels are consistent with the weakened gel network observed in the rheological properties (Fig. 1) and reduced water mobility revealed by LF-NMR (Fig. 2), collectively indicating that excessive CLHPs incorporation compromises matrix integrity, while moderate replacement levels maintain a more stable and cohesive emulsion structure. Colour properties of emulsion-type chicken sausages formulated with various levels of CLHPs. The surface colour parameters of emulsion-type chicken sausages were given in Table 4. Compared with the CON group, all CLHPs substituted groups (5CLHP, 10CLHP, 15CLHP, and 20CLHP groups) exhibited lower (P < 0.05) L* and hue values but higher (P < 0.05) a*, b* values and chroma values. Additionally, the colour difference (ΔE*) relative to CON progressively increased (P < 0.05) with rising CLHP levels, reaching 26.35 in 20CLHP samples. Similarly, the colour parameters in cross section showed the same trend as the surface colour, with all CLHPs substituted sausages exhibited lower (P < 0.05) L* and hue values but higher (P < 0.05) a*, b*, and chroma values compared with the CON group (Table 4). These results demonstrated that even partial replacement of chicken breast with CLHPs substantially influence the colour characteristics in cross section. Moreover, the total colour difference (ΔE*) relative to CON further confirmed the marked impact of CLHPs on internal colour. All substitution levels showed ΔE* > 10, indicating strong and readily perceivable colour differences. The changing trend in ΔE* values likely reflects the inherent reddish-brown colour of CLHPs (L*, a*, b*: 43.21, 6.21, and 12.62, respectively). As heme rich ingredients, CLHPs increased the redness of the white chicken-sausage batters upon incorporation, thus shifting the hue angle closer to the red attribute. Among organoleptic attributes, colour plays a dominant role in shaping the first impression, contributing up to 90% of consumer preference. Pandiselvam et al. (2023) further reported that the higher redness (a* value) is generally associated with perceived freshness, a greater perceived proportion of meat ingredients, and improved visual appeal, all of which may positively influence consumer preference. Similar results have been reported in studies where the inclusion of heme-containing byproducts, such as liver or blood-derived proteins, enhanced the redness and overall colour uniformity of meat emulsions (Kim et al., 2017; Toldrá et al., 2021). Therefore, substituting up to approximately 15% of chicken breast with CLHPs may offer a practical threshold, allowing their use as a natural pigment source or functional colour-enhancing ingredient in processed meat formulations, providing improved visual appeal and potential clean-label advantages.

Microstructural observation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to examine the protein matrix structure and visualize the spatial arrangement of components in sausages, thereby elucidating the microstructural changes induced by replacing chicken breast meat with CLHPs (Fig. 3). At 50 X magnification, the CON group exhibited relatively smooth surfaces with minimal undulations and few pores. With increasing incorporation of CLHPs, surface irregularities became more pronounced, accompanied by deeper and more numerous pore-like structures. Notably, the 20CLHP group exhibited more evident surface cracks and larger void-like regions than the CON group, suggesting that the protein network structure was weakened and less capable of maintaining matrix integrity. At 500 X and 1000 X magnifications, the SEM images highlighted the presence of oil globules (indicated by the red triangles) in sausage samples. The CON group exhibited a microstructure in which oil droplets were uniformly distributed within the batter matrix after emulsification, with only a few small droplets appearing on the surface. This result suggested that oil globules are effectively stabilized through their physical entrapment within a gelled protein matrix. However, as the inclusion levels of CLHPs increased, the emulsification efficiency deteriorated, leading to appearance of oil droplets on the surface, which becomes more numerous and larger in size, particularly evident in the 20CLHP samples. These observations were in consistent with the results of rheological properties and water distribution in chicken-sausage batters (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), and physical properties (yield, WHC, and textural properties) of emulsion-type chicken sausages (Table 2). Previous research has demonstrated that emulsion-type sausages exhibit common microstructural features, including a spongy appearance formed by dense protein aggregates with irregular cavities, along with dispersed fat globules within the continuous protein matrix (Horita et al., 2014). The integrity of this gelled protein network is essential for providing structural stability, occupying the interstitial spaces of the matrix, and ensuring effective entrapment of fat droplets during emulsification and heating (Tahmasebi et al., 2016). As the replacement level of the main meat ingredient increased, its limited structural functionality and the absence of gel-like consistency, coupled with weak WHC, prevented it from effectively filling the interstitial spaces within the meat matrix (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a). Similarly, Fu et al. (2023) indicated that replacing chicken breast with mushrooms in emulsion-type sausages led to a loosening of the protein network structure and which appeared as empty pores and sponge-like structures and a negative impact on texture as the mushroom content increased. As we know, a well-formed gel protein network can effectively encapsulate fat globules, thereby maintaining their stability and preventing their release onto the surface. As the replacement level of the main meat ingredient increased, its limited structural functionality reduced the ability to encapsulate fat globules, thereby compromising their stability and allowing them to migrate to the surface (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a). Consequently, excessive incorporation of CLHPs was unable to support the formation of a continuous and compact protein network, leading to a reduction in the product’s WHC, a concurrent decline in yield and an increase in free-water proportion, while the destabilized matrix further leads deteriorated textural properties, ultimately producing a coarser and less uniform microstructure.

Conclusion

Chicken-liver-hydrolysate pastes (CLHPs), which contain high levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly ω−3 fatty acids than chicken breast, can enhance the nutritional value on meat products and serve as a functional raw-meat replacer in processed meat products (Fig. 4). Based on the Agrocycle concept, this study aimed to explore the potential integration of CLHPs into meat products to improve the utilization and maximize their added value. Increasing the substitution level of CLHPs reduced the elasticity (G′) of chicken-sausage batters and altered water distribution, as evidenced by higher portion of bound water and lower portion of free water. In emulsion-type chicken sausages, an excessive incorporation of CLHPs lead to inferior physicochemical and textural properties, including reduced yield, poor water retention, elevated lipid oxidation, and a disrupted microstructure. Overall, moderate substitution levels (≤ 15%) maintained acceptable product quality. However, limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Further studies are needed to assess sensory attributes and long-term storage stability of CLHP-incorporated sausages, which would provide a more comprehensive understanding of consumer acceptance and practical shelf-life performance. Nevertheless, the incorporation of CLHPs offers a sustainable approach for valorizing poultry by-products into value-added emulsified meat products, thus enhancing the competitiveness of the meat industry and demonstrating their potential as a sustainable functional material.
