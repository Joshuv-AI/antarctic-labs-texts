Bone Anabolic Treatment in Patients With Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review
Authors: Huriye Seyma Kafa, Sabina Chaudhary Hauge, Ditte Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) associated osteoporosis leads to an increased risk of bone fracture in patients with CKD. Evidence on the efficacy and safety of anti‐osteoporotic medications is limited for this group. This systematic review aimed to examine the efficacy and safety of anabolic parathyroid hormone (PTH) treatment in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D.MethodsMEDLINE, EMBASE and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched systematically for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating teriparatide, natpara, or abaloparatide in adult patients with CKD Stages 4–5D, including kidney transplant recipients. The primary outcome was the change in bone mineral density (BMD) during follow‐up measured at the lumbar spine (LS), femoral neck (FN) and total hip (TH). The secondary outcomes included bone turnover markers, fractures, histomorphometric results from bone biopsies and adverse events (AEs).ResultsTwo RCTs were included. A subgroup analysis of patients with CKD Stage 4 (n = 5) showed that teriparatide improved BMD at the LS and FN. Another trial (n = 61) in CKD Stage 5D showed significant BMD improvement at the TH compared to the control group. AEs were observed with higher frequency in the teriparatide‐treated group.ConclusionTreatment with teriparatide seems to improve BMD in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D, but evidence on efficacy and safety is limited.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO: CRD42024527086Keywords: abaloparatide, bone mineral density, chronic kidney disease, natpara, randomized controlled trial, teriparatide

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a worldwide public health problem, and CKD is estimated to occur in around 10% of the global population [1]. Patients with CKD have an increased fracture risk compared to the background population [2, 3]. Both traditional and non‐traditional risk factors influence the risk of future fracture. Traditional factors include female gender, Caucasian ethnicity, physical inactivity and insufficient intake of calcium or vitamin D, whereas the non‐traditional risk factors include chronic metabolic acidosis, systemic inflammation, hormonal imbalances, medications used in CKD treatment and renal osteodystrophy [4]. Low bone mineral density (BMD) predicts an increased risk of future fracture [5], and guidelines recommend dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scanning to assess fracture risk in patients with CKD, highlighting the importance of BMD assessment [6].

At present, there are no definitive recommendations for the treatment of osteoporosis in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D due to a lack of evidence on the efficacy and safety of anti‐osteoporotic medications for this group [7].

Anabolic agents can be used to treat osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. These include teriparatide, natpara and abaloparatide. Teriparatide, a recombinant (1–34) N‐terminal amino acid sequence of human parathyroid hormone (PTH), is an effective treatment for osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. It increases BMD in lumbar spine (LS) and femoral neck (FN), and it also reduces the risk of vertebral and non‐vertebral fracture [8]. Similarly, natpara, a full‐length form of recombinant human PTH (1–84) (rhPTH (1–84)), has been found to reduce the incidence of fractures and significantly improve BMD in both the LS and FN in women with postmenopausal osteoporosis [9]. Finally, abaloparatide, a synthetic 34‐amino acid peptide analogue of the 1–34 portion of the human PTH‐related protein (PTHrP) has been shown to increase BMD in total hip (TH) and in FN, with a reduction in both vertebral and non‐vertebral fractures in postmenopausal women [10]. These randomized trials of teriparatide, natpara and abaloparatide all excluded participants with severe CKD or disturbances in the mineral metabolism.

There are several theoretical concerns regarding the use of anabolic medications in patients with CKD [11]. First, PTH hyporesponsiveness in patients with CKD may lead to resistance to the effects of anabolic PTH treatment [12]. Second, renal osteodystrophy in patients with CKD may present with high bone turnover which could be exacerbated by anabolic treatment [13]. Finally, administering a PTH analogue to patients with CKD who often have developed secondary hyperparathyroidism and disturbances in the mineral metabolism may increase the risk of hypercalcaemia.

The aim of this systematic review was to explore the efficacy and safety of treatment with anabolic PTH, such as teriparatide, natpara and abaloparatide in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D.

Methods

This systematic review complies with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [14]. It complies with the registered protocol (CRD42024527086) on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [15]. As prespecified in the protocol, a meta‐analysis was planned only if sufficient data were available.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [16].

Electronic Search Strategy

The search for this review included three different databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE and ClinicalTrials.gov. The search string consisted of three aspects: CKD, anabolic treatment and randomized clinical trials (RCTs). Within each aspect, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and text words were used for the search string in MEDLINE. Similarly, this method was used in EMBASE with the use of Emtree terms and text words. To identify randomized studies, the validated search string (Cochrane Highly sensitive Search Strategy for identifying randomized controlled trials) developed by the Cochrane Collaboration was used [17]. We received assistance in refining the search strategy from an information specialist. The search strings for MEDLINE, EMBASE and ClinicalTrials.gov are provided in Table S1.

In addition, snowballing was performed by screening the reference lists of all included studies to identify potentially relevant articles that were not captured by the electronic database searches.

Study Selection

Studies that met all of the following criteria were included in our review: (a) participants ≥ 18 years with CKD Stages 4–5D (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) including kidney transplant recipients with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; (b) an intervention with either teriparatide, natpara or abaloparatide; (c) a control group consisting of either an untreated control group, patients receiving placebo or patients receiving alternative anti‐osteoporotic medications; (d) at least one outcome registered (as defined below); and (e) RCTs. Studies were excluded if they were not performed in humans or if they were not written in English.

The primary outcome was the change in BMD from baseline to follow‐up, and the secondary outcomes consisted of the following: bone turnover markers (bone‐specific alkaline phosphatase [BALP], total alkaline phosphatase [T‐ALP], C‐terminal telopeptide type I collagen [CTX], total procollagen type I N‐terminal propeptide [PINP], intact PINP [iPINP], tartrate‐resistant acid phosphatase isoform 5b [TRAP5b]), adverse events (AE) defined as hypotension, changes in PTH, changes in ionized calcium and all AEs with a statistically significant difference between the groups, fractures (prevalent and incident) and bone biopsy results. Changes in PTH and ionized calcium were assessed due to their central role in CKD‐mineral and bone disorder (CKD‐MBD) and their relevance to the safety profile of PTH analogues. Bone biopsy results were retrieved as they provide direct information on changes in bone quality.

The search results were imported into Covidence [18], and duplicates were removed. All retrieved abstracts were screened by two reviewers who read the title and abstract independently of one another (H.S.K. and S.C.H.). Articles were excluded if they did not meet the inclusion criteria. After screening the title and abstracts, full‐text articles were retrieved and assessed for inclusion (H.S.K. and S.C.H.). Disagreements during both the first and second screenings were resolved between the two reviewers (S.C.H. and H.S.K.), and in cases of further disagreement, assistance from a third reviewer (DH) was sought.

Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers (H.S.K. and S.C.H.) extracted the following data from the final articles: article title, author name, publication year, study characteristics (study design, trial size, country, funding, blinding, randomization), patient characteristics (race, age, sex, kidney function), information on kidney replacement therapy modality (kidney transplantation, haemodialysis [HD], peritoneal dialysis [PD]), osteoporotic medications (teriparatide, natpara, abaloparatide), the type of comparator (control group, placebo group, or a group treated with another anti‐osteoporotic medication), information from different scanning techniques assessing BMD (high‐resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography [HRpQCT], DEXA), bone turnover markers, fractures and bone histomorphometry.

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

The assessment of quality and risk of bias in the included RCTs was performed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0) [19]. The assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers (H.S.K. and S.C.H.). The RoB 2.0 contains five domains that the reviewers addressed. Domain 1 describes the randomization process; Domain 2 describes deviations from intended interventions; Domain 3 describes missing outcome data; Domain 4 describes measurement of the outcome; and Domain 5 describes the selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as either ‘low risk’, ‘some concern’ or ‘high risk’. If at least one domain was rated as ‘some concern’, the overall rating of the article was ‘some concern’. The same applied to ‘high risk of bias’.

Certainty Assessment

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, classifying each outcome as high, moderate, low or very low certainty [20].

Results

Study Selection

Figure 1 shows the results of the electronic database search with a total of 2981 articles, including 2292 from EMBASE, 685 from MEDLINE and 4 from ClinicalTrials.gov. From these, 474 duplicates were removed, leaving 2507 articles for screening. Based on title and abstract screening, 2489 articles were excluded. A total of 18 articles were included for full‐text screening, after which one study was removed due to lack of access to data and because its purpose was different from that of our study, leaving us with 17 studies in total. Of the 17 studies, 15 were excluded for the following reasons: wrong language (n = 2), wrong study design (n = 5), wrong patient population (n = 5), a conference abstract (n = 1) and ongoing studies (n = 2). The snowballing technique was also used, but no new articles were found. In total, the database search resulted in 2 articles for inclusion for this systematic review.

Miller et al. conducted a post hoc study [21] based on data from a previous large double‐blinded RCT [8]. The original study included 1637 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis aged 42–86 years. Participants were divided into three groups: One group received subcutaneous (SC) 20 μg teriparatide daily, another group received 40 μg teriparatide daily, and the third group received placebo. The treatment duration had a median of 19 months [8]. In the post hoc study by Miller et al., the effect on BMD and fracture risk in patients with mild to moderate CKD were explored. Of these, five patients met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review, having an eGFR of less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 [21].

The second trial by Malluche et al. [22] was an open label study including 141 patients with CKD Stage 5D, consisting of 81 men and 60 women. Patients were divided according to bone turnover into two groups: high bone turnover and non‐high bone turnover. The criteria for the non‐high bone turnover group were determined based on race, with specific thresholds for serum levels of intact PTH, TRAP‐5b and the PTH ratio (the ratio of the level of PTH 1–84 to level of large carboxy‐terminal PTH fragments) [22]. Patients of African descent: (1) iPTH < 350 pg/mL and PTH ratio < 1.2, or (2) iPTH 350–800 pg/mL and PTH ratio < 1.6 and TRAP‐5b < 6.7 U/L, or (3) iPTH < 150 pg/mL. Patients of European descent: (1) iPTH < 400 pg/mL and PTH ratio < 1.0, or (2) iPTH < 400 pg/mL and PTH ratio ≥ 1.0 and TRAP‐5b < 6.7 U/L, or (3) iPTH < 150 pg/mL. The patients with non‐high bone turnover were randomized to receive either 20 μg teriparatide once daily or no treatment. In addition, 30 participants were added to the already randomized control group due to unforeseen financial constraints related to the medication Forsteo. The intervention period was 12 months [22]. Only the group of patients randomized to teriparatide or control (patients with non‐high bone turnover) was included in this review.

In total, 66 patients with CKD Stages 4–5D were included from the retrieved studies in this systematic review. Both included studies had BMD change as the primary outcome, measured by DEXA scan [21] or pQCT [22]. Because only two clinically and methodologically heterogeneous studies met the inclusion criteria, a quantitative synthesis was not feasible. Therefore, the findings are presented as a narrative synthesis.

In the study by Miller et al., more AEs were reported in the teriparatide group, compared to the placebo group. However, no kidney‐related AEs were observed in either group. There were two patients treated with teriparatide, who had serum uric acid levels > 8.3 mg/dL, but no arthralgias or gout were observed. None of the participants developed hypercalcaemia [21]. In the study by Malluche et al., hypercalcaemia was not reported and there were no data on AEs, kidney‐related AEs or results for serum uric acid [22]. Miller et al. reported one non‐vertebral fracture in all five patients with CKD Stage 4, and this occurred in the patient treated with 40 μg teriparatide once daily. No fractures were reported by Malluche et al.

The certainty of evidence for safety outcomes, including AEs and serum calcium levels, was very low, primarily due to high risk of bias, very serious imprecision and incomplete reporting.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a thorough examination of the available evidence from RCTs for efficacy and safety of anabolic PTH treatments including teriparatide, natpara and abaloparatide in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. We identified two RCTs, of which one was a subgroup analysis of a larger, previous randomized trial. Both studies were associated with a high risk of bias and the overall certainty of evidence was very low, substantially limiting confidence in the estimated treatment effects. The evidence for anabolic treatment in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D is sparse. The two included studies both found an increase in BMD after treatment with teriparatide [21, 22].

To our knowledge, there are only these two RCTs that have investigated the efficacy and safety of anabolic PTH treatment in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. The efficacy of anabolic treatment in patients with CKD has been explored in several observational studies which overall support an improvement in BMD during teriparatide treatment [23, 24, 25, 26, 27].

The study by Miller et al. also explored the effect of teriparatide in patients with normal kidney function (eGFR greater than or equal to 80 mL/min/1.73 m2), mild kidney impairment (eGFR between 50 and 79 mL/min/1.73 m2) and moderate kidney impairment (eGFR between 30 and 49 mL/min/1.73 m2). A significant increase in BMD at LS was observed in all three patient groups, and a significant increase in BMD at FN was found in patients with normal and mild kidney function. Treatment with teriparatide reduced the incidence of vertebral and non‐vertebral fractures, regardless of whether patients had normal or impaired kidney function (eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2). Irrespective of kidney function, teriparatide significantly increased PINP, a marker of bone formation [21]. Other studies have also reported an increase in PINP following teriparatide treatment [23, 26, 28], as well as a reduction of the levels of TRAP5b, a bone turnover marker representing osteoclast activity [26, 27]. These findings point towards an effect of teriparatide that may be independent of the underlying kidney function.

The study by Malluche et al. is particularly noteworthy due to its targeted approach, where patients with CKD Stage 5D were stratified according to bone turnover status before initiating treatment. This approach reflects an important clinical rationale: Anabolic PTH therapy, such as teriparatide, may be most appropriate in patients with low or normal bone turnover, although it may be ineffective or even harmful in those with high turnover. Malluche et al. aimed to identify patients with non‐high turnover, based on biochemical markers, and found that teriparatide treatment in this subgroup led to a significant increase in BMD. This individualized approach to assess bone turnover before initiating anti‐osteoporotic therapy may be highly relevant in patients with CKD and is recommended by several stakeholders [3, 29], supporting the concept of individualized treatment in this complex patient population [30].

Several observational studies have reported the occurrence of different AEs after treatment with teriparatide including hypercalcaemia [3, 10, 22, 23, 24, 25], hypotension [10, 26, 27], hyperuricaemia [10, 21], injection site erythema [3] and malaise [3, 27]. A previous study has evaluated the effect of a single dose of teriparatide (56.5 μg) on kidney function, pharmacokinetics and safety. The results showed that teriparatide was eliminated more slowly in patients with severe kidney failure compared to those with normal, mild or moderate kidney function. Nevertheless, nearly all teriparatide was cleared within 24 h, and no serious AEs were reported [3].

Miller et al. found that AEs in patients with normal, mild, moderate and severe kidney function were independent of kidney function. In the small number of patients with severe kidney dysfunction, an increase in serum uric acid was observed in those with eGFR less than30ml/min/1.73m2, but none developed arthralgia or gout. In patients with moderate kidney function, a higher frequency of hypercalcaemia and hyperuricaemia was observed compared to those with normal or mild kidney function [21]. In the study by Malluche et al., no serious AEs were reported, with only a single injection site reaction recorded [22].

Similarly, data on abaloparatide in patients with CKD are limited, and we found no trials in patients with CKD Stages 4–5. In a Phase 3 trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of abaloparatide in patients with CKD Stages 1–3, no serious AEs were reported. The participants in the abaloparatide treated group also had a slightly greater increase in BMD compared to placebo [31, 32].

Our findings align with those of a previous systematic review [33] examining the use of different anti‐osteoporotic medications in patients with CKD Stages 3‐5D. Compared to the previous review, we focused on patients with CKD Stages 4–5D and included an additional trial [21, 22]. Although the evidence is sparse, this further strengthens the hypothesis that anabolic PTH improves BMD in patients with advanced CKD.

Overall, the available evidence remains sparse and of very low certainty. Nevertheless, the present systematic review provides an important overview of the currently available literature regarding anabolic PTH treatment in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. By systematically evaluating and synthesizing the existing data, this review highlights both the potential benefits and the substantial evidence gaps in this field. Several methodological strengths support the validity of this review, including a comprehensive literature search, dual independent screening of abstracts and the use of the RoB 2.0 to rigorously assess the methodological quality of the included studies. Furthermore, by specifically focusing on patients with CKD Stages 4–5D, this review addresses an important evidence gap in a population that is frequently underrepresented in osteoporosis trials. This review also has several important limitations. Only two studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising a very limited number of participants, including one post hoc subgroup analysis with only five patients. Both studies were judged to have a high risk of bias, and the certainty of evidence for all outcomes was rated as very low according to the GRADE approach. Furthermore, the scarcity of available studies and the clinical and methodological heterogeneity between studies precluded the performance of a meta‐analysis. Consequently, the available evidence is insufficient to support general recommendations regarding the use of anabolic PTH treatment in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. Additional limitations include the restriction to studies published in English, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages.

The findings underscore the need for caution when interpreting the available data and emphasize the urgent need for high‐quality RCTs in this understudied patient population. Future research should focus on adequately powered RCTs in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. Such studies should evaluate clinically relevant outcomes including fractures, AEs and changes in BMD and should include longer follow‐up periods to better assess efficacy and safety. Furthermore, future studies should investigate whether treatment effects differ according to bone turnover status and evaluate the potential role of other anabolic PTH analogues, including natpara and abaloparatide, in this patient population.

Conclusion

This systematic review provides preliminary evidence that treatment with teriparatide may increase bone mineral density in patients with CKD Stages 4–5D. However, the currently available evidence is insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding the efficacy and safety of anabolic PTH treatment in this population. This review highlights the scarcity of evidence on anabolic PTH treatment in patients with advanced CKD and underscores the substantial limitations of the existing literature, including small sample sizes, high risk of bias and very low certainty of evidence. Although the limited available data suggest a potential beneficial effect of teriparatide on BMD, additional high‐quality RCTs are required before definitive conclusions can be made or general clinical recommendations can be provided.
