Abstract
. Link-analysis algorithms, such as PageRank, are instrumental in understanding the structural dynamics of networks by evaluating the importance of individual vertices based on their connectivity. Recently, with the rising importance of responsible AI, the question of fairness in link-analysis algorithms has gained traction. In this paper, we present a new approach for incorporating group fairness into the PageRank algorithm by reweighting the transition probabilities in the underlying transition matrix. We formulate the problem of achieving fair PageRank by seeking to minimize the fairness loss, which is the difference between the original group-wise PageRank distribution and a target PageRank distribution. We further define a group-adapted fairness notion, which accounts for group homophily by considering random walks with group-biased restart for each group. Since the fairness loss is non-convex, we propose an efficient projected gradient-descent method for computing locally-optimal edge weights. Unlike earlier approaches, we do not recommend adding new edges to the network, nor do we adjust the restart vector. Instead, we keep the topology of the underlying network unchanged and only modify the relative importance of existing edges. We empirically compare our approach with state-of-the-art baselines and demonstrate the efficacy of our method, where very small changes in the transition matrix lead to significant improvement in the fairness of the PageRank algorithm.

Introduction

Machine-learning algorithms and data-driven decision making are playing
increasingly crucial roles in our lives,
impacting job markets, healthcare, government, industry, and education.
As our dependence on automated decisions grows, however,
so does the concern for fairness in algorithms.
Ensuring that machine-learning algorithms are reliable
and satisfy strong notions of fairness
has become an issue of societal concern and,
justifiably, has received significant attention in the literature
(
12
;
19
)
.
To date, algorithmic fairness has been studied for primarily supervised
(
15
;
16
;
27
;
41
)

but also unsupervised learning
(
4
;
7
;
8
)
.
In the context of network and link-analysis algorithms,
a few different approaches
have been proposed
(
20
;
29
;
37
;
36
;
42
)
,
however, the topic has received significantly less attention
than the study of fairness in other settings.

Link-analysis algorithms, such as the PageRank family of methods,
are fundamental tools for analyzing large-scale network data
(
14
;
3
)
,
and have been used in a variety of applications, including
identifying authoritative vertices
(
38
)
,
detecting communities
(
34
)
,
uncovering patterns of influence
(
23
)
, and
mitigating network vulnerabilities
(
18
)
.

Fairness-aware PageRank.

The problem of fairness-aware PageRank
(
37
)

can be broadly formulated as follows.
We are interested in computing the PageRank scores
on a graph whose vertices are partitioned into
K
groups,
where the partition is defined by, say, a protected attribute.
For simplicity, let us assume
K=2
and refer to the two groups
of vertices as
red
and
blue
.
The fairness of the PageRank distribution is then evaluated
based on the total PageRank scores assigned to
red
and
blue
vertices.
We would like to avoid situations where the PageRank mass is distributed unevenly (/unfairly)
between the two groups.
For example, given a target score tuple

\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{\text{red}},\phi_{\text{blue}})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{(\phi_{\text{red}},1-\phi_{\text{red}})}}
,
we say that PageRank is
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-fair if the total PageRank score of the
vertices in the
red
group is equal to
\phi_{\mathrm{red}}
;

and consequently, the total PageRank score of the
vertices in the
blue
group is equal to
1-\phi_{\text{red}}=\phi_{\mathrm{blue}}
;

Intervention strategies.

To improve fairness for PageRank problems,

the task is to design an intervention strategy so that the PageRank scores of the graph vertices become
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-fair, or “close enough” to
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-fair.
Considering the PageRank algorithm,
there are several possible ways to design intervention strategies, including:

(i)
modifying the PageRank restart probability;

(ii)
modifying the restart vector;

(iii)
adding edges to the graph; and

(iv)
modifying the edge weights of the graph in a controlled manner to ensure that the modified graph remains faithful to the original.

In this paper, we focus on intervention strategies of type (
iv
),
namely, achieving fair PageRank by reweighting the graph edges.
Our motivation is that such interventions are

intrinsic to the graph structure
and can be implemented with actionable operations.
For example, edge reweighting can be achieved by prioritizing/demoting content from one’s connections in a social network. Strategies of type (
iii
) involving edge additions or deletions are also intrinsic to the graph structure but tend to be less practical. These strategies rely on link recommendations and have to account for the likelihood of accepting the recommended links, which introduces uncertainty and makes them challenging to implement.

Strategies of type (
i
) and (
ii
), i.e., modifying the PageRank restart probability or restart vector are
extrinsic to the graph structure
and rely on tuning hyper-parameters of the PageRank algorithm. In many cases, it is not meaningful, nor actionable, to modify those hyper-parameters. For example, in the personalized PageRank algorithm, the restart vector has a clear interpretation, which is to model the user interests and is not amenable to modification.

Locally- vs. globally-fair PageRank.

Existing papers

(
20
;
29
;
37
)

make the observation
that if fairness is ensured at each step of the PageRank random walk,
then fairness is guaranteed for the final PageRank distribution.
Ensuring fairness at each step (which we call locally-fair PageRank)
translates to having equal probability of visiting all groups from each vertex.
While this is a sound approach,
the requirement of locally-fair PageRank can be too rigid and may lead to a
disproportionate modification of the graph structure or the edge weights.
Instead, in this paper, we pose the question of
whether it is possible to
achieve globally-fair PageRank without having to impose the strict requirement of
locally-fair PageRank
.

As we will see, we answer this question in the affirmative.

Our approach.

We present a new approach to incorporate fairness into the PageRank algorithm by reweighting the transition probabilities of the PageRank matrix.
More concretely, we seek to minimize the PageRank distance to a fair distribution while allowing only for reweighting the network edges within prespecified limits.
Our approach handles multiple vertex groups.
Unlike earlier approaches,
we do not recommend that new edges be added to the network, nor do we adjust the restart vector.
Instead, we keep the topology of the underlying network unchanged.

If perfect fairness cannot be achieved,
our formulation aims to find a solution as close as possible to the target fairness objective.

Our approach provides an actionable environment to improve PageRank fairness,
as the newly computed edge weights
can be lead to concrete actions, e.g.,
reweighting and prioritizing the feed of users in the social-network platform.

We propose a projected gradient descent method for computing locally-optimal edge reweightings and show how it can be implemented efficiently.
We empirically compare our approach with state-of-the-art baselines
and demonstrate the efficacy of our method,
where small changes in the transition matrix lead to significant improvement
in the fairness of the PageRank algorithm.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

•

We incorporate group fairness into PageRank by reweighting transition probabilities to achieve solutions as close as possible to the target fairness objective with minimal modifications.

•

We aim for globally-fair PageRank without the strict requirement of achieving
local fairness at each vertex.
This can lead to solutions with smaller modifications in the transition matrix.

•

We introduce a group-adapted fairness notion that accounts for group homophily via group-biased restart distributions.

•

We propose an efficient projected gradient-descent method for finding locally-optimal edge weights.

•

We empirically compare our approach with state-of-the-art baselines and demonstrate the efficacy of our method,
where very small changes in the transition matrix lead to significant improvement in the fairness of the PageRank algorithm.

Due to space limitations, proofs and additional material are in the appendices.

2.
Related work

In recent years, algorithmic fairness in graph mining has emerged as a prominent area of research

(
32
)
. This paper focuses on enhancing fairness in the content of the well-known PageRank algorithm, which has been widely used for ranking, classification, community detection, and more. The objective is to allocate PageRank scores to vertices in a manner that considers both the vertices’ group affiliations and the overall structure of the network
(
37
;
32
)
.

In the standard PageRank algorithm
(
14
;
6
;
34
)
,
a random-walk process defines the transition from one vertex to its neighbors,
while also with certain probability considering a “jump” to one of the vertices defined by a restart vector.
When considering the group association of vertices, defined by a protected attribute,
analytical studies in the literature
(
11
;
13
)

have unveild the unfairness of PageRank.
To address such potential group bias,
FairWalk
(
29
)
proposes to modify the transition matrix of the random walk
so that the transition probabilities from each vertex to all groups become equal.
CrossWalk
(
20
)
further refines this idea by considering
the local group structure and reweighting edges based on their distance from group boundaries.
Both FairWalk and CrossWalk adjust the transition matrix using heuristic-based methods.

37
introduce a novel fairness metric for PageRank and propose two families of algorithms:
\mathrm{FSPR}
and
\mathrm{LFPRs}
.
Methods in the first family,
\mathrm{FSPR}
, retain the original graph structure and edge weights,
while seeking an optimal jump vector to minimize the disparity between the fair PageRank vector and
the original PageRank vector, subject to fairness constraints.
On the other hand, similar to FairWalk and CrossWalk, methods in the second family,
\mathrm{LFPRs}
,
aim to achieve fairness at the individual vertex level and include three variants:

\mathrm{LFPR}_{N}
,
\mathrm{LFPR}_{U}
, and
\mathrm{LFPR}_{P}
.
Each variant reweights the transition matrix in a different way while incorporating a fair jump vector.
Although the authors suggest that
\mathrm{FSPR}
and
\mathrm{LFPRs}

can be extended to scenarios involving more than two groups,
they do not provide details
for such an extension.

Additionally, link recommendation has been explored as an alternative approach to promoting fairness in PageRank.
In one such paper,
36
present analytical formulas
for calculating the impact of adding edges on fairness.
In a different direction,
42
propose a set of de-biasing methods
for fair network representation learning, using techniques based on
sampling, projections, and graph neural networks.

Other papers consider the problem of infusing fairness into rankings
without focusing specifically into the PageRank algorithm.
For instance,
21
propose a model that
weights training samples to make classifiers fair and
adapt it to estimate an unbiased personalization that yields fairer vertex ranks.

In this paper, we propose an edge-reweighting approach to enhance fairness in PageRank.
Our method differs from previous studies in several ways:
First, we consider only edge reweighting, which can lead to more actionable operations to achieve fairness.
Second, we focus on global fairness,
without enforcing the more rigid requirement of fair transitions from each vertex.
As a result, our method can lead to significantly smaller modification of the transition matrix.
Finally, our approach offers a clear and practical framework for handling effectively
fairness over multiple groups.

3.
Problem definition

3.1.
Preliminaries

We consider a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with a set
V
of
n
vertices and a set
E
of
m
edges.
We assume that the set of vertices is partitioned into
K
groups
\{V_{1}\cdots V_{K}\}
.
Let
\ell_{v}
indicate the group that a vertex
v\in V
belongs to.

The PageRank algorithm takes
G
as input, and produces a PageRank vector
\mathbf{{p}}
that indicates the relative importance of each vertex in the graph
(
6
)
.
Despite the existence of multiple equivalent formulations, the PageRank algorithm is fully characterized by three parameters: the transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
, the restart (or jump) vector
\mathbf{{v}}
, and
the restart probability
\gamma
.
The PageRank vector
\mathbf{{p}}
is the solution to the equation

\mathbf{{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}=(1-\gamma)\mathbf{{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{P}}+\gamma\mathbf{{v}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Here, the transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
is row stochastic, derived by normalizing each row of the adjacency matrix of
G
. The restart vector
\mathbf{{v}}
specifies a distribution over all vertices, indicating where a random walk might restart. The parameter
\gamma
represents the probability that the random walk will restart at any given step. In the case of directed graph, there may exist sink vertices that have no outgoing edges, so their rows in the transition matrix would be all zeros.
To handle sink vertices, we replace the corresponding zero rows of
\mathbf{{P}}
with the restart vector
\mathbf{{v}}
.

By solving
the equation of PageRank definition,
we obtain the PageRank vector as

\mathbf{{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\gamma\mathbf{{v}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{{I}}-(1-\gamma)\mathbf{{P}})^{-1}.

3.2.
Fair PageRank

Building on the earlier definition of fairness-aware PageRank
(
37
;
36
)
, we consider the vertices of a graph to be partitioned into distinct groups. These groups may represent social categories based on sensitive attributes such as gender, ethnicity, or sexual orientation. We begin by defining the fair PageRank problem, followed by a discussion of various approaches to achieving PageRank fairness, and conclude with a formal definition of the
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank (
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank) problem and the Restricted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank (
R
-
\boldsymbol{\phi}
PR
) problem.

3.2.1.
Group-wise fair PageRank

To establish the concept of PageRank fairness, we assume a
target PageRank score
,
\phi_{k}
for each vertex group
k=1,\ldots,K
,
with
\sum_{k}\phi_{k}=1
. This score represents the total PageRank mass that the algorithm is expected to allocate to the vertices in group
V_{k}
. A common approach is to set
\phi_{k}
proportionally to the size of
V_{k}
, i.e.,
\phi_{k}=|V_{k}|/n
. However, other choices may be more suitable depending on the specific application. In this work, as in prior studies, we assume that the target PageRank score
\phi_{k}
for each group is provided as input to our problem formulation.

Let
\mathbf{{1}}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote the indicator vector for group
V_{k}
, where
\mathbf{{1}}_{k}[i]=1
if vertex
i\in V_{k}
and
\mathbf{{1}}_{k}[i]=0
otherwise. Let
\mathbf{{p}}
be the PageRank vector computed by the PageRank algorithm using a transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
, with restart probability
\gamma
and restart vector
\mathbf{{v}}
. The total PageRank score for group
V_{k}
is then given by
\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{p}}
.

Given an input graph
G
and its associated transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
, our goal is to find a new transition matrix
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
, defined on the same input graph
G
, such that the group-wise PageRank distribution computed over
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
(defined shortly) closely matches the target group-wise PageRank distribution
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{K})
.

Remark.

For any given
\mathbf{{P}}
,
\gamma
, and
\mathbf{{v}}
, the total PageRank score for group
V_{k}
is lowered bounded by
\gamma\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}
and upper bounded by
1-\gamma+\gamma\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}
. The lower bound represents the probability that a random walk restarts from group
k
, and it can be derived from the equation
\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{p}}=(1-\gamma)\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{P}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{p}}+\gamma\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}\geq\gamma\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}
. The upper bound can be derived from the equation
1-\sum_{j\neq k}\mathbf{{1}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}\leq 1-\gamma\sum_{j\neq k}\mathbf{{1}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{v}}=1-\gamma+\gamma\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Thus, for fixed
\gamma
and
\mathbf{{v}}
, it is clear that it may not always be possible to find a
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
that exactly achieves the desired target distribution
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

3.2.2.
Rationale for PageRank fairness via edge-reweighting

Consider the case where both
\mathbf{{v}}
and
\gamma
can be freely adjusted. In this scenario, achieving the target fair distribution
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is straightforward: we can simply set
\mathbf{{v}}[i]={\phi_{\ell_{i}}}/{|V_{\ell_{i}}|}
and
\gamma=1
. However, adjusting
\mathbf{{v}}
and
\gamma
is impractical
as these parameters are not within our control in actual models; they are merely parameters of PageRank and do not reflect the actual graph structure. In contrast, modifying the transition matrix is more meaningful; this can be accomplished, for example, by appropriately prioritizing or demoting content from an individual’s connections in a social network.

Therefore, we focus solely on adjusting the transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
to achieve the desired fairness distribution. To maintain the structure of the input graph
G
,
we impose the constraint that the new transition matrix
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
must satisfy
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}[i,j]=0
whenever
\mathbf{{P}}[i,j]=0
. This condition ensures that no new edges are introduced.

3.2.3.
Fairness-loss function and the
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank problem

To formalize our problem, we first define the fairness-loss function. It evaluates how much the actual fairness distribution deviates from the target distribution for a given transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
.

Definition 0 (PageRank Problem Instance).

A
PageRank problem instance
consists of a graph
G
with vertices partitioned into groups
V_{1},\ldots,V_{K}
, a row-stochastic transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
, a restart probability
0<\gamma<1
, a restart vector
\mathbf{{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{{v}}\|_{1}=1
, and group-wise target PageRank scores
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{K})
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{1}=1
.

Definition 0 (Fairness-loss function).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
, the fairness-loss function is defined as:

(3.1)

L(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{p}}-\phi_{k}\right)^{2},

where
\mathbf{{p}}
is the PageRank vector corresponding to parameters
(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})
.

The proof of the following claim can be found in Appendix
A

Proposition 1.

The fairness-loss function
L(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})
,
as defined in
Equation

3.1
, is not convex with respect to
\mathbf{{P}}
.

With the fairness-loss function defined, we introduce two problems that aim to achieve fair PageRank by minimizing this loss function through edge reweighting.

Problem 1 (
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
, the goal is to find a new transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}^{*}
that minimizes the fairness-loss function
L(\mathbf{{P}}^{*},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})
, i.e.,

(3.2)

\mathbf{{P}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})}L(\hat{\mathbf{{P}}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}}),

where

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})=\{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}\mid\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\,\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}_{ij}=0\text{ if }~\mathbf{{P}}_{ij}=0\}

is the set of row-stochastic matrices that have non-negative entries, and the edge-reweighting of
G
can take place only on existing edges.

Problem 2 (Restricted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
, a relative modification bound
\delta\geq 0
and an absolute modification bound
\epsilon\geq 0
, the goal is to find a new transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}^{*}
that minimizes the fairness-loss function
L(\mathbf{{P}}^{*},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})
, i.e.,

(3.3)

\mathbf{{P}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})\cap\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})}L(\hat{\mathbf{{P}}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}}),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
is defined as in Problem
1
, and

\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})=\{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}\mid(1-\delta)\mathbf{{P}}[i,j]-\epsilon\leq\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}[i,j]\leq(1+\delta)\mathbf{{P}}[i,j]+\epsilon\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})
restricts the entries of the new transition matrix
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
within a range of
(1-\delta)
and
(1+\delta)
of the corresponding entries in the original transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
, while also imposing an absolute modification bound
\epsilon
. The
relative
bound is necessary because, for very small values in
\mathbf{{P}}
, even slight absolute changes can result in large relative variations.

By choosing
\delta
and
\epsilon
such that
(1-\delta)\mathbf{{P}}-\epsilon>0
, we can guarantee no edges are deleted.

The motivation for defining the Restricted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank problem (Problem
2
)
is to consider only solutions that make small modifications in the transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}
.

Without loss of generality, in this paper, we study both
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank and Restricted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank with a uniform restart vector
\mathbf{{v}}=\left[\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}\right]
.

3.3.
Group-adapted fair PageRank

Real-world networks are mostly homophilic,
i.e., vertices in the same group are more likely to be connected to each other

(
24
;
35
)
.
Hence, the total PageRank scores in group
V_{k}
are largely contributed by random walks
(re)starting at vertices in group
V_{k}

(
37
)
.
Thus, we propose the notion of
group-adapted fairness
,
which enhances PageRank by considering random walks that (re)start at each group and
by penalizing the fairness loss for each such (re)start.

More specifically, let
\mathbf{{v}}_{k}=\frac{1}{|V_{k}|}\mathbf{{1}}_{k}
be
the vector indicating that the random walk restarts uniformly only from group
k
.
We define the
group-adapted fairness-loss function
as follows:

Definition 0 (Group-adapted fairness loss).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
,
the group-adapted fairness-loss function is defined as

(3.4)

L_{\mathit{g}}(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}L(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}}_{\ell})=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{p}}_{\ell}-\phi_{k}\right)^{2},

where
\mathbf{{p}}_{\ell}
is the PageRank vector with respect to parameters

(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}}_{\ell})
.

In the group-adapted fairness loss,
we iterate through all groups and sum up the fairness loss
when restarting the random walk at each group.
Minimizing the group-adapted fairness-loss function
leads to the Group-adapted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank problem.

Problem 3 (Group-adapted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
, find a new transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}^{*}
that minimizes the group-adapted fairness-loss function

(3.5)

\mathbf{{P}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})}L_{\mathit{g}}(\hat{\mathbf{{P}}},\gamma),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
is defined as in Problem
1
.

Similarly, we define the restricted
version of the problem.

Problem 4 (Restricted group-adapted
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-PageRank).

Given a PageRank problem input as defined in
Definition

1
, a relative modification bound
\delta\geq 0
and an absolute modification bound
\epsilon\geq 0
, find a new transition matrix
\mathbf{{P}}^{*}
that minimizes the group-adapted fairness-loss function
L_{\mathit{g}}(\mathbf{{P}}^{*},\gamma)
, i.e.,

\mathbf{{P}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}\in\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})\cap\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})}L_{\mathit{g}}(\hat{\mathbf{{P}}},\gamma),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
is defined as in Problem
1

and
\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})
as in Problem
2
.

Matrix inversion acceleration.

The calculation of
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}=\gamma\mathbf{{U}}^{-T}\mathbf{{1}}_{k}
involves the inverse of the matrix
\mathbf{{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{{I}}-(1-\gamma)\mathbf{{P}}
, which is computationally expensive. To avoid computing matrix inversion, we approximation
\mathbf{{U}}^{-T}
with the Neumann series

\mathbf{{U}}^{-T}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-\gamma)^{i}\mathbf{{P}}^{i},

and substituting into
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
gives

\mathbf{{y}}_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-\gamma)^{i}\mathbf{{P}}^{i}\mathbf{{1}}_{k}.

As the series terms decrease exponentially in magnitude,
due to the decaying factor
(1-\gamma)
,
we can approximate
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
efficiently
by computing only the first few series terms.
We show that truncating the Neumann series to its first
50
terms yields a relative error below
0.0003
. The detailed analysis is provided in Appendix

Gradient interpretation.

In Proposition
2
we have defined

\mathbf{{y}}_{k}=\mathbf{{U}}^{-T}\mathbf{{1}}_{k}
.
The
j

\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
is given by

\mathbf{{y}}_{k}[j]=\mathbf{{1}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}(\gamma\mathbf{{U}}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{j})
,
which represents the sum of PageRank scores for the vertices in the group
V_{k}
, with the random walk restarting at the unit vector
\mathbf{{e}}_{j}
. In other words, the random walk always (re)starts from vertex
j
.

Hence, the gradient of the fairness loss has the following intuitive interpretation:
the
[i,j]
-th entry of the gradient matrix is a weighted sum of the products of

\mathbf{{p}}[i]
and
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}[j]
.
The coordinate
\mathbf{{p}}[i]
represents the frequency with which a random walk, restarting at
\mathbf{{v}}
, visits vertex
i
,
while the coordinate
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}[j]
represents the frequency with which a random walk, restarting at vertex
j
,
visits vertices in the group
V_{k}
.
Therefore, the
k
-th term of the summation in Eq. (
4.1
)
reflects the weighted contribution of the edge
(i,j)

to the overall PageRank score of the group
V_{k}
.
The weight of the
k
-th term in Eq. (
4.1
)
is proportional to the difference between the total PageRank scores
of group
V_{k}
and the group-wise target score
\phi_{k}
.

Gradient projection.

After each gradient descent step,

\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
needs to be projected back into the feasible area subject to constraints
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
or
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})\cap\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})
.
We perform the projection for each row of
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
separately, and since both constraints forbid adding non-existing edges, we solve the projection only for its non-zero
entries
. Given any row of
\hat{\mathbf{{P}}}
, let
\mathbf{{s}}
be
the vector comprises the
non-zero
entries of the corresponding row vector
, and
\mathbf{{s}}^{*}
be the vector after projection. We use
N=|\mathbf{{s}}|
to denote the cardinality of
\mathbf{{s}}
.

We first discuss the constraint

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
. Projecting
\mathbf{{s}}

onto a vector

\mathbf{{t}}

that lies in the feasible set

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
is equivalent to solving the following problem:

\mathbf{{s}}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{{t}}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}}{\arg\min}\left\|\mathbf{{s}}-\mathbf{{t}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\{\mathbf{{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{t}}=1,\mathbf{{t}}\geq 0\}
is a probability simplex. This is a well studied problem
(
25
;
26
;
10
;
9
)
, and the computational complexity for
finding

\mathbf{{s}}^{*}
is
\mathcal{O}(NlogN)
.
In
Algorithm

2

9
–
12
, we use the method detailed by
10
to perform the projection of
\mathbf{{s}}
after every gradient update step.

We then discuss the constraint
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})\cap\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})
. Solving the projection subject to this constraint is equivalent to
finding
a Euclidean projection of
\mathbf{{s}}
onto the intersection of a probability simplex and a box. Namely,

\mathbf{{s}}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{{t}}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}}{\arg\min}\left\|\mathbf{{s}}-\mathbf{{t}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\mathcal{F}_{2}=\{\mathbf{{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{{t}}=1,\ell_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq u_{i}\ \text{ for all }i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\}

\ell_{i}=\max\{0,(1-\delta)s_{i}-\epsilon\}
and

We provide the details in Algorithm

4.3.
Extensions

While not explicitly studied in the paper, we note that the proposed fairness loss can be generalized in several ways. First, when fairness scores are specified at the individual-node level, an auxiliary loss term can jointly enforce both group-level and individual-level constraints. Second, for graphs with hierarchical group structures, where each layer defines a partition with associated fairness goals, the overall objective can be formulated as the sum of layer-specific losses, enabling simultaneous satisfaction of fairness requirements across multiple scales. Finally, the restricted fairness losses can incorporate node-level constraints by specifying
\delta
and
\epsilon
for each node, with our algorithm remaining applicable.

5.
Complexity and convergence analysis

The primary computational cost arises from computing PageRank and approximating
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
.
The PageRank vector is obtained by iterating the update equation
\mathbf{{p}}^{\top}=(1-\gamma)\,\mathbf{{p}}^{\top}\mathbf{{P}}+\gamma\,\mathbf{{v}}^{\top}

for
t_{1}
iterations, resulting in computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(t_{1}n^{2})
.
The computation of
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
involves multiplying a vector by a matrix
t_{2}
times.
Evaluating
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
therefore requires
\mathcal{O}(Kt_{2}n^{2})
operations.
Consequently, the overall complexity of
Algorithm

1
is
\mathcal{O}\big((Kt_{2}+t_{1})n^{2}\big)
.
Since
AdaptGD
performs an additional loop over
K
groups when computing
\mathbf{{y}}_{k}
, its complexity becomes
\mathcal{O}\big((K^{2}t_{2}+t_{1})n^{2}\big).

Both loss objective functions,
L(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma,\mathbf{{v}})
and
L_{\mathit{g}}(\mathbf{{P}},\gamma)
, are differentiable functions concerning
\mathbf{{P}}
, and their first derivatives are smooth. Since the feasible set
\mathcal{C}_{R}(\mathbf{{P}})\cap\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{{P}})
is convex and closed, the proposed projected gradient update method with constant step size converges to a stationary point, which is a local minimum
(
5
)
.
We provide detailed analysis in
Appendix

F
and
Appendix

G
.

7.
Conclusion

We presented a novel approach for incorporating group
fairness into the PageRank algorithm by reweighting the entries of the underlying transition matrix.
We focus on matrix reweighting interventions,
as we argue that those are more actionable strategies,
and further, reflect the network structure, rather than the hyper-parameters of PageRank.
Our goal is to minimize fairness loss (with respect to a target group-fairness distribution)
while also minimizing the changes to the transition matrix.
We achieve our objective by foregoing the idea of ensuring fairness locally, at every vertex,
and instead aim to ensure fairness at a global level.
Technically, our method is an efficient projected gradient-descent algorithm for
finding locally-optimal edge weights.
We presented a comprehensive empirical evaluation,
demonstrating that our method offers significant advantages compared to strong baselines.

8.
Ethical considerations

This work focuses on reweighting the transition matrix of a graph to achieve target group-wise PageRank scores, with the goal of improving fairness across groups. While the proposed approach aims to improve fairness and mitigate bias in ranking systems, interventions that alter item rankings could be misused to deliberately amplify or suppress the visibility of certain content, potentially leading to manipulation or censorship. Furthermore, if applied to sensitive domains, such interventions could unintentionally disadvantage specific users or groups, even when fairness metrics improve. Our experiments use only publicly available datasets with no personal user or item-identifying information, reducing privacy risks. To mitigate potential misuse, we recommend deploying such methods with transparent governance, independent auditing of fairness outcomes, and clear documentation of intervention goals and criteria.

In this section, we prove that for approximating

.

According to the Neumann series formula, we can calculate the matrix inverse of a matrix
\mathbf{{I}}-\textbf{X}
as:

(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\textbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Let
\textbf{S}_{t}
denote the partial sum of the Neumann series up to the
t
-th term, that is,

\textbf{S}_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{t}\textbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}=\textbf{I}+\textbf{X}+\textbf{X}^{2}+\dots+\textbf{X}^{k}.

We denote the truncation error as

\textbf{E}_{t}=(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}-\textbf{S}_{t}
, then we can express
\textbf{E}_{t}
as the sum of the remaining terms:

\textbf{E}_{t}=\sum_{i=t+1}^{\infty}\textbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}

By factoring out
\textbf{X}^{t+1}
from the series we get:

\textbf{E}_{t}=\textbf{X}^{t+1}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\textbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Using the formula for the Neumann series, the infinite sum

(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}
. Thus:

\textbf{E}_{t}=\textbf{X}^{t+1}(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}

By taking the matrix norm of both sides, we get

\|\textbf{E}_{t}\|\leq\|\textbf{X}^{t+1}\|\cdot\|(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}\|

Given that

\|\textbf{E}_{t}\|\leq\|\textbf{X}\|^{t+1}\cdot\|(\textbf{I}-\textbf{X})^{-1}\|

Thus, we conclude that the relative error for approximating

We present here a description of the baselines we use in our evaluation.

LFPR
U

(
37
)
.

\mathbf{{A}}
residual-based algorithm that revises both the restart vector and the transition matrix to achieve perfect
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-fairness in PageRank scores. The intuition is that each node distributes a portion of its PageRank to the under-represented groups, and distributes the remaining portion to its neighbors uniformly.

The revised transition matrix is

, where:

(H.1)

\mathbf{{P}}_{L}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{1-\phi_{1}}{out_{2}(i)},&\text{if }(i,j)\in E\text{ and }i\in L_{1}\\

\end{matrix}\right.

k

\phi_{k}
.
\mathbf{R}

(H.2)

\mathbf{R}=\delta_{1}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+\delta_{2}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}

.
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{y}

.

LFPR
N

(
37
)
.

\mathbf{{A}}
neighborhood-based algorithm that achieves perfect
\boldsymbol{\phi}
-fairness in PageRank scores. The revised transition matrix is given by
