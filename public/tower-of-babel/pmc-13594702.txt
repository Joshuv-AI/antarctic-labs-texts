S-nitrosylation topology in melanoma: established mechanisms, unresolved redox-immune links, and a testable framework
Authors: Jyoti Srivastava, Sanjay Premi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelanoma therapies can produce substantial tumor regression without durable control, raising two separable questions: how efficiently tumor cells are eliminated and whether therapy-induced death supports immune recognition. This review examines whether melanocytic-lineage redox biology and site-selective protein S-nitrosylation help connect these outcomes. We distinguish mechanisms demonstrated directly in melanoma from mechanistic precedents in other systems and from hypotheses that require validation. In melanocytic cells, active pigment-forming chemistry can support a nitric oxide synthase (NOS)-active state. In melanoma, S-nitrosylation has been linked to MAPK persistence during MEK inhibition, TSC2-dependent mTOR activation, and NOS1-dependent suppression of interferon programs through HDAC2 and IRF7. Pharmacological S-nitrosylation blockade also increases ER stress signaling, calreticulin exposure, HMGB1 release, immune cell recruitment, and tumor control, but these observations do not establish a site-specific effect on the immunological quality of cell death. We therefore propose a two-gate framework in which S-nitrosylation may regulate both susceptibility to irreversible death and the signaling competence of dying cells. Establishing this model will require quantitative site occupancy, cell type-resolved nitrosoproteomics, causal site replacement, and immune assays that distinguish altered signaling from simply increased cell killing. The available evidence supports selective interrogation of causal S-nitrosylation nodes rather than indiscriminate suppression of NO biology.Keywords: Melanoma, Melanogenesis, Nitric oxide, S-nitrosylation, Therapy persistence, Tumor immunity

Therapeutic response as a redox-information problem

Melanoma illustrates a recurring problem in cancer therapy:radiographic regression is not equivalent to durable control. Combined BRAF and MEK inhibition can produce high initial response rates and substantial tumor regression, yet residual disease and later progression remain common [1]. Durable benefit from immune checkpoint blockade depends on the presence or successful generation of tumor-reactive immunity. Clinical outcome therefore has at least two separable dimensions: the depth of tumor-cell elimination and the capacity of treatment to support immune recognition.

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) formalizes this distinction. ICD is not a morphological synonym for apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, or lytic death. It is a functional outcome in which dying cells provide antigenic and adjuvant information sufficient to initiate protective adaptive immunity [[2], [3], [4]]. Calreticulin exposure, ATP secretion, HMGB1 release, and tumor-cell-intrinsic type I interferon signaling are mechanistically important components [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]], but no single marker proves that death is immunogenic. The most stringent test remains vaccination with treatment-killed tumor cells followed by protection against viable tumor rechallenge [2,4].

This distinction is particularly relevant to melanoma because MAPK-directed therapy can alter survival signaling, melanocytic antigen expression, inflammatory output, and immune cell recruitment at the same time. BRAF inhibition can increase MART-1 and gp100 expression, improve T-cell recognition, and increase intratumoral CD8-positive T cells [[11], [12], [13]]. In sensitive melanoma cells, MAPK pathway inhibition can also promote calreticulin or HSP90 exposure and dendritic-cell activation, whereas resistant derivatives lose these danger-associated features [14]. These observations show that targeted therapy can change both antigenicity and danger signaling, but they do not make tumor regression synonymous with immune priming.

The clinically important question is therefore mechanistic:which reversible processes determine whether therapeutic stress is buffered as persistence, resolved through immune-silent cytoreduction, or transmitted as immunologically productive tumor destruction? We evaluate melanocytic-lineage chemistry and site-selective S-nitrosylation as a redox framework that may help answer this question.

We use three evidence levels throughout the review:findings demonstrated directly in melanoma, mechanistic precedent from other cancers or cell types, and melanoma-specific hypotheses that remain to be tested. Melanogenic chemistry establishes a lineage-conditioned redox state

Melanoma arises from a lineage specialized for melanin synthesis, compartmentalization, and transfer. This history matters because melanogenesis is an oxygen-dependent redox process that consumes substrates, redistributes thiols, produces electrophilic and radical intermediates, and is normally compartmentalized within a specialized organelle. Three variables must be separated experimentally:accumulated pigment, expression of the melanocytic differentiation program, and active melanogenic flux. They are often correlated, but they are not interchangeable.

Visible pigmentation reports the amount, composition, and distribution of pigment at the time of measurement. Active melanogenesis instead denotes ongoing tyrosinase-dependent conversion of tyrosine through l-DOPA and dopaquinone. Dopaquinone is a chemical branch point from which enzymatic and non-enzymatic reactions generate cysteinyldopas, dopachrome, indolic intermediates, quinones, and semiquinones before polymerization [26,27]. Cysteine and glutathione availability strongly influence whether this chemistry proceeds toward eumelanin or pheomelanin, linking pigment synthesis directly to thiol allocation and redox buffering.

Melanosomal compartmentalization limits cytosolic and nuclear exposure to these intermediates, but containment is conditional rather than absolute. UVA-associated oxidative and nitrosative chemistry can fragment or solubilize melanin-derived material and permit pigment-like species to appear outside the conventional melanosomal compartment, including in the nucleus [28]. This establishes chemical mobility under stress without implying constitutive leakage. The biological consequences of melanogenesis therefore depend jointly on flux, pigment composition, melanosomal integrity, thiol availability, metal chemistry, and the capacity of antioxidant systems to terminate secondary reactions.

Melanogenic activity can also reshape cellular physiology beyond pigment accumulation. Pharmacological stimulation of melanogenesis in B16-F10 melanoma cells altered mitochondrial oxygen consumption, the ADP/ATP ratio, mitochondrial content, and proliferative state [29]. These findings do not establish a universal metabolic effect of pigmentation, but they demonstrate that pigment-forming chemistry can be coupled to bioenergetic remodeling.

The melanocytic program is dynamic in melanoma. Cells transition among differentiated melanocytic, transitory, neural-crest-like, and undifferentiated states [30,31]. These states differ in MITF activity, pigment-cell gene expression, mitochondrial organization, antioxidant capacity, inflammatory signaling, and susceptibility to stress-induced death. Dedifferentiated states are more vulnerable to ferroptosis, whereas PGC-1α-high melanomas display increased mitochondrial capacity and oxidative-stress resistance downstream of MITF and oncogenic BRAF [[31], [32], [33]]. These associations define lineage-linked redox phenotypes but do not prove that melanogenesis itself causes each phenotype.

Therapy further remodels lineage state. BRAF inhibition can increase melanocytic antigens in patient tumors [13], whereas residual and resistant populations frequently adopt neural-crest-like or drug-tolerant states through non-genomic reprogramming [34,35]. Such transitions alter metabolism, thiol use, antioxidant dependence, and antigen presentation independently of bulk pigment. Pretreatment pigmentation therefore cannot be assumed to represent the biochemical state in which residual melanoma cells experience sustained therapy.

A more precise premise is that melanocytic differentiation establishes a dynamic biochemical context, while active pigment-forming chemistry can further modify oxygen use, thiol allocation, electrophile handling, organelle communication, and antioxidant dependence. Assigning a therapeutic phenotype specifically to melanogenesis therefore requires controlled manipulation of pigment flux while preserving genomic background and, as far as possible, lineage identity.

Genomic subtype adds an independent layer. BRAF-mutant, NRAS-mutant, NF1-mutant, and triple-wild-type melanomas differ in signaling architecture and therapeutic pressure [36], but no genotype specifies a single lineage state. The same genotype can occupy differentiated or dedifferentiated states, and state transitions can precede stable genetic resistance. A lineage-redox model should therefore be tested within genotype rather than inferred across genotypes. The relevant variable is not whether a tumor is simply pigmented, but whether a defined cell population is actively executing a melanogenic program when NOS activity, SNO occupancy, and therapeutic response are measured.

Melanogenic chemistry and NOS/NO signaling form a context-dependent circuit

The redox demands of melanogenesis make an interaction with nitric oxide biology plausible, but the direction of causality must be resolved experimentally. NO is generated from l-arginine by NOS1, NOS2, and NOS3, which differ in regulation, cellular source, amplitude, duration, and subcellular organization. NO production, nitrosative stress, and protein S-nitrosylation are related but distinct. Their biological effects depend on local concentration, oxygen and superoxide availability, metal centers, thiol accessibility, and denitrosylase activity. A nitrosative state is therefore better defined as a cellular environment that supports spatially and temporally organized NO-derived signaling than as a simple increase in bulk NO.

The best-established direction of the circuit is from NO toward melanogenesis. In cultured human melanocytes, UVB radiation increases tyrosinase activity and melanin production through a NOS/NO/sGC/cGMP pathway; NO donors reproduce the response, whereas inhibition of NOS or cGMP signaling attenuates it [37]. NO released by UV-irradiated keratinocytes can stimulate melanogenesis in a paracrine manner, and exogenous NO increases tyrosinase transcription [38,39]. These studies support a mechanistic sequence from NOS activity to NO/cGMP signaling to melanogenic gene expression and pigment synthesis.

Evidence for the reverse direction, from a pigment-forming state to NOS activity, is more recent and mechanistically incomplete. Melanin chemiexcitation established that pigment chemistry can operate within a reaction system containing reactive nitrogen species:after UV exposure, NO and superoxide generate peroxynitrite, which oxidizes melanin-derived species and produces electronically excited intermediates capable of transferring energy to DNA [28]. This work demonstrates close coupling between pigment and nitrogen-oxide chemistry, but it does not show that mature melanin directly activates a NOS isoform.

Controlled depigmentation-repigmentation experiments provide stronger directional evidence [40]. Tyrosine deprivation reduced intracellular pigment and total NOS activity, whereas tyrosine repletion restored both. Importantly, DHICA restored pigmentation and NOS activity in isogenic tyrosinase-deficient albino melanocytes. Canonical upstream tyrosinase activity was therefore not required for the observed restoration once a downstream melanogenic intermediate was supplied. The most defensible interpretation is that restoration of a pigment-forming cellular state creates conditions permissive for NOS activity, not that mature melanin directly binds or activates NOS.

The operative signal remains unresolved. Candidate mechanisms include l-DOPA or downstream indolic intermediates, quinone and semiquinone chemistry, glutathione redistribution, pigment polymerization, melanosomal pH or copper handling, calcium signaling, and organelle communication. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. Their resolution will require controlled perturbation of individual melanogenic steps combined with isoform-specific NOS activity, localization, coupling state, and site-resolved S-nitrosylation.

Melanoma can amplify and partially uncouple this physiological circuit. NOS activity is increased in melanoma cell lines relative to nonmalignant melanocytic controls [41]. NOS1 depletion or pharmacological inhibition reduces invasion and alters BCL-2, MMP1, JUN, and APE1/Ref-1, while NO-dependent APE1/Ref-1 signaling reinforces AP-1 activity and invasive behavior [42,43]. NOS2-derived NO can also originate from melanoma cells or myeloid populations [[44], [45], [46]].

Among the melanoma studies considered here, NOS1 provides the clearest site-resolved connection to immune dysfunction. Tumor-cell NOS1 impairs IFN-α-induced STAT1 activation in cocultured lymphocytes, and NOS1 depletion, inhibition, or NO scavenging partially restores responsiveness [47]. IFN-γ can induce nNOS and intracellular NO in melanoma, while nNOS inhibition attenuates STAT1/STAT3 signaling and PD-L1 expression [48]. More recently, NOS1-dependent S-nitrosylation of IRF7 was linked directly to suppression of interferon and antigen-presentation programs, with functional effects on dendritic-cell maturation, CD8-positive T-cell infiltration, and tumor control [23]. In parallel, suppression of protein S-nitrosylation sensitizes NRAS-mutant melanoma to MEK inhibition and enhances ER stress signaling, DAMP exposure, dendritic-cell activation, and antitumor immune responses [15].

The relevant enzyme and cellular source nevertheless cannot be assumed from total NOS activity. NOS1 and NOS3 typically generate calcium-regulated, spatially restricted signals, whereas NOS2 can sustain larger and more prolonged flux. Enzyme uncoupling can also redirect electron flow toward superoxide, changing both NO bioavailability and peroxynitrite formation. Tumor cells, keratinocytes, endothelial cells, and myeloid populations may therefore contribute chemically distinct inputs to the same lesion. Isoform-specific genetics, direct activity measurements, localization, and coupling-state analysis are required before a pigment-responsive NOS circuit can be assigned to NOS1 or any other isoform.

The evidence therefore supports a bidirectional but asymmetric relationship. NO/cGMP signaling is an established upstream regulator of melanogenesis, whereas controlled pigment restoration can restore NOS activity without identifying the relevant sensor or NOS isoform. Oncogenic and inflammatory inputs can subsequently maintain NOS signaling independently of visible pigmentation. This context-dependent circuit, rather than a simple pigment-high/NO-high correlation, forms the upstream basis of the proposed framework (Fig. 1).

S-nitrosylation topology encodes redox specificity

NOS expression and bulk NO abundance do not explain how NO-derived chemistry changes melanoma behavior. Once generated, NO can activate soluble guanylyl cyclase, react with superoxide to produce peroxynitrite-associated oxidation and nitration, or participate in reversible protein S-nitrosylation [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. These pathways can coexist, but they are chemically and functionally distinct. Nitrotyrosine, for example, reports nitrating chemistry but does not uniquely identify peroxynitrite because peroxidase-dependent nitrite oxidation can produce the same endpoint [50,51]. Likewise, cGMP activation does not imply extensive protein S-nitrosylation.

Protein S-nitrosylation forms an S-nitrosothiol at a cysteine residue and can arise through nitrosating species, metal-assisted reactions, thiyl-radical chemistry, or transnitrosylation [52,53]. Selectivity is imposed by spatial organization and protein context. Positioning a NOS enzyme near a protein complex creates a local NO-reactive microdomain in which neighboring targets can be modified without a proportional increase in whole-cell NO. Compartmentalized NOS signaling can thereby regulate trafficking and other local processes through restricted protein S-nitrosylation [54].

Cysteine accessibility, local electrostatics, thiol pKa, hydrophobicity, protein conformation, and proximity to carriers of nitrosating equivalents further determine modification. Proteomic mapping identifies recurrent structural features but no universal linear consensus sequence [55]. Selectivity can also propagate through protein-directed transfer:S-nitrosylated GAPDH enters the nucleus and transnitrosylates proteins including HDAC2, SIRT1, and DNA-dependent protein kinase [56].

The resulting landscape is constrained by denitrosylation. GSNOR, encoded by ADH5, metabolizes GSNO and thereby regulates the equilibrium between low-molecular-weight and protein S-nitrosothiols, whereas cytosolic and mitochondrial thioredoxin systems directly denitrosylate selected substrates [57,58]. A protein's modification state therefore reflects the balance between local SNO formation or transfer and site-specific removal.

This balance motivates the concept of S-nitrosylation topology. SNO abundance describes the total quantity of S-nitrosothiols in a cell or sample. Topology specifies which proteins are modified, at which cysteines, in which compartments and cell types, at what fractional occupancy, and for how long [[54], [55], [56], [57], [58]]. Two melanoma cells with comparable NOS1 expression can therefore maintain different nitrosoproteomes because of differences in NOS localization, target abundance, cysteine accessibility, glutathione/GSNO metabolism, or thioredoxin activity. Conversely, an increase in global SNO cannot identify which causal sites gained or lost modification [[54], [55], [56], [57], [58]].

Topology is also dynamic. Therapy can alter protein abundance, complex assembly, organelle localization, glutathione availability, and denitrosylase activity before any change in NOS expression is detectable [54,57,58]. A site can therefore gain occupancy because its target moves into an NO-reactive microdomain, because denitrosylation is locally suppressed, or because competing thiols are depleted. Time-resolved occupancy is essential:an early, transient modification that preserves signaling may have a different consequence from persistent modification during ER stress or DAMP release. Static endpoint nitrosoproteomics risks collapsing these mechanistically distinct states [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58]].

Melanoma provides direct evidence that site identity can matter. NOS1-dependent S-nitrosylation of HDAC2 at Cys262 and Cys274 alters HDAC2-dependent regulation of interferon-stimulated genes; replacement of these cysteines attenuates effects on interferon signaling, lymphocyte infiltration, and metastasis [22]. NOS1-dependent modification of IRF7 at Cys435 in mouse IRF7 and Cys481 in human IRF7 suppresses type I interferon initiation, antigen-presentation programs, dendritic-cell activation, and CD8-positive T-cell-dependent tumor control [23]. Thus, the same NOS isoform can suppress immunity through distinct substrates operating at different regulatory levels.

S-nitrosylation topology also explains why broad denitrosylation is not intrinsically beneficial. In other cancer contexts, S-nitrosylation can activate EGFR-Src signaling, impair DNA repair, or expose treatment-specific metabolic liabilities; it can also redirect TNF signaling from survival toward cell death [[59], [60], [61], [62]]. Direction must therefore be assigned site by site. Removing SNO from a survival-promoting MAPK node may be therapeutic, whereas removing a pro-death or immune-activating modification could be counterproductive.

Measurement must match the biological claim. NOS transcript or protein abundance does not report catalytic coupling, localization, or the chemical fate of NO. Nitrite and nitrate integrate NO metabolism but provide little spatial information. Diaminofluorescein probes report accumulated nitrosating chemistry and are influenced by cellular oxidants and antioxidants rather than functioning as direct stoichiometric NO sensors [63]. Total protein SNO does not define site occupancy or functional consequence.

Quantitative occupancy is especially important when a target is abundant or when only a small functional pool resides within a signaling complex. A large change in enriched peptide intensity can still represent low absolute occupancy, whereas modification of a small, compartmentalized fraction may be sufficient to reorganize pathway output. Whenever possible, enrichment-based discovery should be followed by targeted quantification relative to the corresponding unmodified peptide, parallel measurement of total protein, and analysis of the subcellular fraction in which function occurs [64,65]. At this level, SNO topology becomes experimentally distinguishable from a list of candidate targets.

Because S-nitrosothiols are labile, switch-based assays require no-ascorbate controls, positive controls, and verification that enrichment depends on the reduction step; orthogonal capture methods strengthen central claims [64,65]. Protein identification alone is insufficient. For causal inference, a site should be mapped under endogenous NOS activity and linked to phenotype using matched wild-type and modification-resistant variants. Substitution mutants must retain folding, localization, interactions, and basal activity so that phenotypes can be attributed to loss of modification rather than structural disruption. The strongest evidence combines endogenous site mapping, quantitative occupancy, biochemical mechanism, phenotypic rescue, and validation in an immunocompetent model.

Evidence for a two-gate S-nitrosylation checkpoint

Direct melanoma evidence is strongest for the death-susceptibility arm of the framework. In human NRAS-mutant melanoma cells, endogenous S-nitrosylation was detected on ERK and other MAPK regulators, and pharmacological SNO suppression sustained MEK-inhibitor control of ERK-MAPK [15]. Candidate ERK1 and MEK1 cysteines were subsequently predicted and tested by site-directed mutagenesis in overexpression experiments in HEK293 cells. Several substitutions reduced S-nitrosylation and altered ERK-MAPK output, but these experiments did not quantify fractional occupancy or establish site-specific modification of those residues under endogenous conditions in melanoma cells [15]. The distributed MAPK pattern is consistent with the established precedent that S-nitrosylation of RAS Cys118 can promote nucleotide exchange and spatially restricted NRAS activation [66,67].

This distributed network may buffer MEK inhibition through coordinated effects on activators, kinases, feedback regulators, and phosphatases rather than through a single modified protein. TSC2 S-nitrosylation can also activate mTOR and support melanoma proliferation [16]. Together, these findings support SNO as a reversible, non-genetic contributor to the depth and duration of targeted-therapy response.

Pharmacological SNO blockade also changes features associated with the quality of therapy-induced death. Combined SNO blockade and MEK inhibition activate PERK-eIF2α signaling, increase surface calreticulin, and increase extracellular HMGB1 [15]. These events occupy different positions in the ICD program: eIF2α phosphorylation precedes calreticulin exposure, ecto-calreticulin promotes phagocytic uptake, and HMGB1 can support dendritic-cell activation and antigen processing [5,6,[8], [9], [10]]. The same intervention suppresses melanoma growth in immunocompetent models, increases intratumoral dendritic cells, macrophages, and CD8-positive T cells, and enhances immune activation in dendritic-cell-T-cell cocultures [15].

These findings support an immunostimulatory death program but do not yet establish ICD functionally. Molecular hallmarks and immune recruitment do not replace vaccination-based proof of protective adaptive immunity [4]. In addition, stronger immune activation could result simply from greater or differently timed tumor-cell death. The key comparison is therefore immune output at comparable levels and kinetics of cytotoxicity, with death modality also defined. We use the two-gate model (Fig. 2) to separate these questions: a death-susceptibility gate determines whether stress produces irreversible death, whereas a signal-competence gate asks whether dying cells provide immunologically productive information. The gates may be coupled, and independent regulation must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

DAMP abundance and DAMP competence illustrate this distinction. The melanoma nitrosoproteome contains proteins associated with danger signaling, and nitrosylation inhibition shifts DAMP-related proteins toward non-nitrosylated forms while strengthening dendritic-cell and T-cell responses [15]. HMGB1 provides a clear redox precedent: all-thiol, disulfide, and terminally oxidized forms have distinct chemotactic, cytokine-inducing, or inactive functions, and caspase-dependent oxidation can render apoptotic-cell-derived HMGB1 tolerogenic [19,20]. The information carried by a DAMP therefore resides in molecular state as well as abundance.

The same principle applies to coordinated release pathways. ATP secretion requires autophagy-dependent trafficking and membrane transport, whereas calreticulin exposure requires premortem ER stress and translocation rather than nonspecific membrane rupture [9,10]. Tumor-cell-intrinsic type I interferon signaling is also required for some immunogenic therapy responses [7], and its output depends on competent upstream sensing and downstream transcription. S-nitrosylation could act at several levels, including stress sensing, vesicle movement, channel activity, or transcriptional execution. Calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release alone therefore cannot define the signal-competence gate.

S-nitrosylation cannot be assumed to silence every DAMP. HMGB1 Cys106 S-nitrosylation promotes secretion and inflammatory activity in activated microglia and macrophages, while S-nitrosylation of surfactant protein D changes multimeric structure and redirects signaling toward a pro-inflammatory calreticulin-CD91 pathway [21,68]. The melanoma hypothesis must remain site- and context-specific: S-nitrosylation may alter release kinetics, receptor selectivity, stability, or composition of the DAMP repertoire rather than uniformly suppressing it.

Death modality adds a further dimension. Apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis can each be immunogenic or tolerogenic depending on the initiating stress, kinetics, membrane integrity, molecular state of released signals, and host context [69]. S-nitrosylation regulates caspases, ER stress sensors, and trafficking machinery in other systems [17,18,70]. It may therefore influence not only whether melanoma cells die but also which death program is executed and when immunogenic signals become available.

Antigen processing and presentation form a distinct downstream boundary. Proteasomal processing, TAP-dependent transport, ER aminopeptidase activity, beta-2-microglobulin, MHC-I loading, and surface stability determine whether melanoma-derived peptides can be recognized [71]. Cross-presenting dendritic cells add requirements for uptake, endosomal handling, migration, costimulation, and licensing. S-nitrosylation of IRF7 and HDAC2 suppresses the transcriptional environment that supports interferon-responsive and antigen-presentation programs [22,23]; however, direct SNO regulation of most processing and loading components remains unproven. Relevant readouts should therefore include peptide-MHC display and functional T-cell recognition, not only transcript abundance or total MHC-I.

This reversible checkpoint should be positioned alongside fixed escape mechanisms such as JAK1/JAK2 defects, B2M loss, and PTEN loss rather than presented as their replacement [72,73]. Its therapeutic value is most plausible when antigen-presentation machinery is genetically intact but functionally suppressed. In a tumor with biallelic B2M loss, denitrosylation may deepen cytotoxicity without restoring MHC-I-mediated recognition; in a genetically intact but IFN-suppressed tumor, reversal of HDAC2- or IRF7-associated S-nitrosylation could plausibly restore one component of immune competence.

Nitrosative signaling may extend beyond melanoma cells. NO can act over limited tissue distances [74], and S-nitrosothiols can transfer NO-related bioactivity between cells through mechanisms such as cell-surface protein disulfide isomerase [24]. In other host-cell contexts, nitration of CCL2 can exclude tumor-specific T cells [75], whereas S-nitrosylation of macrophage CD40 attenuates inflammatory signaling [25]. These precedents raise, but do not establish, a spatial field involving tumor, stromal, and immune proteins. Bulk analysis could mask opposing cell-type-specific effects, supporting spatial proteomics and cell-resolved SNO capture before and after therapy.

Table 1 summarizes the uneven strength of the evidence. Site-resolved melanoma data are strongest for the interferon arm through HDAC2 and IRF7 [22,23]. MAPK persistence is supported by endogenous pathway-level S-nitrosylation in melanoma together with candidate-site mutagenesis performed in a heterologous overexpression system [15]. By contrast, ER stress, calreticulin exposure, HMGB1 release, and immune recruitment remain linked mainly through pharmacological SNO blockade rather than mapped causal sites. This distinction defines what the signal-competence arm currently explains and what still requires direct testing.

Translational implications and critical tests

The translational objective is selective remodeling of S-nitrosylation topology, not indiscriminate suppression of NO biology. Global NOS inhibition can perturb vascular and immune-cell functions [25,45,46,74] and could also remove context-dependent pro-death SNO events documented in other cancers [61,62]. Generalized manipulation of GSNOR or thioredoxin systems could likewise shift large portions of the nitrosoproteome in opposing directions [57,58]. Intervention should therefore be directed, where possible, at a causal NOS isoform, subcellular signaling compartment, transnitrosylation pathway, denitrosylase, or substrate site.

Current melanoma evidence is most developed in two related but distinct settings: NOS1-linked suppression of interferon and immune signaling [23,47,48] and SNO-dependent persistence during MEK inhibition in NRAS-mutant melanoma [15]. These data do not establish equivalent relevance across BRAF-mutant, NF1-mutant, or triple-wild-type disease. Genotype, melanocytic-lineage state, endogenous NOS activity, denitrosylase status, and antigen-presentation competence should therefore be treated as stratification variables rather than collapsed into a single melanoma category [15,23,[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]].

Treatment schedule is another mechanistically important variable. If SNO buffering contributes to early adaptation during MAPK inhibition, pretreatment or concurrent SNO suppression may differ from later intervention [15]. If SNO state also influences DAMP signaling, the duration of suppression could matter during cell death and antigen uptake. These possibilities remain unproven. Pretreatment, concurrent, and temporally restricted schedules should therefore be compared using site occupancy, pathway response, and immune function as pharmacodynamic endpoints.

Combination with immune checkpoint blockade should be framed as a testable hypothesis, not as an expected consequence of restoring interferon signaling or antigen presentation. Intact IFN signaling and MHC-I presentation are important for T-cell recognition, and irreversible defects in these pathways can confer checkpoint resistance [71,72]. However, improvement in these tumor-intrinsic programs is not sufficient to predict clinical response. Melanoma resistance also reflects immune exclusion, lineage dedifferentiation, antigen loss, T-cell state, dendritic-cell abundance and maturation, and other tumor and microenvironmental programs. In a recent cohort of checkpoint-resistant melanomas, genetic alterations in antigen-presentation or IFN-γ signaling pathways accounted for only about one quarter of resistant cases, indicating that most resistance could not be explained by those pathways alone [76]. Single-cell profiling of metastatic melanoma has further linked mature and migratory dendritic-cell states, together with CD8 T-cell state, to clinical outcome after checkpoint therapy [77].

Accordingly, restoration of IRF7-dependent interferon signaling or antigen-presentation transcripts after SNO remodeling should be interpreted as mechanistic target engagement, not as a surrogate for checkpoint sensitivity. A convincing combination study should demonstrate restored peptide-MHC display, productive dendritic-cell-T-cell priming, expansion or activation of tumor-reactive T cells, and additional benefit from anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 therapy in an immunocompetent model. The mechanistic sequence should be established rather than assumed: SNO remodeling, target engagement, therapeutic death, functional antigen presentation, T-cell activation, and durable tumor control. This ordering would distinguish a redox-immune mechanism from nonspecific additivity between two active agents.

NOS immunohistochemistry alone is unlikely to be an adequate biomarker because abundance does not report activity, localization, or site occupancy [54,[63], [64], [65]]. A candidate modular signature could combine the relevant NOS isoform, GSNOR and thioredoxin status, validated SNO-site occupancy, lineage state, and immune competence. Pretreatment occupancy could identify dependence on an SNO-buffered state, whereas early on-treatment changes could confirm target engagement. Such a signature should initially be treated as mechanistic or pharmacodynamic; it should not be considered predictive of checkpoint response until validated against clinical outcomes.

One informative early human study would longitudinally analyze paired pretreatment and early on-treatment melanoma biopsies. Spatially resolved sampling should determine whether tumor-cell SNO signatures coexist with active melanogenesis, whether they change before radiographic response, and whether their reversal precedes restoration of antigen-presentation programs, dendritic-cell recruitment, or CD8-positive T-cell activation [12,13,15,23]. In checkpoint-treated cohorts, these molecular changes would also need to be related to objective response and durability rather than interpreted as surrogate evidence of benefit.

Four criteria distinguish a causal checkpoint from a descriptive nitrosoproteome. First, an immune phenotype should remain after the contribution of cytotoxicity magnitude, timing, and modality has been resolved [4]. Second, prioritized sites should meet the causal replacement standard defined above, including preserved protein function [22,23]. Third, tumor-cell-restricted and host-cell-restricted manipulations should distinguish the cellular source and recipient of the relevant chemistry. Fourth, occupancy of a small set of validated sites should predict pathway or immune phenotypes more accurately than NOS abundance or total SNO [[63], [64], [65]].

A practical sequence should pair controlled depigmentation-repigmentation with isoform-specific NOS perturbation and quantitative SNO mapping [[37], [38], [39], [40]], then prioritize sites by occupancy change, pathway position, conservation, and tractability for causal testing [15,22,23,64,65]. Sites that pass these molecular criteria should then be evaluated for dendritic-cell uptake, cross-presentation, and tumor-specific T-cell function [2,4]. The strongest in vivo test of immunogenic death remains a vaccination-challenge assay or tumor rechallenge in an immunocompetent model, with immune-subset depletion used to identify the effector arm [2,4].

Fig. 3 translates these criteria into explicit falsification points. If controlled pigment gain or loss fails to alter defined NOS activity or SNO sites, the proposed upstream lineage connection should be revised [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. If a prioritized site fails the causal validation standard despite preserved protein function, evidence for a site-specific mechanism would be weakened [15,22,23]. If immune activity tracks only with the extent of tumor-cell killing, the framework contracts to therapeutic sensitization rather than an independent signal-competence mechanism [4,15].

The framework also has explicit boundaries. It does not predict a uniform relationship between pigmentation and SNO, or a uniform direction for the effects of S-nitrosylation. Phenotype switching, inflammatory cytokines, tissue site, vascular state, and stromal composition can uncouple NOS signaling from visible pigment [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36],[44], [45], [46]]. The model instead predicts a context-dependent, target-resolved program whose upstream probability is influenced by melanocytic lineage and whose therapeutic consequence depends on genotype and immune competence [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53],[59], [60], [61], [62]].

Conclusions

Melanoma retains a lineage-conditioned redox context in which pigment-forming chemistry can support NOS activity and NOS-derived signaling can reinforce melanogenic differentiation. The upstream sensor, relevant melanogenic species, and responsible NOS isoform remain unresolved, while oncogenic or inflammatory signaling can uncouple NOS activity from visible pigmentation.

Current evidence places S-nitrosylation at several mechanistically distinct melanoma nodes. Defined HDAC2 and IRF7 modifications regulate interferon-related programs, TSC2 S-nitrosylation activates mTOR, and pathway-level SNO contributes to MAPK persistence during MEK inhibition [15,16,22,23]. The unifying concept is not bulk NO abundance but S-nitrosylation topology: target, cysteine, compartment, occupancy, timing, and cell type.

The central translational question is therefore whether a small number of site-defined SNO events can be shown to control therapeutic persistence or tumor-immune communication. Demonstrating endogenous site occupancy, causal function, and an immune phenotype that cannot be explained solely by increased cytotoxicity would convert the two-gate framework from an organizing hypothesis into a stronger mechanistic basis for selective intervention.
