CT-derived skeletal muscle predicts lung transplant approval
Authors: Alexa Lavergne, Brandi Bottiger, Mohamed Sobhi Jabal, Tommi Jarvinen, John M. Reynolds, Mustafa R. Bashir, Michael Rosenthal, Jacob Klapper, Kirti Magudia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung transplant candidate selection relies on assessment of physiologic reserve, with body mass index commonly used to guide eligibility despite its limitations in distinguishing muscle from adipose tissue. CT-derived body composition analysis enables precise quantification of skeletal muscle and fat compartments. This study evaluates the association between CT-derived body composition metrics and transplant committee decisions.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of 704 adult lung transplant candidates evaluated from 2019 to 2024 with available preoperative abdominal CT imaging at a single academic institution. CT scans were analyzed using an automated deep learning workflow to quantify skeletal muscle, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat at the third lumbar vertebral level. Metrics were normalized by age, sex, and race reference values. Patients were categorized as approved or denied using transplant committee decisions. Multivariable logistic regression with likelihood ratio testing was used to identify independent predictors of approval.ResultsOf 704 patients, 437 (62%) were approved and 267 (38%) were denied. Greater skeletal muscle area was independently associated with increased odds of transplant approval (per 50 cm2: OR 2.969, 95% CI 1.728-5.321, p<0.001). Visceral fat, subcutaneous fat, and body mass index category were not associated with approval. Skeletal muscle was lower in patients labeled as frail/deconditioned and positively associated with 6-minute walk distance (p<0.001).ConclusionCT-derived skeletal muscle is independently associated with lung transplant approval and may provide an objective marker of physiologic reserve that is not captured by body mass index, with potential to improve risk stratification in transplant candidate selection.

Lung transplantation is a life-saving treatment for patients with end-stage lung disease, yet it carries high morbidity and mortality compared to other solid organ transplants.1, 2 Given the scarcity of donor organs, transplant centers seek to select patients most likely to derive a net benefit from lung transplantation.3 This selection process relies on a complex multidisciplinary evaluation of pulmonary function, physical fitness, comorbid conditions, and psychosocial readiness, guided by variable center-specific criteria and consensus-based contraindications by the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT).3, 4, 5

Body mass index (BMI) has long served as a surrogate marker of nutritional status and a tool for risk stratification during the lung transplant evaluation. Both underweight and overweight recipients have demonstrated an increased risk of mortality and worse transplant outcomes.6, 7 The ISHLT consensus guidelines list a BMI > 35 and BMI < 16 as relative contraindications.4 However, BMI is an imprecise measure of body composition, as it cannot differentiate between muscle and adipose tissues, nor their distribution.8, 9, 10, 11 Consequently, BMI may inadequately capture the nutritional risk profile of transplant candidates, while also contributing to delays and disparities in transplant listing as patients attempt to gain or lose weight12, 13, 14 to achieve eligibility.

Computed tomography (CT)-derived body composition analysis has emerged as a more precise alternative, enabling direct quantification of skeletal muscle, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat. Magudia et al. developed a validated, fully automated, and high-throughput body composition workflow using deep learning for analysis of abdominal CT exams, and demonstrated an association between body composition and overall survival as well as cardiac events.15, 16 In the lung transplant population, CT-derived body composition metrics have also been associated with adverse outcomes, including primary graft dysfunction, prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer intensive care unit stays, and increased mortality.10, 17, 18, 19

Despite growing evidence supporting the prognostic value of CT-derived body composition in transplant outcomes, its role in transplant candidacy decision-making remains unexplored. Understanding how skeletal muscle, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat relate to listing decisions could contribute to a more objective candidate selection process. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between CT-derived body composition metrics and transplant committee decisions in a cohort of patients evaluated for lung transplantation at a single, high-volume academic center.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent and was conducted in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Study design

We performed a retrospective cohort study that included adult patients evaluated for lung transplantation at a single academic institution between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2024. Patients were identified through institutional transplant evaluation records. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent transplant evaluation and had available abdominal CT imaging within the year prior to their final committee review. Patients were excluded if they withdrew from evaluation, did not have a final committee decision available, deemed too early for transplant, were being considered for multi-organ transplant, had a prior lung transplant, did not have abdominal CT imaging available, or failed body composition analysis. The final cohort comprised 704 patients (see Figure 1).Figure 1Consort diagram.

Consort diagram.

Body composition analysis

Abdominal CT exams obtained within 1 year before the final committee review were analyzed using an automated workflow at the L3 vertebral level.16 The majority of examinations were contrast-enhanced CT angiography studies obtained as part of routine pre-transplant evaluation for surgical planning. When these studies were unavailable, the closest available clinically obtained abdominal CT exams, including non-contrast exams and abdominal CT exams acquired at outside institutions, were analyzed. The automated body composition algorithm has previously been validated across both contrast-enhanced and non-contrast CT acquisition protocols.16 Raw cross-sectional areas (cm2) were quantified for skeletal muscle, subcutaneous fat, and visceral fat. Body composition metrics were converted to normalized z-scores using age, sex, and race-based (race -specific) or age and sex-based (race-neutral) institutional reference values (unpublished). CT-derived body composition measurements were generated retrospectively for research purposes and were not available to the multidisciplinary transplant committee during candidate evaluation. These measurements were not incorporated into transplant candidacy decisions or used as formal selection criteria.

Clinical and demographic data collection

Demographic and clinical variables were extracted from the electronic medical record, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI at time of CT scan and final committee review, native lung disease, prior smoking history, and prior chest surgery. Pulmonary function testing, oxygen requirement with exertion, laboratory values, the Psychosocial Assessment of Candidates for Transplantation (PACT) score and 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) were extracted closest in time to final transplant committee review. Transplant evaluation outcomes were categorized based on committee decisions as either approved (waitlisted or transplanted) or denied.

Barriers to transplant were identified through manual chart review of clinical documentation and transplant committee notes. Risk factors were adapted from the ISHLT guidelines, such as cardiovascular disease, frailty/deconditioning, esophageal dysfunction, renal dysfunction, psychosocial barriers. Each risk factor was categorized using a three-level system: 0 if not present or not documented, 1 if present but not explicitly cited as a reason for denial, 2 if documented as a reason for denial. These variables were used primarily for descriptive analyses of documented contributors to decision-making rather than as definitive causal factors.

Statistical analyses

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using means with standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Differences in body composition across BMI categories were assessed using ANOVA with Tukey post hoc comparisons. Univariate associations between candidate variables and transplant approval were assessed using logistic regression. For categorical variables, overall significance was evaluated using likelihood ratio testing.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to identify independent predictors of transplant approval. Candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance and univariate associations. Model 1 included unadjusted body composition metrics, age, sex, race, native lung group, 6MWD, mean pulmonary artery pressure, glomerular filtration rate, albumin, BMI category, and PACT score. Model 2 included the same covariates but incorporated race-specific body composition z-scores in place of unadjusted measurements. Model 3 included the same covariates as Model 1 but utilized race-neutral body composition z-scores. Statistical analyses were performed by A.L. using Microsoft Excel and RStudio. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Univariate Associations With Transplant Approval

In the primary multivariate logistic regression model, greater skeletal muscle area was significantly associated with increased odds of transplant approval (per 50 cm2: OR 2.969, 95% CI 1.728-5.321, p<0.001) when adjusting for age, sex, race, native lung disease group, BMI group, PACT score, 6MWD, mean pulmonary artery pressure, GFR, and albumin (Figure 4). Similar multivariable testing was performed using normalized body composition measures (Table S2). In a model incorporating body composition z-scores normalized for age, sex, and race, skeletal muscle remained significantly associated with transplant approval (OR 1.689, 95% CI 1.228-2.347, p=0.001). Sex was no longer significantly associated with approval, while age (p<0.001) and race (p=0.001) remained significantly associated with transplant approval. In a model incorporating body composition z-scores normalized for age and sex, skeletal muscle remained significantly associated with transplant approval as well (OR 1.748, 95% CI 1.260-2.451, p<0.001). Similarly, sex was not significantly associated with approval whereas age (p<0.001) and race (p<0.001) were associated with transplant approval.

Model discrimination was similar across all models, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.893, 0.895, and 0.899 for models 1-3, respectively.

Skeletal muscle area was significantly lower among patients described as frail/deconditioned compared to patients not described as frail/deconditioned (p<0.001, Figure 5A). Discussion

In this single-center retrospective study of 704 patients evaluated for lung transplantation, increased CT-derived skeletal muscle area measured at the third lumbar vertebral level was independently associated with transplant approval across all multivariable models, while subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, and BMI category were not. These findings suggest that skeletal muscle mass, rather than overall body habitus or adiposity, is the body composition metric most strongly associated with transplant committee approval in our cohort. Because CT-derived skeletal muscle measurements were generated retrospectively, our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating that skeletal muscle directly influenced decision-making. Rather, CT-derived muscle quantification may reflect elements of clinical fitness that BMI alone cannot and could serve as an objective marker of the clinical gestalt for frailty assessment used in transplant evaluation.

Our finding that greater skeletal muscle area is associated with transplant approval is consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating the prognostic importance of muscle mass in lung transplantation. Weig et al. showed that lean psoas area was significantly associated with duration of mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy requirement, and ICU length of stay after transplant, whereas BMI was not.20 Furthermore, Kelm et al. found that low muscle index was associated with a nearly 4-fold increase in post-transplant mortality.21 We have also seen that skeletal muscle area is associated with textbook outcome and 1-year survival at our institution (unpublished). While these studies have focused on post-transplant outcomes, our study expands this paradigm by demonstrating that skeletal muscle mass is also associated with the candidacy decision itself. This suggests that muscle mass may be capturing physiological reserve that is implicitly assessed by transplant committees via other measures and assessments.

The persistence of the association between CT-derived skeletal muscle and transplant approval regardless of whether using unadjusted muscle area, race-specific muscle z-scores, and race-neutral muscle z-scores, further supports this notion. Of note, when normalized z-scores were used instead of unadjusted muscle areas, sex was no longer significantly associated with transplant approval, suggesting that adjusting for sex-based differences in muscle mass accounts for sex-based variation in transplant approval. This supports the use of adjusting skeletal muscle area by at least sex.

Transplant committees integrate objective data with multidisciplinary clinical judgment to assess candidate fitness, with particular emphasis on frailty and functional status.3, 4, 5 However, these factors are often assessed without standardized measurements and may therefore vary across institutions, including ours. In our cohort, frailty/deconditioning reflected the transplant committee’s documented language and clinical assessment rather than a formal frailty score. Frailty/deconditioning was the most frequently documented contributor to transplant denial, underscoring its importance in candidate evaluation. Notably, patients described as frail or deconditioned had significantly lower skeletal muscle area on CT, and muscle area was positively associated with 6-minute walk distance, a commonly used measure of functional capacity. Importantly, CT-derived skeletal muscle remained independently associated with transplant approval after adjustment for 6-minute walk distance, suggesting that skeletal muscle may capture aspects of physiologic reserve not fully reflected by this functional test alone. Together, these findings suggest that CT-derived skeletal muscle may provide an objective, reproducible marker that complements existing clinical assessment. Of note, many documented risk factors, including frailty, were also present among approved candidates, indicating that individual risk factors were not uniformly exclusionary but instead likely contributed to the transplant committee’s overall clinical assessment.

The finding that BMI category was not independently associated with transplant approval in multivariate analysis is notable given its prominent role in current guidelines.4 Several factors may be contributing to this result. Patients at the extremes of BMI may be less likely to be referred for transplant evaluation or may be deferred prior to formal committee review, resulting in a narrower BMI distribution within our study population. Furthermore, our cohort demonstrated a decrease in the number of patients classified as obese between time of initial CT exam and final committee review, which may impact the relationship between BMI and candidacy. In addition, BMI is an indirect measure that cannot differentiate between lean muscle and adipose tissue. Prior studies have demonstrated that BMI performs poorly in identifying excess adiposity and fails to detect sarcopenia, including sarcopenic obesity, which is common in advanced organ disease.10, 22, 23, 24 Our body composition analysis across BMI categories illustrates this limitation. While skeletal muscle, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat all increased in a stepwise fashion across BMI groups, the overlap between categories suggests that patients with the same BMI may have markedly different underlying body composition profiles that influence candidacy.

From a practical standpoint, CT-derived body composition analysis could be implemented opportunistically rather than through additional imaging. Most transplant candidates undergo some form of cross-sectional imaging during their evaluation for surgical planning or assessment of underlying disease, which would allow automated body composition analysis to be performed without additional radiation exposure or cost of additional imaging. Chest CT examinations, which are routinely obtained during evaluation, frequently include the upper abdomen and future studies could help determine whether they provide comparable body composition information.

A key clinical implication of our findings is that skeletal muscle mass may represent a potentially modifiable risk factor associated with transplant candidacy. Exercise and prehabilitation programs have demonstrated the ability to improve or maintain functional capacity in lung transplant candidates.25, 26 If CT-derived skeletal muscle area reflects the physiologic reserve that influences candidacy decisions, then targeted interventions aimed at improving muscle mass may improve a patient’s likelihood of transplant approval.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study, and candidacy decisions reflect the practices and thresholds of one transplant program. Significant variability exists across centers in candidate selection criteria, and these findings may not be generalizable to programs with different evaluation protocols or patient populations. Second, more than half of otherwise eligible transplant candidates were excluded because abdominal CT imaging was unavailable within 1 year prior to the committee decision. At our institution, abdominal CT imaging is not routinely obtained in younger candidates and imaging practices vary based on clinical indication. Consequently, the study cohort may not fully represent the broader lung transplant evaluation population, limiting generalizability and introducing potential selection bias. Third, the study period (2019 to 2024) overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which transplant practices underwent substantial changes. Pandemic-era disruptions such as delayed referrals, decreased transplant activity, reduced rehabilitation access, and shifting healthcare resource utilization may have influenced both the composition of the study cohort and committee decision-making.27, 28, 29 While the pandemic’s effects were not explicitly modeled, these factors represent a potential source of confounding that should be considered when interpreting the study findings. Fourth, body composition was assessed at a single time point from CT scans obtained within 1 year of committee review, and temporal changes in body composition were not captured. Preliminary data from our institution suggest that CT-derived body composition measures are relatively stable over time in the general population (unpublished data). However, rates of change may be accelerated in patients with advanced lung disease. Fifth, although skeletal muscle was associated with frailty and 6-minute walk distance, this study was not designed to determine whether CT-derived skeletal muscle provides independent information beyond established frailty assessments. Standardized frailty measures were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort, limiting our ability to directly compare skeletal muscle with existing frailty metrics. Future studies should evaluate the relative and incremental contributions of CT-derived body composition metrics and formal frailty assessments to transplant candidate selection. Lastly, identifying barriers to transplant relied on manual chart review, which is subject to variability in documentation variability and interpretation.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort study, CT-derived skeletal muscle area was associated with lung transplant approval, whereas BMI and adiposity metrics were not. These findings suggest that skeletal muscle may reflect aspects of physiologic reserve considered during transplant candidate evaluation and provide complementary objective information beyond BMI. Additional multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings across diverse institutional settings and to determine whether CT-derived skeletal muscle provides incremental value beyond established clinical assessments. Longitudinal studies evaluating changes in body composition throughout the transplant evaluation process may further clarify its role as a biomarker of transplant candidacy.
