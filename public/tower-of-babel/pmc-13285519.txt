Childhood adversity, benevolent experiences, and perceived stress among pregnant women in Mexico: psychosocial determinants of maternal well-being in a middle-income setting
Authors: Yolanda Palacios-Fong, Nancy de los Ángeles Segura-Azuara, Daniela León-Rojas, Nissa Yaing Torres-Soto, Oscar Manuel Chávez-López, Fabiola Castorena-Torres, Julieta Rodríguez-de Ita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are strongly associated with poor physical and mental health across the life course. Maternal ACEs have been linked to prenatal stress, hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, shorter gestation, lower infant birth weight, and increased risk of prenatal and postpartum depression. Benevolent childhood experiences (BCEs) and social support may buffer these effects; however, ACE-informed perinatal care remains uncommon in low- and middle-income settings. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of ACEs and BCEs among pregnant women in Mexico and to examine their associations with perceived stress and depressive symptoms during pregnancy.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional observational study at a large public maternity hospital (Hospital Materno Infantil y de Especialidades de Nuevo León) in Monterrey, Mexico, between September 2023 and March 2024. Pregnant women receiving prenatal care were invited to participate; of 400 eligible women, 368 consented, and 330 provided complete data for analysis. Participants completed the WHO Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ), the Benevolent Childhood Experiences (BCE) Scale, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), and the Social Support Questionnaire (SSQ-6). Sociodemographic and pregnancy-related variables were self-reported. Descriptive statistics summarized sample characteristics. Bivariate associations were assessed using chi-square tests and Spearman correlations. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to evaluate hypothesized pathways linking ACEs, BCEs, social support, perceived stress, and depressive symptoms. Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and χ²/df.ResultsOverall, 90% of participants reported at least one ACE, and 45.2% reported four or more ACEs. The most prevalent ACE domains were violence- and neglect-related adversities. Social support levels were moderate to high, and BCE scores were high (mean = 8.55). Perceived stress levels were moderate (mean = 17.87), and 13% of participants screened positive for depressive symptoms. SEM analyses indicated that higher ACE exposure was associated with lower benevolence and social support and with increased perceived stress. Both benevolence and social support were associated with lower stress and fewer depressive symptoms, with perceived stress acting as a central mediator. Overall model fit was acceptable (CFI = 0.93; RMSEA = 0.06).ConclusionsPregnant women in this public maternity hospital sample experienced high levels of childhood adversity alongside substantial protective factors. ACE exposure was associated with increased perceived stress and depressive symptoms during pregnancy, while benevolent childhood experiences and social support appeared to mitigate these associations. These findings underscore the importance of integrating trauma- and resilience-informed approaches into prenatal care in low- and middle-income settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09180-4.

Background

Extensive evidence demonstrates a clear dose–response relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and negative outcomes during a lifetime, including multimorbidity, mental health problems, pregnancy complications, and poor infant health [1–4]. Meta-analytic data indicate that each additional ACE increases the odds of multimorbidity by approximately 12.9% [95% confidence interval (CI) = 7.9%–17.9%], underscoring the profound cumulative impact of early adversity on a person’s long-term physical and psychological well-being [5]. Importantly, increasing evidence shows that the effects of ACEs extend into the perinatal period, in which higher ACE exposure is associated with elevated risks of prenatal stress, hypertensive disorders, preterm birth, low birth weight, and impaired early childhood development. The proposed biological mechanisms include dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, immune and neuroendocrine alterations, and epigenetic modifications that accelerate cellular aging [6–8].

In the perinatal context, maternal ACEs have been consistently linked to higher risks of gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, abnormal gestational weight gain, and poor mental health during pregnancy [9]. The associations are particularly strong among women reporting four or more ACEs [9–11]. Prospective data further show that ACEs contribute to adverse birth outcomes, such as shortened gestation and lower infant birth weight, through direct biological effects, health-risk behaviors (e.g., prenatal smoking), and psychiatric comorbidities. These findings show that ACEs are a life-course determinant of reproductive health, underscoring the need to integrate adversity-informed approaches within prenatal and inter-conceptional care models [12].

From a mental-health perspective, maternal ACEs are significantly correlated with prenatal and postpartum depression (r = 0.19 and r = 0.23, respectively) and prenatal anxiety (r = 0.14), with stronger associations when depressive symptoms are measured later in the postpartum period (≤ 12 months) [13]. These outcomes reflect the cumulative allostatic load produced by early adversity, resulting in neurobiological vulnerabilities that interact with the neuroendocrine and psychosocial demands of pregnancy [14]. Given that approximately 46% of women in prenatal care report at least one ACE [15], trauma-informed perinatal care is essential not only to detect risk but to provide continuous support for mood and anxiety regulation throughout the postpartum period, thereby preventing intergenerational effects on child health [16].

Importantly, resilience and social support have emerged as key protective factors. Resilience significantly attenuates the relationship between ACEs and perinatal depression, anxiety, and exposure to intimate partner violence [17]. Women with high resilience show similar rates of anxiety regardless of ACE exposure, but those with low resilience exhibit a nearly three-fold higher prevalence of anxiety and depression [17]. Similarly, social support during pregnancy buffers the effect of ACEs on fetal development, promoting healthier birth outcomes, although this effect diminishes at the highest levels of adversity. Social connections may mitigate HPA-axis dysregulation and reduce fetal cortisol exposure, indicating that the sense of belonging and tangible aid are targets that can be modified to promote maternal–infant health [18].

Parallel research on Benevolent Childhood Experiences (BCEs) provides a complementary framework for understanding resilience. Higher BCE scores predict lower prenatal post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms and reduced exposure to stressful life events, even after accounting for ACEs [19]. Having ≥ 5 BCEs appears to be with attention to cultural and contextual variability a threshold for adaptive functioning, suggesting that positive early experiences can partly offset the effects of adversity [19]. Therefore, the BCE scale, designed with attention to cultural and contextual variability, offers a resilience-based approach to perinatal screening, analogous to universal ACE assessment but oriented toward identifying promotive influences that enhance maternal adaptation and intergenerational well-being [19].

A nationally representative study in Mexico found that most adults reported at least one ACE, with physical and emotional neglect most common, findings that have critical implications [20]. Although the prevalence of depression remains high (33.8%), positive caregiving experiences, such as feeling safe with a caregiver (reported by 89.6% of the participants), can help build resilience [20]. Together, these findings show the dual importance of addressing adversity and reinforcing protective factors in public health strategies, particularly in regions marked by social inequality and violence [21].

Despite robust evidence linking ACEs to poor maternal and infant outcomes, standardized interventions remain limited. Current ACE screening practices do not yet meet established population-screening criteria, so concerns remain regarding the ethics and safety of trauma inquiry without appropriate follow-up resources [22]. Nonetheless, recent studies demonstrated that ACE screening was feasible and acceptable within prenatal care when combined with provider training and clear referral pathways [15].

Overall, the literature has established that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are strong predictors of maternal mental and physical health and are associated with pregnancy complications and adverse child developmental outcomes [23]. However, resilience-promoting factors, such as benevolent childhood experiences (BCEs) and social support, may mitigate these effects and support trauma-informed, strength-based approaches to care. Despite growing interest in these constructs, there is limited evidence examining their combined relationships during pregnancy, particularly in low- and middle-income settings.

Within this context, the present study aimed to estimate the prevalence of ACEs and BCEs among pregnant women in Mexico and to assess their associations with perceived stress and depressive symptoms during pregnancy.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional observational study was conducted in Monterrey, Nuevo León, Mexico from September 2023 to March 2024 to evaluate the prevalence of childhood adversity among pregnant women receiving prenatal care at the Hospital Materno Infantil y de Especialidades de Nuevo León, a tertiary academic medical center in Northeastern Mexico. This maternity clinic is a referral-based service provided by the social health system and includes obstetricians, social workers, mental-health consultants, and a violence-prevention program. New patients are referred to the clinic by their family doctor from local health centers. At approximately 18–20 weeks, patients are directed by their local health centers to participate in a low-risk maternity consultation. If any complications arise during their pregnancy, they are referred by the local health centers to appropriate medical professionals for a high-risk consultation at any time. The prenatal care program serves an annual population of approximately 10,000 patients.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Maternal and Child Hospital and Specialized Services of Nuevo León, Secretariat of Health of Nuevo León, Mexico (Protocol No. DEISC-PR-19-01-23-019).

Participants

Pregnant women in prenatal care at the Hospital Materno Infantil y de Especialidades de Nuevo León.

Inclusion, exclusion, and elimination criteria

Patients were eligible to participate if they were pregnant and able to complete the questionnaires. Patients with missing responses > 30% were excluded from the analysis. Sample-size calculation

The sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of at least one ACE among pregnant women. Based on previous studies reporting a prevalence of approximately 60% [13], a single-proportion formula with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error was used. The minimum required sample size was 294 participants. Anticipating up to 20% attrition due to refusals or incomplete questionnaires, we targeted a final recruitment of 368 women.

Materials

This study included the following instruments:

Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ)

The WHO Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) [24], which includes 43 items covering 13 domains (e.g., abuse, neglect, household dysfunction, community and collective violence), was used to assess childhood adversity. Scores range from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating greater adversity. The ACE-IQ has demonstrated acceptable reliability in Latin American populations (Cronbach’s α = 0.69–0.90) [25–27].

The “collective violence” domain refers to exposure to organized or group-based violence (e.g., armed conflict or group-related violence), whereas “community violence” refers to interpersonal violence occurring within the community. Additionally, “contact sexual abuse” refers specifically to unwanted sexual touching or physical sexual contact, distinguishing it from non-contact forms of sexual abuse.

Sociodemographic variables

Sociodemographic information was collected through items included in the ACE-IQ questionnaire and supplementary survey questions. Variables included age, marital status, educational level, employment status, and ethnicity.

Benevolent Childhood Experiences (BCE) Scale

The 10-item BCE Scale was used to measure positive early experience. The scale items assess supportive relationships, perceived safety, and community belonging before age 18 years. Responses are dichotomized (yes/no), yielding a total score of 0–10, with higher scores indicating greater benevolence. The BCE Scale shows good reliability and predictive validity for resilience [19].

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10)

The 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) was used to evaluate perceived stress during the past month. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = very often), producing scores from 0 to 40, with higher values indicating greater stress. The Spanish version has been validated in Mexico with good internal consistency (α = 0.81–0.87) [28, 29].

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)

The 10-item Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [30] was used to measure depressive symptoms. Items are scored from 0 to 3, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30; scores ≥ 13 indicate clinically relevant depressive symptoms. The EPDS has been validated for pregnancy and postpartum populations, including Spanish-speaking samples (α ≈ 0.78) [31, 32].

Social Support Questionnaire – Short Form (SSQ-6)

The 6-item Social Support Questionnaire (SSQ-6) [33], which measures both the number of supportive persons and satisfaction with the available support, was used to assess social support. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction. The Spanish version has shown good internal consistency in perinatal samples (α ≈ 0.83) [34].

Procedures

Recruitment

For this study, 15 patients were randomly selected each day from the prenatal-care appointment list. All pregnant women were eligible except those who were unable to read or complete the scales independently. A pseudo-random number generator was used to generate random numbers (1–X), reviewed manually for duplicates, and matched to appointment-list positions. While the patients waited for their appointments, they were invited to participate in this study and asked to provide informed consent. If a patient declined, the next person on the list was approached until 15 participants per day were enrolled. This procedure was repeated daily until the required sample size (n = 368) was reached.

Data collection and analysis

Participation was voluntary and no financial compensation or other incentives where provided. After written informed consent was obtained from adult participants and from the parents or legal guardians of participants younger than 16 years, participants were directed to a private office and provided with a tablet to complete the electronic survey administered through REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based platform designed for research data capture [35, 36]. For participants younger than 16 years, verbal assent was obtained prior to survey completion. Participants were allowed to remain in the office throughout the process to ensure privacy when responding to sensitive questions. The survey required approximately 15–20 min to complete and included self-reported questionnaires and brief clinical information about the current pregnancy. A thank-you message and information on childhood adversity, local violence resources, and mental-health services were provided on the final page. The participants who expressed a need for support were referred directly to on-site mental-health consultants. No personal identifiers were collected at any stage of data collection, ensuring confidentiality.

SPSS version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software was used to perform all statistical analysis of the data. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, and standard deviations) were calculated to summarize sample characteristics and study variables. Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables, and Spearman’s rho was used to assess correlations among non-normally distributed continuous variables, where appropriate. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using EQS version 6.1 (Multivariate Software, Inc., Encino, CA, USA) to evaluate the hypothesized relationships among adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), benevolent childhood experiences (BCEs), social support, perceived stress, and depressive symptoms.

Model fit was assessed using standard indices, including the Bentler–Bonett Normed Fit Index (NFI), Bentler–Bonett Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the relative chi-square (χ²/df). Values above 0.90 for NFI, NNFI, and CFI were considered indicative of acceptable model fit, whereas RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 and χ²/df values < 5 were considered indicative of adequate fit [37–39].

Given the sample size and model complexity, an item parceling strategy was implemented to reduce the number of estimated parameters and improve model stability [40]. Parcel construction followed a theoretically and empirically informed approach, avoiding arbitrary aggregation of items. Specifically, items were grouped based on their conceptual similarity within each construct and supported by factor loadings obtained from preliminary analyses. Prior to parceling, the unidimensionality (or essential unidimensionality) of each construct was evaluated, ensuring that items reflected a single dominant latent factor before aggregation.

The Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-Q) was selected for structural equation modeling due to its parsimonious structure and suitability for the available sample size [1]. The ACE-Q is a 10-item self-report measure of childhood adversity (range 0–10), covering abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, with acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.70–0.76) [41, 42]. Consistent with its theoretical framework, items were conceptually grouped into these three domains, which were used as indicators of the latent ACE construct.

To reduce model complexity and improve parameter stability, three parcels were created for each latent variable. For ACEs, parcels corresponded directly to the ACE-Q domains (abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction). For the remaining constructs, parcels were generated using an item-to-construct balance approach, ensuring a comparable number of items and similar distribution of factor loadings across parcels. Accordingly, parcels for benevolent childhood experiences were defined as BEN1 (items 1–3), BEN2 (items 4–6), and BEN3 (items 7–10); for social support as SS1 (items 1–2), SS2 (items 3–4), and SS3 (items 5–6); for perceived stress as PS1 (items 1–3), PS2 (items 4–6), and PS3 (items 7–10); and for depressive symptoms as DEP1 (items 1–3), DEP2 (items 4–6), and DEP3 (items 7–10). Each parcel was computed as the mean of its constituent items and used as an observed indicator of the corresponding latent construct.

Results

Participant recruitment and sample derivation

A total of 400 patients were invited to participate, among whom 30 declined. Two additional patients were excluded due to ineligibility (one unable to read and one who had undergone a recent abortion). Among the 368 patients who consented to participate, 30 (8%) were excluded for having > 30% of missing responses, resulting in a final analytical sample of 330 participants.

Thirty-six participants (12%) reported birthdates corresponding to the date of the interview; these values were considered invalid and treated as missing in subsequent analyses. The standardized path coefficients indicated that ACEs were negatively associated with social support (λ = − 0.39) and benevolence (λ = − 0.28) and positively associated with perceived stress (λ = 0.28). Benevolence was negatively associated with perceived stress (λ = − 0.26) and depressive symptoms (λ = − 0.29). Social support was negatively associated with perceived stress (λ = − 0.32), whereas perceived stress was positively associated with depressive symptoms (λ = 0.46).

Depressive symptoms were modeled as a latent variable with three parcel indicators (DEP1–DEP3), with factor loadings of 0.72, 0.75, and 0.65, respectively.

The model demonstrated acceptable fit: χ² = 198.513 (df = 78), p = 0.001, χ²/df = 2.54, Bentler–Bonett Normed Fit Index = 0.90, Bentler–Bonett Non-Normed Fit Index = 0.91, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.93, and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.06.

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the psychosocial profiles of pregnant women receiving care in a public hospital in northeastern Mexico, revealing a substantial burden of ACEs alongside notable protective factors, including BCEs and perceived social support. Together, these findings contribute to a growing body of evidence highlighting the complexity of risk and resilience processes influencing maternal mental health during pregnancy.

Interpretation of the main findings

Participation in the study was high, and refusal and exclusion rates were within acceptable methodological standards, supporting the reliability of the collected data. The sample was predominantly young, consistent with demographic patterns observed in public healthcare settings in Mexico.

Overall, the prevalence of ACEs in this sample was notably high, with nearly all participants reporting at least one exposure and almost half reporting four or more. Violence- and neglect-related adversities—particularly community violence, physical neglect, and household violence—were among the most frequently reported. These findings are clinically relevant, as such exposures have been consistently associated with increased psychosocial vulnerability during pregnancy.

Despite the pervasive exposure to adversity, the participants demonstrated meaningful resilience. Their BCE scores were high, with many women reporting stable relationships, perceived safety, and emotional warmth during childhood. The levels of perceived social support were also substantial, suggesting that relational resources may buffer the effects of cumulative adversity. Psychological measures indicated moderate levels of perceived stress and 13% prevalence of depressive symptoms, consistent with global estimates of antenatal depression.

The structural equation model offered a nuanced understanding of these relationships. ACEs were associated with reduced benevolence and social support and increased perceived stress, whereas benevolence and social support each independently reduced stress and depressive symptoms. A key mediator linking early adversity to depression was found to be perceived stress. The model showed good fit indices, supporting its conceptual robustness and highlighting the central role of both risk and protective factors in shaping maternal psychological outcomes.

Comparison with previous research

The high prevalence of ACEs in this cohort aligns with national data from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography, which indicate that a majority of Mexican women have experienced violence at some point in their lives [43]. The present findings further suggest that such violence often originates early in life, a pattern consistent with literature linking structural and community-level adversity to early trauma exposure. International studies conducted in prenatal care settings also report substantial rates of early adversity; however, the prevalence observed in this study exceeded the approximately 46% reported in Canadian and U.S. cohorts [13]. This difference may be partly explained by variations in measurement instruments, as the ACE-IQ captures a broader range of experiences—including community and collective violence—compared with shorter tools commonly used in high-income settings.

The sociodemographic patterns observed in this study, including young maternal age, limited formal employment, and high rates of cohabitation, mirror those reported in other Latin American perinatal populations. These characteristics have been associated with elevated stress and reduced access to psychosocial resources [44, 45]. Although ethnicity was assessed, a substantial proportion of participants declined to respond, limiting comparability with studies that examine ethnic disparities in maternal health. The rates of depressive symptoms was consistent with global antenatal depression estimates [46–49] and with prior findings from Mexico [50].

The coexistence of adversity and benevolence observed in this cohort aligns with emerging evidence indicating that benevolent childhood experiences are not merely the absence of adversity but instead constitute independent resilience factors that can mitigate the psychological effects of early trauma [19]. Similarly, the protective role of social support observed in this study is consistent with extensive literature demonstrating its buffering effect on prenatal stress and depressive symptoms [51, 52]. The structural pathways identified in the present study are also consistent with established models linking ACE exposure to stress dysregulation and adverse perinatal mental-health outcomes.

These findings highlight the importance of using context-sensitive instruments when assessing ACEs in low- and middle-income settings, where exposure to community-level violence may be more prevalent.

Practical and policy implications

The high burden of childhood adversity documented among pregnant women in this setting highlights the urgent need to incorporate trauma-informed approaches into routine prenatal care. Systematic screening for ACEs, BCEs, and psychosocial needs when paired with accessible referrals to mental health and social services may enhance early identification of women at risk for adverse perinatal outcomes. Interventions that build social support, such as partner-inclusive prenatal programs, peer-support groups, and culturally grounded psychoeducational initiatives, may strengthen resilience processes and reduce stress and depressive symptoms during pregnancy.

At the policy level, the study findings underscore the necessity of addressing structural determinants of adversity, including community violence, gender inequality, and economic vulnerability. Integrating perinatal mental health into national maternal- and child-health strategies, expanding community-based mental-health services, and strengthening violence-prevention policies may contribute to improved maternal well-being and reduced intergenerational transmission of trauma. Additionally, the challenges encountered with demographic items, such as ethnicity, highlight the importance of culturally adapted and contextually meaningful measurement tools for Latin American populations.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was its use of comprehensive validated instruments, including the WHO ACE-IQ and standardized measures of stress, depression, benevolence, and social support, to examine risk and resilience factors within a large sample of pregnant women. The use of SEMs provides valuable insights into the pathways through which early adversity influences perinatal mental health.

However, the study’s limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and all measures were self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability biases. Missing age data and nonresponses on the ethnicity item also suggest areas for methodological improvement in future research. The high proportion of nonresponse to the ethnicity question (43%) may reflect sociocultural factors in Mexico, including the historical influence of the ideology of mestizaje, which has been described as promoting a “raceless” national identity and may contribute to ambiguity or reluctance in self-identification [53]. This limitation restricted the ability to examine potential disparities related to ethnic background and may introduce bias. The findings were also obtained in a single public urban hospital setting, which may limit generalizability to private-sector or rural populations.

Conclusion

The study findings revealed a complex interplay between high levels of childhood adversity and meaningful protective factors among pregnant women in Mexico. We found that ACEs were strongly associated with increased stress and depressive symptoms, whereas benevolence and social support emerged as key resilience mechanisms that may mitigate these effects. These findings underscore the importance of integrating trauma-informed and resilience-focused approaches into prenatal care and highlight the need for broader policy efforts to address the structural determinants of adversity. A critical finding was that strengthening protective factors during pregnancy should improve maternal mental health and promote healthier developmental trajectories for the next generation.
