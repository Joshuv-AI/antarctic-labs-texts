Exoscopic resection of tentorial meningiomas: Technical note from the exoscope era
Authors: Noriyuki Kijima, Haruhiko Kishima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Tentorial meningioma resection is among the most challenging neurosurgical procedures. Various surgical approaches must be considered, including subtemporal, lateral suboccipital, occipital interhemispheric supratentorial, and supracerebellar transtentorial (SCTT) techniques. However, performing tentorial meningioma resections in the SCTT approach using operative microscopy often requires awkward positioning for the neurosurgeon. In this study, we report two cases of tentorial meningioma resection and discuss the utility and limitations of the 4KHD 3D operating room layout, showing the use of the exoscope (ORBEYE) system for this procedure.Methods: At our institution, we use the ORBEYE system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for resecting tentorial meningioma. Peri-embolization and perioperative clinical and surgical data were retrospectively analyzed.Results: Our preferred exoscope system not only enhances visualization during neurosurgical procedures but also improves surgeon ergonomics during surgery.Conclusion: Exoscopic surgery using ORBEYE may help simplify complex neurosurgical procedures, including the resection of surgically challenging lesions such as tentorial meningiomas, although limitations remain in the management of large tumors.

INTRODUCTION

Tentorial meningiomas constitute 3–6% of intracranial meningiomas,[4,7] but their resection is a challenging neurosurgical procedure owing to their deep location within the cerebrum or cerebellum and their complex vascular supply.

Depending on tumor size and precise location along the tentorium, various approaches may be considered to safely achieve resection, including subtemporal, lateral suboccipital, or occipital interhemispheric supratentorial approaches for tumors with supratentorial extension.[12,16,19]

In addition, a previous report described the use of the supracerebellar transtentorial (SCTT) approach to avoid the risk of postoperative visual field deficits associated with occipital lobe retraction.[8] The SCTT approach has also been employed in the resection of tentorial meningiomas with superior extension, as well as for anterior medial temporal lesions, including vascular malformations and epileptogenic foci.[14,21,23,24] However, performing SCTT-based resections with an operative microscope often requires awkward positioning for the neurosurgeon.

Although the operative microscope has advanced neurosurgical visualization, the recently developed exoscope system, operating room layout showing use of the exoscope (ORBEYE) (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), improves both visualization and ergonomics during surgery.[1,9,11,13,15,18] At our institution, ORBEYE is routinely used for most neurosurgical procedures. It is expected to be particularly advantageous in the SCTT approach for tentorial meningioma resection.

In this study, we report two cases of tentorial meningioma resection using ORBEYE and discuss the utility and limitations of the high-definition (4K-HD 3D) exoscope system in this context.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient characteristics

We performed two tentorial meningioma resections using ORBEYE. Peri-embolization and perioperative clinical and surgical data were retrospectively analyzed. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Osaka University Graduate School of Medicine (Approval number: 14231-6) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion.

Case 1

Clinical background

A 32-year-old woman presented to our neurosurgical department with a tentorial meningioma. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a right tentorial meningioma that showed homogeneous gadolinium (Gd) enhancement with slight peripheral edema on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging [Figure 1a-c]. The tumor extended upward toward the right occipital lobe, and the presence of a dural tail sign supported the diagnosis of tentorial meningioma. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) demonstrated that the tumor was supplied by the dural branch of the superior cerebellar artery (SCA) [Figure 1d]. The patient was asymptomatic at presentation; therefore, we initially opted for observation. However, follow-up MRI after 7 years revealed gradual tumor growth, prompting a decision to proceed with surgical resection preceded by embolization of the feeding arteries.

(a-c) Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging and (d) digital subtraction angiography images from Case 1.

Preoperative tumor embolization procedure

A right transfemoral artery approach was used. A 4F Fubuki guiding sheath (Asahi Intecc, Seto, Japan) was inserted into the right vertebral artery. We then advanced a Guidepost (Tokai Medical, Kasugai, Japan) into the lower part of the basilar artery and a Defrictor Nano (Medico’s Hirata, Inc., Osaka, Japan) into the superior SCA. After confirming selective insertion of the microcatheter into the tumor feeder, we injected n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA), which resulted in the complete disappearance of the tumor stain [Figure 2a and b]. Postembolization MRI revealed a lack of Gd enhancement in the anterior portion of the tumor (20.6% of the preoperative tumor volume), consistent with tumor devascularization [Figure 2c].

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) during tumor embolization using (a) n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate, (b) postembolization DSA image, and (c) postoperative magnetic resonance imaging from Case 1.

Tumor resection procedure

Two approaches may be used to resect this tumor: the occipital supratentorial approach and the infratentorial supracerebellar approach. The occipital supratentorial approach carries a risk of occipital lobe injury due to retraction, which may result in homonymous hemianopsia.

Although most of the tumor mass was located in the supratentorial space, we anticipated that adjusting the viewing angle would allow visualization of the tumor without retraction. Therefore, we selected the infratentorial supracerebellar approach for tumor resection.

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position with the neck slightly flexed [Figure 3a]. A right suboccipital craniotomy was performed [Figure 3b], and the dura was reflected superiorly. After exposing the cerebellar surface, we gently retracted the cerebellum downward to visualize the tentorium and lower tumor margin [Figure 3c and d]. We began coagulating the tentorium at the site of tumor attachment. Embolization of the supplied arteries made it easy to detach the tumor from the tentorium. After detachment, we debulked the tumor, dissected it from the cerebellum and occipital lobe, and completed the resection. Simpson Grade I resection was achieved.

(a) Operating room layout showing use of the exoscope during resection in case 1. (b) Intraoperative image after craniotomy. (c) Tentorial incision performed with a monopolar device. Yellow arrow indicates the tentorium. (d) Tumor visualized through the tentorial incision using the supracerebellar transtentorial approach. “Tm” indicates tumor.

The supratentorial component of the tumor was easily visualized by adjusting the camera angle of the exoscope, which may have been difficult using an operative microscope. In addition, perioperative embolization proved useful, as tumor debulking was facilitated, particularly in the anterior portion, which had been devascularized during preoperative embolization [Video 1].

Intraoperative video of Case 1.

Postoperative course

The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged with a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score of 100. Postoperative MRI revealed no enhanced lesions [Figure 4a-c]. Histopathological examination confirmed a transitional meningioma (World Health Organization Grade I).

(a) Postoperative computed tomography and (b and c) magnetic resonance imaging images from Case 1 demonstrating complete tumor resection.

Case 2

Clinical background

A 57-year-old woman with a tentorial meningioma was referred to our department. She presented with acalculia and hemianopia at the time of evaluation. MRI revealed a left tentorial meningioma with a maximum diameter of 60 mm. The tumor showed homogeneous Gd enhancement with slight peripheral edema on FLAIR imaging [Figure 5ad]. The tumor extended upward toward the left lateral ventricle. DSA demonstrated tumor supply from the middle meningeal artery (MMA) and the dural branch of the SCA [Figure 5e-f]. Since the tumor is large upward, we decided to embolize the tumor’s vessels first and to detach the tumor by ORBEYE and resect the tumor as much as possible by subtemporal approach to minimize brain damage. Any residual tumor will then be resected via a transcortical approach.

(a-d)Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging and (e and f) DSA images from Case 2. Red arrows indicate feeders from the SCA (e) and MMA (f). DSA: Digital subtraction angiography, MMA: Middle meningeal artery, SCA: Superior cerebellar artery.

Preoperative tumor embolization procedure

A right transfemoral artery approach was used. A 4F Fubuki guiding sheath (Asahi Intecc) was inserted into the right vertebral artery. We then advanced a Guidepost (Tokai Medical) into the lower basilar artery and a Defrictor Nano (Medico’s Hirata, Inc.) into the tumor feeder from the SCA.

After confirming selective insertion of the microcatheter into the tumor feeder, we injected 20% NBCA, resulting in the complete disappearance of the tumor stain [Figure 6a]. Next, we embolized the tumor feeder from the petrosquamous branch of the MMA using 20% NBCA with the same device and confirmed complete disappearance of the tumor stain [Figure 6b]. Postembolization computed tomography showed that 5.2% of the total tumor volume was embolized.

(a and b) DSA during tumor embolization using NBCA. Feeder from the SCA (a) and feeder from the MMA (b) were embolized. (c and d) Intraoperative images from Case 2. (c) First-stage resection via the subtemporal approach. Yellow arrow indicates the tentorium. (d) Second-stage resection through the transcortical parietal approach. DSA: Digital subtraction angiography, MMA: Middle meningeal artery, NBCA: N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate, SCA: Superior cerebellar artery.

Two-stage tumor resection procedure

During the first surgery, a subtemporal approach using ORBEYE was performed. The patient was placed in the right lateral position. A left temporal craniotomy was performed, and the dura mater was reflected inferiorly. After exposing the temporal surface, we retracted the temporal lobe and visualized the tumor and tentorium. We began coagulating the tentorium at the site of tumor attachment and detached the tumor from the tentorium [Figure 6c]. The trochlear nerve was visualized and preserved. After detachment, we aimed to debulk the tumor as much as possible via the subtemporal approach; however, only the lower portion of the tumor could be resected, even after adjusting the ORBEYE camera angle.

Postoperatively, the patient developed transient sensory aphasia, right-sided hypoesthesia, left–right confusion, and right hemispatial neglect, all of which improved with corticosteroid therapy.

Two weeks after the first surgery, we performed a second operation using the transcortical parietal approach with ORBEYE. The patient was again placed in the right lateral position. A left parietal craniotomy was performed. After exposing the parietal surface, a parietal corticotomy was performed, and the tumor was visualized [Figure 6d].

The tumor was not hypervascular, likely owing to preoperative embolization and detachment performed during the initial resection. It was debulked using an ultrasonic aspirator and carefully dissected from the parietal lobe. At the base of the resection cavity, the tentorium was visualized, and complete tumor removal was achieved. As the tentorium was homogeneously thickened around the tumor, we coagulated it. Simpson Grade II resection was then performed [Video 2].

Intraoperative video of Case 2.

Postoperative course

Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and her acalculia gradually improved, although her hemianopia persisted. The patient was discharged with a KPS score of 80. Postoperative MRI revealed no residual enhancing lesions [Figure 7a-d]. Histopathological examination confirmed a World Health Organization grade I meningioma.

(a) Postoperative computed tomography and (b-d) magnetic resonance imaging images from Case 2 demonstrating complete tumor resection. MMA: Middle meningeal artery, SCA: Superior cerebellar artery.

RESULTS

Both surgical procedures were successfully completed using the 4K-HD 3D exoscope system (ORBEYE). Simpson Grade I and Grade II resections were achieved, respectively. Preoperative tumor embolization effectively devascularized the tumor, which significantly facilitated intraoperative tumor resection. The exoscope system provided superior visualization with flexible camera-angle adjustment, allowing optimal viewing without excessive brain retraction. Furthermore, the heads-up surgery improved surgeons’ ergonomics throughout the complex procedures. While Case 1 was successfully managed via single-stage supracerebellar transtentorial approach, Case 2 required a planned two-stage approach to ensure safe and maximal tumor removal while preventing procedure related complications. Postoperative MRI confirmed total resection in both patients with no residual enhanced lesions

DISCUSSION

This is the first technical case series to examine the utility of ORBEYE in the resection of tentorial meningiomas. In this preliminary experience, we demonstrated that resections could be successfully performed using the 4K-HD 3D exoscope system, ORBEYE. Resection of tentorial meningiomas is a complex neurosurgical procedure; thus, the choice of surgical approach must be carefully considered based on the tumor’s location along the tentorium and its size.

The subtemporal, lateral suboccipital, and occipital supratentorial approaches are commonly used for resecting tentorial meningiomas with supratentorial extension,[12,16,19] but these approaches carry a risk of postoperative homonymous hemianopsia.[4,16,20] A previous study described the use of the SCTT approach to avoid this risk by minimizing occipital lobe retraction.[8] The SCTT approach has also been used for tentorial meningiomas with superior extension and for anterior medial temporal lesions, including vascular malformations and epileptogenic foci.[14,21,23,24]

However, using the SCTT approach with an operative microscope requires an ergonomically challenging position for neurosurgeons. Although the operative microscope has improved neurosurgical visualization, the newly developed exoscope system enhances both visualization and surgeon ergonomics during surgery.[1,5,9,10,11,13,15,18] Recent advancements have expanded the application of exoscopic systems across a variety of complex neurosurgical pathologies. Notably, high-quality studies by Vasankari et al. have demonstrated the efficacy of the digital 3D exoscope in the resection of giant olfactory groove meningiomas, falx and parasagittal meningiomas, as well as in performing extradural anterior clinoidectomies.[3,5,22] Our present findings on tentorial meningiomas build upon this foundation, contributing to the growing body of evidence that supports the “exoscope paradigm shift” in neurosurgery. Based on these advantages, ORBEYE is expected to be useful for tentorial meningioma resection. One potential risk associated with the SCTT approach is injury to the transverse sinus, transverse–sigmoid junction, and cerebellar draining veins during transtentorial dissection. Therefore, careful tentorial incision is essential for safe SCTT performance.[8]

We also utilized preoperative tumor embolization in our cases. However, its utility remains controversial. One meta-analysis showed that preoperative embolization significantly reduced blood loss and operative duration,[6] whereas other studies reported no clear benefit in reducing intraoperative blood loss.[2,17] These discrepancies may be attributed to variation in patient selection for embolization.

In our cases, safe resection was achievable because we were able to embolize tumor feeders located deep within the cerebrum, which might have been difficult to access before tumor debulking. Therefore, in situations where coagulation of feeders is not feasible before debulking, preoperative embolization can facilitate safer resection. A combination of exoscopic visualization and preoperative tumor embolization may contribute to the safe and minimally invasive resection of tentorial meningiomas.

However, for large tentorial meningiomas, as in Case 2, staged resection is necessary for safe tumor removal, even with camera angle adjustments using ORBEYE. Resection using only a single approach, such as the subtemporal approach, is challenging for large tumors with superior extension because it requires significant temporal lobe retraction, increasing the risk of temporal lobe injury. Thus, a staged approach is preferable to achieve complete resection while minimizing procedure-related complications.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the use of ORBEYE in combination with preoperative embolization may contribute to the safe resection of tentorial meningioma, although limitations remain in the management of large tumors.

CONCLUSION

Our initial experience suggests that resection of tentorial meningiomas using an exoscope is both safe and feasible. When selecting the surgical approach, it is important to consider the ergonomic and visual advantages offered by exoscopic systems. Exoscopic surgery using ORBEYE may help simplify complex neurosurgical procedures, including the resection of surgically challenging lesions such as tentorial meningiomas.
