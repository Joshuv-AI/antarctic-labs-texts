Intimate partner violence during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Richard Gyan Aboagye, Husna Razee, Nuworza Kugbey, Blessing Jaka Akombi-Inyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate partner violence (IPV) has health and human rights implications for victim-survivors. During pregnancy, IPV is associated with several morbidities, injuries, and mortality among women and their foetuses. Identifying the prevalence and factors associated with IPV during pregnancy is crucial towards developing preventive strategies to curb its occurrence. This review estimated the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy and examined its associated factors in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsThis review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase, and CINAHL for articles published on IPV experienced during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa from January 01, 2011, to May 30, 2025. Additional literature search was conducted in Google Scholar and Google to complement the articles retrieved from the six databases. All the published articles that met the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis. Stata 18.0 was used for data analysis. Microsoft Excel was utilised for synthesising the factors associated with IPV during pregnancy.ResultsA total of 3,988 records were retrieved from the six databases, and 16 articles were identified through Google Scholar and Google literature search. Sixty-five (65) articles met the inclusion criteria and were finally included in the study. The pooled prevalence of IPV was 38.3% (95% confidence interval [CI] = 33.5 - 43.2). The prevalence of physical, sexual, and emotional violence was 15.7% (95%CI = 13.2–18.3), 18.0% (95%CI = 14.6–21.5), and 27.7% (95%CI = 23.6–31.9), respectively. The risk factors for IPV during pregnancy were substance use, unplanned pregnancy, justification of violence against women, exposure to violence during childhood, experience of controlling behaviour, past experiences of IPV and marital conflicts, rural residency, and polygyny. Women’s empowerment, social support, and education were identified as key factors that reduce women's likelihood of experiencing IPV during pregnancy.ConclusionOur study highlights the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa, emphasising the need for targeted strategies that address key risk factors. It advocates for promoting women’s empowerment, improving girls’ education, and strengthening legal frameworks to effectively prevent IPV. Ministries of Health across sub-Saharan African countries could integrate routine, confidential IPV screening into antenatal care and ensure immediate access to evidence-based interventions with clear referral pathways to supportive services to prevent further violence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02293-1.

Introduction

Violence against women is a widespread phenomenon associated with a myriad of negative health and social consequences that extend beyond individual victim-survivors [1]. It is recognised both as a significant public health issue and as a violation of women’s fundamental human rights [2, 3]. Violence against women refers to any act of violence based on gender that causes or is likely to cause physical, sexual, or mental harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether taking place in public or private life [3]. Within intimate relationships, intimate partner violence (IPV) comprises behaviours or acts that can cause physical, sexual, or psychological harm to the individuals in the relationship [4]. In this study, IPV against women includes physical, sexual, emotional or psychological violence, economic abuse, and controlling behaviours [5].

Recent evidence from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that nearly one in three women worldwide has been subjected to physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner, non-partner sexual violence, or both [6]. Sardinha et al. [5] similarily reported that 27% of ever-partnered women aged 15–49 have experienced physical or sexual violence, or both from their intimate partners. A global meta-analysis study further showed that the prevalence of physical, psychological or emotional, and sexual violence during pregnancy was 9.2%, 18.7%, and 5.5%, respectively [7]. While these studies [5, 7] employed rigorous, systematic methods to estimate the global prevalence of IPV and IPV during pregnancy using quantitative data, no further analysis was conducted to examine the risk and protective factors associated with IPV. In contrast, another synthesis of 92 global studies reported that the pooled prevalence of emotional, physical, and sexual abuse during pregnancy was 24.8%, 13.8%, and 8.0%, respectively [8]. Additionally, the study examined the risk factors associated with IPV during pregnancy; however, there was no evidence of subregional estimates specific to sub-Saharan Africa [8]. In low and lower-middle-income countries, Halim et al. [9] reported wide ranges in the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy: physical (2-35%), sexual (9-40%), and psychological violence (22-65%), underscoring substantial contextual variation. Taken together, evidence from global and low and lower-middle-income countries indicates that IPV during pregnancy is prevalent and warrants multifaceted and multisectoral interventions to prevent its occurrence.

In sub-Saharan Africa, a systematic review drawing on quantitative and qualitative data from nine studies focused on adolescents reported that the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy was 8.3% in Nigeria, 17.1% and 37.9% in Ethiopia, and 41% in South Africa [10]. Similarly, a study conducted in Africa, including thirteen studies, reported a pooled prevalence of 15.23% for IPV during pregnancy, with country-level estimates ranging from 2.3% in Nigeria to 57.1% in Uganda [11]. Shamu et al. [11] concluded that IPV during pregnancy is prevalent, while arguing that its occurrence could be influenced by factors such as partner alcohol consumption, risky sexual behaviour, lower educational level, socioeconomic status, and younger age. Collectively, the reviews by Nyemgah et al. [10] and Shamu et al. [11] suggest that IPV during pregnancy is prevalent in Africa and requires urgent attention.

Despite the review studies conducted in Africa [10, 11], a significant gap remains. Evidence specific to sub-Saharan Africa on the prevalence and risk and protective factors of IPV during pregnancy is still limited. For instance, Nyemgah et al. [10]’s study focused exclusively on IPV during adolescent pregnancy, whereas our review includes women of reproductive age (15–49 years), who are also at heightened risk of IPV. In addition, the review by Shamu et al. [11] included articles published before 2011 and estimated only the overall prevalence of IPV during pregnancy in Africa, without disaggregating by the forms of IPV. To address these gaps, we aim to provide an up-to-date estimate of IPV prevalence during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa using studies published from 2011 to 2025, and further examine the pooled prevalence of individual forms of IPV.

The present review aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals 3 and 5, which emphasise improving the health and wellbeing of pregnant women and achieving gender equality by eliminating all forms of violence [2, 5]. Understanding the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy and the associated risk and protective factors is essential for developing targeted interventions for IPV prevention and, ultimately, elimination. Our review aims (i) to estimate the prevalence of IPV during pregnancy among women of reproductive age in sub-Saharan Africa, and (ii) to examine the factors associated with IPV during pregnancy using recently published peer-reviewed studies.

Methods

This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in writing and reporting the findings [12] (Fig. 1). Prior to the commencement of the study, the protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), with reference number: CRD42024526989. The review was registered on 21 March 2024 and approved on 31 March 2024.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram showing the search and screening process of the articles

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across six electronic databases using search terms to retrieve articles published from January 2011 to May 2025. These databases include PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Embase, PsycINFO, and Web of Science. In addition, we conducted a complementary literature search in Google Scholar and Google to identify articles not indexed in the six databases used for the study. The articles extracted from the six databases and the literature search in Google Scholar and Google were exported into Covidence where duplicates were removed, and the subsequent screening workflow, including title, abstract, and full-text screening, was conducted systematically.

The combination of keywords or terms used to retrieve the articles consisted of the following:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included articles that met the following criteria: (i) focused on experiences of IPV (physical, sexual, financial, psychological/ emotional violence, and controlling behaviours) (ii) focused on pregnant women or the pregnancy period (iii) conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (iv) used an observational (cross-sectional, case-control, or cohort), interventional, and mixed-method design; (v) published in English; and (vi) published from January 01, 2011 to May 30, 2025. However, we excluded books, theses or dissertations, case studies, policy briefs, previous reviews, commentaries, and articles that used qualitative design. Also, studies with no clear data on IPV during pregnancy were excluded.

Data extraction

Screening and selection of articles exported to Covidence were conducted in three stages. First, duplicate records were removed. Next, titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria, and ineligible records were excluded. Third, full-texts were assessed for eligibility, and those that met the inclusion criteria were selected for data extraction. Two independent reviewers (RGA and BAI) conducted the screening. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached.

For studies meeting the inclusion criteria, we conducted two complementary data extraction exercises. First, we extracted data to estimate the pooled prevalence of physical, emotional, and sexual violence, as well as the overall IPV. A Microsoft Excel template was used to capture the following data from each included article: author(s), year of publication, country of publication, study design, study population, sample size, sampling method, response rate, IPV assessment tool, and form of IPV (Table 1). Quality assessment

Methodological quality was appraised using Joanna Briggs criteria for quantitative and mixed-method studies [78–82]. We assessed the appropriateness of the design, data collection procedures and instruments, data analysis, presentation of findings, and limitations. Using the Joanna Briggs criteria appraisal criteria, studies were assigned quality scores as follows: 0–8 for cross‑sectional, 0–11 for cohort, and 0-13 mixed‑methods. We classified quality as high (cross‑sectional: 7–8; cohort: 9–11; mixed‑methods: 10–13), moderate (cross‑sectional: 4–6; cohort: 6–8; mixed‑methods: 7–9), or poor (cross‑sectional: <4; cohort: <6; mixed‑methods: <7). The studies included in this review were generally of moderate to high quality (Supplementary file 1, Table 1).

Data analyses

Data were collated in Microsoft Excel and analysed using Stata software version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). To estimate the prevalence, we used the meta-analysis command in Stata to generate forest plots of IPV during pregnancy (overall and by forms of IPV), showing the study-specific estimates and weights. Additionally, heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Cochran’s Q and I2 statistic in Stata. Where heterogeneity was statistically significant, we applied a random-effect model to pool prevalence estimates. Also, we performed subgroup analyses of IPV prevalence by country, study design, and year of publication to assess the robustness of the IPV estimate and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity (Supplementary file 2). Publication bias was assessed using the Egger's test for small-study effects (Supplementary file 2). Microsoft Excel was used for synthesising the factors associated with IPV during pregnancy.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 3,988 records were retrieved from the six databases, and 16 were identified through Google Scholar and Google literature search. After screening 128 full-text articles, 65 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study. Figure 1 shows the review and selection process of the included articles.

Characteristics of included studies

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the included studies. Most studies (26; 40.0%) were conducted in Ethiopia, followed by Nigeria (12; 18.5%). One study each was retrieved from Malawi, Namibia, and Rwanda. One multi-country study covered twenty-six countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Of the 65 studies included in the quantitative analysis, 54 (83.1%), 46 (70.8%), 45 (69.2%), and 49 (75.4%) reported on physical violence, emotional or psychological violence, sexual violence, and overall IPV, respectively. Also, two studies used a mixed-methods design, two employed a prospective cohort design, and the remainder used a cross-sectional design.

Factors associated with IPV during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa

Table 2 summarises the factors associated with IPV during pregnancy identified across 35 studies in sub-Saharan Africa. Majority of the frequently reported factors associated with IPV during pregnancy were partner substance use or abuse, sexual and reproductive health issues, acceptance or justification of violence against women, exposure to violence during childhood, previous experiences of violence, socio-cultural norms, women’s empowerment indicators, availability of social support, and socio-demographic characteristics (place of residence, education, and polygyny status).

Substance use

Substance use, such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and khat chewing, was cited as a risk factor associated with women’s experience of IPV during pregnancy. Partner substance use was the predominant risk factor for IPV during pregnancy, reported by 21 studies. Of this, ten were conducted in Ethiopia [22, 29, 31–33, 38, 53, 62, 70, 73], three in Kenya [20, 26, 61], two each from Gambia [37, 52] and Uganda [25, 41], and one each from Ghana [65], Nigeria [72], and South Africa [54]. Additionally, one study found that women who abstained from alcohol consumption were less likely to experience IPV during pregnancy [70].

Unintended pregnancy

Unintended or undesired pregnancy was consistently mentioned across reviewed studies to increase women’s risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy. Six (6) studies reported this association, five from Ethiopia [17, 22, 31, 32, 63, 73] and one from Nigeria [18]. In Ethiopia, discordant fertility intentions between partners was associated with IPV during pregnancy [70], and women whose pregnancy was intended by their partner were less likely to experience IPV [55]. In South Africa, dissatisfaction with pregnancy was associated with higher risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy [54].

Acceptance of wife beating or violence

Acceptance or justification of violence against women increases the risk of IPV during pregnancy. Studies from Ethiopia [17, 22, 29] and South Africa [54] reported high risk of IPV during pregnancy among women who viewed violence against women as normal or justified.

Exposure to violence during childhood

Women and their partners who experienced or witnessed violence in childhood had elevated risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy. In Ethiopia, both women's and partners' childhood exposure (as victim-survivors or witnesses interparental violence) were associated with IPV during pregnancy [29]. Another Ethiopian study found an association for women's exposure to interparental violence and IPV during pregnancy [31]. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted in Kenya [26] and The Gambia [37].

Experience of partner controlling behaviour

In four (4) studies, women’s experiences of controlling behaviours by their partners increased their risk of becoming victim-survivors of IPV during pregnancy. Studies from Ethiopia [17, 29], Uganda [41], and Nigeria [72] reported IPV during pregnancy among women exposed to controlling behaviours from their partners.

Past experience of IPV and marital conflicts

Studies conducted in South Africa [43, 54] and Uganda [41, 68] reported that a history of or previous abuse from their partners was associated with their current experience of IPV during pregnancy. In Nigeria, living in communities with higher past-year IPV prevalence among women was associated with subsequent IPV during pregnancy [59]. Marital conflict or frequent fights also predicted IPV during pregnancy in Ethiopia [29, 55], Nigeria [72], and Uganda [68].

Women’s empowerment indicators

Across 12 studies, lower autonomy and decision-making were associated with higher risk of IPV during pregnancy. Low women’s decision-making was reported in studies from Ethiopia [22, 55, 56] to be a risk factor for IPV during pregnancy. Other empowerment indicators that increased women’s experience of IPV during pregnancy were the husband or partner as the sole decision-maker [25, 38, 71], lack of women’s autonomy [35], women’s sole decision over their income spending [59], moderate women’s household empowerment status [70], and decision-making for family planning by someone other than the women [63]. Also, the absence of shared decision-making was reported in Ethiopia to predict IPV during pregnancy [73]. Joint decision-making was reported in Ethiopia to reduce women’s likelihood of experiencing IPV during pregnancy [62].

Sociocultural beliefs

Living in communities where violence against women is regarded as a demonstration of men’s masculinity [29], where women are compelled to obey their husbands [53], and where strict gender roles [29, 55] as well as the patriarchal societal structure [72] are predominant, were cited to influence IPV during pregnancy.

Social support

Support during pregnancy was notably mentioned in the reviewed studies to influence IPV. Women with low social support [31], those with no practical financial social support and perceptions that one's family of origin would not offer support [48], poor social support [56], and lack of support from siblings and family [61] were associated with IPV during pregnancy. Conversely, availability of social support in terms of communication [48] and perceived social support from significant others [54] were reported to reduce women’s risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Socio-demographic characteristics, such as educational attainment, place of residence, and polygyny status were identified in a couple of studies to consistently predict IPV during pregnancy. Four (4) studies reported increased likelihood of IPV during pregnancy among rural residents [31, 33, 38, 73]. Women in polygamous relationships in Uganda [25], Kenya [26], and Nigeria [66] were at higher risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy. Having co-wives or a partner with other sexual partners was associated with IPV during pregnancy [22, 73], as was having other sexual partners for either spouse in Nigeria [19]. The risk of IPV during pregnancy was higher among women whose partners had no education [20, 33, 71, 72, 77] and among women who cannot read and write [56], and those with no formal education [71, 73].

Furthermore, women with primary education or higher [18, 38, 55, 70, 77] had lower likelihood of experiencing IPV during pregnancy. Also, women with educated partners were less likely to experience IPV during pregnancy [26, 59, 66]. However, one study reported higher risk of IPV during pregnancy among women with grade 1–8 education [73]. In addition, the risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy was lower among women with illiterate husbands in Ethiopia [47].

Discussion

This review estimated the pooled prevalence of IPV during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa and examined its associated factors. The overall prevalence of IPV was 38.3% (33.5 - 43.2), ranging from 7.1% (4.8–9.5) in Ethiopia to 68.7% (64.2–73.3) in Nigeria. In terms of the forms of IPV, the prevalence of physical, sexual, and emotional or psychological violence was 15.7% (13.2–18.3), 18.0% (14.6–21.5), and 27.7%% (23.6–31.9), respectively. Across the included studies, the factors consistently identified to influence IPV during pregnancy were substance use, unplanned pregnancy, justification of violence against women, exposure to violence during childhood, experience of partner controlling behaviour, past experience of IPV and marital conflicts, women’s empowerment, socio-cultural beliefs, social support, and socio-demographic characteristics (place of residence, education, and polygyny status).

Approximately four in ten women experienced IPV during pregnancy in this study, underscoring that IPV is prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa. Our finding is comparable to a systematic review and meta-analysis from Iran, which reported that about five in ten women experienced IPV during pregnancy [83]. In contrast, a previous review conducted in sub-Saharan Africa reported the overall prevalence of IPV during pregnancy to be 15.23%, spanning from 2.25% in Nigeria to 56.99% in Uganda [11]. Discrepancies in the prevalence of IPV across the studies likely reflect differences in IPV screening tools, assessment timing, and cultural context, as well as the potential effects of social, economic, and political transitions that may alter risk profiles over time [84–86]. In addition, both under-and over-reporting of IPV experience due to stigma or differences in survey privacy could have contributed to the variations in the prevalence of IPV across the settings [84, 86]. Also, a global meta-analysis study by Román-Gálvez et al. [7] reported a lower overall prevalence of IPV during pregnancy (25.0%) compared to the findings in our study, again highlighting methodological and contextual heterogeneity [86].

The subgroup analysis indicates that approximately 28%, 18%, and 16% of women experienced emotional, sexual, and physical violence during pregnancy, respectively. These findings highlight a concerning burden of IPV during a time of increased vulnerability in a woman’s life. The high prevalence of emotional violence suggests that violence during pregnancy often manifests as psychological control and coercion, either independently or in co-occurrence with physical and sexual violence. Exposure to various forms of IPV during pregnancy has serious consequences for maternal health and obstetric outcomes, including an increased risk of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, injuries, preterm birth, and low birth weight [2, 6].

The specific estimates for forms of IPV in our review exceed those reported in a global systematic review and meta-analysis study, where the prevalence of physical, psychological or emotional, and sexual violence during pregnancy was 9.2%, 18.7%, and 5.5%, respectively [7]. Similarly, James et al. [8] found lower prevalence of IPV subtypes in their study, with the prevalence of emotional, physical, and sexual abuse during pregnancy being 24.8%, 13.8%, and 8.0%, respectively. Although the forms of IPV in our study are prevalent than in previous studies [7, 8], the estimates remain alarmingly high, reinforcing the need for routine identification and response within antenatal care. A systematic and culturally sensitive approach to IPV screening, integrated within antenatal services and accompanied by clearly outlined referral pathways to psychosocial and legal support, is essential to identify victim-survivors of violence during pregnancy as a measure to prevent further violence and reduce its associated effects.

Evidence across the included studies shows that partner substance use, especially alcohol consumption, was a key risk factor for women’s experience of IPV during pregnancy. Similar findings have been reported in earlier studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [11,84], where the partner’s alcohol consumption increased the risk of IPV during pregnancy. Heavy alcohol consumption can intoxicate men, subsequently influencing their behaviour toward their wives [85–87]. Intoxication from alcohol consumption may impair judgement and understanding, hindering men's ability to interpret social cues and maintain responsible behaviour [88]. Additionally, alcohol consumption can negatively affect men’s cognitive abilities, thereby impairing communication and conflict resolution with their wives [85], and increasing the likelihood of violence perpetration. Other scholars posit that partners’ alcohol consumption may impose financial strain on the family, and that can exacerbate conflict and precipitate violence [85, 89].

Unplanned pregnancy was found to be a risk factor for IPV during pregnancy. This finding is supported by previous review, which reported that unplanned pregnancy increases IPV among women [8]. Unplanned pregnancies can create stress, uncertainty, and emotional tension within relationships, particularly when partners are not aligned in their expectations. These situations can exacerbate existing vulnerabilities or power imbalances within the relationship, increasing the likelihood of conflict and violence. Also, the male partner's failure to take responsibility for the unplanned pregnancy may result in disagreements that could lead to IPV [90]. Strengthening access to family planning and promoting shared decision-making around fertility intentions are therefore critical prevention strategies [63, 73].

Women’s justification or acceptance of violence was reported to be associated with IPV during pregnancy, consistent with findings from Arab countries [91], sub-Saharan Africa [92], and Pakistan [93]. Early exposure to interparental violence can normalise coercion and abuse, fostering acceptance of violence in adulthood [88]. For instance, Yilmer et al. [84] argued that witnessing maternal abuse may cultivate norms that view violence as a legitimate means of resolving conflicts. In sub-Saharan Africa, childhood exposure to interparental violence is associated both with the justification of violence [94] and IPV victimisation in adulthood [95], the intergenerational transmission of violence.

We also found that witnessing or experiencing violence as a child was associated with IPV during pregnancy. Evidence has shown that children’s exposure to IPV at early stages could instigate an intergenerational transmission of violence into adulthood [88, 96]. For example, exposure to violence during childhood could culminate in violence as a normative activity, which could be transferred across generations [96]. Boys who witness paternal perpetration of violence may model such behaviour, while girls may internalise victim-role norms, contributing to male perpetration and female victimisation later in their lives [96, 97]. Our findings concur with those of previous systematic reviews, which found childhood exposure to violence as a significant risk factor for IPV [91, 98].

Across the reviewed studies, women’s experience of controlling behaviours from their partners was a risk factor for IPV during pregnancy, mirroring findings from studies conducted in Spain [99], Nigeria [100], and India [101]. A pooled analysis of the Demographic and Health Surveys from sub-Saharan African countries similarly showed that controlling behaviours increase the risk physical, sexual, and emotional violence as well as multiple forms of IPV [102]. Patriarchal norms and values in sub-Saharan African countries, which encourage women’s acceptance of male domineering behaviour and power, could have accounted for this significant finding from our study [103]. It is worth noting that unequal power and gender relations make women more vulnerable and highly dependent on their partners, thereby creating an avenue for exercising controlling behaviours towards them [103]. The act of controlling women can significantly impact their sexual, reproductive and human rights negatively [104]. With partner controlling behaviour recently recognised as a form of violence, it is unsurprising that past experience of IPV was found in the included studies to be associated with IPV during pregnancy. Previous studies by James et al. [8] and Nguyen et al. [105] reported that previous IPV experience in intimate relationships was associated with recent IPV during pregnancy, indicating a continuous IPV victimisation. Breaking this cycle requires early detection and sustained supportive services.

Women’s empowerment in the form of decision-making and autonomy was identified to be associated with IPV during pregnancy. A higher risk of IPV was reported among women with low autonomy and decision-making capacity. In contrast, joint household decision-making was associated with a lower likelihood of IPV during pregnancy. Similar findings have been reported in sub-Saharan African countries [106–108]. Empowered women can initiate open communication with their partners, solve problems to reduce marital conflicts, participate actively in decision-making, and reject traditional gender norms and values inciting male dominance and violent acts to demonstrate men’s masculinity [107]. Also, women with high autonomy are more likely to be educated, and this helps to identify early signs of violence and make concerted efforts to address them [108].

We found poor or no social support as a significant risk factor for IPV during pregnancy. Our finding corroborates those of Nguyen et al. [105], who suggested that a lack of social support exposes women to experiencing IPV during pregnancy. Another study conducted in Spain found the presence of social support to reduce the risk of IPV among women [109]. The availability of social support during pregnancy aids women in having positive self-esteem, alleviates negativity towards violence, and ultimately helps to have open communication and resolve conflicts amicably [56, 110].

Socio-demographic characteristics such as level of education, place of residence, and polygyny status were reported in the reviewed studies to be associated with IPV during pregnancy. Educated women and those with educated partners were less likely to experience IPV, findings that concur with those from a review study, which reported a higher risk of IPV when either or both partners are illiterate [98]. Similar findings have been identified in another systematic review study conducted among Arab countries [91]. The lower likelihood of IPV could be due to their understanding of the consequences associated with violence, especially during pregnancy, based on their ability to read and write. With lower-level education linked to acceptance of socio-cultural norms and values such as male dominance and control in intimate relationships [98], higher education tends to enhance a deeper understanding of the dire implications of adhering to the traditional gender norms and assertions.

Rural residence, as identified in a previous systematic review of low- and middle-income countries [98] and a cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia [90], was found to influence IPV during pregnancy in our study. The increased risk of IPV during pregnancy associated with residence in rural areas may be attributed to the underlying influence of cultural values and traditions that promote tolerance and justification of violence [88]. We suggest that rural women’s difficulty in shielding their personal lives from the public, coupled with physical distance from support systems and services and patriarchal beliefs, may have accounted for the higher risk of IPV during pregnancy. Rural pregnant women often have limited access to adequate information on gender equality, women’s rights, and IPV prevention strategies [90], which may have contributed to their experiences of IPV.

Women in polygamous relationships were at higher risk of experiencing IPV during pregnancy. This finding corroborates previous studies [91, 111], indicating that women engaged in polygyny or polygamous marriages or relationships experienced IPV relative to those in monogamous relationships. Possibly, in polygamous marriages, competition for resources between the wives could have created disunity and a de-harmonised relationship between the pregnant woman and the husband, which can be a key factor for IPV perpetration. Also, weaker communication and bonds between the spouses in a polygamous relationship can fuel IPV occurrence, especially during pregnancy when women’s demands increase [111].

Implications for policy and practice

The findings from this study indicate that pregnancy does not protect women from experiencing IPV. Given the high prevalence of IPV during pregnancy, sub-Saharan African countries should prioritise comprehensive prevention and response strategies that address key risk factors across the life course. Reducing children's exposure to interparental violence could disrupt intergenerational transmission and shift norms that legitimise violence [96]. Women’s empowerment and girl-child education initiatives should be advocated for and spearheaded by governments, civil society organizations, non-governmental organisations, and human rights organizations at the sub-regional and national levels. Also, men’s involvement in designing and implementing interventions aimed at reducing violence against women is essential towards the prevention of IPV during pregnancy. Further, substance use, such as alcohol, smoking, and khat chewing, control and management programmes should be integrated into IPV prevention initiatives, considering their association with IPV perpetration from individuals who use them. Ministries of Health across sub-Saharan African countries could integrate routine, confidential IPV screening into existing antenatal care services, accompanied by provider training, evidence-based first-line support, and clear referral pathways to psychosocial, legal, and safeguarding services. Also, IPV screening tools and protocols should be culturally sensitive and aligned with best practices to ensure safety and acceptability.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s key strength lies in the search and extraction of recently published peer-reviewed articles on IPV during pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa from major electronic databases. Also, the use of independent reviewers with predefined inclusion criteria, and rigorous appraisal ensured that only quality articles were included in the study. Additionally, the meta-analysis provided precise pooled estimates of IPV during pregnancy, and the complementary narrative synthesis enriched the understanding of multilevel risk and protective factors of IPV. However, there are inherent limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity in IPV assessment tools and timing may have contributed to under- or over–reporting of IPV during pregnancy. Although most studies used the WHO's Domestic Violence Module questionnaire in measuring IPV during pregnancy, the women’s experience of IPV was based on self-reports that are susceptible to social desirability and recall bias. The predominance of cross-sectional designs limits causal inference, and some studies had small samples, suboptimal response rates, or non-probability sampling, potentially affecting precision and generalisability of the findings. Also, the exclusion of non-English studies may have introduced language bias, which could have influenced the findings in our review. Additionally, we could not estimate the pooled prevalence of controlling behaviour and economic or financial violence due to limited data availability. Hence, inferences drawn from the pooled IPV prevalence should be made with caution. Finally, the exclusion of studies with no clear data on IPV experienced during pregnancy, such as IPV in the last twelve months and lifetime IPV among pregnant women, may have influenced the number of studies included in the current review and, subsequently, the pooled estimate of IPV during pregnancy.

Conclusion

Our study has shown that four in every ten women in sub-Saharan Africa experienced IPV during pregnancy, with variation across countries. The high prevalence of IPV during pregnancy was driven by factors such as partner substance use, exposure to interparental violence, unwanted pregnancies, justification of violence, polygyny, rural residency, level of education, previous experience of violence, women’s empowerment, and inadequate social support. Our findings advocate for coordinated and comprehensive strategies that integrate preventive measures, such as the modification of societal norms, empowerment initiatives, educational programmes, and substance use policies, with responsive healthcare interventions, including routine antenatal screening, initial support services, and resilient referral networks. Additionally, strengthening legal and policy framework and ensuring effective implementations are essential to protect women during pregnancy.
