Second trimester adnexal torsion of a large ovarian mature teratoma: Diagnostic value of whirlpool sign
Authors: Inês Taborda Leal, Kristina Hundarova, Fernanda Santos, Sara Campos, Filipa Castro Coelho, Fernanda Águas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdnexal torsion is a gynecological emergency and an important cause of acute pelvic pain, occurring most frequently in women of reproductive age. In pregnancy, torsion is classically associated with ovarian stimulation and small-to-medium sized mobile adnexal masses. We report the case of a 35-year-old nulliparous woman who presented to the emergency department with acute left flank pain and vomiting at 18 weeks and 6 days of a spontaneous singleton pregnancy. Ultrasound revealed a 150 mm left adnexal mass with features suggestive of a mature cystic teratoma, including a unilocular lesion with mixed echogenicity, echogenic nodules (“cotton wool” pattern), linear echogenic interfaces (“starry sky” sign), and posterior acoustic shadowing. Importantly, a heterogeneous, vascularized nodular structure was identified adjacent to the mass, displaying the characteristic sonographic whirlpool sign, strongly suggestive of adnexal torsion. Laparoscopic left adnexectomy was performed during the second trimester, as irreversible adnexal necrosis was already established. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the pregnancy progressed to term, resulting in the delivery of a healthy newborn. This case highlights the diagnostic value of meticulous ultrasound assessment and in particular, the recognition of the whirlpool sign as a key sonographic marker of adnexal torsion. The presence of this sign enabled prompt diagnosis and timely surgical management, demonstrating that adnexal torsion should remain a diagnostic consideration even in spontaneously conceived pregnancies with large mature cystic teratomas during the second trimester.Keywords: Adnexal torsion, Pregnancy, Ultrasound, Mature teratoma, Whirlpool sign

Introduction

Adnexal torsion is a gynecological emergency characterized by partial or complete rotation of the ovary and/or fallopian tube on its vascular pedicle, leading to venous and lymphatic congestion and, ultimately, arterial compromise and ischemic necrosis if not promptly treated [1,2]. It accounts for a small but significant proportion of acute pelvic pain presentations in women of reproductive age and is typically associated with the presence of an underlying adnexal mass [3].

Pregnancy represents a specific risk context for adnexal torsion, with an estimated incidence higher than in the nonpregnant population and a peak in the first trimester [4,5]. Functional cysts and corpus luteum are the most commonly identified adnexal lesions in early pregnancy, whereas mature cystic teratomas constitute a smaller subset [3,6,7]. Torsion in pregnant women is frequently described after ovarian stimulation and assisted reproductive techniques, and it more often involves small to moderately sized, mobile adnexal masses. In contrast, very large lesions that become relatively fixed as the uterus enlarges are considered to have a lower torsion risk [1,4,5,8,9].

Ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating adnexal masses during pregnancy, given its safety profile and high diagnostic yield [10]. Mature teratomas have well described sonographic characteristics, including a predominantly cystic lesion with mixed content, comprising echogenic nodules (“cotton wool” nodules), echogenic hair containing interfaces and areas of marked acoustic shadowing, which help distinguish them from other benign and malignant adnexal masses [2,9,11]. In the context of suspected torsion, additional ultrasound features evaluation is of high value since early recognition has a major role. Signs such as ovarian enlargement, peripheral displacement of follicles, free pelvic fluid, and altered Doppler flow may support the diagnosis, but their sensitivity is variable [2,11,12]. The “whirlpool sign,” representing direct visualization of a twisted vascular pedicle, has emerged as one of the most specific and reliable sonographic markers of adnexal torsion, but it is not always easy to identify, especially when the ovary is displaced to more unusual locations and the uterus is large [4,8,12,13].

Surgical management of adnexal torsion in pregnancy requires balancing maternal benefit, ovarian preservation whenever possible, and fetal safety [3,6,7,14]. Laparoscopy in the second trimester is generally favored to laparotomy for benign appearing masses, as it offers less postoperative pain, shorter hospitalization, and comparable obstetric outcomes [1,3,7,14,15].

This report describes an unusual case of second trimester adnexal torsion in a spontaneously conceived pregnancy involving a 150 mm ovarian mature teratoma, in which detailed ultrasound assessment, including recognition of the whirlpool sign, allowed rapid diagnosis and timely laparoscopic adnexectomy, resulting in an uneventful continuation of pregnancy to term [1,4,6,11,12].

Case presentation

A 35-year-old nulliparous woman at 18 weeks and 6 days of gestation, with no relevant medical or surgical history, presented to a tertiary care emergency department with a 4 hour history of sudden onset left flank pain and vomiting. Vital signs were within normal limits. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the left flank, with an equivocal left sided Murphy sign, and no abnormalities were noted on gynecological examination.

Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound performed in the emergency setting (Voluson P8, GE Healthcare) demonstrated a viable intrauterine pregnancy with fetal biometry consistent with 18 weeks and 6 days, normal amniotic fluid volume, a posterior placenta, and a closed cervix measuring 38 mm in length. Laboratory blood tests and urinalysis were unremarkable. Renal ultrasound was requested to exclude urinary tract pathology and it revealed a large unilocular adnexal mass measuring 150 × 80 mm in the left upper quadrant, containing multiple round echogenic nodules (“cotton wool tufts”), linear echogenic structures (“starry sky” sign), and no internal vascularization on color Doppler, features suggestive of a mature teratoma. Both kidneys had normal position and size, regular contours, preserved cortical thickness, and no dilatation of the collecting system or bladder abnormalities.

Given the sudden onset abdominal pain and the sonographic suspicion of an adnexal mass, a detailed gynecological ultrasound examination was performed to exclude adnexal torsion. Suprapubic and transabdominal scans revealed a unilocular lesion with mixed echogenic content, measuring 149.6 × 76 × 126 mm (Fig. 1) in the left upper quadrant. The mass had regular internal and external contours, was avascular on color Doppler, showed marked posterior acoustic shadowing, and contained no solid components. The “cotton wool ball” and “starry sky” signs were also present, characteristics consistent with a mature cystic teratoma. Inferomedial to the lesion, between the mass and the uterus, a heterogeneous nodular structure measuring 31 × 38 mm with moderate vascularization was visualized, corresponding to the sonographic whirlpool sign (Fig. 2). The right ovary was normal and no free fluid was detected in the pouch of Douglas. The patient was admitted with a diagnosis of adnexal torsion, and the management options were discussed with her by the multidisciplinary team and informed surgical consent was obtained. An umbilical incision using an open technique was performed, and a 12 mm umbilical trocar was inserted. Pneumoperitoneum with carbon dioxide was established to a pressure of 12 mmHg, and a 0° laparoscope was introduced, allowing inspection of the entire abdominal cavity. Intraoperative findings (Fig. 3) included a gravid uterus consistent with 18 weeks gestation, a markedly enlarged left ovary replaced by an approximately 150 mm mass, and torsion of the infundibulopelvic and utero-ovarian ligaments. The right ovary appeared macroscopically normal. Left adnexectomy was performed as irreversible adnexal necrosis was already established. Fetal heart activity was confirmed at the end of the procedure, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 1.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful and the pregnancy progressed without complications. Histopathological examination confirmed a mature cystic teratoma of the left ovary with 150 mm of biggest diameter with areas of necrosis. At 39 weeks of gestation, the patient delivered vaginally a healthy neonate weighing 3100 g, with Apgar scores of 9, 10, and 10 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes, respectively.

Discussion

Adnexal torsion during pregnancy has an estimated incidence of about 5 per 10,000 pregnancies, and occurs most commonly in the first trimester or early second trimester and rarely later on pregnancy due to the increased size of the uterus [1,5]. In pregnant women, torsion is particularly reported after ovarian stimulation and assisted reproductive techniques, making its occurrence in a spontaneous pregnancy, on second trimester, clinically noteworthy [5,8,15].

Adnexal masses in pregnancy incidence is approximately 2 in 1000, 20 times more frequent than in general population, which is attributed mainly to the widespread of antenatal ultrasound [3]. Nevertheless, most masses are asymptomatic and correspond mainly to functional cysts that regress spontaneously [1,6,7]. Mature teratomas are among the most common benign neoplasms detected during pregnancy, but lesions above 100 mm are way less frequent [3,6,7]. In addition, adnexal torsion more often involves small-to-medium sized, mobile cysts as very large masses become relatively fixed in the abdomen as pregnancy advances, which makes torsion of a large teratoma at approximately 19 weeks’ gestation an unusual event [1,4].

Ultrasound is the first-line modality for evaluating adnexal masses in pregnancy because it is safe, generally widely available, and has a good diagnostic performance [10].

Heremans et al. [9] described the characteristic ultrasound appearance of mature teratomas, where they usually present as well-circumscribed unilocular lesions, with highly variable/mixed internal echogenicity, reflecting differing proportions of sebaceous material, fat, hair and calcified tissues. Among their most distinctive features are the echogenic mural protuberance or Rokitansky nodule, often associated with posterior acoustic shadowing due to teeth or calcified components, and the so called “tip of the iceberg” sign, where intensely echogenic anterior contents and shadowing obscure deeper portions of the lesion and adjacent structures [9]. Additional characteristic patterns include dermoid mesh or dot–dash echoes corresponding to hair within the cyst and fat–fluid levels delineating horizontal interfaces between sebum and aqueous fluid. Novel descriptors, less frequently found, such as “cotton wool” tufts, mushroom cap–shaped echogenic caps over more hypoechoic material, and a “starry sky” aspect with multiple floating hyperechoic particles further enlarge the sonographic spectrum of these tumors while remaining pathognomonic. Color Doppler typically demonstrates absent or minimal vascularization within the cyst and mural structures. In this patient, the presence of multiple echogenic nodules (“cotton-wool puffs” aspect), linear echogenic structures (“starry sky” pattern), marked posterior acoustic shadowing, and absence of internal vascularization on Doppler matched the classic spectrum of mature teratomas features reported. This precise morphologic characterization is particularly relevant in pregnancy, where a fast and precise diagnosis is especially important and unnecessary additional imaging should be avoided whenever possible [3,7,10].

The diagnosis of adnexal torsion remains challenging because symptoms are often nonspecific and laboratory parameters are rather poor discriminators [1,2]. Conventional ultrasound signs, such as, enlarged ovary, peripheral follicles, free fluid, and absent or reduced Doppler flow, have variable sensitivity and may be absent in partial or intermittent torsion [2,11,12]. A systematic review and metanalysis showed that direct visualization of a twisted vascular pedicle, the so-called “whirlpool sign”, is one of the most accurate sonographic indicators of adnexal torsion, with high specificity and positive likelihood ratios [12,16]. The whirlpool sign reflects the spiral configuration of the infundibulopelvic ligament and ovarian vessels, and its presence markedly increases diagnostic confidence and can obviate additional imaging such as MRI, thereby shortening time to surgery in time-critical emergencies [8,12,13,16]. In pregnancy, anatomical displacement of the ovaries and limitations in acoustic windows further complicate diagnosis [17]. Stepwise scanning along the utero-ovarian ligament and adnexal pedicle, as recommended in dedicated protocols for detecting the whirlpool sign, improves the chance of visualizing a twisted pedicle, particularly when the mass is displaced cranially, as in this case with a lesion in the left flank [13]. The combination of a sonographically typical mature teratoma with the whirlpool sign between the mass and the uterus strongly supported the diagnosis of adnexal torsion and justified prompt surgical management without further imaging delay [2,8,9,[11], [12], [13]].

Current recommendations favor laparoscopy over laparotomy for most benign appearing adnexal masses in the second trimester, due to lower postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery, with comparable obstetric and fetal outcomes when performed by experienced surgeons [[1], [2], [3],14]. There is ongoing debate regarding conservative detorsion vs adnexectomy, particularly in younger women and in pregnancy [1,14]. In this case, the decision for adnexectomy rather than detorsion alone was guided by the presence of a large, necrotic-appearing mass with a preoperative diagnosis of mature teratoma and concerns about recurrence and need for reintervention later in pregnancy [1,7,14].

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in pregnant patients presenting with acute abdominal pain and an adnexal mass, even in the absence of striking laboratory abnormalities and in the presence of an atypical pain location [1,11]. Early, systematic ultrasound evaluation, including a targeted search for the whirlpool sign, may shorten the diagnostic pathway, avoid unnecessary MRI, and expedite life and ovary-sparing surgery [8,[11], [12], [13],16].

Lastly, it also illustrates that although less frequently reported in literature, adnexal torsion can occur in the second trimester of a spontaneously conceived pregnancy and may involve a very large mature teratoma [1,4,5].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights an uncommon presentation of adnexal torsion in a spontaneously conceived 18 weeks and 6 days pregnancy, involving a large (150 mm) ovarian mature teratoma, challenging the traditional assumption that such large lesions are at lower risk of torsion as gestation advances. Detailed ultrasound evaluation, integrating the characteristic morphologic features of a mature teratoma with targeted assessment for torsion and demonstration of the whirlpool sign, enabled a prompt and confident diagnosis, avoided additional imaging, and minimized delay to surgery. Laparoscopic adnexectomy performed in the midtrimester resulted in rapid maternal recovery and an uneventful continuation of pregnancy to term.
