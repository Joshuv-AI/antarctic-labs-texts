The detection of high X-ray polarization from an accretion disc corona source and its modelling via Monte Carlo radiation transfer simulation 2023 The detection of high X-ray polarization from an accretion disc corona source and its modelling via Monte Carlo radiation transfer simulation – References
Ryota Tomaru Chris Done Hirokazu Odaka

Abstract
We report an X-ray polarization degree (PD) of 8.8 ± 1.4 % ​ ( 1 ​ σ ) 8.8\pm 1.4\%(1\sigma) from the accretion-disc–corona (ADC) neutron-star system 2S 0921–630 (=V395 Car) observed with the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer ( IXPE ). The PD increases with energy, while the polarization angle (PA) varies significantly across the band. These trends are consistent with a high-inclination ADC geometry where the vertically extended disc blocks direct sight of the central X-ray source, and the observed X-rays are those scattered in an equatorial disc wind. We also find tentative PD variability in the 2–3 keV band. To interpret the time-averaged polarization, we build spectropolarimetric models by Monte Carlo radiation transfer simulation with column density distribution of thermal–radiative wind launched by X-ray irradiation of the outer disc under an axisymmetric geometry. The model combines boundary-layer emission, its disc reflection, and the disc continuum, each with its intrinsic polarization. Scattering of this composite spectrum in the wind reproduces both the observed PD and its increase with energy. However, the observed PA evolution is not captured, which may indicate departures from axisymmetry—e.g. misalignment between the inner disc (and/or neutron-star spin) and the outer disc/wind, or a weak disc warping.



1
Introduction

Determining the geometry of accretion flows in low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs) is challenging when relying on spectroscopy and timing alone, because multiple different configurations can reproduce similar spectra and variability.
Instead, X-ray polarimetry directly probes geometry as it is imprinted by departures from spherical symmetry: the polarization degree (PD) and polarization angle (PA) carry the history of how the emitted radiation is scattered and reprocessed in non-spherical media
(
Chandrasekhar 1960
)
.
With the Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (
IXPE
), energy-resolved polarimetry of Galactic compact objects has become feasible, enabling geometric tests of models that are otherwise degenerate
(
Weisskopf et al. 2022
)
.
Early
IXPE
observations of "typical" weakly magnetized neutron-star LMXBs reported low PDs (
\sim 1-2\%
), consistent with a largely direct view of the central emitter and only modest scattering (see e.g. the review by
Ursini et al. 2024
).

Accretion-disc–corona (ADC) systems provide a contrasting, scattering-dominated regime.
These are systems viewed at such high inclination that the vertically extended outer disc obscures the central engine. The observed continuum is produced predominantly by electron scattering and reprocessing in a photoionized medium above the outer disc, plausibly an equatorial thermal–radiative wind
(
White & Holt 1982
;
Jimenez-Garate et al. 2002
;
Kallman et al. 2003
;
Church et al. 2005
;
Ponti et al. 2012
;
Tomaru et al. 2023
)
.
Such a configuration naturally predicts substantially higher PD than in direct-view systems, along with a diagnostic energy dependence that reflects the relative weighting of disc-like versus more isotropic illumination components
(
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
.

2S 0921–630 (=V395 Car) is a prototypical ADC source.
This source shows regular
eclipses in the X-rays, which gives the orbital period of 9 days and requires very high inclination
(
Ashcraft et al. 2012
,
i\sim 82^{\circ}
)
.
The companion star is an evolved K0III (subgiant) in orbit around a neutron star
(
Shahbaz & Watson 2007
;
Steeghs & Jonker 2007
;
Ashcraft et al. 2012
)
, with a Gaia DR3 distance of
9.2^{+2}_{-1.4}
kpc
(
Avakyan et al. 2023
)
with Basian estimation
(
Bailer-Jones et al. 2018
)
.
The observed X-rays have low apparent luminosity,
and there are multiple emission lines,
both of which indicate that the intrinsic X-ray flux is geometrically suppressed, so that reprocessing in the wind dominates the spectrum
(
Shahbaz & Watson 2007
;
Ashcraft et al. 2012
;
Tomaru et al. 2023
)

These properties make this object an ideal laboratory for testing polarimetric predictions for scattering-dominated geometries.

Here we present
IXPE
spectro-polarimetry of 2S 0921-630 and report a high time-averaged polarization of
PD=8.8\pm 1.6\%
in 2–8 keV band, with
marginal evidence for increasing PD with energy, associated with a swing in PA.
To interpret our results, we develop a Monte Carlo radiative-transfer model that combines disc emission (polarized parallel to the disc), its boundary layer with the neutron star surface (approximately unpolarized), reflection of the boundary layer from the inner disc (polarized perpendicular to the disc), and scattering in an outer thermal–radiative wind (polarized perpendicular to the disc). This is the first polarization model to include all these components together. We show that this composite can give the low PD observed in the NS LMXRB seen directly, and the high PD seen in this wind scattered ADC source. It also reproduces the rise in PD with energy at high inclination by mixing disc-like (intrinsic disc emission and reflected photons) and isotropic (boundary layer) illumination patterns.
However, it does not account for the observed energy-dependent PA, as the PA is tied to the system symmetry, and all components in our model (disk, boundary layer, reflection, wind) have the same axis. Instead, changes in PA point to modest departures from axisymmetry—such as a small misalignment between the inner disc
(and/or neutron-star spin) and the outer disc/wind, or a weak warp
(
Ogilvie & Dubus 2001
)

or non-axysymmetric structures in the outer disc from the accretion stream impact
(
Hellier & Mason 1989
)
.

The remainder of the paper describes the
IXPE
observations and analysis (Section 2), the spectro-polarimetric modelling framework (Section 3), followed by a Discussion (Section 4), and Summary (Section 5).

To analyze the time-averaged spectro-polarimetric properties, we perform simultaneous fitting of the time-averaged data sets from each detector unit (DU1, DU2, and DU3) using the model
constant * TBabs * polconst * (diskbb + bbody)
in
xspec
. This model assumes that the intrinsic spectrum comprises a low-temperature disk blackbody and a high-temperature blackbody originating from the neutron star boundary layer (Table
1
).
For the fitting procedure, we fix the Galactic absorption column density at
N_{\rm H}=0.15\times 10^{22}{\rm cm^{-2}}
, consistent with previous analyses
(
Tomaru et al. 2023
)
.
The
polconstant
component in our model assumes a single polarization for the sum of
the two intrinsic emission spectra, with constant
PD and PA across the entire energy range.
We incorporate cross-normalization factors between detector units using the
constant
component.

This model provides an excellent description of the spectro-polarimetric data with
\chi^{2}/\nu=1301/1333
(see the spectrum and Stokes Q and U parameters in Fig.
2
). It
reveals a significant polarization signal with a PD of
8.8\pm 1.6\%
and a PA of
-46\pm 5.4^{\circ}
(errors represent
1\sigma
confidence levels).

Fig.
3
(left) displays the two-dimensional confidence contours for PD and PA, clearly demonstrating the robust detection of both parameters. We plot the time-averaged PD and PA as the orange band on the 3rd and 4th panels of Fig.
1
, and also show the marginal variability about these mean values in the three time intervals.

This exceptionally large PD greatly exceeds that measured for weakly magnetised neutron stars in ordinary LMXBs and provides strong evidence that the X
-
ray emission from this source is produced by scattering in an accretion disk wind, as
Tomaru et al. 2023
showed.
In typical LMXBs, such neutron stars exhibit PDs well below
5\%
.
For example, the atoll source GX 9+9 shows
1.4\pm 0.2\%

(
Farinelli et al. 2023
)
,
and the more luminous Z-source Cyg X
-
2 reaches only
1.8\pm 0.3\%

(
Ursini et al. 2023
)
.

The model above assumes only a single polarization, but the intrinsic spectrum is made from two components (disc and boundary layer) that should have different polarization properties
(
Gnarini et al. 2022
;
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
.
Hence we explore whether there is any energy dependence of the polarization which might be associated with the switch between disc dominating at low energies, to the boundary layer dominating at higher energies. We split the data into two energy bands, from 2-4 keV (red) and 4-8 keV (blue). In this analysis, we retained only the Q and U Stokes parameters for each energy band, whereas the I parameter was integrated over the entire 2–8 keV range. The results are tabulated Tab.
2
, and shown as contours in Fig.
3
(right).
The contours for the two energy bands substantially overlap in PD, so the best fit increase in PD with energy, from
8.5-13\%
,
is not significant. However, the change in PA (from
-37^{\circ}
to
-60^{\circ}
)
is significant at
\geq 1\sigma
.

To investigate this energy dependence in greater detail, we further subdivided the data into four energy bands (Fig.
4
).
This shows that the trend with energy in the time averaged spectrum (blue points) is systematic in both PA and PD.

3
Modeling of polarization

Figure 5:
The schematic geometry of the model. (A) The model of the normal lower magnetised neutron star. We calculated different source distributions and combined them to predict total polarization.
(B) The model of the Accretion disk corona source. We add the outer disc and disc wind to the model (A).

To understand the origin of the polarization from this source, we use the Monte-Carlo radiation transfer code
monaco

(
Odaka et al. 2011
;
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
.
This code calculates the interaction between photons and electrons and tracks the propagation of each photon, including polarization from electron scattering.

In this calculation, we assume an axisymmetric geometry so U=0 and the PD is given by Q/I.
We only consider Compton scattering (both up scattering and down scattering), assuming the material near the compact object is completely ionised.
This is appropriate for the intrinsic emission components, but we note that the wind and the inner disc reflection are not completely ionised, so there can be additional absorption opacity which can slightly change the energy dependence
(
Tomaru et al. 2024
;
Podgorný et al. 2023
)
.

We first calculate the polarization expected from the inner components:
the boundary layer and its inner disc reflection (top left in Fig.
5
), plus intrinsic disc emission (bottom left in Fig.
5
).
This should give results which are appropriate for short period NS LMXRB, where the outer disc is too small to launch a thermal-radiative disc wind
(
Done et al. 2018
)
.
We then include the effect of scattering of these components in an outer disc wind (geometry B in Fig.
5
).
This should be appropriate for long period NS LMXRB systems, and we explicitly calculate extremely high inclination angles where the disc obscures the central source so as to be able to model the ADC sources.

3.1
Modeling short period NS LMXBs

Figure 6:
The energy integrated polarization properties of all the model components for geometry A. Upper panel: The reflected spectrum PD as a function of inclination (red). The dashed line shows the fraction of reflection to the total boundary layer plus reflected emission. Lower panel: Multiplying the fraction of reflection and its PD together gives the PD of the boundary layer plus its reflection from the disc (magenta line). The orange line instead shows the orthogonally polarized intrinsic disc emission.

For the first simulation, we calculate the polarization
expected from all the components of a
(low-magnetisation) NS LMXRB (A in Fig.
5
).
We set a spherical source geometry with a radius of
10R_{g}
to mimic the emission from a boundary layer which covers the entire NS surface, so it is spherical (isotropic) and unpolarized (see
Bobrikova et al. 2025
for a discussion of other boundary layer geometries).
Guided by the observed spectrum, we assume this emits like a black body with
kT_{\rm bb}=1.7~{\rm keV}
.

We follow
4\times 10^{7}
intrinsically unpolarized photons from this boundary layer, allowing them to reflect from a completely ionised, optically thick, geometrically thin disc.
We model this as
extending from

R_{\rm in}\leq R\leq R_{\rm out}
, where
R_{\rm in}=6R_{g}
and
R_{\rm out}=600R_{g}
.
We make a logarithmic radial grid as
R_{i}=R_{\rm in}(R_{\rm out}/R_{\rm in})^{i/N_{R}}
(
i=0,1...N_{R}
,
N_{R}=32
).
We set temperature distribution as
T_{\rm disc}(R)=T_{\rm in}(R/R_{\rm in})^{-3/4}
and
kT_{\rm in}=0.8{\rm keV}
, which is the observational value taken by
IXPE
.
Using the scale height of this temperature distribution, we assume the density of disk as
n_{e}(R)=\tau_{\rm es}/(\sigma_{\rm es}H)
, where scale height as
H=(c_{s}/\Omega_{k})
,
c_{s}=\sqrt{kT_{\rm disc}(R)/(\mu m_{p})}
and
\Omega_{k}=\sqrt{GM/R^{3}}
.
We assume the vertical optical depth is 5 from the mid-plane to the disk surface, which means the total vertical optical depth is
\tau_{\rm es}=10
.

The upper panel of Fig.
6
shows
the inclination angle dependence of the reflected polarization degree (red).
This is polarized perpendicular to the plane of the disc (defined as PA=0, equivalently PD > 0), with larger polarization for more edge on geometries.
However, the reflected intensity also has an angle dependence, similar to that for the intrinsic disc spectrum in that it is brighter for more face on geometries. The dashed red line in the upper panel of Fig.
6
shows the fraction of reflected emission to the total (boundary layer plus reflected) emission. The fraction is large for
face on geometries (up to 25%) but its polarization here is small,
\approx 0
, as a face on disc is circular.
Conversely, the reflected emission makes only a small contribution to the total spectrum at high inclination (less than 10%), but it is highly polarized at
\approx 25
%.
This means that the combination of boundary layer plus reflection (purple, lower panel of Fig.
6
)
which is simply given by multiplying the reflected polarization by the fraction of reflection in the total spectrum, produces a total polarization which has a maximum around PD=3–4% for angles from
60-80^{\circ}
, and 2–3% for
30-60^{\circ}
.
This result is consistent with the earlier work
(
Lapidus & Sunyaev 1985
)

We next distribute the source photon within the inner disc mid-plane following the temperature distribution of the disc.
Thus the photon distribution integral with energy is
n_{\rm ph}(R)[{\rm photons~s^{-1}~cm^{-2}}]\propto T_{\rm disc}(R)^{3}=R^{-9/4}
.
We generate a total of
4\times 10^{7}
photons, and these electron scatter out of the disc to produce the
standard Chandrasekhar polarization and intensity pattern
(
Chandrasekhar 1960
)
.
The polarization direction is parallel to the disk plane (PA=90, equivalently PD<0), and the maximum PD is about
11\%
at
\cos i=0
.
This result is consistent with that of the previous work
(
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
. We show this compared to the boundary layer plus reflected emission as the
orange line
on the lower panel of Fig.
6
.

As well as different angle dependences, these components have different energy dependences.
We assume here that the source has intrinsically similar intensity from both the disc and boundary layer (as is expected in Newtonian gravity, where half of the available gravitational potential energy is radiated in the disc, and the other half kept as kinetic energy in rotation which can be released in the boundary layer) i.e. that

\int\int En_{\rm ph0,disk}(E,\Omega)dEd\Omega=\int\int En_{\rm ph0,BL}(E,\Omega)dEd\Omega
, where
n_{\rm phi0,disk}
is the number of photons per energy per solid angle from the boundary layer, and
n_{\rm ph0,disk}
is the same for disk.

The top panel in Fig.
7
shows the spectrum resulting from this at three different inclination angles (
i=45,60,85^{\circ}
), with the disc (orange), boundary layer (green), reflection (red) and total (blue).
The disc and reflected spectrum both drop with angle, while the boundary layer remains constant.
Our assumption of a completely ionised disc means that the reflected spectrum is identical in its energy dependence to that of the boundary layer, so we add these two together to consider their polarization properties.
The middle panel shows the PD as a function of energy for the boundary layer plus reflection (magenta) and the disc (orange), together with the total spectrum (blue).
There is now a distinct increase in PD for the total spectrum as a function of energy due to the spectral change from being dominated by the disc at low energy to being dominated by the boundary layer and its reflection at high energy.
The bottom panel shows the total PA as a function of energy. This shows a distinct swing from low energies, where the disc dominates and is intrinsically polarized parallel to the disc (PA=90) to high energy where the boundary layer and its reflected photons dominate, which is polarized perpendicular to the disc (PA=0).

This polarization model can explain the PD found by
IXPE
in some low magnetised NS in LMXBs, such as GX 9+9 (orbital period of 4.19 hours), where the PD slightly increases with energy from
1.4\pm 0.4\%
(2-4 keV) to
2.2\pm 5\%
(4- 8 keV)
(
Ursini et al. 2023
)
.
Ursini et al. 2023
explicitly fit reflection using the best current models i.e. including the residual atomic opacity of the highly ionised disc. This gives a good fit to the spectrum, but their reflection normalisation is smaller than predicted by our simulations here for a low-to-moderate inclination, so they require a much higher polarization of their inferred reflected emission. Reflection fitting is complex for highly ionised hot discs, and it may be that they are not sensitive to a completely ionised reflection component from the very innermost disc, and hence they are underestimating the amount of reflection present, and so overestimating the polarization required for their observed reflected component.

3.2
Modelling long period NS LMXRB

A long period, bright NS LMXRB should also have substantial thermal-radiative winds from X-ray illumination of the outer disc.
Thus we add the wind structure outside of the previous disk (see B in Fig.
5
) to predict the additional impact on the polarization properties imprinted by scattering in the wind.

As a simulation setup, we set the same column density structure
\log N_{H}=22+2.4(1-\cos i),(0\leq\cos i\leq 0.174)
as
(
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
although the different column density distribution could make the very different polarization direction
(
Nitindala et al. 2025
)
.
The maximum wind inclination angle
is set by our assumed boundary of the wind and the outer disk at

i_{\rm w}=80^{\circ}
since the inclination of this object is
i\sim 83^{\circ}

(
Ashcraft et al. 2012
)
.
At a higher inclination angle than
i=80^{\circ}
, we set an optically thick disk to separate each wind region as
\tau_{\rm z}=5
from the mid-plane.
This gives the radial optical depth for the outer disk as
\tau_{R}\sim\tau_{\rm z}/\tan(10^{\circ})\sim 28
.

This outer wind can polarize the scattered X-rays by
10-20\%
, in a direction which is perpendicular to the disc

(
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
.
Isotropic illumination gives a larger PD from the wind scattered flux (
\sim 20\%
) than the disk (
\sim 10\%
), as the former illuminates the more equatorial region, where the polarization perpendicular to the disc is larger.
Thus, scattering in the wind will give larger polarization to the NS boundary layer than the disk (and reflected emission).

Fig.
8
shows the inclination dependence of the energy-integrated PD including the wind scattering component on each of the boundary layer and its reflected emission (magenta) and the disc (orange). Compared to the lower panel of Fig.
6
, the boundary layer PD increases by 1%
where the source is seen directly as the additional scattering in the wind gives polarization in the same direction as reflection of the boundary layer from the disc. Conversely, the PD of the disc decreases by
\sim 1
% as the intrinsic disc emission is polarized orthoganlly to the scattered flux. For both disc and boundary layer plus its reflection, there is a marked jump in polarization where the outer disc completely obscures the central soure, so that the emission is
dominated by scattering in the wind.

is the same as

For low to moderate inclination systems (
i=45,60^{\circ}
), scattering by the wind imprints an additional PD of around
1\%
in the direction perpendicular to the disk plane. The effect is small as the ratio of wind scattered photons to the direct photons from the inner region is small.
At the higher inclination than the outer disc (
\cos i<0.17
), the photons from the inner geometry are completely blocked (see B in Fig.
5
), but scattered in the outer wind.
This effect produces very high polarization, with PD of up to
\sim 15\%
at 8 keV.
The total spectrum is dominated by the scattered boundary layer, as the reflected and intrinsic disc photons are suppressed at the high inclination angles, which are most effectively scattered in the wind.
However, there is scattered disc emission at low energies, and this has quite low PD as it scatters on average higher in the wind, where there is more circular symmetry.
Thus, there is a strong energy dependence of the PD, but the PA is constant, as even when the disc component dominates at low energies, it is almost all from the scattered boundary layer photons, and these all have direction perpendicular to the disc (PA=0).

4
Match to the ADC source data

Fig.
10

compares these results for the energy dependence of PD and PA with the time-averaged data. This is a very good match to the observed (marginally significant) increase in PD with energy.
However, the model predicts constant PA across the
IXPE
band, so it cannot match the observed (more significant) swing in PA with energy.

Additionally, our axysymmetric model with constant emission components has no way to give time-dependent behaviour, yet there is
(marginally significant) evidence for
an increase in PD at 2-3 keV in segment 2 of the 3 time intervals, though this is not associated with any change in PA. This could potentially be associated with a change in the source spectrum changing the ratio of disc to boundary layer. The energy spectrum of time segment 2 data shows a lower temperature of both the boundary layer and disk blackbody compared to the time average (Tab.
1
).
This change in best fit model does not look particularly convincing, though, as both disc and boundary layer temperatures drop together to leave the 2-5 keV spectrum relatively unchanged, but with a much softer spectrum above 5 keV. Nonetheless, if it is real (which cannot be easily tested with our data as the small effective area of
IXPE
at high energies means that this is not evident in the hardness ratio plot in Fig.
1
b), it means that the ratio between the boundary layer and the disk blackbody is higher in the 2-3 keV range in segment 2, at 0.5-0.9, than in the time averaged data, where it is
0.4-0.6. This would mean that the boundary layer emission is be more dominant at low energies in the time segment 2 data and this leads to the higher PD since the scattering is stronger for the more isotropic boundary layer emission than for the disc
(
Tomaru et al. 2024
)
. This shows the importance of getting simultaneous data from other X-ray telescopes with better spectral sensitivity and bandpass to support the unique
IXPE
observations.

It seems more likely, though, that whatever non-axisymmetric structure is responsible for the change in PA with energy is also responsible for time dependence. There is almost certainly a bulge on the outer disc where the accretion stream impacts
(
Hellier & Mason 1989
)
, but large discs can also be warped by radiation pressure and can then stably (or chaotically) precess via interaction with the tidal torques
(
Ogilvie & Dubus 2001
)
. The bright (Z source) NS LMXRB Cyg X-2 is likely a system where this chaotic precession of a radiation warped disc operates
(
Ogilvie & Dubus 2001
)
, and we note that its binary period of 9.5 days, and its component masses are very similar to those of 2S 0921-630.

5
Summary

We detect highly polarized X-ray flux from 2S 0921-630 with time-averaged PD
=8.8\pm 1.6\%
across the full 2-8 keV bandpass.
The energy-resolved analysis shows the increasing trend of PD with energy and significant energy dependence of PA.
This is the highest polarization seen so far from a low-magnetised NS LMXRB.
This confirms that the source is indeed an ADC, with the direct source emission blocked by the very high orbital inclination so that scattering in a wind dominates the observed flux, giving the observed high polarization.

We develop a full spectro-polarimetric model for short period NS LMXRB in general, as well as our ADC source in particular. We assume that the intrinsic source emission is the sum of an isotropic, non-polarized boundary layer, its reflection (angle dependent, similar to this, polarized perpendicular to the disc) and the
disc itself (angle dependent, polarized parallel to the disc). We assume that the integrated (over energy and solid angle) luminosities from both disc and boundary layer are the same, as appropriate for a bright atoll system, and this gives up to 3–4% polarization
perpendicular to the disc from the contribution of the
reflected emission for angles
>60^{\circ}
. This
should be appropriate for short period systems, where
the disc is too small for a substantial thermal-
radiative wind to form from the outer disc e.g. GX 9+9

(
Ursini et al. 2023
)
.

We also extend the models to long-period, bright systems where there can be a substantial outer disc wind which imprints additional polarization from scattering (with direction perpendicular to the disc). This generally increases the PD of the total spectrum by around 1%
as it adds to the PD from the reflected boundary layer contribution. This compares well with the X-ray polarization seen in the bright NS LMXRB systems such as Cyg X-2, GX 5-1 and other Z sources
(
Gnarini et al. 2025
)
.

This extended model at very high inclination angles, where the direct photons are blocked by the outer disc, produces very high polarization.
This can quantitatively match the PD seen in our data from the ADC source 2S 0921–630, and the model predicts an increase in PD with energy, which is consistent with the (not statistically significant) trend in the data.
However, our model cannot reproduce the (significant) swing in PA with energy, showing that there is still some aspect of the source which is not captured here.

One possibility is that there is some non-axisymmetry in the source geometry. One obvious potential break in symmetry comes from the impact of the accretion stream onto the disc (e.g.
Hellier & Mason 1989
), and this would also make the wind from the outer disc somewhat non-axisymmetic also. Alternatively (or additionally), large discs are also subject to radiation driven warping and this, coupled with the tidal torques, can lead to outer disc precession.
Both these possible non-axisymmetric structures also predict time dependent behaviour, which is marginally detected in the polarization degree, though not in the angle. This adds to the growing evidence for non-axisymmetric structures in NS LMXRB from polarization studies
(
Di Marco et al. 2025
;
Kashyap et al. 2025
)
.
