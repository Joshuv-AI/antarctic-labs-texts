Advancing Precision Dosing of 5-FU: Population PK Model Development, Limited Sampling Strategies, and Fit-for-Use Application
Authors: Zhiyuan Tan, Aymara Sancho-Araiz, Swantje Völler, Sofía L J Peeters, Thomas Manten, Maarten J Deenen, Jan Gerard Maring, Pierre M Bet, Ron A A Mathôt, Catherijne A J Knibbe, Dirk Jan A R Moes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA significant proportion of patients receiving 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy have been reported to experience grade ≥ 3 toxicities, which range from 21 to 76% depending on the treatment regimen. This may in part be due to interpatient variability in pharmacokinetics. More recently, only about 20% of the patients were reported to achieve the target area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity of 20–30 mg*h/L.ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate existing population pharmacokinetic models and develop a final pharmacokinetic model. In addition, we aimed to identify optimal limited sampling strategies to accurately estimate individual area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity, facilitating model-informed precision dosing using a fit-for-use application.MethodsPatient data from four prospective clinical studies were obtained. Published 5-fluorouracil population pharmacokinetic models were evaluated. A final model was developed, and limited sampling strategies were identified. A user-friendly model-informed precision dosing application was created for initial and subsequent dose adjustments.ResultsUsing the published models, population predictions resulted in underestimation of observed concentrations and misspecification of a 24-h infusion regimen were observed, with the two-compartment Michaelis–Menten model outperforming the other model structures. Of this model, the parameters were re-estimated and covariates analysis was conducted. Body surface area significantly influenced the maximum rate of reaction and was implemented using allometric scaling with an exponent of 1.14 (relative standard error 24%). For a bolus in combination with a 46-h continuous infusion, the best limited sampling strategy was T = 0.1 (end of bolus), 1 and 3 h after treatment initiation; while for the 46-h continuous infusion, the optimal limited sampling strategy was T = 0.8, 2, and 5 h. Model-informed precision dosing algorithms were implemented in an model-informed precision dosing application.ConclusionsThe external evaluation of 5-fluorouracil population pharmacokinetic models against a pooled prospective population resulted in a generic two-compartment Michaelis–Menten pharmacokinetic model with body surface area as a covariate on the maximum rate of reaction. The limited sampling strategy and the shiny model-informed precision dosing application demonstrated the potential for accurate dose individualization of 5-fluorouracil.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01645-1.

Key Points

External evaluation of published 5-fluorouracil population pharmacokinetic models against a pooled prospective population with diverse cancer types and dosing regimens resulted, after refinement in the development of a generic two-compartment, Michaelis–Menten pharmacokinetic model with allometrically scaled body surface area as the covariate on the maximum rate of reaction.Development of an optimal limited sampling strategy to accurately estimate individual area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity, facilitating model-informed precision dosing using a fit-for-use application.

Introduction

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) is the backbone of several first-line chemotherapy regimens, including FOLFOX (folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin) [1], FOLFIRI [2] (folinic acid, fluorouracil, and irinotecan), FOLFIRINOX [3] (folinic acid, fluorouracil, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) [4], and FLOT [5] (fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel) for various types of gastrointestinal cancers. In FOLFIRI/FOLFOX treatment regimens, the 5-FU standard dose is based on body surface area (BSA), with a bolus injection (400 mg/m2 in 5–15 min) followed by a 2400-mg/m2 continuous infusion of 5-FU over 46 h. In the FOLFIRINOX regimen, 5-FU is given either as bolus plus continuous infusion or only as a 2400-mg/m2 continuous infusion for 46 h. In the FLOT regimen or other adjuvant setting, 5-FU is usually given as continuous infusion over 24 h.

Recently, it was reported that 5-FU exposure (area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity [AUC0–inf]) exhibits wide inter-individual variability (IIV) when dosed based on BSA [6]. A narrow therapeutic window was observed in early studies [7, 8] and later defined as 20–30 mg*h/L by an international guideline [9]. Only 20.3% of patients received 5-FU treatment within this target range, whereas the majority of patients (60.6%) were underdosed and 19.1% of patients were overdosed [10, 11]. Here lies a great opportunity for the application of model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) in routine clinical practice to individualize the dose based on covariates and/or pharmacokinetic (PK) measurements. To create a successful MIPD workflow, a population PK (POPPK) model is required to get accurate priori prediction. Optimal limited sampling strategies (LSS) for estimating individual AUC0–inf, and model-based dose adjustment algorithms/applications are the key prerequisites for Bayesian prediction. Previous studies generated AUC0–inf as the product of steady-state concentration and infusion duration. The remaining 5-FU AUC after the end of infusion was ignored because of the short half-life of 5-FU (∼ 10–15 min) [9]. However, the previous methods also ignored the contribution of the bolus, which might contribute considerably to the total exposure of 5-FU with estimations that range from 20 to 50% [12].

Several 5-FU POPPK models have been reported before with different sampling designs, model structure, and covariates [13–18]. However, none of the developed models incorporates comprehensive infusion regimens, cancer diagnosis, and infusion durations. From a bioanalytical perspective, the dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) enzyme inhibitor, gimeracil [6], was not reported to be used to enhance the stability of collected samples in the previous studies, which potentially could have led to the degradation of 5-FU before a sample analysis and subsequently a potentially biased model parameter estimation. Therefore, a generic, precise, and well-validated 5-FU POPPK model for all dosing regimens and diagnosis should be established.

In this study, the primary aim is to evaluate existing POPPK models and develop a new generic model including covariates for accurate prediction of individual 5-FU exposure. The secondary aim is to identify optimal LSS to accurately estimate individual AUC0–inf, facilitating MIPD using a fit-for-use application.

Methods

Study Cohorts and Patient Data

The current analysis was performed using data from clinical studies conducted in three medical centers in the Netherlands (Amsterdam Medical Center, Catharina Hospital Eindhoven, and Isala Hospital Zwolle). All studies were approved by the medical research ethics committee of the participating hospitals. The separate approval numbers and brief descriptions of included studies are listed below:

FUUT study (NL-67140.100.18): the study was a prospective cohort study to determine the correlation between the baseline endogenous DPD substrate plasma ratios with the pharmacokinetics of 5-FU in patients with pancreatic or colorectal cancer treated with intravenous 5-FU-based chemotherapy [19].

FUUT study (NL-67140.100.18): the study was a prospective cohort study to determine the correlation between the baseline endogenous DPD substrate plasma ratios with the pharmacokinetics of 5-FU in patients with pancreatic or colorectal cancer treated with intravenous 5-FU-based chemotherapy [19].

FLOT study (NL-81831.100.22): the study was a prospective cohort study to use therapeutic oncolytic monitoring as a tool for effective exposure to 5-FU in patients with locally advanced, resectable gastric, or gastro-esophageal junction cancer treated with perioperative FLOT [20, 21].

FLOT study (NL-81831.100.22): the study was a prospective cohort study to use therapeutic oncolytic monitoring as a tool for effective exposure to 5-FU in patients with locally advanced, resectable gastric, or gastro-esophageal junction cancer treated with perioperative FLOT [20, 21].

CombiGaPP study (NL-70778.075.19): the CombiGapp study was a prospective cohort study to investigate the pharmacokinetics of 5-FU for the four most common DPYD variants and to define a safe and effective starting dose for 5-FU in DPD-deficient patients [22].

CombiGaPP study (NL-70778.075.19): the CombiGapp study was a prospective cohort study to investigate the pharmacokinetics of 5-FU for the four most common DPYD variants and to define a safe and effective starting dose for 5-FU in DPD-deficient patients [22].

PERFU study (NL-71410.029.19): the PERFU study was a prospective cohort study to investigate the effect of type 2 diabetes mellitus on patients with pancreatic and colorectal cancer on the percentage of patients achieving optimal 5-FU exposure within two dose cycles of 5-FU [23].

The included patients had received chemotherapy with FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, FOLFIRINOX, or FLOT regimens as routine clinical practice in the three centers until June 2024. Relevant demographic data, clinical laboratory data, and PK data were collected based on the previously published international consensus guideline [9] and a systematic review of 5-FU published models with reported covariates [12]. Baseline demographic data that were collected included age, total body weight, height, BSA, sex, body mass index, cancer diagnosis, creatinine, and DPYD gene-activity score, which was assessed following the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group guideline [24]. To this end, all patients were genotyped for four clinically relevant DPYD variants as part of routine clinical practice: DPYD*2A (c.1905+1G>A, IVS14+1G>A), DPYD*13 (c.1679T>G), c.2846A>T, and c.1236G>A [24] with a DPYD gene activity score that ranged from 0 (no DPD activity) to 2 (normal DPD activity) based on the patients’ genotype [24]. Additionally, creatinine clearance was calculated with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration CKD-EPI equation expressed as mL/min/1.73 m2 [25].

Bioanalytical Assay and Blood Sampling

Blood samples were collected in 4-mL EDTA tubes. Immediately after collection of the blood sample, 12 µg of gimeracil in 100 µL of 30% methanol/water solution was added as a DPD inhibitor preventing ex-vivo metabolism of 5-FU. The 5-FU concentrations were measured in the local centers separately. For the CombiGaPP study and FLOT study, plasma samples were analyzed on an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry system (Waters Acquity™ T3, 1.8 µm; 3.0 × 50 mm column and T3 1.8 1.8 µm 2.1 × 5 mm pre-column at 50 °C, TQD detector Electrospray+ mode), according to a validated method as described previously [26]. For the FUUT study, plasma samples were analyzed on another previously validated assay using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry system with hypercarb column (Thermo Electron, porous graphitic carbon, 150 mm × 2.1 mm, 5-µm particle size) with a mobile phase gradient of acetonitrile and water [27]. For the PERFU study, plasma 5-FU concentrations were measured using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with electrospray ionization in negative mode on a Shimadzu LC-30 system coupled to an ABSciex API5500 Qtrap MS. All methods were validated over a plasma concentration range of 50–5000 ng/mL. Accuracy ranged from 94.2 to 99.0%, intra-day precision was ≤ 5.1%, and inter-day precision was ≤ 7.7%.

The sampling schemes varied across different studies and are visualized in Fig. 1 to indicate the general sampling information. CombiGaPP-1 and PERFU had intensive sampling with a median of three to four samples per individual at steady state and PERFU also provided one sample per individual at the early distribution phase (during or at the end of the bolus infusion). FUUT and CombiGaPP-2 performed sparse sampling at a steady-state concentration with one sample per individual. FLOT study also applied intensive sampling; however, the infusion duration in this regimen is 24 h instead of 46 or 48 h.

Population Pharmacokinetic (POPPK) Modeling

The 5-FU POPPK and simulation analysis was performed using the non-linear mixed-effects models implemented in NONMEM Version 7.4.4 (Icon Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA) and Perl Speaks for NONMEM Version 5.0.0. The estimation method was the first-order conditional estimation method with interaction. Data processing, basic statistics analysis, and results visualization were performed in R (Version 4.3.1).

The 5-FU POPPK model was established in three steps. First, the published 5-FU POPPK models were reproduced and used for external evaluation with our cohort data. Second, published models were explored to improve the description of the present datasets. Finally, a covariate analysis was performed to investigate potential clinically significant covariate effects.

Reproduction and External Valuation of the Publicly Available 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) POPPK Models

Published 5-FU POPPK models were retrieved from a previous systematic review [12]. Additionally, models that were available in the Population Approach Groupe Europe (PAGE) meetings abstract database (https://www.page-meeting.org/default.asp?keuze=search) with comprehensive model descriptions were also evaluated. All publicly available 5-FU PK modeling publications were reproduced by either using the code provided by the publication or from the model structure, parameter estimates, and covariates provided in the results section. The published models were externally evaluated with our cohorts by performing maximum a posterior Bayesian estimation, using “MAXEVAL = 0 POSTHOC” in the estimation step in NONMEM.

Final 5-FU POPPK Model Development

If none of the published 5-FU POPPK models was qualified to describe the present dataset precisely according to the criteria as defined in Sect. 2.3.3, subsequent model development was performed sequentially and was defined as the final 5-FU POPPK model for further analysis. First, the base population model, with the focus on the estimation of the population parameters, IIV, and residual unexplained variability, providing an adequate description of data before entering any covariate was selected, thereafter, the process of covariate selection was performed and, last, the final model was evaluated and explored. The initial covariate selection used for the covariate analysis was based on data availability and on previous reports [12]. Potential covariates were tested on model parameters using stepwise covariate modeling [28]. The inter-occasion variability (IOV) between the first cycle and subsequent cycles was also evaluated [29]. The inter-study and inter-assay differences were evaluated by (i) including a study/center indicator as a covariate, and different residual error model for different centers/studies (proportional and additive components) and (ii) visual inspection by splitting goodness of fit (GOF) and individual estimates (empirical Bayesian estimate [EBE]) distribution plots by different centers.

Model Selection Criteria

Model selection criteria included: (i) visual inspection of GOF plots; (ii) stratified GOF plots and stratified individual EBE distribution by covariates; (iii) the loglikelihood ratio test for nested models and Akaike information criterion for non-nested models; and (iv) numerical predictive performance evaluation metrics included mean absolute error, mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and root mean square error (RMSE). In addition, the newly developed 5-FU POPPK model was also evaluated by (v) parameter estimation precision expressed as percentage relative standard error (%); (vi) covariate effect size evaluation; and (vii) bootstrap, visual predictive check, or normalized prediction distribution errors. The detailed selection criteria are reported in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

Limited Sampling Schedule Exploration

The final 5-FU POPPK model was used to evaluate the performance of several LSS comprising clinically convenient sampling times to estimate the 5-FU AUC. A general workflow including two different methods to determine LSS is described in Fig. S1 of the ESM.

Method 1 was an empirical manual method. Clinically feasible sampling times were prespecified on a 15-min grid within practical post-dose windows, excluding night-time draws and requiring at least 30 min between samples. All feasible 2-point, 3-point, and 4-point schedules within these constraints were evaluated exhaustively. All possible sampling designs were screened manually as depicted in Fig. S2 of the ESM. The model was used to simulate intensive concentration–time profiles for 1000 typical virtual patients with cancer. Herein, the reference AUC0–inf (AUCREF) was derived by adding an area under the concentration-time curve compartment to the NONMEM control stream. Subsequently, different sampling time schedules were empirically evaluated by estimating the AUC0–inf using maximum a posterior Bayesian estimation in NONMEM, based on reduced datasets containing only the selected timepoints. Method 2 was model-based sampling optimization. Parameter precision and model parameter estimating ability was obtained by assessing the Bayesian Fisher Information Matrix providing expected individual EBE uncertainty [30]. The in-built function $DESIGN in NONMEM [31] was selected (OFVTYPE = 8) to generate the model-based LSS. Using this method, the model parameters were known, and the individual empirical Bayesian estimates were evaluated. Furthermore, an optimization step was performed to obtain the recommended sampling time.

The final LSS selection was based on the following parameters: I. Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE, mg*h/L); II. RMSE (%); III. Mean percentage error (MPE, %); IV. Absolute percentage error >10% (APE10) among 1000 virtual patients. Because of the narrow 5-FU AUC0–inf target of 20–30 mg*h/L , several criteria were defined for MAPE, RMSE, and MPE.

MAPE: according to clinical practice requirements, MAPE ≤ 2.5 mg*h/L was defined as excellent accuracy. This would indicate that the estimated AUCLSS is within 10% of the true AUCREF on average magnitude, which is ideal in clinical practice [32]:MAPE=1n∑i=1nei-riri.

MAPE: according to clinical practice requirements, MAPE ≤ 2.5 mg*h/L was defined as excellent accuracy. This would indicate that the estimated AUCLSS is within 10% of the true AUCREF on average magnitude, which is ideal in clinical practice [32]:MAPE=1n∑i=1nei-riri.

RMSE: RMSE ≤ 10% was defined as excellent accuracy; < 20% was also acceptable in clinical practice:RMSE=1n∑i=1nei-riri×100\%2.

RMSE: RMSE ≤ 10% was defined as excellent accuracy; < 20% was also acceptable in clinical practice:RMSE=1n∑i=1nei-riri×100\%2.

MPE: the absolute value of MPE ≤ 10% was defined as a small bias with the estimated AUCLSS being within 10% of the true AUCREF; the bias > 10% was considered to be not clinically acceptable.MPE=1n∑i=1nei-riri∗100\%,where ei was the AUCLSS, ri was the AUCREF, and n was the number of observations (=1000).

Simulations and MIPD Shiny Application Development

To optimize dosing strategies of the initial cycle, the final 5-FU POPPK model was used to simulate the AUC0–inf in NONMEM for patients based on individual BSA. One thousand virtual patients were generated by random resampling with replacement from the original modeling dataset (n = 140), retaining only BSA. In R, this was implemented by sampling row indices from the original dataset, after which the resampled covariates were used as input for simulation under the final population PK model. Virtual patients were given 5-FU doses that ranged from 2100 to 3000 mg/m2 as a continuous infusion with or without a 200–400 mg/m2 bolus infusion of 5-FU. First-cycle dose modification recommendations were generated according to the results to achieve the 20–30 mg*h/L AUC0–inf target in simulated individuals.

For subsequent cycles’ dose optimization, the final 5-FU POPPK model was used to optimize dosing regimens by integrating individual estimates and simulations into a user-friendly Shiny application in R. The PK model was implemented using mrgsolve, an open-source ODE-based modeling platform [33]. Both bolus plus continuous and continuous infusion scenarios were considered. The application allowed for input of dosing parameters (e.g., bolus dose and duration, infusion rate, infusion duration, BSA) with/without observed plasma concentrations to generate estimated/simulated AUC0–inf for dose adjustment reference. For cases with observed plasma concentrations, individual estimates were estimated using R package “mapbayr” [34] and generated dosing recommendations by simulations. Target AUC0–inf ranges (20–30 mg*h/L) [9] were predefined to evaluate whether the simulated or estimated regimens met therapeutic goals.

Results

Demographics

In the present POPPK study, data from 143 patients across three different medical centers who participated in four prospective clinical studies were collected. Three patients were excluded because of unknown bolus duration. The characteristics of the remaining 140 patients are presented in Table 1. The median age was 63 years (interquartile range 56–70 years) and was comparable across different studies at the time of initiating chemotherapy. The median BSA was 1.94 m2. The median bolus dose was 385 mg/m2 (interquartile range 293–400 mg/m2). The median continuous infusion dose was 2340 mg/m2 (interquartile range 1790–2422 mg/m2). Of the included patients, 42.1% were diagnosed with colorectal cancer and 34.3% with pancreatic cancer. The median number of samples per individual was 2 (range 1–17). A total of 435 measured 5-FU concentrations were obtained with no below quantification limits, as shown in Fig. S3 of the ESM. The sampling time distributions were visualized as a histogram in Fig. S4 of the ESM.

External Evaluation of Publicly Available 5-FU POPPK Models

The detailed information of the six evaluated 5-FU POPPK models is summarized in Table S1 of the ESM. The prediction-based diagnostics results for all the published POPPK models are shown in Table S2 of the ESM. The diagnostic GOF plots of each published model shown in Fig. S5 of the ESM, indicated that none of the six published models could be directly used for the present dataset. The dependent variable (DV) versus population prediction (PRED) results and conditional weighted residuals (CWRES) versus PRED, with PRED being crucial for a priori dose optimization, are provided in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2, the best model performance was observed using the two-compartment Michaelis–Menten (MM) elimination model by Briki et al. (Briki 2024 model) [35] with the lowest RMSE of 82%, and least MAPE of 40.28%. The Briki model, although exhibiting a good description of both IPRED and PRED with the least MAPE, the CWRES across the whole observations were unacceptably high (> |3| for > 10% of observations) as depicted in Fig. 2. Another two-compartment MM model, van Kuilenburg 2012 model [17] also performed well in numeric diagnostics while the PRED underestimated observations at steady state and biased CWRES versus time after last dose was observed. As shown in both numerical Table S2 of the ESM and graphical external evaluation Fig. 2, the two-compartment non-linear MM elimination models described the present data better than linear or mixed elimination (linear plus MM). The individual fit plots also indicated an improvement on the data related to bolus administrations for the two-compartment models when compared with one-compartment models.

Final 5-FU POPPK Model Development

One-compartment, two-compartment, and three-compartment models were evaluated with linear or MM elimination. A three-compartment model was unidentifiable because the collected time course of 5-FU measured concentrations did not exhibit the characteristics of a three-compartment model, as shown in Figure S3 of the ESM. In addition, when running the three-compartment model, the parameters were shown to be unidentifiable with a relative standard error >106. All the linear elimination models overestimated the observations and therefore were not considered for numeric comparison.

For two-compartment models, MM elimination model described the data significantly better than the one-compartment MM elimination model as shown in Table S3 of the ESM. The IIV was tested on volume of distribution (V1) and/or maximum rate of reaction (Vmax). However, the shrinkage of IIV on V1 was >50% and the individual estimates distribution plot of V1 indicated the IIV of most collected samples were shrinking to 0 as shown in Figure S6 of the ESM. Therefore, only IIV on Vmax was considered for further analysis. The relative standard error parameter estimates were all within < 30%. Therefore, the two-compartment MM model was selected as the structural model and selected for the covariate analysis for the final POPPK model.

The intra-individual difference between the first cycle and subsequent cycles was tested by adding IOV on top of the IIV of Vmax. The IOV was estimated to be 12% and no significant improvement of the model fit was observed (difference in objective function value < 3.84), which indicated that the difference between cycle variability could be ignored because of the relatively small variability observed. Before the covariates analysis, visual screening of the individual estimates of Vmax versus covariates indicated both body size descriptor (total body weight or BSA) and sex as potential significant covariates. No statistically significant improvement was observed when including inter-study or inter-assay variability and sperate error model by different centers/studies. The proportional residual error across the centers was 30–34%. In the covariate analysis, BSA was identified as a significant covariate. Therefore, BSA-normalized allometric scaling of Vmax was performed by either fixing the exponent to 1 or estimating the exponent to be 1.14, and both improved the model fit significantly (decrease of OFV >12). The EBE versus BSA plot, as depicted in Fig. S7 of the ESM, indicated that estimating the exponent model may improve performance. Both covariate analysis and individual estimates versus DPYD gene score plot did not indicate that DPYD has an impact on Vmax as shown in Fig. S8 of the ESM. Therefore, in the final 5-FU model, only BSA was kept, and the exponent was estimated and not fixed. The median AUC0–inf for patients with different BSA and different dosing regimens are illustrated in Table S4 of the ESM. The percentage coefficient of variation of IIV on Vmax was 35% in the base model and 31% in the final model. The parameter estimates of the final 5-FU POPPK model are shown in Table 2. The GOF plot of final 5-FU POPPK model, as shown in Fig. 3, indicated an adequate model fit. The stratified GOF plots also depicted an adequate fit across all tumor types (Fig. S9 of the ESM). The visual predictive check and normalized prediction distribution error were also indicated that the model captured the median and variability of the observed data (Figs. S10 and S11 of the ESM).

LSS Exploration

Model-based LSS exploration indicated that for bolus and continuous infusion, T = 0.1 (end of bolus), 1 and 3 h post-administration was the best sampling schedule. For continuous infusion only, T = 2, 3, and 5 h after the start of infusion was the best sampling schedule. As shown in Fig. 4a, which presents the MAPE of tested scenarios for bolus and continuous infusion, the best three-sampling or four-sampling LSS were similarly precise in estimating the 5-FU AUC0–inf. However, estimating AUC0–inf from two samples only resulted in biased estimation for bolus and continuous infusion. The mean MAPE with standard deviation (± SD) for all the two-sample designs was 3.76 ± 1.02 mg*h/L. For all evaluated three-sample designs, the mean MAPE ± SD was 3.03 ± 1.05 mg*h/L. For all evaluated four-sample schedules, the mean MAPE ± SD was 2.70 ± 0.63 mg*h/L.

When it comes to continuous infusion-only regimens, two-sample, three-sample, and four-sample scenarios performed similarly well. The mean MAPE ± SD for all two-sample designs was 3.48 ± 0.11 mg*h/L. For the three-sample design, it was 3.03 ± 0.27 mg*h/L and 2.65 ± 0.09 mg*h/L for the four-sample design.

Sampling near the end of infusion (40 h after the start of administration) exhibited similar accuracy with early samples. The RMSE and other evaluation metrics indicated similar trends with MAPE as shown in Table S5 of the ESM.

Model-Informed Dosing Recommendations and Shiny Application Development

The initial dose exploration is shown in Table 3, where representative virtual patients, who had the same patient characteristics in comparison with the data used for POPPK model development, were treated with different 5-FU dose of both bolus plus continuous infusion and continuous infusion-only regimens. For bolus + continuous dosing, the median AUC0–inf for a standard starting dose 400 mg/m2 + 2400 mg/m2 is 29 mg*h/L with 45% of the population resulting in exposure beyond the 30-mg*h/L threshold, while decreasing the dose to 200 mg/m2 + 2400 mg/m2 could improve the fraction of patients achieving the target. For continuous infusion only, the 5-FU starting dose of 2400 mg/m2 will result in a median AUC0–inf of 21 mg*h/L while still 45% of the population likely will not achieve the efficacy threshold of 20 mg*h/L. When increasing the initial dose to 2800 mg/m2, a higher target achievement rate is expected. To visualize the results of different dosing regimens, median exposure over time and median AUC0–inf of the standard regimens are depicted in Fig. 5. To adjust doses in subsequent cycles, a user-friendly MIPD application was developed that enables dose optimization based on the input of obtained 5-FU concentrations during the initial cycle. The application is ready to use and can be tested at https://tanzy1995.shinyapps.io/5FU-shiny-app/ (research use only). A detailed illustration is provided in Fig. S12 of the ESM.

Discussion

In this study, concentration–time data of a pooled 5-FU dataset consisting of various patients with gastrointestinal cancer and different administration regimens was best described using a two-compartment POPPK model with MM elimination. Body surface area allometrically scaled on Vmax was identified as a significant covariate. Subsequently, different methods to determine the best LSS of 5-FU were tested. The best three-sampling design for bolus and continuous infusion is 0.1, 1, and 3 h after the start of 5-FU administration and a two-sampling design without the 3-h point is also applicable. The best three-sampling design for continuous infusion is 2, 3, and 5 h after the start of 5-FU administration. Model-informed precision dosing algorithms were generated for routine clinical dose adjustments with a user-friendly shiny application that enables both dose recommendation for the first cycle and dose adjustments based on concentration measurements in subsequent cycles.

Previously, several 5-FU PK models have been reported [12] with a range of different model structures (one-compartment or two-compartment, linear or non-linear elimination), indications, dosing regimens, and identified covariates as shown in Table S1 of the ESM. These models did not consider the differences in tumor types, administration schedules, and infusion duration. Our study combined different administration schedules, tumor types, and chemotherapy regimens, which allows for a broader application to estimate 5-FU AUC0–inf. Our study only identified IIV on Vmax as most samples were obtained at steady state. Another difference is the sample preservation. In all participating centers of our study, the DPD inhibitor gimeracil was added immediately after the blood collection to avoid the degradation of 5-FU while the other studies did not use this stabilizer, which most likely lead to higher estimated parameters of 5-FU clearance higher than the “true” value. Comparison of the best published model (Briki model [35]) with the present model depicted systematically lower population predicted concentrations at steady state, which indicates that our model could be more precise when estimating the AUC. The body size descriptor BSA was identified and was best captured using an allometric function, while 5-FU is to date also dosed based on BSA with a simple linear BSA-based algorithm. The IOV between first cycle and subsequent cycles was tested; however, no significant or clinically relevant variability was identified, which indicated that the AUC0–inf derived from the previous cycle is sufficiently informative for the prediction of pharmacokinetics in subsequent cycles.

The current study focuses on optimizing LSS for precise 5-FU individual AUC0–inf estimation, using both empirical and model-based approaches. Previous studies of 5-FU proposed different LSS recommendations based on their developed POPPK models while only a few sampling regimens were tested [14, 36–39]. Without model-based methods, trial-and-error selection may miss the best sampling scenarios. The first published strategy was to use one sample at steady state multiplied by the time of continuous infusion in hours to obtain the estimated AUC [39] . This method however has a relatively low precision with a R2 of only 0.67 [37]. Recently, Jacob et al. used multiple linear regression equations and trapezoidal rule to generate a five-sample strategy for bolus and continuous infusion. Two samples were scheduled for the bolus part and three samples were scheduled for the continuous part among which the last sample is obtained at 44 h, which is still a burden for patients [37]. Two prior studies have employed the two-compartment non-linear elimination 5-FU model for limited sampling after bolus administration only: van Kuilenburg et al. [17] demonstrated that a single Bayesian-estimated concentration at 60 min post-bolus could identify DPD-deficient patients (positive predictive values 96%), and Centanni et al. [40] subsequently used this model with a three-sample design in a 0–2 h window post-bolus in simulations showing that MIPD outperforms noncompartmental analysis for AUC estimation. However, neither study provided a validated LSS for AUC-guided dose individualization applicable to a modern combination regimen with a 46-h continuous infusion. In our analysis, we developed a generic model-based LSS workflow to get reasonable and practical LSS by evaluating the Bayesian Fisher information matrix and manually designing 240 sampling time combinations (120 regimens for both administration schedules). Our results refine LSS designs by establishing a simulation-based workflow and achieve high agreements with the “true” AUC values while maintaining low prediction errors. For instance, the selected three-point LSS showed MAPE < 10% and < 10% RMSE, underscoring its reliability. These metrics exceed those reported in prior studies, demonstrating that the inclusion of the Fisher information matrix in LSS development enhances accuracy and adaptability to various clinical scenarios. This approach balances patient adherence and clinical utility, a key challenge identified in previous research [14, 37].

In this analysis, DPYD variants were not found to have a significant impact on the POPPK parameters of 5-FU. Previous studies have similarly failed to identify DPYD variants as covariates in POPPK models, with none reporting a clear association between these genetic factors and 5-FU exposure [12]. An explanation for the lack of association may be the fact that DPYD variants are relatively rare (an estimated 3–8% of the population being partially DPD deficient [41]). Although the van Kuilenburg model [26] identified different PK parameters for DPYD variant carries, the dosing regimen was bolus only and the results are not applicable to a continuous infusion. The limited number of prospective studies evaluating the association between 5-FU exposure and DPYD genotype-guided dosing further complicates definitive conclusions [41]. While dose recommendations for DPYD variant allele carriers are derived from in-vitro and in-vivo data, these guidelines may not be universally applicable because of the high IIV in DPD enzyme activity [41]. Notably, 50% of patients experiencing severe 5-FU toxicity have no documented alterations in the DPYD gene [9]. Individuals with normal DPD enzyme activity may still exhibit elevated plasma 5-FU concentrations, leading to increased toxicity [9]. This suggests that other factors, such as the intracellular accumulation of 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine-5′-monophosphate, might play a more significant role in adverse events such as diarrhea, hand-foot syndrome, mucositis, and myelosuppression [9, 42, 43]. Given the rarity of DPYD variants in the general population, our inclusion of data from the CombiGaPP-2 study, where 87% of patients exhibited partial DPD dysfunction, offers valuable insight into the impact of these variants and contributes to an estimated 90% statistical power at a 0.05 significant level [44]. However, by combining both DPYD testing and MIPD, both toxicity and suboptimal exposure should be improved for the whole population.

To the best of our knowledge, no MIPD tool has previously been developed for 5-FU, and therefore, in the clinic, the dose is empirically adjusted according to observed toxicities. Based on the final 5-FU POPPK model, a dose adjustment application was developed that enables a priori dose optimization based on simulation with patients’ BSA, as well as the subsequent Bayesian dose adjustment with the estimated individual estimates based on measured 5-FU concentrations. Despite this, because of the unclear regulatory pathway of MIPD applications, the developed 5-FU shiny application is currently intended only for research use, rather than as a clinical decision tool. Nevertheless, the final model could also be easily adopted in a certified commercial MIPD tool. The efficacy and safety of MIPD-based dosing and BSA-based dosing of 5-FU will be further evaluated in the ongoing PERFUPANC study (EUCT number: 2024-510576-21-00).

The cellular circadian clock was found to modulate 5-FU metabolic pathways, which is primarily driven by fluctuations in DPD activity [45]. A recently published physiologically based PK model [46] integrated circadian oscillations in DPD activity through sinusoidal functions and simulated drug distribution across tissues to predict individual-specific exposure patterns, and ultimately optimize personalized chronomodulated infusion regimens. However, several sources of evidence revealed that the variability introduced by circadian rhythm on Vmax was only 20% [47], which is relatively small compared with the total variation in plasma concentration contributed by other sources [48] and has no relationship with patient outcomes [49–51]. Therefore, the circadian rhythm was not considered in the present study because of unavailable samples at night.

Our study has some limitations. The first limitation is the individual sample size of the CombiGaPP study where the primary aim was to investigate 5-FU PK across four common DPYD variants and to better define a safe starting dose for DPD-deficient patients. Although this study provided a uniquely high number of patients with DPYD variants that allows us to achieve 90% statistical power to investigate the difference of PK parameters, a median of only two samples per individual at steady state were available in this dataset, and the DPYD variant carriers received pre-emptive dose reductions per Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group guidelines, which could have affected identifiability on covariate effect on Vmax. Therefore, there was insufficient information to properly quantify the impact of the DPYD genotype on 5-FU pharmacokinetics. In addition, variability in infusion pump speed will translate into variability in steady-state plasma concentrations of 5-FU [9], especially with the use of elastomeric pump balloons, which was not considered in our analysis as it is difficult to monitor infusion speed in clinical practice. Further studies are warranted to include this variability in the model. Further studies should also focus on the (bolus) dose regimen optimization as a higher percentage of patients with a bolus and continuous infusion regimen appears to have an exposure beyond the upper target AUC0–inf of 30 mg*h/L and was reported to have more toxicity events [52]. Last but not least, LSS exploration was based on our final developed model only. Therefore, the sampling designs should be evaluated in other 5-FU POPPK models and preferably an external validation with clinical data is warranted to investigate the robustness of the LSS in clinical practice.

Conclusions

The external evaluation of published 5-FU POPPK models against a pooled prospective population with diverse cancer types and dosing regimens resulted after refinement in the development of a generic two-compartment MM PK model with allometrically scaled BSA as covariate on Vmax. Based on these results, we propose to decrease the initial dose to 200 + 2400 mg/m2 for the bolus and continuous infusion, while for the continuous infusion only, the initial dose could be increased to 2800 mg/m2 to reach the target AUC0–inf. The utilization of LSS together with the MIPD shiny application allows for precise estimation of 5-FU AUC0–inf in routine clinical practice and may provide dose individualization recommendations that reduce severe toxicities without compromising efficacy.
