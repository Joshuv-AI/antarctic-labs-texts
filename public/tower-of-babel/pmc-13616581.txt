Rare Variant of a Rare Anomaly: Stenotic Persistent Right-Sided Fifth Aortic Arch with Fourth Arch Interruption
Authors: Alqasem Al Mosa, Michelle Noga, Luke Eckersley, Darren H. Freed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Persistent fifth aortic arch (PFAA) is a very rare anomaly, with an estimated incidence of 0.01%.1 From the initial description in 1969 until 2023, only 104 cases have been reported in the literature,2,3 and few of those have been right-sided PFAA.4, 5, 6

The anomaly is hypothesized to arise from failure of regression of the fifth embryonic aortic arch on one side, which results in a wide range of arch and great vessel configurations including double-lumen aortic arch and true bovine aortic arch.7 Several classifications for PFAA exist based on the presence and type of an interruption and the fifth arch insertion.3 There is, however, little embryological evidence for a fifth pharyngeal arch in normal embryologic development, and some suggested naming rather than a numbering convention for the pharyngeal arches.8, 9, 10

Case Presentation

The patient is a 6-week-old female infant with a prenatal diagnosis of a right aortic arch and aberrant left subclavian artery. The patient was delivered vaginally at 37+1/40, weighing 2.44 kg (16th centile) to a G1P1 mother and was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for respiratory support. Initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was suspicious for a persistent narrowed fifth aortic arch with a peak gradient of 29 mm Hg (Figure 1A, 1B). A cardiac computed tomography (CCT) scan confirmed the diagnosis (Figure 2). The preoperative aortic anatomy was visualized with sweeps through the aortic arches (Videos 1-4). The complex anatomy was further defined by correlating the CCT with the TTE (Figure 3). A right PFAA supplies the descending aorta with focal stenosis proximal to the descending aorta. The true (fourth) arch was moderately hypoplastic, giving rise to the left and right common carotid arteries and the right subclavian artery. The left subclavian artery arises as a fourth branch aberrantly with a Kommerell diverticulum connecting the ligamentum arteriosum to the left pulmonary artery, forming a complete vascular ring. When the patient came for surgery, the immediate preoperative TTE confirmed the above findings and also showed that the patient had a small patent foramen ovale with an otherwise normal intracardiac and segmental anatomy. The fifth aortic arch had narrowed down to 2 mm with a peak pressure gradient of 61 mm Hg and a mean pressure gradient of 22 mm Hg. Intraoperative surgical visual inspection and assessment revealed an atretic connection between the fourth and fifth arches that had not been visualized on preoperative CCT or TTE (Figure 1B, Figure 2A).

The surgical repair was performed via a median sternotomy on full cardiopulmonary bypass at mild hypothermia with cardioplegic arrest and unilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion (Figure 4, Video 5). The cardiopulmonary bypass time was 145 minutes, and the cross-clamp time was 61 minutes. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography and epicardial echocardiography revealed an unobstructed aortic arch with good biventricular function (Videos 6-8). There was no gradient from the ascending aorta to the femoral arterial line by direct needle measure.

The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1 but remained dependent on continuous positive airway pressure. Rigid bronchoscopy revealed moderate-severe tracheal and right dynamic bronchomalacia and bilateral vocal cord paralysis possibly related to intraoperative tissue manipulation, which has been described with variable frequencies after pediatric cardiac surgery.11 Endoscopic repair of glottic and infraglottic stenosis with left arytenoidpexy and lateralization, posterior cricoid split, and right-sided supraglottoplasty was performed. The patient remained in hospital for 1.5 months and was discharged home on nasogastric tube feeds. On follow-up, the patient underwent G-tube insertion and a revision supraglottoplasty.

Discussion

Fifteen years after its original description in humans, the persistence of the fifth aortic arch postnatally has been labeled the “great pretender” for its mimicry and elusive embryologic origin.2,12 Although this anomaly can be suspected on prenatal ultrasound, as in our case and previous publications, the precise diagnosis often requires multiple postnatal imaging modalities.13 Echocardiography is greatly valuable in the assessment of this anomaly; however, even with the use of specific approaches such as the left ventricular outflow tract and suprasternal views, it remains a screening tool for the diagnosis of PFAA, and further tomographic imaging is usually necessary for confirmation.1 Cardiac computed tomography has been found to be more precise in delineating the vascular tree origins, branching patterns, and arch anomalies with their special orientation compared to echocardiography, but blood flow pattern assessment remains better studied with echocardiography.14 Cardiovascular magnetic resonance can be a great tool in diagnosing vascular and aortic arch anomalies, but its applicability is limited by its availability, expense, and the effects of the heart rate on the imaging quality.14,15

This patient's configuration of PFAA is unique in that the aortic arch is right sided and stenotic and the true fourth arch is interrupted along with an aberrant left subclavian artery. Multimodality imaging along with intraoperative inspection had to be undertaken to fully understand the anatomy. The surgical repair entailed amalgamation of the 2 aortic arches and reimplantation of the aberrant left subclavian artery anteriorly, in essence turning this anatomy into a right-sided aortic arch with mirror image branching in which the first branch is a left innominate artery giving rise to the left carotid and left subclavian arteries, the second branch is a right carotid artery, and the third branch is a right subclavian artery.

Conclusion

We report on a rare variation of PFAA and our repair technique. The PFAA was right sided with distal stenosis, and the true arch was interrupted with aberrant left subclavian artery. Fetal diagnosis was suspected, and the diagnosis was confirmed with echocardiography and computed tomography. An elective repair was performed, which included amalgamating the 2 arches.
