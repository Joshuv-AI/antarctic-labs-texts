Spatio-temporal analysis of dengue outbreaks in India (2021–2025): evidence from the secondary analysis of Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme
Authors: Shrinivasa B Marinaik, Vani H Chalageri, Preethi R Reval, VidyaShree Manjunath, Harshitha Jithendra, Avinash Manjunath, Kajol Bankoti, Gunjan Negi, Viprasha Tomer, Anup Anvikar

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDengue fever is an emerging public health challenge in India, with outbreaks influenced by environmental factors. Understanding these dynamics is essential to advance from containment to early warning systems. The study aimed to (1) categorising outbreaks by Indian Meteorological Department climatic zones to assess burden; (2) mapping transmission architecture via Global Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi Hotspot Analysis, spatial network graphs (K-nearest neighbour, distance threshold, minimum spanning trees (MSTs)) and degree centrality and (3) calculating velocity, measured by effective reproductive number (Rt), using Bayesian renewal equation models.MethodsThe study analysed weekly outbreak reports from a national-level report from the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme between 2021 and 2025.ResultsAnalysis of 774 outbreaks (35 202 cases; 195 deaths; case fatality rate (CFR): 0.55%) identified transmission patterns. Maharashtra and Kerala accounted for 52.4% of all outbreaks, with South Peninsular states recording pre-monsoon transmission windows. Rural transmission correlated with a CFR (3.08%) compared with urban clusters (0.29%), indicating case ascertainment bias. MST topology identified ‘super-spreader’ district hubs that served as focal points for dissemination. These hubs accelerated transmission with effective reproductive number (Rt) at 4.0 within a 3-week period, representing a 1.38-fold increase relative to state-level baseline Rt estimates.ConclusionIntegrating syndromic data with climatic classifications, spatial network topology and Bayesian Rt modelling provides a framework for understanding disease spread and informing targeted public health interventions across low- and middle-income countries.Keywords: Disease Vectors, Disease Outbreaks, Sentinel Surveillance, Basic Reproduction Number, Space-Time Clustering

Introduction

Dengue fever is one of the mosquito-borne diseases and a public health issue across the subcontinent.1 2 Dengue is caused by the flavivirus agent, mosquito acts as a vector and the humans host. Infection is caused by co-circulating serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4).3 The virus is transmitted by anthropophilic Aedes aegypti in urban environments, alongside secondary vectors Aedes albopictus.3 The survival and proliferation of these vectors are influenced by environmental thresholds; inter-epidemic transmission remains low, but monsoon conditions, specifically temperatures of 25°C±5°C coupled with ~80% relative humidity, trigger vector proliferation in water collections.2 4 The geographic spread of the virus into non-endemic territories is driven by the movement of human hosts during their viraemic phase.3

The burden of dengue in India includes outbreaks, morbidity and economic impacts on the healthcare system.5 6 Despite vector control, transmission persists due to urbanisation, demographic vulnerabilities and endemic reservoirs that sustain endemicity.7–10 While the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) provides a national framework for tracking febrile illnesses and mapping the burden of co-circulating arboviruses,11 interventions remain containment-focused. Containment strategies are usually deployed only after a localised incidence curve has accelerated past thresholds.

Transitioning to surveillance requires understanding the transmission dynamics driving these outbreaks. Recent epidemiological studies using meteorological data show that vector proliferation and the incubation period depend on temperature and monsoonal fluctuations.2 4 Because the Indian subcontinent has climatic diversity, outbreaks must be categorised by Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) climatic zones rather than national averages.12 This reveals vulnerabilities, such as the transmission windows driven by the Northeast Monsoon in the South Peninsular states.13 14 Spatial transmission is rarely uniform. Recent advances in spatial network topology demonstrate that transmission is driven by ‘super-spreader’ district hubs. These are geographic hubs that seed broader regional outbreaks through localised clustering and network connectivity.1 15 Broad state-level incidence masks these micro-dynamics.7 It is necessary to apply mathematical tools like minimum spanning trees (MSTs) and Bayesian velocity models at the district level.

A gap exists in epidemiological literature. With few resolutions, nationwide analyses integrate syndromic surveillance data with spatial network topologies and velocity metrics like the effective reproduction number (Rt).5 To establish the foundation required for climate-informed early warning systems, this study analyses the architecture of dengue transmission in India. The study pursued three objectives: to (1) categorising outbreaks by IMD climatic zones to assess burden12 16; (2) mapping transmission architecture via Global Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi Hotspot Analysis, spatial network graphs (K-nearest neighbour (KNN), distance threshold, MSTs) and degree centrality1 15; (3) calculating velocity, measured by Effective Reproductive Number (Rt), using Bayesian renewal equation models,17 contrasting the macro-level trajectories of states against the incidence surges of focal district hubs. This analysis establishes the empirical baseline required to transition national frameworks from reactive containment to predictive early warning systems.

Methods

Overview of the data source: IDSP and Integrated Health Information Platform transition

The IDSP is a surveillance framework established in India in November 2004 by the Government of India, initially launched with assistance from The World Bank. The system is anchored by the Central Surveillance Unit integrated within the National Centre for Disease Control in Delhi, which coordinates with a network of State Surveillance Units and District Surveillance Units across all Indian states and territories. Under the IDSP mandate, weekly surveillance data on epidemic-prone diseases is collected via paper-based reporting in three formats: ‘S’ (syndromic), ‘P’ (probable) and ‘L’ (laboratory-confirmed). However, starting from 2021, the system underwent a transition to the Integrated Health Information Platform (IHIP).

The IHIP enables case-based, disaggregated surveillance in near real-time via mobile and electronic devices. For this study, the IHIP framework automatically links the forms and introduces mandatory geo-tagging for geographic referencing. This transition provides the methodological justification for the selected study period (2021–2025), as it marks the era where inclusive syndromic data became available for spatial and mathematical modelling.

Study design and data extraction

This study employs an observational and spatial-temporal design to analyse the epidemiological and climatic determinants of dengue outbreaks in India. Data regarding dengue outbreaks were extracted from the IDSP weekly PDF reports published by the Government of India.18 Under the updated IHIP mandate, even NIL reporting is mandatory across all units, ensuring complete spatial coverage. Dengue fever represents one of the 36 priority conditions (33 diseases/syndromes and 3 animal bites) continuously monitored by the platform.

To increase statistical power and ensure national representation, the study size was determined by sampling all available IDSP reports over the 5-year window (January 2021–December 2025). Through manual review and review of archival documents, outbreak data was extracted during the second and third weeks of May 2026 from IDSP weekly PDF reports spanning the 5-year study period. For each identified dengue outbreak, a set of variables was extracted from the tabular data. This included the outbreak ID, an alphanumeric code introduced by the system to track epidemic clusters (formatted as state/district/year/week/disease code), alongside the state/UT, district, number of cases, number of deaths, date of start of outbreak and date of reporting. Resolution of geographic markers (eg, villages, sub-districts or health centres) and clinical confirmation details were manually parsed from the qualitative narratives within the ‘Comments/Action taken’ column. This manual extraction yielded a baseline dataset of 800 geographically resolved outbreak records (2021: 128; 2022: 88; 2023: 273; 2024: 181; 2025: 130).

Case definition and co-infection exclusion

As per the National Center for Vector Borne Diseases Control and IDSP modified case definitions,19 dengue cases are classified during outbreaks. A probable case (P form) is defined as a febrile illness of 2–7 days duration presenting with two or more manifestations (headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, haemorrhagic manifestations or leucopenia) or testing positive via a non-ELISA Diagnostic Test (RDT). A laboratory-confirmed case (L form) is defined by compatible clinical presentation and laboratory evidence of dengue infection, including NS1 antigen, IgM antibodies, PCR detection, virus isolation or seroconversion. As IDSP reports include both dengue-only and co-infection outbreaks, 26 mixed outbreaks (eg, dengue with chikungunya) were excluded to avoid confounding, restricting the analysis only to dengue outbreaks.

Study variables

For clarity, two analytical units were established for this study. For the spatial network topology models, the unit of analysis was the outbreak centroid, enabling the structural mapping of cluster connectivity. Conversely, for the estimation of transmission velocity, the analytical unit was the case of incidence volume aggregated at both the state and district boundaries. The outcome variables included the count of dengue outbreaks, case volumes and velocity (effective reproduction number, Rt). The exposure variable was the climatic zone and monsoon phase (derived from IMD data). Confounding from co-circulating arboviruses was controlled by excluding multi-pathogen outbreaks (dengue-chikungunya and dengue-malaria co-infections) during the data extraction phase. Autocorrelation was accounted for via network topology models to prevent overestimating the independence of outbreaks.

Spatiotemporal mapping and climatic categorisation

Total of 774 outbreaks were categorised into the four IMD climatic zones.12 Rather than integrating climatic datasets, Indian states and Union Territories were grouped into these zones to reflect their profiles: Northwest India (eg, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Jammu & Kashmir), Central India (eg, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha), East and Northeast India (eg, Bihar, West Bengal, Assam, Manipur) and South Peninsular India (eg, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh). Temporal distribution was subsequently stratified to contrast Southwest Monsoon transmission calendar (June–September) against the Northeast Monsoon cycle (October–December) driving transmission in South Peninsular states. This stratification of dengue outbreaks by monsoonal and climatic phase is supported by previous spatiotemporal studies across the Indian Subcontinent.13 14 16

Spatial network topology modelling

To mathematically quantify the structural connectivity and spatial diffusion patterns of dengue outbreaks (objective 2), a pipeline was employed. First, Global Moran’s I was calculated to test for autocorrelation and validate the distribution of outbreaks across the landscape. Next, Getis-Ord Gi Hotspot Analysis was conducted at the district level to mathematically identify clusters (hotspots, p<0.05) versus cold spots.

Following this validation, graph G=(V, E) was constructed for each state containing two or more outbreaks each year, where vertices V represent outbreaks and edges E represent connectivity. Three spatial network algorithms were applied using the ‘NetworkX’20 and ‘scikit-learn’21 Python libraries: (1) a KNN network (k=3) identifying clustering; (2) distance threshold network (d≤150 km) acting as a proxy for commuting routes and (3) an MST connecting all outbreak nodes while minimising distance without cycles, thereby extracting the ‘backbone’ of transmission corridors. Finally, to mathematically isolate the districts acting as engines, degree centrality was calculated for each node. Estimation of the effective reproductive number (Rt)

Building on the framework identified via spatial network topology, this study sought to evaluate velocity and contrast trajectories (objective 3). Based on the literature, a serial interval with a mean of 16 days and a deviation (SD) of 4 days was used, alongside a Gamma prior (mean=5, SD=5). To mitigate discretisation bias inherent in IDSP data, incidence was reconstructed using a distribution across the week, and Rt was evaluated over a 28-day smoothing window. To prevent instability driven by denominators, Rt was mathematically censored during periods via a mask (<50 cases for states; <20 cases for districts). Finally, to account for artefacts, interpretations were clamped to a limit of 4.0 for dengue virus transmission, ensuring only phases were analysed.

Results

During the 5-year study period (2021–2025), a baseline dataset of 800 geographically resolved outbreak records was identified through the IDSP archives. After applying exclusion criteria, 26 records representing co-infections were removed to prevent confounding. The analytical cohort consisted of 774 outbreak events.

Spatiotemporal distribution and climatic patterns

Overall burden and annual trends

Between 2021 and 2025, a total of 774 geographically resolved, dengue outbreaks were recorded through the IDSP network. These outbreaks were associated with 35 202 reported cases and 195 deaths. The cumulative case fatality rate (CFR) was 0.55% (95% CI 0.48% to 0.63%) (figure 1). The median outbreak size was 25.0 cases per outbreak (IQR: 12.2–41.9; range: 1–3007), reflecting heterogeneity in cluster size driven by ‘super-spreader’ district hubs.

Annual incidence demonstrated variability. Epidemic intensity peaked in 2023, which accounted for 34.6% of total outbreaks (n=268) and 11 218 cases. This represented a +219.0% year-on-year (YoY) increase in outbreak count and a +499.3% surge in case burden relative to 2022 (online supplemental table 1). This volatility was driven by district-level outbreaks. A different district dominated the national case burden each year, ranging from Imphal West (Manipur) in 2021, to Jammu (J&K) in 2023 and Firozabad (Uttar Pradesh) in 2024 (online supplemental table 1). The preceding year (2022) exhibited lower epidemic activity (n=84 outbreaks; −33.3% YoY; cases: 1872; −79.4% YoY); however, this window recorded the highest CFR of the study period (1.23%, 95% CI 0.82% to 1.84%). Following the 2023 peak, an epidemic contraction was observed through 2024 (−34.7% outbreaks; −26.7% cases) and 2025 (−30.9% outbreaks; −41.5% cases), with declining CFRs (2024: 0.36%, 95% CI 0.26% to 0.52%; 2025: 0.27%, 95% CI 0.16% to 0.46%).

Spatial distribution and endemicity

Dengue transmission was concentrated across the Indian subcontinent (figure 1). The top three states, Maharashtra (34.4%, n=266), Kerala (18.0%, n=139) and Madhya Pradesh (10.7%, n=83), accounted for 63.0% of all national outbreaks. Expanding to the top five states (adding Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu) captures 74.2% of total outbreak events, demonstrating geographic clustering (online supplemental table 2).

Outbreak frequency did not linearly correlate with case burden. Uttar Pradesh (6.3% of outbreaks; n=49) reported the highest case burden nationally (n=7927 cases; 161.8 cases/outbreak). Firozabad represented the largest outlier, where a single outbreak event (5754 cases) accounted for 72.6% of the state’s 5-year burden. In contrast, Maharashtra recorded a high volume of outbreaks (n=266) with a low cases-per-outbreak ratio (14.3 cases/outbreak).

While Kerala maintained a low CFR (0.25%, 95% CI 0.14% to 0.45%), Maharashtra recorded a CFR of 3.08% (95% CI 2.58% to 3.67%), accounting for 60.0% of all national dengue deaths (117/195). High-mortality anomalies were observed in Punjab (CFR 8.82%, 95% CI 4.16% to 17.82%; n=6 deaths/68 cases) and Odisha (CFR 4.00%, 95% CI 1.59% to 9.70%; n=4 deaths/100 cases). While these percentages are partially artefacts-driven by low case denominators, the anomaly in Punjab aligns with recent geospatial studies confirming the stability of persistent febrile illness hotspots across the state over the last decade.22

Seasonal and climatic dynamics

Temporal analysis mapped against India’s climatology revealed two seasonal transmission paradigms (figure 2A–E). In most of the subcontinent (dominated by the Southwest Monsoon), outbreaks were concentrated during the rainy season from June to September (n=327, 58.9%), extending into a post-monsoon tail from October to December (n=161, 29.0%).

Conversely, the South Peninsular states, which are influenced by the Northeast Monsoon, exhibited a temporal distribution. This region experienced its highest frequency during the pre-monsoon period (March–May, n=93, 42.5%), exhibiting a fourfold divergence compared with the Southwest Monsoon-dominant regions of India (10.5%), maintaining sustained transmission through both the Southwest and Northeast monsoon phases (online supplemental table 3). Spatial network topology and super-spreader district hubs

Global autocorrelation confirmed that dengue outbreaks were clustered rather than randomly distributed (Global Moran’s I=0.040, Z=3.13, p=0.0017; online supplemental table 4). Getis-Ord Gi hotspot analysis (figure 3D) identified statistically significant incidence of hotspots concentrated along the coastal corridors and southern peninsula. Topological analysis of the 774 resolved outbreaks confirmed spatial heterogeneity of transmission. Rather than geographical wave propagation, spread was characterised by localised clustering (figure 3A,B). To quantify this architecture of outbreak propagation, MST spatial network graphs were constructed from district-level outbreak centroids (figure 3C). Across the 5-year study period, the MST networks had a mean of 135.6±67.2 edges, a mean degree of 1.72±0.11 and a mean largest cluster size of 58.8±33.6 outbreaks (online supplemental table 4). The 2023 epidemic year produced the largest transmission backbone: 244 MST edges, a graph diameter of 18 topological steps, and a largest connected cluster encompassing 109 outbreak nodes, consistent with its status as the highest-incidence year.

Network degree centrality (CD) identified topological ‘super-spreader’ district hubs acting as anchors (online supplemental table 5). The national transmission network was centralised across all years. Satara (Maharashtra) functioned as the hub in 2021 with the highest centrality recorded in the entire study (CD=11), meaning it was directly connected to 11 other outbreak nodes. This dominance persisted independently of its moderate case count (n=7). In subsequent years, Raichur (Karnataka, CD=8), Kathua (J&K, CD=8), Indore (Madhya Pradesh, CD=6) and Palakkad (Kerala, CD=6) functioned as primary regional anchors. Notably, several of these high-centrality districts recorded only moderate case counts despite their structural connectivity.

Temporal transmission dynamics and velocity (Rt)

To quantify the velocity of these identified hubs and assess whether spikes in transmission potential (Rt) preceded outbreak surges, temporal overlays were generated for both the macro (state) and micro (district) scales.

State-level macro-dynamics

At the state level (figure 4, left column), population denominators produced Rt trajectories representing seasonal baselines (online supplemental table 6). Kerala demonstrated the endemic persistence nationally, recording 53 weeks where Rt>1.0 across the study period, with a mean Rt of 1.59 (SD: 0.71). Maharashtra similarly maintained sustained transmission (49 weeks, mean Rt=1.48, SD: 0.65). Across all five state-level trajectories, Rt curves breached the epidemic threshold (Rt=1.0) during the pre-monsoon weeks, preceding peak case burdens by 4–6 weeks.

District-level micro-dynamics

At the focal district level (figure 4, right column), velocity exhibited higher mean Rt and SD values indicating burst dynamics. Across the five paired district-state comparisons, district-level mean Rt exceeded the corresponding state baseline in four of five cases, resulting in a mean macro-micro amplification ratio of 1.38 (online supplemental table 6).

The divergence was observed in Jammu District (J&K): the state of J&K averaged a baseline mean Rt of 1.79 (SD: 0.89) over 14 active weeks, while Jammu District escalated to a mean Rt of 2.99 (SD: 1.15; amplification ratio: 1.67), achieving this velocity over a window of only three epidemic weeks before the susceptible population depleted and Rt collapsed below 1.0. Palakkad (Kerala) generated a mean Rt of 2.35 (SD: 0.94) versus Kerala’s state baseline of 1.59 (amplification: 1.48); Pune (Maharashtra) produced mean Rt=2.33 (amplification: 1.57). The SD values at the district level (range: 0.76–1.15) were consistently elevated relative to state-level SDs (0.65–0.89).

Discussion

Findings: national syndromic burden

The aim of this study was to architecture and velocity of dengue outbreaks across India. By integrating spatial network topology with Bayesian renewal equation models, this study demonstrates that dengue burden in India is heterogeneous in both scale and transmission dynamics. The analysis of 774 outbreaks (2021–2025) revealed a dissociation between outbreak frequency and endemic intensity (figure 1; online supplemental tables 1 and 2). The macro-level clustering observed a single localised outbreak in Firozabad accounting for 72.6% of Uttar Pradesh’s 5-year case burden, highlighting the need for geographic precision. Clinical and epidemiological studies confirm that 2021 surge in Firozabad was driven by the DENV-2 serotype, leading to a crisis of clinical manifestations and paediatric hospitalisations.23 The geographic variations in CFR, including the mortality spike observed during the 2022 transmission window (figure 1), are consistent with the under-ascertainment of mild cases during low-intensity surveillance periods. The data expose a case of ascertainment bias linked directly to outbreak morphology (online supplemental table 2). Maharashtra experienced the highest CFR (3.08%) despite relatively diffuse transmission (14.3 cases/outbreak), consistent with under-detection of mild infections in rural settings where surveillance may preferentially capture severe, hospitalised cases. This estimate closely aligns with recent systematic reviews reporting a pooled laboratory-confirmed CFR of approximately 2.6% in India.24 Uttar Pradesh, on the other hand, exhibited point-source outbreaks (161.8 cases/outbreak) but maintained low CFR (0.29%). From a vector ecology standpoint, these urban clusters are driven by human populations and breeding sites, enabling the virus to deplete the susceptible population pool. While this generates high case volumes, it simultaneously triggers mass-screening efforts (especially in response to virulent DENV-2 strains) that capture the base of mild infections, diluting the reported CFR.

Climatic drivers and the atypical peninsular transmission window

These findings are consistent with recent studies highlighting the complexity of dengue transmission. Although ensemble models have demonstrated the impact of climatic factors on annual dengue incidence across India,2 our spatiotemporal classification indicates that national-level surveillance frameworks may be insufficient for effective early warning. As depicted in figure 2A–E and online supplemental table 3, while most regions of the country experience transmission patterns linked to Southwest Monsoon-driven vector expansion, the South Peninsular region displays a distinct epidemiological profile associated with the Northeast Monsoon. Further analysis reveals that the South Peninsular experiences 42.5% of its total outbreak burden during the pre-monsoon months (March–May), diverging from the rest of the country (10.5%). This pre-monsoon surge aligns closely with recent entomological evidence demonstrating that water scarcity during summer forces domestic water storage (eg, cement tanks, plastic drums) in Southern India. This creates drought-resistant Aedes breeding habitats well before the rain arrives.25 These temporal changes confirm that climatic boundaries4 necessitate tailored models rather than national alert thresholds.

Spatial network topology: validating ‘super-spreader’ hubs

Rather than state-level epidemics, the topological model confirms transmission is driven by connected ‘super-spreader’ district hubs (such as Satara and Palakkad) that act as an anchor for regional dissemination (figure 3A,B, online supplemental table 4). This phenomenon is epitomised by Satara (Maharashtra) in 2021 (online supplemental table 5). Despite recording only seven total cases, Satara reported the highest centrality (CD=11) in the entire study, functioning as the primary bridge node. This validates network epidemiology theory, confirming that a district does not need to be an epicentre to drive regional epidemics: it must act as a transport crossroads connecting communities.26 These bridge nodes derive their centrality from major interstate transport corridors that facilitate high levels of human mobility. Recent spatiotemporal modelling has shown that infected travellers moving along highway networks contribute to dengue dissemination beyond the biological flight range of Aedes mosquitoes.27 The identification of these hubs supports their role as structural bridges rather than transmission epicentres, indicating that geographic connectivity and human movement, rather than incidence alone, drive regional spread.15

Recent spatial studies in India have increasingly applied Getis-Ord Gi hotspot analysis to identify localised dengue vulnerability, including ward-level hotspots in Delhi, Bhopal and Chennai.28 29 While such approaches are valuable for municipal vector control, integrating Global Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi hotspot analysis, KNN and distance-threshold models, and MSTs extends the analysis from local clusters to broader transmission networks. Quantified through degree centrality, this framework links district-level hotspots into a transmission backbone, illustrating how localised hotspots can function as super-spreader nodes that seed epidemics across state boundaries.

Rt as a predictive early warning signal

Temporal modelling confirms that state-level incidence masks district-level transmission. As evidenced in figure 4 and online supplemental table 6, a mean of 1.38-fold amplification in Rt was observed within structural ‘super-spreader’ district hubs compared with their respective states. The Rt values observed at the state level reflect transmission dilution across geographies. In contrast, the SD values at the district level confirm the nature of super-spreader hub dynamics. Epidemiologically, passive surveillance suffers from reporting delays. By using a Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo framework with smoothing windows, the Rt models successfully mitigate artefacts to provide real-time ‘nowcasting’.30 While Bayesian inference in LMICs is often restricted to genomic studies, these temporal overlays validate that Bayesian Rt applied to aggregate syndromic data serves as a leading indicator, providing a signal that precedes peak case burdens by 4–6 weeks. The intersection of climate, topology and velocity creates a public health issue. A connected bridge node (such as Palakkad) becomes dangerous if its velocity accelerates during a pre-monsoon window. By seeding the virus across the network well before monsoonal alert thresholds are triggered, these hubs can trigger regional dissemination.

Dengue outbreak in India demonstrated marked spatial and temporal heterogeneity, where climatic variability and highly connected district hubs shape the timing and intensity of spread. Integrating routine syndromic surveillance with climatic classification, spatial network analytics and Bayesian Rt modelling provides an evidence-based framework for early warning, risk stratification and targeted public health interventions in India and other low-and middle-income countries.

Limitations and potential bias

Several limitations must be acknowledged, stemming from the use of syndromic surveillance data. The IDSP framework relies on reporting, which suffers from under-reporting and misclassification, especially in rural areas with diagnostic infrastructure. This is a challenge recognised across the entire WHO South-East Asia Region.31 As recent decadal analyses have proven, the IDSP remains the primary source for tracking the syndemic burden of co-circulating arboviruses on a national scale.11 The incidence of dengue is likely underestimated in this study, reflecting a reporting bias. While data hygiene protocols (such as excluding co-infections) were implemented to mitigate confounding, the lack of disaggregated, individual-level clinical data prevents analysis of socioeconomic or demographic determinants. Ecological fallacy remains at risk if these macro-level spatial dynamics are incorrectly extrapolated to predict individual risk profiles.

Generalisability

Regardless of certain limitations, this study supports external validation by incorporating the frameworks like geocoding, network topology, Rt estimation by using Bayesian renewal equations. This study can be generalised to other monsoon-dependent endemic regions globally, where transmission dynamics are focal.
