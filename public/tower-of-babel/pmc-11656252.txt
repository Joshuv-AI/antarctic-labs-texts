TiO2–Mo2C Heterostructure for Enhanced Electrocatalytic Nitrogen Reduction to Ammonia
Authors: Junmei Wang, Qingkun Tian, Li Chen, Maoyou Yang, Xia Zhang, Xiaodan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The development of catalysts with high activity and selectivity for the electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) remains crucial. Molybdenum carbide (Mo2C) shows promise as an electrocatalyst for NRR but faces challenges due to the difficulty of N2 adsorption and activation as well as the competitive hydrogen evolution reaction. In this study, we propose a strategy of combining TiO2 with Mo2C to form heterostructure catalysts. Our first-principles theoretical calculations indicate that the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure exhibits enhanced N2 adsorption and activation, attributed to the increased interaction between the π4d* orbital of Mo and the π2p* orbital of N2, facilitated by the directional modulation of Mo’s d-orbitals by TiO2. A more positive integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population and an elongated N≡N bond length prove this. Additionally, the higher Gibbs free energy for N2 compared to that for H demonstrates a preference for N2 adsorption. We further elucidate the catalytic mechanism for converting N2 to NH3 on the TiO2–Mo2C surface, identifying the associative distal pathway as the dominant route over the associative alternating pathway. This work highlights unique advantages of the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure for the NRR and provides theoretical guidance for designing efficient NRR electrocatalysts.

Introduction

Ammonia (NH3), an essential chemical product and carbon-free energy carrier, is traditionally synthesized via the Haber–Bosch process, which demands high temperature and pressure (350–550 °C and 150–350 atm, respectively).1−3 These harsh reaction conditions lead to significant energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions.4,5 Therefore, the development of sustainable and cost-efficient methods for ammonia synthesis is crucial. The electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) has emerged as a promising alternative, converting earth-abundant N2 and H2O into NH3 under ambient conditions using electrocatalysts.6,7 Nevertheless, the achievable ammonia production rates and Faradaic efficiencies remain limited due to constrained catalyst activity rooted from the high energy barrier for N2 adsorption and activation, alongside intense competition of the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER).8−11 Exploring suitable catalysts with high activity, selectivity, and stability to enable efficient electrocatalytic NRR is urgent.12−17

Significant efforts have been dedicated to investigating Mo-based catalysts for NRR, inspired by the natural N2 fixation abilities of Fe–Mo enzyme nitrogenases.18−20 Recently, molybdenum carbide (Mo2C) has attracted attention due to the special d-orbitals of Mo, which can donate slightly electrons to the π-antibonding orbitals of N2, reinforcing N2 adsorption and weakening N≡N bonds.21−23 For instance, Zhang et al. demonstrated that N2 molecules can be stably adsorbed and effectively activated on the basal planes of 1T-Mo2C, with a maximum reaction Gibbs free energy of 0.52 eV, as calculated using density functional theory (DFT).18 Their studies indicated that the reaction involves the elongation of N≡N bonds and charge transfer to *NxHy groups (x = 1 and 2; 0 ≤ y ≤ 5), except *N2H4 and *NH3, primarily from the exposed Mo atoms. Additionally, Lv et al. synthesized Mo2C nanosheets anchored on hierarchically porous carbon via a salt-template method, achieving a high NH3 yield rate of 12.55 ± 1.04 μg/h/mg at −0.20 V vs RHE.24 However, the competitive HER often impedes the NRR process, resulting in a low Faradaic efficiency. Moreover, the catalytic mechanisms for most catalysts are not clear.

Constructing heterostructures is an effective strategy to modify the electronic structures of Mo2C and suppress HER, thereby enhancing NRR activity and selectivity.25−27 Numerous studies have shown that TiO2 is highly suitable for NRR due to its long-term thermodynamic stability, natural abundance, and nontoxicity.28−31 The experiments have demonstrated that composite materials made from TiO2 and Mo2C exhibit exceptional nitrogen fixation performance.32 In this study, we combined TiO2 with Mo2C to form a heterostructure and investigated its performance through DFT calculations. The orbital interactions and electron transfer between N2 and Mo reveal that the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure not only exhibits enhanced N2 adsorption and activation but also effectively suppresses HER. The directional modulation of Mo’s π4d* orbital by TiO2 significantly improves the interaction with the π2p* orbital of N2, which is a key factor driving this enhancement. For a more detailed analysis, we calculated the values of the crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) and the bond lengths of reaction intermediates. Additionally, we compared the Gibbs free energy of N2 and H adsorption on the catalyst surface, confirming the heterostructure’s preference for N2, which is crucial for optimizing catalytic selectivity. By mapping the energy profile of the NRR, we identify the associative distal pathway as the dominant mechanism on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure. This work not only highlights the unique advantages of the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure but also provides theoretical guidance for exploring Mo-based materials with great potential for the NRR.

Methods

DFT calculations were conducted using the Quantum ESPRESSO (QE) package with projector-augmented wave (PAW) methods and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation (PBE-GGA) exchange-correlation functional.33−35 The plane wave and density plane wave cutoffs were set to 100 and 800 Ry, respectively. The convergence accuracies for atomic relaxation and self-consistency were set to 10–3 Ry/Bohr (∼0.025 eV/Å) and 10–12 Ry, respectively. The electronic structure was sampled using the Monkhorst–Pack method in the Brillouin zone with a k-point grid of 5 × 5 × 1 determined for the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure after testing (as shown in Figure S1). A vacuum spacing of 15 Å was introduced to prevent interactions between periodic slabs. The DFT-D3 method was used to account for van der Waals interactions between the adsorbed molecules and layers, effectively capturing long-range dispersion interactions. The Local-Orbital Basis Suite Towards Electronic-Structure Reconstruction (LOBSTER) software package was then utilized to calculate the density of states (DOS) and COHP.36

Results and Discussion

Adsorption Characteristics for N2

The lattice constants obtained from our calculations for the TiO2 (110) surface are a = 10.45 and b = 3.80 Å, while those for the Mo2C (100) surface are a = 5.22 and b = 6.06 Å. We constructed the heterostructure using a 1 × 3 supercell of the TiO2 (110) surface with a thickness of 2.94 Å and a 2 × 2 supercell of the Mo2C (100) surface with a thickness of 3.58 Å. By employing the method of balanced mismatch, the lattice constants of the heterostructure were set to a = 10.44 Å and b = 11.77 Å, resulting in mismatches of 0.06% in the x direction and 3.02% in the y direction. The average Mo–O bond length at the interface between TiO2 and Mo2C in the heterostructure is 2.265 Å. The top and side views of the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, with Mo2C (101) as the active surface, are illustrated in Figure 1. The adsorption configurations and energies of N2 at different sites on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure are shown in Figure 2. Here, Ti (e.g., T1 and T2) denotes the top-site adsorption of N2 on various Mo atoms, while Hi (e.g., H0 and H1) represents hollow-site adsorption. Additional information regarding these adsorption configurations is provided in Figures S2–S4. The adsorption energy for N2 molecules (ΔEN2) on the pure Mo2C structure is −1.13116 eV, while most ΔEN2 values on the hollow sites of the heterostructure are lower than this. In particular, the H3 site exhibits the lowest ΔEN2 of −1.32514 eV, indicating the preferential adsorption of N2. This enhanced adsorption is primarily attributed to the electronic structure modulation of Mo by TiO2, which will be discussed further below.

We calculated the DOS for two surface and one subsurface Mo atoms in the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, as indicated in Figure 1a, and compared to that of pure Mo2C. As depicted in Figure 3a, the d-band center of Mo atoms in the heterostructure generally shifts upward compared to that in the pure Mo2C structure. Typically, we employ the d-band center as a descriptor of the catalytic capability. However, the linearity between the d-band centers and ΔEN2 is weak for both top and hollow-site adsorptions. To further investigate the adsorption and activation of N2 molecules, we introduce the COHP of free and adsorbed N2 on both the TiO2–Mo2C and pure Mo2C systems in Figure 3b. For free N2 molecules, the molecular orbitals include σ2s, σ2s*, π2p, σ2p, π2p*, and σ2p*, with the σ2s orbital omitted for clarity. Here, the * indicates antibonding orbitals. Notably, the σ2s* and σ2p orbitals form secondary bonds due to their small energy difference, causing the originally antibonding σ2s* orbital to exhibit weak bonding characteristics, while the inherently bonding σ2p orbital becomes a weaker antibonding orbital.

For N2 adsorption on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure and pure Mo2C, we should consider the orbital interactions between Mo and free N2. Figure 3a,b shows that the σ4d orbital of Mo aligns closely in energy and symmetry with the σ2s* and σ2p orbitals of N2. Their interaction leads to an overall downward shift in energy for the σ2s* and σ2p orbitals of N2. In the case of weakly overlapping top-site adsorption on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, the σ2p orbital retains its antibonding character. However, for the hollow sites of pure Mo2C and the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, stronger overlap causes the σ2p orbital to transition from weakly antibonding to weakly bonding and partial electron transfer from the σ2p orbital to levels above the Fermi level. This is manifested as electrons transfer from the 2s and 2pz orbitals of N2 to the 4d orbitals of Mo, reinforcing the N≡N bond and impeding N2 adsorption and subsequent hydrogenation. Moreover, the π4d* orbital of Mo interacts with the π2p* orbital of N2 due to their close energies and matched symmetries. This overlap leads to the filling of some π2p* orbitals below the Fermi level. This classic antibonding filling mechanism significantly weakens the N≡N bond, promoting the adsorption of N2 and subsequent hydrogenation reactions. For the π2p orbital of N2 and the π4d orbital of Mo, as well as the σ2p* orbital of N2 and the σ4d* orbital of Mo, the substantial energy difference leads to a minor impact on the overall energy.

Therefore, the adsorption of N2 on Mo2C can be understood as the transfer of electrons from the σ2p orbital of N2 to Mo and from Mo to the π2p* orbital of N2. The former process is detrimental, while the latter is beneficial for the NRR processes. Due to the stronger bonding strength and closer to the Fermi level of the π2p* orbital of N2, the contribution of the antibonding filling process is more significant during N2 adsorption. To achieve higher NRR performance, it is essential to shift the π4d* orbital of Mo closer to the π2p* orbital of N2. As shown in Figure 3a, the π-bonds of Mo in pure Mo2C and Ti in pure TiO2 are relatively close to the Fermi level but are farther from the π2p* orbital of N2. However, when Mo2C and TiO2 form a heterostructure, the π4d* orbital of Mo at the interface is influenced by TiO2 and shifts upward, subsequently affecting the surface Mo atoms, as indicated by the atoms labeled 0–2 in Figure 3a. Thus, by directionally modulating the d-orbitals of Mo through the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, the surface adsorption capacity of N2 is enhanced, leading to an improved NRR performance.

The integrated COHP (ICOHP), calculated by integrating the energy up to the highest occupied bands, serves as a quantitative indicator of N≡N activation. The closer the ICOHP value to zero, the weaker the N≡N bond. Compared to free N2 molecules, N2 adsorbed on Mo2C and TiO2–Mo2C surfaces shows varying degrees of activation. A more positive ICOHP value (−14.37 eV) of N2 on the H3 site of TiO2–Mo2C indicates a weakened N≡N bond, facilitating the subsequent hydrogenation process. The charge analysis in Figure 3c(i,ii) reveals a transfer of 0.83 e– from TiO2–Mo2C to N2, which is more than the 0.76 e– transferred in the case of pure Mo2C catalysts. This indicates a stronger electronic interaction between the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure and the N2 species. As the adsorbed N2 molecules accept electrons, the N≡N bond is weakened and activated, which is consistent with the previous results.

Catalytic Selectivity Competing with HER

The HER is a major competitive reaction during the NRR process. The Gibbs free energy of hydrogen adsorption (ΔGH) is an important parameter for evaluating the HER catalytic performance. As shown in Figure 4a, the ΔGH values for TiO2–Mo2C are all closer to zero than those for pure Mo2C, indicating that H atoms can bond at all sites of the heterostructure. The much lower ΔGH for HER on the H3 site compared to those on other sites for TiO2–Mo2C indicates relatively limited HER activity, which is favorable for NRR. To investigate the initial selectivity between N2 for NRR and H for HER, we compared the Gibbs free energy changes of ΔGN2 and ΔGH at potential active sites for TiO2–Mo2C and pure Mo2C catalysts. The results in Figure 4b reveal that NRR predominates over HER (ΔGN2 < ΔGH) for TiO2–Mo2C catalysts. Notably, the H3 site of the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure shows a significantly higher ΔGN2 value than ΔGH, further enhancing the NRR selectivity of the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure compared to pure Mo2C.

Structures above the black dashed line indicate HER dominance, and those below the black dashed line indicate NRR dominance.

NRR Mechanism and Catalytic Performance

To accurately assess the performance catalyzed by TiO2–Mo2C catalysts, we concentrate on the H3 site as the active center to investigate the thermodynamic process of the NRR. We consider two possible reaction mechanisms: the associative distal pathway and the associative alternating pathway, as shown in Figure 5. In the distal pathway, a proton–electron pair first adds hydrogen to the nitrogen atom away from the active site in N2 until the first NH3 is released and then adds hydrogen to the other nitrogen atom, generating NH3. In the alternating pathway, the proton–electron pair alternately adds hydrogen to the two nitrogen atoms in N2 to produce NH3.

Schematic diagram of the NRR pathway on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure from the top and side view.

The Gibbs free energy for both pathways is calculated to reveal the reaction mechanism on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure, as shown in Figure 6. We found that the potential determining step is from *NH to *NH2 in the associative distal pathway, with an energy barrier of 0.17 eV. However, the determining step in the alternating pathway is from *NH–NH2 to *NH2–NH2, with a limiting potential of 0.65 eV. Therefore, the associative distal pathway is more energetically favored over the associative alternating pathway. Bond Length Change

To further explain the exceptional performance of TiO2–Mo2C for NRR, we examined the variation in the N≡N bond length and Mo–N bond length of NRR intermediates along the distal pathway (Figure 7). The continuous increase in the N≡N bond length and the decrease in the Mo–N bond length at the initial stage demonstrate the effective activation of N2 on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure surface, promoting the generation and release of the first NH3 molecules. After that, the Mo–N bond length begins to increase, facilitating the subsequent hydrogenation process and production of the second NH3 molecule.

Conclusions

We developed an efficient TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure catalyst and elucidated the relationship between its electronic structure and catalytic activity using DFT calculations. Our findings indicate that the introduction of TiO2 modulates the d-orbitals of Mo in the heterostructure in a directional manner and significantly enhances the interaction between the π4d* orbital of Mo and the π2p* orbital of N2, facilitating charge transfer from Mo and promoting the antibonding filling of the π2p* orbital. This transfer improves N2 adsorption and activation, yielding a maximum adsorption energy of −1.33 eV and an elongated N≡N bond length of 1.29 Å, further supported by ICOHP values and variations in the N≡N bond length. The comparison of the Gibbs free energies for N2 and H adsorption indicates a preference for N2 adsorption over H on the TiO2–Mo2C heterostructure. The reaction proceeds through an associative distal pathway with a maximum Gibbs free energy change of 0.17 eV. This work provides a clear mechanism for the NRR process and offers valuable guidance for exploring high-performance NRR catalysts.
