History of Presentation

A 74-year-old man with a history of hypertension and a remote inferior myocardial infarction with a drug-eluting stent placed in the proximal right coronary artery presented to the emergency department with fatigue and syncope. His medications included carvedilol 6.25 mg twice daily, aspirin 81 mg daily, and atorvastatin 20 mg daily. On arrival, the patient was normothermic and hypertensive at 149/92 mm Hg, and had a heart rate of 82 beats/min, a normal respiratory rate, and an oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. Physical examination results were otherwise unremarkable. On auscultation, he exhibited a regular rate and rhythm and no cardiac murmurs. There was no evidence of sustained injury after syncope.

Past Medical History

The past medical history included hypertension and a remote inferior myocardial infarction with a drug-eluting stent placed in the proximal right coronary artery.

Differential Diagnosis

Syncope can be categorized into cardiac, metabolic, neurologic, and situational etiologies. Differential diagnoses for this patient’s presentation included arrhythmia, structural heart disease, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, orthostatic hypotension, vasovagal syncope, and, less likely, hypoglycemia, electrolyte abnormalities, dehydration, or seizure based on the history obtained.

Investigations

The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with a first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block and an alternating bundle branch block (ABBB) (Figure 1A), highly suspicious for an arrhythmia-related cause. The patient’s complete blood count, blood glucose, electrolytes, and troponin levels were within normal limits. The patient was admitted for syncope evaluation. Carvedilol was held on arrival. An echocardiogram showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% with hypokinesis of the inferior septal walls from the base to apex. There was no evidence of valvular disease or septal hypertrophy.

Management

Within 1 day of his admission, the patient had a repeat syncopal episode requiring emergent resuscitation. The patient’s subsequent ECG is shown in Figures 1B and 1C.

The best next step is option 4 (permanent pacemaker implantation). The ECG (Figure 1A) shows sinus rhythm, a first-degree AV block, and an alternating left and right bundle branch block in a patient with recurrent syncope. Immediately after a syncopal event, another ECG captured a high-grade AV block without a stable ventricular escape rhythm (Figures 1B and 1C). The patient experienced cardiac syncope. Discharging the patient with a cardiac monitor (option 1) would not be appropriate given the unstable rhythm. Administration of atropine (option 2) would not be effective for this patient’s infra-Hisian bilateral bundle branch block because atropine targets the AV node. Emergent transcutaneous pacing would not be necessary except for in an acute setting. Indeed, the patient has a class 1 indication for permanent pacemaker implantation (option 4).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient has evidence of conduction disease with a left bundle branch block and an alternating right bundle branch block, and subsequently a complete AV block, giving this patient a class 1 indication for eventual pacemaker implantation. The patient was resuscitated with transcutaneous pacing and underwent successful permanent pacemaker implantation.

Discussion

Alternating bundle branch is defined by the presence of a transient spontaneous block in the right bundle and left bundle (including left anterior or posterior fascicles) diagnosed on consecutive ECG tracings or on different leads on an ECG (Figures 2 and 3). This case illustrates the presence of an infra-Hisian ventricular conduction disease in both the left and right bundle branches alternating with succession to a complete AV block. According to American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society and European Society of Cardiology Guidelines, pacing is a class 1 indication for ABBB with or without symptoms.1,2 Patients with ABBB have an increased risk for developing high-degree AV block requiring pacemaker implantation.

Conclusions

This case captures a rare ECG demonstrating a sequential alternating bundle branch block followed by a complete AV block and highlights the need for increased awareness of early recognition and timely pacemaker implantation.
