Free energy fluctuations in SK and related spin glass models: A literature survey
Elizabeth W. Collins-Woodfin, Han Gia Le

Abstract
Over the past 50 years, spin glass models have generated a broad range of literature in mathematics, physics, and computer science. There has been much progress in characterizing and proving the limiting free energy of various models, stemming from the original formulas of Parisi. Comparatively less is known about the more detailed topic of free energy fluctuations. This paper concerns a family of models in which there has been considerable progress on fluctuations, namely the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) and spherical Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SSK) models, along with their multi-species analogs. We present a survey of the literature on free energy fluctuations in these 2-spin models, discussing results from different temperature regimes, with and without an external field, including results on phase transitions.



1
Introduction

The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
[
94
]
is one of the earliest and most well-studied models in the spin glass literature (see
[
95
]
for a brief history). While it was initially motivated by physics, it is also interesting from the perspective of optimization and, despite being simple relative to other models, it continues to be a source of open problems.
The SK model and its variants fall within the class of
mean-field
, or infinite-range models, meaning that the interactions between pairs of spin sites have a distribution that is independent of any notion of distance between the sites. While these models do not mimic the physical reality of particle interactions that decay with distance, they nevertheless exhibit rich mathematical phenomena, as we will see.

Scope of the survey

The spin glass literature is immense, so the focus of this survey is necessarily narrow, guided by the following question:

What is known about free energy fluctuations in 2-spin, mean-field models?

More specifically:

•

The models we discuss
do include
SK and spherical SK, with and without an external field, or with a Curie-Weiss term, but
do not include

p
-spin or mixed-spin models.

•

Under the umbrella of “mean-field”, we
do include
multi-species models but
do not include
sparse models or models with geometric/graphical structure to their interactions.

•

Within the free energy literature, we focus on
fluctuation results
and only briefly mention seminal works related to the limiting free energy, Parisi formulas, and AT-line. We briefly discuss overlaps as they relate to free energy, but do not emphasize the literature on overlaps.

•

For literature beyond the scope of this survey, we provide a brief list of references in Section
1.3
.

We have chosen this scope in order to highlight some exciting themes that emerge within the literature on free energy fluctuations of 2-spin, mean field models. In particular, the discrete and spherical models exhibit strikingly similar behavior at high temperature, but this similarity breaks down as one moves through the critical temperature threshold and into the low temperature regime. Fluctuations in the SSK model are driven by deep connections to random matrix theory, which persist at all temperatures and provide insight into various phase transitions within this model. In contrast, SK model exhibits a complex energy landscape, in which random matrix methods do not suffice but one needs other techniques such as Gaussian interpolation and cavity methods.

We hope this survey provides an instructive summary of what is known (and what remains open) regarding free energy fluctuations in SK, SSK, and related models. We look forward to seeing how other scholars will extend and build upon these results!

1.1
Model set-up

Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model

This model in statistical mechanics was introduced in 1975 to study magnetic alloys
[
94
]
. It involves a spin vector
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{N})^{T}
in the configuration space
\{-1,1\}^{N}
. For any
\sigma
, we define the Hamiltonian

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},

where
(g_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
are independent standard Gaussian random variables. Equivalently, one can write
H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{T}M\sigma
where
M
is a random matrix from the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE)
1
1

1

A GOE matrix
M
is symmetric with independent entries (up to symmetry) where
M_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,N^{-1}(1+\delta_{ij})).
. Using this Hamiltonian, one can define the
Gibbs measure
on the configuration space,

G(\sigma)=\frac{\exp(\beta H_{N}(\sigma))}{Z_{N}}\qquad\text{where }Z_{N}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\exp\left(\beta H_{N}(\sigma)\right).

The
free energy
of the model at each inverse temperature
\beta>0
is defined as

F_{N}(\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N}.

As
N\to\infty
, the free energy
F_{N}(\beta)
converges almost surely to some
F(\beta)
. This limiting free energy is known for every
\beta>0
, established in breakthrough works
[
87
,
88
]
and proven rigorously in
[
68
,
101
,
84
]
. The fluctuations are also known in the high temperature regime
\beta<1
, but remain open for the low temperature regime. The fluctuation results will be discussed in Section
2
. For the fluctuations of this model and other models of this survey, we focus particularly on the large-
N
asymptotics.

Spherical Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SSK) model

The SK model has a continuous analog, SSK, in which the configuration space is the hypersphere of radius
\sqrt{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, i.e.

S_{N-1}:=\left\{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|\sigma\|=\sqrt{N}\right\}

This is a natural extension of SK to continuous space, since
S_{N-1}
embeds the SK configuration space
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
. The Hamiltonian and free energy for SSK take the same definitions as for SK except that the partition function
Z_{N}
is now an integral rather than a finite sum; i.e.,

Z_{N}:=\int_{S_{N-1}}\exp\left(\beta H_{N}(\sigma)\right)\mathrm{d}\omega_{N}(\sigma),

where
\omega_{N}
is the uniform measure on
S_{N-1}
. The SSK model was first introduced by Kosterlitz, Thouless, and Jones
[
77
]
. They provide a non-rigorous computation of its limiting free energy, which is extended to a broader class of models in
[
51
]
and rigorously proven in
[
100
]
and
[
44
]
. The fluctuations have also been proved in the high and low temperature regime as well as the critical temperature threshold (see Section
3
).

Ground state energy

Closely related to the free energy is the ground state energy, which is defined as

\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\frac{1}{N}H_{N}(\sigma)\quad\text{or equivalently }\quad\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}F_{N}(\beta)

(1.1)

where
\Sigma_{N}
denotes
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
(resp.
S_{N-1}
) in the case of SK (resp. SSK) and
F_{N}(\beta)
is the free energy of the corresponding model. Because of these two equivalent definitions, the ground state energy can be viewed as the free energy of the model at zero temperature and the free energy can be viewed as a positive temperature relaxation of a constrained optimization problem.

SK (or SSK) with an external field

Both the SK and SSK model can include an external magnetic field. In this type of model the Hamiltonian becomes

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+h\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\sigma_{i}

(1.2)

where
v=(v_{1},...,v_{N})
is called the external field vector and can be chosen to be either random or deterministic. The properties of the model are similar with either choice, provided the vector is independent from the disorder variables
\{g_{ij}\}
.

SK (or SSK) with ferromagnetic/Curie-Weiss interaction

Another variation of the standard SK or SSK model is the model with ferromagnetic Curie-Weiss interaction. Instead of the linear external field term discussed above, the Curie-Weiss term is quadratic in the spin variables. More specifically,

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\frac{J}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

(1.3)

where
J
is called the
coupling constant
. This model exhibits three phases depending on the temperature and coupling constant (see Figure
1
). The phases are: spin glass, paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, which correspond to the cases in which
\max\{1,\beta^{-1},J\}
is equal to
1,\beta^{-1},J
respectively. Note that the previously mentioned high- and low-temperature regimes of the standard SK or SSK model (with
J=0
) fall within the paramagnetic and spin glass phases respectively.

[
14
]
.

Conventions

With respect to definitions and notations, there are a variety of conventions that are not entirely consistent within the spin glass literature. We summarize here the conventions that we choose in this paper. When we cite theorems from other papers, we translate their results to match our conventions. In most cases, the choice of convention is simply a rescaling and does not substantively change the results. We highlight any cases in which the choice of convention matters.

•

Scaling of inverse temperature
– We define the Hamiltonian as
H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
. In contrast, some papers omit the factor
\frac{1}{2}
, using the definition
H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
. This amounts of a change of variable for
\beta
in the formula of
Z_{N}
, such that the critical inverse temperature is
\beta=1
with our definition but
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
with the other definition.

•

Scaling of free energy (with respect to
N
)
– We define the free energy as
F_{N}(\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N}
. In contrast, some papers omit the factor
\frac{1}{N}
, using the definition
F_{N}(\beta)=\log Z_{N}
. In our scaling, the free energy has order 1 and the fluctuations of the free energy have order between
N^{-1}
and
N^{-1/2}
, depending on the model regime (variance order between
N^{-2}
and
N^{-1}
). In literature using the other convention, the free energy is of order
N
and the fluctuations are scaled accordingly.

•

Scaling of free energy (with respect to
\beta
)
–
In contrast to our definition
F_{N}(\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N}
, some papers rescale the free energy by
\beta
, using the definition
F_{N}(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta N}\log Z_{N}
. That definition can be useful when one is interested in the asymptotics of the free energy as
\beta
goes to 0 or
\infty
. In the finite, positive
\beta
regime that we consider, it is simply a rescaling.

•

Scaling of ground state energy
– We define the ground state energy as
\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\frac{1}{N}H_{N}(\sigma)
. In contrast, papers that omit the factor
\frac{1}{N}
in the definition of free energy also omit it in the definition of ground state energy.

•

Diagonal of the disorder matrix
– We take the disorder variables
\{g_{ij}\}
in the Hamiltonian to be standard normal random variables for all pairs
(i,j)
. Some papers take the diagonal entries
\{g_{ii}\}
to be zero. This choice is motivated by the physics context in which it does not make sense to define the interaction of a particle with itself. The effect of these entries is negligible in the asymptotics, so the results are equivalent for either convention. However, setting all
g_{ij}
to be standard Gaussian random variables allows one to directly apply theorems about GOE from the random matrix literature.

•

External field vector
– As mentioned above, the external field vector can be take to be random (e.g. standard Gaussian) or deterministic (e.g. all-one vector) or even a sum of deterministic and random vectors. We do not fix a convention here, but specify it in each theorem.

1.2
Some foundational results

Before discussing fluctuation results for the free energy of SK and SSK models, we briefly touch on the rich history of their limiting free energies. These two models are said to have
self-averaging
free energy, meaning that
F_{N}
stays bounded (below and above) and the fluctuations diminish as
N\to\infty
. In many cases, convergence of the disorder expectation
\mathbb{E}F_{N}
is demonstrated by providing an explicit description of its limit. For Ising spins, the celebrated Parisi formula, proposed by Parisi
[
87
,
88
]
, states that the limiting free energy is the infimum of a functional, over all probability measures supported on
[0,1]
. The formula is stated for general
mixed
p
-spin models
(see Section
1.3
), and mathematical breakthroughs proving the formula are due to Talagrand
[
101
]
and Panchenko
[
84
]
. In the special case of the SK model, the functional has an explicit expression that involves a solution of a PDE, to be solved backward in time with space-time parameter in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
, and initial condition at
t=1
. An analogous formula for the spherical model was developed by Crisanti and Sommers
[
51
]
, and mathematical proofs of the Parisi formula in this case are due to Talagrand
[
100
]
and Chen
[
44
]
. In the special case of the SSK model, we have a closed-form expression (see Eq. (
3.9
)).

After the development of the Parisi formula, a central question to the study of spin glasses concerns minimizers of the Parisi functional. Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer, known as the
Parisi measure
, is highly non-trivial, especially for discrete spins
[
9
]
. Another important question concerns the temperature-external field phase transition of the Parisi measure
\mu
, leading to the topic of replica symmetric versus replica symmetric breaking regimes. The model is said to be in the
replica symmetric
(RS) phase if
\mu
is a singleton, and is said to have
replica symmetric breaking
otherwise. Within RSB, we have
k
-step Replica Symmetry Breaking (kRSB) if
\mu
has
k+1
atoms and full Replica Symmetry Breaking (FRSB) if
\mu
has an absolutely continuous part.

Under zero external field, the SK model is shown to be RS at high temperature
0<\beta<1
by
[
1
]
and is not RS at low temperature
\beta>1
by Toninelli
[
105
]
. A recent result of Zhou
[
106
]
shows that at temperatures slightly below the critical value, i.e.
1<\beta<1+\eta
for some
\eta>0
, the system is in the FRSB phase. The picture gets more complicated under positive external field for the SK model. In this case,
[
105
]
shows that the system exhibits RSB when
(\beta,h)
is such that

\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\cosh^{4}(\beta z\sqrt{q}+h)}\right]>1,

where the expectation is with respect to the standard Gaussian random variable
z
and
q
is the solution to
q=\mathbb{E}\tanh^{2}(\beta z\sqrt{q}+h)
. The equality case
\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\cosh^{4}(\beta z\sqrt{q}+h)}\right]=1
is known as the AT line, named after de Almeida and Thouless
[
54
]
. For more discussions on the AT line and RSB regime, see
[
105
,
70
,
36
]
(single-species) and
[
22
,
56
,
75
]
(multi-species) for the discrete case and
[
71
,
23
,
31
]
, for example, for the spherical case.

The Parisi measure is closely connected to the
order parameter
, and studies of the RS and RSB regimes have also been done in terms of the order parameter (e.g.,
[
22
]
). In mean-field models such as SK and SSK, the order parameter refers to the limiting distribution of the absolute value
|R_{1,2}|
, where
R_{1,2}
denotes the replica overlap

R_{1,2}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma^{1}_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}

between two independent samples
\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}
from the Gibbs measure. For the SSK model, the order parameter is a single-point mass at all temperatures, regardless of external field strength. Thus, we say the model is RS in the whole parameter space.

It is expected that the Parisi measure and the order parameter coincide, yet at the moment, this is verified only for a certain class of mixed
p
-spin models (which does not include the 2-spin or any pure
p
-spin SK model). For further discussion on the two quantities, see
[
8
,
9
,
6
]
.

1.3
Brief remarks on literature beyond this survey

Much of the recent work in mean-field spin glasses has focused, not on the SK or SSK model, but on their
p
-spin and mixed spin generalizations. The Hamiltonian for a pure
p
-spin model is

H_{N,p(\sigma)}=N^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{p}=1}^{N}g_{i_{1},...,i_{p}}\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{p}}

where SK and SSK are pure 2-spin models. A mixed-spin model has Hamiltonian
H(\sigma)=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\beta_{p}H_{N,p}(\sigma)
for coefficients
\{\beta_{p}\}
with a suitable decay rate (a constraint typically imposed is
\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}2^{p}\beta_{p}^{2}
).

In this more generalized setting, the limiting free energy does not have a closed-form formula such as for SSK and SK (at high temperature). Rather, the Parisi formula (or the Crisanti-Sommers formulation for the spherical case) provides a variational representation of
F(\beta)
. In addition, formulating the limiting free energy in the general multi-species setting is an area of continuing research. A Parisi formula for the multi-species SK model is obtained in
[
21
,
85
]
, and the Crisanti-Sommers-type expression for (mixed
p
-spin) multi-species SSK model is derived in
[
24
]
. Both of these results rely on the assumption of convexity of the
mixture polynomial
for the proof of the upper bound. In addition, Subag
[
99
]
computes the limiting free energy for the (pure
p
-spin) multi-species spherical SK model via the so-called TAP approach. The statement relies on the assumption of convergence of certain free energies, instead of the convexity assumption.

Another important line of research is related to complexity of the energy landscape, which refers to the number of critical points (of various orders) of the Hamiltonian. Much of the progress in this area stems from the seminal work of Auffinger, Ben Arous, and Černý in the spherical setting
[
5
,
4
]
. See
[
32
,
10
,
26
,
63
,
98
,
93
]
and the references therein for recent progress in this area.

While our focus in this survey is on fluctuations of the free energy in SK and SSK models, there has also been work on the fluctuations of overlaps. See, e.g.,
[
13
,
48
,
79
,
80
,
83
]
. The fluctuation results in these works are with respect to the Gibbs measure and hold for fixed disorder. They are all related to SSK and are proven using a contour integral technique. Treatment of overlaps of SK is done in
[
102
, Section 1.10]
.

The limiting free energy and fluctuations of the free energy are
static questions
about spin glass models, but one can also consider
dynamical questions
related to how a spin distribution evolves under a particular algorithm (e.g., Glauber dynamics for SK or Langevin dynamics for spherical models). Key ideas that are studied in relation to dynamics include mixing times and aging. For a small sampling of the literature on these topics, see
[
65
,
30
,
53
,
59
]
(discrete models) and
[
28
,
92
,
29
,
64
,
52
]
(spherical models) and the references therein.

Finally, there is wealth of literature beyond the context of mean-field spin glasses, related to spin systems with nearest-neighbor or graph structure. Other models beyond the scope of this survey include diluted spin models, vector-valued spins, Potts model, and many more. Such models have been studied not only in the mathematical literature, but also in physics and computer science. Our literature review is inevitably incomplete due the limited scope of our paper and the vast amount of literature in this area. We apologize in advance to anyone whose work we have omitted and welcome the reader to reach out with additional references that may be of interest.

Organization of the paper

2
Free energy fluctuations in the SK model

In this section, we discuss fluctuation results for the free energy of the SK model, where spins take value
+1
or
-1
. As a prototypical model of spin glasses, the SK model has been the subject of an extensive amount of work.

We first consider the model with no external field, before presenting the literature for the model under the presence of one. In some cases, the situation actually becomes simpler when there is an external field. The external field term acts as a bias in one direction, breaking the symmetry among the spin configurations, which provides an analytical advantage.

2.1
Results for SK model without external field

High temperature results.

After the SK model was introduced in 1975, Aizenman, Lebowitz and Ruelle showed in 1987 that, in the high-temperature regime, the free energy fluctuations are asymptotically Gaussian
[
1
]
.
The results were shown for i.i.d. interaction coefficients
(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
where
J_{12}
is symmetric about zero, has moments of all orders, and satisfies
\mathbb{E}\exp(\alpha J_{12})<\infty
for some
\alpha>0
. In the Gaussian set-up of this survey, the result is as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(SK at high temperature
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

For all
\beta<1
, we have the convergence in distribution as
N\to\infty

N\left(F_{N}(\beta)-\left(\log 2+\beta^{2}/4\right)\right)\to X,

where
X\sim\mathcal{N}(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2},\sigma^{2})
and
\sigma^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta^{2})-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
is stated using the convention that the diagonal entries of the interaction matrix are zero. If the interaction matrix is a GOE matrix, the expression of
\sigma^{2}
becomes
\sigma^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta^{2})
. For more details, see
[
58
]
for the Ising spins and
[
16
]
for the spherical case.

The main result in
[
1
]
is stated as the convergence of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}/\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
to a log-normal random variable
T
, i.e.,
T=\exp(u-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}u^{2})
for a centered Gaussian random variable
u
. The moment method is not applied directly to this ratio, as log-normal random variables are not uniquely determined by their moments. Instead, the proof is obtained via cluster expansion, which works for general symmetric disorder. In this approach the configuration space is decomposed into subgraphs of an underlying graph. First, the partition function is written as

Z_{N}=2^{N}\left(\prod_{i<j}\cosh(\beta J_{ij}/\sqrt{N})\right)\hat{Z}_{N},\quad\hat{Z}_{N}=\sum_{\Gamma}\prod_{b\in E(\Gamma)}\tanh(\beta J_{b}/\sqrt{N}),

where the sum is taken over all graphs
\Gamma
of a specified form, and the product is taken over all edges of
\Gamma
. The next step is to show that the main contribution to
\hat{Z}_{N}
comes from clusters of cycles, and the fluctuations are asymptotically Gaussian via moment method. The high-temperature condition
\beta<1
guarantees that the contribution from large graphs decays exponentially in graph size.

Other methods have also been used to prove the Gaussian fluctuations at high temperature, such as stochastic analysis
[
61
,
49
]
and the moment method
[
103
]
. In
[
103
, Chapter 11]
, Talagrand refines the above Gaussian fluctuation result, providing an upper bound on the second-order term in the expansion of the moments of the quantity
N(F_{N}(\beta)-F(\beta))-\frac{1}{4}\log(1-\beta^{2})
. Using one-variable calculus and Gaussian integration by parts, the author derives a recurrence on moments of
N(F_{N}(\beta)-F(\beta))
, which allows for recovering the moments via simple induction and integration.

In
[
49
]
, Comets and Neveu use a stochastic analysis approach, where they consider the process
\{\tilde{Z}_{N}(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
given by

\tilde{Z}_{N}(t):=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\sigma}e_{N}(t,\sigma),

where

e_{N}(t,\sigma):=\exp\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i<j}B_{ij}(t)\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\frac{(N-1)t}{4}\right].

The sum on the right hand side resembles the Hamiltonian of the SK model, with the Brownian motions at time
t
replacing the Gaussian interactions. Indeed, this process
\tilde{Z}_{N}(t)
, at
t=\beta^{2}
, is related to the partition function
Z_{N}(\beta)
of the SK model via

\tilde{Z}_{N}(\beta^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z(\beta)/\mathbb{E}Z(\beta).

On the other hand,
\tilde{Z}_{N}(t)
is the exponential martingale:

\tilde{Z}_{N}(t)=\exp\left[M_{N}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\langle{M_{N}}\rangle(t)\right],

(2.1)

where
\{M_{N}(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is a martingale given by

M_{N}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}_{N}(s)/\tilde{Z}_{N}(s)

and
\langle{M_{N}}\rangle
is its bracket (not to be confused with Gibbs average). The bracket is the unique random process such that
M_{N}^{2}(t)-\langle{M_{N}}\rangle(t)
is a martingale, and is equivalent to the quadratic variation when the local martingale is continuous. The next step is to show that
M_{N}
is a centered Gaussian process via
\langle{M_{N}}\rangle
. By Rebolledo’s CLT for local martingales
[
91
]
, if
M_{N}
is a martingale whose bracket
\langle{M_{N}}\rangle
converges in probability to a deterministic function
\phi
defined on
[0,1)
, then the martingale
M_{N}
itself converges in probability to a centered Gaussian process on
[0,1)
with independent increments and variance
\phi
. Gaussian fluctuations of the free energy then follows directly from (
2.1
). Indeed,
\langle{M_{N}}\rangle(t)
converges in probability to the deterministic function
\phi(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{1}{1-t}-t\right)
. The expression of
\phi
matches with the variance established in Theorem
2.1
.

Low temperature results.

In contrast to the high-temperature regime, the fluctuations of the free energy in the low-temperature regime remains an open problem. Regardless, we discuss the upper bound and lower bound that are obtained for the fluctuations of the free energy and those of the ground state energy (the zero-temperature case). In our discussion, we follow the convention that “finite temperature” refers to finite, non-zero temperature.

First, we clarify some terminologies. Given a sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of random variables, we say that
X_{n}
has fluctuation exponent
\rho
if it has fluctuations of order
n^{\rho+o(1)}
. Moreover, we say
X_{n}
has a fluctuations of order at most
\Delta_{n}
if there is a constant
c>0
such that for all large
n
, we have
\Var(X_{n})\leq c\Delta_{n}^{2}
. In the case of a lower bound, we follow
[
39
, Definition 1.1]
and say that
X_{n}
has a fluctuations of order at least
\delta_{n}
if there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all large
n
, for all
-\infty<a\leq b<\infty
where
b-a\leq c_{1}\delta_{n}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(a\leq X_{n}\leq b)\leq 1-c_{2}.

In the zero-temperature regime, there has been an extensive effort on determining the fluctuation exponent
\rho
of the ground state energy. Several works, including
[
35
,
34
]
, support the claim that
\rho=-3/4
, while other works such as
[
50
,
2
,
86
]
predict that the exponent is actually
-5/6
. Notably, it is incredibly hard to obtain numerical estimation that distinguishes between the two scalings, and exact algorithms are limited to very small systems. However, we have that
\rho\leq-1/2
(see
[
39
, Section 2.2]
). A formal argument for
\rho\leq-3/4
via the non-rigorous replica method is provided in
[
3
]
.

On the mathematical front, Chen, Handschy and Lerman show in
[
41
]
that the ground state energy fluctuations are
o(N^{-1/2})
. In turn, Chatterjee provides a lower bound in
[
39
]
, showing that the ground state energy has fluctuations of at least order
N^{-1}
.

Compared to the zero-temperature regime, it is even more difficult to obtain numerical estimations in the finite temperature. It has been proposed in
[
3
]
that this can be done by simulating chaos. However, various works
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
of Chatterjee provide upper and lower bounds on the fluctuations of the free energy. These bounds hold at all finite temperatures.

Theorem 2.3

(Upper bound under finite temperature,
[
37
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

For the SK model, there exists an absolute constant
C
such that, for any
\beta\in(0,\infty)
,

\Var(F_{N}(\beta))\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}\log(2+C\beta)}{N\log N}.

The above upper bound is not sharp for high temperature, as the variance is of order
N^{-2}
in this regime by
[
1
]
. However, to our knowledge, Theorem
2.3
is the most recent progress for the low-temperature regime.

In
[
37
]
, the theorem is proven using Gaussian interpolation. Although the proof is shown for general mixed
p
-spin SK models, we provide a proof sketch for the 2-spin SK model. We observe that

\mathrm{Cov}(H_{N}(\sigma),H_{N}(\sigma^{\prime}))=\frac{(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})^{2}}{2N}=N\xi(R_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}),

where we set
\xi(x):=x^{2}/2
and
R_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime}}{N}
, which satisfies
|R_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}|\leq 1
.
Upon this observation, the three key steps of the proof are as follows.

•

Take two independent copies
H^{\prime}_{N},H^{\prime\prime}_{N}
of
H_{N}
. Given
t\geq 0
, we define

\displaystyle H^{t}_{N}

\displaystyle:=e^{-t}H_{N}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}H_{N}^{\prime},

\displaystyle H^{-t}_{N}

\displaystyle:=e^{-t}H_{N}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}H_{N}^{\prime\prime}.

•

Via Gaussian integration by parts, we have the variance identity

\Var(F_{N}(\beta))=\frac{\beta^{2}}{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\mathbb{E}\langle{\xi(R_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}})}\rangle_{t}\mathrm{d}t,

(2.2)

where the expectation
\langle{\cdot}\rangle_{t}
is given by

\langle{f}\rangle_{t}=\frac{\sum_{\sigma,\rho}f(\sigma,\rho)e^{\beta H^{t}(\sigma)+\beta H^{-t}(\rho)}}{\sum_{\sigma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}}e^{\beta H^{t}(\sigma^{\prime})+\beta H^{-t}(\rho^{\prime})}}.

•

One then shows that there exists an absolute constant
C
such that, for all
t\geq 0
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\langle{(\xi(R_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}))^{k}}\rangle_{t}\leq(Ck)^{k}N^{-k\min\left\{1,t/\left(C\log(1+C\beta)\right)\right\}}.

Applying this statement with
k=1
to the variance identity and integrating, we obtain the right hand side of theorem, with the main contribution coming from the integral over the sub-interval
[0,C\log(1+C\beta)]
(contribution from the rest is
O(N^{-2})
).

We would like to highlight the key ingredient that is Gaussian analysis. In all models discussed in this survey (and their mixed
p
-spin extensions), the collection
\{H_{N}(\sigma)\}_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}
forms a mean-zero Gaussian process, and consequently, their joint distribution is determined by the covariances. Mean-field models in general do not share this property of Gaussianity (e.g., the Hopfield model
[
69
]
and other models in
[
102
]
). The covariances are functions of the overlap, so controlling the overlap is a key part of the analysis. We will see this recipe of a variance identity via interpolation plus control of the overlap again in the derivation of an upper bound for the variance in the critical temperature case, as done in
[
42
]
.

A lower bound is also established for all
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. In
[
39
]
, Chatterjee shows that, at all finite temperatures and under the absence of the external field, the order of fluctuations is at least
N^{-1}
. The idea behind the method, generally speaking, involves perturbing the disorder
\{g_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
and then comparing the total variation distance between law of original free energy and that of the perturbed one. In the process, convexity in
\beta
of free energy and monotonicity in
\beta
of the limiting free energy are used.

Critical temperature results.

In light of the above two sections, we observe that the behavior of the free energy in the high-temperature regime is well-understood, while the situation is complicated in the low-temperature regime. Naturally, we question how the free energy fluctuates at the critical temperature, i.e.
\beta=1
.

Various works, from numerical computations to heuristic argument, such as
[
89
,
3
]
, support the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2.4
.

For the SK model, at the critical value
\beta_{c}=1
, we expect

\Var(F_{N}^{SK}(\beta_{c}))=\frac{1}{6}N^{-2}\log N+O(N^{-2}),\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Notable progress towards this conjecture for the critical temperature regime is
[
42
]
of Chen and Lam.

Theorem 2.5

(SK model at the critical temperature,
[
42
]
)
.

The following statements hold:

1.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Var\left(F_{N}(\beta_{c})\right)\leq C\left(N^{-2}(\log N)^{2}+1\right).

(2.3)

2.

For fixed
\alpha>0
and
d>0
, there is a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\alpha
and
\delta
, such that

\Var\left(F_{N}(\sqrt{\beta_{c}^{2}+dN^{-\alpha}})\right)\leq C\left(N^{-2}(\log N)^{2}+N^{-1-\alpha}\right).

(2.4)

Proof of Theorem
2.5
in
[
42
]
follows the general steps that we highlight for Theorem
2.3
. In particular, the method requires an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\langle{R_{1,2}^{2}}\rangle_{t}
.

Prior to
[
42
]
, Talagrand proves in
[
103
, Section 11.7]
that, for all
\beta\leq 1
, we have
\mathbb{E}\langle{R_{1,2}^{2}}\rangle\leq C/\sqrt{N}
. Next, Chatterjee observes that
\mathbb{E}\langle{R_{1,2}^{2}}\rangle_{t}
is a decreasing function of
t
. Combining these two facts with the variance identity (
2.2
), one obtains the bound

\Var(F_{N}(\beta_{c}))\leq CN^{-3/2}.

By providing a tighter,
t
-dependent bound of the double expectation
\mathbb{E}\langle{R_{1,2}^{2}}\rangle_{t}
, the authors of
[
42

are able to obtain an improvement (
2.3
). In order to derive such a bound with explicit dependence on

[
67
]
. Later in Section

to a subset of
multi-species
models, which contains the SK model.

2.2
Results for SK model with external field

We now review the literature on free energy of the SK model under the presence of the external field. In particular, we consider the following Hamiltonian:

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\sum_{i\leq N}(h+\beta_{1}g_{i})\sigma_{i},

where
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables. Here, the external field term has both a deterministic direction that is the all-one vector and a random direction that is the gaussian vector
(g_{1},\dots,g_{N})^{T}
. The non-negative constants
h
and
\beta_{1}
denote the external field strengths.

In
[
40
]
, Chen, Dey and Panchenko show that, under the presence of an external field, the free energy has Gaussian fluctuations of order
N^{-1/2}
at all temperatures. Prior to
[
40
]
, fluctuation results for the free energy are verified in the high temperature case in
[
66
, Theorem 5]
and in
[
104
]
, the latter of which obtains the same CLT statement for a subset of the high-temperature regime via the stochastic calculus approach.

Theorem 2.6

(SK model with external field,
[
40
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Suppose
h^{2}+\beta_{1}^{2}\neq 0
. For some
\nu=\nu(h,\beta_{1})>0
, it holds that

\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\sqrt{N/\nu}(F_{N}-\mathbb{E}F_{N}),g\right)=0,

where
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
is the total variation distance and
g
is a standard Gaussian random variable.

The result holds for mixed
p
-spin model with no odd
p
, and the parameter
\nu
in the limiting Gaussian distribution is explicitly described. Note that the asymptotics of the expected free energy
\mathbb{E}F_{N}
are not provided in their paper. We direct the readers to
[
40
]
for a full description of
\nu
.

Combining the theorem with fluctuation results for the case of no external field in Section
2.1
, we arrive at the following summary. In the finite temperature case, the free energy fluctuations for the SK model are:

•

Gaussian and of order
1/N
at high temp with no external field (
\beta<1
and
h=0
),

•

of order at least
1/N
and at most
1/\sqrt{N\log N}
at low temperature with no external field (
\beta>1
and
h=0
),

•

Gaussian and of order
1/\sqrt{N}
at any temp with external field (
h>0
).

In the zero temperature case, due to the work
[
41
]
by Chen, Handschy and Lerman, the fluctuations of ground state energy for the SK model are:

•

o(N^{-1/2})
under no external field (
h=0
),

•

Gaussian and of order
N^{-1/2}
under positive external field (
h>0
).

Furthermore, the transitional regime between zero external field and positive external field is also understood for the high-temperature regime, due to the work
[
57
]
of Dey and Wu. In particular, by considering the limit
h\downarrow 0
in the form of

h=\rho N^{-\alpha}\quad\text{where the constants $\rho,\alpha$ lie in }(0,\infty),

the authors obtain limit statements for the fluctuations in three regimes: sub-critical (
\alpha>1/4
), critical (
\alpha=1/4
), and super-critical (
\alpha<1/4
). Heuristic ideas on finding the correct threshold
\alpha=1/4
are discussed in
[
57

, and the details are included in the analysis of path clusters in
[
57

.

Theorem 2.7

(SK model at high temp, with weak external field
[
57
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Consider the SK model with Gaussian disorder. Assume that the external field is scaled as
h=\rho N^{-\alpha}
for some positive constants
\rho
and
\alpha
. Let

v_{1}^{2}:=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta^{2})-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad v_{2}^{2}:=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2(1-\beta^{2})}.

(2.5)

We then have the following statements of convergence in distribution.

1.

(Sub-critical) For
\alpha>1/4
and
\beta<1
,

N\left(F_{N}-\left(\log(2\cosh h)+\frac{N-1}{4N}\beta^{2}\right)\right)\to\mathcal{N}(-\frac{1}{2}v_{1}^{2},v_{1}^{2}).

2.

(Critical) For
\alpha=1/4
and
\beta<1
,

N\left(F_{N}-\left(\log(2\cosh h)+\frac{N-1}{4N}\beta^{2}\right)\right)\to\mathcal{N}(-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{1}^{2}+\rho^{4}v_{2}^{2}\right),v_{1}^{2}+\rho^{4}v_{2}^{2}).

3.

(Super-critical) There exists
\beta_{0}\leq 1
such that, for
\alpha<1/4
and
\beta<\beta_{0}
,

\frac{\sqrt{N}}{h^{2}}\left(F_{N}-\left(\mathbb{E}\log(2\cosh(\beta\sqrt{q}\eta+h))+\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}(1-q)^{2}\right)\right)\to\mathcal{N}(0,v_{2}^{2}),

where
\eta
is a standard Gaussian random variable and
q\in[0,1]
is the solution to

q=\mathbb{E}\tanh^{2}(\beta\sqrt{q}\eta+h).

Remark 2.8
.

Our notations
v_{1}^{2},v_{2}^{2}
differ slightly from those of
[
57
]
.

In all three regimes, the fluctuations are asymptotically Gaussian. The comparison between the zero external field and order-one external field is as follows. The three cases—no external field, sub-critical external field, and critical external field—all have the same fluctuation order
N^{-1}
, while the super-critical regime has fluctuation order
N^{-1/2}
, matching the fluctuation order of the order-one external field case in Theorem
2.6
. Furthermore, the statement of the sub-critical external field regime is almost identical to that of the zero external field case (see Theorem
2.1
). Lastly, one can verify that the deterministic shift applied to the free energy in statement 3 is
O(N^{-1})
from the shift used in the other two regimes, using the fact that
q
as above is
q\approx\frac{h^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}
as
h\downarrow 0
.

The methods of smart-path interpolation and characteristic trick, of quite similar flavor to the works
[
37

38
,
39
]
of Chatterjee and
[
42
]
of Chen and Lam, are used to prove the three statements. As we have seen in our discussion of these previous works, the methods rely crucially on the assumption of Gaussian disorder. Notably, an alternative proof for the sub-critical regime for Gaussian disorder
is also provided in
[
57
]
, showing that the free energy in this regime is close to free energy in the case of no external field.

Remark 2.9
.

A large part of
[
57
]
proves the above central limit theorems in the case where the disorder is not necessarily Gaussian. The authors achieve this goal in the sub-critical and critical regimes, using the approach of cluster expansion. In particular, statements 1 and 2 of Theorem
2.7
hold for non-Gaussian disorder, provided the interaction coefficients
J_{ij}
are i.i.d. symmetric with unit variance and finite sixth moment. In that case, the means and variances of the limiting Gaussian distributions in the two regimes depend on the fourth moment
\mathbb{E}[J_{12}^{4}]
. This assumption on the disorder is similar to the one used in
[
1

2.3
Results for SK model with ferromagnetic Curie–Weiss interaction

Free energy fluctuations have also been studied in the setting where the SK model has the Curie–Weiss interaction term. As introduced in (
1.3
), the Hamiltonian in this case is given by

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\frac{J}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

where
(g_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables and
J
is a positive constant.

In
[
20
]
, Banerjee proves a limit theorem for fluctuations of the free energy in the
paramagnetic
regime (
\beta<1
and
\beta J<1
), in which the system is at high temperature and has weak ferromagnetic strength. In this regime, the free energy is asymptotically Gaussian, with fluctuations of order
N^{-1}
. We note that
[
20
]
uses a slightly different convention for the Hamiltonian, and the following theorem has been adjusted to follow our definition.

Theorem 2.10

(SK model with CW interaction
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

For
\beta<1
and
\beta J<1
, we have the convergence in distribution as
N\to\infty
:

N(F_{N}(\beta)-F(\beta))\to\mathcal{N}(f_{1},\alpha_{1})

where
F(\beta)=\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta^{2}),

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta J)+\frac{1}{4}\log(1-\beta^{2}).

The proof relies on analyzing the so-called signed cycles. In contrast to cycles arising from cluster expansion as in
[
1
,
57
]
, the cycles in
[
20
]
arise due to a refined second moment method devised by the author. In particular, the partition function, rescaled by its expectation, is formulated as a Radon-Nikodym derivative of two probability measures, depending on
N
. In the language of statistics, they rewrite the free energy as a log-likelihood ratio of two measures
\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{Q}_{N},
that are induced by the random matrices with entries
\frac{g_{ij}}{2\sqrt{N}}
and
\frac{g_{ij}}{2\sqrt{N}}+\frac{J}{2N}
respectively. The Radon-Nikodym derivative (likelihood ratio) is then approximated by the (random) signed cycles, which are shown to be asymptotically Gaussian under the two measures since the cycles closely approximate the fluctuations of linear spectral statistics of a GOE matrix (with zero diagonal entries). This approach is used in a subsequent work to derive the free energy fluctuations of the mixed
p
-spin SK model (with non-zero 2-spin term) at sufficiently high temperature
[
19
]
.

3
Free energy fluctuations in the SSK model

We saw in the previous section that the fluctuations of the SK model are much better understood at high temperature than at low temperature. In contrast, the fluctuations of the SSK are well understood at all temperatures. One sees Gaussian fluctuations at high temperature (as in the SK model). At low temperature, the fluctuations follow the GOE Tracy--Widom distribution.
2
2

2

The GOE Tracy–Widom distribution arises in a variety of settings, notably as the rescaled fluctuations of the largest eigenvalue of a GOE matrix.
Finally, the free energy fluctuations at critical temperature converge to a sum that interpolates between the two distributions.

The successful analysis of this model is due to the fact that many questions about SSK can be directly translated into questions about the eigenvalues of the underlying random matrix. Drawing on the extensive random matrix literature, this unlocks many additional tools for analyzing SSK.

Contour integral representation of the partition function

The starting point for much of the recent progress on SSK fluctuations is a contour integral representation for the partition function
Z_{N}
. This representation first appeared in the paper of Kosterlitz, Thouless, and Jones
[
77
]
. More recently, Baik and Lee
[
15
]
demonstrate that it can be used to prove precise asymptotic formulas for the distribution of the SSK free energy in both the high and low temperature regimes. Since then, the contour integral representation has been used in numerous papers analyzing the SSK model and its variants
[
15
,
16
,
14
,
17
,
13
,
48
,
46
,
73
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
83
]
.

Below, we present the contour integral representation of the SSK partition function along with its short proof, reproduced from
[
15
]
. Similar representations can be derived for the SSK with external field, SSK with Curie-Weiss interaction, and bipartite SSK. The contour integral approach can be used to study overlaps as well as the free energy.

Lemma 3.1

(Contour integral for
Z_{N}
)
.

Given a symmetric matrix
M
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}
, the partition function
Z_{N}
with the corresponding Hamiltonian
H(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{T}M\sigma
can be expressed as

Z_{N}=C_{N}\int_{\gamma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\gamma+\mathrm{i}\infty}e^{\frac{N}{2}\mathcal{G}(z)}\mathrm{d}z

(3.1)

where the contour is a vertical line crossing the real axis at any
\gamma>\lambda_{1}
and

\mathcal{G}(z)=\beta z-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(z-\lambda_{i}),\qquad C_{N}=\frac{\Gamma(N/2)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}(N\beta/2)^{N/2-1}}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Let
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{1},\cdots\lambda_{N})
and
O
be an orthogonal matrix satisfying
M=O\Lambda O^{T}
. Then, using the change of variable
x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sigma
, we have

Z_{N}=\frac{1}{|S^{N-1}(1)|}\int_{S^{N-1}(1)}\exp\left(\frac{\beta N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{N-1}(x)

(3.3)

where
S^{N-1}(1)
denotes the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of radius 1 and
\Omega_{N-1}
denotes its surface area measure. We can express this as

Z_{N}=\frac{I(\frac{\beta N}{2})}{|S^{N-1}(1)|},\qquad\text{where }I(t)=\int_{S^{N-1}(1)}\exp\left(t\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{N-1}(x).

(3.4)

Now we consider the related quantity
J(t)=t^{\frac{N}{2}-1}I(t)
. The key observation is that the Laplace transform of
J(t)
is a Gaussian integral that can be evaluated in closed form. By performing this integration and then taking the inverse Laplace transform, one obtains a simplified expression for
J(t)
and thus
Z_{N}
. More specifically, the Laplace transform of
J(t)
is

L(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}J(t)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\int_{S^{N-1}(1)}\exp\left(-t\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z-\lambda_{i})x_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{N-1}(x)\mathrm{d}t

(3.5)

Using the change of variable
t=r^{2}
and
y=rx
where
t,r\geq 0
, we convert from spherical to Cartesian coordinates and obtain

L(z)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z-\lambda_{i})y_{i}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}^{N}y=2\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{z-\lambda_{i}}}.

(3.6)

Taking the inverse Laplace transform of this expression and requiring that
\mathrm{Re}(z)>\lambda_{1}
, we arrive at a contour integral representation for
J(t)
. Evaluating at
t=\frac{N\beta}{2}
, one can obtain the contour integral representation for
Z_{N}
.
∎

3.1
Results for SSK model without external field

Baik and Lee prove that the SSK has Gaussian fluctuations of order
N^{-1}
at high temperature, but GOE Tracy–Widom fluctuations of order
N^{-2/3}
at low temperature

. A more precise statement of their results is given below. While this survey focuses on the SSK model with a GOE interaction matrix, Baik and Lee actually prove their result for a more general class of interaction matrices (see

Theorem 3.2

(SSK at high and low temperature
[
15
]
)
.

When no external field is present (
h=0
), the free energy of SSK exhibits the following convergence in distribution as
N\to\infty
.

(i)

In the high temperature regime,
\beta<1

N(F_{N}(\beta)-F(\beta))\to\mathcal{N}(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2},\sigma^{2})

(3.7)

where

\sigma^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\beta^{2})
.

(ii)

In the low temperature regime,
\beta>1

\frac{2N^{2/3}}{\beta-1}(F_{N}(\beta)-F(\beta))\to TW_{1}.

(3.8)

The limiting free energy
F(\beta)

F(\beta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}&\text{for }\beta\leq 1,\\
\beta-\frac{1}{2}\log\beta-\frac{3}{4}&\text{for }\beta\geq 1.\end{cases}

(3.9)

We note that the formula for

Rough heuristic

The proof method for this theorem uses Lemma
3.1
along with a delicate steepest descent analysis.

•

At high temperature, the saddle point
\gamma
of the function
\mathcal{G}
in Lemma

is well approximated by a deterministic quantity, away from the random spectrum. Thus,
\mathcal{G}(\gamma)
has Gaussian fluctuations given by the CLT for linear statistics of random matrices
[

•

At low temperature,
\gamma
is extremely close to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, so the behavior of the free energy is controlled by the fluctuations of
\lambda_{1}
, which follow the GOE Tracy–Widom distribution.

External field transition

Summarizing the above results, we see that the free energy fluctuations for SSK are:

4
Free energy fluctuations in multi-species models

The SK and SSK models discussed in Sections
2

3

multi-species
models, in which the particles are divided into a fixed number of species, and the proportion of each species is asymptotically fixed as the number of spins goes to infinity. Moreover, the variance of the spin interactions
g_{ij}
is no longer constant, but depends on the species structure.

Definition 4.1

(Multi-species 2-spin model)
.

Fix

. Let
\Lambda=\diag(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
be a matrix satisfying
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1)

•

\{1,2,\dots,N\}=\cup_{s=1}^{k}I_{s}
, where the species density

\Lambda_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\diag(|I_{1}|,...,|I_{k}|)\quad\text{satisfies}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\Lambda_{N}=\Lambda.

•

Let
(g_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
be independent centered Gaussian variables such that
\mathbb{E}[g_{ij}^{2}]=\Delta^{2}_{s,t}
for
i\in I_{s}
and
j\in I_{t}
. The Hamiltonian of the model, at each spin configuration
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
, is defined as

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}.

Here,
\Sigma_{N}=\{\pm 1\}^{N}
in the case of Ising spins and is the product of spheres

in the case of spherical model.

We discuss the literature on the multi-species extensions of the SK model in Section
4.1
, where we refer to them via the shorthand MSK. The discussion includes recent fluctuation results under weak external field of
[

]

[

The literature on multi-species spherical SK models is discussed in Section

4.1
Results for SK multi-species model

The inhomogeneity in the MSK model leads to several mathematical challenges. In the case where
\Delta^{2}
is positive-definite, Barra, Contucci, Mingione and Tantari propose a Parisi formula for the free energy and prove the upper bound via Gaussian interpolation in
[

[
85

\beta>0

\Delta^{2}

There has also been progress towards free energy fluctuations, under various assumptions such as definiteness of
\Delta^{2}
, sufficiently high temperatures, and strength of the external field. As observed from Section
1.3
, the discussion of different regimes for the free energy involve the notions of RS and RSB phases in a nontrivial way. This is also the case for multi-species models.

In the MSK model with
k

has an order parameter
\mu_{s}
, which is the limiting distribution of the species-specific overlap
|R_{1,2}^{s}|

R_{1,2}^{s}:=\frac{1}{|I_{s}|}\sum_{i\in I_{s}}\sigma^{1}_{s}\sigma^{2}_{s}.

We say that the system is replica symmetric if
\mu_{s}
is a singleton for every

Note that classification of the RSB phase becomes more complicated for multi-species models, as it is unclear how much symmetry breaking in one species affects symmetry breaking in others.

The work
[
22
]

\Delta^{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta^{2}_{11}&\Delta^{2}_{12}\\

4.2
Results for SSK multi-species model

When it comes to spherical multi-species models, there has been much work on characterizing the limiting free energy for various multi-species set-ups
[
7
,
99
,
24
,
25
]
. However, precise results on the fluctuations of the free energy are only available in the special case of bipartite spherical SK (BSSK). Baik and Lee
[
17
]
showed that the free energy of BSSK is similar to that of SSK in the sense that it has Gaussian fluctuations of order
N^{-1}
at high temperature, but Tracy-Widom fluctuations of order
N^{-2/3}
at low temperature. In
[
46
]
, the authors of the current survey then show that, in a window around the critical temperature, the fluctuations of BSSK converge to an independent sum of Gaussian and Tracy-Widom random variables, where the precise formula depends on the species-size ratio.

Before presenting the theorems for BSSK, we remark on the striking similarity between SSK and BSSK and try to provide some intuition behind this similarity. On the one hand, we saw in the previous section that the bipartite model with Ising spins (BSK) poses considerable challenges that do not arise in analyzing SK, particularly due to the indefinite covariance structure. In the spherical setting, on the other hand, BSSK, like SSK, has a contour integral representation for its partition function and is thus amenable to analysis via techniques from random matrix theory.

The key idea is that, for the various spherical spin glasses discussed in this survey, the free energy fluctuations are driven by the behavior of eigenvalues in the underlying random matrix model. For SSK, the relevant matrix is GOE; for SSK with a Curie-Weiss term, this becomes a spiked GOE; and for BSSK, the relevant matrix is the real Wishart or Laguerre Orthogonal ensemble (LOE), constructed as
\frac{1}{m}JJ^{T}
where
J
is an
n\times m
matrix (with
n\leq m)
of i.i.d. standard Gaussians.
With this picture in mind, it is not surprising that BSSK behaves similarly to SSK, because their underlying random matrices have similar spectral properties. In particular, both GOE and LOE display eigenvalue rigidity
[
60
,
90
]
, CLT for linear statistics of eigenvalues
[
72
,
12
]
, and convergence of the largest eigenvalue to a Tracy-Widom distribution
[
96
,
97
]
.

Theorem 4.6

(BSSK at high and low temperature
[
17
]
)
.

Consider a BSSK model with species sizes
N_{1},N_{2}
such that
N_{1}+N_{2}=N
and their ratio converges as
N_{1}/N_{2}=\lambda+O(N^{-1-\delta})
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and some
\delta>0
. Then the critical inverse temperature is
\beta_{c}:=\sqrt{1+\lambda}/\lambda^{1/4}
and the free energy exhibits the following convergence in distribution as
N_{1},N_{2}\to\infty
.

(i)

In the high temperature regime,
\beta<\beta_{c}
,

N(F_{N_{1},N_{2}}(\beta)-F(\beta))\to\mathcal{N}(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}-\log 2,\sigma^{2}),

(4.5)

where
\sigma^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-(\beta/\beta_{c})^{4}),
with a slightly more complicated formula in the case of a general Wigner matrix rather than GOE.

(ii)

In the low temperature regime,
\beta>\beta_{c}
,

\frac{N^{2/3}}{A(\beta,\lambda)}(F_{N_{1},N_{2}}(\beta)-F(\beta))\to\TW_{1},

(4.6)

where the formula for
A(\beta,\lambda)
is given in Theorem 2.3 of
[
17
]
.

The limiting free energy
F(\beta)
is given in Theorem 2.1 of
[
17
]
(we omit the formula here but note that it depends on the ratio
\lambda
and is continuous but non-differentiable at
\beta_{c}
).

Theorem 4.7

(BSSK at critical temperature
[
46
]
)
.

Consider a BSSK model where
N_{1}/N_{2}=\lambda+O(N^{-1})
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and let
\beta=\beta_{c}+bN_{1}^{-1/3}\sqrt{\log N_{1}}
for some fixed
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the free energy exhibits the following convergence in distribution as
N_{1},N_{2}\to\infty
.

\frac{N}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}\log N_{1}}}\left(F_{N_{1},N_{2}}(\beta)-F(\beta)+\frac{\log N_{1}}{12N}\right)\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)+C_{\lambda}\max\{0,b\}\TW_{1}

(4.7)

where
F(\beta)
and
C_{\lambda}
are given in Theorem 1.1 of
[
46
]
.

Both of the above theorems rely on a contour integral representation for the partition function, which is proved in
[
18
]
. This formula involves a double rather than single contour integral and the location of the (random) saddle point of the integrand depends on both variables. Thus the contour analysis is somewhat more complicated than in the SSK case. To treat the critical temperature case, an additional random matrix theory input is needed. As with the SSK critical temperature analysis, Theorem
4.7
requires a CLT for the sum
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(z-\lambda_{i})
where
\{\lambda_{i}\}
are eigenvalues and
z
is near the spectral edge. This edge CLT was proved for Laguerre matrices (the relevant ensemble for BSSK) in
[
45
]
.
