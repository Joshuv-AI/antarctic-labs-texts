Hedging with memory: shallow and deep learning with signatures
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Louis-Amand Gérard

Abstract
We investigate the use of path signatures in a machine learning context for hedging exotic derivatives under non-Markovian stochastic volatility models. In a deep learning setting, we use signatures as features in feedforward neural networks and show that they outperform LSTMs in most cases, with orders of magnitude less training compute. In a shallow learning setting, we compare two regression approaches: the first directly learns the hedging strategy from the expected signature of the price process; the second models the dynamics of volatility using a signature volatility model, calibrated on the expected signature of the volatility. Solving the hedging problem in the calibrated signature volatility model yields more accurate and stable results across different payoffs and volatility dynamics.

Deep-hedging, non-Markovian stochastic volatility models, path-signatures, exotic derivatives, Fourier methods.

1
Introduction

The problem of hedging derivatives represents a central challenge in financial markets. Under Markovian models, the theory is very well developed, specifically for European-style derivatives. However, significant challenges arise when considering path-dependent options where the payoff depends on the asset’s entire price path, or further still, when the underlying asset has non-Markovian dynamics, where conventional parametrized hedging approaches tend to be too restrictive or untractable. In response to these challenges, non-parametric approaches have gained a lot of popularity, and more specifically with the improvement of machine learning software and hardware, deep hedging approaches for their versatility, ease of train and ability to capture nonlinearities, see for instance
Bühler et al. 2018
. However, standard neural networks present certain trade-offs. Feedforward networks for example can not retain memory and are thus not well-suited to process historical data by themselves. Moreover, LSTMs (Long Short Term Memory) have been designed in
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
to recursively handle together new information and its stored memory, but at the cost of poor parallelization and significantly slower training.

Aim.

The first aim of this paper is to investigate the use of (truncated) path signatures in a deep learning context as a feature engineering technique to alleviate both challenges. The signature of a path provides a universal representation basis for its whole trajectory allowing the processing of memory in a feedforward network, without the limitations of recursivity and slow training. For this purpose, we will compare a
simple
feedforward neural network enhanced with signature features, the SNN, with a LSTM network of the same size with only Markovian features, the RNN. We will also compare them in terms of computational efficiency and how long it takes to reach certain performance marks. As we will show, signature features capture most of the path-dependent information required for hedging while allowing for a very fast training.

The second aim of this paper is to compare two data-driven signature-based methods in a shallow learning context. The first, non-parametric, uses data to recover a hedging strategy that is linear in the signature of the (time-augmented) price process, following the approach introduced in
Lyons et al. 2020
. The second uses data to recover the volatility of a the price process as a linear combination of the signature of an underlying (time-augmented) Brownian motion, enabling semi-analytic computations via Fourier inversion, building on
Abi Jaber and Gérard 2025
. We will study those approaches for both European and path-dependent derivatives and under both Markovian and non-Markovian models. We will see that for Fourier-invertible payoffs, calibrating the signature volatility significantly improves the hedging compared to using the completely model-free hedging method.

Related literature.

Path signatures have been applied to finance for over a decade now, specifically in the context of machine learning as feature set. As a first notable mention,
Gyurkó et al. 2014
have used signatures as linear classifiers for atypical market behaviors in a multidimensional setting. Later,
Levin et al. 2016
have proposed the now famous universal approximation theorem (UAT) for linear forms on the signature which they applied to recover an approximation of a process’ dynamics as a function of the signature of a Brownian motion. Building upon it,
Lyons et al. 2020
have developed a non-parametric method for pricing options by regressing their payoffs’ dynamics against the signature of the observable spot price, and hedging by then doing a non-convex optimization. Their method has the main advantages of being model-free, data-driven and working with most exotic payoffs, but it is still subject to the curse of dimensionality. To alleviate some of these limitations,
Abi Jaber and Gérard 2025
have derived Fourier inversion methods for a certain signature stochastic volatility model, in order to leverage known and fast algorithms while remaining very general. As last notable mentions,
Futter et al. 2023
have used path signatures for mean-variance portfolio optimization and
Cuchiero et al. 2023a
have extended the Sig-SDEs framework of
Arribas et al. 2020
to jointly calibrating SPX and VIX options.

Furthermore, path signatures have also been applied more specifically in the context of deep learning, at first on character and movement recognition
Xie et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2019
, as the signature is invariant under time reparametrization and then more broadly, for example in
Kormilitzin et al. 2016
;
Perez Arribas et al. 2018
for emotions and behavioral classifications or in
Bank et al. 2024
;
Bayer et al. 2023
;
Bayer et al. 2025
for optimal control, optimal stopping and option pricing respectively.

Finally, deep hedging has been at the center of recent research. For instance
Bühler et al. 2018
, later extended to rough volatility in
Horvath et al. 2021
, present a framework for hedging a portfolio of derivatives in the presence of market frictions,
Zhu and Diao 2023
have proposed to apply a GRU network
Cho et al. 2014
for exponential hedging of European call options in the under rough Bergomi model. In a similar framework of hedging a European options under rough Bergomi,
Tong et al. 2023
have used path signatures together with a transformer
Vaswani et al. 2017
.

We refer the interested reader to
Schweizer 2001
for a comprehensive tour of quadratic hedging approaches,
Ruf and Wang 2020
for a literature review on deep option pricing,
Chevyrev and Kormilitzin 2016
for an introduction to path signatures and
Kidger et al. 2019
for an introduction the use of signature transforms as layers inside a neural network.

2.2
Path signature

We define the (path) signature of a semimartingale process as the sequence of iterated stochastic integrals in the sense of Stratonovich. Throughout the paper, the Itô integral is denoted by
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\mathrm{d}X_{t}
and the Stratonovich integral by
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{t}.
If both
X
and
Y
are semimartingales then, we have the relation
\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{t}\mathrm{d}X_{t}+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]_{\cdot}
.

Definition 2.3

(Signature)
.

Fix
T>0
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous semimartingale in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
. The signature of
X
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}:\Omega\times[0,T]

\displaystyle\to T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\displaystyle(\omega,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{X}_{t}(\omega):=(1,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}(\omega),\dots,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{n}(\omega),\dots),

where

\mathbb{X}_{t}^{n}:=\int_{0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n}<t}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\circ\mathrm{d}X_{u_{n}}

takes value in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}
,
n\geq 0
. Similarly, the truncated signature of order
M\in\mathbb{N}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{\leq M}:[0,T]

\displaystyle\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(2.5)

\displaystyle(\omega,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{X}_{t}^{\leq M}(\omega):=(1,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}(\omega),\dots,\mathbb{X}_{t}^{M}(\omega),0,\dots,0,\dots).

(2.6)

Example 2.3.1
.

Let
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
, then the first few orders of
\mathbb{X}_{t}
are given by

\mathbb{X}_{t}^{0}=1,\quad\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{t}^{1}\\
X_{t}^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbb{X}_{t}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{(X_{t}^{1})^{2}}{2!}&\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}^{1}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{2}\\
\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}^{2}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{1}&\frac{(X_{t}^{2})^{2}}{2!}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.7)

\blacksquare

One of the interesting properties of signature is its ability to linearize products of the signature, just like the Cauchy product for power series.

Proposition 2.4

(Shuffle properties)
.

For
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\bm{\ell}_{1}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\bm{\ell}_{2}\in T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\bm{\ell}_{1}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}_{2}\in T^{M+N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

and
\bm{\ell}_{1}\succ\bm{\ell}_{2}\in T^{M+N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{s}\right\rangle\circ\mathrm{d}\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{s}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{\ell}_{1}\succ\bm{\ell}_{2},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle.

Recursively apply an integration by parts. See
Gaines 1994
for more details.

3
Quadratic hedging

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{Q})
be a probability space supporting a two-dimensional Brownian motion
(W,W^{\perp})
generating the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. We set
B=\rho W+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}W^{\perp}

for some
\rho\in[-1,1]
. We consider that the dynamics of the risky asset
S
, under the risk neutral probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
, are given by a stochastic volatility model where the volatility process
\Sigma
is an adapted process such that
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{t}^{2}]\mathrm{d}t<\infty
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}S_{t}

\displaystyle=S_{t}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(3.1)

Let
\xi
be an
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable non-negative random variable such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]<\infty
that we are looking to hedge using a self-financing portfolio. A self-financing hedging portfolio
X
consists of an initial wealth
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and a progressively measurable strategy
(\alpha_{t})_{t\leq T}
of the amount of shares invested in asset
S
at time
t\leq T
, which leads to the following dynamics

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\alpha}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{u}\mathrm{d}S_{u},

(3.2)

with
\alpha
in the set of admissible hedging strategies
\mathcal{A}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\left\{\alpha\text{ progressively measurable such that }\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[(\alpha_{t}S_{t}\Sigma_{t})^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t<\infty\right\}.

(3.3)

A quadratic hedging strategy aims at minimizing the following objective function

\displaystyle P(X_{0},\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{T}^{\alpha}-\xi\right)^{2}\right]

(3.4)

over
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Following
Föllmer and Sondermann 1985
and
Schweizer 2001
, it is easy to derive a solution of the quadratic hedging problem using the martingale representation theorem. Note that
(\mathbb{E}[\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}])_{t\leq T}
is a square integrable martingale with terminal value
\xi
at
T
. An application of the martingale representation theorem
(
Karatzas and Shreve 1988
, Theorem 4.15)
yields the existence of two progressively measurable and square integrable processes
Z
and
Z^{\perp}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\xi-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\int_{t}^{T}Z^{\perp}_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{\perp}.

(3.5)

The optimum is attained for
(X_{0}^{*},\alpha^{*})
given by

\displaystyle X_{0}^{*}=\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{S_{t}\Sigma_{t}}\left(\rho Z_{t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}Z_{t}^{\perp}\right),\quad t\leq T.

(3.6)

We compare three numerical approaches for solving this quadratic hedging problem:

•

Fourier-based method in signature volatility models
(Section
3.1
): An exact method using Fourier inversion, applicable when the payoff
\xi
belongs to a suitable class and the volatility process
\Sigma
is modeled as a linear combination of truncated signatures of a Brownian motion. This approach yields semi-explicit solutions for (
3.6
) and will serve as a benchmark.

•

Deep hedging with and without signatures as features
(Section
3.2
): A data-driven method that parameterizes the self-financing portfolio using neural networks with various architectures and input features.

•

Shallow linear payoff/strategy approximation
(Section
3.3
): An approach where both the payoff and the hedging strategy is approximated by a linear combination of signature terms.

Remark 3.0.1
.

Although alternative objective criterions could be considered, we focus here on the quadratic criterion due to its analytical tractability. It provides a framework for evaluating and comparing approximation methods, such as deep learning or signature-based approaches, against the semi-explicit benchmark provided by the Fourier method, whenever the latter is applicable.

3.1
Fourier hedging under signature volatility models

We start by summarizing the signature volatility hedging method of

It is valid for models where the stochastic volatility

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,

(3.7)

where
\bm{\sigma}:[0,T]\to T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
M\geq 0
. In this setting, the following expression holds for the characteristic functional of
\log S

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}f(s,u)\mathrm{d}\log S_{s}\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\exp\left(\left\langle\bm{\psi}_{t}(u),\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle\right)=:\phi_{t}(u),\quad t\leq T,

(3.8)

\displaystyle-\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{t}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\psi}_{t}(u)|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2}+\rho f(t,u)(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\psi}_{t}(u)|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}})+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\psi}_{t}(u)|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{22}}}}+\bm{\psi}_{t}(u)|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}}+\frac{f(t,u)^{2}-f(t,u)}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{T}(u)

\displaystyle=0.

(3.10)

•

3.2
Deep hedging

Deep hedging as studied by
(
Bühler et al. 2018
)
parametrizes the hedging strategy
\alpha=\alpha^{\theta}
and the initial wealth
X_{0}=X_{0}^{\theta}
by a neural network with parameters
\theta
, and then trains the network to find the
\theta
that minimizes the objective criterion (
3.4
).

In practice, we discretize time as
0=t_{0}<\dots<t_{J}=T
, choose input features
X_{t_{j}}
for
j=0,\dots,J-1
, e.g.,
X_{t_{j}}=(t_{j},S_{t_{j}})
for a naive feedforward network or
X_{t_{j}}=(t_{j},S_{t_{j}},\alpha^{\theta}(X_{t_{j-1}}))
for a recurrent one, and fix a class of architectures
\Theta
. The training problem then becomes:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(X_{0}^{\theta}+\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\alpha^{\theta}(X_{t_{j}}^{i})\Delta S_{t_{j+1}}^{i}-\xi^{i}\right)^{2},

(3.11)

where
\Delta S_{t_{j+1}}=S_{t_{j+1}}-S_{t_{j}}
and
I
is the number of training samples.

Thanks to the universal approximation property of neural networks, restricting the search to a parametrized class is not too limiting: in principle, neural networks can approximate the optimal solution arbitrarily well.

However, the choice of architecture
\Theta
and input features
X
is crucial and can drastically affect performance. More complex architectures, such as recurrent networks, often perform better on sequential tasks, as evidenced in fields like language modeling
(
Wu et al. 2016
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
and reinforcement learning
(
Vinyals et al. 2019
;
OpenAI et al. 2019
)
. Yet these models also increase computational costs and may not be parallelizable, as is the case with LSTM
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)
or GRU
(
Cho et al. 2014
)
networks.

The goal of Section
4
is to evaluate, within the deep hedging framework, whether signature features can efficiently capture relevant path information and mitigate the need for recurrent complexity. Specifically, we will compare a vanilla feedforward network using signature features to a recurrent LSTM using Markovian inputs. Figure
1
highlights the difference in architectures and complexity.

3.3
Linear hedging with signature

The main idea of linear hedging with signatures is to restrict the set of admissible strategies
\alpha
and initial wealth
X_{0}
, resp., to those that are linear functions of the signature of the time-augmented observable price path
\widehat{S}_{t}:=(t,S_{t})
and its lead-lag counterpart resp. Specifically, one considers strategies of the form

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{\textnormal{lin}}\approx\left\langle\bm{\alpha},\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{t}\right\rangle,\quad 0\leq t<T,

(3.12)

with
\bm{\alpha}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The linear functional
\bm{\alpha}
can either be learned by gradient descent, as in the previous section, or, when the payoff can be approximated by a linear signature functional, explicitly computed by reducing the problem to a deterministic optimization problem, as described by
Lyons et al. 2020
.

To proceed, we introduce the
Hoff lead-lag transform

(
Flint et al. 2016
, Definition 2.1)
, which is crucial for combining Itô integrals with (Stratonovich) signatures. Let
X:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a continuous semi-martingale discretely sampled at times
t_{j}
, for
j=0,\dots,J
. The Hoff lead-lag transform
X^{\textnormal{LL}}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a piecewise linear interpolation given by

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\textnormal{LL}}=(X_{t}^{\textnormal{lag}},X_{t}^{\textnormal{lead}}),

where for
t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1})
, and
\Delta t=t_{j+1}-t_{j}
, we define:

\displaystyle(X_{t}^{\textnormal{lag}},X_{t}^{\textnormal{lead}})=\begin{cases}(X_{t_{j}},X_{t_{j+1}}),&\text{if }t\in[t_{j},t_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),\\
\left(X_{t_{j}},X_{t_{j+1}}+4\frac{t-t_{j+1/2}}{\Delta t}(X_{t_{j+2}}-X_{t_{j+1}})\right),&\text{if }t\in[t_{j+\frac{1}{2}},t_{j+\frac{3}{4}}),\\
\left(X_{t_{j}}+4\frac{t-t_{j+3/4}}{\Delta t}(X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_{j}}),X_{t_{j+2}}\right),&\text{if }t\in[t_{j+\frac{3}{4}},t_{j+1}),\end{cases}

and finally
X_{t_{J}}^{\textnormal{LL}}=(X_{t_{J-1}},X_{t_{J}})
. Here,
t_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
t_{j+\frac{3}{4}}
denote the midpoints obtained via linear interpolation. This transformation allows us to express Itô integrals involving signature processes. For example, if
S
is a semi-martingale and
\widehat{S}_{t}:=(t,S_{t})
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\bm{\alpha},\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{t}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\alpha}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{4}}},\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}\right\rangle,

(3.13)

for
\bm{\alpha}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with
\bm{\alpha}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{4}}}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Note that
\bm{\alpha}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{4}}}
is sparse and
\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}
is typically evaluated over a finer (e.g., tripled) time grid.

Suppose the payoff
\xi
can be written or approximated by

\displaystyle\xi^{\textnormal{lin}}\approx\left\langle\bm{\xi},\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}\right\rangle,

(3.14)

for some
\bm{\xi}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Then, plugging this expression into the optimal hedging condition (
3.6
), we obtain by linearity:

\displaystyle X_{0}^{\textnormal{lin}}=\left\langle\bm{\xi},\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}]\right\rangle,

(3.15)

where
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}]
denotes the expected signature which can be estimated via Monte Carlo simulations or implied from market options.

Concerning the hedging strategy, injecting the linear parameterizations (
3.12
) and (
3.14
) into the objective criterion (
3.4
), and using the shuffle product property of the signature together with (
3.13
), leads to the equivalent deterministic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\langle\left(X_{0}^{\textnormal{lin}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\bm{\alpha}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{4}}}-\bm{\xi}\right)^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}2},\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}]\right\rangle,

(3.16)

where
\bm{\xi}\in T^{(M+1)}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
,
\bm{\alpha}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{T}^{\textnormal{LL}}]\in T^{2(M+1)}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. This is now a deterministic (albeit possibly high-dimensional) optimization problem in
\bm{\alpha}
, which can be solved using gradient descent techniques. These methods are studied in Section
5
.

4
Signatures as Features in Neural Networks

The goal of this section is to evaluate the impact of incorporating signature features into deep hedging strategies under two complementary axes:

•

when the dynamics of the underlying asset exhibit non-Markovianity, and

•

when the payoff is path-dependent, even under Markovian dynamics.

We consider a spot process
S_{t}
with dynamics

\mathrm{d}S_{t}=S_{t}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

where
(W,W^{\perp})
is a two-dimensional Brownian motion and
B=\rho W+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}W^{\perp}
, with
\rho=-0.7
fixed across all experiments. The stochastic volatility component
\Sigma_{t}
is driven by
W
, and we consider two volatility models:

Model 1:
Heston 1993
(Markovian).

A classical Markovian stochastic volatility model where
\Sigma_{t}=\sqrt{V_{t}}
with

\mathrm{d}V_{t}=\kappa(\theta-V_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\eta\sqrt{V_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad V_{0}=\theta,

with parameters
\kappa=4
,
\theta=0.0625
, and
\eta=0.7
. The model admits a closed-form expression for the characteristic function, enabling pricing and hedging via Fourier methods for certain payoffs.

Model 2: Shifted Fractional Bergomi (non-Markovian).

A non-Markovian model where the volatility is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}=\sqrt{f_{0}(t)}\exp\left(\frac{\varsigma}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(\epsilon+t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\frac{\varsigma^{2}}{8H}\left((\epsilon+t)^{2H}-\epsilon^{2H}\right)\right),

(4.1)

with parameters
f_{0}(t)\equiv 0.0625
,
\varsigma=1.6
,
H=0.1
, and
\epsilon=1/52
. The shifted kernel enhances modeling flexibility, as shown in
Jaber et al. 2025
. Unlike Model 1, Model 2 lacks an explicit characteristic function in the standard sense. However, it fits naturally within the signature volatility framework of Section
3.1
, which opens the door to approximate Fourier pricing and hedging via signature methods (see Section
3.1
). Indeed, we can represent the volatility process
\Sigma
as an infinite series of the signature of the time-extended Brownian motion
\widehat{W}_{t}=(t,W_{t})
. More precisely, using the representation formula for Gaussian Volterra process
(
Abi Jaber et al. 2024
, Section 4.3)
and the shuffle product, we get that (
4.1
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\sigma}_{t},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle,\quad\bm{\sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{f_{0}(t)}e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}u_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\bm{k}_{t}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}},

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{t}:=\frac{\varsigma}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\epsilon+t)^{H-\frac{1}{2}-n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-H\right)^{\bar{n}}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{1}}}^{\otimes n},\quad u_{t}:=\frac{\varsigma^{2}}{8H}\left(\epsilon^{2H}-(\epsilon+t)^{2H}\right),

(4.3)

and
(\cdot)^{\bar{n}}
is the rising factorial.

Remark 4.0.1
.

The shuffle exponential in (
4.2
) could be naively computed as its
K
-truncated power series expansion around
a\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}}\approx e^{a}\sum_{n=0}^{K}\frac{(\bm{\ell}-a{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}})^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}n}}{n!},\quad\bm{\ell}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
K
needs to be large enough to have satisfactory convergence with arbitrary
a
. However, the right choice of
a
can lead to a simplified recursive formula for
e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}}
: take
a=\bm{\ell}^{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}
, then we have

e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}}=e^{\bm{\ell}^{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}}\succ\bm{\ell}=e^{\bm{\ell}^{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i}}}\in{A}_{d}}\left(e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{\ell}|_{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i}}}}\right){{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{i}}},\quad\bm{\ell}\in T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This better way to construct the shuffle exponential allows for only
M\times d
shuffle products to get the true coefficients up to order
M
. Furthermore, in case of the shifted fractional Bergomi, one only needs
M
shuffle products as only one projection is non-zero, namely against
{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}
, leading to only
M
shuffle products, i.e.

e^{\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}u_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\bm{k}_{t}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}\underset{\leq M}{=}e^{u_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\underbrace{\left(e^{u_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\left(\cdots e^{u_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}\cdots\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{k}_{t}\right){{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}\mathrel{\sqcup\mkern-3.2mu\sqcup}\bm{k}_{t}\right){{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}}_{\times M}.

\blacksquare

\bm{\sigma}_{t}

For both models, we train and compare three neural network architectures to approximate the quadratic hedging strategy, as described in Section

•

•

The VNN serves as a baseline. Signatory
of

Recall that all networks are 3 layers deep with 10 neurons wide. Despite similar architecture, the parameter counts differ significantly: VNN has 272 trainable parameters, SNN has 1442 (due to the larger 120 signature input), and RNN has 2252 (due to its recurrent structure).

5
Signatures as linear regressors

The goal of this section is to compare two hedging methods based on linear functions of the signatures of observables.
Both approaches use an estimate of the expected signature. The spot price
S_{t}
for the linear method in Section
3.3
, and the spot volatility
\Sigma_{t}
for the Fourier method in Section
3.1
.
We assume throughout this section that we are given such data (either exactly or through Monte Carlo simulations). Our goal is to extract, from these observations, an approximate pricing function and hedging strategy.

More precisely:

•

One approach directly learns the initial wealth
X_{0}
and hedging strategy
\alpha
as a linear functional of the path signature of the underlying price as described in Section
3.3
.

•

The other approach consists in calibrating a signature volatility model as in (
3.7
) so that it reproduces the observed expected signature. More precisely, one learns the coefficients
\bm{\sigma}
such that the model volatility
\Sigma_{t}=\langle\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\rangle
(with
W
a Brownian motion) yields a model-consistent
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}]
where
\widehat{Y}_{t}=(t,\Sigma_{t})
. Once the model is calibrated, a numerical method, e.g., Fourier inversion, can be used to compute the initial wealth
X_{0}
and hedging strategy
\alpha
. See Section
3.1
.

The purpose of this section is to investigate which approach performs better under various data and model assumptions. Conceptually, we aim to answer the following question:

Is it more effective, given observations of
S_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}
, to directly linearize the hedging strategy (using the signature of the observed spot price), or to linearize the volatility dynamics (using the signature of a Brownian motion), and then use model-based methods to derive prices and strategies?

Methods.

We benchmark the following approaches, comparing not only each method with the other, but also against their respective
best-case scenarios
(denoted BCS), i.e., situations where full information and computational resources are available.

•

Linear signature methods
, where both initial wealth and hedging strategy are expressed as a linear functional of
\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{t}
:

(a)

Linear Regression-Expectation (Linear REG):
Learn the expected payoff by regressing on the signature of
S
using samples of
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{S}}_{t}]
, via problem (
3.16
). See Section
3.3
.

(b)

Linear Reinforcement-Learning (Linear BCS):
Learn directly the hedging strategy by reinforcement optimization over trading P&L, using access to full model simulations. This gives the best linear signature strategy achievable with enough data and compute, bypassing the linearization of payoff and computation of the expected signature altogether.

•

Signature volatility model methods
, where the volatility process is
\Sigma_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
and option prices are computed in the model:

(a)

Signature Fourier Regression (Fourier REG):

Retrieve a truncated approximation of the linear volatility
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
from data and then use Section
3.1
to recover the optimal strategy via Fourier inversion (when available).
In order to retrieve
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
, it suffices to compute the expected signature of the stock volatility process under the signature volatility model. This proceeds in two steps:

(i)

In a first step, one computes the (truncated) signature elements of the time-augmented process
\Sigma
, which are explicit functions of the signature of the driving Brownian motion. This is detailed for instance in
(
Cuchiero et al. 2023b
, Theorem 3.16)
. More precisely, for a volatility given by
\Sigma_{t}=\left\langle\bm{\sigma},\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{t}\right\rangle
and
\widehat{Y}_{t}=(t,\Sigma_{t})
, its signature
\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}_{\cdot}
satisfies

\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}_{\cdot}^{{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}}=\left\langle\bm{\ell}(\bm{\sigma},{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}),\widehat{\mathbb{W}}_{\cdot}\right\rangle\quad\text{for all words }{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{v}}}\in V,

where
\bm{\ell}:T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times V\to T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a deterministic function mapping the model parameters and a word to a set of signature coefficients. This allows one to express each coordinate of
\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}_{\cdot}
as a linear functional of the Brownian signature.

(ii)

In a second step, calibration reduces to a simple reverse optimization problem. Using
Fawcett 2003
formula, which gives closed-form expressions for the expected signature of a Brownian motion, one can match the model-implied expected signature of
\widehat{Y}_{t}=(t,\Sigma_{t})
to the observed values
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}]
, and solve for the optimal coefficients
\bm{\sigma}
. This regression-like procedure is described in Appendix
A
.

(b)

Signature Fourier Representation (Fourier BCS):
Use the true model parameters (oracle setting), truncated at higher order, as a benchmark for the best achievable performance in the model class.

We emphasize that these methods are not always comparable: Fourier-based approaches rely on specific assumptions about the price dynamics (e.g., geometric Brownian motion with stochastic volatility) and payoff regularity (e.g., Fourier-invertible). By contrast, the linear signature regressions are agnostic to the underlying dynamics and can be applied in more general contexts. In this section, we restrict our attention to settings where both methods are applicable to allow for a meaningful comparison.

Remark 5.0.1
.

Other alternatives exist although they will not be studied in this paper. The first notable mention, discussed in
(
Abi Jaber and Gérard 2025
, Section 5.3)
, calibrates
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
on option prices across strikes and maturities. Note that a similar approach could be to calibrate
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
to minimize the mean square hedging P&L over some observations. The second notable mention, discussed in
(
Cuchiero et al. 2023b
, Example 4.1)
, uses higher frequency spot price data to estimate the spot covariation and then recover the underlying Brownian increments, allowing a simple regression to recover the coefficients.

\blacksquare

Payoff Types.

We compare both methods on three payoff functions with gradual complexity:

5.1
.

Polynomial payoff:
a payoff that is linear in the truncated signature

\displaystyle\xi=\sum_{k=0}^{4}c_{k}S_{T}^{k},\quad\text{with }c=\left\{-\tfrac{1}{16},\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{7}{8},\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{16}\right\},

(5.1)

where
S
is a geometric Brownian motion in order to have an explicit solution to compare the methods to.

5.2
.

European Call Option:
a payoff to showcase the first limitations of signature approximations of non-linear payoffs:

\displaystyle\left(S_{T}-K\right)^{+},

(5.2)

where
S
follows the Heston model with its variance
V_{t}=\Sigma_{t}^{2}
having CIR dynamics (
5.4
). This allows us to compare the methods to the Fourier solution.

5.3
.

Asian Call Option:
a path-dependent payoff to further test signature methods

\displaystyle\left(\exp\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\log S_{t}\mathrm{d}t\right]-K\right)^{+},

(5.3)

where
S
will be geometric with
delayed-equation
(DE) volatility (
5.5
). In this model we can compute the true truncated signature representationfor the volatility and hence compare Fourier REG with its
best-case-scenario
counterpart.

Remark 5.0.2
.

Note that in order to alleviate the influence of the starting point for both optimizations in Linear REG and Fourier REG, we compute the gradient descent over 10 different initial guesses and at the end of training select the solution with lowest
training
loss. Furthermore, every computation is linear and can be broadcasted, so in the end it doesn’t impact at all the duration of training. However, it did improve significantly the found local minima. Initial guesses were drawn at random around 0 for Linear REG and around a Ornstein-Uhlenbeck representation with maximum likelihood parameters
(
Tang and Chen 2009
)
for Fourier REG.

\blacksquare

5.1
Polynomial Payoff under Black-Scholes

This first numerical experiment is done in a very simplistic case to test the framework and, eventually, remark some artifact that was not specifically expected. We simulate geometric Brownian motions
S
for the spot price with constant volatility
\sigma=0.25
and no drift, and compare Linear REG to its
best-case-scenario
counterpart Linear BCS, against the polynomial payoff (
5.1
) with maturity 6 months

This experiment was initially only intended for checking the implementations, as both the linear payoff (
3.14
) and the linear strategy (
3.12
) can be found explicitly. However, as can be seen in Figure

, the Linear BCS comes very close to the true solution, about 1.7% higher mean square P&L, while the Linear REG is 23% higher. The linear (ridge) regression in (

3.16
). To make sure, as the true linear payoff is explicitly

\bm{\xi}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{4}c_{k}\binom{k}{m}S_{0}^{k-m}{{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\mathbf{2}}}^{\otimes m},

where
M\geq 4
is the truncation order of
\bm{\xi}
, we have also optimized
\hat{\bm{\ell}}
with the true linear payoff
\bm{\xi}
and the results are sensibly the same. Moreover, increasing the number of samples for computing the expected signature did not give large absolute differences. This leads us to believe that most of the difference between Linear REG and Linear BCS is due to the (non-linear) optimization having trouble finding the global minima. There are ways that could improve the optimization step in Linear REG, however it would not get any better than Linear BCS. Also, improving the truncation order will give better results at the cost of non trivial RAM and compute, as showcased in Appendix
C
.

In this final experiment, we compare both methods under a non-Markovian volatility model with a path-dependent at-the-money Asian call option. \displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}

(5.6)

({\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}-\bm{\ell})^{-1}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bm{\ell}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\bm{q}

(5.7)

Appendix B
Log-signature or not?

In this section we are interested in two main questions of Section
4
. The first one, which is the easiest to answer, asks whether one should use the log-signature, defined below, instead of the signature to reduce the number of terms. Indeed, the log-signature introduced in
Chen 1957
preserves the full information of the signature in a more compact form, as the formal logarithm is bijective, see
Lyons et al. 2007
. To give an intuition, the log-signature removes polynomial redundancies present in the signature, e.g. all terms up to order
M
of the signature can be computed with polynomials of degree at most
M
of terms of log-signature of order up to
M
.

The short answer to this first question is that the use of the log-signature relatively under-performs and takes longer to train. We speculate that it is due to the additional requirement of the network to learn polynomials, e.g. when
S_{t}^{2}
was perfectly accessible with the signature, the network has the approximate the square when trained on the log-signature.

Definition B.1

(Formal logarithm)
.

Let
\bm{p}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that
\bm{p}^{\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}=0
, then the logarithm map of
\bm{p}
is defined

\log({\color[rgb]{0.06,0.46,1}\textup{{\o{}}}}+\bm{p})=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\bm{p}^{\otimes n}.

Definition B.2

(Log-signature)
.

The log-signature of a path
X
denoted by
\log\mathbb{X}_{t}
is the logarithm of the signature of the path
X
.

The reader might also refer to
Reizenstein 2017
for more details on how to compute it.

Example B.2.1
.

Let
X_{t}=(t,S_{t},\Sigma_{t})
, then the first few orders of
\mathbb{X}_{t}
are given by

\log\mathbb{X}_{t}^{0}=0,\quad\log\mathbb{X}_{t}^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}t\\
S_{t}\\
\Sigma_{t}\end{pmatrix},\quad\log\mathbb{X}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t}s\mathrm{d}S_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}S_{s}\mathrm{d}s\\
\int_{0}^{t}s\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{s}\mathrm{d}s\\
\int_{0}^{t}S_{s}\circ\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}S_{s}\circ\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(B.1)

\blacksquare

The second question we study in this section, which is harder to answer, arises naturally when constructing the signature objects: what truncation order that leads to the best hedging and fastest training networks. The conclusions are much less clear but we can still see that most of the performance can be attributed to the first 2 or 3 orders of the signature. A similar observation was made by
(
Bank et al. 2024
)
in an optimal control setting. Note that the terms of the signature of order 2 of some
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
are the squares of
\frac{1}{2}X^{i}
and linear combinations of the product
X^{1}X^{2}
and the Lévy area
\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}\mathrm{d}X^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}\mathrm{d}X^{1}
.

All numerical experiments have been averaged over 50 iterations for the SNN and 10 for the RNN, lower for obvious time reasons. By iteration we mean a random initialization, a training and a test. This is to ensure that we are not making conclusions with networks that performed better or worse because of their initialization.
