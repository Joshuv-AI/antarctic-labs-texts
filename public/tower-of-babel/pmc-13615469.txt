Profitability of Nonprofit and For-Profit Ambulatory Surgery Centers
Authors: Elizabeth Plummer, Kahler Doyle, Ge Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This economic evaluation examines the financial performance of ambulatory surgery centers in Pennsylvania in 2024.

Introduction

Ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs) are facilities that primarily provide outpatient surgical procedures to patients not requiring an overnight stay. As services continue to shift from hospital-based settings to outpatient facilities, ASCs will play an increasingly important role in patient care.1,2,3 However, little is known about their financial performance because, unlike hospitals, the Medicare program does not require ASCs to submit cost reports. Using data from Pennsylvania—where ASCs must disclose annual financial and operational information to state agencies—we conducted an academic study to examine ASCs’ financial performance and provide financial measures separately for nonprofit and for-profit ASCs.

Methods

For this economic evaluation, we obtained financial and operational information for 2024 (most recent year available) from the Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council and the Pennsylvania Department of Health for all ASCs in Pennsylvania reporting complete data (eMethods in Supplement 1).4 We compared patient volume, specialty, system affiliation, net patient revenue, profit margin, and other key operational and financial metrics between nonprofit and for-profit ASCs. No human participants were involved, so IRB approval was not sought. This study followed the CHEERS guideline for economic evaluations.

We plotted kernel density functions of profit margins for nonprofit and for-profit ASCs to compare the full distributions by ownership type. Differences between distributions were evaluated using the 2-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. We also examined the distribution of profit margins by ASC system affiliation. We calculated P values from 2-sided Wilcoxon tests or tests of differences in proportion between for-profit and nonprofit ASCs. Statistical significance was determined by P < .05. SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Among 257 ASCs reporting complete data, 204 (79.4%) were for-profit and 53 (20.6%) were nonprofit. ASCs exhibited remarkably high profitability in 2024, with 14 nonprofit facilities (26.4%) reporting margins above 51% and 49 for-profit ASCs (24.0%) reporting margins above 29% (Table). Nonprofit ASCs accounted for 51.5% of net income while representing only 20.6% of facilities, 32.2% of patient volume, and 35.8% of patient revenues. Nonprofit ASCs were larger (median [IQR] visits: 4598 [2953-6786] vs 3613 [2002-6215]; median net patient revenue: $8 480 000 [$4 428 000-$17 540 000] vs $4 739 000 [$2 228 500-$8 461 500]; P < .001), with median (IQR) full-time employment double that of for-profit ASCs (27.0 [12.0-45.0] vs 13.5 [7.0-23.5] workers; P < .001). Nonprofit ASCs also had significantly higher median [IQR] per-visit net patient revenue ($1721 [$1226-$3311] vs $1297 [$701-$2120]; P < .001), per-visit net income ($592 [$128-$1326] vs $197 [$43-$404]; P < .001), and profit margin (37.8% [7.9%-51.4%] vs 16.4% [6.4%-28.9%]; P < .001).

Nonprofit ASCs were overwhelmingly system-owned (50 [94.3%]), while 55 for-profit ASCs (27.0%) were system-owned. Both nonprofit (40 [75.5%]) and for-profit (166 [81.4%]) ASCs were predominantly located in urban areas. Thirty-three nonprofit (62.3%) and 61 for-profit (29.9%) ASCs were multispecialty. Nonprofit ASCs’ most commonly reported specialty was gastroenterology (39 [73.6%]); ophthalmology (77 [37.7%]) was the most common among for-profit ASCs.

Medicare payments accounted for a median (IQR) of 23.6% (16.6%-32.0%) and 31.9% (20.3%-48.2%) of revenues for nonprofit and for-profit ASCs, respectively. Both groups incurred low Medicaid payments and negligible uncompensated care.

Profit margin distributions differed significantly between nonprofit and for-profit ASCs (2-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, D = 0.408; P < .001) (Figure). Nonprofit ASCs exhibited a profit margin distribution shifted toward higher values relative to for-profit ASCs.

Kernel Density Estimation of Ambulatory Surgical Center Profit Margins, by Ownership Type

The continuous curves map the estimated probability density functions for nonprofit and for-profit ambulatory surgical centers. Kernel density estimates were calculated using the full sample. For graphical presentation, the x-axis was limited to the 1st and 99th percentiles of the profit margin distribution to improve visualization.

Because 94.3% of nonprofit ASCs were system-owned vs only 27.0% of for-profit ASCs, we also plotted distributions for 3 groups: nonprofit ASCs, for-profit ASCs affiliated with a health system, and for-profit ASCs not health-system-affiliated. The distribution of profit margins for nonprofit ASCs remained shifted toward higher values than either for-profit subgroup, regardless of health system affiliation. The 2 for-profit subgroup distributions were not statistically distinguishable from each other.

Discussion

Using a unique data source from Pennsylvania, we conducted the first empirical study on ASCs’ financial characteristics, addressing an important evidence gap created by the lack of ASC financial disclosure to federal agencies. We documented the strong profitability of ASCs and identified higher profitability and larger size among nonprofit ASCs. Although nonprofit ASCs represented only one-fifth of facilities and one-third of patient volume, they accounted for more than half of total net income in Pennsylvania’s ASC market and exhibited significantly higher profit margins than for-profit ASCs, while continuing to maintain their tax-exempt status.

Nearly all nonprofit ASCs were system-owned, whereas fewer than one-third of for-profit ASCs were system-affiliated. Facility-level financial measures derived from Medicare cost reports, particularly balance-sheet measures, may be less reliable for system-affiliated ASCs. We examined income statement measures (ie, facility-level revenues and expenses), which are less likely to be affected by system affiliation. Nevertheless, measurement error may be greater among nonprofit ASCs because they are more commonly system affiliated.

Recent policy activities—such as Medicare’s phasing out of the Inpatient-Only list, expansion of covered ASC procedures, introduction of site-neutral payments, and state reforms to Certificate of Need laws—highlight ASCs’ growing role in US health care.5,6,7,8,9 As health systems continue to grow and pursue site-of-care optimization, and self-insured employers and other cost-conscious purchasers increasingly seek lower-cost-of-care options, expansion of ASCs is likely to continue.10 This trend has important implications for public programs’ payment policies, market competition, and the operating efficiency of health care facilities.
