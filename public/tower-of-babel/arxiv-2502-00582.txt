Uniform-in-time weak propagation of chaos for consensus-based optimization
Erhan Bayraktar, Ibrahim Ekren, Hongyi Zhou

Abstract
. We study the uniform-in-time weak propagation of chaos for the consensus-based optimization (CBO) method on a bounded searching domain. We apply the methodology for studying long-time behaviors of interacting particle systems developed in the work of Delarue and Tse [ DT25 ] . Our work shows that the weak error has order O ⁡ ( N − 1 ) O(N^{-1}) uniformly in time, where N N denotes the number of particles. The main strategy behind the proofs are the decomposition of the weak errors using the linearized Fokker-Planck equations and the exponential decay of their Sobolev norms. Consequently, our result leads to the joint convergence of the empirical distribution of the CBO particle system to the Dirac-delta distribution at the global minimizer in population size and running time in Wasserstein-type metrics.

1.
Introduction

The
consensus-based optimization
(CBO) is a gradient-free optimization algorithm inspired by Laplace’s principle

\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\mu(dx)\right)=\inf_{x\in\supp(\mu)}\mathcal{E}(x)\,,

which holds for any absolutely continuous probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the objective function to be minimized.
Restricting the searching scope to a bounded domain, the CBO method runs a dynamic system of
N
interacting particles, denoted by
(X^{N,i})_{i=1,\dots,N}
, described by the system of stochastic differential equations

(1.1a)

dX^{N,i}_{t}=-\lambda(X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t})dt+\sigma\left|X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t}\right|\phi(X^{N,i}_{t})dW^{i}_{t}

for
i=1,\dots,N
, where
W^{i}
are i.i.d. standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motions,
\phi
is a compactly supported smooth function, and

(1.1b)

m^{N}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{N,j}_{t}\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(X^{N,j}_{t}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(X^{N,j}_{t}))}\,,\qquad\nu^{N}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{N,j}_{t}}\,.

Previous works on the CBO methods have shown that the particles can run arbitrarily close to the unique global minimizer
x^{\ast}
(if it exists), for large enough
t
,
N
, and
\alpha
, whereas the choices of those quantities may depend on each other.

In this research, we study the proximity of the above system to its mean-field limit process over the infinite time horizon.
We will demonstrate that, under appropriate choices of the constant parameters
\lambda,\sigma
, and
\alpha
, the empirical distribution
(\nu^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the system (
1.1
) converges to a limit
(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
uniformly in time, in the sense that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})-\mathbf{E}\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})\right|\leq O(N^{-1})

for a family of functions
\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
.
This admits independent choices of the number
N
of particles and the running time
t
when given an error tolerance in practice.
In the end, our work leads to the convergence of
\nu^{N}_{t}
to some limit point
\tilde{x}
as
N,t\to\infty
independently, in the
centered Fourier-Wasserstein distance
and the
centered Wasserstein distance
.
Our result admits independent choices of
t
and
N
subject to a given error tolerance (so the system size
N
need not grow when the running time
t
gets large).

1.1.
Background

Optimization has long been a significant concern in every aspect of science.
Traditional optimization algorithms, such as the gradient descent method, rely heavily on the convex structure of the objective function and require computations of derivatives.
While optimization problems in reality show high non-convexity, there is a rapid rise in the field of gradient-free algorithms.

In recent decades, several searching-based algorithms have been introduced, including Simulated Annealing
[
AK91
]
, evolutionary algorithms
[
BFM97
,
Fog06
]
, genetic algorithms
[
Hol92
,
RR02
]
,
and most notably, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) introduced in the 1990s
[
KE95
,
SE98
]
.
In the PSO method, multiple agents in the system explore the state space according to their currently best-known positions in the state space.
The information-sharing and best-position searching mechanism in PSO later inspired Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO).

Formally established in
[
PTTM17
]
, CBO is an innovative optimization algorithm that uses opinion dynamics of multi-agent systems.
It relies solely on the values of the objective/cost function and avoids the heavy computations of gradients, which is notably helpful for problems on non-smooth, non-convex, and even noisy landscapes.
Technically, the CBO method operates an interacting particle system
\{X^{N,i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, where particles (agents) share their performances with each other to form a
consensus
of the (regularized) “current best” at time
t
defined by

m^{N}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{N,j}_{t}\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(X^{N,j}_{t}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(X^{N,j}_{t}))}\,.

The particles follow the dynamics

dX^{N,i}_{t}=-\lambda(X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t})dt+\sigma\left|X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t}\right|\phi(X^{N,i}_{t})dW^{i}_{t}\,,

which drive the particles towards the current best in infinitesimally small steps.

The CBO method displays high performance in practice, even for high-dimensional optimization problems, because of its gradient-free essence.
With additional controls in the particle dynamics, it is applicable to solve constrained optimization problems
[
CJZZ24
]
.
The work of Huang, Qiu, and Riedl
[
HQR24
]
presents a consensus-based algorithm searching for Nash equilibria (or saddle point) in minimax games, wherein two groups of agents actively exchange information and weight the observed performances to conditionally optimize their choices, approaching an equilibrium.
Such methods do not require the convexity-concavity of objective functions and also avoid computing gradients.

1.2.
Main problem and our contributions

Similar to many other interacting particle systems, the large population (mean-field) limit of the CBO system, given by

(1.2a)

d\bar{X}_{t}=-\lambda(\bar{X}_{t}-\bar{m}_{t})dt+\sigma\left|\bar{X}_{t}-\bar{m}_{t}\right|d\bar{W}_{t}\,,

where

(1.2b)

\bar{m}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\int xe^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\bar{\nu}_{t}(dx)}{\int e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\bar{\nu}_{t}(dx)}\,,\qquad\bar{\nu}_{t}=\law(\bar{X}_{t})\,,

has drawn high interest among researchers.
Properties of the limit process are studied in the work of Carrilio et al. (see
[
CCTT18
]
for instance) and also
[
FKR24
]
.
Those works show an exponential convergence rate of
\bar{X}_{t}
to some limit point, and the practical efficacy of the algorithm depends on the quantitative proximity of the particle system (
1.1
) to its mean-field limit (
1.2
), which is called the
propagation of chaos
.
The strong propagation of chaos (measured in
L^{2}
-norm) over finite time horizons has been established in
[
HQ22
,
GHV23
]
under the framework of
[
Szn91
]
.
Those results rely strictly on the finiteness of the time horizon, which causes a trade-off against the convergence of the mean-field process in a long time.

However, as demonstrated in
[
CCTT18
]
, the CBO mean-field limit process displays exponential convergence in infinite time, subject to some regularity conditions.
This raises the question of whether the propagation of chaos still holds when the algorithm runs for an arbitrarily long time.
Denoting by
(\nu^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the empirical distributions of the CBO particle system over the infinite time horizon, we would like to show that it is close to the weak limit
(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in the sense that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})\right|\leq\frac{C}{N}

for some constant
C
, where
\Phi
is some smooth enough function.
One nonlinear example of concern is the centered Fourier-Wasserstein distance

\left\|\nu^{N}_{t}\circ\tau_{\langle\textup{Id},\nu^{N}_{t}\rangle}-\rho_{\textup{ref}}\right\|_{-s,2}

to some reference measure
\rho_{\text{ref}}
with mean 0.
Here the Sobolev
(-s,2)
-norm is defined by

\left\|q\right\|_{-s,2}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\left|\xi\right|^{2})^{-s}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i2\pi\xi^{\top}x}q(dx)\right|^{2}d\xi\,.

Such a result belongs to a specific category, the
uniform-in-time propagation of chaos
.
In fact, it is more challenging because of the commonly seen accumulation of errors in large time intervals.
The propagation of chaos uniformly in time thus relies on some sense of ergodicity of the mean-field limit process.
Some examples are studied in
[
Mal01
,
Mal03
,
GLBM23
,
RS23
,
Lac21
,
LLF23
]
, and more details are discussed in
[
DT25
]
.
In fact, one notices that the CBO particle system (
1.1
) stops evolving when all particles are at the same point.
Along with the results in
[
CCTT18
,
FKR24
]
, it can be seen that for any initial distribution
\mu
, there is some point
\tilde{x}^{\mu}
such that the Dirac-delta measures
\delta_{\tilde{x}^{\mu}}
is the attractive invariant measure, which is the key to the global time estimates.

In this paper, we show that the methodology in
[
DT25
]
applies to the study of the uniform-in-time propagation of chaos for the consensus-based optimization algorithm.
Our main contribution is an upper bound of the form

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})\right|\leq\frac{C}{N}\,,

where the constant
C
has no dependence on time.
As a consequence, it gives an estimate of the form

\left|\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\delta_{x^{\ast}})\right|\leq C(N^{-1}+e^{-\kappa t}+\varepsilon_{0})\,,\qquad\forall N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\;t\geq 0\,,

where
\varepsilon_{0}
is some intrinsic error of the CBO method due to the finite-ness of
\alpha
(see Section 4 in
[
CCTT18
]
for detail).
Besides the existence of
\varepsilon_{0}
, this is a substantial improvement from the previous works since it establishes a quantitative proximity between the particle system and its mean-field limit independent of the time horizon.
That provides users with a clear reference to balance between computational accuracy and computing resources.
Users may choose the number
N
of particles to run without consideration of the running time
t
, while the strong propagation-of-chaos results of
[
HQ22
,
GHV23
]
requires a choice of
N
depending on
t
when subject to the same error tolerance.
It shows that the CBO algorithm gives an acceptable approximation to the optimizer even on occasions where computing resources are scarce.

To demonstrate our argument, for the first step, we decompose the temporal weak error
\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})
into integrals over the solution
\mathcal{U}
to the master equation of the CBO process, following the ideas of
[
DT25
,
Tse21
,
CST22
]
.
The integrands therein can be further expressed as linear operations on solutions to the linearized Fokker-Planck equation defined in
[
Cor24
,
MMW15
]
.
In particular, the main objective is to show that the second derivatives

(1.3)

\partial_{(y_{2})_{i}}\partial_{(y_{1})_{i}}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t,\mu,y_{1},y_{2})

has exponential decay in the time variable
t
, which relies on the ergodicity of the CBO Fokker-Planck equations.

We adopt the methodology of
[
DT25
]
to split the norm of some linearized Fokker-Planck equation (to be defined in Section
2
) at an attracting invariant measure
\delta_{\tilde{x}}
,

\partial_{t}q_{t}=(\mathcal{L}^{\ast}+\mathcal{A}^{\ast})q_{t}+r_{t}\,,

using its adjoint backward Cauchy equation of the form

\partial_{t}w(t)+(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{A})w(t)=0\,,\quad t\in[0,T]\,.

It is worth noticing that, under the setting of the consensus-based optimization, this equation does not directly exhibit a backward-in-time exponential decay as in Lemma 3.11 of
[
DT25
]
.
The magnitude of
w
has a strict lower bound of order
O(\left\|w(T)\right\|)
.
This is because the second-order term in
\mathcal{L}
, which comes from the volatility of (
1.1
), is not bounded from below, and the operator
\mathcal{L}
is thus not fully diffusive.

Instead, the derivatives of
w
satisfy some equation of the form

\partial_{t}\partial_{x_{i}}w(t)+(\mathcal{L}^{1}+\mathcal{A}^{1}-\lambda)\partial_{x_{i}}w(t)=0\,,\quad t\in[0,T]\,.

Thus, using the Feynman-Kac formula, we see that the derivatives of
w
admit some exponential decay up to normalization, in the sense that

\left|\partial_{x_{i}}w(t)-\varphi^{\textup{rem}}\cdot\nabla_{x}w(T,\tilde{x})\right|\leq Ce^{-\lambda(T-t)}

for some
\varphi^{\textup{rem}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is also seen by a sequence of analyses on geometric Brownian motions (GBM) with non-constant volatilities,
where both the GBMs themselves and their derivatives with respect to the initial positions display exponential decay.
Those results give us an analog of Proposition 4.8 of
[
DT25
]
, for (only) the derivatives of
w
, and consequently the decay of the linearized Fokker-Planck equation, in the form

\left\|q_{T}-q_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\delta_{\tilde{x}}\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}\leq Ce^{-\kappa_{0}T}\,.

Finally, we follow the idea of Section 4 in
[
DT25
]
to demonstrate our main arguments.

One concession we have made throughout the problem is the cutoff on the consensus-based optimization algorithm.
When the users are confident about the possible range of the global minimizer, it is reasonable to research only within the scope while discarding the rest.
The reason for such concession is that, without the cutoff function, the estimates on the quantity (
1.3
) depend exponentially on the moments of the measure argument
\mu
,
which then leads to the demand for a uniform-in-time upper bound on the quantity

\mathbf{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{C}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|X^{N,j}_{t}\right|^{2}\right)\right]\,.

This need not be finite when
C
is very large.
To avoid such occasions, we add the cutoff function
\phi
to the volatility so that the particles never escape from a compact domain.
Although it largely intensifies the computations, especially in the analysis of the geometric Brownian motions, we still successfully obtained reasonable estimates that guarantee the exponential decay of (
1.3
).

Organization of this paper

2.6
) in Section

.
After that we expand the details of the decompositions with master equations and the linearized Fokker-Planck equations.

A
.

4
and Appendix

) verifies the technical results in Section
3
.

1.4.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we will mainly work in the
d
-dimensional real Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Euclidean 2-norm
\left|x\right|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}
.
A closed ball
B(c,r)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|x-c\right|_{2}\leq r\}
.
The subscript 2 is usually ignored when the context is clear.

For a function
f
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its partial derivatives by

\partial^{k}f=\partial_{x_{1}}^{k_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{k_{d}}f

for every multi-index
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
The size of the multi-index
k
is measured in its Euclidean 1-norm
\left|k\right|_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}
.
The subscript 1 is usually ignored when it is clear that
k
is a multi-index.
Given a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Sobolev space
W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)
contains the functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded derivatives up to order
n
.
Then the Sobolev norm is then defined as

\left\|f\right\|_{n,\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\left|k\right|_{1}\leq n}\left\|\partial^{k}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,,

with the norm of its dual space
(W^{n,\infty}(\Omega))^{\prime}
being

\left\|q\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{n,\infty}\leq 1}\langle f,q\rangle\,.

The domain
\Omega
is usually taken to be
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or some closed ball
B(c,r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here we denote the linear operation of a distribution
q
in the dual space
(W^{n,\infty}(\Omega))^{\prime}
on a function
f\in W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)
by

\langle f,q\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\Omega}f(x)q(dx)\,.

The Fourier transformation of any
q\in(W^{n,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{\prime}
is then defined by

\mathcal{F}q(\xi)=\langle e^{-i2\pi\xi^{\top}(\cdot)},q\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i2\pi\xi^{\top}x}q(dx)\,,\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

The space of time-continuous functions over the time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
taking values in the metric space
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\mathcal{C}([t_{1},t_{2}];\mathcal{X})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is usually the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or the Sobolev dual space
(W^{n,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{\prime}
in the context of this paper.

The set of probability measures on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
.
The subset
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
contains all elements of
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
with finite second moments.
For a function
\mathcal{V}
taking values from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define its derivatives with respect to the measure argument in the following way, as seen in
[
CDLL19
,
CD18a
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

A function
\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
has a
continuous directional derivative
at some
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if there exists a bounded continuous function
\frac{\delta\mathcal{V}}{\delta m}(\mu,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
for any
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0^{+}}\mathcal{V}(\mu+\varepsilon(\tilde{\mu}-\mu))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{V}}{\delta m}(\mu,y)(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)(dy)\,.

We call
\frac{\delta\mathcal{V}}{\delta m}
the
linear functional derivative
of
\mathcal{V}
.
It is unique up to an additive constant, so we may enforce

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{V}}{\delta m}(\mu,y)\mu(dy)=0\,,\qquad\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

Analogously, for
p\geq 2
, we define
(\mu,y_{p})\mapsto\frac{\delta^{p}\mathcal{V}}{\delta m^{p}}(\mu,y_{1},\dots,y_{p})
to be the linear functional derivative of
\frac{\delta^{p-1}\mathcal{V}}{\delta m^{p-1}}(\cdot,y_{1},\dots,y_{p-1})
for all
y_{1},\dots,y_{p-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.
Main result and strategy

2.1.
Setting of the CBO method

Objective functions rising from real-world applications are typically non-convex but admit some degree of smoothness and polynomial growth away from minima.
Throughout the paper, we focus on those functions
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions.

Condition 2.1
.

(1)

There exists a unique global minimizer
x^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{E}
, and
\underline{\mathcal{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{E}(x^{\ast})\geq 0
,
\nabla\mathcal{E}(x^{\ast})=0
.

(2)

There exists some constant
c_{\mathcal{E}}
such that

\mathcal{E}(x)-\mathcal{E}(x^{\ast})\geq c_{\mathcal{E}}\left|x-x^{\ast}\right|^{2}\,,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(3)

The function
\mathcal{E}
has derivatives up to the 4th order, and there exists some constant
\ell_{\mathcal{E}}
such that
\left\|\partial^{k}\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\ell_{\mathcal{E}}
for all multi-index
k
such that
\left|k\right|_{1}=2
.

Recall that the CBO method is a dynamic searching algorithm over the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while it saves resources to restrict the search to certain compact domains.
Thus it is reasonable to have
a priori
information about the scope of searching.
We then make an additional assumption on the objective function.

Assumption 2.2
.

It is known to the user that the (unique) global minimizer
x^{\ast}
lies within some closed ball
B(c_{0},r_{\textup{cut}})
.

With the
a priori
information, one can safely run the CBO method without consider the region exterior to
B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})
.
This allows us to introduce a smooth cutoff function
\phi_{c,2r}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\phi_{c,2r}(x)=\begin{cases}1\,,&\left|x-c\right|_{2}\leq r\,,\\
0\,,&\left|x-c\right|_{2}>2r\,,\end{cases}

and for every multi-index
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
\left|k\right|_{1}\leq 6
there exists some constant
c_{k}>0
such that

\left\|\partial^{k}\phi_{c,2r}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c_{k}}{r^{\left|k\right|_{1}}}\,.

Note that
c_{k}=1
for
\left|k\right|_{1}=0
.
We abbreviate
\phi_{c,2r}=\phi
when the context is clear.
Then the modified algorithm runs the cutoff system

(2.1)

dX^{N,i}_{t}=-\lambda(X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t})dt+\sigma\left|X^{N,i}_{t}-m^{N}_{t}\right|\phi_{c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}}}(X^{N,i}_{t})dW^{i}_{t}

for
i=1,\dots,N
, with the consensus
m^{N}_{t}
and the empirical measure
\nu^{N}_{t}
defined as in (
1.1
), and the initial data are given by
X^{N}_{0}\sim\nu_{\textup{init}}^{\otimes N}
with
\supp(\nu_{\textup{init}})\subset B(c_{0},r_{\textup{cut}})
.
It is not hard to see that the above system is always restricted to the compact domain
B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})
, as interpreted in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the system (
2.1
) admits a unique strong solution on the time interval
[0,\infty)
, and

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|X^{N,i}_{t}-c_{0}\right|\leq 2r_{\textup{cut}}

almost surely for all
t\geq 0
.

The purpose of the cutoff function, additional to the original CBO method in
[
CCTT18
,
FKR24
]
, is to maintain a bounded support for the empirical distributions
\nu^{N}_{t}
.
This is crucial to the estimates of the quantities to be introduced in the rest of this section.

2.2.
Weak propagation of chaos

Note that
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
can be viewed as a subspace of any Sobolev dual space
(W^{n,\infty}(\Omega))^{\prime}
for
\Omega=B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where we recall the Sobolev norm

\left\|f\right\|_{n,\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\left|k\right|\leq n}\left\|\partial^{k}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}}))}\,,

and the dual norm

\left\|\mu\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}=\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{n,\infty}\leq 1}\langle f,\mu\rangle\,.

The goal of this work is to show the uniform-in-time proximity of the empirical distributions
(\nu^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to its mean-field limit process
(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})))
, a time-continuous weak solution to the stochastic differential equation

(2.2)

dX_{t}=-\lambda(X_{t}-M(\mu_{t}))dt+\sigma\left|X_{t}-M(\mu_{t})\right|\phi(X_{t})dW_{t}\,,\qquad\mu_{t}=\law(X_{t})\,,

where
M:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
consensus operator

M:\mu\mapsto\frac{\int xe^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\mu(dx)}{\int e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\mu(dx)}\,.

Equivalently, one may view
(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as a solution to the (nonlinear)
Fokker-Planck equation
, interpreted in the weak sense that

(2.3)

\frac{d}{dt}\int f(x)\mu_{t}(dx)=\int\left({b(x,\mu_{t})}^{\top}\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\tr[a(x,\mu_{t})\nabla^{2}f(x)]\right)\mu_{t}(dx)

holds for all
f\in W^{2,\infty}
, with

b(x,\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}-\lambda(x-M(\mu))\,,\qquad a(x,\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sigma^{2}\phi(x)^{2}\left|x-M(\mu)\right|^{2}I_{d\times d}\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{CBO}}\subset\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);\mathcal{P}(B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})))
the set of time-continuous weak solutions to the above Fokker-Planck equations.

The behaviors of (
2.2
) has been well studied in
[
CCTT18
,
FKR24
]
, and in this work we make use of only the most important decay property of the corresponding weak solution.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies Condition
2.1
, and

\lambda\geq d\sigma^{2}e^{18c_{\mathcal{E}}\alpha r_{\textup{cut}}^{2}-\alpha\underline{\mathcal{E}}}+4r_{\textup{cut}}^{2}e^{9c_{\mathcal{E}}\alpha r_{\textup{cut}}^{2}}\,.

Then, for any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}}))
there exists a unique weak solution
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{CBO}}
to (
2.3
) and a point
\tilde{x}^{\mu}\in B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})
such that, for all
t\geq 0
,

\left|\int x\mu_{t}(dx)-\tilde{x}^{\mu}\right|\leq Ce^{-\kappa t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|M(\mu_{t})-\tilde{x}^{\mu}\right|\leq Ce^{-\kappa t}

with
\kappa=2(\lambda-d\sigma^{2}e^{9\alpha c_{\mathcal{E}}r_{\textup{cut}}^{2}-\alpha\underline{\mathcal{E}}})
and
C
independent on
\mu
.

Remark 2.5
.

The above result is an adaptation of Theorem 4.1,
[
CCTT18
]
.
Thanks to the boundedness of the domain
B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})
, we can synthesize the conditions on the initial data to a single inequality.
It simply requires
\lambda
to be large enough.

Note that the limit point
\tilde{x}^{\mu}
need not be the global optimizer
x^{\ast}
.
The deviation of
\tilde{x}^{\mu}
from
x^{\ast}
can be controlled by choosing some large
\alpha
, as shown in Theorem 4.2,
[
CCTT18
]
.

Recall that the proximity is measured in the form
\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})
.
Now we state the following result on the weak propagation of chaos.

Theorem 2.6

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
(\nu^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the empirical distribution of the particle system (
2.1
), and let
(\bar{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the weak solution to the mean-field system (
2.2
) with
\bar{\nu}_{0}=\nu_{\textup{init}}
.
Suppose
\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

there exists some constant
C_{\Phi}
such that

(2.4)

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(\mu)\right\|_{6,\infty}<C_{\Phi}\,,\qquad\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|\frac{\delta^{2}\Phi}{\delta m^{2}}(\mu)\right\|_{6,\infty}<C_{\Phi}\,,

(2)

and

(2.5)

\left.\frac{d}{dz}\right|_{z=0}\Phi(\mu\circ\tau_{z}^{-1})=0\,,

where
\tau_{z}:x\mapsto x+z
is the translation operator.

Also assume that
\supp(\nu_{\textup{init}})\subset B(c_{0},r_{\textup{cut}})
.
Then for all
N\geq 2
we have

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})\right|\leq\frac{C_{\textup{main}}}{N}\,,

where
C_{\textup{main}}
is a constant independent of
\nu_{\textup{init}}
.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.6
, we obtain some estimates on the weak errors between
\nu^{N}_{t}
and
\delta_{x^{\ast}}
, in the Wasserstein-type metrics, with convergence rates in
N
and
t
separately.
Here we present two examples as corollaries of the main theorem.
The proofs will be given in Section
2.4
.

Corollary 2.7

(Convergence in centered Fourier-Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
s>\frac{d+7}{2}
and
\textup{Id}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
the identity map such that
\textup{Id}(x)=x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
There exists some constant
C_{FW}>0
such that

\mathbf{E}\left\|\nu^{N}_{t}\circ\tau_{\langle\textup{Id},\nu^{N}_{t}\rangle}-\delta_{0}\right\|_{-s,2}^{2}\leq C_{FW}(N^{-1}+e^{-2\kappa t})

for all
N\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
.

Corollary 2.8

(Convergence in centered Wasserstein distance)
.

Define the Wasserstein 2-distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\bar{[}0,\infty)
by

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint\left|x-y\right|\pi(dx,dy)\,,

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
.
The identity map
\textup{Id}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as in Corollary
2.7
.
Then there exists some constant
C_{W}>0
such that

\mathbf{E}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu^{N}_{t}\circ\tau_{\langle\textup{Id},\nu^{N}_{t}\rangle},\delta_{0})^{2}\leq C_{W}(N^{-1}+e^{-\kappa t})

for all
N\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
.

Remark 2.9
.

We make a few comments on the above corollaries.

(1)

The upper bounds tend to 0 as
N,t\to\infty
in both corollaries.
This means the particles cluster to the global optimizer when there are a large number of them and the algorithm runs for a sufficiently long time.

(2)

The Wasserstein distance is in general non-smooth on the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
But since our target is the convergence towards
\delta_{0}
, the unique centered attracting invariant measure, there is a clear explicit formula for the centered Wasserstein distance.
Its regularity still admits the conditions of our main theorem, which gives Corollary
2.8
, a stronger argument than Corollary
2.7
.

(3)

Notice that the errors due to the finiteness of the particle numbers
N
and the running time
t
appear separately.
The choice of
N
can be independent of
t
when given an error tolerance in practice, which improves the allocation of computational resources.

2.3.
Strategies of the proof

Observe that the Fokker-Planck equation actually defines a solution operator
P_{t}
that pushes
\nu_{\textup{init}}
to
\bar{\nu}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
In general, this sequence of operators map any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the corresponding probability flow
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that solves (
2.3
).
Thus, for any function
\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, we may define
\mathcal{U}:[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
via

\mathcal{U}(t,\mu)=\Phi(P_{t}\mu)\,,

and we often abbreviate
P_{t}\mu=\mu_{t}
and sometimes abuse the notation
\mu
for
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Then the proximity between
\nu^{N}_{t}
and
\bar{\nu}_{t}
translates to

\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\nu^{N}_{t})]-\Phi(\bar{\nu}_{t})=\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{U}(0,\nu^{N}_{t})]-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu_{\textup{init}})\,.

We shall analyze the behavior of the function
\mathcal{U}
to obtain an estimate on the above quantity.

2.3.1.
Decomposition via the master equation

We may decompose the objective by

(2.6)

\mathcal{U}(0,\nu^{N}_{t})-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu_{\textup{init}})=(\mathcal{U}(t,\nu^{N}_{0})-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu_{\textup{init}}))+(\mathcal{U}(0,\nu^{N}_{t})-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu^{N}_{0}))\,.

Notice that the first component in (
2.6
) is static in time.
We apply Theorem 2.14 of
[
CST22
]
to see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{U}(t,\nu^{N}_{0})-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu_{\textup{init}})]=

(2.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{E}\left[s_{1}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t,\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s_{1},s_{2}},\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\eta})-s_{1}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t,\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s_{1},s_{2}},\tilde{\eta},X^{N,1}_{0})\right]ds_{1}ds_{2}\,,

with
\tilde{\eta}\sim\nu_{\textup{init}}
independent of
(X^{N,j}_{0})_{j=1}^{N}
and

\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s_{1},s_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(1-s_{1})\nu_{\textup{init}}+s_{1}\left(\nu^{N}_{0}+\frac{s_{2}}{N}(\delta_{\tilde{\eta}}-\delta_{X^{N,1}_{0}})\right)\,.

For the second component in (
2.6
), we notice that the function
\mathcal{U}:[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as previously defined solves the
master equation

[
BLPR17
,
CD18b
]

(2.8)

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big({b(x,\mu)}^{\top}\nabla_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{U}}{\delta m}(t,\mu,x)+\frac{1}{2}\tr[a(x,\mu)\nabla^{2}_{x}\frac{\delta\mathcal{U}}{\delta m}(t,\mu,x)]\Big)\mu(dx)\,.

Then, by Itô’s formula and the master equation, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{U}(0,\nu^{N}_{t})-\mathcal{U}(t,\nu^{N}_{0})]=

(2.9)

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a(z,\nu^{N}_{s})\partial_{(x_{2})_{i}}\partial_{(x_{1})_{i}}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t-s,\nu^{N}_{s},z,z)\nu^{N}_{s}(dz)\right]ds\,.

Our target is to find a uniform-in-time upper bound on
\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and an exponential decay in time of
\partial_{(x_{2})_{i}}\partial_{(x_{1})_{i}}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{U}}{\delta m^{2}}(t,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
.
Those will put uniform-in-time upper bounds on (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), which finally leads to the conclusion.
To analyze the two objects, we express them as solutions to some linear differential equations and study their evolution in time.

2.3.2.
Linearized Fokker-Planck equation

Recall the Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{t}}^{\ast}\mu_{t}\,,

where

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left|x-M(\mu)\right|^{2}\phi(x)^{2}\Delta f(x)-\lambda(x-M(\mu))^{\top}\nabla f(x)\,.

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{t}}^{\ast}\mu_{t}
depends nonlinearly on
\mu_{t}
.

As in
[
Tse21
]
, we consider a linearization of the Fokker-Planck equation in the form

\displaystyle q\mapsto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\tr(a(x,\mu)\nabla^{2}f(x))q(dx)+\int b(x,\mu)^{\top}\nabla f(x)q(dx)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int\tr\left(\frac{\delta a}{\delta m}(x,\mu)(q)\nabla^{2}f(x)\right)\mu(dx)+\int{\frac{\delta b}{\delta m}(x,\mu)(q)}\cdot\nabla f(x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle+\text{(remainder)}\,,

where

\frac{\delta b}{\delta m}(x,\mu)(q)=\int\frac{\delta b}{\delta m}(x,\mu,y)q(dy)\,,\qquad\frac{\delta a}{\delta m}(x,\mu)(q)=\int\frac{\delta a}{\delta m}(x,\mu,y)q(dy)\,,

with

\displaystyle\frac{\delta b}{\delta m}(x,\mu,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda(y-M(\mu))e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(y)}}{\langle e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}},\mu\rangle}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta a}{\delta m}(x,\mu,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\sigma^{2}\phi(x)^{2}e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(y)}(x-M(\mu))^{\top}(y-M(\mu))}{\langle e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}},\mu\rangle}I_{d\times d}\,.

So we define the linearization operator

(2.10)

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\langle\lambda\nabla f-\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}(\cdot)(\cdot-M(\mu))\Delta f,\mu\rangle\cdot(x-M(\mu))\frac{e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}}{\langle e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}},\mu\rangle}\,.

Then, given the solution
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
2.3
), an initial state
q_{0}\in(W^{n,\infty})^{\prime}
, and the remainder terms
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in\bigcap_{T>0}L^{\infty}([0,T];(W^{n,\infty})^{\prime})
,
we define the
linearized Fokker-Planck equation
with
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{CBO}}
by

(L-FPE)

\partial_{t}q_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{t}}^{\ast}q_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mu_{t}}^{\ast}q_{t}+r_{t}\,,

where the solution
(q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is denoted by
\textup{Linear-[}\mu,q_{0},r\textup{]}\in\bigcap_{T>0}L^{\infty}([0,T];(W^{n,\infty})^{\prime})
.

Then we make use of Propositions 3.3 and 3.4 of
[
DT25
]
to express the derivatives of
\mathcal{U}
in terms of the solutions to some particular L-FPEs.

Plugging into (

3.
Core estimates for the proofs

In this section, we present the essential steps towards Lemmata

-
2.15
.
The core of the estimates, those with exponential decay in particular, has the following form,

(Erg)

\left\|q_{t}-q_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\delta_{\tilde{x}}\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}\leq Ce^{-\kappa_{0}t}\,,

where
(q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the solution to some
\textup{Linear-[}\mu,q_{0},r\textup{]}
.
We say
(q_{t})_{t\geq 0}

satisfies (
Erg
) at
\mu
in the above case.

Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{CBO}}
with attracting invariant measure
\mu_{\infty}=\delta_{\tilde{x}^{\mu}}
.
The strategy to expand the Sobolev dual norm

\left\|q_{T}\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}=\sup_{\left\|\xi\right\|_{n,\infty}\leq 1}\langle\xi,q_{T}\rangle=\sup_{\left\|\xi\right\|_{n,\infty}}\int_{B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})}\xi(x)q_{T}(dx)

is to consider a backward Cauchy problem

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w_{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{t}}w_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{\mu_{t}}w_{t}=0\,,&t\in[0,T)\,,\\
w_{T}=\xi\,,\end{cases}

with solution
w=w^{T,\xi}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];W^{n,\infty}(B(c_{0},2r_{\textup{cut}})))
.
Then, for any
T>0
and
\xi\in W^{n,\infty}
, we have

\langle\xi,q_{T}\rangle=\langle w^{T,\xi}(0),q_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\langle w^{T,\xi}(t),r_{t}\rangle dt\,.

So the objectives become

\left\|q_{T}\right\|_{(n,\infty)^{\prime}}=\sup_{\left\|\xi\right\|_{n,\infty}\leq 1}\left(\langle w^{T,\xi}(0),q_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\langle w^{T,\xi}(t),r_{t}\rangle dt\right)

and similarly

\displaystyle\left\|q_{T}-q_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\delta_{\tilde{x}^{\mu}}\right\|_{(n+2,\infty)^{\prime}}=

(3.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\sup_{\left\|\xi\right\|_{n,\infty}\leq 1}\left(\langle w^{T,\xi}(0),q_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\langle w^{T,\xi}(t),r_{t}\rangle dt+q_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\xi(\tilde{x}^{\mu})\right)\,.

Thus, the estimates reduce to the properties of the backward equation (
3.1
).
We study its behaviors in the rest of this section and present the most general result regarding its connection to (
Erg

3.1.

Proof of Lemma

Step 1: Expression of

Fix

Note that

is well-defined as an element of

We may write the above equation in a more generic form,

Step 2: Case

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{2};z)-\gamma_{j}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{1};z)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{j}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{2};0,z)-\varphi_{j}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{1};0,z)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\varphi_{i}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{2};t,\cdot)-\varphi_{i}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{1};t,\cdot),R^{i}_{t}\rangle dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{T_{1}}^{T_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\varphi_{i}^{\textup{rem}}(T_{2};t,\cdot),R^{i}_{t}\rangle dt

Now that the term

respectively, the bound of which follows in the same way as above.
Therefore

\left\|q_{T}\right\|_{(n\lor(1+n_{0}),\infty)^{\prime}}\lesssim O(1+K)\,.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider the following generic stochastic differential equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

dS_{u}=-\lambda S_{u}du+\sigma\left|S_{u}\right|\phi(S_{u})dW_{u}\,.

We denote by
(S^{t,x}_{u})_{u\in[t,T]}
the process that satisfies the above SDE on time interval
[t,T]
with initial data
S_{t}=x
.
When
\lambda
is large enough, for any
i\in[d]
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and nonzero multi-exponent
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
p+\left|\beta\right|\leq 5
, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\partial_{x}^{\beta}(\partial_{x_{i}}S^{t,x}_{u})\right|\left|\partial_{x_{i}}S^{t,x}_{u}\right|^{p}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}(u-t)}\,,\qquad u\in[t,T]\,.

Proof.

We first look at
\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}
for an arbitrary
j\in[d]
.
Observe that, for
u\in[t,T]
,

d\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}=-\lambda\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}du+\sigma\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{S^{t,x}_{u}\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})}{\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|}+\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|\nabla\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})\big)dW_{u}\,,

where with
c_{1}
defined for
\phi
we have

\left|\frac{S^{t,x}_{u}\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})}{\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|}+\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|\nabla\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})\right|\leq 1+c_{1}d\,,\qquad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.,}

Applying Itô’s formula to
\left|\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}\right|^{p}
with
2\leq p\leq 10
, we see that

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}\right|^{p}\leq-(p\lambda-\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\sigma^{2}(1+c_{1}d))\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}\right|^{p}\,.

When
\lambda
is large enough,
2\lambda-(p-1)\sigma^{2}(1+c_{1}d)>\lambda
, which proves for
\left|\beta\right|=0
and
p\geq 2
.
Also, Jensen’s inequality gives the conclusion for
p=1
as well.

On the other hand, for multi-index
\beta
, we have

d\partial_{x}^{\beta}(\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u})=-\lambda\partial_{x}^{\beta}(\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u})du+\sigma\partial_{x}^{\beta}\left[\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{S^{t,x}_{u}\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})}{\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|}+\left|S^{t,x}_{u}\right|\nabla\phi(S^{t,x}_{u})\big)\right]dW_{u}\,.

Similarly, with the
W^{6,\infty}
-boundedness of
\phi
, we have

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x}^{\beta}(\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u})\right|^{2}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2,\beta})\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x}^{\beta}(\partial_{x_{j}}S^{t,x}_{u})\right|^{2}

for some
\kappa_{2,\beta}
depending only on
\phi
and
\beta
.
We choose
\lambda>\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2,\beta}
and apply Jensen’s inequality.
This proves for any
\beta
and
p=0
.

Finally, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality leads to the most general form.
∎

to see that

d\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}=-\lambda\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}du+\sigma\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{(Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u}))\phi(Y_{u})}{\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|}+\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|\nabla\phi(Y_{u})\big)dW_{u}\,.

Then

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}]=-\lambda\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}]\,.

Note that
\mathbf{E}[Y_{t}]=x
, and
\partial_{x_{i}}x=\mathbf{1}_{i=k}
.
So

\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}]=e^{-\lambda(u-t)}\mathbf{1}_{i=k}\,.

Similarly, taking a further derivative gives also

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}Y_{u}]=-\lambda\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}Y_{u}]\,,

while
\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}x=0
.
Thus

\mathbf{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}Y_{u,k}]=0\,.

Recall that

\displaystyle d\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}=-\lambda\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}du

\displaystyle\qquad+\sigma\left[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{(Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u}))\phi(Y_{u})}{\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|}+\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|\nabla\phi(Y_{u})\big)\right]dW_{u,k}\,.

For
p\geq\{2,4\}
, we apply Itô’s formula to see that

\displaystyle d(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p}=-p\lambda(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p}du

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{p(p-1)\sigma^{2}}{2}(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p-2}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{(Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u}))\phi(Y_{u})}{\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|}+\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|\nabla\phi(Y_{u})\big)\right]du

\displaystyle\qquad+p\sigma(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p-1}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\cdot\big(\frac{(Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u}))\phi(Y_{u})}{\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|}+\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|\nabla\phi(Y_{u})\big)\right]dW_{u,k}\,.

Notice that

{\frac{(Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u}))\phi(Y_{u})}{\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|}+\left|Y_{u}-M(\mu_{u})\right|\nabla\phi(Y_{u})}

is bounded almost surely, so there exists some constant
c_{p}>0
, depending solely on
\phi
, such that

(B.1)

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p}]\leq-p\lambda\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p}]+\frac{p(p-1)\sigma^{2}c_{p}}{2}\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{p-2}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}]\,.

In particular,

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{2}]\leq-2\lambda\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{2}]+\sigma^{2}c_{p}\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}\,,

so that

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2}c_{2})\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}\,.

Note that
\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{t}\right|^{2}=1
.
We may choose
C_{2}=1
,
\kappa_{2}=c_{2}
so that

\mathbf{E}(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}\leq C_{2}e^{-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2}\kappa_{2})(u-t)}\,.

Moreover, from (
B.1
) we have

\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{4}]\leq-p\lambda\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{4}]+6\sigma^{2}c_{4}\mathbf{E}[(\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k})^{2}\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|^{2}]\,,

so

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}\right|_{4}^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq-p\lambda\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}\right|_{4}^{4}]+6\sigma^{2}c_{4}\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|_{2}^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq-p\lambda\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}\right|_{4}^{4}]+6\sigma^{2}c_{4}d\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u}\right|_{4}^{4}]\,.

This gives

\mathbf{E}[\left|\partial_{x_{i}}Y_{u,k}\right|_{4}^{4}]\leq e^{-(4\lambda-6\sigma^{2}c_{4}d)(u-t)}\,.

Taking
C_{4}=d
and
\kappa_{4}=6dc_{4}
proofs the claim for
p=4
.
∎
