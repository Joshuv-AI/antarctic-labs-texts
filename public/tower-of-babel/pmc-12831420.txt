Background

Meckel’s diverticulum (MD), first identified in 1809 by J.F. Meckel, is a true intestinal diverticulum with a congenital origin and is the most prevalent anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. MD develops when the omphalomesenteric duct, a structure linking the yolk sac to the embryonic intestine, fails to fully close during fetal development [2]. MD is often described by the “rule of twos”: it affects approximately 2% of the population, occurs twice as frequently in male individuals, typically presents before the age of 2 years, and measures about 2 in in length. It is usually located roughly 2 ft from the ileocecal junction [2, 3]. In the majority of cases, MD remains clinically silent and does not require medical intervention; however, when complications arise, a diagnosis is established through imaging, and appropriate treatment is subsequently undertaken [2]. Diagnosing MD can be challenging, as the condition can be asymptomatic or present symptoms that mimic differentials such as Crohn’s disease, appendicitis, and peptic ulcer disease; therefore this overlap often leads to misdiagnosis, particularly in the absence of appropriate examinations [4]. MD can lead to various complications such as bleeding, rupture, formation of enteroliths, torsion, ulcer development, and tumors. Of these, torsion is an exceedingly rare presentation. with less than 50 cases reported in literature, resulting from the diverticulum rotating around its own axis. This rotation can compromise vascular supply, potentially leading to necrosis [1, 3]. Individuals under 50 years of age, male sex, diverticulum longer than 2 cm, the presence of ectopic tissue, a broad-based diverticulum, and fibrous bands connected to the diverticulum all are considered predisposing factors that may increase the risk of developing symptoms. Due to the nonspecific nature of its presentation, diagnosis is often delayed and only established during surgery, which includes open or laparoscopic approaches [1, 2]. In this report, we document a rare case of MD torsion in a 16-year-old male patient in a resource-constrained care setting, while focusing on the diagnostic challenges posed by the absence of appropriate imaging.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old Syrian male patient with no significant medical history presented to our emergency department with progressively worsening right lower quadrant abdominal pain in the last 24 hours, rated 9/10, migrating to the periumbilical region, accompanied by nausea and vomiting. The patient reported 2 days of obstipation prior to pain onset along with anorexia. His vitals were all within normal ranges except for slightly elevated body temperature of 37.8 °C. Rectal examination yielded unremarkable findings, while laboratory tests were all within normal ranges except for leukocytosis (13,500 cells/mm3) with 84% neutrophils. Consequently, our patient had a Mantrel score of 8/10, and the diagnosis of early acute appendicitis was proposed on the basis of the patient’s symptoms and the clinical assessment without further confirmatory imaging, particularly since computed tomography (CT) was not conducted in our case due to the scanner being out of service in our hospital. In addition, the patient’s father declined transfer to another hospital for imaging due to financial constraints, and our hospital lacks the equipment needed for laparoscopic approach, which limited the therapeutic options of the patient. Subsequently, the patient received a single prophylactic dose of 1 g intravenous ceftriaxone and 500 mg intravenous metronidazole preoperatively, and was transferred for emergency appendectomy, where an incision in right lower quadrant was performed, only to reveal a grossly normal appendix. Discussion

MD is the most frequent congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract and is a true diverticulum as it encompasses all intestinal wall layers: mucosa, muscle, and serosa‌. It arises from a persistent portion of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct that normally involutes by the sixth to eighth week of gestation, leaving a blind-ended pouch on the distal ileum when it fails to regress. MD occurs more frequently in male individuals, with studies reporting an incidence of 0.3–2.9%, which commonly arise in the first decade, with an average onset age of 2.5 years [1]. Our patient was a 16-year-old male individual and had no remarkable history. This congenital pouch usually lies 40–50 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve [1]. The dimensions of Meckel’s diverticulum vary; it commonly measures about 2.9 cm long and 1.9 cm wide and MD exceeding 5 cm are relatively rare and classified as giant. Additionally, it is believed that complications such as bowel obstruction, gastrointestinal bleeding, inflammation, and perforation correspond to the diverticulum size, though these complications are rare [1, 5, 6]. While most patients with MD remain asymptomatic, only about 4–6% develop symptoms, which include abdominal pain with or without fever, nausea and vomiting or constipation, rectal bleeding, and abdominal distension [1]. Torsion of MD represents an exceedingly rare complication, with less than 50 cases documented in literature; it occurs due to axial rotation at MD base, which may emerge either in the presence of a persistent mesodiverticular band or in cases where the diverticulum is freely mobile, though the precise pathophysiological mechanism remains uncertain. The extent of the rotation varies and may impair vascular supply to the diverticulum, potentially resulting in ischemia, necrosis, and eventual perforation, along with its corresponding symptoms [7]. Our patient suffered from a progressively worsening right lower quadrant abdominal pain and had mild abdominal distention accompanied by tenderness and guarding in McBurney’s point. According to the literature, torsion of MD may present with symptoms that can closely resemble other causes of acute abdominal pain, such as appendicitis, bowel obstruction, or pancreatitis [8]. Consequently, preoperative identification remains challenging due to these nonspecific presentations [6, 8]. Identifying the underlying causes of small bowel obstruction before surgery is often challenging, especially when complications from MD are encountered, as an accurate diagnosis is established in only around 6% of cases, especially in the absence of appropriate examinations and radiological modalities. CT remains the most effective imaging modality for evaluation [9]. CT accompanied by ultrasound and plain X-ray radiography may identify complications of MD such as small-bowel obstruction, enteroliths, or intussusception, but their sensitivity and specificity are limited according to Munasinghe et al. [6]. The abdominal plain X-ray imaging of our patient demonstrated air-fluid levels consistent with small bowel obstruction, but unfortunately, a CT scan was not conducted at our hospital, as the device was out of service. Furthermore, the patient’s father declined to have CT conducted at another facility due to financial constraints. Moreover, given the socioeconomic conditions in limited-income third-world countries, many clinicians rely solely on the patients’ symptoms and manifestations to diagnose acute appendicitis without confirmatory imaging to minimize the financial burden on these patients, leading to an increased rate of appendicitis misdiagnosis. Our patient was initially misdiagnosed with acute appendicitis. However, an incision in the right lower quadrant revealed a normal appendix and our patient was subsequently referred to exploratory laparotomy, which revealed a torsed MD located 45 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve measuring 3 × 4 × 7 cm. Symptomatic MD and its pertinent complications are typically managed through surgical removal. This procedure may be performed using either a laparoscopic or traditional open approach. In most cases, both the diverticulum and the surrounding segment of the ileum are resected to ensure complete excision and recovery [2]. MD resection in our patient was completed without intraoperative complications and an end-to-end anastomosis was performed. Postoperatively the total resected specimen was subjected to histopathological examination. Histopathology after complete resection is required to rule out malignancy. Typically, histopathological examination of MD demonstrates normal cytological findings of the small intestines; however, ectopic mucosa occurs in as many as half of Meckel’s diverticulum cases; most often gastric and sometimes pancreatic, duodenal, or jejunal mucosa [7]. In rare cases such as MD torsion, histopathological examination might reveal signs of ischemic necrosis due to restricted blood supply. Histopathological examination in our case revealed foci of hemorrhage and tissue necrosis without discrete lesions or perforation, and no malignant features or cell atypia were noted. The patient completed the postoperative course uneventfully and the follow-up for the next 6 months was unremarkable.

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of MD complications, particularly torsion of the diverticulum with small-bowel obstruction, which can closely resemble acute appendicitis. The challenge is especially pronounced in resource-limited settings, where access to advanced imaging and diagnostic tools is restricted. Torsion of MD can present in early adulthood with right lower quadrant pain and can easily be misdiagnosed for appendicitis in the absence of CT imaging. Further research is needed to establish appropriate diagnosis and management approaches.
