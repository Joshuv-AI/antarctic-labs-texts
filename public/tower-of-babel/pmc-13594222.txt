HOMEODOMAIN-LIKE shapes chromatin architecture and rewires leaf epidermal patterns
Authors: Ansar Ali, Chi Kuan, Tsai-Chen Chen, Fu-Yu Hung, Pei-Yu Lin, Ding Hua Lee, Guan-Jun Lin, Hui-Chun Lee, Shao-Li Yang, Yun-Ru Feng, Chung-Ju Rachel Wang, Pao-Yang Chen, Keqiang Wu, Chin-Min Kimmy Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring development, cells balance proliferation and differentiation to generate diverse cell types, yet how these transitions are fine-tuned remains unclear. In the Arabidopsis leaf epidermis, stomata form through regulated cell division, whereas pavement cells and trichomes arise through differentiation, making this tissue a tractable system for identifying modulators of developmental decisions. Here, we identified a previously uncharacterized homeodomain-like protein HDL, that is required for chromocenter organization through interactions with the histone deacetylase HDA6 and the methyltransferase KRYPTONITE. hdl mutants exhibit disrupted chromocenters and reduced cell division, resulting in fewer stomata and increased trichome formation. Chromatin immunoprecipitation and ATAC-sequencing reveal increased chromatin accessibility at HDL-associated regions and reduced accessibility and expression of stomatal lineage–related genes. Chromatin-state and H3K9me2 analyses further suggest that HDL functions as a chromatin organizer that maintains epigenetic balance at the interface between active and repressive chromatin domains, thereby shaping genome-wide transcriptional programs. Together, these findings identify HDL as a chromatin-associated factor linked to coordinated changes in chromocenter architecture and developmental plasticity.Subject terms: Plant development, Plant cell biology, Plant molecular biology, Chromatin remodelling

Introduction

The leaf epidermis acts as a barrier between plants and their environment, regulating CO2 entry for photosynthesis while protecting against excessive water loss. Assembling this functional tissue requires the coordinated division of meristematic cells, followed by their differentiation into various cell types. Meristemoids, stem cell–like cells on the leaf epidermis, divide asymmetrically to produce more epidermal cell precursors or differentiate into polyploid trichomes or pavement cells through endoreduplication, a cell cycle variant that bypasses the mitotic phase (Fig. 1a). The basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor SPEECHLESS (SPCH) promotes the stomatal lineage cells in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana)1,2, whereas the homeodomain leucine zipper transcription factor GLABRA2 (GL2) positively regulates trichome differentiation3. Despite extensive research into the regulation and signaling pathways that control cell fate in specific cell types, how cell state transitions are coordinated across the leaf epidermis remains elusive.

Cell fate is influenced by the specific phase of the cell cycle and by chromatin structure. Nearly 40 years ago, cell differentiation was shown to be induced specifically at the G1 phase in isolated pluripotent embryonal carcinoma cells and xylem cells4,5. During the G1 phase, the chromatin landscape dynamically undergoes remodeling, changing chromatin accessibility and influencing gene expression. Facultative heterochromatin, present in this phase can reversibly switch between condensed and decondensed states, restricting or allowing the transcription-factor binding, thereby controlling gene transcription in response to the environmental or developmental cues6. In contrast, constitutive heterochromatin remains condensed and transcriptionally inactive, as seen in heterochromatic chromocenters7,8. In Arabidopsis, chromocenters comprise the centromeric and pericentromeric regions of all five chromosomes which form similar nuclear speckles when stained with the DNA dye 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)9. The dense nuclear structures of chromocenters play an important role in organizing and stabilizing the genome during development10. However, the relationship between chromatin organization and cellular plasticity remains largely unclear.

Stomatal lineage ground cells (SLGCs) exhibit flexible potency, functioning as a stem cell–like pool to replenish stomatal precursors and differentiate into pavement cells. Our previous SLGC transcriptome analysis indicated that regulators of mitosis and endoreduplication maintain the balance between proliferation and differentiation11. To identify factors that modulate this cell state transition during leaf epidermal development, we focused on genes highly expressed in SLGCs and identified AT3G53440, a previously uncharacterized homeodomain-like (HDL) gene. HDL is highly expressed in meristematic cells and its expression decreases as leaves mature. Functional analyses revealed that HDL interacts with histone modifiers and is required for proper heterochromatic chromocenter formation, linking chromatin organization to cell states between proliferative potential and differentiation.

Results and discussion

HDL associates with chromocenters

Plants overexpressing HDL-YFP (HDL fused to yellow fluorescent protein) contained more small cells on their leaf epidermis than wild-type (WT) plants, suggesting more cell division events in SLGCs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Phylogenetic analysis of HDL and homologous proteins showed that HDL is a plant lineage–specific protein encoded by a single-copy gene in Arabidopsis (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Data 1). The homeodomain of HDL is also predicted to be a SANT/Myb-like DNA-binding domain, which is present in the subunits of many chromatin remodeling complexes of plants and animals (Supplementary Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 1)12,13. To explore HDL localization, we expressed HDL-YFP driven by the native HDL promoter in Arabidopsis seedlings. In transgenic seedlings, we detected HDL-YFP signals in nuclear speckles of leaf epidermal cells and root cells (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2b), with 3–10 (median = 7) HDL-YFP speckles per nucleus (Fig. 1d). These numbers are reminiscent of Arabidopsis heterochromatic chromocenters.

To assess whether HDL accumulates at chromocenters, we immunostained the nuclei of HDLpro:HDL-YFP seedlings for H3K9me2, a chromocenter marker. Indeed, HDL and H3K9me2 colocalized in chromocenters, which appeared as areas of dense DAPI staining (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). HDL also colocalized with the histone variant H2A.W.6, another heterochromatin marker that marks constitutive heterochromatin and is required for chromatin condensation14,15 (Fig. 1f). DNA methylation is required for heterochromatin silencing, and loss of the chromatin remodeler protein DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION 1 (DDM1) disrupts chromocenter structure16,17. Therefore, to investigate whether HDL localization depends on chromocenters, we performed colocalization analysis using CENH3–mRFP as a centromere marker. In WT nuclei, HDL–YFP showed clear colocalization with CENH3–mRFP at chromocenters (Fig. 1g). In ddm1, where chromocenters are largely decondensed, HDL–YFP foci were smaller and more dispersed, yet consistently colocalized with CENH3–mRFP (Fig. 1g, arrows). These results suggest that HDL associates with centromere-enriched chromocenter regions.

HDL is required for chromocenter organization

To explore HDL function, we obtained a T-DNA insertion mutant line from the SALK collection. The T-DNA (hdl-1, SALK_052138) was inserted in the 3′ end of the single exon (Supplementary Fig. 3a), leading to substantially reduced HDL transcript levels, as shown by RT-PCR and RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The hdl-1 mutant had smaller leaves and shorter roots, both of which were complemented by introducing the HDLpro:HDL-YFP construct (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). We also generated a second hdl mutant allele, hdl-2, harboring a 481-bp deletion, using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated nuclease 9 (Cas9)-mediated editing (Supplementary Fig. 3g–i). This second allele exhibited the same short-root phenotype as hdl-1 (Supplementary Fig. 3j, k).

In some cases, mutations in heterochromatin-associated proteins have been reported to alter chromatin compaction14,18,19. To test whether HDL is required for heterochromatin formation, we used DAPI staining to reveal chromocenters. Chromocenters were counted using three-dimensional (3D) images, and we observed that fewer chromocenters in hdl-1 nuclei compared to the WT (most with 5–7 speckles in hdl-1 compared to 8–10 speckles in WT; Fig. 2a, b; Supplementary Fig. 3l, m). In WT nuclei, H3K9me2 is enriched at DAPI-dense chromocenters. In hdl-1, H3K9me2 signals are fainter and more diffusely distributed throughout the nucleus, while DAPI-dense chromocenters remain clustered and fewer in number (Fig. 2c). These observations prompted us to investigate the reactivation of transposable elements and repetitive sequences constituting these chromocenters. TEs such as TA3, CACTA20, as well as the pericentromeric repeat TRANSCRIPTIONALLY SILENT INFORMATION (TSI) are normally repressed in the chromocenters of WT plants; however, they were highly expressed in hdl-1 (Fig. 2d). These results indicate that HDL is required to maintain chromocenters in a heterochromatic state. To further assess chromocenter organization in hdl-1 mutants, we performed DAPI staining and DNA FISH using probes against CEN180 centromeric repeats and 45S rDNA (Fig. 2e, f). In WT nuclei, DAPI-dense chromocenters colocalized with CEN180 and 45S rDNA signals (Fig. 2e). In contrast, hdl-1 nuclei exhibited fewer discrete DAPI-stained chromocenters, accompanied by CEN180 signals that appeared as irregular clusters with diffuse boundaries (Fig. 2f, arrows), while overall nuclear heterochromatin could still be observed. Quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction in the number of DAPI-stained chromocenters, CEN180 foci, and 45S rDNA foci per nucleus in hdl-1 compared to WT (Fig. 2g). In contrast, the relative nuclear area occupied by DAPI, CEN180, and 45S rDNA signals was not significantly altered (Fig. 2h). These results indicate that loss of HDL does not lead to large-scale heterochromatin loss or chromocenter decondensation, but instead is associated with chromocenter reorganization, characterized by fewer, larger, or more clustered heterochromatic domains enriched in centromeric and pericentromeric repeats.

HDL modulates developmental choices

Because HDL is highly expressed in SLGCs, we also analyzed its role in leaf epidermal development. Both hdl mutants contained fewer stomata on the abaxial leaf surface than the WT (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 3n). In addition, trichome density in the first pair of true leaves was higher in hdl compared to WT (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 3o). Consistent with these phenotypes, hdl-1 showed significantly lower expression of SPCH, encoding a key transcription factor that drives asymmetric cell division in the stomatal lineage (Fig. 3g). The expression of MUTE, a later stomatal lineage–stage gene, was not significantly different between WT and hdl-1. GL2, which promotes trichome formation, was more highly expressed in hdl-1, whereas the negative regulators of trichome formation CAPRICE (CPC) and TRIPTYCHON (TRY) were expressed at comparable levels in WT and hdl-1 (Fig. 3h)3,21.

Leaf morphogenesis depends on the balance between mitosis and endoreduplication11,22. SLGCs are generated by mitosis, whereas trichomes and pavement cells are generated by endoreduplication. Therefore, we wondered whether changes in division potential and ploidy might explain the phenotypes of hdl-1 seedlings. A lower stomatal density may result from fewer divisions of SLGCs and fewer protodermal cells entering the stomatal lineage (Fig. 1a). When we quantified the number of stomatal precursor cells, which are small cells on the epidermis, using 300 μm2 as a size cutoff, we counted fewer small cells in the cotyledons of 3-day-old hdl-1 seedlings compared to the WT (Fig. 3i–k). The hdl-1 mutant had shorter roots (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e, j, k), which could result from diminished cell divisions in the root meristem. Indeed, hdl-1 roots had a smaller meristem and fewer meristem cells than the WT (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Introducing the HDLpro:HDL-YFP transgene into hdl-1 rescued both its short roots and smaller root meristems (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). To confirm that the smaller meristems resulted from fewer cell divisions in the meristem, we used a reporter line consisting of the β-glucuronidase (GUS) driven by the CYCLIN B1;1 promoter (CYCB1;1pro:GUS) to estimate cell division activity. The GUS staining pattern was much weaker in the roots (Supplementary Fig. 4c) and emerging true leaves (Supplementary Fig. 4d) of hdl-1 vs. WT seedlings, suggesting lower mitotic activity following the loss of HDL function. To further examine cell proliferation, we performed 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining to label the cells in the S phase. In hdl-1 seedlings, EdU incorporation in the root tip was significantly reduced relative to WT (Supplementary Fig. 4e), indicating that fewer cells are entering or progressing through S phase and that cell proliferation is decreased in the absence of HDL.

Since trichomes are polyploid cells, the higher trichome density in hdl-1 (Fig. 3d–f) may indicate a higher ploidy. Flow cytometry revealed that hdl-1 had more cells with 16C and 32C nuclei than the WT (Fig. 3l). Together, these data indicate that the epidermal phenotypes observed in hdl-1 are associated with a shift from mitotic cell division toward endoreduplication, accompanied by a transition from proliferative to differentiated cellular states.

HDL interacts with histone modifiers

Heterochromatin is established through DNA methylation and histone modification. Loss of histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and histone deacetylases (HDAs) disrupts heterochromatin condensation8,23. In plants, H3K9me2 is highly enriched at constitutive heterochromatin and is required for TE silencing. Its colocalization with HDL (Fig. 1e) prompted us to investigate whether HDL interact with HMTs and/or HDAs to regulate heterochromatin formation. The Arabidopsis H3K9 methyltransferases SU(VAR)3-9 HOMOLOG 4 (SUVH4, also known as KRYPTONITE [KYP]), SUVH5, and SUVH6 are mediators of H3K9me deposition that function redundantly in TE silencing and heterochromatin maintenance24,25. KYP, SUVH5, and SUVH6 directly interact with the histone deacetylase HDA6 to synergistically regulate TE expression26. Reanalyzing our previous HDA6 immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP-MS) dataset revealed that HDA6 interacts with HDL26. Consistent with this result, in bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, HDL interacted with HDA5, HDA6, HDA19, KYP, SUVH5, and SUVH6 in the nucleus when the corresponding constructs were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Dual-luciferase assays further showed that HDL represses HDA6 and KYP promoter activity (Supplementary Fig. 5b). CYCLIN-DEPENDENT KINASE INHIBITOR 1 (CKI1/KRP1) is known to colocalize with chromocenters, and its truncated form alters endoreduplication levels27. Based on the functional similarity to hdl-1, we investigated whether HDL physically interacts with KRP1 using a BiFC assay; however, no YFP fluorescence was detected, indicating no interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 4a). To validate the above positive interactions, we infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with HDA6-Myc and KYP-FLAG for co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in planta; we detected HDA6 and KYP among the proteins precipitated with HDL (Fig. 4b). These data suggest that HDL regulates heterochromatin organization via HDAs and HMTs.

In Arabidopsis, KYP catalyzes H3K9 methylation, and HDA6 removes acetylation marks, suggesting that the loss of HDL might affect histone methylation or acetylation levels. Immunoblot analysis detected lower H3K9me2 levels but higher H4Ac levels in hdl-1 relative to WT (Fig. 4c–e). This idea is consistent with the finding that hda6 mutants harbor hyperacetylation of histone H428. By contrast, there is no effect on the acetylation of histone H3 in hdl-1. These results demonstrate that HDL associates with H3K9me2 and, in conjunction with histone modifiers like HDA6 and KYP, establishes and maintains heterochromatin. We also tested the genetic interaction of HDL with HDA6 or KYP by generating the hdl-1 hda6 and hdl-1 kyp double mutants. Whereas hdl-1, kyp, and hda6 had a lower stomatal density than WT plants, the double mutants did not show additive effects on stomatal density (Fig. 4f). These results suggest that HDL, HDA6, and KYP act within the same regulatory pathway influencing stomatal development, with the altered phenotype likely arising from the broad effects of HDA6- and KYP-mediated histone modifications on gene expression programs.

HDL binds to both heterochromatin and euchromatin regions

The subcellular localization of HDL and its colocalization with DAPI-stained chromocenters indicate that HDL is preferentially enriched at heterochromatin (Fig. 1e). To investigate its genome-wide binding profile in vivo, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) in the HDLpro:HDL-YFP plants using an anti-GFP antibody. Genome-wide mapping revealed that HDL-bound regions were concentrated in the centromeric and pericentromeric regions across all five Arabidopsis chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 6a), consistent with the observed chromocenter localization. We detected 6,397 HDL-binding peaks globally (Supplementary Data 2), most of which were located in promoter regions (55.3%), suggesting a role for HDL in transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5a). HDL-binding peaks were also enriched in transposable elements (TEs) (8%) and intergenic regions (IGR, 15.3%), but less frequent in introns (7.5%) and exons (13.9%) compared to their genome-wide distribution (Fig. 5a). This enrichment suggests that HDL preferentially associates with non-coding regulatory regions, including promoters, TE-associated sequences, and intergenic regions that can function as cis-regulatory elements or chromatin domain boundaries29, suggesting a role for HDL in higher-order chromatin organization and transcriptional regulation.

To further characterize these binding sites, we analyzed the top 5% of HDL-bound peaks (181 genes; Supplementary Data 2) which included 68 TE genes (37.6%) and 86 protein-coding genes (47.5%) (Fig. 5b). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these loci revealed strong enrichment for chromatin-related processes such as histone deacetylation, histone modification, and transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Data 3). In addition to analyzing TE genes, we further examined HDL-binding regions across transposable elements. The results showed that HDL preferentially binds to elements belonging to the RC/Helitron, LTR/Gypsy, and DNA/MuDR superfamilies (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Among these, the Gypsy superfamily is known to be enriched in pericentromeric heterochromatin, consistent with HDL’s localization to chromocenter regions30. In line with our immunostaining data (Fig. 1e), genome-wide profiles showed that HDL is highly enriched at regions marked by H3K9me2 and predominantly localized to centromeric and pericentromeric regions (Fig. 5d). Although HDL showed preferential association with heterochromatin, it also bound to euchromatin characterized by low H3K9me2, as demonstrated by plotting relative H3K9me2/H3 level against HDL binding intensity (Fig. 5e). To assess how loss of HDL affects heterochromatin states, we examined H3K9me2 profiles normalized to total H3. In WT, H3K9me2 was highly enriched at centromeric and pericentromeric regions (Supplementary Fig. 6c). In hdl-1, these regions showed a clear reduction in H3K9me2 enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 6c, arrows), which was also reflected by the hdl-1/WT ratio (Supplementary Fig. 6d). This reduction was accompanied by a modest redistribution of H3K9me2 signals toward chromosome arms, suggesting localized heterochromatin reorganization rather than global relaxation.

To identify the specific sites of HDL–chromatin association, we extracted 100-bp fragments centered on the HDL-bound peaks using motif analysis (MEME-ChIP, E-value < 0.05)31. This analysis identified a significantly enriched motif recognized by BARLEY B RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BBR/BPC), also known as the GAGA motif, composed of tandem GA repeats (Fig. 5f). BPC6, a GAGA-binding factor, recruits LIKE HETEROCHROMATIN PROTEIN 1 (LHP1), a PRC1 component, and VERNALIZATION 2 (VRN2), a PRC2 component, to Polycomb-responsive elements (PREs) to maintain gene silencing in Arabidopsis32. The other most enriched motifs were TRP2 (MYB-related) and C2C2, which also contain GA repeats. The enrichment of the BBR/BPC motif particularly drew our attention because this motif is bound by plant-specific BBR/BPC transcription factors that regulate organogenesis by altering the transcriptional regulatory network of homeobox genes33,34. In fact, MUTE was shown to recruit BPC proteins to BBR/BPC motifs to suppress entry into proliferative divisions of the stomatal lineage35. HDA6 and KYP also bind to genomic regions enriched in GA repeats36,37. To investigate whether HDL binds to the same sites as HDA6, we compared the HDL-bound regions to previously published ChIP-seq data for HDA637 and discovered that 726 genes were bound by HDL and HDA6 (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Data 4). GO enrichment analysis of these 726 genes indicated enrichment for genes involved in the regulation of transcription, meristem, and leaf development (Fig. 5h). Therefore, the BBR/BPC motifs may guide HDL to the same genomic regions as HDA6 and KYP, resulting in altered H3K9me2 methylation levels at HDL-bound sites (Fig. 5i), in turn leading to chromatin modifications that influence cell fate specificity.

Chromatin accessibility and stomatal-related gene expression are altered in hdl mutants

The interaction of HDA6 and KYP with HDL (Fig. 4), together with the enrichment of HDL at heterochromatin (Fig. 5), suggested that HDL might regulate chromatin accessibility. To explore this possibility, we performed an Assay for Transposase Accessible Chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) in WT and hdl-1 seedlings. Although chromocenters remain the least accessible region in both WT and hdl-1, these regions showed the greatest increase in accessibility in hdl-1 compared with WT (Fig. 6a). This observation, along with the finding that HDL localizes to chromocenters (Fig. 1) and analysis of HDL-bound regions (Fig. 5d), suggests that HDL plays a crucial role in heterochromatin organization. To narrow down the genomic sites of HDL action, we compared our ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq datasets. Chromatin in HDL-bound regions and 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTRs) was more accessible in hdl-1 than in WT (Fig. 6b). Focusing on HDL-bound genes, we observed greater accessibility in hdl-1, especially around gene centers, compared to WT (Fig. 6c, d). When we plotted chromatin accessibility in hdl-1 normalized to that in WT against H3K9me2 levels, genomic regions with high H3K9me2 levels associated with heterochromatin were more accessible in hdl-1 (Fig. 6e, upper-right quadrant). These results suggest the potential local effect of HDL in heterochromatic regions.

To investigate the relationship between stomatal development and chromatin accessibility, we assessed chromatin accessibility specifically at stomata-related (Supplementary Data 5) and SPCH-bound genes38. Consistent with the lower stomatal density of hdl-1, we observed lower chromatin accessibility upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) for stomata-related and SPCH-bound genes in hdl-1 (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 7a). To assess whether this altered chromatin accessibility was correlated with changes in expression levels, we conducted transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) of epidermal cells sorted from WT and hdl-1 seedlings by fluorescence-activated cell sorting, using an epidermal cell–specific reporter. Overall, we identified 1932 upregulated and 2540 downregulated genes in hdl-1 compared to WT (P < 0.05, |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1, Supplementary Fig. 7b, Supplementary Data 5). Among 527 transposable element (TE) genes identified (Supplementary Data 6), 12 TE genes were found to be upregulated and 51 downregulated in the hdl-1 mutant compared to WT (P < 0.05), |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1, (Supplementary Fig. 7c). However, among the top 5% most highly enriched HDL-bound loci, corresponding to 181 genes and TEs, only seven were significantly downregulated and two were significantly upregulated in the hdl mutant (Supplementary Data 2). Thus, despite the widespread transcriptional changes observed in hdl-1, only a small fraction of the most strongly HDL-bound loci exhibited altered expression. This limited overlap between HDL occupancy and transcriptional changes indicates that HDL plays a broader role in maintaining chromatin architecture and proper H3K9me2 distribution across the genome.

To determine whether HDL preferentially associates with specific chromatin environments, we compared HDL-bound peaks (Supplementary Data 2) with the nine chromatin states defined by Sequeira-Mendes et al.29. HDL is enriched in heterochromatic states 8 and 9, which are associated with transposable elements and pericentromeric heterochromatin, consistent with its localization at chromocenters (Supplementary Fig. 7d and Fig. 1e). HDL-bound peaks were also enriched in states 1, 2 and 4, corresponding to promoters, 5’ UTRs and intergenic regions, consistent with their genomic distribution (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 7d). To examine the relationship between chromatin configuration and transcriptional outcomes, we analyzed the chromatin-state distribution of all differentially expressed genes in hdl-1. Differentially expressed genes were enriched in chromatin states 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9 (Supplementary Fig. 7e). The enrichment in heterochromatic chromatin states 8 and 9 suggests that loss of HDL perturbs chromocenter organization, leading to transcriptional changes in heterochromatin-associated genomic regions. When focusing specifically on HDL-bound genes, upregulated genes were preferentially enriched in chromatin states 2 and 4, whereas downregulated genes were enriched in chromatin states 4 and 5 (Supplementary Fig. 7f, g). Chromatin state 4 has previously been described as a major interaction hub linking active chromatin (states 2 and 6), Polycomb-associated chromatin (state 5), and heterochromatin (state 8) (Supplementary Fig. 7g)29. These analyses indicate that the transcriptional consequences of HDL loss are strongly influenced by chromatin context and suggest that HDL plays an important role in maintaining transcriptional balance at the interface between active and repressive chromatin environments.

To further investigate whether the transcriptional changes observed in the hdl mutant could be explained by redistribution of heterochromatic marks, we examined H3K9me2/H3 enrichment at HDL target genes. If gene repression resulted from local spreading of H3K9me2 from neighboring transposable elements (TEs), genes located closer to TEs would be expected to exhibit stronger increases in H3K9me2. However, among HDL target genes downregulated in the hdl-1 mutant, H3K9me2/H3 changes were comparable regardless of their distance to the nearest TE (proximal <1 kb, intermediate 1–5 kb, or distal >5 kb; Supplementary Fig. 7h), arguing against a simple linear spreading model from adjacent TEs. We next examined whether transcriptional changes at direct HDL targets were associated with alterations in H3K9me2 within specific chromatin states. HDL-bound genes were grouped according to their expression changes in the hdl-1 mutant, revealing that both downregulated and upregulated genes exhibited significantly increased H3K9me2/H3 levels compared with non-differentially expressed HDL targets, particularly within chromatin state 4 (Supplementary Fig. 7i, j). Given that chromatin state 4 represents an interface between active, Polycomb-associated, and heterochromatic domains, these findings suggest that HDL helps maintain epigenetic balance within chromatin transition zones. Rather than acting as a conventional sequence-specific transcription factor, HDL appears to function as a chromatin organizer whose loss perturbs local chromatin states and ultimately contributes to the widespread transcriptional changes observed in hdl mutants (Supplementary Fig. 7b, e).

Genes related to stomatal development and the cell cycle were significantly downregulated in hdl-1 (Fig. 6g); this finding correlates with the lower accessibility of the promoters of stomata-related genes (Fig. 6f). To validate the downregulation of stomata-related genes, we measured the abundance of the master stomatal transcription factor SPCH in transgenic lines carrying the SPCHpro:SPCH-CFP transgene in the WT and hdl-1 backgrounds. We detected significantly fewer cells with SPCH-CFP fluorescence in hdl-1 compared to the WT (Fig. 6h, i), supporting our observations of lower chromatin accessibility and lower expression of stomata-related genes. We then visualized HDL-bound regions, chromatin accessibility, H3K9me2 methylation levels, and transcript levels of stomata-related genes in the integrated genome viewer (IGV). HDL-bound stomata-related genes, such as the cell cycle gene CYCLIN D3;1 (CYCD3;1) and EPIDERMAL PATTERNING FACTOR 2 (EPF2), were significantly downregulated in hdl-1 vs. the WT, leading to fewer divisions of meristemoid cells (Supplementary Fig. 8). For genes not bound by HDL such as SPCH and YODA, their expression levels were also lower in hdl-1. These observations indicate that HDL differentially affects chromatin accessibility across heterochromatin and euchromatin, and changes in stomata-related gene expression appear to arise only partially from direct binding.

During early development, stem cells may undergo transcriptional bursts associated with specific fates; the frequency of such bursts may lead to variability in gene expression. Histone deacetylation can influence expression levels within genetically identical cells, thereby generating heterogeneity in a population39. Because trichome development occurs early in small, immature leaves, from which protoplasts are challenging to isolate, detecting changes in the expression of trichome-related genes in specific cells is difficult. Thus, we measured GL2 expression in WT and hdl-1 using the transcriptional reporter GL2pro:nucGFP in which nucleus-localized GFP reflects the transcriptional output from the GL2 promoter. We quantified fluorescence intensity from nuclear GFP in early trichome precursor cells or petiole cells, which undergo rapid division from the adaxial epidermis of the emerging true leaf. GL2 expression was significantly higher in hdl-1 than in WT (Fig. 6j, k). However, the variation in GFP intensity was larger in hdl-1 compared to WT in the petiole region, where all cells expressed GL2 (Fig. 6l, m). These observations suggest that loss of HDL function is associated with increased variability in cell fate–related gene expression during early leaf development.

Here, we identify HDL as a chromatin-associated factor that colocalizes with CENH3 and functions together with HDA6 and KYP to regulate chromocenter organization. hdl mutants exhibit reduced cell proliferation, leading to a diminished stomatal precursor pool. Genome-wide analyses show that HDL binding is enriched at regulatory elements, indicating its role in transcriptional regulation. However, the relatively small overlap between HDL-bound loci and differentially expressed genes suggests that HDL does not primarily function as a sequence-specific transcription factor. Instead, HDL appears to influence gene expression through chromatin context and higher-order genome organization.

Several observations support this model. HDL preferentially associates with chromatin states located at the interface between active, Polycomb-associated, and heterochromatic domains, particularly chromatin state 4, a major chromatin interaction hub. Consistent with this distribution, loss of HDL resulted in activation of genes enriched in promoter-associated chromatin states (states 2 and 4) and repression of genes associated with Polycomb-regulated domains (states 4 and 5) (Supplementary Fig. 7). In parallel, hdl-1 mutants exhibited reduced H3K9me2 enrichment at chromocenters accompanied by a relative redistribution of H3K9me2 along chromosome arms (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). H3K9me2 accumulation did not correlate with proximity to transposable elements, arguing against a simple model of local heterochromatin spreading from neighboring TEs. Instead, increased H3K9me2 levels within chromatin state 4 indicate that loss of HDL disrupts higher-order chromatin organization and alters epigenetic states at the interface between active and repressive chromatin domains. Consequently, transcriptional outcomes cannot be explained solely by H3K9me2 accumulation but are shaped by the interplay between altered H3K9me2 distribution and the local chromatin context.

ATAC-seq analyses further support a context-dependent role for HDL in chromatin regulation. Although HDL-bound regions and 5’ UTRs generally become more accessible in hdl-1, key stomatal regulatory genes display reduced accessibility upstream of their transcription start sites together with reduced expression. Thus, loss of HDL does not result in a uniform increase in chromatin accessibility but rather causes localized and heterogeneous chromatin reorganization. Together with the disruption of chromocenter architecture, these findings suggest that transcriptional changes in hdl mutants arise from both direct effects on promoter-associated chromatin and indirect effects of altered higher-order chromatin organization.

In hdl-1 mutants, the accumulation of polyploid cells is accompanied by reduced expression of multiple cell-cycle regulators, including A-type CYCLINs (CYCAs), B-type CYCLIN-dependent kinases (CDKBs), and CYCD3;1, a direct HDL target. These transcriptional changes are consistent with altered cell-cycle dynamics, while broader chromatin reorganization may further impair efficient cell-cycle progression. Perturbations of the cell cycle are known to influence cell state regulation. For example, the cell cycle inhibitor SIAMESE-RELATED1 (SMR1), together with CYCA and CYCLIN-DEPENDENT KINASE B1 (CDKB1), terminate the self-renewal potential of SLGCs40. SMR4 promotes guard mother cells differentiation by slowing cell cycle progression41. In addition, cell-cycle context can modulate transcription factor activity; for example, SPCH stability is influenced by CDKA;1-mediated phosphorylation42. Accordingly, reduced mitotic activity in hdl-1 may create cellular conditions less favorable for SPCH phosphorylation and stability, consistent with the diminished SPCH-CFP signal observed (Fig. 6h).

Together, our data support a model in which HDL coordinates chromatin organization, transcriptional regulation, and cell-cycle progression. While altered heterochromatin organization correlates with the misexpression of cell-fate regulators, the developmental defects observed in hdl-1 likely reflect broader changes in gene regulatory networks (Fig. 6n). Future studies dissecting the molecular role of HDL within chromatin regulatory complexes will help clarify how chromatin architecture and gene expression programs are integrated to direct cell-fate decisions.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) accession Columbia (Col-0) was used as the wild type (WT) in all experiments, and all transgenic lines were created in this background. The hdl-1 mutant SALK-052138 was obtained from ABRC. The hda6 (axe1-5), kyp36, and ddm1 mutants were described previously16,17. The plant reporter lines used in this study include AtML1pro:RCI2A-mCherry, SPCHpro:SPCH-CFP43, and 35Spro:CENH3-GFP44. The primers used to generate the constructs are listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

Seeds were surface sterilized with ethanol and sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium solidified with 1% (w/v) agar. The plates were placed at 4 °C for 2 days in the dark for seed stratification. The plates were then transferred to a plant tissue culture room set to 22 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod (light intensity of 80–100 µmol m−2 s−1, wavelength 400–720 nm, bulb type: fluorescent tubes). Plant materials used for phenotyping and for extracting mRNAs and proteins were grown on half-strength MS plates for 7 days unless otherwise specified.

Plasmid construction

The reporter constructs were created by Gateway (Invitrogen) cloning using the pENTR 5′-TOPO, pENTR-D-TOPO, and pDONR P4-P1R plasmids as entry clones. To facilitate cloning, the EcoRI site (NEB) of pENTR 5′-TOPO was replaced by NotI and AscI sites; the resulting plasmid was named pENTR5′S. The full-length coding sequences of all genes were cloned into pENTR-D-TOPO (Invitrogen) using conventional TOPO isomerase (Invitrogen) or by restriction enzyme digestion with NotI and AscI (NEB) depending on the primers (listed in the Supplementary Table 1). The HDLpro:HDL-YFP construct was generated using pENTR 5′-TOPO-HDLpro, pENTR/D-TOPO-HDL, and R4pGWB54045. 35S:HDL-YFP was obtained using pENTR/D-TOPO-HDL and the destination vector pH35GY. H2A.W.6pro:H2A.W.6-CFP was obtained using pENTR 5′S-H2A.W.6pro, pENTR/D-TOPO-H2AW.6, and R4pGWB443. 35Spro:CENH3-mRFP was obtained using pENTR 5′S-35Spro, pENTR/D-TOPO-CENH3, and R4pGWB553. GL2pro:nucGFP was cloned using pENTR/D-TOPO-GL2pro in the binary destination vector pGGN. To generate hdl-2 lines, two single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences were introduced into plasmids containing AtU6-26pro:sgRNA by BsaI (NEB). These constructs were then introduced into pCAMBIA1300-221 containing the YAOpro:Cas9 cassette by restriction digestion with SpeI and NheI46. All constructs are listed in the Supplementary Table 2.

For subcellular localization, co-localization, and complementation assays, the constructs were introduced into Arabidopsis plants via Agrobacterium (Agrobacterium tumefaciens) (strain GV3101)-mediated transformation by the floral dip method47.

Phenotyping of roots and stomata

To analyze root growth, seedlings were grown on half-strength MS medium under long-day conditions (16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod) on plates positioned vertically in the growth chamber for 4 days before being transferred to new plates and incubated for 3–5 days to track and measure root elongation. After the roots were stained with propidium iodide (PI), root meristem cells in the cortex layer were counted in a file from the quiescent center to the first elongated cell (i.e., a cell double the size of the previous cell).

DAPI staining

Seven-day-old seedlings of Col-0 and hdl-1 were treated with 1 mL of freshly prepared fixation solution (4% (w/v) formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich F1636 in PBS)). Vacuum was applied using a speed vacuum concentrator for 5 mins, followed by incubation on ice for 25 mins without vacuum. The fixation solution was removed, and the samples were washed three times for 5 mins each in PBS on ice. Samples were stained with 10 µg/mL DAPI containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then rinsed with water. The samples were placed on glass slides, root tips approximately 5 mm long were cut and transferred onto polysine-coated slides, then mounted with a drop (5 µL) of ProLong Diamond mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36962) containing DAPI. Finally, the slides were covered with coverslips (24 × 40 mm).

All fixed sample DAPI fluorescence intensity microscopy measurements were performed on an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning microscope coupled to a 405 nm pulsed laser. A 60 × water immersion objective 1.2 NA was used for all experiments and the cells were imaged at room temperature.

Transient expression method

Transient expression in Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings used vacuum-based Agrobacterium (GV3101 strain) infiltration, adapted from48,49. Briefly, Agrobacterium carrying 35Spro:CENH3-mRFP and 35Spro:HDL-YFP was grown on YEB medium, resuspended to optimal density, and vacuum-applied to 5-day-old seedlings to promote entry into cotyledons. Post-infiltration, seedlings were dried briefly, returned to growth conditions (e.g., 22 °C, 16/8-h light/dark), and imaged via confocal microscopy for fluorescent proteins after 2 days. For DNA staining, seedlings were incubated with 1 µg mL⁻¹ Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62249) for 30 mins prior to imaging.

Confocal microscopy

Protein subcellular localization and the plasma membrane marker AtML1pro:RCI2A-mCherry were monitored in the first pair of true leaves from 6-day-old seedlings under a Leica Stellaris 8 (Leica Microsystem) or Zeiss LSM880 (Carl Zeiss system) microscope. The fluorescence of CFP, GFP, YFP, and mCherry was excited with a wavelength of 458-nm, 488-nm, 514-nm, and 561-nm, respectively.

To analyze trichomes, the first pair of true leaves was imaged under a Stereo Fluorescent Microscope (Zeiss Lumar V12). The GUS staining signal was monitored with an Upright Fluorescent Microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager Z1).

GUS and EdU staining

WT and hdl-1 seedlings carrying the CYCB1pro:GUS reporter50 were grown vertically on solid half-strength MS medium plates for 5 days. The seedlings were immersed in ice-cold 90% (v/v) acetone for 10 min, rinsed once with ddH2O, and submerged in GUS staining solution (100 µM K3Fe(CN)6, 100 µM K4Fe(CN)6, 50 mM phosphate buffer [pH 7.0], and 1 mM X-GLUC in dimethyl formamide). The seedlings were subjected to two rounds of vacuum infiltration for 10 min each time. The seedlings were incubated at 37 °C for 3–5 h, followed by 80% (v/v) ethanol overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the samples were washed with a mixture of methanol and acetic acid (7:1, v/v) for 15 min at room temperature and mounted on slides in ethanol for imaging using an Imager Z1 microscope (Zeiss).

Five to ten seedlings were grown vertically on half-strength MS medium for 5 days and incubated for 1 h in 20 µM EdU (Invitrogen, Cat.10337) in a 12-cell plate containing liquid basal medium. The liquid medium was removed, after which the seedlings were washed twice with ddH2O and fixed with 4% (w/v) formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 15 min. After fixation, the seedlings were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each time. The roots were incubated at room temperature for 30 min in EdU detection cocktail (Invitrogen). After three washes with PBS, the roots were visualized by confocal microscopy (LSM 880, Zeiss).

RT-qPCR analysis

For gene expression analysis, total RNA was extracted using an RNA Plus Mini Kit (LabPrep, FATRK-P 050) from 5-day post-germination (5dpg) seedlings for Ta3, TSI, trichome, and stomatal genes. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1–5 µg of total RNA using SuperScript II transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 18064014). The qPCR mixture comprised a cDNA template (100 ng), specific primers, and Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4367659). qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 12 K Flex Real-Time PCR cycler system (Applied Biosystems). The primers used for RT-qPCR can be found in the Supplementary Table 1.

BiFC assay

The full-length coding sequences of HDA6, KYP, and HDL were amplified from cDNA, cloned into the pCR8-GW-TOPO vector, and recombined into the YN vector pEarleyGate201-YN or the YC vector pEarleyGate202-YC via LR cloning36. The resulting plasmids were co-infiltrated as appropriate pairs into the abaxial sides of N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration using strain GV3101. The infiltrated leaf epidermal cells were observed under a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope 3–4 days after infiltration.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

The reporter constructs (HDA5p:LUC, HDA6p:LUC, HDA9p:LUC, HDA15p:LUC, HDA17p:LUC, and KYPp:LUC) and effector constructs (HDLp:HDL-YFP or 35S:nucGFP) were transiently co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. Firefly luciferase (LUC) activity was normalized to co-expressed Renilla luciferase (REN) activity using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910). Two independent biological experiments were performed.

Flow cytometry

To measure the ploidy levels of hdl-1 and WT seedlings, the first pair of true leaves from 12-day-old seedlings was finely chopped with a sharp blade in a 55-mm-diameter plastic petri dish containing 250 µl nuclei extraction buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 4 mM MgCl2, and 0.5% [v/v] Triton X-100) (Sysmex, CyStain PI Absolute P) for 1 min. Nuclei were stained by incubation in 1000 µl staining buffer (50 μg/ml PI and RNase A, for excitation at 561 nm). The mixture was passed through a Partec 50-µm CellTrics disposable filter and incubated on ice for 30 min in the dark. The samples were analyzed with a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX Cell Analyzer).

Immunostaining

Five-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde prepared in 1× PBS. Root tip cells were gently scraped with a razor blade to facilitate antibody penetration. Fixed cells were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides (Matsunami, cat. SUAPS19)51,52. The slides onto which samples were mounted were incubated in 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min to block non-specific binding at 37 °C. After adding primary antibodies (1:200 dilution for anti-GFP Abcam, ab290 and anti-H3K9me2, Abcam, ab1290), the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed three times with 1× PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (1:20,000 dilution for Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L [Alexa Fluor 488], Abcam, ab150113 and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L [Alexa Fluor 555], ab150078) for 1 h at 37 °C or 3 h at room temperature. After one wash with 1× PBS, the slides were counterstained with 1 mg/ml DAPI and examined under a Leica Stellaris 8 microscope.

To quantify chromocenters, seedlings were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 1 h and rinsed with ddH2O. Nuclei were isolated from roots as described above, mounted on slides with 1 mg/ml DAPI, and observed under a Leica Stellaris 8 microscope.

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from 2 g fresh weight of 10-day-old seedlings. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with anti-H3K9me2 (Abcam, ab1220; 1:8,000), anti-H3Ac (Merck Millipore, 06-599; 1:10,000), anti-H3 (Abcam, ab1791; 1:10,000), anti-H4Ac (Merck Millipore, 06-866; 1:10,000), or anti-H4 (Abcam, ab10158; 1:10,000). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab6721; 1:25,000) or HRP-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 715-035-150; 1:25,000) was used as the secondary antibody.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Five-day-old seedlings were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS and subjected to three cycles of vacuum/release, followed by fixation at room temperature for 45 min.

The nuclei extraction protocol was modified from53. Briefly, fixed cotyledons or roots were placed in a 1.5‑ml tube containing nuclei extraction buffer (10 mM Tris‑HCl (pH 7.5), 4 mM spermidine, 1 mM spermine, 5 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X‑100) and gently homogenized using a plastic pestle. The resulting nuclei suspension was filtered through a 50 μm CellTrics filter and pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 3 min. The nuclei pellet was washed several times with 1× PBS. The nuclei suspension in 1× PBS was spotted onto a poly-L-lysine–coated glass slide, followed by the application of a coverslip. The slide was then frozen in liquid nitrogen, allowing for the easy removal of the coverslip using a surgical blade. Subsequently, slides were then dehydrated in 50%, 75%, and 95% ethanol, and air-dried overnight at room temperature.

The CEN180 FISH probe for centromere labeling was amplified by PCR using primers ATH_cen180F and ATH_cen180R (Supplementary Table 1) according to54. The ribosomal 45S DNA (rDNA) probe was amplified from plasmid containing 45S rDNA gene of Oryza sativa L. ssp. Japonica using universal M13 primers. PCR products were purified using the PCR Clean-up kit (Geneaid). The DNA probes were labeled with fluorescein-12-dUTP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), cyanine 3-dUTP (NEL578001EA) or Cy5-dUTP (Cytiva PA55022) by nick translation (Sigma, 10976776001). Prior to hybridization, slides were treated with RNase. The hybridization mixture contained 50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× SSC, 0.2% SDS, 2 ng/ml probe, and 100 ng/ml salmon sperm DNA. The probe mixture was denatured at 85 °C for 10 min and then immediately cooled on ice for 3 min. Twenty ul of probe mixture were applied to each slide, and then denatured simultaneously at 75 °C for 10 min. Hybridization was taken place in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 16 h. Following hybridization, slides were washed with 20% formamide in 0.1× SSC for 10 min at 42 °C, followed by three rinses in 2× SSC. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/ml) and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade mountant. Images were acquired using a Deltavision core microscope equipped with a Plan Apochromat 100X oil immersion objective lens.

The FISH images were reconstructed from z-stacks using ImageJ’s Z Max projection plugin. Nucleus were segmented via Otsu’s automatic thresholding, while chromocenters were isolated using manual or fixed-intensity thresholds. Signal percentage per nucleus was calculated as (total signal area/nucleus area) × 100, normalizing occupancy within each nucleus. Two-sided Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon tests compared WT and hdl-1 groups.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

The ChIP assays were performed using chromatin extracts prepared from seedlings treated with 1% (w/v) formaldehyde55. The chromatin was sheared to an average length of 500 bp by sonication (Bioruptor, Diagenode), and protein–DNA complexes were immunoprecipitated using specific antibodies against YFP (Abcam, no. ab290 (1:200)), H3 (Abcam, no. ab1791(1:2000)) or H3K9me2 (Abcam, no. ab1220 (1:200)). The crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65 °C under high-salt treatment for 6 h, followed by incubation in proteinase K at 55 °C for 1 h, and the samples were examined by qPCR analysis.

ChIP-seq and data analysis

To ensure an adequate starting amount of DNA for library construction, 2 ng of DNA from ChIP was utilized. The ChIP DNA was initially subjected to qPCR and employed for ChIP-seq library preparation using a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit (cat no. E7645), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A NovaSeq instrument was used for high-throughput sequencing of the ChIP-seq libraries as paired-end 150-bp reads. Raw sequence data were processed using the GAPipeline Illumina sequence data analysis pipeline. The resulting clean reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using Bowtie256. The hdl-1 HDLpro:HDL-YFP line was used for ChIP-seq. Over 30 million paired-end (150 bp) reads were used. The alignments were converted to Wiggle (WIG) files using deepTools and imported into Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV)57 for visualization. MACS2 software58 was used to identify ChIP-enriched peaks, and the distribution of the ChIP peaks was analyzed with ChIPseeker59 and Python. A subset of random Arabidopsis genomic regions (1,350,000 regions) was used to represent the ratio of the total Arabidopsis genomic regions. HDL ChIP-seq peaks were mapped to transposable elements annotated in the TAIR10_transposable_elements and read enrichment was quantified across TE superfamilies. To identify enriched motifs, a 100-bp fragment encompassing each peak summit (50 bp upstream, 50 bp downstream) was obtained and used as a query against known DNA motifs using MEME-ChIP with default parameters31. The HDL-YFP ChIP-seq short read data have been submitted to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE223849. The H3K9me2 ChIP-seq short read data have been submitted to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE294312.

ATAC-seq

Protoplast Isolation: Nine-day-old Arabidopsis Col-0 and hdl-1 seedlings harboring the AtML1pro:RCI2A-mCherry reporter were harvested, and ~180–260 seedlings were placed into 10 ml protoplasting solution: 1.25% (w/v) Cellulase R10 (Yakult), 0.3% (w/v) maceroenzyme R10 (Yakult), 0.4 M D-mannitol (Sigma M4125), 20 mM KCl (Sigma P9541), 20 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES; Sigma M8025), 10 mM CaCl2 (Sigma C3306), and 0.1% (w/v) BSA (Sigma A7906), with the pH adjusted to pH 5.7 with 1 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 (Invitrogen #15567027). After 2 h of digestion with gentle shaking at room temperature, the protoplast solution was filtered through a 40-µm mesh (EASYstrainer small, Greiner Bio-One International GmbH) and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 20 °C in a swinging-bucket microcentrifuge. The supernatant (~7 ml) was discarded and the remaining 3 ml was retained and used to resuspend the cells. To distinguish between epidermal cells and more internal cells, the epidermal cells were sorted based on mCherry fluorescence. The protoplast solution was filtered through a 40-µm mesh and sorted using a FACSAria II (BD Biosciences) instrument fitted with a 100 micrometers nozzle. Approximately 50,000–200,000 protoplasts were sorted into 300 µl PBS.

Transposition: Freshly sorted cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The following steps were carried out on ice. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 50 µl ice-cold cell lysis buffer: 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% (v/v) IGEPAL GA-630, 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20, and 0.01% (w/v) digitonin. Following incubation on ice for 3 min, 1 ml wash buffer was added to the sample: 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20. The nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was removed by aspiration. The nuclei were resuspended in 50 μl of tagmentation reaction mix, as per the manufacturer’s instructions (TDE1, Illumina) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in a thermomixer with shaking at 1000 rpm. The reactions were immediately terminated by adding 250 µl (five volumes) of DNA-binding buffer from a DNA Clean and Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research D4013) and mixing well by pipetting. DNA was purified following the manufacturer’s instructions and eluted in 12 µl of nuclease-free water.

Barcoding of Transposed Fragments: DNA was amplified by PCR (50-µl reactions) using ATAC barcoded primers and NEB Next High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (5 min at 72 °C, 30 s at 98 °C, then 5 × [10 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 63 °C, 1 min at 72 °C], hold at 4 °C). Five microliters of the PCR mixture was further amplified for qPCR (30 s at 98 °C, then 40 × [10 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 63 °C, 1 min at 72 °C]) to determine the required number of additional cycles. For each reaction, the number of additional cycles was determined as the cycle number for which a reaction reached one-third of its maximum, plotting the linear fluorescence as a function of cycle number. All libraries were purified with a DNA Clean and Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo research D4013) and eluted in 12 µl of nuclease-free water.

AMPure XP Beads Double Size Selection of Library DNA: All libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads at a ratio of 0.5:1 beads: DNA (v/v); the resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The DNA was purified again with AMPure XP beads at a ratio of 1.3:1 beads: DNA (final ratio of 1.8:1 beads: DNA). The DNA that bound to the beads was eluted in 20 µl of nuclease-free water.

ATAC-seq and data analysis

The quality of the raw ATAC-seq reads was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9; the reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.3360. The clean reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10)56 with Bowtie 2 v2.3.5.161. The removal of mitochondrial DNA, plastid DNA, and duplicated reads, the calculation of fragment length distributions, and peak calling were performed with the aid of the ATACgraph pipeline62. To evaluate the quality of the ATAC-seq libraries, the Fraction of Reads in Peaks and Irreproducible Discovery Rate were calculated following the ATAC-seq guidelines from the ENCODE project (https://www.encodeproject.org/atac-seq/). The final alignment files were converted to bigwig format for visualization on the genome browser IGV using deepTools v3.5.0. Post-alignment analysis and figure generation were conducted with R and Python scripts. The data were deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE294311.

RNA-seq

Protoplasts were prepared from 9-day-old seedings of the reporter lines and used for protoplast isolation and FACS as described above. FACS was performed on an Aria II (BD Biosciences) instrument with a 100 micrometer nozzle. Approximately 20,000 mCherry-positive cells per sample were sorted into 350 µl RNA extraction buffer, and total RNA was extracted from the samples using an RNeasy Plant Mini kit (QIAGEN, Cat.74904). RNA from three biological replicates was sequenced separately, and sequencing libraries were constructed using an Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Preparation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq platform as 150-bp paired-end reads.

Reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis reference genome (TAIR10) using the Qiagen CLC genomic workbench (https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/) with default settings. Reads that mapped to multiple regions were discarded. Gene annotation was performed using Araport11 to identify transposable_element_genes. Calculations to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were performed with DESeq2. Genes with at least a 1-fold change in expression (P < 0.05) were considered to be differentially expressed. The data were deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE294313.

Phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequence of HDL was retrieved from the TAIR website (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Proteins related to HDL in green algae were identified by a BLAST search against individual species, using Arabidopsis HDL as a query (https://phycocosm.jgi.doe.gov/phycocosm/home). Proteins related to HDL in other plant species were identified by a BLAST search at Phytozome v13 using Arabidopsis HDL as a query (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). Predicted homologs were again subjected to BLAST analysis to verify their conserved domains. All sequences were then submitted to MAFFT or MUSCLE (https://ngphylogeny.fr/tools/tool/268/form) for multiple sequence alignment. The phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the IQ-TREE web server (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at) with the maximum likelihood method. The model was set as JTT with 100 bootstrap replications. The tree was edited in FigTree.v1.4.4 software.
