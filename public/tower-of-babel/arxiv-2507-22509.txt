A quasi-optimal upper bound for induced paths in sparse graphs
Basile Couëtoux, Oscar Defrain, Jean-Florent Raymond

Abstract
In 2012, Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez proved that graphs of bounded degeneracy that have a path of order n n also have an induced path of order Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log\log n) . In this paper we give an almost matching upper bound by describing, for arbitrarily large values of n n , a 2-degenerate graph that has a path of order n n and where all induced paths have order O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ⋅ log ⁡ log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n) . Keywords: long induced paths, unavoidable induced subgraphs, sparse graphs, degeneracy.



1
Introduction

In this work, we aim to understand the relation between long paths and induced paths in graphs.
Let us denote by
\lp(G)
and
\lip(G)
the maximum orders of a path and of an induced path in a graph
G
. We trivially always have
\lip(G)\leq\lp(G)
since an induced path is in particular a path. Conversely, in which graph classes is
\lip
lower-bounded by (a function of)
\lp
? More formally, we are interested in graph classes
\mathcal{G}
for which there is an unbounded function
f
satisfying, for every
G\in\mathcal{G}
,
\lip(G)\geq f(\lp(G))
.
This topic is studied since the 80’s at least when
Galvin, Rival, and Sands proved the following general result.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
GRS82
]
)
.

For every
t
there is an unbounded function
f
such that if
G
is a
K_{t,t}
-subgraph free graph that has a path of order
n
, then
G
has an induced path of order at least
f(n)
.

Actually, the authors of the above result were not interested in an explicit expression of the function
f
, but it can be extracted from their proof and the currently best-known bounds on the multicolor Ramsey numbers that
f(n)=\Omega((\log\log\log n)^{1/3})
.
This result has been improved to
f(n)=\Omega((\log\log n)^{1/5})
in
[
DER24
]
. Very recently, Hunter, Milojević, Sudakov, and Tomon gave an elegant and short proof that
f(n)=\Omega(\log\log n/\log\log\log n)

[
HMST24
]
. At the time of writing this is the best-known lower-bound on
f
and no matching upper bound is known.

On the other hand, there are graph classes where a statement such as Theorem
1.1
does not hold. This is the case of any class containing arbitrarily large cliques or balanced bicliques. Indeed, such graphs have a path containing all their vertices, yet no induced path of order more than 2 or 3, respectively. Note that an hereditary graph class that does not contain arbitrarily large cliques or balanced bicliques is
K_{t,t}
-subgraph free for some
t
. Hence, for hereditary graph classes Theorem
1.1
is best possible in the sense that the
K_{t,t}
-subgraph freeness assumption cannot be dropped.

Let us say that a graph
G
is
k
-degenerate
if every subgraph of
G
has a vertex of degree at most
k
. Note that
k
-degenerate graphs are
K_{k+1,k+1}
-subgraph free, hence they are covered by the aforementioned results.
In the
Sparsity
textbook
[
NOdM12
]
, Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez gave a simple yet clever proof of the following.
Unless otherwise specified, all logarithms are binary.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
NOdM12
]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be a
k
-degenerate graph. If
G
has a path of order
n
, then
G
has an induced path of order at least

{\log\log n}/{\log(k+1)}.

The authors of the above explicitly asked the question of finding the optimal bound.

Question 1.3

(
[
NOdM12
, Problem 6.1]
)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, what is the maximum function
f_{k}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that every
k
-degenerate graph
G
that has a path of order
n
has an induced path of order at least
f_{k}(n)
?

We note that several recent papers also study the length of induced paths in graph classes such as planar graphs or graphs of bounded treewidth, see
[
DER24
]
for an overview.

Since
[
NOdM12
]
appeared, no improvement over the bound of Theorem
1.2
was made. On the other hand, in 2023 the second and the third author gave the following upper bound: there are 2-degenerate graphs that have a path of order
n
and where all induced paths have order
O((\log\log n)^{2})

[
DR24
]
. Observe that 2-degenerate graphs are also
K_{t,t}
-subgraph free for every
t\geq 3
, hence this also provides an upper bound on the function of Theorem
1.1
.

Contribution and techniques.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a constant
c
such that for infinitely many integers
n
, there is a 2-degenerate graph
G
that has a path of order
n
and no induced path of order more than
c\cdot\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n
.

This is an improvement over the
O((\log\log n)^{2})
bound of
[
DR24
]
and answers Question
1.3
up to a
\log\log\log n
factor. Note that 2-degenerate graphs are also
k
-degenerate for any
k\geq 3
hence the above result is indeed an upper bound on the funtion of Theorem
1.2
, for any value of
k
. As a consequence, the bounds on the functions of the aforementioned theorems become:

•

for the function
f
of Theorem
1.1
, there are two constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that for every integer
n
,

c_{1}\cdot\frac{\log\log n}{\log\log\log n}\leq f(n)\leq c_{2}\cdot\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n;

•

for the function
f_{k}
of Question
1.3
, there is a constant
c
such that for every integer
n
,

\frac{\log\log n}{\log(k+1)}\leq f_{k}(n)\leq c\cdot\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n.

Our proof of Theorem
1.4
is inspired by
[
DR24
]
but our construction and the arguments are much more involved. Let us give an overview of its different steps.

The first step is the explicit construction of the graph whose existence is posited by Theorem
1.4
. This graph should be 2-degenerate, contain a long path
P
and no long induced paths.
Observe that vertices outside of
P
are not useful to show any of the desired properties. So actually our construction will not contain such vertices: the path
P
will span all the vertices, i.e., it will be a Hamiltonian path.
More precisely, for every integer
\ell
, we construct a
2
-degenerate graph
G
of order
\smash{\Omega\big(2^{2^{\ell}}\big)}
that admits a Hamiltonian path and in which all induced paths have order
O(\ell\log\ell)
.
The different steps of the construction can be sketched as follows (see Figure
1
for an overview):

1.

We start with a complete binary tree on
\smash{2^{2^{\ell}-1}}-1
nodes, and that we call the
skeleton-tree
. This tree is obviously
2
-degenerate, has the desired number of vertices, but there are two drawbacks: it has no Hamiltonian path and its longest induced paths too long (or order
\Theta(2^{\ell}))
.

2.

This skeleton-tree is transformed into a a graph called
ribbed-tree
obtained by adding a number of
barriers
.
Informally, barriers are paths replacing the edges of the skeleton-tree together with a set of edges called
ribs
that connect nodes of such paths to certain well-chosen ancestor nodes in the tree.
The role of these barriers is to block induced paths and they do so without increasing the degeneracy.
Their definition uses an auxiliary tree called
index-tree
and certain walks on this tree.
While it can be shown that the induced paths have now order
O(\ell\log\ell)
as desired (for an appropriate choice of the index-tree), the obtained graph does not have a Hamiltonian path.

3.

Finally, we apply on the ribbed-tree an operation called
blow-up
that results in a graph
G
that contains a Hamiltonian path, remains
2
-degenerate, has
\smash{\Omega(2^{2^{\ell}})}
vertices (hence a path of the same order), and where induced paths have order
{O(\ell\log\ell)}
, as desired.

How do we show that
G
does not have long induced paths? This is the crux of the proof and it relies on the properties of the auxiliary structures mentioned above.
The approach is to define a notion of
rank
of a vertex and to show that when we follow an induced path, the vertices it uses are more and more constrained according to the ranks of the vertices the path has visited so far.
More precisely, the ranks are in bijection with the vertices of the index-tree and we show that, as the path goes along, the ranks of the vertices it visits are further nested in the index-tree, thanks to the barriers.
This results into a bound on the orders of induced paths which roughly equals the maximum size of a barrier. This number depends on the choice of the index-tree.

Finally, the last step of the proof consists in bounding the size of barriers by a function of
\ell
. When the index-tree is a complete binary tree, the barriers have size
O(\ell^{1.6})
.
We show that the index-tree can be chosen so that barriers have size
O(\ell\log\ell)
. This value is in fact the result of a trade-off between the maximum size of a left subtree, and the maximum depth of the tree.
This proves the desired upper bound on the order of induced paths in
G
.

\displaystyle|B_{\alpha}(n)|

\displaystyle=2|B_{\alpha(\alpha-1)}(\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor)|+|B_{\alpha}(m-\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor-1)|+4

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4\cdot\frac{\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\log\big(\alpha(\alpha-1)\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\big)}{\log\big(\alpha(\alpha-1)-1\big)}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq 2}\hskip-2.19073pt+4\cdot\frac{(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\cdot\log\big(\alpha(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\big)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4\cdot\frac{\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\log(\alpha m)}{\log\big((\alpha-1)^{2}+\alpha-2\big)}+4\cdot\frac{(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\cdot\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4\cdot\frac{\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\log(\alpha m)}{\log\big((\alpha-1)^{2}\big)}+4\cdot\frac{(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\cdot\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4\cdot\frac{(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\cdot\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{\log\alpha m}{\log(\alpha-1)}\cdot(\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor+\big(m-\lfloor m/\alpha)\rfloor-1)\big)+4

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}\cdot m=4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}\qed

We conclude this section by showing that the sum of Lemma
4.14
is similarly bounded under the condition that subtrees are properly included.

Lemma 5.4
.

Given an integer
m
and a real
\alpha>2
, let
{\sf T}:=T_{\alpha}(m)
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{p}
be any sequence of indices of
{\sf T}
such that
{\sf T}(k_{i+1})
is a proper subtree of
{\sf T}(k_{i})
for all
i\in\left\{1,\dots,p-1\right\}
.
Then

\sum_{i=1}^{p}|B(k_{i})|\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)\alpha}{\log(\alpha-1)}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m
. The inequality is vacuously true for
m=0
as
{\sf T}
is empty.
Let us first show that the inequality holds for
m=1
.
In that case, note that
{\sf T}
has one node, and hence
p=1
.
By Lemma
5.3
we have
|B(k_{1})|\leq 4\cdot(m\log(\alpha m))/(\log(\alpha-1))
which is less than the desired bound, so the statement holds.

Suppose now that
m>1
and that the statement holds for any
m^{\prime}<m
and
\alpha^{\prime}>2
.
We distinguish cases depending whether
k_{2}
lies in the subtree rooted at the left or right child of the root of
{\sf T}
. In each case we use Lemma
5.3
to bound the size of
B(k_{1})
and the induction hypothesis for the rest of the sum, i.e.,
\sum_{i=2}^{p}|B(k_{i})|
.
When
k_{2}
is in the subtree rooted at the left child of the root of
{\sf T}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}|B(k_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|B(k_{1})|+\sum_{i=2}^{p}|B(k_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+4\cdot\frac{\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\log\big(\alpha(\alpha-1)\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor\big)(\alpha(\alpha-1))}{\log\big(\alpha(\alpha-1)-1\big)}

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\left(\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+\frac{m\log(\alpha m)(\alpha(\alpha-1))}{\alpha\cdot\log(\alpha(\alpha-1)-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\left(\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+\frac{m\log(\alpha m)(\alpha-1)}{\log(\alpha-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}\cdot(1+\alpha-1)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)\alpha}{\log(\alpha-1)}.

Similarly when
k_{2}
lies in the right subtree we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}|B(k_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|B(k_{1})|+\sum_{i=2}^{p}|B(k_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\left(\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+\frac{(m-\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor-1)\log(\alpha\cdot(m-\lfloor m/\alpha\rfloor-1))\alpha}{\log(\alpha-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\left(\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}+\frac{m(1-1/\alpha)\log(\alpha m)\alpha}{\log(\alpha-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)}{\log(\alpha-1)}\cdot(1+(1-1/\alpha)\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{m\log(\alpha m)\alpha}{\log(\alpha-1)}.

Hence in both cases we get the desired bound, and by induction the result holds for every
m
and
\alpha
as claimed.
∎

6
The proof of the main theorem

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.4
, that we restate below for convenience.

See
1.4

Proof.

Let
\ell\geq 3
be some integer. We set
{\sf T}:=T(3,\ell)
,
G:=G({\sf T})
, and
n:=2^{2^{\ell}}
.
By Remark
3.17
,
G
has
\Omega(n)
vertices and by Lemma
3.20
it has a Hamiltonian path, so it has a path of order
\Omega(n)
. By lemma
3.21
,
G
is
2
-degenerate. It remains to show that
G
does not have long induced paths.

Let
Q
be an induced path of
G
and consider a subpath
P
of
Q
of maximum order that starts from a triangle-vertex of largest rank in
Q
and ends with a triangle-vertex.
Note that the order of
Q
is bounded by twice the order of
P
, plus twice the size of
B(\ell)
, the latter part being a bound on the order of the extremities of
Q
before the first triangle-vertex, and after the last.
Indeed, by construction, these extremities must lie in a single barrier each, as they do not contain any triangle-vertex.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{p}
be the sequence of vertices in
{\sf special}\text{-}{\sf seq}(P)
, as defined in Section
4
.
Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{p}
be the indices such that
{\sf T}(k_{i})={\sf range}(v_{i})
for every such
i
.
By Lemma
4.14
we have that
{\sf T}(k_{i+1})
is a proper subtree of
{\sf T}(k_{i})
for every
2\leq i\leq p
, and the total length of
P
is bounded by

13\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}|B(k_{i})|\right)+1\leq 13\cdot\left(|B(\ell)|+\sum_{i=2}^{p}|B(k_{i})|\right)+1.

Applying Lemma
5.3
for the size of
B(\ell)
, and Lemma
5.4
for the right hand sum, with
m=\ell
and
\alpha=3
, the above is upper bounded by
624\ell\log\ell
.

Applying Lemma
5.3
with the same parameters for each of the two extremities of
Q
, we overall get that
Q
has order at most
936\cdot\ell\log\ell=O(\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n)
, as desired.
This concludes the proof.
∎
