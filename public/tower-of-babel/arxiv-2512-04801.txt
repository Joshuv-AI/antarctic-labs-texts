Hybrid VQE-CVQE algorithm using diabatic state preparation
John P. T. Stenger, C. Stephen Hellberg, Daniel Gunlycke

Abstract
We propose a hybrid variational quantum algorithm that has variational parameters used by both the quantum circuit and the subsequent classical optimization. Similar to the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), this algorithm applies a parameterized unitary operator to the qubit register. We generate this operator using diabatic state preparation. The quantum measurement results then inform the classical optimization procedure used by the Cascaded Variational Quantum Eigensolver (CVQE). We demonstrate the algorithm on a system of interacting electrons and show how it can be used on long-term error-corrected as well as short-term intermediate-scale quantum computers. Our simulations performed on IBM Brisbane produced energies well within chemical accuracy.

Introduction

Quantum algorithms that simulate quantum systems can be designed for Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) and Fault-Tolerant Quantum (FTQ) computers. Algorithms that require a large number of quantum logic gates must use FTQ computers. Conversely, algorithms that require relatively few quantum logic gates, can be used on NISQ computers but are not designed for FTQ computers. These two classes of algorithms often function very differently, which may pose a challenge when transitioning from near-term to long-term quantum computing.

II
Method

The basic process for our method. Diabatic state preparation is performed on the quantum computer to generate a guiding state. A collection of state states are measured. These state states form a subspace and this subspace can be optimized classically.

Our goal is to calculate the ground-state energy of a fermionic quantum system described by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
on the Fock space
\mathcal{F}

\ket{\Psi_0}
, (2) measure
\ket{\Psi_0}
and use those measurement results to form a collection of basis states
\mathcal{B}
, and (3) search for the ground state of
\hat{H}
on the Hilbert subspace
\mathcal{V}
spanned by
\mathcal{B}
. This method is depicted in Fig.
1
.

(1)

In the limit
N_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty

C

The set

\mathcal{V}=\text{span}(\mathcal{B})

(3)

(N_{\tau},\Delta\tau)

to improve the solution, lowering

III
Demonstration

III.1
Model

We demonstrate the algorithm for a spinless electronic toy model with orbitals that are equally spaced in energy and with 1-electron excitations and 2-electron interactions restricted to nearest-energy orbitals. The Hamiltonian for this model is

\begin{split}\hat{H}=&\Delta\mu\sum_{q=0}^{Q-1}q\penalty\ \hat{n}_{q}-t\sum_{q=0}^{Q-2}\big(c^{\dagger}_{q}c_{q+1}+c^{\dagger}_{q+1}c_{q}\big)\\
&+V\sum_{q=0}^{Q-2}\hat{n}_{q}\hat{n}_{q+1},\end{split}

(4)

where
c^{\dagger}_{q}
and
c_{q}
are creation and destruction operators acting on orbital q,
\hat{n}_{q}=c^{\dagger}_{q}c_{q}
is the number operator,
\Delta\mu

is the electron-electron interaction strength. In order to build the guiding state we define the initial Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{0}=\Delta\mu\sum_{q=0}^{Q-1}q\penalty\ \hat{n}_{q}.

(5)

The eigenstates of
H_{0}
can be conceptualized as electrons filling the energy levels

We initialize the quantum register to the eigenstate of

that has

We use the Jordan–Wigner transformation

B
. From these gates we obtain the quantum circuits that generate the guiding states.

IV
Conclusion

We have presented a hybrid VQE-CVQE algorithm that use diabatic state preparation to generate the quantum variational guiding state. The quantum register is measured and the measurements are processed to form a collection of basis states, which are then used to form a subspace on which we represent the system Hamiltonian. The lowest eigenvalue of this Hamiltonian is then calculated using a classical computer.

We find that this hybrid algorithm performs better for NISQ computers than either VQE or CVQE on their own. We have described three regimes for which the optimal implementation of the algorithm changes. Although we demonstrate the presence of these regimes only for our toy model, we expect that these regimes are present for many physically relevant models. For the quantum computers that are currently available, minimizing circuit is the most critical consideration, thus
N_{\tau}=1
gives the best results for large systems. In this case, there is no need to update the variational parameters.

In the near term, allowing the variational parameters to update could help optimize the energy. In the medium term, the energy can optimized using CVQE without changing the variational parameters. Eventually, when we have FTQ computers, we can do adiabatic state preparation directly without the need for any additional optimization. Our generalized method is effective in all of these regimes.

Appendix A
Diabatic State Preparation

We need a
\hat{U}
that evolves the state of the quantum register towards the ground state of
\hat{H}
so that
\mathcal{B}
contains the necessary states to build a good approximation of the ground state. If we had access to an error-free quantum computer, we could perform adiabatic state preparation

\hat{U}_{A}(\tau)=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{\tau}\text{d}\tau_{1}\hat{H}(\tau_{1})}.

(6)

where

\hat{H}(\tau)=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{T}\right)\hat{H}_{0}+\frac{\tau}{T}\hat{H},

(7)

where
H_{0}
is trivial to diagonalize and
T>>1/|\hat{H}|
is a long time range.

To apply this operator on a quantum computer we have to discretize time as in Eq. (
1
) of the main text where
T=\Delta\tau N_{\tau}
. In the limit
N_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty
and
\tau=T
we recapture the evolution operator
\hat{U}(N_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty,\Delta\tau=T/N_{\tau})=\hat{U}_{A}(T)
. By initializing the quantum computer to an eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{0}
and applying
\hat{U}_{A}(T)
, we are guaranteed to prepare the exact ground state of
\hat{H}
when
T\rightarrow\infty
, assuming the spectrum of
\hat{H}(\tau)
is gaped throughout. However, fast error rates prevent us from approaching the
N_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty
limit.

Figure 5:
Eigenstates for an example
H_{0}
.
The spheres represent electrons. The lines represent energy levels. On the left, the lowest energy levels are filled with electrons. In the second from left, one excitation has occurred. The two right most graphs show two excitations.

Fortunately, for our purposes, we only need to reach an approximation of the correct subspace, not of the correct eigenstate itself. Figure
5
shows energy levels filled with four electrons in different excitation configurations. We expect that the ground state should be a linear combination of the lower-order excitations. These lowest order excitation are well captured by diabatic state preparation. Let us compare a single time step
N_{\tau}=1
to the full adiabatic evolution

\begin{split}\hat{U}(1,T)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}T}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-iT)^{n}\Pi_{n-m,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1}),\\
\hat{U}_{A}(T)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{0}T}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-iT)^{n}\tilde{\Pi}_{n-m,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1}),\end{split}

(8)

where
\hat{H}_{1}=\hat{H}-\hat{H}_{0}
is the non-trivial part of
\hat{H}
and both
\Pi_{n,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})
and
\tilde{\Pi}_{n,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})
are sums over all permutations of operator strings with a number of
\hat{H}_{0}
operators equal to
n
and a number of
\hat{H}_{1}
operators equal to
m
but with different weights. For
\Pi_{n,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})
, all operators have the weight
w=1/(n+m)!
. For example

\Pi_{2,1}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})=\frac{1}{3!}(\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}).

(9)

For
\tilde{\Pi}_{n,m}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})
, the weights depend on the position of the
\hat{H}_{1}
operators in the operator string. In general, the weight is

\bar{w}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3})\ldots},

(10)

where
\gamma_{j}=2
if the operator
j
positions from the left is
\hat{H}_{1}
and
\gamma_{j}=1
otherwise (a derivation for this expression is found below in
A.1
). For example,

\tilde{\Pi}_{2,1}(\hat{H}_{0},\hat{H}_{1})=\frac{1}{24}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}+\frac{1}{12}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}+\frac{1}{8}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}.

(11)

We see that both
\hat{U}(1,T)
and
\hat{U}_{A}(T)
contain the exact same operators with the general rule that the longer the operator string, the smaller the weight. The difference is that
\hat{U}_{A}(T)
turns on
\hat{H}_{1}
slowly and so the operator strings with
\hat{H}_{1}
towards the beginning (towards the right) are less relevant.

We are only interested in the subspace that contains the ground state and not on the specific weights of each state. Therefore, we expect that
\hat{U}(1,T)
is often sufficient for our purposes as it produces the same excitations as
\hat{U}_{A}(T)
with a similar ordering of the wieghts.

A.1
Weights in the evolution operator

The full-adiabatic evolution operator can be written as a sum over integrals

\hat{U}_{A}(T)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}\hat{I}_{n}(T),

(12)

where each integral is

\hat{I}_{n}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\text{d}\tau_{1}\hat{H}(\tau_{1})I_{n-1}(\tau_{1}),

(13)

with
\hat{I}_{0}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{1}
. Let us perform the first few integrals and then the pattern for the weights becomes clear

\begin{split}\hat{I}_{1}(\tau)=&\frac{1}{1}\hat{H}_{0}\tau+\frac{1}{2}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{2}}{T},\\
\hat{I}_{2}(\tau)=&\frac{1}{2\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\tau^{2}+\frac{1}{3\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{3}}{T},\\
+&\frac{1}{3\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\frac{\tau^{3}}{T}+\frac{1}{4\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{4}}{T^{2}},\\
\hat{I}_{3}(\tau)=&\frac{1}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\tau^{3}+\frac{1}{4\cdot 3\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{4}}{T},\\
+&\frac{1}{4\cdot 3\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\frac{\tau^{4}}{T}+\frac{1}{5\cdot 4\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{5}}{T^{2}},\\
+&\frac{1}{4\cdot 2\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{0}\frac{\tau^{4}}{T}+\frac{1}{5\cdot 3\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{5}}{T^{2}},\\
+&\frac{1}{5\cdot 3\cdot 1}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{0}\frac{\tau^{5}}{T^{2}}+\frac{1}{6\cdot 4\cdot 2}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{1}\hat{H}_{1}\frac{\tau^{6}}{T^{3}}.\end{split}

(14)

We see that the weights are coming from integration over
\tau
. Each integration increases the power of
\tau
by one but also each
\hat{H}_{1}
increases the power of
\tau
by one. Therefore, we arrive at the expression for the weight in Eq. (
10
).

Quantum Gates To Build The Diabatic State Preparation Unitary

The unitary operator
\hat{U}(N_{\tau},\Delta\tau)
can be decomposed into quantum gates. First, we use the JW transformation and the Trotter–Suzuki decomposition to write the operator as a product of exponentials of Pauli strings. Let
\sigma^{a}_{q}
be the Pauli-a operator acting on qubit
q
and let
R^{a}_{q}(\phi)
be the rotation around the a-axis of the block sphere for qubit
q
. A general exponential of Pauli strings can be decomposed into quantum gates in the following way:

•

On the last qubit with a non-identity Pauli string, insert an
R^{z}_{q}(\phi)
gate with an angle equal to the weight of the Pauli string.

•

Sandwich the
R^{z}_{q}(\phi)
gate between two chains of CNOT gates that connect all of the qubits with non-identity Pauli operators.

•

For each qubit with a
\sigma^{x}_{q}
Pauli operator, sandwich the circuit with an
R^{y}_{q}(-\pi/2)
to the left and an
R^{y}_{q}(\pi/2)
to the right.

•

For each qubit with a
\sigma^{y}_{q}
Pauli operator, sandwich the circuit with an
R^{x}_{q}(\pi/2)
to the left and an
R^{x}_{q}(-\pi/2)
to the right.

shows an example of a circuit to create the exponential of the Pauli string
\sigma^{x}_{0}\sigma^{z}_{1}\sigma^{y}_{3}
.

Appendix C
Collecting Shots

We describe two methods for collecting the shots from the quantum computer and, subsequently, using those shots to create a subspace. In the first method we divide the Hamiltonian into a sum of Pauli strings and then
collect shots in the diagonalized basis of each Pauli string. This first method is the standard for calculating the expectation values of Hamiltonians on a quantum computer. In the second method, we collect the shots only in the original basis of the quantum computer. We use the second method in the main text of the manuscript. We show that the two methods are equivalent for k-local Hamiltonians.

For the first method, we partition the Hamiltonian into a sum of
L
Pauli strings

\hat{H}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{P}_{l},

(15)

where each
\hat{P}_{l}
is a Pauli string. Each
\hat{P}_{l}
has an associated rotation operator
\hat{R}_{l}
, which diagonalizes
\hat{P}_{l}

\hat{R}_{l}\hat{P}_{l}\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{l}=\sum_{n}E_{nl}\outerproduct{n}{n},

(16)

where
E_{nl}
are the eigenvalues of
\hat{P}_{l}
.

Let the quantum computer be in a state
\ket{\Psi_0}
. In this first method, we apply each rotation to the quantum state before measurement. After a rotation
\hat{R}_{l}
the amplitude of the basis state
\ket{n}
is

\tilde{A}_{nl}=\bra{n}\hat{R}_{l}\ket{\Psi_0}.

(17)

We refer to
\{n_{l}\}=\{\hat{R}_{l}\ket{n}\}
as a rotated basis set and
\{\ket{n}\}
as the unrotated basis.
Let us imagine that we find a shot in state
\ket{n_l}
if
|\tilde{A}_{nl}|>\epsilon
for some cutoff threshold
\epsilon
. Then the set of states that we obtain from the quantum computer are

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0l}=\{\ket{n_l}:|\tilde{A}_{nl}|>\epsilon\}.

(18)

However, we need to form a single basis using all of the collected measurement. Thus, we form the set of all unrotated basis states that might have contributed to each measured rotated basis state

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{l}=\{\ket{m}:r^{l}_{mn}\neq 0\text{ and }|\tilde{A}_{nl}|>\epsilon\},

(19)

where
r^{l}_{mn}=\bra{m}\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{l}\ket{n_l}
. The total set of states is the union of all
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{l}

\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\bigcup_{l}\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{l}.

(20)

Note that if we are simply calculating expectation values as in VQE then we do not have to form
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. However,
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is necessary to perform the CVQE step. Note also that the lowest eigenvalue in this basis
E_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
is less than or equal to the energy expectation value calculated from the rotated measurement basis. Therefore, hybrid VQE-CVQE finds energies that are at least as accurate as VQE on its own. This completes our description of the first method.

In the second method we do not perform any rotations before the measurement. Therefore, the amplitude of the basis state
\ket{n}
is simply

A_{n}=\innerproduct{n}{\Psi}.

(21)

We form the initial set of states from this amplitude

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{\ket{n}:|A_{n}|>\epsilon\},

(22)

where we have again assumed that we collect shots in all states with amplitudes bigger than
\epsilon
. Using this initial set alone, we are likely to miss important properties of the Hamiltonian. Therefore, we find the full set of states by hitting the initial set with the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{B}=\{\ket{m}:h_{mn}\neq 0\text{ and }|A_{n}|>\epsilon\},

(23)

where
h_{nm}=\bra{n}\hat{H}\ket{m}
and
\hat{H}
is defined such that
h_{nn}\neq 0
for all
n
. This completes our description of the second method. Next, we compare the two methods.

It is difficult to compare the two sets
\mathcal{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
as they are written. To help make the comparison, let us define the total amplitudes
\bar{A}_{n}
and
A_{n}
such that if
\bar{A}_{n}\neq 0
then
\ket{n}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
and if
A_{n}\neq 0
then
\ket{n}\in\mathcal{B}
.
For the first method we have

\bar{A}_{n}=\sum_{l}\sum_{m}r^{l}_{mn}(\tilde{A}_{ml})|_{>\epsilon},

(24)

gor the second method we have

A_{n}=\sum_{m}h_{nm}(A_{m})|_{>\epsilon},

(25)

where we use the notation

(B)|_{>\epsilon}=\begin{cases}B&\text{if }B>\epsilon\\
0&\text{if }B<\epsilon\end{cases}.

(26)

We can better compare these two expressions if we use the identities

\tilde{A}_{al}=\sum_{m}(r^{l}_{ma})^{*}\innerproduct{m}{\Psi},

(27)

and

h_{nm}=\sum_{l}\sum_{a}r^{l}_{na}(r^{l}_{ma})^{*}E_{al}.

(28)

Plugging in these two identities, we have

\bar{A}_{n}=\sum_{l}\sum_{a}r^{l}_{na}\left[\sum_{m}(r^{l}_{ma})^{*}\innerproduct{m}{\Psi}\right]\Bigg|_{>\epsilon},

(29)

and

A_{n}=\sum_{l}\sum_{a}E_{al}r^{l}_{na}\sum_{m}(r^{l}_{ma})^{*}\big(\innerproduct{m}{\Psi}\big)|_{>\epsilon}.

(30)

The value of
E_{al}
is insignificant as we are only concerned with whether or not the probabilities are non-zero.
The important difference is the evaluation of the cutoff. In the first method, the cutoff is evaluated after the summation over
m
. In the second method, the cutoff is evaluated before the summation over
m
. The effect of the cutoff, therefore, depends on how many non-zero values
r^{l}_{ma}
there are for a given
l
and
a
. For a Pauli string that contains
\chi_{l}
Pauli-X and Pauli-Y operators, there are
N_{r}=2^{\chi_{l}}
non-zero
r^{l}_{ma}
terms.

We can think of the second method as effectively increasing the cuttoff (i.e.
\epsilon\rightarrow N_{r}\epsilon
). This can be compensated for by increasing the number of shots. If we take
N_{s}
times the number of measurements then
\epsilon\rightarrow\epsilon/N_{s}
. For fermionic Hamiltonians
\chi_{l}\leq 4
. Thus,
N_{r}\leq 16
. Therefore, we need only take sixteen times the number of shots for the second method to achieve the same accuracy as the first method. Furthermore, the first method requires
L
times as many circuits to achieve this increased accuracy. Even for simple molecules, we can have
L\approx 1000
. So it is often much more efficient to use the second method. We use the second method throughout the main text.

Appendix D
Overview of CVQE

In the CVQE algorithm, one uses an ansatz of the form

\ket{\bar\Psi(\theta)}=e^{i\hat{\lambda}(\theta)}\hat{U}\ket{\Phi_0},

(31)

where
\hat{\lambda}
is an operator,
\theta
is a collection of parameters,
\hat{U}
is a unitary operator, and
\ket{\Phi_0}
is some initialization state. The state

\ket{\Psi_0}=\hat{U}\ket{\Phi_0},

(32)

is simulated on a quantum computer. We calculate the energy expectation value

E=\frac{\Upsilon(\theta)}{\Lambda(\theta)},

(33)

where

\begin{split}&\Upsilon(\theta)=\bra{\Psi_0}e^{-i\lambda^{*}(\theta)}\hat{H}e^{i\lambda(\theta)}\ket{\Psi_0},\\
&\Lambda(\theta)=\bra{\Psi_0}e^{-i\lambda^{*}(\theta)}e^{i\lambda(\theta)}\ket{\Psi_0},\end{split}

(34)

in terms of the measured probability distributions

|\tilde{A}_{nl}|^{2}=|\bra{\Psi}\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{l}\ket{n}|^{2},

(35)

for various rotations
\hat{R}_{l}
. An important insight of CVQE is that the number of required
\hat{R}_{l}
to calculate
\Upsilon(\theta)
and
\Lambda(\theta)
is the same as the number required to measure an expectation value of
\hat{H}
, as long as
\hat{\lambda}(\theta)
is diagonal in the
\ket{n}
basis.

D.1
Comparison between CVQE and our method

The state created on the quantum computer in CVQE
\ket{\Psi_0}
acts as our guiding state for particular values of
N_{\tau}
and
\Delta\tau
. Instead of calculating
\Upsilon
and
\Lambda
from the probability distributions (
35
), we project the Hamiltonian onto a subspace. However, the final ansatz is the same in both CVQE and our method. Let
u_{n0}=\bra{n}\hat{U}\ket{0}
, then the guiding state is

\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}u_{n0}\ket{n}.

(36)

Applying the second piece of the CVQE ansatz gives

\ket{ \bar\Psi(\theta)}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}e^{i\hat{\lambda}(\theta)}u_{n0}\ket{n}.

(37)

If we choose
\hat{\lambda}(\theta)
such that
\hat{\lambda}(\theta)
is diagonal and
\bra{n}e^{i\hat{\lambda}(\theta)}\ket{n}=0
if
\ket{n}\notin\mathcal{B}
and we redefine our variational parameter as
\phi_{n}=\bra{n}e^{i\hat{\lambda}(\theta)}\ket{n}u_{n0}
then the state is

\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}=\sum_{n\in B}\phi_{n}\ket{n},

(38)

which covers the state subspace
\mathcal{V}
. Thus, for a set values (
N_{\tau}
,
\Delta\tau
), our method is CVQE with the specific ansatz. However, if either
N_{\tau}
or
\tau
are allowed to vary then our method can be thought of as a hybrid VQE-CVQE method.

D.2
Effective Hamiltonian

We can optimize
\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}
variationally, however, we can find the exact minimum from diagonalizing an effective Hailtonian instead. Let use define an effective Hamiltonian on the subspace as in Eq. (
3
) of the main text.
As the size of the basis
\mathcal{B}
is subexponential with the number of qubits, we can use a classical computer to diagonalize the effective Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathcal{B}}\ket{E_{\mathcal B n}}=E_{\mathcal{B}n}\ket{E_{\mathcal B n}},

(39)

where
\ket{E_{\mathcal B n}}
are the eigenstates and
E_{\mathcal{B}n}
are the eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{\mathcal{B}}
.

We can show that the ground state
E_{\mathcal{B}}\equiv E_{\mathcal{B}0}
is the minimum possible expectation value of
\hat{H}
with
\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}
. We know that
\ket{E_{\mathcal B n}}
forms a complete basis of the subspace
\mathcal{V}=\text{span}(\mathcal{B})
, therefore, we can write the state as

\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}=\sum_{n}\bar{\psi}_{n}(\phi)\ket{\bar E_n},

(40)

where
\bar{\psi}_{n}(\phi)=\bra{\bar E_n}\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}
are complex coefficients. Thus, the variational principle applies

\bra{\bar\Psi(\phi)}\hat{H}_{\mathcal{B}}\ket{ \bar\Psi(\phi)}=\sum_{n}|\bar{\psi}_{n}(\phi)|^{2}\bar{E}_{n}\geq\bar{E}_{0}.

(41)

Furthermore, the expectation value of
\hat{H}
with
\ket{\bar\Psi(\phi)}
is the same as that of
\hat{H}_{\mathcal{B}}

\begin{split}E_{0}\leq&\bra{\bar\Psi(\phi) }\hat{H}_{\mathcal{B}}\ket{ \bar\Psi(\phi)}\\
=&\sum_{nm\in B}\phi^{*}_{n}\phi_{m}\bra{n}\Big(\sum_{pq\in B}\bra{p}\hat{H}\ket{q}\ket{p}\!\bra{q}\Big)\ket{m}\\
=&\sum_{nm\in B}\phi^{*}_{s}\phi_{m}\bra{n}\hat{H}\ket{m}\\
=&\bra{\bar\Psi(\phi) }\hat{H}\ket{ \bar\Psi(\phi)}.\end{split}

(42)

Appendix E
Approximating an expectation value using a subspace

Anytime we use a quantum computer to calculate the expectation value of an operator, we are using the subspace defined by the shots retrieved from the quantum computer. Therefore, any issues involving approximating the expectation value from a subspace is an inherent issue with quantum computing and not a fault of our method. However, the question still remains, is it appropriate to calculate an expectation value from a subspace?

The answer depends on the state in question. We show that if the state is an eigenstate of the operator then the subspace defined by the basis states with the highest weights gives a good approximation of the expectation value. Because the variational principle guarantees that the minimum expectation value corresponds to an eigenstate, and because we are searching for the minimum expectation value, we can be confident that our final result is a good approximation of the true expectation value. However, it should be noted that the expectation values of other states may not be good approximations.

To show that expectation values of eigenstates are well approximated using a subspace, let us define some helpful expressions. Let
\hat{P}_{l}
be terms in the Hamiltonian as in Eq (
15
).
We write the eigenstates of each term of the Hamiltonian as
\Psi_{nl}

\hat{P}_{l}\ket{\Psi_{nl}}=E_{nl}\ket{\Psi_{nl}},

(43)

where
E_{nl}
is an eigenvalue of
\hat{P}_{l}
. We can expand a general ansatz state in terms of any one of these sets of bases states

\ket{\Psi(\phi)}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\phi_{nl}\ket{\Psi_{nl}},

(44)

for any
l
, where
\phi_{nl}
is a complex coefficient.
We can then define the energy expectation value in terms of
E_{nl}
and
\phi_{nl}

E(\phi)=\sum_{l}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}E_{nl}|\phi_{nl}|^{2}.

(45)

When we calculate the energy expectation value from the quantum computer we have to use the shots

\bar{E}(\phi)=\sum_{l}\sum_{n\in\tilde{B}_{l}}E_{nl}|\bar{\phi}_{nl}|^{2},

(46)

where
|\bar{\phi}_{nl}|^{2}
is the normalized number of shots measured in
\ket{n_l}
given that
\hat{R}_{l}
is applied to the quantum state before measurement.

While we are guaranteed that
|\bar{\phi}_{nl}|^{2}\approx|\phi_{nl}|^{2}
, there can be situations where
\bar{E}(\phi)\not\approx E(\phi)
. Take for example, the case were
E_{00}
is the only non-zero eigenvalue but
\phi_{nl}
is evenly spread. In this case, the energy expectation value from the quantum computer most likely returns
0
while the true expectation value is
E_{00}|\phi_{00}|^{2}
. Thus, not all energy expectation values are well approximated using a quantum computer. However, if
\ket{\Psi(\phi^*)}
is an eigenstate of
\hat{H}
then there is a relationship between
E_{nl}
and
\phi_{nl}
. We show that this relationship protects the expectation value of eigenstates.

The intuition is that the more a state contributes to the total energy expectation value, the greater its weight in the eigenstate. Thus, we calculate the contributions
\bra{\Psi_{nl}}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi(\phi^*)}
in two ways. Firstly, we break the Hamiltonian up into its terms

\bra{\Psi_{nl}}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi(\phi^*)}=\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\zeta^{nl}_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}},

(47)

where
\zeta^{nl}_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}=\bra{\Psi_{nl}}\ket{\Psi_{n'l'}}
. We have that
\zeta^{nl}_{n^{\prime}l}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}
, however, the basis states for different
l
are not necessarily orthogonal.
Secondly, we use the fact that
\ket{\Psi(\phi)}
is an eigenstate

\bra{\Psi_{nl}}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi(\phi^*)}=E(\phi^{*})\phi_{nl}.

(48)

setting Eq .(
47
) and Eq. (
48
) equal we have

\phi_{nl}=\frac{\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\zeta_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{nl}}{E(\phi)-E_{nl}}.

(49)

Thus, the number of shots that are measured in a state
\ket{n_l}
during the measurement of
\hat{R}_{l}\ket{\Psi(\phi^*)}
is proportional to

|\phi_{nl}|^{2}\propto\frac{1}{|E(\phi^{*})-E_{nl}|^{2}}.

(50)

This means that we only drop states with energies that are far from the true expectation value and, therefore, we get a good approximation of the true expectation value.

To be more explicit, for Eq. (
46
) to fail, there are two conditions. Firstly, the absolute value of the weight
\phi_{nl}
must be small enough that we do not find it in any shot

|\phi_{nl}|\ll 1.

(51)

Second, the value of
E_{nl}
must be large enough that its contribution to the sum is substantial

\left|E_{nl}|\phi_{nl}|^{2}\right|\sim|E(\phi^{*})|,

(52)

or equivalently

\left|E_{nl}|\phi_{nl}|^{2}\right|\gtrsim\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|^{2}\right|.

(53)

For both Eq. (
51
) and Eq.(
52
) to hold we must have that
|E_{nl}|\gg|E(\phi^{*})|
. Using
|E_{nl}|\gg|E(\phi^{*})|
and Eq. (
49
), we can rewrite Eq. (
53
) as

|\phi_{nl}|\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\zeta_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{nl}\right|\gtrsim\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|^{2}\right|.

(54)

Using Eq. (
51
) and the fact that
|\zeta_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{nl}|\leq 1

\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\right|\gg\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}E_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}}|^{2}\right|.

(55)

For Eq. (
55
) to hold, each of the
\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}\ll 1
. If any
\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}\sim 1
then those terms dominate both sides of the inequality and the inequality no longer holds. However, normalization enforces

\sum_{n}|\phi_{nl}|^{2}=1,

(56)

for all
l
. Thus, if
\phi_{n^{\prime}l^{\prime}\neq nl}\ll 1
then
|\phi_{nl}|\sim 1
and Eq.
51
does not hold. Therefore, if
\ket{\Psi(\phi)}
is an eigenstate then Eq.
46
is a good approximation of the energy expectation value.
