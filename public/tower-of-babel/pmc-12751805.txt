Diagnostic accuracy of panoramic radiography in detecting styloid process elongation: a cone-beam computed tomography referenced retrospective study
Authors: Gökçen Akçiçek, Dilara Kara, Serdar Uysal, Hatice Yağmur Zengin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the reliability of panoramic imaging in assessing styloid process (SP), using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) as the reference standard. CBCT is preferred for assessing SP as it displays the dimensions and morphology of the SP without superimpositions.MethodsSP elongation, calcification type (type 1: elongated, type 2: pseudoarticulated, type 3: segmented, and type 4: distant), and calcification pattern (calcified outline, partially calcified, nodular, and completely calcified) were evaluated on both panoramic and CBCT images of the same 407 patients. All measurements were recorded for the left and right sides independently. The efficacy of panoramic imaging in assessing SP was analysed through sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, accompanied by their 95% confidence intervals, with a significance threshold of about 0.05 for all studies.ResultsThere were 113 (27.8%) male and 294 (72.2%) female, for a total of 407 patients with a median age of 43 years (18–69). The prevalence of elongated SP was 43.5% in panoramic images, whereas in CBCT images, it was 52.6%. When compared to CBCT imaging, panoramic imaging exhibited good specificity, with values of 81.2% on the right side and 83.7% on the left side, but demonstrated low sensitivity, recording 67.3% on the right side and 64.4% on the left side.ConclusionsAlmost one-third of the elongated SP cases were detected as false negative in panoramic images. Considering these findings, it is advisable to assess the region using CBCT in situations where SP examination is critical.

Background

The styloid process (SP) is a component of the temporal bone, situated anterior to the mastoid foramen and posterior to the tympanic plate, extending from the inferior surface of the temporal bone in an antero-inferior direction [1]. It derives from the second branchial arch within Reichert’s cartilage, begings to ossify between the ages of 5 and 8 years, and continues to elongate until approximately 30 years of age [2, 3]. SP is positioned laterally in the neck and closely related to the internal and external carotid arteries, jugular vein, glossopharyngeal, facial, accessory, hypoglossal, and vagus nerves. The stylohyoid, stylopharyngeus, and styloglossus muscles originate from the distal part of SP. In addition, the stylomandibular ligament, which functions to limit the maximum opening and protrusion of the mandible, is positioned between the SP and the ramus of the mandible, and the stylohyoid ligament, which functions to elevate the hyoid bone, is positioned between the SP and the lesser horn of the hyoid bone [4]. The stylohyoid complex comprises the styloid process, the stylohyoid ligament, and the lesser cornu of the hyoid bone [5].

SPs longer than 30 mm are considered elongated, and it has been suggested that the stylohyoid ligament calcification refers to elongated SP [5, 6]. The elongated SP typically manifests as a narrow bony protrusion, extending from the temporal bone and potentially continuing towards the lesser cornu of the hyoid bone. Depending on the extent of the calcification, it could be visualized as smooth and well-corticated or bulky with irregular contours, with uninterrupted integrity or segmented [1, 7, 8]. While the majority of elongated SPs are asymptomatic, 4–10% exhibit symptoms of Eagle’s syndrome or styloid syndrome, such as pharyngeal pain in various cervicofacial areas, such as headache, cervicalgia, otalgia, trigeminal neuralgia, periorbital pain, orbital pain, throat pain, and a feeling of a foreign object in the throat; particularly during mouth opening, swallowing, and head rotation, tinnitus, articular clicking, and altered hearing like hyperacusis [1, 2, 4, 9–12].

SP was evaluated with different imaging techniques, including panoramic imaging [2, 4, 8, 13–17], ultrasonography (US) [18], computed tomography (CT) [6, 19–21], and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) [22–26]. Panoramic imaging is preferred by many dentists as the first imaging modality, as it provides examination of the maxilla, mandible, and surrounding anatomical structures with a rationally low radiation dose, cost-effectiveness, and ease of use [27]. Although there are numerous studies that used panoramic imaging for SP evaluation, superimpositions, magnification, the positioning, and original jawbone morphology, as well as different calcification degrees, could cause difficulties. CBCT presents definitive measurements of the osseous anatomical structures of the craniofacial region with excellent image quality with a reduced radiation dosage and shorter examination duration compared to CT [28], while it entails a higher radiation dose than panoramic imaging [29]. In our preliminary investigation, we observed a moderate agreement (58.4%) between panoramic imaging and CBCT in the assessment of SP [30]. The current study aims to examine the reliability of panoramic imaging in assessing SP, utilizing CBCT as the reference standard. CBCT is preferred for assessing SP, as it displays the dimensions and morphology of the SP without superimpositions [31, 32].

Methods

In this retrospective study, a total of 3733 CBCT images of the patients aged 18 and above obtained for various causes between January 2017 and December 2019 were evaluated. A sample size of at least 162 achieves 90% power to detect a Kappa of 0.85 (α = 0.05) for agreement between two methods (panoramic, CBCT) with the following prior pilot study marginal classification frequencies (0.85, 0.15) and K0 = 0.60. The calculations were carried out using NCSS PASS software. Therefore, the study comprised 407 participants (113 males and 294 females) aged 18 to 69 who received both panoramic and CBCT imaging during the same year. In this study, images, both panoramic and CBCT, with inadequate quality (artifacts, positioning errors, low resolution due to patient movement during imaging), images where SP cannot be displayed completely; and patients with developmental disorders, disease, trauma, and/or surgical intervention history in the area that has to be investigated were excluded. The Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Board of Hacettepe University reviewed and approved the study protocol (Registration No: GO 21/1189).

The CBCT scans were obtained using an I-CAT Next Generation device (Imaging Sciences International, Hatfield, PA, USA). Digital panoramic images were acquired with Orthophos XG 5 (Sirona Dental Company, Bensheim, Germany) or Veraview IC5 (Morita Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) apparatus.

In panoramic images, the SP was classified as elongated if it was situated inferior to the mandibular foramen [1, 15]. Subsequently, the type of elongation and the patterns of calcification were documented. For elongation type, modified Langlais’s [7] classification was used. The Langlais classification distinguishes three types:Type 1 = Elongated; a continuous integrity of the SP,Type 2 = Pseudoarticulated; the SP appears to be connected to the mineralized stylomandibular or stylohyoid ligament by a singular pseudoarticulation,Type 3 = Segmented; comprises either brief or extended non-contiguous sections of the SP or disrupted segments of mineralized ligament.

Alongside the Langlais classification [7], Reddy et al. [33] introduced a fourth category, designated as type 4 = Distant, characterized by extension of the SP due to remote ossification. In this study, SP calcification was recorded according to these four types, and in the absence of SP, it is coded as type 0 (Fig. 1).

The SP calcification pattern was categorized as follows: -calcified outline: a slender radiopaque margin surrounding a core radiolucency, -partially calcified: a denser radiopaque perimeter and nearly total opacification, with tiny, occasionally discontinuous radiolucent centres, -nodular: characterized by a knobby or scalloped contour, exhibiting partial or complete calcification, -completely calcified: entirely radiopaque, devoid of any radiolucent interior (Fig. 2) [7]. All measurements and classifications were recorded for the left and right sides independently (Fig. 3).

We evaluated SPs on CBCT images utilizing the volume rendering functionality of Invivo5 Software (Version 5.2, Anatomage Dental, San Jose, CA, USA). SP length was measured from the point where it emerges from the temporal bone to the tip, including the interrupted non-ossified parts [6] (Fig. 4). In addition, elongation types and calcification patterns were recorded on volume-rendered 3D images. The SP elongation types (Type 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4) and calcification patterns (calcified outline, partially calcified, nodular, and entirely calcified) were documented independently for the left and right sides. One oral and maxillofacial radiologist with 21 years of experience and one well-trained oral and maxillofacial radiology doctoral student with 2 years of experience examined 61 randomly selected panoramic and CBCT images and performed the measurements, representing 15% of the research population. The images were reassessed by the same observers one month after the initial review. Interobserver and intra-observer agreements were assessed by the Kappa test, and the Kappa coefficient was interpreted as < 0.20 poor, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 good, and > 0.80 very good [34]. The agreement between two observers was good to very good (65.1–91.6) for categorical measurements, while the agreement was very good for CBCT SP length measurement on both sides (92.7 for the right side and 86.8 for the left side). Hence, for further statistical analysis, the assessments of only one of the observers (DK) were used. Correspondingly, the concordance between the initial and subsequent measurements by the same observer was rated as good to very good (71.9–100.0) across all categories.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were represented using frequency and percentage (n, %), while numerical variables were summarized using the median (P25-P75). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized to assess the normality of numerical data. The Kappa coefficient was employed to assess both intra- and inter-rater agreement for categorical variables. The intra-class correlation coefficient, utilizing a two-way mixed effects model with consistency in a single measurement formula designated as ICC (3,1)c, is employed to assess agreement in numerical measures, such as length.

Statistical analysis was conducted utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows v23.0 (Released 2015, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Confidence intervals were calculated using Python 3.11.8 with the statsmodels, numy and scipy packages, employing the Wilson score interval method for proportions.

The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to assess the difference between two independent groups due to the violation of parametric test assumptions. The relationship between categorical variables was evaluated using the Pearson Chi-square test, contingent upon the fulfilment of the test’s prerequisites. Otherwise, the Fisher-Freeman-Halton Exact test was employed. CBCT is regarded as the gold standard for assessing bone structure. The efficacy of panoramic imaging for assessing SP was analysed through sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, along with their 95% confidence intervals. The significance level was established at α = 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

There were 113 (27.8%) male and 294 (72.2%) female, for a total of 407 patients with a median age of 43 years (18–69). The prevalence of elongated SP was 43.5% in panoramic images, whereas in CBCT images, it was 52.6%. In panoramic images, 230 (56.5%) patients had normal, 39 (9.6%) had unilateral, and 138 (33.9%) had bilateral elongated SP. In CBCT images, 193 (47.4%) patients had normal, 81 (19.9%) had unilateral, and 133 (32.7%) had bilateral elongated SP.

Table 1 displays the characteristics of right- and left-sided SP in panoramic and CBCT images. On both panoramic and CBCT images, elongated (type 1) calcification was the most common type, followed by pseudoarticulated (type 2). The most common calcification pattern was the same for panoramic and CBCT images as partially calcified. While a calcified outline follows in panoramic images, completely calcified was the second most common calcification pattern on CBCT images (Table 1). There was no significant relationship between gender and SP elongation and calcification type except between gender and calcification pattern in panoramic images (right side p = 0.014, left side p < 0.001). The partially calcified (type 2) calcification pattern was more common in males.

The median length of SPs in CBCT images was 29.97 mm for the right side with an interquartile range (the range between the 25th percentile and 75th percentile) of 10.17 mm and 30.56 mm for the left side with an interquartile range of 9.68 mm. There was no significant relationship between age and SP length on either the right side (p = 0.352) or the left side (p = 0.171). Similarly, there was no significant relationship between gender and SP length (right side p = 0.276, left side p = 0.569).

Comparing the two imaging methods and accepting CBCT as the gold standard reveals that 193 individuals had non-elongated SP, with 79.3% correctly classified in the panoramic images. However, on the right side in CBCT images, elongated SP was detected in 35 patients, but 57.1% of them were classified as non-elongated on panoramic images. Similarly, on the left side in CBCT images, 46 patients had elongated SP, and 58.7% of them were not detected in panoramic images. In CBCT images, 133 patients had bilateral elongated SP, and 22.6% of them were classified as non-elongated in panoramic images (Table 2). In comparison with CBCT imaging, panoramic imaging showed high specificity, but the sensitivity was low (Table 3).

Discussion

The diagnosis and treatment of head and neck pain can be challenging due to the multitude of contributing factors. The elongated SP could be the reason for the head and neck pain; thus, it is important that all healthcare professionals be aware of its presence. The pressure on the nerves and muscles surrounding the SP would cause dysphagia, headache, cervicalgia, tinnitus, otalgia, trigeminal neuralgia, and even more serious symptoms such as transient ischemic attack or stroke because of the close anatomical relationship of SP and carotid artery [3, 35, 36]. Several imaging methods have been used to assess SP, such as panoramic imaging [2, 4, 8, 13–17], US [18], CT [6, 19–21], and CBCT [22–26]. Zang et al. [18] indicated that the dimensions and morphology of the SP can be assessed with US, yielding results comparable to those obtained through CT. However, they emphasized the significance of the sonographer’s experience in the accuracy of ultrasound measurements of SP.

Among the studies that used panoramic imaging for SP screening, in some studies SP elongation was evaluated with millimetric (ruler) measurements [2, 4, 13, 17, 37], while in others, certain anatomical structures such as an imaginary line connecting the anterior nasal spine and the mastoid process [14, 38] or the mandibular foramen [1, 5, 8, 15] were referenced, and it was evaluated whether the SP exceeded them or not. Panoramic imaging may distort the dimension of the SP, as it is located medial to the focal through, and magnification may vary with the angulation of the process. Moreover, determination of the exact location of the point of emergence of the SP from the temporal bone could be difficult because of the superimposed surrounding structures [38]. Therefore, in this study, considering the magnification and superpositions arising from the nature of the panoramic imaging, to determine the length of SP, ruler measurement was not chosen. In panoramic images, SP was classified as elongated if situated inferior to the mandibular foramen [1, 15], and the prevalence of elongated SP was 43.5% in panoramic images.

Many studies report SP prevalence using panoramic imaging, with a wide prevalence spectrum ranging from 3.7% to 84.4% [2, 4, 5, 8, 13, 14, 17, 38]. Likewise, the prevalence values derived from research utilizing CT or CBCT exhibit a broad spectrum [22–24, 26]. The reasons for these huge discrepancies between the results may be the different study populations and different methods used in the evaluation of SP. Nogueira-Reis et al. [39] conducted a recent systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence and features of elongated SP, revealing an overall prevalence of 30.2%. This study’s findings showed higher prevalence than Nogueira-Reis et al. [39] for both panoramic (43.5%) and CBCT (52.6%) images. In this study, similar to some studies [8, 14, 22, 24, 38–40], there was not a significant difference between male and female subjects; however, some studies reported different prevalence between male and female [4, 5, 16]. Diverse study populations and varying evaluation methodologies may account for these discrepancies. As mentioned before, there are numerous studies that used panoramic imaging for SP evaluation [2, 4, 8, 13–17]; however, in some cases, correct diagnosis of SP elongation in panoramic images is difficult due to the superpositions of adjacent bone structures like the mandible, teeth, or the base of the skull [41]. Also, magnification of panoramic machines and the angle between the stylohyoid complex and the cranial base can influence the perceived length of the SP in panoramic images [37]. Three-dimensional imaging techniques like CT and CBCT solve these problems [6, 19]. CBCT is more suitable for use in these types of cases due to its lower radiation dose and lower cost compared to CT [28]. It is important to note that CBCT exposes patients to a greater radiation dosage than panoramic imaging and should be utilized solely, when necessary, in accordance with the ALADA (As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable) principle [29]. Although Nogueira-Reis et al. [39] concluded that the imaging modality did not influence the diagnosis of elongated SP, Centurion et al. [40] reported different SP prevalence for the same 100 patients in panoramic (42%) and CBCT (63%) images, similar to our findings. Centurion et al. [40] also reported 40 false negatives, where CBCT revealed stylohyoid chain ossification not visible in panoramic images, and 31 false positives, where panoramic imaging revealed stylohyoid chain ossification not confirmed by CBCT. In the present study, there were 32.7% (55) false negatives and 18.8% (45) false positives on the right side and 35.6% (64) false negatives and 16.3% (37) false positive examinations on the left side. These findings revealed that in comparison with CBCT imaging, panoramic imaging showed high specificity, 81.2% on the right side and 83.7% on the left side, but low sensitivity, 67.3% on the right side and 64.4% on the left side. Subtle and tenuous calcifications in segmented elongated instances may be challenging to identify on panoramic imaging, perhaps leading to false-negative outcomes (Fig. 5).

The median length of SPs in CBCT images was 29.97 mm for the right side with an interquartile range of 10.17 mm and 30.56 mm for the left side with an interquartile range of 9.68 mm. These findings were similar to some studies [21, 42]; however, they were slightly lower than Buyuk et al. [23] and slightly higher than the others [6, 25, 26]. Thomas et al. [42] compared the length of the SP in panoramic images to CT, regarded as the gold standard, in a restricted cohort of merely 15 patients, revealing a moderate positive correlation on the left side and a high positive correlation on the right side. In this study, the absence of millimetric measurements of SP on panoramic images precluded a comparison with CBCT. There was not a significant difference in length values of SP between males and females, and this was convenient with other studies [22]. Despite reports of an increase in both the presence and length of SP with age [2, 8, 38], this study found no significant relationship between age and SP length on either the right side (p = 0.352) or the left side (p = 0.171). Similarly, Nogueira-Reis et al. [39] reported in a recent meta-analysis study that age did not influence the prevalence of elongated SP. The sample group, imaging, and measurement methods used may contribute to these differences between the studies. The results could be influenced by the age distribution of the study group, as certain studies may include young individuals who have not yet completed their skeletal development [2], potentially contributing to the increase in SP length with age.

SP elongation occurs more bilaterally than unilaterally [4, 5, 14, 24, 26, 38, 39], and this was convenient with our results. This study found that elongated (type 1) was the most prevalent calcification type, and partially calcified was the most common calcification pattern in both panoramic and CBCT images, which aligns with previous studies [4, 13, 24, 26]. There were only 5 (1.2%) type 0 on the right-sided SP in CBCT images and 7 (1.7%) type 0 on the left-sided SP in CBCT images, and this was convenient with previous studies [6, 15, 22]. When the relationship between gender and SP calcification types and patterns was examined, there was a significant relationship between gender and calcification pattern in panoramic images (right side p = 0.014, left side p < 0.001). The partially calcified (type 2) pattern of calcification was more frequently observed in males.

The lack of angulation measurement of SP is a limitation of this study, as not only the elongated SP but also the angulation of the SP could cause orofacial pain or dysphagia [43]. Besides measuring the SP length, three-dimensional images like CT or CBCT can also measure angulation, a feature that panoramic images cannot display. The objective is to evaluate the reliability of panoramic imaging in assessing SP, using CBCT as the reference standard. Therefore, due to the inability to quantify angles in panoramic images, angle assessment was not conducted in CBCT images. In pain-related cases, measuring both the angle and the length might be beneficial. Future research using other imaging modalities, such as ultrasound, which could reveal the positional relationship between the SP and surrounding blood vessels [13], will be helpful in examining the length, angle, and form of the SP in various patient groups. Another disadvantage is the absence of patients’ clinical findings, as the study was planned as a retrospective analysis, relying solely on images from the radiology archives.

Conclusion

Although Nogueira-Reis et al. [39] concluded that the imaging modality did not influence the diagnosis of elongated SP, in this study, in comparison with CBCT imaging, panoramic imaging showed high specificity but low sensitivity. Similar to our findings, Centurion et al. [40] reported a significant difference in the identification of SP elongation between panoramic and CBCT images. Although the superiority of CBCT was expected, as it is three-dimensional, almost one-third of the elongated SP cases were detected as false negative in panoramic images. Considering these findings, it is advisable to assess the region using CBCT in situations where SP examination is critical, such as in identifying the source of pain or prior to surgery.
