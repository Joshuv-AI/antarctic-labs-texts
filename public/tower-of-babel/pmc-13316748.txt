Large Language Models for Ophthalmology Training in China: A Prospective Evaluation
Authors: Zuhui Zhang, Changke Huang, Xinxin Yu, Tao Lv, Xiaoyu Chen, Chenchen Wang, Ying Li, Fen-Fen Li, Shuangqing Wu, Ya-na Fu, Fan Lu, Qi Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study explored large language models (LLMs) as a scalable solution to the global shortage and uneven distribution of ophthalmologists, particularly their actual effectiveness and potential risks in ophthalmic training.DesignThis is a prospective study.SubjectsEleven leading LLMs (ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, DeepSeek-R1, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Tencent Yuanbao T1, HuatuoGPT II, Baichuan4-Turbo, Kimi k1.5, GLM-4) and 10 resident physicians (RPs) from a tertiary ophthalmology hospital in China.MethodsPhase 1: all LLMs were tested on the Chinese and English versions of the Chinese National Health Professional Technical Qualification Examination (Intermediate Level) in Ophthalmology (CNHPTQE-O). Phase 2: the best-performing LLM was used to assist the 10 RPs in 2 tasks: (1) answering the same CNHPTQE-O text questions and (2) classifying 4 types of keratitis images. Resident physicians completed unassisted and assisted phases with a 1-month washout period.Main Outcome MeasuresThe primary outcomes were accuracy (%) on the CNHPTQE-O for LLMs, and change in accuracy for RPs with versus without LLM assistance (text and image tasks). The secondary outcomes included postassessment survey ratings and confusion matrix analysis.ResultsSeveral Chinese LLMs, especially ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, demonstrated superior performance on the CNHPTQE-O, achieving accuracies of 98.00% (Chinese) and 86.50% (English). ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo significantly outperformed all RPs on the Chinese examination (P = 0.001). With LLM assistance, all 10 RPs passed the text examination; the mean accuracy improved from 60.75% to 79.00% (mean difference 18.25%, 95% confidence interval: 10.30%–26.20%, P = 0.001). Questionnaire feedback was positive. However, on the keratitis image task, LLM assistance did not improve RP accuracy (41.25% vs. 40.56%, P = 0.662); questionnaire feedback was markedly less favorable.ConclusionsLarge language models possess a solid foundation in ophthalmic knowledge and can effectively enhance trainee performance in text-based assessments, demonstrating clear potential as a training aid. However, their limitations in image-assisted diagnostic tasks and the associated risk of “artificial ignorance” should not be overlooked.Financial DisclosuresThe author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Large language models, Artificial intelligence, Ophthalmology training, Resident physicians, Artificial ignorance.

Impaired vision is a major public health challenge worldwide, with approximately 2.2 billion people worldwide living with vision impairment or blindness, of which >1 billion are at risk of blindness because of untimely treatment.1 This challenge is complicated by a global shortage of health care workers.2 In China, the density of ophthalmologists falls below the global average, compounded by the uneven distribution of ophthalmic care services and disparities in human resource development.3 Competent clinical workforce development requires rigorous medical education and standardized residency training. However, the duration, content, and assessment methods of residency training programs vary nationwide. A consensus, reflected by 91% of surveyed ophthalmology experts, underscores the urgent need to harmonize specialized training standards for ophthalmologists.4 Moreover, significant variability in the diagnostic proficiency of resident physicians (RPs) suggests the need for more effective, scalable, and structured subspecialty training paradigms in ophthalmology.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), has transformative potential in medicine and medical education. Large language models may offer professional guidance in ophthalmic training in 3 ways: (1) generating high-quality, interactive instructional content; (2) providing rapid access to synthesized clinical knowledge; and (3) offering diagnostic reasoning support to augment clinical decision-making.

Recently developed Chinese LLMs, including DeepSeek-R1,5 Baidu ERNIE Bot,6 Alibaba Tongyi Qianwen,7 Tencent Yuanbao,8 Dou Bao,9 Baichuan,10 Kimi,11 Zhipu GLM,12 and HuatuoGPT II,13 have exhibited strong performance in a wide range of queries. Large language models offer unique opportunities to revolutionize digital eye care, address clinical workflow inefficiencies, and enhance patient experiences across diverse global eye care contexts.14 However, robust evidence regarding the performance of these region-specific LLMs on standardized, professional ophthalmology examinations in China remains scarce. A systematic evaluation of their competencies is essential to assess their viability as reliable assistants in clinical training.

In China, ophthalmologists must pass the Chinese National Health Professional Technical Qualification Examination (Intermediate Level) in Ophthalmology (CNHPTQE-O). Large language models’ clinical potential is commonly assessed using standardized medical examination performance. While studies have shown that ChatGPT performs competitively on English-language ophthalmology board examinations,15,16 evidence regarding LLM accuracy in ophthalmology remains limited, and their performance on Chinese-language professional examinations has not been systematically evaluated. Furthermore, most comparative analyses of LLMs have been conducted in English. Thus, their ability to answer Chinese ophthalmology-specific questions and recognize ocular surface disease images remains largely unexplored.

Therefore, this study first comprehensively evaluated the performance of multiple LLMs, including leading Chinese models, on text-based questions from the CNHPTQE-O and image-based recognition tasks for keratitis subtypes. Second, we assessed whether LLM assistance could enhance the examination performance of RPs and evaluated the perceived utility and reliability of LLMs.

Methods

All participants were recruited from the Eye Hospital, Wenzhou Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Eye Hospital, Wenzhou Medical University (approval number: H2025-023-K-22-01), and was registered on http://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2500102817). Informed consent to publish was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. Figure 1 shows the whole study design flowchart.

Study design flowchart. Eleven LLMs were selected to take the CNHPTQE-O. They answered 92 examination questions, including single-choice, shared-stem single-choice, and case-study questions. The examination questions were sampled from official sources and excluded those with images. Additionally, 8 LLMs were selected for ophthalmic image recognition of 160 slit-lamp images, including 40 bacterial keratitis, 40 fungal keratitis, 40 viral keratitis, and 40 noninfectious keratitis images. The LLMs’ responses were differentiated based on the official answers and subjected to statistical analysis for comparison. Then, the LLMs with the highest correct rates were selected to participate in the subsequent stage of the RPs’ examinations. The RPs were then administered the examinations, with and without LLM assistance, and the results were analyzed to determine the extent to which the LLM could play a supporting role. Additionally, feedback from the RPs was considered. Ophthalmic-Related Questions and Keratitis Images

The text-based evaluation utilized questions sourced from the CNHPTQE-O.17 The CNHPTQE-O questions used in this study were sourced from the 2023 CNHPTQE-O, publicly available on the online document sharing platform max.book118.com (URL: https://max.book118.com/html/2023/0525/8037055020005072.shtm). This platform allows user-uploaded content and is freely accessible to the public without registration for viewing. The assessment comprised 92 items with a total possible score of 100 points, spanning single-choice, shared-stem single-choice, and case-study questions. Single-choice questions presented a stem followed by 5 options containing 1 correct answer and 4 distractors. Shared-stem single-choice questions were based on a clinical vignette, generating 2–6 independent subquestions, each requiring the selection of 1 correct option. Case study questions simulated progressive clinical scenarios, wherein information was sequentially disclosed to mirror patient management. These questions could have ≥1 correct answers. The scoring was as follows: full credit (1 point) for identifying all correct options, partial credit (0.5 points) for incomplete but error-free selection, and a penalty of 1 point for selecting any incorrect option. All questions were professionally translated into English to assess potential language-based performance variance. Both the original Chinese and translated English versions were reviewed for validity and reliability by a senior ophthalmologist with >8 years of clinical experience.

Because corneal diseases are among the most diagnostically challenging subspecialties in ophthalmology and are heavily reliant on image interpretation, keratitis diagnosis was selected as the image-based evaluation task. The image data set was curated from an internal repository of keratitis cases previously annotated by a panel of 3 ophthalmologists. The data set quality was ensured using a Hybrid Prior-Net model for quality control, which demonstrated 99.03% accuracy in filtering suitable images.18 For the keratitis image recognition task, we used an internal data set collected from our hospital between 2018 and 2023. The ground truth label for each image was established based on comprehensive clinical evaluation, including disease characteristics, clinical history, treatment course, and relevant laboratory findings. Bacterial keratitis and fungal keratitis defined as compatible clinical manifestations with ≥1 positive laboratory test result (smear or microbial cultures). From this data set, 160 slit lamp images were randomly selected, forming a balanced set of 40 images for each of 4 categories (bacterial keratitis, fungal keratitis, viral keratitis, and noninfectious keratitis). All images were from unique patients; no patient contributed >1 image to the data set.

To ensure consistency and prevent carryover effects, a dedicated account was created for all LLM interactions. Conversation history was cleared before each new question or image was submitted. All prompts were input by the same ophthalmologist to maintain uniformity throughout the testing process.

LLM Performance Evaluation on Textual and Image-Based Tasks

Both Chinese and English versions of the CNHPTQE-O question and answer choices were systematically input into all 11 LLMs. Each model was prompted to generate the correct answer along with a corresponding explanatory rationale for every question in both languages. Similarly, the keratitis slit lamp image set was submitted to the 8 LLMs able to process visual inputs (the remaining 3 models did not support image recognition). For each image, the models were prompted to provide a diagnostic classification along with a brief explanation. All responses, including the selected answers, diagnoses, and accompanying explanations, were evaluated against the official answer key. Accuracy rates were calculated for each LLM across both question sets and the image-recognition task to enable a comparative performance analysis. Details of prompts and workflow are provided in Supplementary Appendix S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Evaluation of LLM-Assisted Resident Physician Training

To evaluate the potential of LLMs to augment clinical training, we integrated the top-performing model, ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, into a structured assessment involving RPs. Ten RPs were recruited for the study. All RPs held physician qualification certificates with certification durations ranging from 1 to 14 years. Five were senior RPs certified for >3 years (RP1: 6 years; RP2: 8 years; RP3: 5 years; RP4: 14 years; RP5: 7 years), while the other 5 were junior RPs certified for <3 years (RP6: 1 year; RP7: 1 year; RP8: 1 year; RP9: 2 years; RP10: 1 year).

Each RP completed the 92-item CNHPTQE-O question set under 2 conditions in sequential phases: first independently (Round 1) and then with the assistance of ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo (Round 2). The same model completed the question set independently to establish a performance benchmark. Accuracy rates were recorded for all attempts. To ensure consistency in experiment timing, ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo also participated in the first-round testing simultaneously by answering all the CNHPTQE-O questions. To explore the application of LLMs in assisting ophthalmologists with ophthalmic image recognition, we selected high-quality keratitis images from our previous research. All RPs underwent 2 additional rounds of keratitis image-recognition tests. The first round was conducted without assistance, while the second round utilized assistance from ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo. In the initial round, each RP were required to analyze the images independently, and their respective accuracy rates were documented.

After a 1-month washout period, RPs were notified of the second round of testing. Before the second round of testing began, all participants were informed that they could use ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo to inquire about examination questions but were also informed that answers in ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo were not completely correct and that they would have to rely on themselves to recognize the correctness of their answers and explanations. Accuracy rates of all participants in the second round of the CNHPTQE-O examination and ophthalmic image-recognition testing were then evaluated and compared with their performance in the first round. Furthermore, RPs who received assistance from ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo in the prospective study were asked to complete a postdeployment questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of 10 items aimed at gauging the RPs’ opinions on LLMs. Each question was rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 representing strong disagreement and 5 representing strong agreement.

Statistical Analysis

The performances of all LLMs and RPs in providing correct responses were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM). The accuracy rate (%) of each chatbot and RP was calculated as the percentage of correct answers out of the total number of questions. Values are expressed as means ± standard deviation or medians (interquartile range). Chi-square and Fisher exact tests were employed to compare chatbot performances between different LLMs. Paired t tests, along with their 95% confidence intervals and effect sizes, were used to compare the performance of the RPs in the CNHPTQE-O examination with and without assistance using ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo.

Results

The performances of the 11 LLMs on the Chinese version of CNHPTQE-O are summarized in Figure 2. All models achieved a passing score. ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo demonstrated significantly superior accuracy compared with 7 other LLMs (P < 0.05, chi-square tests), with the exception of Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and DeepSeek-R1, against which its performance was not statistically different. Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and DeepSeek-R1 significantly outperformed GLM-4, Gemini 2.5 Flash, HuatuoGPT II, and ChatGPT-4o (P < 0.05, chi-square test). The results for the English version of the examination are shown in Figure 3. While all 11 LLMs passed, HuatuoGPT II significantly underperformed compared with ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and Kimi k1.5 (P < 0.05). Its performance relative to the other 6 models was not significantly different.

To provide a more granular view of LLM performance, we stratified the results by question type on the Chinese version of the CNHPTQE-O. As shown in Table 2, the examination consisted of 3 types: single-choice questions (89 points), shared-stem single-choice questions (3 points), and case-study questions (8 points). All LLMs performed well on shared-stem single-choice questions, with most achieving 100% accuracy. On single-choice questions, ERNIE Bot 4.5-Turbo and Dou Bao achieved the highest accuracy (97.75%), followed by DeepSeek-R1 and Tongyi Qianwen 3 (94.38%). Performance on case-study questions showed the greatest variability: ERNIE Bot 4.5-Turbo and Baichuan4-Turbo achieved perfect scores (100%), while ChatGPT-4o and Tencent Yuanbao T1 scored 62.50% and 68.75%, respectively. Overall, the relative ranking of models remained consistent across question types, with ERNIE Bot 4.5-Turbo, Dou Bao, and Tongyi Qianwen 3 consistently among the top performers.

The 10 RPs achieved a mean accuracy of 60.75% (±10.07) on the CNHPTQE-O during the independent baseline assessment (range: 46.00%–71.50%). Six RPs met the passing threshold, whereas 4 did not (RP5: 46.00%; RP6: 51.00%; RP8: 54.00%; RP10: 50.50%). Given its superior performance on the Chinese CNHPTQE-O (accuracy: 98.00%), ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo was selected as an assistive tool for the subsequent phase. ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo also surpassed all RPs in the first-round examination. With access to the ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, all 10 RPs successfully passed the examination in the assisted phase. The mean accuracy significantly increased to 79.00%, with a mean difference of 18.25% (t = 5.193, P = 0.001; 95% confidence interval: 10.30%–26.20%), representing individual improvements ranging from 4.50% to 41.50%. A structured postassessment survey was conducted to evaluate the subjective experience of 10 RPs after using the LLM as an aid during the CNHPTQE-O. As shown in Table 4, the RPs provided positive ratings regarding the explanatory accuracy (Item 1) and content reliability (Item 2) of the LLM. In terms of willingness to use, participants indicated a preference for seeking assistance from the LLM (Item 6) and widely agreed that its use increased their confidence in task completion (Item 7). Regarding usability, the LLM was rated as intuitive and easy to use (Item 4) and was perceived to improve task efficiency (Item 5). Furthermore, most participants expressed a clear intention to use the LLM in future ophthalmology training (Item 9) and to recommend it to others (Item 10). However, the survey revealed potential risks: approximately 70% of RPs reported encountering instances where the LLM provided false information (Item 3), and about half of the physicians exhibited a tendency to over-rely on the LLM when their own judgment conflicted with its suggestions (Item 8).

After the LLM-assisted keratitis image-recognition task, the same structured questionnaire was administered to the 10 RPs who participated in this substudy (Table 5). In stark contrast to the positive feedback received after the text-based examination, physicians rated the LLM’s performance in the image-based diagnostic task to be considerably lower. Specifically, the accuracy of the explanations (Item 1) and reliability of the content (Item 2) provided by the LLM did not reach the “agreement” threshold. Furthermore, all RPs reported encountering instances where the LLM supplied false information (Item 3). In terms of user experience, evaluations of the LLM’s ease of use (Item 4) and its perceived ability to improve task efficiency (Item 5) were neutral to negative. Regarding willingness to use, participants’ inclination to prioritize the LLM for assistance (Item 6) fell within the neutral range, indicating ambivalent or reserved attitudes toward relying on the LLM as a primary aid in keratitis image recognition, with no clear preference emerging. Notably, only 20% of RPs reported increased confidence after using the LLM (Item 7), while 30% of RPs still exhibited a tendency to over-rely on the LLM when their own judgment conflicted with its suggestions (Item 8). Despite these reservations, participants maintained a neutral to slightly positive stance toward the future use of the LLM in ophthalmology training (Item 9) and recommending it to others (Item 10).

Discussion

This study leveraged a standardized national ophthalmology examination (CNHPTQE-O) to benchmark LLM performance across diverse questions, formats, and languages. Most LLMs, particularly ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, achieved high accuracy on both language versions of the examination. Notably, ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo outperformed all RPs in text-based assessment and also enhanced their examination scores. In contrast, LLMs exhibited markedly lower diagnostic accuracy in ophthalmic image recognition than RPs, highlighting a persistent limitation in multimodal clinical reasoning and their current inability to augment trainee performance in image-based diagnostic tasks.

Recent studies have explored the applications of LLMs in ophthalmology. Early evaluations focused on the performance of knowledge-based assessments. Mihalache et al16 reported that ChatGPT correctly answered 46.4% of text-based ophthalmic board questions in early 2023, with performance varying considerably by subspecialty and improving to 58% within 1 month, although the authors noted insufficient accuracy for reliable examination preparation. Antaki et al15 similarly found that while general-purpose LLMs showed promising knowledge retrieval, their performance on simulated Ophthalmic Knowledge Assessment Program questions remained below that of expert trainees and was fundamentally limited by their inability to process images. In contrast, later studies employing more advanced models or specialized prompting demonstrated higher proficiency. Raimondi et al19 found that models, such as Bing Chat and strategically prompted ChatGPT-4.0, could exceed the typical human passing threshold on Royal College of Ophthalmologists examinations.

Concurrently, research on region-specific models, particularly those developed in China, has expanded. Evaluations of models such as DeepSeek-R1 have shown competitive or superior performance to their international counterparts on bilingual ophthalmology reasoning tasks, while also highlighting shared limitations such as errors in complex clinical reasoning and outdated therapeutic recommendations.20,21 Other studies have explored the cost-effectiveness of open-weight models and their potential in patient-facing roles such as health education.22,23 In a study assessing complex clinical reasoning, Milad et al24 reported that ChatGPT-4 achieved diagnostic and management accuracies of 48.0% and 63.0%, respectively, in 422 ophthalmology cases, performing comparably to board-certified ophthalmologists but below senior residents. The authors positioned LLMs as complementary aids rather than replacements for expert judgment. Advancing to multimodal applications, Li et al25 developed the Multimodal Ocular Surface Assessment Intelligent Copilot, a multimodal LLM-based system that performs automated screening of ocular surface diseases from smartphone images, with accuracies of up to 95.0% for quality assessment and 87.0% for disease detection.

The CNHPTQE-O assesses knowledge at the specialist level and provides a robust benchmark for evaluating LLMs with intermediate ophthalmic competency. In the Chinese version, several domestic LLMs (ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and DeepSeek-R1) scored >93%. ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo achieved 98%, significantly outperforming most other models and demonstrating a sufficiently solid knowledge base to potentially support specialist training in China. Most Chinese LLMs also performed well on the English version of the examination, with accuracies >80% and comparable to ChatGPT-4o, although HuatuoGPT II underperformed despite its focus on clinical training. This observation aligns with the findings reported by Niu et al,26 in which Tongyi Qianwen 2.5 exhibited superior clarity in Chinese-language responses to ptosis-related inquiries from both patients and medical professionals compared with ChatGPT-4o. The strong performance of domestic LLMs, particularly ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, demonstrates their linguistic capabilities and suggests that they can satisfy certain standards required by medical professionals. However, compared with single-choice questions, LLMs exhibited higher error rates when processing multiple-choice questions, likely because of a lack of specialized training in medical knowledge integration and clinical reasoning. The difficulty presented by the multiple-choice questions underscores the current limitations of effectively applying LLMs in clinical practice and highlights the need for regular systematic evaluation, oversight, and fine-tuning of chatbots to ensure consistent, reliable, and clinically safe medical advice.

During the evaluation, we observed technical and behavioral variations. For instance, DeepSeek-R1 occasionally returned responses such as “The server is busy, please try again later,” while HuatuoGPT II sometimes prompted, “The number of dialogues has exceeded the system limit. Please refresh the page to start a new session.” Additionally, when presented with the Chinese version of the CNHPTQE-O, all LLMs responded in Chinese. When given the English version, Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and Kimi k1.5 provided responses mixing Chinese and English, whereas other LLMs replied entirely in English.

Most LLMs delivered accurate responses and clinically relevant explanations for both Chinese and English versions of the CNHPTQE-O. Several Chinese LLMs outperformed international models on the Chinese examination while matching their performance on the English version. ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo excelled in both languages, achieving near-perfect accuracy. When used to assist RPs, ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo significantly improved the examination scores. Although traditional training enabled RPs to pass the CNHPTQE-O, their baseline accuracy (46.00%–71.50%) was substantially lower than that of the model (98.00%). With LLM support, all RPs passed, and marginal performers showed marked improvement. The participants consistently reported high satisfaction with and willingness to integrate LLMs into training. In this exploratory, single-center pilot study, these preliminary results suggest that LLMs may have value in enhancing knowledge acquisition and examination readiness. However, their potential to generate plausible but incorrect content underscores the need for supervised and critical use, particularly among trainees, and reinforces the rationale for evaluating these tools within a professional cohort, rather than the general public.

Overall, most of the LLMs demonstrated a broad knowledge base and reasoning ability to answer ophthalmology-related questions. Consequently, the capacity of LLMs to provide physicians with foundational ophthalmic knowledge and their potential for ophthalmic medical training are of significant importance and show promising prospects. Growing evidence suggests that domestic LLMs are effective for medical licensing examinations, which may transform medical training. However, although current LLMs can efficiently process medical information and provide appropriate answers to questions, they cannot replace critical thinking, innovation, and creativity, which are essential physician attributes.27

Although AI has considerable diagnostic potential, it is not infallible. Studies investigating the interactions of clinicians with erroneous AI have demonstrated that nonspecialists and residents show decreased diagnostic accuracy when the AI is wrong, whereas corneal specialists maintain their performance, highlighting how expertise buffers against misleading AI guidance.28 This vulnerability is compounded by a performance gap in image-based reasoning. Multimodal LLMs excel with structured text and achieve high diagnostic and management accuracy when provided with detailed clinical narratives. However, their performance drops when inputs shift to images with minimal context, and falls far below that of junior ophthalmologists when diagnosing from images alone.29,30 A meta-analysis further confirmed that while advanced LLMs like ChatGPT-4 perform well on text-based ophthalmology questions, their accuracy remains significantly lower on image-based items and varies widely across subspecialties.30 The 2 key challenges proposed by the authors included “hallucinations,” wherein the model may generate plausible but incorrect or fabricated information, and knowledge obsolescence, as the training data are only current up to a specific cutoff date, leaving it unaware of the latest medical advances. Our findings align with this pattern: LLMs suggested low diagnostic accuracy for keratitis images and, compared with the unassisted scenario, the diagnostic accuracy of RPs decreased when LLM assistance was introduced. The core explanation for the suboptimal performance of current LLMs in diagnosing keratitis is the inherent lack of domain specialization, which manifests in 3 key aspects. First, a data mismatch: LLMs are predominantly trained on publicly available general images and text rather than on specialized medical images and corresponding reports, resulting in a deficiency in ophthalmic expertise. Second, a capability mismatch: while LLMs excel in object recognition, ophthalmic diagnosis requires identifying subtle, pixel-level pathological changes and clinical reasoning, tasks that fall outside their core competencies. Third, uncontrollable risk: LLMs are prone to hallucinations, a critical failure in high-stakes medical scenarios. Essentially, expecting a generalist model trained on broad Internet data to perform the work of an ophthalmologist, a role requiring highly specialized training, leads to inadequate performance and error-prone outcomes. Addressing this limitation will likely require dedicated models trained on curated medical data. Thus, while AI can enhance diagnostic efficiency, physician expertise remains crucial in complex or atypical cases to ensure reliability and prevent misdiagnosis, underscoring the indispensable value of human judgment in human–AI collaborations. Therefore, whether referring to international LLMs such as ChatGPT or Gemini 2.5 Flash, or domestic LLMs like ERNIE Bot 4.5 Turbo, Dou Bao, Tongyi Qianwen 3, and DeepSeek-R1, the most suitable role for LLMs in ophthalmic medicine is that of a “copilot” to aid less-experienced ophthalmologists in efficiently processing textual information and suggesting diagnostic approaches. However, they cannot substitute for physicians, especially for independent image-based diagnoses. The final decision-making authority must unequivocally rest with human clinicians.

The decline in diagnostic accuracy among RPs when using AI assistance suggests automation bias exacerbated by the tendency of current multimodal LLMs to generate convincing but factually flawed rationales. These models, while adept at generic object recognition, lack the nuanced pixel-level discernment and integrative reasoning required for ophthalmic image interpretation. When an uncertain RP encounters a complex keratitis image, LLMs may produce a confident and detailed yet erroneous diagnosis. Our observations suggest the possibility of a “trust trap,” leading RPs to over-reliance on the flawed rationale. Such plausible hallucinations not only fail to correct RPs’ initial errors but may also actively steer them away from their own correct preliminary judgments, resulting in incorrect recommendations. This aligns with findings from the Japanese “CorneAI” study,28 which reported a significant drop in diagnostic accuracy among noncorneal specialists when AI provided wrong diagnoses. Thus, the observed effect is not incidental but rather reflects a typical cognitive–psychological response of junior physicians when exposed to immature AI tools. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, we observed a declining trend in accuracy (from 41.25% to 40.56%). This study, through structured surveys, reveals a notable divergence in RPs' evaluations of LLMs: they were viewed positively for text-based examinations but received markedly lower ratings for image-based diagnostic tasks. Concurrently, participants reported encountering false information generated by the LLM in both settings, yet still exhibited a tendency to over-rely on its suggestions. This split in perception and behavior raises the possibility of what could be termed “artificial ignorance”—a hypothesized cognitive bias where users over-rely on AI outputs despite recognizing limitations in certain domains. However, we emphasize that this interpretation is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, as our study was not designed to directly measure over-reliance or automation bias. In text-based tasks, where LLM outputs were generally reliable, the potential influence of artificial ignorance remained relatively concealed. Even when their own judgment conflicted with the LLM’s suggestion, some RPs were still inclined to follow it, which may reflect a latent cognitive dependence. These observations are consistent with the concept, but future studies using dedicated instruments are needed to rigorously define and measure artificial ignorance in human–AI collaboration. In image-based tasks, however, the limitations of the LLM became starkly apparent. Errors were more obvious and often contradicted the physicians’ own visual assessments. Although RPs recognized the lower reliability and frequent generation of false information, a degree of dependency persisted, ultimately contributing to a decline in diagnostic accuracy. Moving forward, the integration of LLMs into ophthalmology training must be accompanied by an awareness of the task-dependent risks posed by artificial ignorance. In language-dominant scenarios such as knowledge review and text, LLMs can serve as effective aids. Yet in areas requiring deeper professional understanding—such as image diagnosis and visual differentiation—current LLMs remain fundamentally limited. Not only is their auxiliary value restricted, but their systematic “hallucinatory” outputs may disrupt the learning process and even induce clinical misjudgments. Therefore, future LLM development should prioritize transparency and explainability in specialized visual tasks. Concurrently, training curricula should incorporate education on recognizing artificial ignorance and cultivating critical reflective usage and thus fostering synergistic evolution between AI and physicians, rather than one-sided dependence.

Our study has several limitations. First, a significant methodological limitation of this study is the sequential design, in which the assisted phase always followed the unassisted phase using identical question and image sets. Although a washout period of 1 month was implemented to reduce short-term memory effects, this design cannot adequately exclude the influence of learning, recall, or test-familiarity. Consequently, the higher performance observed in the assisted phase on text-based tasks cannot be solely attributed to LLM assistance. The improvement may partly reflect repeated exposure to the same test material. Future studies should employ a counterbalanced or crossover randomized design, with half of the participants using LLM assistance first, to isolate the true effect of LLM support. Second, this keratitis data set was derived from a single institution and included only 4 categories, and it has not been externally verified. Consequently, our findings on LLM performance should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The absence of external validation means that the reported accuracy may reflect institution-specific image characteristics rather than true model generalizability. Moreover, the relatively narrow scope and modest sample size further limit the generalizability of the conclusions. Future studies should include external, multicenter, and more diverse data sets to verify the reproducibility and robustness of our observations. Third, hallucinations can mislead users lacking specialized knowledge. This necessitates ongoing rigorous validation against gold standard answers and established clinical guidelines and underscores the need for further professional training before such tools can be deployed in a clinical setting. Fourth, our CNHPTQE-O questions were sourced from a publicly accessible online document. Given that most commercial LLMs are trained on massive web-crawled data sets, it is plausible that some or all of these questions were present in the training data of the models evaluated in this study. Consequently, the superior performance observed in these models may not fully reflect genuine clinical reasoning or domain knowledge, but rather memorization or pattern recognition of previously seen examination content. We emphasize this limitation to caution against overinterpretation of the absolute performance scores. Future evaluations of LLMs in medical education should preferentially use newly developed, nonpublicly available question sets. Finally, the sample size of the RPs was relatively small, which may have affected the statistical power and external validity of the findings. All prospective findings from the RP cohort should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive or broadly generalizable. Future multicenter studies involving larger and more diverse cohorts of RPs are warranted to strengthen this evidence and improve its generalizability. Additionally, our study design did not fully account for potential biases in image selection or varying levels of clinical experience among the participants, which should be addressed in subsequent research.

In summary, the comprehensive benchmarking of 11 leading local and global LLMs using a standardized intermediate-level ophthalmology examination in China suggested the substantial ophthalmic knowledge and reasoning capabilities of these models and their significant potential for enhancing RP training and, in the future and with rigorous validation, for supporting clinical decision-making. However, these findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating; they require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts before any firm conclusions or practical recommendations can be made. The integration of LLMs into ophthalmology education represents a meaningful step toward more personalized, efficient, and scalable training paradigms.
