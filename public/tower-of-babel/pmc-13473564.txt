A passive islanding detection method using deep neural bidirectional LSTM-CNN
Authors: Hakan Ozturk, Secil Varbak Nese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrogrids based on local generation principle has advantages as reducing power losses and improving voltage profile. One of the biggest operational concerns in presence of a microgrid connected to a power system is unintentional islanding. Unintentional islanding events have potential to cause equipment damage and endanger human safety. According to IEEE Std 1547–2018, unintentional islanding must be detected and controlled in 2s. Therefore, a novel One-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Network-Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (1D CNN-BiLSTM) method is introduced for islanding detection. In the proposed method, power system is firstly controlled and observed to select busbars that are sensitive to islanding, then selected critical busbars are used for measurement. The optimally selected current and voltage time series data are then processed by neural network. Simulation results on the IEC 61850-7-420 test system demonstrate that the introduced method achieves a detection time of 10ms and a Non-Detection Zone (NDZ) rate of only 0.02%. These results indicate that the method significantly outperforms existing techniques in terms of both detection speed and reliability.Keywords: Deep neural networks, Islanding detection, Microgrid, Distributed generationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Microgrids containing Distributed Generation (DG) combine the positive advantages of DG as environmentally friendly, energy security, reliability, stability with better management. So, such microgrids are becoming more increasingly integrated. The situation supplied portion of grid by local generation source is electrically isolated from utility is called as islanding. While islanding can made as intentional to ensure energy security and continuity, it can also occur as an unintentional that can cause risk consumer and utility. Microgrid Islanding Detection (ID) is more important in modern power systems, as a result of increased penetration. In this perspective, effective ID is a necessity for microgrid operation stability by preventing serious security risks and operational problems that may arise. Passive and active approaches are used for detection, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. While passive methods monitoring system characteristics, active approaches typically inject external signals into the system for ID.

Numerous studies on ID methods have introduced to understand and improve of detection performance to the literature. Wang et al. proposed a frequency-reactive power feedback method that accelerates frequency deviation during islanding, enabling fast detection without dead zone and with little impact power quality1. Azim et al. introduced a passive method using a set of derived from voltage and current measurements and decision tree-based classifiers for ID2. Harikrishna et al. identified sequence-based phase angle and compared several approaches in hybrid microgrids for passive detection to improve accuracy during power imbalances3. Emphasizing the need of post-detection frequency management Eshraghi et al. concentrated on demand response strategies to control frequency in islanded microgrids4. Ghalavand et al. has also been used mathematical morphology as dilation and erosion filters to improve detection accuracy under varying conditions5. Kim et al. categorized ID methods, stressing the need of fast and reliable detection mechanisms in the context of increasing DG penetration6.

Among the studies that attempt to effectively detect islanding, Roy et al. investigated a method to detect islanding for DC microgrid. The machine learning-based method using voltage parameters employs support vector machines obtained high accuracy even with small power imbalances7. Using a harmonic filter-based approach, Dhua et al. greatly improved detection accuracy reducing false detection during load switching8. Moreover, to reduce power quality issues with current injection methods Ganivada et al. proposed low frequency current injection during grid disturbance. The technique was successful effectively detects islanding conditions and minimize power quality disturbances9. Raju et al. assessed passive techniques for single line to ground fault detection in microgrids, proposed method has better detection time, lower and stable NDZ10. Xia et al. developed a novel algorithm using deep learning techniques with advanced computational methods to categorize islanding conditions with high accuracy11.

Despite advances in ID methods, challenges remain. Minimal impact on power quality and fast detection remain the primary goals in this field. Furthermore, standards and regulations such as IEEE-1547-201812 influence the direction of research and applications. It is expected that integration of smart systems and machine learning algorithms will play an important role in improving ID in microgrids. Also, there are methods that use voltage at Point of Common Coupling (PCC) between grid and microgrid and/or voltage of DG for ID13,14. In another study, a deep learning method was applied for a microgrid that includes a diesel generator and a battery15. Neural networks with multiple Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units have been studied for a grid containing solar, wind and hydroelectric power plants16. Hussain et al. introduced a statistical feature-based Deep Neural Network (DNN) technique for ID hybrid systems with both synchronous and inverter-based DGs17. Furthermore, time based analyses and resonance-based fault detection techniques used for ID of microgrid18.

However, decision trees are used for ID in microgrids. An ID method has been developed which is based on sensitivity analysis using transient kinetic energy of wind turbines and uses kernel density estimation to take into account cyberattack19. A decision tree based fuzzy logic method is proposed for fast ID for a system containing multiple microgrids20. A decision tree method for ID is developed combining noise-assisted ensemble empirical mode decomposition and a hybrid optimization based random forest classifier. In the method, binary grey wolf optimizer is also used to reduce computational burden21. Choudhury et al. showed that an AlexNet-based deep learning algorithm strengthens decision mechanism for microgrids22. Another method in the literature for ID is 1D CNN-LSTM based deep learning by using total harmonic distortion at the PCC23. Moreover, reinforcement learning and variational mode decomposition method are applied in microgrids for ID24. Consequently, ID is an area requiring improvement in modern power systems to ensure reliable and efficient operation of microgrids.

In this study, a novel hybrid neural network architecture is proposed to address challenges of ID for Microgrids containing DGs. Differently previous studies in the literature, this study developed a 1D CNN-BiLSTM method for ID challenges. The proposed method utilizes both local feature extraction and temporal dependencies from sequential electrical signals. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of the method was conducted with a baseline LSTM network and a hybrid CNN-LSTM model. The method significantly outperformed existing techniques in terms of both detection speed and reliability.

Despite the extensive use of deep learning in islanding detection, existing studies primarily focus on improving performance metrics using variations of established architectures. However, limited attention has been given to structured framework design, measurement selection, and physically grounded validation. To address these gaps, this study proposes an integrated islanding detection framework that combines model architecture, signal selection, and system-level validation. Materials and methods

Long short-term memory (LSTM)

LSTM networks are a specialized form of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). LSTM introduced in the mid-1990s to overcome the difficulty of learning long-range temporal dependencies in sequence data25,26. Traditional RNNs suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients when trained by backpropagation through time, complicated to capture information over long intervals. LSTM cells were designed with an internal memory state and learnable gating mechanisms so that gradients can flow unchanged across many time steps26.

The core of an LSTM network is its memory cell maintaining a hidden state vector Ct across time. A single LSTM cell contains three multiplicative gates (input, forget, and output) that regulate information flow into and out of the cell as shown in Fig. 1. The forget gate controls how much of previous cell state should be retained or discarded, input gate controls how much new information to add to cell, and output gate controls how much of cell state to output at this time. This gating structure allows cell to accumulate information additively in Ct, thereby preserving gradient flow (often referred to as constant error carousel). In other words, LSTM cell can carry information forward with minimal decay, while gates (via sigmoid activations) learn which parts of that information to keep.

Bi-directional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM)

RNNs are designed to process sequential data maintaining a hidden state that captures information about past inputs. However, standard RNNs have limitations in capturing long-range dependencies and contextual information from both past and future inputs29. To address these limitations, Bi-LSTM networks were developed. Bi-LSTMs enhance capabilities of traditional LSTMs by processing input sequences in both forward and backward directions, allowing model to capture dependencies from both past and future contexts. This makes Bi-LSTMs particularly effective in tasks understanding the context surrounding an element in a sequence is crucial.

Concretely, one LSTM layer reads sequence in normal (left-to-right) order, producing a sequence of forward hidden states , while another LSTM reads in reverse (right-to-left), producing backward states . These two sub-networks are independent, so forward and backward states do not feed into each other30. Each sub-network uses same gate Eqs. (1)–(6) but on its respective time indexing. Because backward LSTM uses future context relative to any position, a Bi-LSTM can “leverage . past and future” information simultaneously.

At each time step t, the outputs of two LSTMs are combined. Typically, the forward and backward hidden vectors are concatenated to form Bi-LSTM output. In other words, if comes from forward pass and from backward pass (both computed via (6)) the Bi-LSTM output is a concatenated vector as (7)–(10).

This doubling of dimension means each output carries information from both directions. In summary, a BiLSTM is “a specialized variant” of LSTM architecture consisting of two parallel LSTMs31,32. It thus captures bidirectional temporal dependencies by computing LSTM gate updates (forget, input, output, cell update) in both directions and combining the resulting hidden states at each time step.

The architecture integrates 1-D CNN with BiLSTM layers, thereby exploiting both local feature extraction and temporal dependencies from sequential electrical signals. This framework is specifically designed to classify dynamic patterns that characterize islanding and non-islanding conditions with high temporal precision as shown in Fig. 5.

The basic graphical structure of islanding classification by CNN-BiLSTM model.

The model input is a three-dimensional sequence tensor in size 9 × 1 × 20, where 9 is number of extracted features (channels), 1 is batch dimension, and 20 is time steps in each windowed segment. The network initiates with a 1-D convolutional layer (conv1) that applies 64 filters in size 3 × 9, capturing local temporal patterns across all features. Batch normalization (bn1) and a ReLU activation (relu1) follow to stabilize and introduce nonlinearity, respectively. Subsequent max pooling (pool1) reduces temporal resolution, and dropout (drop1) prevents overfitting. Second convolutional block (conv2), similarly to the conv1, has one more additional convolutional layer for feature expansion with 128 channels filters (5 × 64 pad), normalization (bn2), ReLU (relu2) and then pooling (pool2), dropout (drop2). Convolutional stage 3 (conv3) applies 256 filters in size 7 × 128 for more sophisticated features-in detail normalization (bn3), ReLU (relu3), pooling (pool3) and dropout (drop3) provide output tensor size of 256 × 1 × 2.

The extracted feature sequences are passed through two stacked BiLSTM layers. The first BiLSTM layer (bilstm1) processes the time series bidirectionally, producing a 512-dimensional context-aware sequence output. A dropout layer (drop_bilstm1) is applied to reduce overfitting, followed by a second BiLSTM layer (bilstm2) that reduces the representation to a 256-dimensional output vector. These layers enable the model to retain information from both past and future contexts in the sequence, essential for accurately modeling transient grid behaviors such as those occurring during islanding.

Result flattening and attention mechanism [a fully connected layer (fc_attn) with a tanh activation function and dropout (drop_attn)] is applied to extract final feature sequence (this is how it is handled temporal data). Used here for attention mechanism that test model to learn focus on temporally most informative features before classification. Output layer (FC), finally, followed by softmax activation, classifying an islanding/non islanding state in binary form.

The proposed 1-D BILSTM-CNN framework thus combines hierarchical feature extraction via CNN with sequence modeling via BiLSTM and incorporates an attention mechanism to enhance discriminative learning. The modular and deep structure of this network facilitates the detection of slight anomalies under various power mismatch conditions, including challenging cases with zero power imbalance.

The detection latency of the proposed method is directly governed by the sliding window length used as the model input. In the Matlab/Simulink environment, voltage and current signals are sampled at a fixed step size of ms, corresponding to a sampling frequency of 2 kHz. Each input window spans T=20 consecutive time steps, yielding a window duration of as in (29).

The model processes each incoming window and produces a softmax probability vector for two classes (islanding / non-islanding). A binary detection decision is triggered when predicted islanding probability exceeds a fixed classification threshold of , which corresponds to standard argmax decision rule of softmax output layer. No additional delay filter or confirmation timer is applied. Consequently, minimum achievable detection time equals window duration, ms, provided the islanding event generates a sufficiently discriminative pattern within that window.

A potential concern when employing BiLSTM architectures in protection systems is their reliance on bidirectional processing, which is often interpreted as requiring access to future data. In the proposed framework, this issue is addressed through a segment-level (block-based) processing strategy.

Instead of processing the signal in a fully streaming manner, the model operates on fixed-length windows of T = 20 time steps. Each window is fully collected before inference is performed. At the moment of classification, all samples within the window are already available; therefore, the backward LSTM pass operates only on previously acquired data within the closed window.

From the perspective of the deployed system, no future information beyond the current observation window is required. Thus, the model behaves causally at the system level, while utilizing bidirectional processing internally within each window to enhance temporal feature extraction.

The only latency introduced by this approach corresponds to the window duration which remains well within the 2-second detection requirement defined in IEEE Std 1547–2018. This mechanism is conceptually similar to frame-based processing used in conventional relay protection algorithms, where decisions are made after acquiring a finite data window.

Training configuration and optimization strategy

The training process of the proposed deep learning model was configured using the Adam optimization algorithm to ensure efficient and stable convergence. A bidirectional LSTM structure with 256 hidden units was employed to effectively capture temporal dependencies in the sequential data, while a dropout rate of 0.3 was introduced to reduce the risk of overfitting and improve generalization performance.

The network was trained for a maximum of 250 epochs using a mini-batch size of 8 samples. The initial learning rate was set to 1 × 10− 2, and a piecewise learning rate scheduling strategy was adopted to progressively refine the optimization process. Specifically, the learning rate was multiplied by a factor of 0.3 every 5 epochs, allowing rapid learning during early stages and more precise weight updates in later epochs. In addition, an L2 regularization coefficient of 1 × 10− 4 was applied to further enhance model robustness.

To improve statistical reliability, the training data were randomly shuffled at each epoch. Model validation was performed periodically using the validation dataset at intervals of every 10 iterations. Furthermore, an early stopping criterion was implemented through a custom output function, terminating the training procedure once the target classification accuracy of 99.8% was achieved. All simulations and training operations were executed in a CPU-based computational environment. These training strategies collectively ensure stable convergence while reducing the risk of overfitting and improving generalization capability.

Dataset generation and preprocessing

The datasets used in this study were generated using MATLAB/Simulink-based dynamic simulations performed on the IEC benchmark system and the IEEE 33-bus distribution system. Various operating conditions, including islanding and non-islanding scenarios, were simulated under different load levels and distributed generation operating conditions to improve dataset diversity and model generalization capability.

The IEC benchmark system implementation and certain CNN-based modeling approaches were developed with reference to previously published open-source studies, which are appropriately cited in this manuscript. Performance evaluation

Compared neural architectures

Two different neural network architectures, a baseline LSTM network and a hybrid CNN-LSTM model, are developed and compared to evaluate the effectiveness of temporal and spatiotemporal modeling for ID. Each model is trained using same input data and classification targets to ensure an accurate comparison.

The LSTM-based architecture is designed to capture long-range temporal dependencies in sequential input data. It consists of a single LSTM layer with a reduced number of hidden units to produce a compact representation using the ’last’ output mode, followed by a fully connected layer, a softmax classifier, and a classification output layer. This model is particularly suitable for modeling pure temporal patterns without spatial convolution.

In contrast, CNN-LSTM architecture integrates convolutional layers before LSTM block to extract higher-level spatial features from raw input signals. The network includes three successive 1-D convolutional layers, each followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling. These layers progressively downsample and transform input sequence, enabling network to capture localized temporal patterns. The final convolutional output is then passed to an LSTM layer, which summarizes temporal dynamics of learned features. As in LSTM-only model, output is processed through a fully connected layer, softmax layer, and classification output.

The dataset comprises a total of 1761 scenarios, divided into islanding and non-islanding conditions. Dataset expansion and leakage-free split protocol

Each of the 1761 simulated scenarios produces a continuous multivariate time series of approximately 0.75 s duration with a sampling interval of 0.5 ms. To construct the dataset, the signals are segmented into fixed-length windows of T = 20 time steps. The segmentation is performed such that each raw sample is assigned to only one window, resulting in approximately 75 windowed segments per scenario and a total dataset of approximately 132,000 windows across all scenarios.

To prevent data leakage -a critical concern in time-series analysis- the train/validation/test split is performed exclusively at the scenario level, prior to any windowing operation. Specifically, the 1761 scenarios are partitioned into training (40%, n = 705), validation (30%, n = 528), and test test (30%, n = 528) subsets using stratified random sampling to preserve the islanding/non-islanding class distribution. Windowed segments are then generated independently within each subset. This protocol guarantees that no segment from a test scenario appears in the training or validation sets, even partially, ensuring a leakage-free evaluation. Case studies

Zero power mismatch

In microgrid islanding, the zero power mismatch condition is a scenario immediately after grid disconnection when local generation exactly equals local demand, so that there is virtually no net power flow with the utility46. In this worst-case, voltage and frequency at the PCC change negligibly, so passive islanding detectors (which monitor such parameters) unable to sense transition. The NDZ is defined as the set of load-generation imbalance conditions (active/reactive power mismatch) under which an islanding event may not be detected47,48. Therefore, the zero-mismatch case is at the center of NDZ for many passive methods. This is a critical safety issue. For secure and reliable operation, anti-islanding schemes are designed to minimize NDZ so that even zero mismatches detection.

Mechanistic interpretation of model performance

The observed performance differences between the evaluated architectures can be explained by their ability to capture the temporal structure of islanding transients. Islanding events are characterized by short-duration, non-stationary transients whose exact position within the observation window depends on the switching instant. As a result, an effective detection model must not only capture local transient features but also interpret their temporal context relative to both pre- and post-disturbance signal behavior.

The LSTM-only model processes the input sequence in a unidirectional manner, accumulating temporal information progressively. This structure limits its responsiveness to transient events occurring at arbitrary positions within the input window, as reliable representation can only be formed after the transient has fully propagated through the sequence. This behavior explains its relatively higher misclassification rate.

The CNN-LSTM architecture improves upon this limitation by introducing convolutional layers that extract localized temporal features prior to sequence modeling. These layers act as feature compressors, transforming raw signals into higher-level representations that emphasize transient patterns. As a result, the LSTM receives a more informative input, leading to faster convergence and improved classification accuracy. However, the sequence modeling stage in CNN-LSTM remains unidirectional, which restricts its ability to fully exploit the temporal symmetry inherent in islanding events.

The CNN-BiLSTM model overcomes this limitation by employing bidirectional temporal processing. Within each input window, the forward pass captures the evolution of the signal leading up to the transient, while the backward pass encodes the post-transient settling behavior. This dual perspective enables the model to capture subtle asymmetries between pre- and post-islanding signal patterns, which are particularly critical under low power mismatch conditions.

The attention mechanism further enhances this capability by dynamically assigning higher importance to time steps that contain the most discriminative information. Instead of uniformly processing the entire window, the model selectively focuses on transient segments where islanding signatures are most pronounced.

Overall, the superior performance of the CNN-BiLSTM architecture can be attributed to its ability to jointly model local transient features, bidirectional temporal dependencies, and selective temporal emphasis, all of which align with the physical characteristics of islanding phenomena. This interpretation indicates that the performance gain is structurally grounded rather than purely data-driven.

In microgrids, islanding occurs when the utility grid is disconnected while local distributed generation continues to supply the connected load. The NDZ is defined as the set of operating points for which the islanding detection method fails to detect islanding within the required 2-second limit specified in IEEE Std 1547–2018. In this study, the NDZ is evaluated over a two-dimensional mismatch space defined by and , both with increments of 2%. This results in a total of 861 operating points.

For each operating condition, an islanding event is simulated by opening the grid-side circuit breaker at t=0.32s under the specified mismatch. The resulting time-series data are processed and segmented into non-overlapping windows of 20 samples.

These validated windows are then evaluated using the trained CNN-BiLSTM model, which outputs binary classification results indicating detection success or failure.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel CNN-BiLSTM-based deep learning architecture was developed for accurate and fast detection of islanding events in microgrids. The proposed method achieved a detection time of only 10 ms, demonstrating its suitability for real-time protection applications. The effectiveness of the approach was further validated by an extremely narrow NDZ of just 0.02%, indicating high sensitivity in islanding conditions from normal operation modes.

Performance metrics including accuracy and loss were evaluated comprehensively. The results were visualized through training and testing confusion matrices, highlighting the ability of classifier to generalize well across unseen data. Comparative analysis with previous works confirmed that the proposed method outperforms existing algorithms in terms of detection reliability and speed.

The proposed method is developed and evaluated using the IEC 61850-7-420 simulation test system. While the results demonstrate strong performance, several factors influencing generalization to real-world deployments should be considered.

The generalization capability of the model is primarily supported by the diversity of the training dataset and the use of physically meaningful input features. The dataset includes a wide range of operating conditions, such as varying active and reactive power mismatches, multiple non-islanding disturbance types, and different fault scenarios. This diversity enables the model to learn invariant temporal patterns associated with islanding events rather than system-specific signatures.

In addition, the model predominantly relies on transient current measurements, which are fundamental indicators of islanding behavior across different microgrid configurations. This physics-consistent representation improves the transferability of the learned features to other systems.

The generalization capability is further supported by validation on an independent IEEE 33-bus distribution network, where the model maintains high detection performance under unseen system conditions.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The current dataset is generated under balanced three-phase operating conditions, and therefore does not capture unbalanced loading scenarios or single-phase distributed generation units. Furthermore, the noise robustness analysis considers moderate measurement noise levels, while more severe sensor degradation or failure conditions are not included.

Finally, the study is based on simulation data, and validation using hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) platforms or real measurement systems remains an important step for practical deployment.

In future studies, the introduced model can be validated by testing it with real measurement data (PMU/SCADA) or laboratory-based microgrid hardware (RTDS, CHIL/HIL). In the future, accuracy of the proposed model can be increased by adding multiple features (power, harmonics, dq components, THD, flicker measurements). As part of future work, the proposed method will be further validated on larger and more complex distribution systems, such as the IEEE 69-bus and IEEE 118-bus networks, in order to investigate its scalability and performance under more realistic grid conditions.

Beyond performance improvements, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation framework for islanding detection. The proposed approach integrates model architecture design with physical interpretability and system-level validation. The NDZ-based analysis offers a physics-driven assessment of detection sensitivity, while the feature attribution results reveal that the model decisions are aligned with meaningful transient current signatures. Furthermore, the validation on an independent test system and the discussion of practical deployment aspects demonstrate the robustness and applicability of the proposed method under diverse operating conditions.
