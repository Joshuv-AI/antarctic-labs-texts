Tumor microenvironment-rearranging Nanoassemblies overcome angiogenesis-immunotherapy resistance to potentiate osteosarcoma treatment
Authors: Zili Lin, Qing Liu, Xiangyao Li, Wenlong Wang, Hao Luo, Weiping Xia, Yuhao Yuan, Haibin Zheng, Wei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional anti-angiogenic cancer therapy is frequently undermined by adaptive tumor hypoxia and the consequent establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which together drive therapeutic resistance. To overcome this limitation, we engineered a tumor-targeted nano-platform (Reg@CeO2@HA) that co-delivers low-dose regorafenib with enzymatically versatile cerium oxide nanoparticles. This system concurrently normalizes tumor vasculature, scavenges pathological reactive oxygen species, and alleviates hypoxia—collectively reprogramming the immunosuppressive tumor landscape. Mechanistically, the platform could downregulate PD-L1 expression, reduce infiltration of M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages, and attenuate myeloid-derived suppressor cell-mediated T-cell exhaustion. Furthermore, it could induce immunogenic cell death, thereby priming a systemic anti-tumor immune response. In combination with PD-L1 blockade, Reg@CeO2@HA could elicit potent synergistic efficacy, marked by robust CD8+ T-cell infiltration and profound tumor suppression. Taken together, the present study established a novel therapeutic paradigm that concurrently addresses vascular abnormality and immune dysfunction within the TME. This integrated nano-strategy could not only overcome the key limitations of conventional anti-angiogenic therapy but also provide a versatile and potent approach to sensitize osteosarcoma and other immunologically cold solid tumors to immunotherapy.Keywords: Anti-angiogenesis, Immunotherapy, Hypoxia mitigation, ROS clearance, Osteosarcoma

Introduction

Tumor angiogenesis, a canonical cancer hallmark, can drive osteosarcoma (OS) progression, and anti-angiogenic agents such as regorafenib (Reg) have been clinically deployed as second-line therapy [[1], [2], [3]]. However, mounting evidence reveals that anti-angiogenic monotherapy often fails to improve patient survival and may inadvertently promote tumor adaptation, including enhanced drug resistance, invasion, metastasis, and immunotherapy resistance, due to the induction of a hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) [4,5]. Hypoxia recruits and activates immunosuppressive myeloid populations, notably myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which undermine immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and accelerate disease progression [6,7]. Mirroring these observations, the present study demonstrates that Reg, while exerting measurable antitumor and immunomodulatory effects, simultaneously enhances MDSCs intratumoral accumulation. This mechanistic insight helps explain the limited survival benefit observed in clinical trials of Reg in OS [3,8], and underscores an urgent need for combinatorial or nanotechnology-enabled strategies to overcome the inherent limitations of anti-angiogenic monotherapy.

Immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative approach in oncology, with tumor immunoreactivity now recognized as a defining hallmark of cancer that influences disease progression and, in some contexts, determines therapeutic response and patient survival [[9], [10], [11]]. However, immunotherapies for OS fail to obtain certain breakthrough progresses and remain to be further investigated [12,13]. Multi-omics studies consistently reveal that the OS TME is immunologically hyporesponsive, dominated by immunosuppressive cell populations such as M2-polarized TAMs and MDSCs [14,15]. Extensively accepted, MDSCs and M2-polarized TAMs, two major immunosuppressive components within TME, can exert formidable immunosuppressive effects including the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, consumption of nutrients, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induction of T cells exhaustion, leading to the formation of immunosuppressive TME and resistance to ICB therapies [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Consequently, reprogramming the OS immune landscape, particularly by attenuating M2-polarized TAMs and MDSCs activity, represents a compelling strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance [15,[20], [21], [22]]. Notably, our study demonstrated that while Reg could promote macrophages reprogramming and mitigate MDSCs-mediated T cell exhaustion in vitro, its administration in vivo conversely enhanced MDSCs recruitment. This paradoxical effect underscored both the complexity of the OS TME and the critical need for rationally designed therapeutic combinations or nanotechnology-enabled strategies to optimize the immunomodulatory potential of anti-angiogenic agents [4,23].

Cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles (NPs) represent a versatile class of redox-active nanomaterials, exhibiting intrinsic enzyme-mimetic activities, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and oxidase functions, which underpin their broad utility in biomedical applications ranging from anti-inflammatory and antibacterial therapies to immune modulation and anticancer strategies [[24], [25], [26]]. Recently, regulating pathological angiogenesis, especially tumor angiogenesis, has been identified as one of the most significant medical applications of CeO2 NPs [24,26,27]. Unlike conventional anti-angiogenic agents, CeO2 NPs modulate tumor vasculature not only through interference with VEGF signaling but also via ROS scavenging and oxygen release, enabled by the reversible redox switching between Ce3+ and Ce4+ states [[26], [27], [28]], which allow them to mitigate hypoxia and acidosis, key drivers of drug resistance, metastasis, and immunosuppression within TME. Moreover, CeO2 NPs can selectively induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) in cancer cells, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity and potentiating response to immunotherapy [29,30]. Concurrently, through ROS elimination and hypoxia alleviation, they remodel the phenotype and function of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, such as TAMs and MDSCs [7,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]], favoring an immune-permissive TME. Collectively, these multifunctional attributes position CeO2 NPs not only as potent therapeutic agents but also as promising delivery platforms for next-generation cancer therapies.

Herein, to overcome the limitations of Reg monotherapy and amplify its vascular-regulating and immunomodulatory potential, we engineered a tumor-targeted nanoplatform, Reg@CeO2@HA, based on hollow CeO2 NPs functionalized with hyaluronic acid (HA). This design leveraged the complementary actions of low-dose Reg and enzymatically active CeO2 NPs to cooperatively drive tumor vascular normalization, thereby suppressing tumor growth and reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME. Within this system, Reg exerted direct immunostimulatory effects, including downregulation of PD-L1 expression, reprogramming of TAMs, and mitigation of MDSC-mediated T cells exhaustion. Simultaneously, CeO2 NPs could scavenge pathological ROS and alleviate hypoxia, thereby attenuating M2 polarization of TAMs and limiting the recruitment and activation of MDSCs. Furthermore, the nanoplatform could trigger cancer cells ICD, releasing danger signals that prime adaptive anti-tumor immunity. Capitalizing on this multifaceted immune remodeling, we combined Reg@CeO2@HA NPs with PD-L1 blockade, observing potent synergistic efficacy characterized by enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and tumor control. In summary, anchored in the hallmarks of cancer, we have developed an integrated nanosystem that remodels OS TME through coordinated vascular normalization, hypoxia mitigation, ROS clearance, and immune activation. This approach not only overcomes the drawbacks of conventional anti-angiogenic therapy but also establishes a versatile platform for enhancing immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Results

Reg exerts anti-angiogenic and immunoactivated roles under OS circumstance

Mounting evidence suggests that monotherapy targeting angiogenesis often fails to achieve optimal outcomes in cancer and may inadvertently promote tumor resistance and metastatic spread [4,23]. To evaluate the relative importance of angiogenesis and immune activity in OS, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis of the TARGET-OS cohort. Using a curated set of 43 core human tumor angiogenesis signatures [37], ConsensusClusterPlus classified patients into two angiogenic subgroups (C1 and C2) (Fig. S1A–C). Although patients in C2 showed a trend toward better survival than those in C1, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.20) (Fig. S1D), indicating that angiogenesis alone may not serve as a strong independent prognostic factor in OS. In contrast, immune activity emerged as a potent predictor of outcome. Stratifying patients by immune score (calculated via the ESTIMATE algorithm) revealed that the high immune-score group had significantly better overall survival than the low-score group [p = 6.2 × 10-3, HR = 0.34, 95% CI (0.15-0.76)] (Fig. 1A), supporting the prognostic relevance of the tumor immune microenvironment. Interestingly, the angiogenic subtype C2, which trended toward better survival, also exhibited a higher immune score than C1 (Fig. S1E-F). Moreover, angiogenesis-related scores correlated significantly with multiple immune activity metrics, including NK cell recruitment (R = 0.445, P = 2.188 × 10-5), macrophage recruitment (R = 0.416, P = 8.136 × 10-5), cancer antigen presentation (R = 0.388, P = 0.00026), and expression of immune modulators such as EDNRB (R = 0.561, P = 2.8 × 10-8) and HAVCR2 (R = 0.401, P = 0.00016) (Fig. 1B). Collectively, these findings suggest that while anti-angiogenic monotherapy may have limited efficacy, immune activation was strongly linked to favorable prognosis in OS. The observed correlation between angiogenic and immune signatures further supports the emerging paradigm that combining anti-angiogenic agents with immunotherapies may offer a more effective strategy for improving outcomes in OS patients.

Reg has been established as a second-line anti-angiogenic agent for OS per NCCN guidelines [38]; however, its specific role and mechanisms in OS remain incompletely understood (Fig. S2A). In the present study, we confirmed the anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory roles of Reg in both vitro and vivo OS models, which might contribute to the therapeutic improvement of Reg. First, we determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of Reg in human and murine OS cell lines and HUVECs, yielding values of 98.41 μM (MG63), 18.87 μM (143B), 164.0 μM (HOS), 213.3 μM (U2OS), 24.9 μM (SAOS2), 138.9 μM (K7M2), and 61.83 μM (HUVECs) (Fig. S2B). To isolate specific anti-angiogenic effects from direct cytotoxicity, subsequent functional assays were conducted using a sub-cytotoxic concentration range (0-15 μM), which lies below the IC50 for all tested cell types. Using tube formation, CCK-8, EdU, and transwell assays, we demonstrated that Reg dose-dependently inhibited angiogenesis, proliferation, and migration of HUVECs (Fig. S2C, Fig. S3A-E). These findings indicated that Reg could suppress OS-associated endothelial activity at concentrations that did not induce overt cell death, highlighting a direct and specific effect on vascular function. Consistent with the in vitro data, in vivo administration of Reg significantly suppressed tumor growth, reducing final tumor volume by 50% compared to the control group, without evidence of systemic toxicity as assessed by body weight and histology of major organs (Fig. 1C–E, Fig. S4). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells revealed that Reg remodeled OS TME. While it did not promote M1-polarized macrophage polarization, it effectively suppressed the M2 phenotype (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, Reg treatment enhanced the infiltration of both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and promoted DCs maturation, collectively indicating heightened anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 1G–H). Notably, however, we observed a concomitant and marked increase (1.74-fold) in MDSCs infiltration within Reg-treated tumors (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, Ki67 staining confirmed that Reg significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation, while Tunel staining verified its pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 1J). Reflecting its anti-angiogenic mechanism, CD31 staining showed a pronounced decrease in microvessel density (Fig. 1J). Importantly, this vascular reduction was associated with a marked upregulation of HIF1α, indicating the induction of tumor hypoxia following treatment (Fig. 1J). Accumulating evidence indicates that TME hypoxia could stabilize HIF-1α, which in turn directly drives the transcription of multiple chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL5, CCL2, and SDF-1α), thereby efficiently recruiting MDSCs to the tumor site via the CXCR2/CXCR4 pathways [39]. Concurrently, HIF-1α upregulates effector molecules on MDSCs, such as PD-L1 and Arg1, potentiating their immunosuppressive functions and establishing a hypoxia-driven positive feedback loop of MDSCs recruitment and activation [40,41]. Therefore, tumor hypoxia resulting from the potent anti-angiogenic effects of Reg may represent an underlying cause of both MDSCs recruitment in TME and the limited antitumor efficacy observed.

To delineate the direct immunomodulatory effects of Reg independent of its impact on the tumor vasculature, we performed a series of in vitro analyses. Transcriptomic profiling of K7M2 OS cells treated with a sub-cytotoxic dose of Reg (5 μM) revealed 1539 upregulated and 1324 downregulated genes (Fig. S5A and B). Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA) of upregulated genes showed significant enrichment for immune-activatory pathways, including positive regulation of natural killer cell proliferation, T cell receptor binding, positive regulation of T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, positive regulation of TRAIL production, and CD8-positive alpha-beta T cell activation (Fig. S5C and D). Consistent with this immunostimulatory transcriptional signature, Reg treatment concentration-dependently downregulated PD-L1 surface expression on OS cells, as confirmed by IF and flow cytometry (Fig. S6A–E). A greater than 50% reduction was quantified in K7M2 cells at 5 μM (Fig. S6B and C), with a similar effect observed in the drug-resistant U2OS line (Fig. S6D and E), indicating a direct reprogramming of the immunogenic phenotype in tumor cells. We next assessed the direct effects of Reg on immune cells. In vitro, Reg potently inhibited M2 polarization of macrophages without altering M1 polarization, aligning with our in vivo findings (Fig. S7A–C). Furthermore, in functional co-culture assays, Reg treatment dose-dependently reversed the ability of MDSCs to suppress the proliferation and activation of CD8+ T cells (Figs. 8–9). Collectively, these in vitro data demonstrated that Reg possessed intrinsic, direct immunomodulatory properties. It could promote an immune-favorable transcriptome in tumor cells, suppress an immunosuppressive checkpoint (PD-L1), inhibit protumorigenic macrophage polarization, and blunt the T cell-suppressive function of MDSCs. The stark contrast between this direct MDSCs-inhibitory activity in vitro and the observed accumulation of MDSCs in Reg-treated tumors in vivo strongly implied that the hypoxic TME induced by anti-angiogenic therapy might paradoxically foster MDSCs recruitment or survival, thereby offsetting the drug's direct immunotherapeutic benefits.

Synthesis, characterization, and cell uptake of NPs

To overcome the limitations of anti-angiogenic monotherapy and to enhance the therapeutic efficacy and pharmacokinetics of Reg, we engineered a multifunctional NP platform. The design employed CeO2 NPs as a core scaffold for drug loading, which are conjugated with the near-infrared fluorophore CY5.5 for tracking and further functionalized with a HA shell to confer active tumor-targeting capability [42] (Fig. 2A). The final construct, designated Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5, achieved a drug encapsulation efficiency of 46.12% ± 3.42% and a loading capacity of 10.34% ± 2.31 wt%. This design integrated distinct therapeutic actions: the Reg payload and the CeO2 scaffold each independently modulate pathological angiogenesis and the immunosuppressive TME, while the CeO2 framework additionally provides ROS-scavenging and oxygen-generating capacities that was hypothesized to mitigate tumor hypoxia, a key resistance mechanism to vascular-targeting agents.

The resulting formulation was a homogeneous, pale blue aqueous suspension that significantly enhanced the aqueous solubility of Reg (Fig. 2B–C), a critical improvement given that poor solubility has been a major limiting factor for its clinical efficacy [43,44]. TEM revealed spherical, monodisperse CeO2 cores of ∼90 nm with a mesoporous structure, and a visible outer coating on Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 NPs confirmed successful HA encapsulation (Fig. 2D–E). DLS demonstrated a stepwise increase in hydrodynamic diameter from 109.8 nm (CeO2 NPs, PDI = 0.051) to 150.5 nm (Reg@CeO2, PDI = 0.186), 191.8 nm (Reg@CeO2-CY5.5, PDI = 0.142), and finally 513 nm for the complete HA-coated construct (PDI = 0.207), indicating controlled assembly and colloidal stability (Fig. 2F–G). Zeta potential values systematically shifted from +41.1 mV (CeO2) to +32.9 mV (Reg@CeO2), −14.1 mV (Reg@CeO2-CY5.5), and −2.72 mV (Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5), corroborating sequential surface modification (Fig. 2H). Elemental mapping confirmed co-localization of cerium (from CeO2) and fluorine (a marker of Reg), verifying successful drug loading (Fig. 2I–J). FTIR spectroscopy showed sequential spectral changes with each functionalization step, providing direct evidence of the stepwise chemical modification (Fig. 2K). Optical characterization confirmed conjugation of CY5.5, with a strong absorption peak at 674 nm and a corresponding fluorescence emission at ∼650 nm (Fig. 2L–M). Additionally, XPS analysis was performed to characterize the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio of Reg@CeO2@HA NPs under different pH conditions mimicking TME. As shown in Figure S10, the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio decreases progressively with decreasing pH: from 1.68 at pH 7.4 to 0.61 at pH 6.5, and further to 0.03 at pH 5.5, the trend of which clearly demonstrated that the Reg@CeO2@HA NPs exhibited a pH-responsive shift toward a higher Ce3+ fraction under acidic TME conditions. This increased Ce3+ proportion is known to favor oxygen vacancy formation and ROS scavenging activity, while the accompanying Ce4+→Ce3+ reduction can also contribute to oxygen release, providing direct evidence for the TME-responsive redox behavior of the NPs. Furthermore, in vitro drug release studies demonstrated a pronounced pH-responsive profile: approximately 56% of the loaded Reg was released within 48 h at physiological pH (7.4), whereas 67.79% and 87.91% were released under acidic conditions at pH 6.5 and pH 5.5, respectively, supporting the potential for TME-triggered drug delivery and efficient lysosomal release (Fig. 2N). To obtain direct evidence of degradation, we further incubated the NPs at pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 for 48 h and characterized their morphology and Ce release. TEM imaging revealed that at pH 7.4 the particles retained a smooth and intact spherical morphology, whereas at pH 5.5 they exhibited obvious surface roughening and dispersion, indicating structural degradation without complete fragmentation (Fig. S11A). Concurrently, ICP-MS measurement of Ce ion concentration in the supernatant showed only a slight increase at pH 7.4 over 48 h, but a marked and significant elevation at pH 5.5, confirming active dissolution of the CeO2 core under acidic conditions (Fig. S11B). These results provide direct evidence for the pH-responsive degradation of the nanocomposite, which correlates well with the pH-dependent Ce3+/Ce4+ shift and the accelerated drug release at low pH. Most importantly, the NPs exhibit excellent colloidal stability in both PBS and serum-containing media, as evidenced by minimal size variation and no detectable aggregation over 48 h (Fig. 2O).

To evaluate the targeting capability of the HA-functionalized NPs, we first examined the expression of CD44, the principal receptor for HA, in OS tissues. IHC staining revealed markedly higher CD44 expression in OS specimens compared with matched adjacent non-tumor tissues, providing a rationale for HA-mediated active targeting [42] (Fig. S12). We next assessed the cellular uptake of NPs in vitro. HUVECs and K7M2 OS cells were treated with either non-targeted Reg@CeO2-CY5.5 NPs or HA-coated Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 NPs. Confocal microscopy demonstrated robust cellular internalization of both formulations, while the HA-coated NPs exhibited substantially enhanced uptake in both cell types, confirming that HA decoration improves targeting to endothelial and OS cells (Fig. S13A and B). To quantify uptake kinetics, we performed flow cytometry after treating cells with Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 NPs for 0 to 4 h. Uptake increased time-dependently in all cell lines tested (Fig. S14A and B). At 4 h, the mean fluorescence intensity was 28.3-fold higher in HUVECs and 33.7-fold higher in K7M2 cells relative to the 0 h control (Fig. S14A and B). Notably, even greater enhancements were observed in the OS lines U2OS (66.3-fold) and 143B (68.5-fold) (Fig. S14A and B). These results collectively demonstrate efficient and HA-dependent cellular internalization of the NPs across endothelial and tumor cell populations.

NPs could strongly inhibit angiogenesis and induce cancer cell ICD

The functional impacts of Reg@CeO2@HA NPs on endothelial and OS cells were investigated, which focused on their anti-angiogenic capacity and ability to induce cancer cells ICD. First, the IC50 of Reg@CeO2@HA in HUVECs was determined to be 35.73 μg/mL (Fig. S15). Accordingly, a concentration of 30 μg/mL Reg@CeO2@HA was selected for the tube-formation assay in HUVECs and for all subsequent experiments. In the tube-formation assay using HUVECs, all treatment groups inhibited vascular network assembly compared to the control. While Reg or CeO2 alone caused mild-to-moderate suppression and CeO2@HA showed a stronger effect, Reg@CeO2 and Reg@CeO2@HA exhibited the most potent inhibition, indicating a synergistic anti-angiogenic effect between Reg and the CeO2 scaffold (Fig. 3A). Given prior evidence that both Reg and CeO2 NPs can trigger ICD [29,30,[45], [46], [47]], we systematically assessed ICD hallmarks in OS cells following NP treatment. In K7M2 cells, IF and flow cytometry for surface CRT revealed that Reg@CeO2@HA induced the strongest CRT exposure, with an 8.22-fold increase over the control (Fig. 3B; D). A similar trend was observed in U2OS cells (Fig. S16A and B). Concurrently, a marked redistribution of HMGB1 was detected: nuclear HMGB1 decreased significantly, while ELISA confirmed its elevated extracellular release (Fig. 3C; E; Fig. S16C-D). In K7M2 cells, extracellular HMGB1 levels rose to 1.42-fold (Reg@CeO2) and 1.63-fold (Reg@CeO2@HA) relative to the control (Fig. 3C; E). In U2OS cells, the increases were more pronounced: 1.73-fold and 2.10-fold, respectively (Fig. S16C and D). Moreover, ATP secretion, a key “eat-me” signal, was substantially enhanced, reaching 30.8-fold (K7M2) and 14.1-fold (U2OS) in the Reg@CeO2@HA group compared to untreated cells (Fig. S3F; S16E). Finally, to further validate the ICD-inducing effects of Reg@CeO2@HA NPs, the maturation status of mouse DCs were evaluated after co-culture with K7M2 cells that had been treated with different formulations. The expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II on DCs was measured by flow cytometry. Compared with control groups, a significantly elevated expression level of MHC-II was also observed in Reg@CeO2@HA-treated group (Fig. 3G; I). Furthermore, treatment with the NPs, especially Reg@CeO2@HA, markedly increased the percentage of CD80+CD86+ double-positive DCs (Fig. 3H; J). These results demonstrated that the NPs could effectively induce OS cells ICD, thereby promoting DCs activation. Collectively, these data demonstrated that Reg@CeO2@HA NPs not only exerted potent anti-angiogenic activity but also functioned as a robust immunogenic stimulus, triggering multiple ICD hallmarks and thereby potentially converting tumor cells into an in situ vaccine that could prime antitumor immunity.

NPs could reprogram immunometabolism via alleviating hypoxia

Hypoxia, a well-established hallmark of TME, is a key driver of tumor progression and immune evasion [33,48]. To investigate the impact of hypoxia on tumor cells, we first analyzed the TARGET-OS cohort and identified a statistically significant positive correlation between HIF1A and PD-L1 expression (p = 0.01, R = 0.28), suggesting hypoxia might upregulate this immune checkpoint in tumor cells (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we evaluated whether the oxygen-releasing capability of NPs could influence tumor cell PD-L1 expression by modulating cellular oxygen metabolism. WB analysis demonstrated that under hypoxic conditions, NP treatment markedly reduced the expression of both HIF1A and PD-L1 in K7M2 cells compared with the control group, indicating that NPs can regulate immune checkpoint expression through interference with the oxygen metabolism of cancer cells (Fig. 4B). This finding was further corroborated by flow cytometry, which showed a significant reduction in PD-L1 expression on K7M2 cells under hypoxia following NPs treatment (Fig. 4C–D). Specifically, Reg@CeO2 NPs decreased PD-L1 levels by 2.95-fold relative to the control, while Reg@CeO2@HA NPs exhibited the most pronounced effect, achieving a 3.27-fold reduction (Fig. 4C–D). Consistent results were obtained via IF staining, and the same phenomenon was validated in U2OS cells (Fig. 4E, S17A–D). Collectively, these data demonstrated that the oxygen-releasing property of NPs could modulate the expression of immune checkpoints in tumor cells by interfering with their oxygen metabolism.

Therapeutic modalities such as antiangiogenic and photothermal therapies often exacerbate intratumoral hypoxia, which in turn recruits and activates immunosuppressive myeloid cells, including MDSCs and TAMs, thereby fostering immune resistance [4,33]. Notably, analysis of OS TME reveals a pronounced infiltration of immunosuppressive myeloid cells [12,15]. Given this, we asked whether the oxygen-releasing capacity of our NPs could counteract this process by alleviating hypoxia and thereby reprogramming the immunophenotype of these myeloid cells. Initially, IC50 of HA@Reg@CeO2 was determined in BMDMs and MDSCs, yielding values of 50.73 μg/mL and 68.54 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. S18). Notably, both IC50 values were substantially higher than the working concentration used in our experiments, indicating that the effects of HA@Reg@CeO2 on BMDMs and MDSCs are not attributable to direct cytotoxicity toward these cell types. WB analysis showed that hypoxic treatment could upregulate Arg1 expression in BMDMs, while NPs treatment reversed this effect, suggesting that NPs could mitigate hypoxia and attenuate the immunosuppressive phenotype of BMDMs (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, flow cytometry was used to examine CD206 expression across treatment groups (Fig. 4G). The results similarly demonstrated that NPs counteract hypoxia-induced M2-polarization of BMDMs. In particular, the CD206 expression levels in the Reg@CeO2 and Reg@CeO2@HA NPs-treated groups were reduced to approximately 10% of those in the hypoxic negative control group (Fig. 4G). Consistent with these findings, qPCR analysis of M2 polarization markers, such as Chil3, Retnla, and Arg1, revealed that hypoxic treatment significantly increased the expression of M2 markers in BMDMs, whereas NPs treatment reversed this trend (Fig. 4H). Therefore, these results supported the conclusion that NPs could suppress the immunosuppressive phenotype of macrophages by alleviating cellular hypoxia.

Parallel to our studies on macrophages, we also explored the role of NPs in modulating the phenotype and function of hypoxia-induced MDSCs. First, we assessed their impact on key functional proteins in MDSCs. WB analysis revealed that hypoxia significantly upregulated the expression of iNOS and Arg1 in MDSCs, whereas NPs treatment substantially attenuated this induction (Fig. 4I). Subsequently, we evaluated the immunomodulatory influence of NPs on MDSCs using a T cell co-culture assay (Fig. 4J). When MDSCs preconditioned under hypoxia were co-cultured with T cells, they strongly suppressed both T cell proliferation and activation, with inhibition of activation being particularly pronounced (Fig. 4K–N). In contrast, hypoxia-exposed MDSCs that had been treated with NPs not only reversed the suppression of T cell proliferation but also partially restored—and in some cases even enhanced—T cell activation (Fig. 4K–N). Therefore, these findings indicated that NPs could alleviate the hypoxia-driven immunosuppressive phenotype of MDSCs, suggesting a potential strategy to improve the effectiveness of immunotherapeutic approaches.

NPs could attenuate myeloid immunosuppression via ROS scavenging

ROS imbalance has been known to reinforce TME immunosuppression, both by enhancing the function of MDSCs and by driving M2-polarization of TAMs [31,49,50]. Leveraging the intrinsic ROS-scavenging capacity of our CeO2-based NPs, we investigated whether this property could further amplify their immunomodulatory efficacy. We first measured intracellular ROS levels in BMDMs under IL-4-induced M2 polarization. IF imaging and quantitative flow cytometry revealed that NPs treatment robustly suppressed IL-4-triggered ROS accumulation (Fig. 5A–C). All CeO2-containing NPs reduced ROS to nearly 25% of the level observed in control cells, confirming potent antioxidative activity (Fig. 5B–C). At the molecular level, WB analysis showed that IL-4 markedly upregulated Arg1 expression in BMDMs and this induction was effectively reversed by NPs treatment (Fig. 5D). Notably, Reg@CeO2@HA NPs performed comparably to a classical ROS scavenger, suggesting that their ability to neutralize ROS underlies the observed phenotypic reprogramming (Fig. 5D). IF staining of the M2 marker CD206 provided further evidence: Reg@CeO2@HA NPs treatment reduced CD206 signal intensity to approximately 7.1% of that in untreated controls, outperforming even the exogenous scavenger (Fig. 5E). Consistent with these findings, qPCR analysis confirmed the downregulation of M2-associated genes, such as Arg1, Chil3, Retnla, following NPs exposure (Fig. 5F). Together, these results demonstrated that NPs could redirect TAMs away from an immunosuppressive phenotype via effective ROS clearance.

Subsequently, we examined whether NPs could similarly modulate ROS in MDSCs. ROS detection assays demonstrated that NPs substantially lowered intracellular ROS levels in MDSCs, with Reg@CeO2@HA NPs achieving a 25-fold reduction relative to controls, underscoring their strong antioxidative capacity in these cells (Fig. 5G). WB analysis further showed that Reg@CeO2@HA NPs reduced the expression of iNOS and Arg1 in MDSCs, to an extent similar to that of the ROS scavenger (Fig. 5J). Furthermore, we evaluated the functional impact on T cells using a co-culture system (Fig. 5I). Compared with the control, all NPs treatment groups enhanced T-cell proliferation and activation to varying degrees (Fig. 5K–L, Figs. 19–20). In particular, Reg@CeO2 and Reg@CeO2@HA NPs promoted T-cell proliferation as effectively as the ROS scavenger, though their effect on T-cell activation was slightly less pronounced (Fig. 5K–L, Figs. 19–20). In summary, these results demonstrate that NPs leverage their potent ROS-scavenging capability to reprogram both TAMs and MDSCs, thereby attenuating TME immunosuppression and potentially overcoming resistance to immunotherapy.

In vivo biodistribution and tumor-suppressing effect of NPs

Effective intratumoral accumulation is crucial for drug efficacy and limiting systemic toxicity. Leveraging the CD44-targeting property of HA and the relatively high expression of CD44 in tumors [42], we employed time-series in vivo imaging to assess whether HA modification could enhance the tumor-targeting capability of NPs. Mice were intravenously injected with either Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 or unmodified Reg@CeO2-CY5.5 NPs, and their biodistribution was tracked over time. Both NP types accumulated substantially at tumor sites (Fig. 6A). Notably, however, Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 NPs exhibited superior tumor-targeting specificity (Fig. 6A). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of tumors and major organs harvested at the final time point confirmed this observation: while Reg@CeO2-CY5.5 NPs showed high tumor accumulation, strong fluorescence signals—comparable to or exceeding those in tumors—were also detected in off-target organs, particularly the intestines and liver (Fig. 6B). In contrast, Reg@CeO2@HA-CY5.5 NPs demonstrated markedly higher accumulation in tumors than in other vital organs (Fig. 6B). These results collectively indicated that HA functionalization of NPs could enhance their tumor-targeting precision, which was essential for improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing adverse effects in vivo.

Subsequently, we evaluated the tumor-suppressing efficacy of the NPs using the K7M2-bearing OS model (Fig. 6C). Measurements of tumor volume revealed that all treatment groups suppressed OS growth to varying degrees compared with the control group (Fig. 6D–E). The Reg@CeO2@HA NP group showed the most pronounced inhibitory effect, with the average tumor volume at the endpoint being only 21.8% of that in the PBS-treated group (Fig. 6E). Moreover, safety assessments, including body weight monitoring, serum biochemistry, complete blood count, and H&E staining of major organs, revealed no significant signs of toxicity, such as structural organ damage, aberrant hematological parameters, or acute body weight loss (Fig. 6F, S21–23). Data across treatment groups showed no statistically significant differences, indicating favorable tolerability and biosafety of the NPs (Fig. 6F, S21–23). Further mechanistic insight was gained through histological analysis of tumor sections. H&E staining confirmed typical OS histopathological features, including nuclear atypia and osteoid deposition (Fig. 6G). IHC against Ki67 revealed varying degrees of proliferation suppression across treatment groups, with the most substantial reduction observed in the Reg@CeO2@HA group (Fig. 6G). Conversely, Tunel staining indicated a corresponding increase in apoptotic cells, most evident in the Reg@CeO2@HA-treated tumors (Fig. 6G). In summary, our engineered NPs could potently inhibit tumor proliferation and promote apoptosis in vivo, thereby suppressing tumor progression within a well-tolerated and safe therapeutic window.

Mechanistic investigation into NPs-mediated tumor-suppressing effects in vivo

Building on our in vitro findings that demonstrated the multifaceted functionalities of NPs, including vascular modulation, oxygen release, ROS scavenging, and immunoregulatory activity, we sought to determine whether these properties contribute to their observed antitumor efficacy in vivo. First, IF staining for the vascular marker CD31 and the pericyte marker α-SMA revealed that tumors in the PBS-treated group contained abundant but disorganized blood vessels with poor pericyte coverage, consistent with a pathological, pro-tumorigenic vasculature (Fig. 7A). In contrast, treatment with the NPs, particularly Reg@CeO2@HA NPs, substantially reduced vascular density and enhanced CD31/α-SMA co-localization, indicating promotion of tumor vessel normalization (Fig. 7A). As vascular normalization has been known to alleviate local hypoxia [4,51,52], we next assessed hypoxia levels via HIF-1α and hypoxyprobe staining. While PBS-treated tumors exhibited pronounced hypoxia, the Reg@CeO2@HA group showed marked alleviation of hypoxic stress, supporting the role of NPs in improving tumor oxygenation (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, given that elevated ROS within TME could impair T-cell function and enhance immunosuppressive myeloid cells suppressor cell activity [50], thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu, we evaluated intratumoral ROS levels using DHE staining on frozen sections. PBS-treated tumors displayed high ROS accumulation, whereas NP treatments, most notably Reg@CeO2@HA-treated group, significantly reduced ROS levels (Fig. 7A). These findings confirmed that the NPs could induce tumor vascular normalization, mitigate hypoxia, and lower pathological ROS levels, collectively contributing to a remodeled TME conducive to antitumor responses.

The normalization of tumor vasculature, alleviation of hypoxia, and reduction of pathological ROS levels collectively favor TME remodeling and the activation of anticancer immunity [4,33,48,[52], [53], [54]]. In addition, given our prior in vitro evidence that NPs could modulate immune checkpoint expression and immune cell function, we proceeded to evaluate changes in the immune landscape following NP treatment in vivo. IF staining for PD-L1 confirmed that NPs treatments significantly reduced PD-L1 expression in tumors compared with the PBS group, with the most pronounced decrease observed in the Reg@CeO2 and Reg@CeO2@HA groups (Fig. S24A). Flow cytometric analysis further delineated shifts in immune cell populations. For TAMs, all NP-treated groups exhibited a marked reduction in M2-polarized TAMs compared with PBS controls, with the Reg@CeO2@HA group showing the greatest suppression (M2-polarized TAMs abundance decreased to 48.5% of the PBS group) (Fig. 7B; F). Although M1-polarized TAMs showed a modest increase in NPs-treated groups, the changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 7C; G). These results suggested that the NPs primarily reshape TAMs phenotype by suppressing M2 polarization, thereby attenuating their immunosuppressive function. Given the critical role of T cells in antitumor immunity, we assessed T cell infiltration. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell numbers were elevated in all NPs-treated groups relative to the PBS control, with the Reg@CeO2@HA group showing the most substantial increase (CD4+ T cells: 4.32-fold; CD8+ T cells: 13.4-fold) (Fig. 7D–E; H-I). These findings indicated that the NPs could enhance antitumor activity by promoting the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with TME. Effective antigen presentation is essential for initiating adaptive immune responses [55,56]. We therefore evaluated DCs maturation and observed a consistent increase in mature DCs across NPs-treated groups, most notably in the Reg@CeO2@HA group, where mature DCs increased by 2.74-fold compared with the PBS group (Fig. S24B and C). These findings suggested that Reg@CeO2@HA treatment could promote DC maturation and enhance antigen-presenting capacity within tumors. Finally, we examined the impact of NPs on MDSCs. No significant differences in MDSCs infiltration were detected among the treatment groups, indicating that the vascular-modulating effects of the NPs did not inadvertently recruit MDSCs to exacerbate immunosuppression (Fig. S24D and E). In summary, our findings demonstrated that NPs could inhibit tumor progression in vivo by inducing vascular normalization, relieving hypoxia, scavenging pathological ROS, and activating a multifaceted antitumor immune response.

NPs as amplifiers of PD-L1 blockade for potent antitumor immunity

Abnormal tumor vessels physically block T cell entry via reduced ICAM-1/VCAM-1 and actively suppress T cells through endothelial PD-L1/FasL while vascular normalization restores pericyte coverage, alleviates hypoxia, and re-expresses adhesion molecules, enabling T cell extravasation [57,58]. Vascular normalization synergy with PD-L1 blockade operates through three axes: (i) spatial-converting “cold” tumors into “hot” ones; (ii) functional-lowering endothelial PD-L1 by reducing HIF-1α; (iii) metabolic-hypoxia relief downregulates PD-1 on T cells and PD-L1 on tumor/MDSCs [57,58]. Moreover, PD-1 blockade itself further normalizes vessels via CD4+ T cell-derived IFNγ, creating a virtuous bidirectional cycle [57,58]. At present, the therapeutic potential of immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-L1/PD-1 agents, in OS remains limited, underscoring the need for effective combination strategies [12,15]. We therefore investigated whether combining our Reg@CeO2@HA NPs with anti-PD-L1 therapy could enhance antitumor efficacy (Fig. 8A). Treatment with either agent alone significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to PBS controls, but the combination regimen proved markedly superior (Fig. 8B–C). By the study endpoint, the average tumor volume in the combination group was only 56.6% of that in the Reg@CeO2@HA monotherapy group, 34.4% of the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group, and 26.5% of the PBS control group, demonstrating potent combinatorial activity (Fig. 8C). Flow cytometry analysis of TME revealed distinct immunomodulatory effects. Both monotherapies and the combination reduced M2-polarized macrophages compared to PBS, with the combination further enhancing this suppression relative to anti-PD-L1 alone (Fig. 8E; K). No statistically significant changes were observed in M1 macrophage polarization across groups (Fig. 8F; L). While the combination increased CD4+ T cell infiltration over Reg@CeO2@HA monotherapy, its effect was not significantly different from anti-PD-L1 alone (Fig. 8G; M). Strikingly, the combination treatment substantially boosted CD8+ T cell recruitment, achieving levels 1.59-fold higher than Reg@CeO2@HA alone, 2.09-fold higher than anti-PD-L1 alone, and 6.82-fold higher than the PBS control (Fig. 8H; N). No further modulation of DCs maturation or MDSCs infiltration was observed beyond the effects of the individual agents (Fig. 8I–J; O-P). Furthermore, safety assessments, including body weight monitoring, haematological and biochemical parameters, and histopathological examination of major organs, revealed no significant signs of toxicity, indicating favorable tolerability of all treatments (Fig. 8; Figs. 25–27). In summary, the combination of Reg@CeO2@HA NPs and anti-PD-L1 could synergistically enhanced antitumor immunity, primarily by amplifying the suppression of M2-polarized TAMs and, most prominently, by driving robust CD8+ T cell infiltration into tumors, thereby delivering superior antitumor efficacy within a safe therapeutic window.

Discussion

Conventional antiangiogenic and photothermal regimens often exacerbate intratumoral hypoxia and acidosis, which recruit immunosuppressive myeloid cells and thereby establish a barrier to therapeutic efficacy [4,53,59]. This clinical reality, coupled with our observation that angiogenic signatures alone failed to stratify OS prognosis whereas immune activity emerged as a strong survival predictor, prompted us to re-examine the multi-kinase inhibitor Reg beyond its canonical anti-angiogenic role. We found that Reg intrinsically downregulates PD-L1, curbs M2 macrophage polarization, and reverses MDSC-mediated T-cell suppression. In vivo, however, this immunostimulatory benefit is offset by hypoxia-driven MDSC accumulation—a well-recognized paradox of anti-angiogenic monotherapy that limits its single-agent efficacy [60]. This counterbalance between vascular pruning and immune evasion provided the impetus for designing a nanoplatform that not only delivers Reg but also actively rectifies the hypoxic and oxidative TME.

The Reg@CeO2@HA construct was conceived as a multi-functional “microenvironment corrector”. The CeO2 core serves a dual role: as a drug depot and as a catalytic ROS scavenger, while its pH-dependent Ce3+/Ce4+ switch enables on-demand oxygen release in the acidic TME. This design effectively disentangles the anti-angiogenic–hypoxia–MDSC axis: in vivo, the NPs promoted vascular normalization (increased CD31/α-SMA colocalization), attenuated HIF-1α expression, and reduced intratumoral ROS, collectively converting an immunosuppressive milieu into one permissive for T-cell infiltration and DC maturation. Unlike free Reg, the NP formulation did not aggravate MDSC accumulation, underscoring the importance of concomitant hypoxia relief. Mechanistically, the platform operates at multiple levels—it directly suppresses PD-L1 on tumor cells, redirects macrophages away from M2 polarization, and, through oxygen supplementation and ROS clearance, dismantles the metabolic checkpoints that sustain MDSC suppressor function. These integrated actions translated into potent tumor growth inhibition, with the combination of Reg@CeO2@HA and anti-PD-L1 producing a synergistic surge in CD8+ T-cell infiltration and near-eradication of established OS xenografts, supporting a virtuous cycle wherein vascular normalization and checkpoint blockade mutually reinforce each other [61,62].

While the therapeutic potential is substantial, the in vivo safety profile of the CeO2 scaffold warrants careful consideration. CeO2 NPs are not inert carriers; their biological effects are highly size-, coating-, and dose-dependent. Recent systematic evaluations in healthy murine models indicate that nanoceria with diameters in the 2-10 nm range and doses ≤5 mg/kg are generally well-tolerated over short observation periods, whereas larger particles and polymer-coated formulations exhibit reduced acute toxicity due to diminished cellular uptake and altered biodistribution [63,64]. Our NPs feature a ∼90 nm core and an HA shell, which not only improves tumor targeting—as evidenced by markedly lower off-target accumulation in the liver and intestine—but also provides a biocompatible surface that mitigates protein corona formation and nonspecific phagocytosis [65]. Moreover, the pH-responsive shift toward Ce3+ predominance in the TME suggests that the redox activity is preferentially unleashed within tumors, potentially limiting oxidative stress in healthy tissues. In line with these considerations, our routine toxicological assessments—body weight, serum biochemistry, hematology, and histopathology of major organs—revealed no significant abnormalities. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that these endpoints primarily reflect acute and sub-acute exposure; long-term retention in the reticuloendothelial system, potential chronic inflammation, and genotoxic effects remain to be systematically addressed [[66], [67], [68]]. Future studies incorporating extended observation periods, detailed pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profiling, and sensitive biomarkers of oxidative injury will be essential to fully establish the translational safety window of this platform.

Beyond safety, the present work has several limitations that warrant mention. The K7M2 syngeneic model, while immunocompetent, does not fully recapitulate the genetic heterogeneity and stromal complexity of human OS; validation in patient-derived xenografts or humanized immune-system models would strengthen the translational relevance. Additionally, although we demonstrate that the NPs alleviate hypoxia and ROS, the relative contribution of oxygen generation versus ROS scavenging to the observed immunomodulation—and whether these effects are additive or synergistic—remains to be quantitatively dissected. The optimal dosing schedule and sequence for combining the NPs with anti-PD-L1 also require further optimization, particularly given the dynamic interplay between vascular normalization and immune infiltration. Notwithstanding these caveats, our findings offer a conceptually novel strategy that moves beyond the conventional "anti-angiogenic" framework, positioning Reg@CeO2@HA as a TME-reprogramming nanomedicine that simultaneously addresses vascular, metabolic, and immunological barriers. By converting the hypoxic, ROS-rich, and immunosuppressive OS microenvironment into a state conducive to checkpoint blockade, this platform provides a promising avenue for improving outcomes in a disease that has long resisted immunotherapy, and it underscores the broader principle that rational combination of vascular modulation with metabolic correction may unlock the full potential of cancer immunotherapies.
