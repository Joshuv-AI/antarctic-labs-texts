Participant feedback on a health care transition conference for youth with medical complexity
Authors: Betsy Hopson, Jessica Brouillette, James Shi, Madeline Eckenrode, Cathy Carver, Phillip Crain, Ashley Parish, Carlie Somerville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEach year in the United States, an estimated 1.2 million youth with chronic conditions or functional limitations reach adulthood and must transition from pediatric to adult health care, a process often fragmented by service loss, limited adult providers, and complex waiver and financial systems. The University of Alabama at Birmingham developed the Staging Transition for Every Patient (STEP) Program which includes an annual Healthcare Transition Conference. Here we describe the conference and analyze two years of attendee feedback.MethodsConference planning followed an iterative, participatory co-design framework in which a steering committee of providers, community partners, caregivers, and individuals with lived experience shaped programming, and attendee feedback from each year informed the next. We evaluated the conference using post-conference surveys from the 2024 (n = 34) and 2025 (n = 43) events, summarized with descriptive statistics and thematic analysis; feedback from 2024 was used to tailor the 2025 program.ResultsAcross both years, attendees valued content on medical transition, Medicaid and community waivers, financial and legal planning, and lived experience. In 2025, 88% reported being "very likely" to attend again. In response to 2024 feedback, the 2025 program added sessions on financial planning, guardianship, day-program sessions and an expanded self-advocate panel. Despite these changes, the 2025 cohort continued to prioritize psychosocial topics including caregiver support and respite (55.8%), social support and relationships (53.5%), and aging with a disability (51.2%).Discussion/conclusionA community-engaged, iterative conference was highly valued but revealed a persistent gap between delivered logistical content and unmet psychosocial support needs. The STEP conference offers a transferable model for community-based transition education and a method for identifying and responding to evolving family needs.

Introduction

Each year in the United States, an estimated 1.2 million youth with chronic health conditions or functional limitations reach adulthood and must transition from pediatric to adult health care1. Historically, many of these individuals did not survive into adulthood2. Due to medical advancements, today most children with conditions such as spina bifida, cerebral palsy, rare genetic disorders, and bleeding disorders are expected to live well into adulthood2, 3. Transition requires more than just a transfer of medical responsibility or location. It includes early preparation, comprehensive planning, and collaboration among patients, families, and both pediatric and adult healthcare providers4. Yet numerous barriers persist that limit independence, including a shortage of adult providers with expertise, disruptions related to insurance changes, reduced access to community resources, and constraints on caregiver participation, which can be affected by caregivers' own health3, 4, 5, 6, 7. In practice, this means a young adult may wait months for an appointment with an adult subspecialist unfamiliar with their condition, lose Medicaid or waiver-funded services at a fixed age cutoff, travel to an unfamiliar health system without reliable transportation, or depend on a parent whose own health limits their ability to attend visits3, 6, 15. The young people themselves face significant barriers in this transition, as do their caregivers and the health systems that serve them. Addressing these barriers is critical to a successful transition and long-term health outcomes for this growing population.

Although the value of a structured transition process is well recognized, significant gaps remain in understanding and meeting the unique needs of this population. Transition is often framed as an adolescent milestone, but the changes and challenges it introduces extend across the lifespan: individuals with medical complexity and their families continue to confront decisions about health care, post-secondary education, employment, independent living, participation in day programs, and long-term planning well into adulthood 2, 8, 9. Many challenges, including aging caregivers and future planning, intensify over time rather than resolving after transfer to adult care.2, 8, 9. Layering medical transition onto these demands often amplifies family anxiety and fear and can contribute to worsening health outcomes10, 11. Compounding this, a persistent lack of communication and coordination among key stakeholders, such as hospitals, specialty clinics, pediatricians, school systems, and community agencies, further exacerbates stress during an already demanding time and undermines efforts to streamline care and support families4, 9, 11. As a result, many individuals with childhood-onset conditions remain in pediatric care well beyond the typical transfer age, and the timing of transfer varies widely from patient to patient rather than following a fixed age12. The absence of standardized metrics of a successful transition further complicates efforts to measure and improve outcomes, 3, 5, 13. Importantly, most transition support is delivered at the individual level, leaving few opportunities for families to connect with peers, access community resources, or learn from adults who have already navigated this path. These shared, largely psychosocial needs are rarely addressed at the community level.

To address these gaps, which span the adult care workforce, the health system, and the families and youth navigating transition, the University of Alabama at Birmingham established the Staging Transition for Every Patient (STEP) Program.14. Through an interdisciplinary team and a dedicated primary care clinic, STEP develops individualized care plans, coordinates specialist referrals, and supports patients and families as they move into an adult model of care, with an emphasis on independence, decision-making support, reduced caregiver burden, and personal growth6, 14. Recognizing that transition needs extend beyond a single clinic visit, the STEP team partnered with community organizations, caregivers, and individuals with lived experience to develop an annual health care transition conference. This manuscript describes the conference and summarizes two years of attendee feedback (2024 and 2025), identifying valued content, persistent gaps, and elements transferable to other institutions seeking to strengthen transitional care for individuals with medical complexity.

Methods

Design and setting

We conducted a two-year evaluation of the STEP Program Transition Conference, an annual community-based health care transition event hosted in Birmingham, Alabama, for youth with medical complexity, their families, and the providers and community organizations who serve them. The conference was first piloted in 2021 and grew out of the STEP Program observations of needs and gaps. Conference planning followed an iterative, participatory co-design approach: a multidisciplinary steering committee that included individuals with lived experience shaped the program, and attendee feedback collected at each conference was used to refine the content and structure of the following year's event. This report focuses on the 2024 and 2025 conferences, for which post-conference evaluation data were available.

Steering committee and conference development

A multidisciplinary steering committee was established to design the conference content, goals, and learning objectives. Including individuals with lived experience was a deliberate priority; their firsthand perspectives on life before, during, and after the transition to adulthood with a disability directly informed topic selection. The committee also included pediatric and adult medical providers, community partners, and STEP Clinic physicians and staff. Each year, the committee reviewed the prior year's attendee feedback and adjusted programming accordingly to address the needs families had identified.

Each conference was a single-day event held annually at United Ability, a community partner, in Birmingham, Alabama, and was accredited for continuing medical education through the University of Alabama at Birmingham Heersink School of Medicine (up to 4 AMA PRA Category 1 Credits). The conference was designed around seven objectives: to identify the key components of a successful transition; to describe the impact of transition on families and caregivers; to clarify how changes in Medicaid waivers, private insurance, and service eligibility affect access to care, equipment, and long-term support; to connect families with statewide vendors, agencies, and community organizations; to evaluate practical strategies for transition readiness; to apply lessons from the STEP Program toward inclusive, interdisciplinary practice; and to prioritize future topics based on caregiver feedback. The program combined a vendor expo and networking time with a mix of expert-led sessions and lived-experience panels (Table 1). Across the two years, sessions addressed supported decision-making, Medicaid community and Elderly and Disabled waivers and ABLE accounts, assistive technology, financial planning and guardianship, essential medical services at risk during transition, the medical transition pathway through STEP and community resources, and day-program options. Presenters included pediatric and adult clinicians, state agency and community-organization representatives, legal and financial advisors, and adults with disabilities who had themselves transitioned to adult care. Funding and community partnership

The conference was supported by a community donor. The STEP Clinic partnered with United Ability, a community organization that connects people with disabilities to resources in their communities and supports them in living full and meaningful lives. United Ability provided the venue, audiovisual staffing, and setup at no cost.

Evaluation surveys

Attendees were invited to complete a voluntary, anonymous post-conference survey at each event to assess their experience and identify priorities for future programming. Because the evaluation instrument evolved between years, the two surveys differed in platform and structure. The 2024 survey was administered in Qualtrics and consisted of five primarily open-ended questions addressing the most helpful and least helpful aspects of the conference, desired future topics, and suggestions for improvement, along with one structured question on the most helpful session topics. Informed by 2024 responses, the 2025 survey was expanded and administered in REDCap; it comprised seven questions (Appendix: STEP Conference Evaluation) capturing respondent role (e.g., caregiver/family member, medical provider, community resource provider), the most valued sessions, desired future topics, the biggest challenges encountered in planning for and navigating the transition to adult care, likelihood of attending a future event, and open-ended comments. Surveys were distributed to all attendees at each conference; 34 of approximately 88 attendees from 2024 and 43 of approximately 118 attendees from 2025 responded.

Analysis

Quantitative responses were summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages); because several items allowed respondents to select all applicable options, percentages may sum to more than 100%. Open-ended responses were independently coded by two reviewers (JB and JS) to identify commonly recurring themes, with a third investigator (BH) providing oversight, adjudicating discrepancies, and making final coding decisions. The resulting themes were summarized descriptively alongside the quantitative findings. Because the 2024 and 2025 instruments differed, responses are reported descriptively by year rather than compared statistically; convergent themes across the two years are highlighted where they occur. We also compared the most valued sessions and most requested future topics descriptively between respondents identifying as caregivers or self-advocates and those identifying as health care or community providers; because respondents could select more than one role, these groups were not mutually exclusive.

Ethical considerations

Because the post-conference evaluations were voluntary and anonymous, completion and submission of the survey was taken to indicate consent to participate. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the University of Alabama at Birmingham Institutional Review Board (IRB 300007699).

Program response to feedback changes

Consistent with the conference's iterative, feedback-driven design, the 2025 program was adapted directly by the priorities attendees identified in 2024 (Table 3). Importantly, the conference program itself changed between the two years, as well as the evaluation instrument. In response to 2024 requests, the 2025 conference added dedicated sessions on financial planning, guardianship and on day-program options, reframed and expanded medical content into a session on services that may be lost during transition and expanded the lived-experience panel of adults who had transitioned. The evaluation instrument was also revised for 2025 to capture more granular data to inform future events.

When asked what they wanted more of in the future, attendees prioritized caregiver support and respite (55.8%, n = 24), social support and relationships (53.5%, n = 23), aging with a disability (51.2%, n = 22), and navigating adult health care (46.5%, n = 20), followed by mental health (41.9%, n = 18) and self-advocacy and independence (39.5%, n = 17).

The most commonly reported challenges to a successful transition were feeling unprepared (44.2%, n = 19), difficulty finding adult providers (34.9%, n = 15), loss of services or supports (32.6%, n = 14), lack of coordination between pediatric and adult care (30.2%, n = 13), and building independence in self-management (30.2%, n = 13).

Open-ended comments on conference logistics were few but actionable. Respondents suggested allowing more time between sessions for networking, lengthening the panel of adults who had transitioned, and providing clearer orientation to the vendor tables, while praising the range of vendors and the opportunities for connection. These suggestions are informing planning for future conferences.

Cross-year patterns and persistent unmet needs

Two patterns were consistent across both years and both survey instruments. First, the content attendees valued most was concentrated in logistical, financial, legal, and medical domains: such as, medical transition, waivers, financial and legal planning, and the experiences of adults who had transitioned ranked among the most helpful sessions in both 2024 and 2025. Second, and despite the program changes made in response to 2024 feedback, the 2025 cohort continued to prioritize psychosocial and life-course topics for the future, including caregiver support and respite, social support and relationships, and aging with a disability.

This pattern was most pronounced among caregivers and family members, the conference's largest audience. Within this subgroup (n = 23), feeling unprepared was the most common transition challenge (60.9%, n = 14), and caregiver support and respite was the most requested future topic (73.9%, n = 17) both notably higher than in the sample overall.

Respondent priorities differed by role. Caregivers, family members, and self-advocates (n = 24) most valued sessions offering concrete, logistical guidance, financial planning (75.0%), the STEP Program overview (62.5%), and community waivers (50.0%), and most wanted future content on caregiver support and respite (70.8%) and navigating adult health care (50.0%). Health care and community providers (medical providers, educators, and community resource providers; n = 25) most valued the lived-experience panel of adults who had transitioned (72.0%), financial planning (68.0%), and networking (56.0%), and prioritized broader life-course topics for the future, including aging with a disability (64.0%), social support and relationships (60.0%), and mental health (56.0%). Because respondents could select all roles that applied, the two groups overlap (six respondents identified with both a family and a professional role) and are small, so these comparisons are descriptive.

Discussion

Across this two-year evaluation, even families already connected to a robust, structured, interdisciplinary transition program valued the conference's logistical and medical sessions yet consistently requested more support in psychosocial and life-course domains. The STEP Program provides what many youth with medical complexity lack, including a dedicated transition clinic, coordinated specialist referrals, and an established model for moving into adult care6, 14. Yet attendees, most of them caregivers, still feel unprepared and requested more content on psychosocial and life-course supports into the future. This pattern suggests that securing the medical components of transition, such as identifying an adult provider and transferring care, addresses only one part of a much larger process. Health care transition is increasingly understood not as a discrete medical handoff but as one element of a broader developmental transition to adulthood that spans education, employment, independent living, relationships, and long-term security, shaped heavily by psychosocial factors and the social determinants of health4, 15, 16. Interpreted cautiously, these attendee-reported priorities suggest that when the medical components of transition are comparatively well supported, families perceive the non-medical dimensions as their most pressing remaining concern. Because our surveys capture attendees' expressed preferences and self-reported priorities rather than independently measured outcomes, we interpret these findings as reflecting perceived rather than objectively assessed needs.

The consistent demand for psychosocial content across cohorts suggests a structural gap rather than a programmatic limitation. Families repeatedly requested caregiver support and respite, social connection, and guidance on aging with a disability, domains for which formal services are limited, fragmented, or difficult to access17. A single conference can surface and partially address these needs, but it cannot substitute for the sustained community infrastructure they require. Because transition is an ongoing, life-course process rather than a single event, families at different stages prioritize different content; future conferences may therefore benefit from offering a broad menu of topics in flexible formats, allowing attendees to select the sessions most relevant to their current stage.

Medicaid waivers exemplify this challenge. Attendees in both years identified waiver navigation as a persistent source of confusion and frustration: the information is difficult to interpret, eligibility and available services differ substantially across programs and even within a single state, and enrollment is frequently constrained by long wait times18, 19. Because each state interprets waiver eligibility and service provision differently, families of children with medical complexity face a fragmented and often opaque system at precisely the moment they are also managing a medical transition18. One of the conference's most valued functions was to address this directly, not only through expert-led sessions, but by creating space for families to share their own experiences and the resources that helped them. This peer exchange, alongside the lived-experience panels, allowed families to learn from those who had already navigated the same systems, a form of knowledge transfer that formal instruction alone rarely provides.

A critical component of this gap is the burden on aging caregivers. As individuals with medical complexity live longer,2, 8 caregivers face declining capacity to meet the extensive needs of their adult children and their activities of daily living declines17, 20. Caregiver burden tracks closely with the care recipient's functional dependence and the physical demands of caregiving, and prolonged caregiving is associated with measurable declines in caregivers' own physical and mental health6, 17, 20. For families of individuals with lifelong disability, this creates a compounding problem with no natural endpoint: the care recipient's needs remain constant or grow while the caregiver's ability to meet them erodes. This dynamic, well documented among aging caregivers of adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities,17, 21 signals an urgent and growing demand for caregiver-directed supports, including respite, in-home assistance, and care models that explicitly anticipate the caregiver's own decline, that current transition services rarely provide.

Comprehensive transition planning must extend beyond medical transfer to include financial planning, legal preparation such as guardianship or supported decision-making, advanced care planning, and what the disability literature terms "future" or "permanency" planning, which explicitly prepares for the possibility that the individual with medical complexity will outlive their primary caregiver17, 21. Research on aging families of adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities has identified distinct domains that planning must address, including housing, legal arrangements, identification of a future primary caregiver, financial security, day-to-day care, medical management, and transportation, and has shown that structured support helps families take concrete steps, such as establishing a letter of intent, arranging residential plans, and creating a special-needs trust, while reducing caregiver burden17, 21. The strong attendee interest in our sessions on financial planning, guardianship, and waivers, combined with the persistent demand for content on aging with a disability, indicates that families recognize these needs even when the systems around them are not yet organized to meet them.

Taken together, these findings offer a scalable approach for other centers seeking to strengthen transition support including community engagement, iterative feedback and inclusion of lived experience. Consistent with the disability community's principle of "nothing about us without us," people who had themselves navigated transition were treated not as an audience but as co-designers and presenters. This was more than an ethical commitment: across both years, the panels featuring adults who had transitioned and caregivers who had been through the process were among the most highly rated sessions, and attendee feedback consistently called for more of this content. The reproducibility of that preference suggests that lived-experience perspectives are not a supplementary feature of transition education but a central one, and that other programs would also benefit from this deliberate inclusion.

Finally, our experience underscores that no single institution can address the full scope of transition needs alone. Hosting the conference in partnership with United Ability built durable relationships among the hospital, the STEP Program, and community agencies, and the inclusion of vendors and resource tables created direct connections between families and the organizations that serve them. Because the needs of youth with medical complexity span medical, educational, financial, legal, and social domains, effective transition support depends on coordination across the health system, schools, and community organizations rather than within any one of them4. A community-engaged conference is one practical mechanism for building these cross-sector relationships, allowing families to encounter, in a single setting, the range of partners whose coordination their transition ultimately requires.

Limitations

This was a single-site evaluation of a convenience sample of conference attendees, who may differ from the broader population of families navigating transition. The surveys were anonymous and brief, precluding linkage to clinical outcomes, and response rates and non-respondent characteristics were not captured. The 2024 and 2025 instruments differed in platform and content, so the two years are reported descriptively rather than compared statistically, and subgroup estimates, particularly for caregivers, are based on small numbers and should be interpreted cautiously. As an evaluation of attendee-reported experience and priorities, this work characterizes perceived needs and program reception; it does not measure whether the conference improved transition readiness or downstream health outcomes.

In addition, fewer than half of attendees completed a survey at each conference (34 of approximately 88 in 2024 and 43 of approximately 118 in 2025), so respondents may not represent all participants. That nearly all 2025 respondents (97.7%) reported being likely to attend a future event suggests they may have viewed the conference especially favorably, and their priorities may not fully reflect those of all attendees.

Conclusion

A community-engaged conference co-designed with families and individuals with lived experience offers a feasible and transferable model for delivering transition education while surfacing the priorities families themselves identify. Across two years, attendees consistently valued the medical and logistical content yet continued to prioritize psychosocial and life-course supports, indicating that, from families' perspective, improving the medical mechanics of transition is necessary but not sufficient. The priorities they raised, including caregiver support and respite, clearer pathways through programs such as Medicaid waivers, comprehensive future planning, and coordinated partnerships across health care, education, and community organizations, are consistent with the broader transition literature and point to where families see the greatest need for continued investment, so that transition supports not only continuity of medical care but a full adult life.
