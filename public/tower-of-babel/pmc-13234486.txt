Profiling heterologous 3′ terminator-dependent expression variation in Aspergillus oryzae
Authors: Chenxi Zhang, Ridwan Elemosho, Elena Sofia Brancato, Casper Robert Balten van der Luijt, Jakob Blæsbjerg Hoof, Nikolas Alexander Mumm, Tiffany Mak, Leonie Johanna Jahn, Morten Otto Alexander Sommer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transcriptional terminators are key to defining transcript boundaries, ensuring mRNA maturation, and maintaining expression stability, yet they remain underexplored in filamentous fungi. Here, we systematically benchmarked 15 heterologous 3′ terminators in Aspergillus oryzae by measuring protein output via mCherry fluorescence and transcriptional readthrough based on a quantitative readthrough index (RTI) derived from RT-qPCR.Across the tested terminators, mCherry expression varied more than 5-fold and RTI spanned over 50-fold, indicating substantial functional diversity. Expression strength and termination efficiency were only weakly correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.39, p = 0.165), indicating that these two properties are largely independent and can be optimized separately.Strong terminators -TactA, Txyn1, TmutA and TtrpC combined good mCherry expression (>ΔT) and small readthrough (RT < 0.15). In contrast, short synthetic sequences (≤70 bp) showed low mCherry output accompanied by transcriptional leakage. Interestingly, widely used toolkit elements such as Ttef1 and Tcyc1 performed poorly in A. oryzae, highlighting the importance of empirical characterization over assumed transferability.Polyadenylation site mapping by 3′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (3′RACE) reveals high-performing terminators that possess focused cleavage sites, continuous poly(A) tails, and recognizable A[AT]TAAA-like motifs with moderately AU-rich upstream regions, whereas weak terminators exhibited dispersed cleavage and sparse upstream sequence elements. Targeted disruption of the canonical AATAAA hexamer in TactA did not significantly impair expression or termination efficiency, demonstrating that the canonical PAS is not strictly required when AU-rich upstream sequence elements are present. Terminator performance rankings were robust across three carbon sources and were confirmed using a secreted NanoLuc luciferase reporter, demonstrating that functional behavior is largely sequence intrinsic. These findings demonstrate a functional terminator architecture and suggest a practical guideline for terminator selection in A. oryzae.

Introduction

Aspergillus oryzae is a generally recognized as safe (GRAS) filamentous fungus with a long record in food fermentation and increasing use as a chassis for enzyme and metabolite production [[1], [2], [3]]. It tolerates industrial conditions, secretes proteins efficiently [4], and is supported by modern genome-engineering tools [5]. These characteristics make A. oryzae an attractive host for heterologous protein production and multi-gene pathway construction. Nevertheless, the rational engineering of multi-gene constructs in this organism remains constrained by a limited repertoire of well-characterized regulatory parts—particularly at the level of transcriptional terminators [6].

Transcriptional terminators are essential cis-regulatory elements that define the 3′ boundary of transcription units. In eukaryotes, termination is mechanistically coupled to pre-mRNA 3′-end processing: cleavage and polyadenylation at defined poly(A) sites triggers RNA polymerase II pausing and eventual dissociation, while the addition of a poly(A) tail stabilizes the mature transcript and promotes its export and translation [7,8]. Beyond their role in transcript maturation, terminators directly influence mRNA abundance and protein output, and their failure to efficiently terminate transcription can cause readthrough into downstream genes—a critical concern in multi-gene constructs where independent expression of each cassette is required [9,19]. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, systematic terminator characterization has demonstrated that terminator identity can modulate protein expression over several-fold ranges [10]. These findings have motivated analogous efforts in other fungal hosts, yet filamentous fungi remain comparatively underexplored in this regard.

In A. oryzae, most applications still rely on a small set of “default” terminators—either derived from endogenous genes (amyB [11]) or borrowed from other fungi such as Aspergillus nidulans and Trichoderma reesei (trpC, tef1) [[12], [13], [14]]—without rigorous, side-by-side comparison under identical host and genomic contexts. This limitation becomes particularly critical in multi-gene engineering applications. Strain improvement typically requires the integration of multiple transcriptional units within a single strain. Repetitive use of a limited terminator set restricts design flexibility and increases the risk of homologous recombination due to sequence redundancy, thereby undermining genetic stability. Expanding and systematically benchmarking the terminator repertoire is thus a prerequisite for scalable and robust strain engineering.

One practical strategy to address this limitation is to introduce heterologous terminators that have been applied or functionally characterized in other fungal systems, thereby expanding sequence diversity and regulatory options. However, terminator activity is not inherently portable across species. Although the cleavage and polyadenylation machinery is broadly conserved among eukaryotes, 3′-end processing depends on host-specific recognition of cis-regulatory elements and genomic context. Consequently, a terminator that performs efficiently in one organism may exhibit different termination efficiency, poly(A) site usage patterns, or levels of transcriptional readthrough in another host. Systematic, quantitative evaluation of heterologous terminators directly in A. oryzae is therefore required to establish chassis-specific performance benchmarks and to guide rational terminator selection in multi-gene engineering.

In this study, we evaluated heterologous 3′ terminators in A. oryzae using a dual-reporter construct that allows simultaneous quantification of protein output (mCherry fluorescence by flow cytometry) and transcriptional readthrough (Readthrough Index, RTI, by RT-qPCR). The terminator panel spans Aspergillus species, Penicillium chrysogenum, Trichoderma reesei, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, as well as short synthetic variants. To investigate the sequence determinants of terminator performance, we mapped polyadenylation sites by 3′RACE and analyzed upstream sequence elements including canonical and variant poly(A) signal (PAS) hexamers and AU-rich upstream sequence elements (USEs). We further validated key findings through targeted mutagenesis of the canonical PAS in the top-performing terminator TactA, and confirmed the robustness of terminator rankings across carbon sources and reporter genes using a secreted NanoLuc luciferase system. Together, these analyses establish a foundation for understanding terminator behavior and developing predictable 3′-end design principles for A. oryzae.

Materials and methods

Strains, media, and cultivation

The parental strain used in this study was A. oryzae JBx_236462 (RIB40, ΔpyrG; chro1-2CP_An_008-Bm3R1-NLS-VP16-TamyB), which contains a synthetic transcription factor (sTF) integrated at the chro1-2 locus [15]. The pyrG gene, encoding orotidine-5′-phosphate decarboxylase, was deleted in this strain and served as the selection marker. Loss of pyrG confers 5.0 g/L uridine (Sigma–Aldrich) and 2.0 g/L uracil (Sigma–Aldrich) auxotrophy, allowing positive selection of transformants by complementation with a functional pyrG allele, as well as counter-selection using 1.0 mg/mL 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA, ThermoFisher), which is toxic to pyrG+ cells.

All A. oryzae strains were cultivated at 30 °C for 5 days on agar plates or in liquid medium with shaking at 200 rpm, unless otherwise specified.

Media were employed for transformation and routine cultivation: PDB (g/L), potato dextrose broth 24.0, pH = 5.5. PDA (g/L), potato dextrose agar 39.0, pH = 5.5. The fermentation-based liquid (FBL) medium was formulated based on previously reported nitrate-based production media for Aspergillus species [[16], [17], [18]] as detailed below: KNO3, 11.5; K2SO4, 1.0; CaCl2·2H2O, 1.0; KH2PO4, 3.0; MgSO4·7H2O, 0.6, pH = 5.5. The medium was supplemented with trace metal solution and buffered with 100 mM MES. A carbon source was added at 20 g/L as indicated (glucose, maltose, dextrin).

Commercially available One Shot Top 10 E. coli competent cells (Invitrogen #C404003) were used as cloning hosts. Selection of transformants was done on LB (10.0 g/L tryptone, 5.0 g/L yeast extract, 10.0 g/L NaCl, 15.0 g/L agar) plates containing 100.0 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C.

Construct design and genome integration

Coding sequences of mCherry, eGFP and NanoLuc were retrieved from UniProt and codon-optimized for A. oryzae using the Twist Bioscience optimization tool. Specifically, NanoLuc was fused downstream of the Ginkgo signal peptide (GinkgoSP) to enable secretion [19]. Candidate terminator sequence was retrieved from NCBI and synthesized by Twist Bioscience. DNA fragments were assembled using NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs) to generate the following transcriptional units [20]: UAS-Ptef1–mCherry–TAG–[test terminator]–eGFP–TGA–TamyB, UAS-Ptef1–GinkgoSP–NanoLuc–TGA–[represented test terminator].

Each cassette was flanked by 1-kb homology arms targeting the chro2-2 locus for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homologous recombination, following established Aspergillus protocols [15]. Construct integrity was verified by nanopore sequencing (Unveil Bio) [21].

The coding sequence and terminators are provided in Table S4 and primers for cloning are listed in Table S5.

Transformation

Protoplast-mediated transformation of A. oryzae JBx_236462 was performed using CRISPR–Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes and PCR-amplified repair DNA fragments, following our established protocol with minor modifications [15]. Briefly, pre-cultures were grown in PDB + UU. Mycelial biomass was harvested and enzymatically digested to generate protoplasts. RNP complexes were assembled from recombinant Cas9 nuclease and synthetic sgRNAs targeting both 5′ and 3′ regions of the chro2-2 locus. These RNPs were co-delivered with repair templates into protoplasts by PEG–CaCl2 transformation. Regeneration was carried out on methionine-supplemented BA + Met, and transformants were restreaked on MMA + Met. Correct integration was verified by colony PCR using primers flanking the homology arms. For marker recycling, strains were counter-selected on PDA + 5-FOA + UU, followed by resteak and cryopreservation as glycerol stocks.

Colony fluorescence imaging

Fungal colonies were imaged using an iBright™ FL1500 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a 530–550 nm excitation filter and a standard fluorescence detection channel. Images were acquired after 5 days of growth on PDA + UU plates at 30 °C. Exposure time and gain were kept constant across samples to ensure comparability of fluorescence intensity.

Flow cytometry of mCherry expression

Flow cytometry for filamentous fungi has been demonstrated previously under defined spore-based conditions [[22], [23], [24]]. Specifically, A. oryzae spores were harvested and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and filtered through a 70 μm EASYstrainer (Greiner Bio-One) to remove hyphal fragments and aggregates [25]. The resulting spore suspensions were analyzed using a NovoCyte Quanteon flow cytometer equipped with the Y615–H (PE–Texas Red) channel. To avoid bias, no gating was applied during data acquisition. Each construct was analyzed in three biological replicates, each containing >10,000 single-cell events.

For visualization of single-cell fluorescence distributions, flow cytometry data (FCS files) were imported into FlowJo™ v10.8 Software (BD Life Sciences), and replicates corresponding to the same construct were grouped and concatenated (“Group → Concatenate”) to generate combined datasets. Up to 10,000 events per replicate were randomly subsampled to ensure balanced representation across constructs. Raw fluorescence values were log10-transformed [log10(value + 1)] prior to plotting [22]. Violin plots illustrate the full single-cell fluorescence distributions for each construct, with internal boxplots indicating the median and interquartile range.

To quantify overall expression levels, single-cell populations were first gated based on FSC-A vs. FSC-H to exclude doublets and aggregates. PBS served as the negative background control. A fluorescence gate on the Y615 channel was defined using the WT (negative) and positive (ΔT) reference strains and applied uniformly to all constructs. For each biological replicate, the frequency of parent events (% positive cells) and the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) were calculated, then Fluorescence values were summarized into an integrated mean fluorescence intensity (iMFI) metric:iMFI=MFI×(%positivecells/100)

This metric captures both expression strength and population consistency, providing a robust measure of central tendency widely used in flow cytometry studies [26]. Bar plots summarize the mean ± SD of replicate iMFI values (n = 3), and pairwise statistical comparisons were performed using Welch's two-sample t-test [27], comparing each construct to the positive (ΔT) control strain.

RNA Extraction, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR readthrough analysis

Mycelium was scraped from agar plates and transferred to pre-chilled mortar and pestle, then homogenized thoroughly to disrupt fungal cell walls. Total RNA was extracted from the ground mycelial material using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions for filamentous fungi. The procedure included on-column DNase I treatment to eliminate residual genomic DNA. After sequential washing steps, RNA was eluted in RNase-free water, quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, assessed for purity, and stored at −80 °C until use.

cDNA synthesis was performed using 100 ng of total RNA and the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Transcript levels of mCherry, eGFP, and the internal reference gene β-tubulin were quantified by TaqMan qPCR using custom oligonucleotide probes (IDT, shown in Table S6) and TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reactions were assembled in 96-well plates and run on a QuantStudio real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under standard cycling conditions. No-RT and no-template controls were included to monitor genomic contamination and reagent background. Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the ΔΔCt method with normalization to β-tubulin [28]. RTI was further calculated asRTI=RelDownRelUp=2−ΔCtDown2−ΔCtUp=2−(ΔCtDown−ΔCtUp)=2ΔCtUp−ΔCtDownwhere Down corresponds to eGFP, Up to mCherry, and Ref to β-tubulin. Thus, RTI values > 1 indicate strong readthrough, whereas RTI <0.1 reflects efficient termination.

3′RACE mapping of polyadenylation sites

Reverse transcription was performed with an adapter-anchored oligo(dT) primer (5′–Adapter–T(18)VN–3′) to minimize internal priming [29]. PCR amplification of 3′ ends used a gene-specific forward primer (GSP-F) positioned 200–350 nt upstream of the mCherry stop codon and a universal primer (UPM) complementary to the adapter. Nested PCR with an internal GSP-F2 primer increased specificity (Table S7). Amplicons were resolved on agarose gels, purified for direct Sanger sequencing using GSP-F2 as the primer. Negative (no-RT and no-template) controls were included in all reactions. Raw Sanger sequencing traces (.ab1 files) were processed using BioPython (v1.81). The first 50 nucleotides of each read were discarded to remove the low-quality initiation region associated with capillary electrophoresis. Reads with a mean Phred quality score below 10 were excluded from further analysis.

Poly(A) tails were identified as runs of ≥8 consecutive adenosines within the sequencing reads. For each poly(A) run, the 12 nucleotides immediately upstream were extracted as an anchor sequence and mapped to the corresponding terminator reference sequence using exact string matching. To account for sequencing orientation, mapping was performed against both forward and reverse-complement strands. Cleavage site coordinates were defined as the last reference-encoded nucleotide immediately upstream of the poly(A) tail (1-based) [30,31].

In-silico terminator sequence analysis and mutation variants

Terminator reference sequences were analyzed using custom Python scripts (v3.11) to annotate cis-regulatory elements associated with 3′-end processing. For each confirmed cleavage site, the upstream sequence was scanned within a 5–150 nt window for all 12 known PAS hexamer variants (AATAAA, ATTAAA, AATATA, AATAGA, AATGAA, AGTAAA, GATAAA, TATAAA, CATAAA, AATACA, AATACT, AATAAT) [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. AU-rich upstream sequence elements (USE) were quantified by counting occurrences of nine degenerate pentameric motifs (TATAT, TTTTT, TATTT, TTTAT, TTTTA, AAATT, ATATT, ATTTA, ATTTT) across the full terminator sequence [37,38]. T-stretch downstream sequence elements (DSE) were defined as runs of ≥5 consecutive thymidines and enumerated across the full terminator sequence [39,40].

Based on the annotated sequence features, the following variants were constructed: TactA-ΔPAS: The canonical PAS (AATAAA; positions 601–606) was disrupted by overlap extension PCR [41] using mutagenic primers PAS_KO_F and PAS_KO_R.

Quantification of Secreted NanoLuc luciferase activity

Transformants expressing GinkgoSP–NanoLuc were cultivated under the conditions described above. Culture supernatants were collected by filtration through miracloth (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min to remove residual mycelial fragments.

NanoLuc activity in the culture supernatant was measured using the Nano-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal volumes of culture supernatant and Nano-Glo reagent were mixed in a white 96-well plate. Luminescence was measured immediately using Synergy™ H1 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). Relative luminescence units (RLU) were recorded and normalized to dry cell weight, where indicated.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R. Significance tests included ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's post hoc comparisons, and Spearman correlation coefficients. Multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Effect sizes were expressed as Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals. Data visualization employed violin, box, and scatter plots as detailed in figure captions.

Functional characterization of terminators reveals coordinated control of expression and transcriptional termination

All strains were cultured on PDA–UU agar plates at 30 °C for 5 days before imaging and harvesting conidial suspensions to ensure comparable developmental stages. Colony fluorescence imaging under 530–550 nm excitation revealed substantial differences in reporter signal among terminator constructs (Fig. S1). To quantify these differences, conidial suspensions from the same colonies were analyzed by flow cytometry (raw scatter plots are shown in Fig. S2). Terminator identity strongly influenced reporter expression, producing more than five-fold variation in iMFI values across the collection (one-way ANOVA, p < 10−4). Using the ΔT construct as a functional baseline (iMFI ≈ 3.40 × 105), terminators were grouped into three expression classes based on their relative iMFI values: high, intermediate (ΔT-level), and low. The high-expression group comprised TmutA (H. lixii), TactA (P. chrysogenum), Txyn1 (T. reesei), and Tcyc1 (S. cerevisiae). Among these, TmutA showed the highest expression level (∼1.05 × 106), whereas TactA, Txyn1, and Tcyc1 displayed comparable fluorescence intensities (∼6.8–7.0 × 105), all clearly exceeding the levels observed for other constructs. The intermediate-expression group included TpenDE (P. chrysogenum), Tsyn25, TtrpC (A. nidulans), Ttif35 (P. chrysogenum) and Toat1 (P. chrysogenum), which produced moderate fluorescence signals above the ΔT baseline (∼3.0–4.7 × 105). In contrast, Ttef1 (A. nidulans) and Tpdc (T. reesei) showed relatively lower fluorescence levels (∼2.0–3.0 × 105) and were therefore classified as low-expression terminators. TxlnA (A. nidulans) and minimal synthetic terminators (Tsyn07, Tsyn15, Tsyn27) produced fluorescence values close to the wild-type background, indicating little to no detectable activity under the tested conditions. Notably, Tsyn25, the only synthetic terminator showing detectable activity, generated a clear mCherry-positive population and moderate expression (∼3.8 × 105), indicating that compact synthetic terminators can retain measurable termination activity despite their reduced sequence length. Interestingly, commonly used fungal terminators such as TtrpC and Ttef1 did not rank among the strongest performers in this system.

In addition to contributing to mRNA stability and overall protein yield, terminators are also supposed to ensure complete transcriptional termination and prevent transcriptional readthrough into adjacent genes—a critical requirement in multi-gene constructs where independent expression of each cassette is essential. To understand this, we initially attempted to quantify GFP fluorescence from downstream. However, GFP signals were near background in most constructs. This reflects a constraint of the reporter architecture: a stop codon following the mCherry open reading frame (ORF) causes ribosome disengagement [17] and the absence of any translational re-initiation (e.g., an internal ribosome entry site, IRES-like element) was included between mCherry and eGFP prevents translation of the downstream ORF even when it is transcribed [[51], [52]]. No experimentally validated fungal IRES has been reported for A. oryzae to date [53]. Consequently, termination efficiency was quantified at the RNA level using RT-qPCR instead of GFP fluorescence. So, we performed RT–qPCR to examine transcriptional termination efficiency. Bar plots of normalized transcript levels (Fig. S4, Table S2) revealed pronounced differences in both upstream (mCherry) and downstream (eGFP) abundance across terminators and RTI was calculated and shown in Fig. 3. As expected, the ΔT construct lacking a terminator displayed comparable mCherry and eGFP transcript levels, yielding RTI ≈0.95, fully consistent with the architecture of the cassette. Based on the RTI distribution, terminators were grouped into three classes: efficient (RTI < 0.1), moderately efficient (0.1 ≤ RTI <1), and weak (RTI ≥1). The efficient termination group included TactA (P. chrysogenum), TtrpC (A. nidulans) and Ttif35 (P. chrysogenum), all of which produced high upstream mCherry transcript levels with minimal downstream eGFP accumulation, effectively prevented RNA polymerase from progressing into the downstream gene. A second set showed intermediate termination, with moderate upstream expression and limited but detectable downstream transcript accumulation, comprising Tsyn15 (∼0.11) and Txyn1 (T. reesei; ∼0.12), which fell at the lower boundary of this group and approached the efficient threshold, followed by TpenDE (P. chrysogenum), TmutA (H. lixii), Tsyn27, Toat1 (P. chrysogenum), and Tpdc (T. reesei). In contrast, readthrough was observed for Ttef1 (A. nidulans), Tcyc1 (S. cerevisiae), and Tsyn25 exhibited elevated GFP relative to mCherry (RTI ≥1). TxlnA (A. nidulans) and Tsyn07 showed extremely high RTI values (∼212 and ∼4150, respectively). These terminators allowed substantial polymerase progression into the downstream ORF.

Notably, expression strength and termination efficiency were not strictly correlated across terminators. Correlation analysis (Fig. 4) revealed only a weak, nonsignificant negative relationship between RTI and fluorescence (Spearman ρ = −0.39, p = 0.165). For example, Tcyc1 supported strong reporter expression despite weak termination efficiency, whereas TactA combined strong expression with efficient termination. In another example, Tsyn25 showed moderate expression but high readthrough. These results indicate that expression output and transcriptional termination efficiency are partially decoupled and likely governed by distinct sequence determinants. To assess whether these functional properties were maintained under industrially relevant conditions, four representative terminators were selected to span the observed functional range: TactA (high expression, efficient termination), Tcyc1 (high expression, weak termination), Tpdc (low expression, intermediate termination), and Tsyn25 (intermediate expression, high readthrough). These were evaluated in glucose-, maltose-, and dextrin-based fed-batch liquid (FBL) media. Across all tested carbon sources, both expression levels and termination efficiencies showed consistent relative trends (Fig. S6 and Tables S1–2). TactA and Tcyc1 maintained high expression (∼5.8–7.1 × 105), Tsyn25 showed intermediate expression (∼2.9–3.9 × 105), and Tpdc remained the lowest (∼2.6–2.9 × 105). RT–qPCR analysis confirmed that the relative hierarchy of termination efficiency was likewise preserved across carbon sources, with Tsyn25 exhibiting the highest readthrough, followed by Tcyc1, Tpdc, and TactA. Although absolute values varied with carbon source, the overall functional ranking remained unchanged.

Together, these results demonstrate that the relative functional ranking of terminators—in terms of both expression output and transcriptional termination efficiency—is robust to changes in carbon source, indicating that the core functional behavior of these elements is largely sequence-intrinsic and maintained under conditions relevant to industrial fermentation.

Cleavage sites could be confidently assigned for most native and engineered terminators. The resulting 3′UTR lengths varied substantially, ranging from approximately 46 nt to over 622 nt, and a median around 250 nt. This distribution is consistent with previously reported genome-wide 3′UTR lengths in A. oryzae [36], suggesting that the mapped cleavage sites are biologically representative. Several terminators, including TactA, TtrpC, TmutA, Toat1, Tpdc, TpenDE, Ttif35 produced well-defined poly(A) tails (18–25 nt) with clean junctions (precise cleavage sites characterized by a single dominant position and minimal heterogeneity) to the 3′UTR, indicating efficient and moderate efficient 3′-end processing at the mechanistic level (RTI < 0.5) [54]. In contrast, Ttef1, Tcyc1 and TxlyA produced a notably shorter poly(A) tail (<10 nt), coinciding with their relatively weak termination efficiency (RTI>1). The synthetic terminators (Tsyn07, Tsyn15, Tsyn25, and Tsyn27) failed to yield reliable cleavage site assignments due to the absence of detectable poly(A) tails or the presence of ambiguous A-rich stretches. These patterns are consistent with inefficient or aberrant 3′-end formation and production of unstable or heterogeneous transcripts. Such low-complexity A-tracts are also prone to internal oligo(dT) priming, generating artefactual 3′ ends rather than true polyadenylation products, an effect widely documented in poly(A)-focused sequencing studies. Importantly, poly(A) tail length did not show a clear relationship with termination efficiency, indicating that cleavage site selection rather than tail length is the primary determinant of functional output.

To identify the upstream sequence signals directing cleavage site selection, we scanned a 5–150 nt window upstream of each mapped cleavage site for all known PAS hexamer variants [55]. The canonical PAS AATAAA was identified in TactA and TtrpC, positioned ∼16 nt upstream of the cleavage site, within the classical near-upstream element (NUE) window (10–40 nt). This positioning is consistent with established models of cleavage and polyadenylation factor (CPF)-mediated processing [56,57]. However, the presence of a canonical PAS was not required for cleavage. Several terminators carried variant PAS motifs at functionally relevant positions, including: Toat1 (AATAGA, 26 nt upstream), Txyn1 (AATATA, 28 nt upstream), Ttef1 (AATGAA, 67 nt upstream). These motifs fall within known functional PAS variants reported across eukaryotes [55]. In contrast, TpenDE contained a PAS-like motif (TATAAA) located 143 nt upstream, near the outer boundary of the defined window, and exhibited only intermediate termination efficiency. This suggests that distance from the cleavage site critically influences PAS functionality [58,59]. Notably, several terminators with clearly defined cleavage sites—including Ttif35, TmutA, and Tpdc—lacked any detectable PAS hexamer within the analyzed window. Despite this, these constructs still supported efficient or intermediate termination, indicating that cleavage can occur in the absence of identifiable PAS motifs [60].

To explain cleavage in PAS-lacking terminators, we next examined AU-rich upstream sequence elements (USE, TATAT, TTTTT, TATTT, TTTAT, TTTTA, AAATT, ATATT, ATTTA, ATTTT), which are known to promote 3′-end processing in fungi [56,60]. Based on AU-rich motif counts, terminators were classified as: USE-rich (≥10 motifs): TactA, Tcyc1, Tpdc, USE-moderate (5–9 motifs): most native terminators, USE-sparse (1–4 motifs): synthetic constructs and TxlnA. Importantly, PAS-lacking but functionally efficient terminators such as Ttif35 and Tpdc exhibited moderate-to-rich USE content, suggesting that AU-rich sequence context can compensate for the absence of a canonical PAS [35]. Conversely, sparse USE in the synthetic terminators likely explains its weak termination despite carrying a canonical AATAAA, as the AU-rich context is required to stabilize the CPA complex at the hexamer-defined cleavage site.

Finally, we examined the occurrence of T-stretches (≥5 consecutive thymidines), which have been described as downstream positioning elements in S. cerevisiae and A. oryzae [56,60]. T-stretches were present in only a subset of terminators and showed no consistent relationship with termination efficiency. For example, Tcyc1 contained multiple strong T-stretches yet exhibited weak termination, while efficient terminators such as Tpdc, TpenDE, and Ttef1 lacked such elements entirely. These observations suggest that, in the A. oryzae system examined here, T-stretch elements are neither required nor sufficient for efficient 3′-end processing and likely play a context-dependent or secondary role.

Together, these results indicate that terminator function in A. oryzae is governed by combinatorial sequence features rather than any single motif [61,62]. Efficient termination can be achieved through multiple architectures, including canonical PAS-driven cleavage (e.g., TactA, TtrpC) or variant PAS-supported cleavage (e.g., Toat1, Txyn1) or PAS-independent mechanisms supported by AU-rich context (e.g., Ttif35, Tpdc). These findings highlight the flexibility of fungal polyadenylation signals and provide a mechanistic framework for rational terminator design.

The canonical AATAAA is not required

To experimentally validate the functional importance of the PAS motif identified by 3′RACE, we constructed targeted variants of TactA, the highest-performing terminator with a well-characterized local sequence architecture. TactA contains a canonical AATAAA hexamer at positions 610–615, embedded within an extended AU-rich region spanning positions 565–679 (69.6% AU content), and its cleavage site was confirmed by 3′RACE at position 622—21 nt downstream of the hexamer, consistent with the classical NUE positioning model [57].

To test the necessity of this hexamer, we introduced a targeted disruption to generate the mutant terminator, TactA-ΔPAS. As is shown in Table 3, surprisingly, the mCherry reporter revealed that disruption of the canonical PAS had no significant effect on overall expression. The iMFI for TactA-ΔPAS was 682,000 AU compared to 685,000 AU for the wild-type construct, representing a statistically insignificant 1.004-fold change. Meanwhile, the termination efficiency was also unchanged (RTI from 0.0035 in complete TactA to RTI = 0.0039 in TactA-ΔPAS). 3′RACE revealed a dramatic upstream repositioning of the cleavage site from position 622 to position 216—a displacement of 406 nucleotides. The new cleavage site was directed by a cryptic AATAGA hexamer located 21 nt upstream, with cleavage occurring at a guanosine residue and generating a 17 nt poly(A) tail. These results demonstrate that the canonical AATAAA in TactA functions primarily to position the cleavage site rather than to determine whether termination occurs. Deletion of the AATAAA disrupts the entire PAS cluster causing the cleavage machinery to bypass this region entirely and activate a cryptic AATAGA signal 437 nt upstream. The maintenance of near-identical termination efficiency (RTI 0.0035 vs 0.0039) despite this large positional shift demonstrates that the terminator sequence harbours redundant cleavage-competent signals distributed across its length, and that the A. oryzae CPA machinery can utilize these signals with comparable efficiency. Discussion

This study demonstrates that heterologous 3′ terminators are functional in A. oryzae. The more than 5-fold variation in mCherry expression and over 50-fold range in RTI observed across the 15-terminator panel demonstrate that the choice of terminator is a consequential design variable in fungal expression constructs—comparable in impact to promoter selection, which has received considerably more attention in the A. oryzae synthetic biology literature [63].

Termination efficiency correlates only weakly with protein-level fluorescence, whereas its influence is transmitted more strongly through mRNA abundance [10,43]. This indicates that high protein output and efficient transcriptional termination are not obligatorily linked properties, and that terminators can be selected to optimize either parameter independently. For instance, Tcyc1 from S. cerevisiae supported strong mCherry expression but exhibited poor termination efficiency, making it suitable for single-gene constructs but potentially problematic in multi-cassette configurations. Conversely, TtrpC from A. nidulans achieved efficient termination with moderate expression. The identification of TmutA, TactA, and Txyn1 as terminators that combine both high expression and efficient termination makes them particularly valuable for multi-gene pathway engineering in A. oryzae, where both properties are simultaneously required.

The polyadenylation maps obtained by 3′RACE provide mechanistic support for these functional differences. Efficient and moderately efficient terminators such as TactA, TmutA, Toat1, Tpdc, TpenDE, and TtrpC generated focused cleavage sites and long, continuous poly(A) tails, characteristic of effective 3′-end formation. These sequences consistently contained recognizable A[AT]TAAA-like PAS hexamers in the near-upstream element (NUE) window and upstream U-rich motifs that align with canonical fungal 3′-end architecture [36,55]. In contrast, weak or nonfunctional terminators—including the minimal synthetic sequences and TxlnA—showed short or heterogeneous A-tracts and internal priming events, suggesting inefficient CPA complex recruitment or poorly defined cleavage windows. Tsyn25, although compact, retained detectable activity, likely reflecting the presence of a minimally functional PAS-like signature or local structural permissiveness rather than any global sequence property. Importantly, neither overall GC content nor terminator length correlated with RTI, indicating that termination efficiency is shaped by local 3′-end features rather than simple compositional metrics [64,65].

The targeted mutagenesis experiment with TactA-ΔPAS provides an important mechanistic nuance: disruption of the canonical AATAAA hexamer did not significantly impair mCherry expression or termination efficiency, despite this motif being the primary PAS in the wild-type terminator. This result demonstrates that the canonical PAS is not strictly required for efficient 3′-end formation in A. oryzae when AU-rich upstream sequence elements (USEs) are present at sufficient density. This finding is consistent with observations in S. cerevisiae and plants, where degenerate PAS variants and upstream efficiency elements can compensate for the absence of the canonical hexamer [66,67], and extends this principle to filamentous fungi. These results suggest that USE density, rather than canonical PAS presence alone, may be a more reliable predictor of terminator strength in A. oryzae—a hypothesis that could be tested by systematic USE mutagenesis in future work.

Interestingly, terminator performance varied substantially across constructs and did not strictly follow species origin. For example, TxlnA from A. nidulans showed low performance across all criteria in A. oryzae, whereas Tcyc1 from Saccharomyces cerevisiae showed inefficient termination but still supported upstream protein expression. These observations indicate that terminator behavior can be highly context-dependent and cannot be reliably predicted from species origin alone. Consistent with previous fungal synthetic-biology toolkits, cross-compatibility of regulatory elements can vary substantially, particularly across larger evolutionary distances [12]. This has practical implications for terminator discovery: rather than restricting searches to closely related Aspergillus species, broader screening across Ascomycota may identify high-performing elements, provided that key sequence features are present. Therefore cross-species portability cannot be assumed and that direct benchmarking in the target chassis is necessary for reliable construct design.

The robustness of terminator rankings across three carbon sources (glucose, maltose, and dextrin) and across two structurally distinct reporter proteins strongly supports the conclusion that terminator function is largely sequence-intrinsic and independent of metabolic context or protein identity. This is an important practical finding: it implies that terminator performance characterized in one expression context can be reliably transferred to other constructs and cultivation conditions, reducing the need for case-by-case re-optimization.

While informative, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. All constructs were integrated at a single genomic locus to minimize positional effects, yet local chromatin variation may still influence absolute expression levels [68,69]. Furthermore, the panel of 15 terminators, while diverse, does not exhaustively cover the sequence space of fungal 3′-end elements, and additional terminators—including fully synthetic variants designed based on the sequence features identified here—may further expand the dynamic range available to engineers. In addition, 3′RACE was applied to a subset of terminators; broader analysis using high-throughput 3′-end sequencing methods such as QuantSeq [70] or 3′-seq would enable comprehensive mapping of cleavage heterogeneity. Finally, the observed difference in correlation strength between RTI and transcript abundance (ρ = −0.55) and between transcript and protein levels (ρ = +0.61) suggests that post-transcriptional regulatory processes may also contribute to expression output. Differences in mRNA stability and 3′UTR-mediated effects on translation efficiency may influence protein production independently of termination efficiency. Although the consistency between transcript and protein trends indicates that transcriptional termination is a major determinant in this system, additional regulatory contributions cannot be excluded. Direct measurements of mRNA half-life and systematic analysis of 3′UTR features were not performed in this study and therefore remain important directions for future work. Future studies combining promoter–terminator libraries and targeted mutagenesis could further clarify minimal sequence requirements for efficient termination, as well as how termination efficiency interacts with post-transcriptional pathways such as nonsense-mediated decay and alternative polyadenylation, which jointly regulate transcript stability and isoform diversity. Together, these findings establish a functional and mechanistic foundation for 3′-end engineering in A. oryzae. By linking termination efficiency to mRNA and protein output and identifying local sequence features associated with effective 3′-end formation, this work provides practical guidelines for selecting or designing terminators with predictable behavior. The availability of strong elements such as TmutA, TactA, Txyn1, and TtrpC—alongside compact options like Tsyn25—offers immediate utility for building insulated expression cassettes, multi-gene pathways, and stable genetic circuits. More broadly, these insights open the door to rational 3′-end engineering and the development of modular, scalable expression toolkits for fungal biotechnology and cell-factory design.
