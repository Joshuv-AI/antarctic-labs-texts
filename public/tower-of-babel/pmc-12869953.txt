Evolutionary genomics of KPC-2-producing Serratia marcescens and characterization of a novel blaKPC−2-harboring plasmid
Authors: Yali Zheng, Ying Jing, Yan Zhang, Jin Zhang, Jianbo Ye, Huimin Chen, Yuting Jin, Jiayu He, Yu Zhang, Xinhua Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerratia marcescens is a common opportunistic pathogen in hospitals. Its carbapenem-resistant strains, especially those carrying the blaKPC−2 gene, pose a serious challenge to clinical infection treatment.MethodsIn this study, we identified 14 strains that were positive for blaKPC−2 from tertiary hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China, through phenotypic tests and sequencing. We also incorporated data from the GenBank public database, resulting in a total of 2005 Serratia strains included for bioinformatic analysis.ResultsA total of 121 strains (6.03%) were found to carry blaKPC−2. These positive isolates predominantly originated from China (76.86%) and the United States (14.88%), with the vast majority (94.21%) identified as clinical isolates. The period from 2018 to 2019 was relatively active for global data collection, particularly for samples from the Yangtze River Delta region in China. Phylogenetic analysis classified the 121 strains carrying blaKPC−2 into six evolutionary clusters (Cluster I-VI), with most strains grouped in Cluster V and Cluster VI. Cluster VI, largely consisting of ST595, was predominantly composed of Chinese isolates. In contrast, Cluster V displayed coexistence of diverse sequence types and geographic origins. Resistance gene profiling identified 52 antimicrobial resistance genes. Besides blaKPC−2, three strains were also positive for blaNDM−1. Additionally, a novel plasmid lineage was identified and designated as pK17-KPC/pK1030-KPC (approximately 87 kb). Its replication initiator gene, repA1, showed low homology (≤ 93.25%) to known plasmids. This plasmid has a complex structure and carries a blaKPC−2 resistance module mediated by unit transposon ΔTn6296.ConclusionsThis study systematically describes the global transmission patterns and molecular genetic features of Serratia marcescens strains carrying blaKPC−2. It reveals the structural characteristics of a novel plasmid harboring blaKPC−2 and provides key scientific evidence for clinical prevention and control of antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04617-w.Keywords: Serratia marcescens, Carbapenem-resistant strains, Phylogenetic analysis, Novel plasmid, Antimicrobial resistance, Unit transposon ΔTn6296

Introduction

Serratia is a genus of Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic bacteria belonging to the family Yersiniaceae in the order Enterobacterales. It is commonly found in nature and hospitals and is a frequent cause of opportunistic infections [1]. Among them, Serratia marcescens (S. marcescens) is one of the most clinically important species in this genus. It is associated with various infections, including urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, meningitis, conjunctivitis, and endocarditis [2, 3]. This bacterium demonstrates a strong capacity to adhere and invade surfaces of medical devices, such as catheters, needles, and oxygen equipment [4, 5]. It is also capable of surviving in harsh environments, including sinks, disinfectants, and even distilled water [3, 6], highlighting its notable environmental adaptability and persistent resistance. In recent years, S. marcescens has garnered global attention as a drug-resistant pathogen. It is frequently implicated in hospital outbreaks, especially among high-risk groups such as neonates and intensive care unit patients. Of greater concern, strains isolated from these clinical settings often exhibit multidrug resistance, posing substantial challenges for clinical anti-infective therapy [7, 8].

Carbapenem antibiotics are widely regarded as a last-line treatment for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections due to their broad-spectrum and potent antibacterial activity [9, 10]. The use of these antibiotics has also contributed to the continuous emergence of carbapenem-resistant S. marcescens. The primary resistance mechanism involves the production of carbapenemases, including Class A (such as KPC), Class B (metallo-β-lactamases, such as NDM, IMP, VIM), and Class D (such as OXA-48-type) enzymes [11]. To date, multiple carbapenemases including KPC, NDM, IMP, VIM, OXA-48, and SME have been detected in S. marcescens, with the KPC type being the most frequently reported [12]. However, most cases of blaKPC−2 carrying S. marcescens have been documented only as isolated incidents or localized outbreaks [13, 14]. Systematic studies remain lacking regarding its global transmission patterns, population structure evolution, and co-dissemination mechanisms with other resistance genes.

Plasmids, as key mobile genetic elements, are the primary vectors for the spread of the carbapenemase gene blaKPC−2. This gene has been identified in various epidemic plasmids, including common types such as IncF, IncN, IncX, and IncR, which together form a complex transmission network [15, 16]. With the advancement and wider adoption of sequencing technologies, novel plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 continue to be identified. Examples include hybrid plasmids, plasmids with atypical replicons, and megaplasmids, such as pK522-B-KPC [17] and pM92-KPC2 [18]. These plasmids often carry multiple resistance genes and mobile genetic elements, enhancing the horizontal transfer potential and spread risk of blaKPC−2. This situation is expected to inevitably accelerate the dissemination of the blaKPC−2 gene across bacterial species [19, 20].

This study collected clinical isolates of S. marcescens carrying blaKPC−2 from multiple tertiary hospitals in China. These isolates were combined with similar strains obtained from seven countries and regions worldwide between 2013 and 2025 for a systematic comparative genomic analysis. The research focused on global transmission patterns, genomic microevolution characteristics, and the co-transmission mechanisms of blaKPC−2 with other resistance genes. We also characterized the genetic structure of novel plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 and conducted in-depth analysis of the genetic environment surrounding this gene to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying its dissemination and accumulation.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, species identification and phenotypic assays

Bacterial strains were initially identified by Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), Further, bacterial species identification was performed using genome sequence-based Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis [21]. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) of bacterial isolates was performed using the BioMérieux VITEK 2 system, with results interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2024 guidelines. The carbapenemase was detected using NG-Test CARBA 5, a rapid diagnostic assay based on immunochromatographic assay.

Sequencing and sequence assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted from all 14 strains using the Gentra Puregene Yeast/Bacteria Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Libraries were constructed with the TruePrepTM DNA Library Prep Kit V2 and the SQK-LSK109 Ligation Sequencing Kit, followed by sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and the GridION X5 platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), respectively. The Illumina platform generated 150 bp paired-end reads with a coverage depth greater than 100X. Raw data from both the HiSeq X 10 and GridION X5 platforms were initially processed using fastp v1.0.1 [22] with parameters set to Q20 > 90% and Q30 > 85% for quality filtering and adapter trimming, followed by further polishing with Canu version 1.8 (https://canu.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) to generate relatively high-quality clean reads. De novo assembly of Illumina paired-end short reads and Nanopore long reads was performed using Unicycler (version 0.4.5; https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler).

Phylogenetic tree construction and ANI analysis

Publicly available genome data of S. marcescens (last accessed July 1, 2025) were downloaded from the GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/), all of which had an assembly level of contig or higher. All genomes were quality assessed using Quast 5.0.2 with an N50 ≥ 40 kb threshold. Strains carrying the blaKPC−2 gene were further selected. ANI values between all strains and the reference strain ELP1.10 (GenBank accession number CP127881.1) were calculated using FastANI v1.34 [21]. All strains included in the analysis met the ANI threshold of ≥ 95% (species delineation cutoff) to ensure taxonomic accuracy.

Pairwise ANI values among all S. marcescens genomes were calculated using FastANI, and an ANI heatmap was generated. this study used the chromosomal sequence of strain ELP1.10 as the reference and the chromosomal sequence of Serratia liquefaciens (S. liquefaciens) ATCC 27,592 (GenBank accession number CP006252.1) as the outgroup. The genomic sequences of all strains were aligned against the complete chromosome sequence of the reference strain ELP1.10 using Mummer v3.23 (https://mummer.sourceforge.net/) to identify core single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Repetitive DNA regions were detected and masked using RepeatMasker (http://www.repeatmasker.org/), and SNPs within these regions were filtered out. Subsequently, ClonalFrameML [23] was used to analyze the core SNPs and remove those introduced by homologous recombination events, thereby minimizing their confounding effect on phylogenetic inference. Based on the chromosomal sequences of 126 bacterial strains, a final set of 32,574 recombinant-filtered core SNPs sites was identified. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the recombinant-filtered core SNPs using RAxML [24] under the GTR model with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de).

Strain metadata extraction

A custom Python script was used to extract key metadata from S. marcescens strains carrying the blaKPC−2 gene, including their source, host information, and isolation location. By integrating this data with metadata from our own sequenced strains, we established a global distribution database for S. marcescens that carries blaKPC−2.

Screening of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), Multi-Locus sequence types (MLST) profiles, and plasmid types

A local sequence database was constructed using abricate (v1.0.1; https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with the ResFinder database (version 2024-03−22), applying thresholds of ≥ 90% for both identity and coverage. The detected ARGs were classified in a binary manner as present or absent, and the resulting matrix was visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12) in R (v4.2.2; https://www.r-project.org). A custom plasmid sequence database was constructed using the PlasmidFinder database (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/) integrated with plasmid sequences obtained through laboratory sequencing for plasmid typing of bacterial strains. MLST is a standardized typing method based on housekeeping gene sequences and serves as an important tool in bacterial epidemiological research [25]. A rapid MLST typing workflow was applied to analyze carrying blaKPC−2 S. marcescens isolates. Standard sequences and allele profile files of six housekeeping genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, recA) were obtained from the pubMLST database(https://pubmlst.org/). A local reference database was established using the abricate tool, followed by sequence matching with 100% strict alignment parameters. Sequence types (STs) were determined through cross-validation. Typing accuracy was ensured by using a standardized database, stringent matching criteria, and cross-validation.

Phylogenetic analysis of plasmid replication initiation genes and evolutionary relationship validation

Based on the novel plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 identified in the sequenced isolates, homologous replication initiation sequences were obtained by BLAST screening with identity and coverage thresholds set at ≥ 85%. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT v7.525 [26]. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed with RAxML-NG v1.1 [24] under the GTR + G model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. Additionally, ANI values among plasmid rep sequences were calculated using pyani (v0.3.0; https://github.com/widdowquinn/pyani)and visualized as a heatmap.

Genome annotation and comparative analysis

Functional annotation was performed using the RAST 2.0 (https://rast.nmpdr.org/) online platform. Resistance genes, mobile genetic elements, and other genomic features were annotated using online databases including CARD [27], ResFinder [28], ISFinder [29], INTEGRALL [30], and DANMEL [31]. Results of the plasmid mobile element structure comparison and resistance region analysis were visualized using Inkscape (https://inkscape.org/).

Conjugal transfer

The conjugation transfer experiment of the novel blaKPC−2-carrying plasmid in S. marcescens was performed following the previously established experimental protocol [32].

Nucleotide sequence accession number

The complete sequences of the chromosomes of K17 and K1030, along with the plasmids pK17-KPC and pK1030-KPC, have been deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers CP099459.1, CP119435.1, ON784343.1, and CP119436.1, respectively. Sequence accession numbers for the other 12 strains are provided in Table S1.

Results

Species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility test

This study isolated 20 carbapenem-resistant S. marcescens strains from sputum and whole blood specimens collected from four tertiary hospitals in Zhejiang Province between 2016 and 2023. Phenotypic enzyme testing confirmed that 14 of these strains produced KPC-type carbapenemases. All strains were subjected to whole genome sequencing using the Illumina platform. ANI analysis revealed that all strains exhibited ANI values no less than 95% when compared with the reference strain ELP1.10, confirming their accurate identification as S. marcescens. Genomic screening for resistance genes revealed that all strains carried the carbapenemase gene blaKPC−2. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed these strains exhibited a multidrug-resistant phenotype: in addition to carbapenem resistance, they were also resistant to most cephalosporins, quinolones (including ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin), and tobramycin. Notably, some strains remained susceptible to tetracycline-class antibiotics such as tigecycline, doxycycline, and minocycline. Furthermore, amikacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole also demonstrated relatively high in vitro susceptibility rates (Table 1).

Spatiotemporal distribution of S. marcescens strains carrying blaKPC−2 gene

A total of 1,991 publicly available S. marcescens genome sequences were collected from the GenBank database, and combined with 14 newly sequenced strains from this study, forming a global analytical dataset of 2,005 strains spanning 46 countries and regions. Among these, 121strains were identified as carrying the blaKPC−2 gene, including 14 from this study and 107 from public databases, yielding an overall carriage rate of 6.03%. Notably, no strains carrying blaKPC−2 gene were detected in several countries and regions, including Australia, Canada, and Switzerland (Table S1).

All blaKPC−2 positive strains were isolated between 2013 and 2025 and distributed across seven countries and regions. Analysis by sample source showed that clinical strains constituted a clear majority (94.21%, 114/121). Environmental isolates accounted for only 1.65% (2/121), and the source information was unavailable for five strains (4.13%). Geographically, the strains were primarily obtained from China (n = 93, 76.86%) and the United States (n = 18, 14.88%). Within China, more than 80% of the positive strains (79/93) were isolated from the Yangtze River Delta region, with fewer strains detected in other parts of the country (Fig. 1; Table S2).

The temporal dynamics of blaKPC−2 prevalence showed distinct phased characteristics. The highest number of blaKPC−2 positive strains (n = 74) were submitted to NCBI between 2018 and 2019, accounting for 57.85% of all positive isolates. In comparison, the prevalence intensity was relatively lower during 2013–2017 and after 2020, though detections continued persistently. This temporal trend was particularly prominent in China (Fig. 1, Table S3).

Phylogenetic and ANI analysis of strains carrying blaKPC−2

To elucidate the genetic background of strains harboring blaKPC−2, we performed a phylogenetic analysis, which grouped all strains into six distinct evolutionary clusters (Cluster I-VI) (Fig. 2). ANI heatmap analysis (Fig. 3) further confirmed the reliability of this clustering pattern: strains formed six distinct and highly homologous (ANI > 98.3%) genomic clusters along the diagonal, showing strong agreement with the topological structure of the phylogenetic tree. Cross-cluster comparisons indicated that ANI values between Cluster I-IV and other clusters ranged from 94.4% to 96.1%, suggesting the possibility of notable genomic divergence among different clusters(Table S4).

MLST typing revealed that 28 distinct sequence types (STs) were identified among all 121 strains. ST595 was the main type accounting for 63.64% (77/121) of all strains, and was exclusively found in Cluster VI. Cluster IV contained only a single strain with a unique sequence type, ST325 (Fig. 2).

Regarding the number of strains in each cluster, Cluster VI was the largest branch, comprising 80 strains (66.12%%), followed by Cluster V with 30 strains (24.79%). Among the 14 newly sequenced strains in this study, 11 were grouped into Cluster VI and 3 into Cluster V. Further analysis of Cluster VI showed that the vast majority of strains (98.75%, 79/80) were of Chinese origin. Among these, ST595 was the dominant type (96.25%, 77/80), and most isolates within this cluster (73.75%, 59/80) were obtained during 2018–2019. In contrast, Cluster V showed a broader time span (2019–2025) and demonstrated a multi-country, multi-sequence type coexistence pattern. Strains in this cluster were isolated from six countries: Argentina, China, the United States, Brazil, Ecuador, and Chile. The dominant sequence type in this cluster was ST640 (30%, 9/30), while other types such as ST367 and ST439 were also present. Notably, unlike the diverse distribution of U.S. blaKPC−2-positive strains across all six evolutionary clusters, Chinese strains were highly concentrated in Cluster VI, indicating a distinct transmission pattern. Overall, this bacterial population displays high genetic diversity(Fig. 2, Table S3).

Distribution characteristics of ARGs

In addition to carrying the blaKPC−2 gene, genomic analysis of the 121 S. marcescens strains also revealed the presence of numerous other acquired antimicrobial resistance genes. A total of 52 different types of resistance genes were identified, spanning 10 categories of antimicrobial agents, including: beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, quinolones, phenicols, rifampicins, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, macrolides, trimethoprim, and fosfomycin(Fig. 4, Table S5). Among the 14 strains we sequenced, in addition to carrying the blaKPC−2 gene, we also identified other resistance genes. These include the aminoglycoside resistance genes aac(3)-IId and aac(6’)-Ic, the beta-lactamase genes blaLAP−2 and blaSRT−1, and the tetracycline resistance gene tet(41). Strain K17 carries the aminoglycoside resistance genes aac(3)-IId and aac(6’)-Ic, along with the beta-lactamase gene blaSRT-1. Strain K1030 carries aac(6’)-Ic and blaSRT−2.

Further analysis revealed significant differences in the distribution frequency of various resistance gene categories and their carriage patterns across different evolutionary clusters. Beta lactam and aminoglycoside resistance genes showed the highest diversity, with 15 distinct genes detected in each category. In terms of prevalence, the aminoglycoside modifying enzyme gene aac(6′)-Ic had the highest carriage rate (99.17%, 120/121), followed by the beta lactamase gene blaSRT−1 (79.34%, 96/121) and the plasmid mediated quinolone resistance gene qnrS1 (72.73%, 88/121). The latter two genes were exclusively detected in Cluster V and Cluster VI. Notably, the beta lactamase gene blaLAP−2 showed a carriage rate of 52.07% (63/121) and was completely confined to Cluster VI. Meanwhile, both blaCTX−M−14 (49.59%, 60/1211) and blaLAP−2 were only identified in strains of Chinese origin, suggesting their transmission may have geographical specificity(Table S6).

On the other hand, some resistance genes showed relatively low prevalence rates and distinctive distribution patterns. The tetracycline efflux pump gene tet(41) (9.92%, 11/121) and the beta-lactamase gene blaSST−1 (8.26%, 10/121) were predominantly enriched in Clusters I–III and exhibited highly overlapping distributions, suggesting potential co-selection or linked transmission. Particularly noteworthy was the simultaneous detection of the carbapenemase genes blaKPC−2 and blaNDM−1 in three strains. This coexistence may lead to broader resistance profiles and poses a serious threat to clinical treatment (Fig. 4, Table S7).

Plasmid typing of the 126 S. marcescens isolates revealed several incompatibility groups, including IncFIIK, IncFII, IncM and IncR. Notably, some strains in Cluster VI were found to carry both IncFIIK and IncFII types. However, isolates K17 and K1030 did not match any known plasmid types (Fig. 4, Table S3).

Genetic characterization of pK17-KPC

Preliminary analysis of the Illumina sequencing data from the 14 strains in this study suggested that the blaKPC−2 gene in strains K17 and K1030 might be located on novel plasmids, while the remaining strains carried known plasmid types. We further performed whole-genome sequencing of these two strains using long-read technology and completed sequence assembly, successfully obtaining two complete circular plasmid sequences designated as pK17-KPC (GenBank accession number ON784343.1) and pK1030-KPC (GenBank accession number CP119436.1). Genomic comparison revealed that these two plasmids share high homology, with nucleotide sequence identity and coverage both exceeding 99.99%, differing only by a few single-base variations.

To determine their taxonomic position, we performed a BLAST comparison of the replication initiation gene repA1 from plasmid pK17-KPC against all known plasmid sequences in the GenBank database. The results showed that, aside from pK1030-KPC, the most similar reference plasmid replication gene (GenBank accession no.: AP028600.1) shared only 93.25% identity. We then selected 44 closely related plasmids with both identity and coverage ≥ 85% based on the repA1 gene sequence for phylogenetic analysis (Tables S8, S9). These plasmids were primarily derived from the genus Serratia (n = 43), with the exception of one originating from K. pneumoniae. They were isolated between 2004 and 2024 from a broad geographic range that includes nine countries, such as Japan, China, and France. Among these, 43 originated from S. marcescens, and except for the two plasmids from this study, none of the others carried carbapenemase genes. Both the phylogenetic analysis of the plasmid repA1 gene and the ANI analysis confirmed that pK17-KPC and pK1030-KPC form a distinct branch independent of all known lineages, representing a novel plasmid lineage (Figure S1, Figure S2).

We further focused on pK17-KPC for in-depth structural characterization. The plasmid has a total length of 87.23 kb with a GC content of 59.72%, and contains 91 predicted open reading frames (ORFs) (Fig. 5), including the critical carbapenemase gene blaKPC−2. We analyzed the sequence using the online tool OriV-Finder (https://tubic.org/OriV-Finder/) and successfully identified a replication origin (oriV). Its structure can be divided into a backbone region and two accessory modules: a 21.1 kb blaKPC−2 resistance region and an IS110 family insertion sequence. The backbone region (54.99 kb) includes functional units related to replication, maintenance, and conjugative transfer. The replication region contains, in addition to the 948 bp repA1 gene, the regulatory genes repA2 and repA6. The maintenance region includes genes such as parA and parB. The conjugative transfer region (38.02 kb) encodes a set of type IV conjugation system proteins, including traA, traB, traC, and traD (Table S10).

Schematic diagram of the plasmid. Note: Genes are shown as arrows colored by functional category. The two innermost circles display GC skew ([G-C]/[G + C]) and GC content, respectively

The 21.1 kb blaKPC−2 resistance region exhibits a complex composite transposon structure, integrating six complete or truncated mobile genetic elements: ΔTn6296, ΔIS26, ΔTn21, IS5075, ΔIS903B, and ISKpn14. We performed BLASTN analysis of this fragment sequence on NCBI. The results showed high homology with plasmid sequences from K. pneumoniae and S. marcescens, with the maximum coverage reaching 93% and the highest identity being 99.91%. Among these, ΔTn6296 originates from the Tn21 family transposon Tn6296 (first identified in Klebsiella pneumoniae strain KP048). Its typical structure includes IRL (inverted repeat left) - tnpA (transposase) - tnpR (resolvase) - res (resolution site) - IRR_1 (inverted repeat right 1) - mcp (methyl accepting chemotaxis protein gene) - IRR_2 (inverted repeat right 2). In this plasmid, the structure is truncated, characterized by a deleted mcp gene (Δmcp) and a partial deletion of the right side Tn6376 segment, resulting in the current truncated ΔTn6296 structure. However, it retains the core resistance module, which consists of ΔISKpn6 - blaKPC−2 - partial ΔTn6376. The other accessory module is an IS110 family insertion sequence, located between the traF and traQ genes within the conjugative transfer region (Fig. 6). We also attempted to perform conjugation experiments, but no transconjugants carrying the pK17-KPC plasmid were obtained. It is speculated that the insertion of an IS110 family insertion sequence in the conjugation transfer region may have disrupted its function, ultimately leading to the failure of conjugation. This mechanism has been similarly documented in previous research, which confirmed that plasmid pACM130 suffered functional impairment of its conjugative transfer region due to the insertion of transposon Tn1331, ultimately leading to the loss of conjugative function [33].

Discussion

S. marcescens is an important opportunistic pathogen in clinical settings. The emergence of carbapenem-resistant strains has posed a serious challenge to clinical infection treatment [34, 35]. Among these, carrying the blaKPC−2 gene is one of the primary mechanisms mediating carbapenem resistance in this bacterium. Therefore, conducting systematic epidemiological research on such resistant strains is of great significance. Several studies have been conducted on blaKPC−2-positive S. marcescens, including individual case reports, localized outbreaks in hospital settings, and global epidemiological analyses of carbapenem-resistant Serratia species [36–38]. However, a systematic understanding of the global transmission dynamics, population structure, and co-transmission mechanisms of the blaKPC−2 gene in this pathogen is still lacking. This study combined public genomic data from the NCBI database with our own sequencing data to conduct the systematic analysis of global transmission trends in blaKPC−2 positive S. marcescens.

This study revealed significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the global dissemination of blaKPC−2 positive S. marcescens. Rather than uniform spread, its transmission patterns were concentrated in specific regions and time periods, suggesting the potential existence of localized, active transmission chains over a short period. Geographically, the strains showed regional clustering. China and the United States jointly represent the major global epidemic centers. Notably, over 80% of Chinese strains were concentrated in the Yangtze River Delta region. This suggests the possible existence of an independent local transmission chain in this region, which has led to the successful establishment and regional spread of one or more dominant clones. In contrast, while the United States also serves as a major endemic country, its strains are phylogenetically dispersed across multiple clades, suggesting multiple independent introduction events and a complex transmission history. Meanwhile, no cases have been reported in several countries including Australia, Canada, and Switzerland. This may reflect differences in infection control, antibiotic stewardship, or genomic surveillance in these regions, but could also be due to sampling bias in public databases. The primary isolation period for these strains, based on NCBI data, fell between 2018 and 2019, with most submissions originating from China.

Subsequently, we further elucidated the genetic basis of this transmission pattern through phylogenetic and ANI analysis. All strains were classified into six evolutionary clusters, which may indicate considerable genetic diversity. However, the most notable among these is Cluster VI, which is dominated by ST595 and comprised almost exclusively (98.75%) of Chinese isolates, and was primarily prevalent during 2018–2019. These findings suggest a possible hospital-associated outbreak driven by this predominant clone (ST595) in China, particularly in the Yangtze River Delta region, during this period. We have searched the NCBI database up to July 2025 and found that no new ST595 isolates from the Yangtze River Delta region of China have been reported in the past two years. This indicates that the ST595 clone was likely a transient local strain that did not show sustained evolution or broader transmission potential. Therefore, continued surveillance of this region for new ST595 strains or emerging lineages remains essential for assessing long-term public health risks and understanding local transmission patterns, and this will be an important focus of our future research. This pattern of clonal dominance contrasts markedly with the polyclonal population structure observed in the United States, where strains were dispersed across multiple clusters. This difference suggests distinct drivers of blaKPC−2 dissemination in the two regions: potentially driven by the expansion of one or a few successful clones in China, as opposed to multiple independent acquisition events or plasmid-mediated horizontal transfer in the United States.

S. marcescens carrying blaKPC−2 not only commonly possesses a multidrug-resistant genetic background, but also exhibits distinct evolutionary lineage specificity and geographical clustering in its resistance gene pool. For example, the β-lactamase genes blaLAP−2 and blaCTX−M−14 were found almost exclusively in Chinese Cluster VI strains, suggesting that this clone may have acquired specific resistance elements during its local spread. On the other hand, the aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme gene aac(6′)-Ic was nearly universally present (99.21%), indicating that it may be located on the chromosome or linked to conserved mobile genetic elements. Strains carrying both blaKPC−2 and blaNDM−1 have now been observed to spread among various species of Enterobacteriaceae. Particularly in studies of K. pneumoniae, ST11-type carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) typically evolves from KPC-producing strains that acquire plasmids carrying blaNDM [39]. This study identified three strains co-harboring blaKPC−2 and blaNDM−1. There is a need for heightened vigilance regarding the co-transfer and accumulation of different carbapenemase genes across distinct plasmids or genetic backgrounds, which may lead to enhanced drug resistance. Furthermore, the co-enrichment of specific resistance genes (such as tet(41) and blaSST−1) in Cluster I-III suggests that these genes may be transmitted together via the same mobile genetic element (such as plasmid or integron) and are likely co-selected and maintained within specific genetic contexts.

Replicon typing is currently the most widely used scheme for plasmid typing. This method classifies plasmids into replicon (rep) groups to reflect their incompatibility (Inc) characteristics. As the replicon type directly determines the Inc grouping, these two terms are often used interchangeably in practice [40, 41]. Previous studies have reported numerous cases of plasmid-mediated transmission of carbapenem resistance genes in S. marcescens. Examples include IncC plasmids carrying blaIMP−4 [42] as well as IncK and IncX6 type plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 [43, 44]. This study identified two novel plasmids, pK17-KPC and pK130-KPC, from clinical isolates collected across different years in two hospitals in Zhejiang Province. These plasmids showed high sequence homology and were confirmed through phylogenetic analysis and ANI comparison to belong to a new plasmid lineage, which forms a distinct clade phylogenetically distant from closely related plasmids lacking carbapenemase genes. This indicates that pK17-KPC/pK1030-KPC did not directly acquire the blaKPC−2 gene from an existing mobile plasmid, but may have undergone an independent and unique evolutionary process. This view is further supported by the low sequence similarity (up to only 93.25%) between its replicon region and those of known types, suggesting that its replication and maintenance mechanisms may possess novel features. Although strains K1030 and K17 are located in different phylogenetic clusters (Cluster 5 and Cluster 6, respectively) and belong to different sequence types (ST394 and ST111), they both carry a novel plasmid encoding blaKPC−2. This finding suggests that this new plasmid lineage may have undergone horizontal transfer among different clonal lineages of S. marcescens. It is necessary to maintain ongoing monitoring of these novel plasmids carrying carbapenemase genes. This discovery reveals new characteristics of carbapenem resistance in S. marcescens and suggests that a transmission network for new types of resistance plasmids may already be established in this region, highlighting the need for enhanced regional surveillance of antimicrobial resistance.

This study has several main limitations. First, the genomic data were primarily obtained from public databases, which may lead to uneven geographic distribution and could affect the representativeness of the samples. Moreover, the antimicrobial susceptibility testing was based on only 14 bacterial isolates that were self-collected and tested in our study, resulting in a limited and single-sourced sample size that may not fully reflect the true distribution of clinical resistance profiles. Although the final dataset used for tree construction consisted of high-quality, recombinant-filtered core SNPs, we acknowledge that the absence of invariant sites in this dataset may introduce some bias in the base frequency estimates derived from the GTR model. Additionally, a systematic investigation of the plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 remains insufficient. Further analysis of their epidemiological transmission characteristics is needed in the future. We plan to include more strains with spatiotemporal diversity to improve the reliability and representativeness of the results, and to provide a more comprehensive summary of the transmission mechanisms of all relevant plasmids.

Conclusion

This study conducted a systematic analysis of S. marcescens carrying the blaKPC−2 gene worldwide, revealing its epidemiological characteristics. Comprehensive findings indicate that S. marcescens carrying blaKPC−2 has become a significant global public health threat requiring heightened attention. The transmission of this bacterium is not randomly distributed but shows notable geographical imbalance and temporal dynamics, with China and the United States currently being the major epidemic centers. The Yangtze River Delta region in China is particularly affected, demonstrating distinct clonal clustering and outbreak phenomena. Evolutionary analysis revealed a complex population structure, which could be divided into six phylogenetic clusters. Notably, the high-risk clone ST595 (representing Cluster VI) was temporally associated with the peak of isolates collected in China during 2018–2019, suggesting potential clonal expansion. In addition to carrying blaKPC−2, these strains also co-harbor a substantial number of other resistance genes, forming an extensive resistance gene reservoir that could potentially leads to broad resistance against multiple antimicrobial agents. Furthermore, this study identified and characterized a novel plasmid lineage (pK17-KPC/pK1030-KPC). This plasmid features a complex structure and carries the blaKPC−2 gene, significantly increasing the risk of horizontal gene transfer of this resistance determinant among different strains and even different bacterial species. The findings of this study provide important scientific evidence for public health authorities to prevent and control carbapenem-resistant S. marcescens.
