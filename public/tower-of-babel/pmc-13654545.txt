RaspberryJalisco: A field-collected multi-class dataset of Raspberry (Rubus idaeus) phenological stages from Mexican commercial orchards with YOLOv8 benchmarks
Authors: Jaime Adan Cuevas-Ramirez, Carlos Alberto Villaseñor-Padilla, Javier Gomez-Avila, Carlos Lopez-Franco, Jesus Hernandez-Barragan, Gehova Lopez-Gonzalez, Nancy Arana-Daniel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present RaspberryJalisco, a field-collected annotated image dataset of raspberry (Rubus idaeus) fruits at distinct phenological stages, captured in a commercial orchard in Jocotepec, Jalisco, Mexico, during November–December 2025. The dataset comprises 11,884 tiled images and 174,763 bounding-box annotations distributed across two complementary labeling schemes: a fine-grained 5-class configuration (Bud, BrightRed C4, DarkRed C5, Green, Orange (red dot)) and a coarser 3-class configuration (Immature, BrightRed C4, Orange (red dot)) designed for yield-prediction and harvest-planning tasks. Images were acquired using a DSLR camera under natural midday illumination and manually annotated by three domain-expert annotators with LabelImg. In response to the risk of parent-image leakage after tiling, the revised release includes persistent parent-image identifiers, random and parent-image grouped split manifests, a leakage audit, corrected random-split baselines, and leakage-resistant grouped YOLOv8 benchmarks. All annotations are provided in YOLO normalized format, with dataset descriptors, scripts, source tables, figures, and YOLOv8 benchmark weights. The data can be reused for in-domain fruit detection, fruit counting, phenological analysis, model benchmarking, and method development, while cross-site or cross-season deployment should be validated externally.

Background

Raspberry (Rubus idaeus) is a high-value soft fruit with growing global demand; Mexico is a leading exporter, with Jalisco - particularly the Lake Chapala region - accounting for approximately 40% of national production [5]. Manual inspection is impractical given labor shortages, dense canopy occlusion, and the asynchronous ripening of buds, green, transitional, and ripe fruits on the same cane, motivating single-stage detectors such as the YOLO family [6,7] for real-time fruit detection [8]. RaspberrySet [3] established an early reference point; prior work by the present authors reached mAP@50 = 0.740 with YOLOv8m [9], with limitations in minority-class detection and occlusion handling.

Because the final images are tiles from higher-resolution photographs, tile-level random splitting can place related tiles from the same parent photograph across subsets, inflating apparent generalization. We report both the original random split and a revised parent-image grouped split assigning all tiles from one source photograph to a single subset.

Recent multi-domain evidence shows that strong in-domain mAP does not guarantee field robustness under domain shifts such as illumination, occlusion, cultivar morphology, or acquisition site [10]. We therefore interpret the present benchmarks as controlled in-domain, leakage-audited reference values requiring external validation before wider deployment.

Split manifests and leakage audit

Each tiled image is associated with a persistent parent_image_id derived from the source photograph identifier and tile offsets. The revision package includes two split manifests: grouped_split_manifest.csv and random_split_manifest.csv. Each manifest contains tile_id, parent_image_id, x_offset, y_offset, split, image_filename, and label_filename. The grouped split assigns all tiles from a parent image to a single subset, while the random split records the original tile-level random partition used as the historical baseline.

The leakage audit quantified parent-image overlap between subsets. In the random split, 1529 parent images were shared between train and validation, 927 between train and test, and 607 between validation and test. In the grouped split, all pairwise parent-image overlaps were zero. The grouped split therefore satisfies the revised leakage-resistant evaluation criterion.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study site and image acquisition

Images were collected in a commercial raspberry orchard in the municipality of Jocotepec, Jalisco, Mexico (Lake Chapala region). Image acquisition was conducted during November–December 2025, covering successive harvest cycles. All images were acquired under natural midday illumination (10:00–14:00 h local time) using a DSLR camera at a native resolution of 4000 × 3000 px, hand-held at approximately 30–60 cm from the plant row with a slightly lateral angle.

Image preprocessing and tiling

Raw images at 2000 × 1500 px were partitioned into non-overlapping tiles of 640 × 640 px using a sliding-window approach, yielding the 11,884 tiles that constitute the training corpus. This strategy preserves fine-grained texture and color information used to discriminate between spectrally similar classes [4].

Manual annotation

Bounding boxes were drawn using LabelImg [1] (v1.8.6) by a team of three domain-expert annotators. Each annotator worked independently on a designated subset; ambiguous cases were resolved by consensus. A senior annotator performed a final quality-control pass over 100% of annotations. The dataset was then imported into Roboflow [2] for the original random split, export to YOLOv8 format, and generation of both class variants. No image-level augmentation was applied in Roboflow; all augmentation was performed on-the-fly by the Ultralytics training engine.

Grouped split and stratified evaluation protocol

The grouped split was reconstructed from tile-level metadata by grouping records by parent_image_id before assigning them to train, validation, and test. Split integrity was assessed by computing the set intersection of parent_image_id values for train-validation, train-test, and validation-test pairs. A grouped split was considered leakage-free only when all three intersections were zero.

To avoid ambiguity between class schemes, the revised package uses immutable data views for random five-class, random three-class, grouped five-class, and grouped three-class labels. The random five-class view contains 8318 train, 2378 validation, and 1188 test images with class IDs 0–4. The random three-class view preserves the same image membership but stores remapped class IDs 0–2.

Fruit-size stratification was computed from bounding-box area after scaling normalized YOLO box width and height to a 640 × 640 px reference frame. Small, medium, and large bins were defined by the one-third and two-third area quantiles within each class configuration. Occlusion was approximated by the maximum IoU between a ground-truth object and any neighboring ground-truth object in the same tile. Objects with maximum neighbor IoU below 0.10 were assigned to low occlusion, objects from 0.10 to below 0.30 to medium occlusion, and objects at or above 0.30 to high occlusion.

YOLOv8 benchmark experiments

Model family

YOLOv8 [7] is a single-stage anchor-free detector built on CSPDarknet53 with a decoupled detection head and composite loss:L=λboxLbox+λclsLcls+λdflLdflwith λbox=7.5, λcls=0.5, λdfl=1.5 [7]. Lbox uses CIoU [12]; Lcls is binary cross-entropy; Ldfl is Distribution Focal Loss. Five scale variants were evaluated: nano (n), small (s), medium (m), large (l), and extra-large (x).

The composite loss includes CIoU bounding-box regression [12], binary cross-entropy classification loss, and Distribution Focal Loss. Revision experiments used Ultralytics 8.4.104 and PyTorch 2.8.0 on a CPU server (Intel Xeon Platinum 8592 V). Evaluation metrics

Performance was assessed on the held-out test split using Precision (P), Recall (R), mAP@50, and mAP@50–95 (standard COCO metric). Per-class precision, recall, AP@50, and AP@50–95 were extracted from the Ultralytics validation API. Numeric confusion matrices were computed with confidence threshold 0.25 and IoU threshold 0.45, then normalized by true-class row. Stratified AP and recall were computed with detections matching same-class ground truth outside the target stratum ignored for that stratum.

Results

After correcting the random five-class labels, five-class random-vs-grouped differences were small: mAP@50 differences ranged from −0.017 to 0.003, where negative values indicate grouped outperforming random. In contrast, the three-class random split was consistently higher than grouped by 0.002 to 0.032 mAP@50, with the largest gaps for YOLOv8l and YOLOv8x. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, all images were collected at a single geographic site (Jocotepec, Jalisco), limiting generalization to other climatic zones. Second, data cover a single seasonal window; intra-season phenological transitions are not represented. Third, although the revised grouped split removes parent-image leakage among train, validation, and test subsets, it does not substitute for external validation on independent orchards, cultivars, seasons, or acquisition hardware. Fourth, dense canopy occlusion and small fruit size remain important failure modes, as shown by the stratified AP analysis. Fifth, annotations are bounding-box only; segmentation masks are not included. Sixth, benchmark inference times were measured on CPU only and may differ on GPU or edge-AI accelerators.
