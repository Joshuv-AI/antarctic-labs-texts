Comparative investigation of extended-gate GaAsSb/InGaAs HTFET biosensors for label-free biomolecule detection
Authors: Mahin Anjum, Md. Mehedi Hasan, Md. Feroz Ali, Md Shafiul Alam, Obaidullah Obaidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dielectric-modulated heterostructure tunnel FET (HTFET)-based nano-cavity integrated biosensors are emerging as energy-efficient, ultra-sensitive label-free detection platforms. This study investigates and compares three GaAsSb/InGaAs-based biosensor architectures: single cavity dual material double composite gate HTFET (SC-DM-DG HTFET), dual cavity dual material double composite gate HTFET (DC-DM-DG HTFET), and single cavity dual material extended double composite gate HTFET (SC-DM-EDG HTFET). The III–V heterostructure in the SC-DM-EDG HTFET enhances tunnelling probability, gate control, and on-current (ION), thereby providing higher sensitivity than previously reported GaAsSb/InGaAs-based HTFET and FET biosensors. Sensitivity and selectivity parameters for ION, transconductance (gm), and threshold voltage (Vth) were evaluated for various neutral and charged biomolecules. Under the standard bias condition (VDS = 1.5 V, VGS = 1.3 V), the SC-DM-EDG HTFET exhibits the highest ION of 3.02 × 10−4 A/μm among the three structures; under a separately re-optimized bias (VDS = 0.9 V, VGS = 1 V), the same structure instead achieves maximum sensitivities of 3.35 × 106 (ION) and 3.49 × 105 (gm) for K = 12, at a correspondingly reduced ION of 1.91 × 10−4 A/μm. The maximum Vth sensitivity of 0.49 is achieved by this structure under the standard bias condition. In biomolecule differentiation, the SC-DM-DG HTFET demonstrates peak ION and gm selectivity values of 9.55 and 4.64 for K = 12, respectively, while the peak Vth selectivity value of 0.3 appears in the SC-DM-EDG HTFET also for the same biomolecule. Furthermore, the SC-DM-EDG HTFET achieves superior ION, gm, and Vth sensitivities for charged biomolecules, confirming its robustness, selectivity, and potential for next-generation, high-performance biosensing applications.

Introduction

The precise identification of nano-biomolecules has become essential with the rapid progress of microbiology. Biomolecules such as DNA, biotin, streptavidin, amino acids, APTES, gelatin, and various bacteria or viruses must be detected efficiently to understand abnormal cellular behavior [1, 2]. FET based biosensors have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional laboratory sensors because of their miniaturization, robustness and low power consumption [1, 3].

Among these, dielectrically modulated MOSFET biosensors with nanocavities have gained prominence for their flexibility and accuracy [4]. A functionalized nano-gap dielectric beneath the gate metal hosts selective receptors that capture target biomolecules, producing a gating effect translated into measurable outputs such as drain-current variation or threshold-voltage shift. Because different biomolecules exhibit distinct dielectric constants—streptavidin (K = 2.1), biotin (K = 2.63), gluten (K = 5), zenin (K = 7), keratin (K = 11), and gelatin (K = 12) [5, 6], their selective detection becomes feasible. However, despite their promise, MOSFET-based biosensors face intrinsic limits: short-channel effects (SCEs) and a 60 mV/dec subthreshold swing (SS) bound at room temperature [7, 8]. These restrict voltage scaling and sensing efficiency, while fabrication complexity and device variability hinder large-scale uniformity.

To address these constraints, TFETs have drawn attention for their steeper SS, stronger gate control, and higher sensitivity [9, 10]. TFETs operate via band-to-band tunnelling (BTBT) in a p-i-n or n-i-p configuration [11, 12], allowing sub-60 mV/dec switching and ultra-low-power operation. Yet early silicon-based homojunction TFETs delivered limited ION due to silicon’s indirect bandgap and high tunnelling mass. Heterojunction TFETs have emerged as a compelling solution to the intrinsic limitations of conventional TFETs by leveraging narrow-bandgap source materials to enhance band-to-band tunnelling through staggered or broken-gap alignment. However, IV–IV heterostructures, such as strained SiGe sources [13] and pocket-doped SiGe channels [14], remain constrained by lattice mismatch, limited band offsets, and low current drivability. This has accelerated the transition toward III–V systems, including InGaAs/Si [5], GaAs/AlGaAs/GaSb [15], and InAs/Si [16], which offer improved tunnelling efficiency but still face trade-offs between performance and manufacturability.

Among these, the GaAsSb/InGaAs material system stands out due to its compositionally tunable type-II alignment, enabling near broken-gap conditions and superior tunnelling characteristics. Notably, [17] experimentally fabricated GaAsSb/InGaAs quantum-well TFETs, establishing strong technological viability. Further validation is provided by [18], which demonstrated heterojunction Esaki diodes with excellent agreement between simulated and measured I–V characteristics, and by [19], which reported a GaAsSb/InGaAs heterojunction TFET with a nanoscale In0.7Ga0.3As pocket layer, achieving a high on-state current of 11.98 μA/μm at room temperature. Additional designs, including GAA and L-shaped TFETs [20, 21], further exploit broken or quasi-broken gap alignment to achieve high ION and steep SS.

Collectively, these studies confirm that GaAsSb/InGaAs heterojunction TFETs are technologically viable; however, the design space of such devices for biosensing remains insufficiently understood. In the present work, we comparatively investigate three GaAsSb/InGaAs HTFET biosensor topologies SC-DM-DG, DC-DM-DG, and SC-DM-EDG to determine how cavity placement, extended-gate engineering and bias condition influence the electrical and sensing characteristics. In contrast to earlier reports that mainly examined a single structure, this study provides a unified comparison of ION, gm, and Vth-based sensitivity and selectivity for both neutral and charged biomolecules, together with a physical interpretation based on electric-field modulation and surface-potential control. While prior biosensor studies such as [22, 23] demonstrated promising sensitivity using GaAsSb/InGaAs-based TFET configurations, they did not systematically address architecture-level trade-offs among current drivability, relative sensitivity, and extended-gate-assisted electrostatic control. This gap motivates the present comparative analysis, which aims to establish an architecture-guided GaAsSb/InGaAs biosensor design framework by revealing how cavity placement and extended-gate engineering govern electric-field enhancement, tunnelling behavior, sensitivity, selectivity, and current drivability. To the best of our knowledge, the extended dual-material-gate (EDG) architecture is reported here for the first time on a GaAsSb/InGaAs type-II heterojunction platform. In particular, by evaluating each architectural change at matched bias before separately re-optimizing the bias point, this work isolates which design element is actually responsible for the reported sensitivity improvement, showing that it is the extended-gate architecture acting jointly with bias re-optimization, rather than cavity placement or gate extension in isolation, that yields the highest sensitivity.

Device structure and simulation framework

The two-dimensional schematic structures along with dimensional parameters of the (a) SC-DM-DG HTFET, (b) DC-DM-DG HTFET, and (c) proposed SC-DM-EDG HTFET are illustrated in Fig. 1. GaAsSb is used as source material and InGaAs is being used as channel and drain material. Overall, it is a p-i-n structure. InGaAs is a low direct bandgap material, with very low electron tunnelling mass (me) compared to silicon. This feature makes it suitable for channel region as tunnelling probability depends on bandgap and tunnelling effective mass. Again, InGaAs has electron and hole mobility many orders higher than that of silicon [24].Fig. 12D schematic diagrams: (a) SC-DM-DG HTFET (b) DC-DM-DG HTFET (c) SC-DM-EDG HTFET

2D schematic diagrams: (a) SC-DM-DG HTFET (b) DC-DM-DG HTFET (c) SC-DM-EDG HTFET

The compositions In0.3Ga0.7As and GaAs0.75Sb0.25 were chosen to form a moderate type-II GaAsSb/InGaAs heterojunction, thereby providing a balanced trade-off between tunnelling enhancement and interface quality. Based on Vegard-law interpolation, the lattice constants of In0.3Ga0.7As and GaAs0.75Sb0.25 are 5.7748Å and 5.7639Å, respectively, corresponding to a lattice mismatch of only ~ 0.19%. This value is comparable to that of the widely used InP-matched In0.53Ga0.47As/GaAs0.49Sb0.51 system [18]. Although In-richer and Sb-richer alloy combinations may further lower the effective tunnelling barrier and enhance ION, they also tend to produce a more aggressive staggered band alignment [20], which can compromise OFF-state controllability and increase the sensitivity of device performance to interface defects. Hence, the selected composition represents a compromise among low lattice mismatch, favorable type-II band alignment, threshold-voltage controllability, and sufficiently high band-to-band tunnelling (BTBT) probability for biosensing applications. Although the selected In0.3Ga0.7As/–GaAs0.75Sb0.25 heterojunction is not exactly lattice matched to a standard binary III–V substrate, its effective lattice constant ( ~ 5.769Å) lies closer to InP (5.8687Å) than to GaAs (5.65325Å). Therefore, an InP-based platform with a metamorphic or compositionally graded buffer is a realistic substrate route for epitaxial realization. Alternatively, a relaxed virtual substrate engineered near 5.769Å may be employed to further reduce mismatch-induced defects.

For all three structures, the source section is heavily doped with p-type dopants at a concentration of roughly 5 × 1019cm−3, guaranteeing a high carrier population required for efficient BTBT. The channel section carries a light n-type doping near 1 × 1016cm−3 to preserve low leakage current and to suppress the ambipolar response of the TFET. The drain section is given a moderate n-type doping of about 1 × 1018cm−3, ensuring that the series resistance stays sufficiently low to support large current conduction when the device operates in the on-state. Silver (Ag) and copper (Cu) serve as the gate and contact metals, with respective work functions of 4.2 eV and 4.7 eV. The gate lengths (Lgate1, Lgate2) are fixed at 10 nm and 30 nm. The higher work function of Ag at the tunnelling gate guarantees robust gate control over the tunnelling zone, whereas Cu delivers stable electrical connections for the auxiliary gate, source, and drain. The dual-gate TFET has dual benefits of higher ION and low off current (IOFF), thereby improving the overall \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{I}_{{\rm{ON}}}}{{I}_{{\rm{OFF}}}}$$\end{document}IONIOFF ratio. The gate metal work function is selected in a way that carrier tunnelling occurs exclusively when the nano-gap is occupied by biomolecules, thereby enabling a precise variation in the sensing parameter to be detected [16]. Moreover, the cavity height (Hgap) is chosen to be 6 nm, a value comparable to those reported in [5, 25].

The SC-DM-DG HTFET represents the fundamental configuration, incorporating only a single cavity on each side of the gate. The cavity length (Lgap) is defined through comprehensive simulations, and the optimal value is then chosen accordingly. The DC-DM-DG HTFET has an additional cavity on the drain side to achieve better control over dielectric modulation. The cavity dimensions are determined through detailed simulations, and the optimal cavity lengths (Lgap1 = 17nm, Lgap2 = 10nm) are subsequently selected. A dual-cavity design provides two sensing regions, so even small changes in dielectric constant produce a larger perturbation in the electric field. MOSFET operation relies on gate-channel coupling, which determines the parameters of the inversion layer in the channel. Thus, introducing a dual cavity can increase the sensitivity with respect to K dramatically. However, in TFETs, ION is very sensitive to the electrostatic potential near the tunnelling junction only, so introducing an extra cavity on the drain side should have very little impact on the sensitivities.

The SC-DM-EDG HTFET is the most structurally complex among the three proposed devices; however, it offers several important advantages. The extended tunnelling gate improves electrostatic control near the source-channel tunnelling junction, thereby enhancing the local electric field and increasing the BTBT rate, while the extended auxiliary gate helps regulate thermionic emission [5]. Additionally, the extended tunnelling gate reduces leakage and ambipolar current [26]. The device incorporates two extended gates: an Ag-based extended gate on the tunnelling side and a Cu-based extended gate on the drain side. Owing to this configuration, the electric field at the source–channel junction is strengthened and the surface-potential profile becomes steeper, leading to improved current conduction and gate controllability. The geometric parameters of the extended gates, namely the thickness (TEG) and length (LEG), are illustrated in Fig. 1c. The proposed device was simulated using SILVACO TCAD to analyze its electrical characteristics. The dielectric constant, K, was varied to model the presence of different biomolecules. To simulate charged biomolecules, a fixed interface charge N is applied uniformly along the entire nano-cavity length (Lgap) at the interface between the nano-cavity and the channel surface, representing the interfacial charge between the biomolecules and the channel. Six charged biomolecules are considered, with K = 5 and N = ( − 2, − 1, − 0.2, 0.2, 1, 2) × 1012C cm−2. The simulation employed the Non-Local Band-to-Band tunnelling (BBT.NONLOCAL) and Forward Bias tunnelling (BBT.FORWARD) models, with the device operating under room-temperature conditions at 300K, as (BBT.NONLOCAL) accounts the energy bands in spatial profile manner and it includes the spatial separation of electrons and holes generated in conduction and valance bands respectively [26]. The Fermi-Dirac distribution model (FERMI) was used to account for Fermi statistics in determining the intrinsic carrier concentration. Additionally, several physical models were incorporated to ensure realistic device behavior. The Shockley–Read–Hall model (SRH) was used to account for trap-assisted carrier generation and recombination via defect states, while the Auger model (AUGER) was included to capture non-radiative recombination under high carrier concentration. The transverse field-dependent mobility model (CVT) was employed to describe mobility degradation under strong normal electric fields, whereas the field-dependent mobility model (FLDMOB) accounts for carrier velocity degradation at high electric fields. The concentration-dependent mobility model (CONMOB) and concentration-dependent SRH lifetime model (CONSRH) were used to include the effects of doping on carrier mobility and lifetime, respectively. Finally, the band-gap narrowing model (BGN) was enabled to account for the effective reduction of band gap in heavily doped regions. This model set is adapted from established practice already used in previously published TFET and dielectric-modulated TFET/MOSFET biosensor simulation studies [5, 15, 16, 26] and then calibrated and validated against the experimental data reported in [18], as shown in Fig. 3. Since this reference calibration was obtained for the closely related, InP-lattice-matched In0.53Ga0.47As/GaAs0.49Sb0.51 composition, the calibration parameters like tunnelling effective masses of our own In0.3Ga0.7As/GaAs0.75Sb0.25 layers were obtained independently via Vegard-law interpolation between the respective binary end-point compounds.Fig. 3Calibration and validation of the simulated transfer characteristics against the experimental data reported in [18].

Sensitivity analysis

Gelatin (K = 12) is treated as the representative high-K case because it is the largest dielectric constant among the commonly reported receptor/target biomolecules in this range [5, 15], allowing the device’s sensitivity and selectivity trends to be evaluated across the full span from near-unity (air) to this upper bound. Figure 9a exhibits the variation of ION sensitivity with K for the three biosensor structures. It is observed that the sensitivity increases with higher K due to stronger gate-to-channel coupling, which enhances the E at the source-channel tunnelling junction, thereby reducing tunnelling width and increasing tunnelling probability. Among the devices, the SC-DM-DG structure exhibits the highest sensitivity, followed by DC-DM-DG. Interestingly, although the SC-DM-EDG HTFET demonstrates the maximum absolute ION owing to its strong electric field and steep surface potential profile, its sensitivity remains lower. This is attributed to the relatively high baseline current in the absence of biomolecules, which reduces the relative change upon biomolecule introduction. Consequently, under the applied bias conditions (VDS = 1.5), the SC-DM-EDG structure already achieves enough band alignment to flow current when there is air in the nano-cavity. Again, as the extended gate is quite close to the tunnel junction, the electric field coupling is higher even if no biomolecule is present. So, under this high supply voltage, the SC-DM-EDG structure has a higher current drive capability at the expense of sensitivity, whereas the SC-DM-DG configuration provides superior sensitivity performance. In a similar manner, the SC-DM-EDG HTFET shows the highest gm value, although its sensitivity is quite lower due to the bias condition, as it allows current to flow even if there is no biomolecule present. The SC-DM-DG HTFET achieves the highest gm sensitivity of 1.3 × 104 for K = 12. However, the pattern is quite opposite in the case of Vth sensitivity. SC-DM-EDG HTFET achieves the highest sensitivity of approximately 0.5 for K = 12, while the figure for SC-DM-DG HTFET is only around 0.3.

Besides sensitivity, the ION is another crucial factor for biomolecule detection. If the on current is not adequately high, it may cause false detection or even complete failure of detection. Conventional MOSFET-based biosensors typically provide large ION values, whereas homojunction TFETs fall short in delivering strong on-current. In contrast, these heterojunction designs offer sufficiently high ION for detecting biomolecules with larger K. Among them, the SC-DM-EDG HTFET structure delivers greater on-current compared to the other two architectures, thereby simplifying the detection process.

At the applied bias (VDS = 1.5 V, VGS = 1.3 V), the SC-DM-EDG HTFET induces enough band bending and reduces the tunnelling distance sufficiently so that current flows even in the absence of biomolecules (K = 1). This explains why the sensitivity remains quite low under this DC bias point, as the current change after biomolecule introduction is not significant. That’s why, optimal bias point (VDS = 0.9 V, VGS = 1 V) was identified by repeatedly sweeping and recalibrating the simulation over a range of VDS and VGS values, rather than being fixed a priori. By adjusting the bias to this value, the sensitivity of the SC-DM-EDG HTFET improves dramatically. However, this improvement in sensitivity comes at the cost of reduced ION. Still, the current achieved by the SC-DM-EDG HTFET under this condition is comparable to that of the SC-DM-DG HTFET at the earlier bias point. Selectivity (ΔS) is a crucial figure of merit in TFET-based biosensors, as it defines the ability of the device to electrically discriminate between the dielectric constants associated with different biomolecules, each individually assumed to fully occupy the nano-cavity. Impact of charged biomolecules

In addition, certain biomolecules, such as DNA, may undergo ionization once they bind with the receptors. It becomes essential to study the operation of a biosensor when both neutral and charged biomolecules are present. As a result, sharp band bending occurs at the source-channel junction. This process lowers the threshold voltage and raises the ION of the structure [16].

On the other hand, if − N increases, then ψs decreases, reducing device sensitivity. This occurs because of the increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{-N}{{C}_{{\rm{eff}}}}$$\end{document}−NCeff factor at the nano-cavity/channel interface, which reduces electron accumulation in the lightly doped n-type InGaAs channel and elevates the threshold voltage. Consequently, the tunnelling width at the source-channel interface grows, which suppresses the tunnelling rate, and thus decreases the ION of the biosensor. Noise and practical detection considerations

The reported sensitivity and selectivity are based on deterministic DC characteristics, whereas practical TFET biosensor operation is also affected by noise, variability, and non-ideal cavity filling. A quantitative noise figure of merit for FET biosensors is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{SNR}}=({\rm{\Delta }}{\psi }_{0}/\sqrt{BW})\times [{g}_{m}/\sqrt{{S}_{I}}]$$\end{document}SNR=(Δψ0/BW)×[gm/SI], where Δψ0 is the biomolecule-induced surface-potential shift, gm the transconductance, SI the current noise power spectral density, and BW the measurement bandwidth [30]. Evaluating it, and the corresponding detection limit, requires noise-aware simulation (flicker, shot, and random-telegraph-noise modelling) or measured device data, neither available within the present deterministic TCAD framework. III–V heterojunction TFETs reportedly show lower flicker-noise impact than homojunction devices, with low-frequency noise dominated by trap states near the tunnelling junction rather than the channel-mobility fluctuations governing MOSFET noise [31], suggesting favorable noise behavior for this device family, though not separately verified for the GaAsSb/InGaAs HTFETs studied here. A quantitative SNR, detection limit, and noise margin remain a direction for future noise-aware simulation and experimental work. Moreover, steric hindrance caused by receptor/linker molecules and partial filling of the nanocavity can weaken dielectric modulation by reducing the effective biomolecule occupancy. As demonstrated in [5], such effects may be modeled more completely in future work using different cavity-filling profiles to emulate incomplete or nonuniform biomolecule immobilization. As a limited check, we approximated fractional gelatin (K = 12) cavity occupancy using an effective dielectric constant Keff = fK + (1 − f)Kair, where f ∈ [0, 1] is the occupied nano-cavity fraction and Kair = 1, for SC-DM-EDG topology under re-optimized bias (VDS = 0.9 V, VGS = 1 V). The ION sensitivity falls from 3.35 × 106 at full occupancy to roughly 2.0 × 106 at 75% and 8.7 × 105 at 50% occupancy, then drops sharply to ~ 7.7 × 104 at 25% occupancy. The same qualitative trend (moderate partial filling has limited impact; low occupancy substantially degrades sensitivity) seen at standard bias condition (VDS = 1.5 V, VGS = 1.3 V) as well. A full quantitative treatment of interface traps, trap-assisted tunnelling, and process-induced variability remains beyond the present study’s scope and is left for future experimental validation. Even so, the strong current and threshold-voltage modulation predicted here still suggests promising intrinsic sensing potential.

Several additional factors relevant to experimental realization remain outside this simulation study’s scope. The proposed fabrication route (Fig. 2) is adapted from demonstrated GaAsSb/InGaAs heterojunction diode processing [18], but integrating the extended dual-material gate and nanocavity at the stated dimensions has not been experimentally verified and may be affected by process variation in gate length, cavity height, and doping. Interface trap states at the gate-dielectric/channel and nano-cavity/channel interfaces, and random dopant fluctuations at the heavily doped source-channel junction, are additional expected sources of device-to-device variability in IOFF and ION not captured by the present deterministic TCAD framework, requiring statistical or atomistic-level simulation to quantify. Because band-to-band tunnelling is inherently less thermally activated than the thermionic emission governing MOSFET operation, TFETs are generally reported to show weaker temperature dependence of subthreshold swing and on-current than MOSFETs; this expected trend has not been separately verified here and is left for future electro-thermal characterization. The simulations also omit electrolyte screening: in a physiological buffer, mobile ions can screen the biomolecule-induced field over the Debye length, potentially reducing the sensitivity reported here for charged-biomolecule detection (Fig. 13 and 14). Receptor chemistry linker length, surface density, and binding orientation was likewise not modeled, though it can affect both the effective dielectric constant and achievable cavity occupancy; experimental fabrication and measurement of the proposed device are needed to validate the predicted performance. Finally, the dielectric constants used here, including gelatin’s K = 12, are each biomolecule’s commonly reported effective/dry-state value assuming uniform nanocavity occupancy; hydration and physiological-buffer effects on the effective permittivity are not modeled explicitly, consistent with the convention adopted in prior dielectric-modulated TFET biosensor studies [5, 6].

Disentangling the sources of sensitivity enhancement

Because three architectural changes are examined here (cavity placement, gate extension, and bias point), it is necessary to isolate which one is responsible for the reported sensitivity gain, rather than crediting the combined architecture in general terms. Table 1 makes this decomposition explicit. At matched bias (VDS = 1.5 V, VGS = 1.3 V), changing only the cavity configuration (SC-DM-DG → DC-DM-DG) slightly reducesION sensitivity (9.26 × 104 → 7.94 × 104), showing that TFET sensing performance is governed by the source-channel tunnelling junction, not by cavity placement. Adding the extended gate at the same bias (SC-DM-DG → SC-DM-EDG) raises ION (2.51 × 10−4 → 3.02 × 10−4 A/μm) but reduces sensitivity by nearly two orders of magnitude, to 2.43 × 103, because the stronger junction coupling sustains measurable current even with air (K = 1) in the cavity, inflating the baseline used to normalize Eq. (3). Neither change alone, therefore, explains the highest reported sensitivity: 3.35 × 106 is obtained only when the extended gate is combined with a re-optimized bias (VDS = 0.9 V, VGS = 1 V) that suppresses the air-cavity baseline while keeping ION (1.91 × 10−4 A/μm) at the same order as the other structures. This bias shift affects selectivity differently: ION selectivity falls from 9.55 to 1.95 at matched bias and only partially recovers, to 7.57, remaining below the original SC-DM-DG value unlike sensitivity, which rises to roughly 36 × the SC-DM-DG baseline. Sensitivity and selectivity are therefore not simultaneously maximized; the re-optimized bias point is best understood as a sensitivity-selectivity-current trade-off, not a uniform improvement. The corresponding air-cavity (K = 1) currents, ION(K = 12)/Son, are 2.71 × 10−9 A/μm (SC-DM-DG), 2.71 × 10−9 A/μm (DC-DM-DG), 1.24 × 10−7 A/μm (SC-DM-EDG, standard bias), and 5.70 × 10−11 A/μm (SC-DM-EDG, optimal bias) confirming ΔION stays comparable ( ~ 10−4 A/μm) across all four cases. As shown in Fig. 10, applying the same bias shift to SC-DM-DG does not reproduce this outcome: its ION collapses to an impractically low level, and DC-DM-DG, sharing the same non-extended-gate electrostatics, is expected to behave the same way, since cavity placement was already shown to have only a marginal effect on sensitivity. The extended gate is therefore not a direct source of higher sensitivity but the architectural feature that makes this ION-sensitivity trade-off exploitable at all distinguishing this work from prior GaAsSb/InGaAs dual-material-gate biosensors [22, 23], which report sensitivities of 104–105 without examining gate extension or bias re-optimization, and from prior extended-gate biosensors on IV–IV or InGaAs/Si platforms [5], which do not combine gate extension with a type-II GaAsSb/InGaAs junction or cavity-placement trade-offs.

Comparison with other work

The comparative analysis in Table 2 highlights that conventional and early TFET biosensors offered limited sensitivity, typically ranging from 10 to 102, due to weak electrostatic control and high tunnelling barriers. Later architectures, including double-gate and dual-metal designs, enhanced gate coupling and achieved sensitivities up to 105-106[32–34]. The proposed GaAsSb/InGaAs SC-DM-EDG HTFET achieves a sensitivity of 3.35 × 106 under a re-optimized bias point (at a correspondingly reduced ION of 1.91 × 10−4 A/μm), while, as shown in Disentangling the Sources of Sensitivity Enhancement section, the same structure separately delivers the highest ION of 3.02 × 10−4 A/μm among all three architectures under the standard bias condition used for the sensitivity and selectivity comparisons in Figs. 9 and 12. Conclusion

A GaAsSb/InGaAs-based SC-DM-EDG HTFET nanocavity biosensor has been proposed and analyzed for ultra-sensitive detection of neutral and charged biomolecules. Leveraging a type-II heterojunction and an extended dual-material gate, the device enhances tunnelling efficiency and electric field coupling at the source-channel interface, achieving a sensitivity of 3.35 × 106 (VDS = 0.9 V, VGS = 1 V) and a high ION of 3.02 × 10−4 A/μm (standard bias, VDS = 1.5 V, VGS = 1.3 V), among the highest reported for GaAsSb/InGaAs-based TFET biosensors. The single-cavity design ensures strong dielectric modulation while simplifying fabrication, and flexible biasing enables operation for either maximum sensitivity or peak current drive. These features, combined with potential material-level optimizations such as high-K dielectrics, tailored gate work functions, and channel engineering, make the SC-DM-EDG HTFET a promising candidate for integration into lab-on-chip and point-of-care biosensing platforms, advancing high-performance, low-power nanoelectronic biosensors.
