Emerging paradigms in nanostructured targeted drug delivery systems
Authors: Anjum N Hasnain, Darshan R Telange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanostructured drug delivery systems have emerged as powerful and versatile approaches to overcome the limitations of conventional therapeutic strategies, including poor bioavailability, non-specific distribution, and dose-limiting toxicity. By enabling precise control over particle size, surface characteristics, drug loading, and release kinetics, nanocarriers offer enhanced pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic performance, along with improved therapeutic specificity. This comprehensive review critically examines emerging paradigms in Nanostructured drug delivery systems, with a particular focus on design strategies, surface functionalization, and translational potential for targeted therapeutics. The review systematically discusses the fundamental principles governing nanocarrier behavior, including physicochemical characteristics, nano–bio interactions, and pharmacokinetic considerations. Various classes of Nanostructured drug delivery systems—such as lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, carbon-based, and biomimetic carriers—are evaluated with respect to their design rationale, advantages, and limitations. Advanced targeting strategies, including passive and active targeting, ligand-based functionalization, and stealth modifications, are highlighted for their role in enhancing site-specific delivery and therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, the review explores cellular uptake mechanisms, intracellular trafficking, and subcellular targeting, providing insights into how nanocarriers can be engineered to overcome biological barriers. The translational landscape of nanomedicine is critically assessed, addressing preclinical evaluation, clinical development, manufacturing scalability, regulatory challenges, and safety considerations. Emerging trends, such as artificial intelligence–assisted nanocarrier design, stimuli-responsive systems, bioinspired platforms, and personalized nanomedicine, are also discussed as future directions. Introduction

Conventional drug delivery systems such as tablets, capsules, injections, and topical formulations, have been used as the foundation of treatment of many diseases for decades but despite their widespread use, these systems have several intrinsic limitations that restrict their therapeutic effectiveness [1]. One of the most significant challenges is poor bioavailability, particularly for drugs with low aqueous solubility, limited permeability, or extensive first-pass metabolism [2]. A substantial proportion of newly discovered drug molecules exhibit unfavorable biopharmaceutical properties, rendering conventional dosage forms insufficient for achieving optimal plasma concentrations.

Another major drawback of traditional delivery approaches is the lack of site specificity. Most conventional formulations distribute drugs systemically, exposing both healthy and diseased tissues to the active compound [3]. This nonspecific distribution often results in dose-limiting toxicity, reduced therapeutic index, and undesirable side effects, especially in the treatment of cancer, neurological disorders, and chronic inflammatory diseases. Furthermore, conventional systems generally fail to maintain drug concentrations within the therapeutic window for prolonged periods, necessitating frequent dosing and compromising patient compliance [4].

Physiological barriers, such as the blood–brain barrier, gastrointestinal degradation, enzymatic metabolism, and rapid renal clearance, further limit the effectiveness of traditional drug delivery. These challenges collectively underscore the inadequacy of conventional systems in addressing complex disease pathophysiology and highlight the urgent need for advanced delivery platforms capable of improving drug stability, targeting efficiency, and therapeutic outcomes [5].

Given the constraints of traditional drug delivery systems, nanotechnology has emerged as a pivotal innovation in the twenty-first century. This cutting-edge technology is instrumental in overcoming the barriers associated with pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. The prefix “nano,” originating from the Greek term for “dwarf,” denotes a scale equivalent to one billionth of a meter (109 m). According to the National Nanotechnology Initiative (NNI), nanotechnology is defined as "a science, engineering, and technology conducted at the nanoscale (1–100 nm), where unique phenomena enable novel applications in a wide range of fields, from chemistry, physics, and biology, to medicine, engineering, and electronics.as well explained in Fig. 1 [6].

Rationale for nanotechnology-based therapeutics

Nanostructured drug delivery systems, typically ranging from 10 to 200 nm for systemic application, have emerged as an Innovative approach to address the restrictions of conventional drug formulations as depicted in Fig. 2. Within this size range, nanoparticles can avoid rapid renal clearance (exceeding the 5 nm threshold), reduce opsonization, and leverage abnormal vascular characteristics to improve tissue accumulation. By designing drug carriers at the nanoscale, it is possible to enhance the solubility of hydrophobic drugs, protect unstable molecules from degradation, extend systemic circulation, enable controlled and stimuli-responsive release, and improve intracellular delivery [7]. Nanocarriers can encapsulate, adsorb, or chemically conjugate therapeutic agents, thereby influencing drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination (ADME) in a predictable manner. Importantly, nanosystems support both passive targeting, through enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects in tumours or inflamed tissues, and active targeting, via ligand-mediated receptor recognition. However, it is now widely recognized that variability in EPR among patients significantly affects clinical translation. Consequently, rational nanoparticle design must incorporate both physicochemical optimization and biological stratification [8].

Nanotechnology permits the concurrent delivery of multiple therapeutic agents, including small molecules, proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids, within a single carrier. This capability is particularly useful for combination therapies and custom-made medicine approaches. Historical evolution of nanomedicine

In 1959, Physicist Richard Feynman envisioned nanotechnology as the manipulation of matter at atomic and molecular scale (1–100 nm) [11]. The conceptual foundation of nanomedicine can be traced back to the late twentieth century, coinciding with advances in materials science, colloidal chemistry, and molecular biology.

Early research focused on colloidal drug carriers, such as liposomes, which represented the first generation of nanotherapeutics. The clinical approval of liposomal formulations marked a pivotal milestone, validating the feasibility of nanoscale drug delivery in human medicine.

First Generation: Passive Nanocarriers This stage mainly aimed to enhance pharmacokinetics and minimize toxicity, as demonstrated by liposomal doxorubicin [12, 13].

Subsequent decades have witnessed the development of a wide array of nanostructured systems, including polymeric nanoparticles, [14] solid lipid nanoparticles, dendrimers, and inorganic nanocarriers [15]. These second-generation nanomedicines emphasized improved stability, controlled release, and enhanced drug loading capacity. With the integration of surface modification techniques, nanocarriers have evolved to incorporate targeting ligands and stealth properties, giving rise to actively targeted and long-circulating systems. This period saw the introduction of surface functionalization methods, including PEGylation, antibodies, and peptides, to improve tissue targeting.

Recently, the field has advanced toward third-generation nanomedicine, characterized by smart, stimuli-responsive, and multifunctional platforms capable of diagnosis, therapy, and monitoring. These systems are defined by their stimuli-responsive and theranostic platforms, which combine therapeutic and imaging functions, often featuring bio-responsive release mechanisms. These designed to respond to triggers i.e. Internal (pH, redox gradient, enzymatic activity) or External (Temperature, Magnetic field, Light) and enable control over drug release while participating in diagnostic and therapeutic functions. [16]

This evolution reflects technological refinement, which signals a paradigm shift from empirical formulation to rational. The convergence of nanotechnology with genomics, proteomics, and computational modeling has further accelerated innovation, positioning nanomedicine as a cornerstone of precision and translational healthcare.

Clinical relevance and market growth of nanocarriers

The clinical relevance of nanostructured drug delivery systems is becoming increasingly evident, as demonstrated by the growing number of approved nanomedicines for clinical use and those currently being investigated. By 2024, over 50 nanomedicine formulations had achieved regulatory approval on a global scale, with a significant focus on oncology and infectious diseases. Clinically approved formulations include liposomal chemotherapeutics, lipid nanoparticle-based mRNA vaccines, and polymers. Examples include Doxil® (liposomal doxorubicin), Abraxane® (albumin-bound paclitaxel), and Onpattro® (siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles), each demonstrating improved pharmacokinetics and reduced systemic toxicity compared to conventional formulations [28]. Nanocarriers have shown particular success in oncology, where they improve drug accumulation at tumor sites while mitigating systemic toxicity. In addition to cancer, nanotechnology-based therapeutics have been explored for the treatment of cardiovascular disorders, infectious diseases, neurological conditions, and gene therapy. [17]

The global nanomedicine market, having surpassed an impressive USD 200 billion in 2023, is on a trajectory of remarkable growth, with a projected compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10–15%. This surge is fueled by groundbreaking advancements in targeted therapeutics and biologic delivery systems [23]. From a commercial standpoint, the market's robust expansion is undeniable, driven by an escalating demand for precision therapies,cutting-edge nanofabrication technologies, and substantial investments from leading pharmaceutical giants. Regulatory agencies are stepping up, establishing clearer frameworks for evaluating nanotherapeutics, thereby bolstering their translational potential. The burgeoning clinical pipeline and favorable market dynamics are clear indicators of the evolution of nanocarriers from experimental constructs to commercially viable therapeutic platforms. However, a critical challenge persists: the localization of less than 1% of injected nanoparticles in solid tumors presents a significant translational barrier. Overcoming this delivery inefficiency is not just important; it is imperative for propelling nanomedicine into its next transformative phase [18].

This review aims to synthesize current knowledge, highlight technological advancements, and identify existing challenges that hinder clinical translation.

Special attention is given to targeting mechanisms, biological interactions, safety considerations, and regulatory aspects that influence real-world implementation [19].

Fundamentals of nanostructured drug delivery systems

Definition and classification of nanocarriers

Nanocarriers are submicron-sized delivery systems, typically ranging in size from 1 to 1000 nm, designed to transport therapeutic agents to specific biological targets while enhancing drug stability, bioavailability, and safety. Unlike conventional dosage forms, nanocarriers function as multicomponent systems, wherein the carrier matrix plays an active role in modulating drug release, biodistribution, and cellular uptake [20].

Based on their composition and structural organization, nanocarriers can be broadly classified into several categories. Organic nanocarriers: this mainly involve Biodegradable and good biocompatibility, which mainly involve Lipid-based nanocarriers, such as liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers, utilize physiological lipids to encapsulate drugs, offering excellent biocompatibility and suitability for both hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules. Polymeric nanocarriers, including polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, and dendrimers, are formed using natural or synthetic polymers and provide high formulation flexibility, tunable degradation profiles, and controlled release characteristics. Protein & polysaccharides-based nanoparticle in involves albumin, gelatin, chitosan, alginate nanoparticles which featured mainly natural origin, high biocompatible, Mucoadhesive [21].

Inorganic nanocarriers such as gold nanoparticles, magnetic nanoparticles, and mesoporous silica nanoparticles are distinguished by their rigid structures, high surface area, and unique physicochemical properties that enable imaging, diagnostics, and stimulus responsiveness. Additionally, Biological and biomimetic nanocarriers, including exosomes and cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, have gained increasing attention due to their inherent biological compatibility and immune evasion capabilities. This classification underscores the diversity of nanocarrier platforms and their tailored applicability across various therapeutic domains [22].

Physicochemical principles governing nanosystems

The therapeutic performance of nanostructured drug delivery systems is strongly governed by their physicochemical attributes. Parameters such as particle size, shape, surface charge, drug loading efficiency, release kinetics, and stability collectively determine how nanocarriers interact with biological systems, circulate in the bloodstream, and deliver their payloads at the target site [23].

Particle size, shape, and surface charge

Particle size is a critical determinant of nanocarrier behaviour in vivo. Nanosystems within an optimal size range are capable of prolonged circulation and enhanced accumulation in pathological tissues. Smaller particles generally exhibit higher cellular uptake due to efficient endocytosis, while excessively small particles may undergo rapid renal clearance. Conversely, larger nanoparticles may be rapidly recognized and eliminated by the reticuloendothelial system. Thus, precise size control is essential for balancing circulation time and tissue penetration [24]. Particles with dimensions smaller than 5 nm are rapidly cleared by the renal system. In contrast, particles sized between 10 and 100 nm demonstrate optimal penetration into tumour tissues. Particles that are 200 nm in size are swiftly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS). Intravenous: 20–150 nm is preferred; Oral factors include stability and mucus penetration; Pulmonary: 100–300 nm is optimal for alveolar deposition The size of these particles plays a crucial role in determining their circulation half-life and biodistribution. Particle shape also influences biological interactions. Spherical nanoparticles are commonly preferred due to ease of fabrication and uniform distribution; however, non-spherical geometries such as rods, discs, and ellipsoids have demonstrated altered cellular uptake, prolonged circulation, and distinct biodistribution patterns. These shape-dependent effects arise from differences in surface contact area and interaction dynamics with cellular membranes [25].

Surface charge is a crucial factor influencing nanoparticle stability and their interactions with cells. Positively charged nanocarriers tend to interact more effectively with the negatively charged cell membranes, enhancing cellular uptake, particularly in the delivery of genes and nucleic acids. However, an excessive positive charge can lead to cytotoxic effects and the adsorption of serum proteins [26]. For systemic delivery, nanocarriers with neutral or slightly negative charges are often preferred, as they help reduce nonspecific interactions and prolong circulation time. While a strong positive surface charge can boost cellular uptake, it also increases toxicity. In contrast, a neutral or slightly negative charge is beneficial for extending the time nanoparticles remain in circulation. Zeta potential which represents electric potential of particle in formulation, mainly expressed in millivolts (mV), a zeta potential greater than ± 20 mV is generally associated with improved colloidal stability [27].

Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency

High drug loading is advantageous as it diminishes the carrier burden and reduces the injection volume. The mechanisms for controlled release encompass diffusion-controlled release, degradation-mediated release, and stimuli-triggered release. Nonetheless, premature burst release remains a significant challenge in translation, necessitating advanced matrix engineering solutions. Nanostructured drug delivery systems represent a pivotal advancement in pharmaceutical sciences, offering engineered platforms that enhance therapeutic outcomes through precise management of drug disposition and biological interactions. A comprehensive understanding of the fundamental principles that govern these nanosystems is crucial for their rational design, optimization, and successful clinical translation. Drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency are key formulation parameters that influence therapeutic dosing and clinical feasibility. Drug loading refers to the amount of drug incorporated relative to the total carrier mass, while encapsulation efficiency represents the proportion of drug successfully entrapped during formulation. High drug loading is desirable to minimize carrier burden and reduce administration volume [29]. The extent of drug incorporation depends on the physicochemical compatibility between the drug and the carrier matrix. Hydrophobic drugs are efficiently incorporated into lipid or polymeric cores, whereas hydrophilic molecules are often entrapped within aqueous compartments or adsorbed onto carrier surfaces. Advanced strategies, such as chemical conjugation, ion pairing, and core–shell engineering, have been employed to enhance encapsulation efficiency and prevent premature drug leakage. Efficient drug loading not only improves therapeutic effectiveness but also contributes to formulation stability and scalability. Poor encapsulation can lead to burst release, inconsistent dosing, and reduced shelf life, thereby limiting translational potential [30].

Release kinetics and stability

Controlled drug release is a defining advantage of nanostructured drug delivery systems. Release kinetics are influenced by carrier composition, matrix degradation, diffusion mechanisms, and environmental stimuli, such as pH, enzymes, & temperature. Sustained and controlled release profiles help maintain drug concentrations within the therapeutic window, thereby reducing dosing frequency and improving patient compliance [31].

Stability is another crucial factor that determines the clinical applicability of nanocarriers. Physical stability ensures resistance to aggregation, sedimentation, or fusion during storage and circulation, whereas chemical stability protects the drug and carrier from degradation. Biological stability is equally important, as nanocarriers must withstand interactions with plasma proteins, immune cells, and enzymatic environments without losing functionality [32].

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics consideration

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK–PD) behavior plays a central role in determining the therapeutic success of nanostructured drug delivery systems. Unlike conventional dosage forms, nanocarriers exhibit complex and dynamic interactions with biological systems that significantly alter drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination. These altered pharmacokinetic profiles, such as maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), half-life (t½), and systemic clearance (Cl), in turn, influence pharmacodynamic responses, including therapeutic efficacy, toxicity, and duration of action.

For example, PEGylated nanoparticles typically exhibit a prolonged circulation half-life and increased AUC compared to free drug formulations, whereas rapid uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system may reduce systemic exposure. These PK alterations directly influence pharmacodynamic outcomes, including the therapeutic index and duration of action.

A thorough understanding of these processes is essential for the rational design and clinical translation of nanotherapeutics [34].

Pharmacokinetics (ADME Integration)

Absorption

The absorption of nanostructured drug delivery systems is highly dependent on the route of administration and the physicochemical characteristics of the nanocarrier. Following oral administration, nanocarriers encounter multiple physiological barriers, including gastric acidity, digestive enzymes, mucus layers, and intestinal epithelium. Engineered nanoparticles can enhance oral bioavailability by protecting drugs from degradation and facilitating transcellular or paracellular transport across the intestinal barrier. Mechanisms, such as endocytosis, lymphatic uptake, and transporter-mediated absorption, contribute to improved systemic exposure. The efficiency of these mechanisms is strongly influenced by nanoparticle size, surface charge, and hydrophobicity. Particles within the 10–200 nm range demonstrate enhanced endocytic uptake, whereas positively charged surfaces promote electrostatic interactions with negatively charged epithelial membranes. Surface functionalization with mucoadhesive or mucus-penetrating polymers further modulates absorption efficiency.

For parenteral routes, particularly intravenous administration, the concept of absorption is replaced by direct entry into the systemic circulation. In such cases, nanoparticle stability, size uniformity, and surface characteristics become critical determinants of immediate biodistribution. Alternative routes such as pulmonary, transdermal, and intranasal delivery have also benefited from nanotechnology, as nanoparticles can enhance mucosal penetration and provide sustained drug release at the site of administration [35].

Distribution

The distribution of nanocarriers differs markedly from that of free drugs because of their size, surface properties, and interactions with biological components. Upon systemic administration, nanocarriers are rapidly exposed to plasma proteins, which leads to the formation of a protein corona that influences their biodistribution and cellular recognition. Depending on their surface composition, nanoparticles may preferentially accumulate in organs, such as the liver, spleen, and lungs, through uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system [36].

Targeted nanocarriers can modulate distribution profiles by exploiting passive or active targeting mechanisms. Passive targeting enables preferential accumulation in diseased tissues with compromised vasculature, whereas active targeting relies on ligand–receptor interactions to enhance localization at specific cellular or tissue sites. In neurological applications, nanocarriers are engineered to traverse the blood–brain barrier, thereby enabling drug delivery to otherwise inaccessible regions. Controlled distribution minimizes off-target exposure and improves therapeutic specificity. These distribution characteristics influence the apparent volume of distribution (Vd) and tissue-specific drug exposure. Nanocarriers designed to evade rapid opsonization often demonstrate reduced hepatic sequestration and prolonged systemic circulation, thereby increasing tumours or target-site accumulation relative to free drug [56].

Metabolism

The metabolism of nanostructured drug delivery systems involves both the biotransformation of the encapsulated drug and the degradation of the carrier material. Encapsulation within nanocarriers can protect drugs from premature enzymatic metabolism, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and enhancing therapeutic exposure. This is particularly advantageous for labile molecules such as peptides, proteins, and nucleic acids [37].

The metabolic fate of a carrier depends on its composition. Biodegradable polymeric and lipid-based nanocarriers undergo enzymatic or hydrolytic degradation into non-toxic metabolites that are subsequently cleared from the body. In contrast, inorganic nanocarriers may exhibit slower degradation rates, necessitating careful evaluation of long-term accumulation and potential toxicity. Understanding metabolic pathways is essential for predicting drug release behavior, duration of action, and safety profiles [38].

Elimination

The elimination of nanocarriers is governed by size, surface charge, and material composition. Small nanoparticles and degraded carrier fragments may be eliminated via renal excretion, whereas larger particles are commonly cleared by hepatic metabolism and biliary excretion. Uptake by macrophages in the liver and spleen represents a major elimination pathway, particularly for non-stealth nanocarriers [39].

Surface modification strategies, such as hydrophilic polymer coatings, are frequently employed to reduce rapid clearance and extend circulation half-life. Prolonged systemic exposure enhances the likelihood of target-site accumulation; however, careful safety evaluation is necessary to avoid unintended tissue retention. Balancing elimination kinetics with therapeutic efficacy is a critical consideration in nanocarrier design. Importantly, elimination kinetics directly impact systemic clearance and terminal half-life. Nanoparticles engineered to reduce rapid renal filtration or mononuclear phagocyte uptake may exhibit extended half-life, but excessive persistence can increase the risk of long-term tissue accumulation and off-target toxicity [40].

Pharmacodynamics

Nanocarriers exert a significant impact on pharmacodynamics (PD) by refining the interaction between therapeutic agents and their biological targets at both cellular and subcellular levels. From a quantitative perspective, nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery may shift the dose–response curve by increasing local drug concentration at the target site while reducing systemic exposure. This shift can improve the therapeutic index (ratio of toxic dose to effective dose) and reduce dose-limiting toxicity. By modifying drug concentration–time profiles, nanocarriers influence both the magnitude and duration of pharmacological responses [41]. By concentrating drugs at the site of disease through targeted delivery, nanocarriers enhance receptor interactions and therapeutic effectiveness while reducing systemic distribution. Once internalized, these carriers can alter intracellular transport pathways to direct drugs to specific organelles, such as the cytoplasm, nucleus, or mitochondria, thereby augmenting their functional impact. Furthermore, nanocarriers support the synergistic effects of combination therapies by co-delivering multiple therapeutic agents, such as chemotherapeutics and siRNA or immunomodulators, in precise ratios, thereby maximizing efficacy and minimizing resistance. In the context of gene therapies, nanocarriers improve the efficiency of gene silencing by safeguarding nucleic acids from enzymatic degradation and promoting cellular uptake. However, the extent of PD enhancement is heavily reliant on successful intracellular delivery and escape from endosomes, as entrapment can hinder cytoplasmic access and diminish biological activity [42]. In addition, nanocarriers may modulate pharmacodynamic variability by reducing fluctuations in plasma drug concentration, thereby maintaining drug levels within the therapeutic window. Sustained-release or stimuli-responsive systems further enable spatiotemporal control over drug action, enhancing efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Consequently, the design of nanocarriers must incorporate mechanisms that facilitate membrane destabilization or pH-responsive release to ensure optimal therapeutic outcomes.

Nano–bio interactions

While pharmacokinetics describes systemic drug disposition, nano–bio interactions represent the mechanistic interface that governs nanoparticle recognition, cellular internalization, and biological response at the molecular level. Nano–bio interactions refer to the complex and multifaceted interactions between nanocarriers and biological components, including proteins, cells, and tissues. Upon exposure to biological fluids, nanoparticles rapidly acquire a dynamic protein corona that alters their identity and influences cellular uptake, immune recognition, and biodistribution. These interactions can significantly impact both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

At the cellular level, nanocarriers interact with the cell membranes via electrostatic forces, receptor-mediated binding, or membrane fusion. These interactions govern internalization pathways and intracellular trafficking, ultimately determining therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, nanocarriers may interact with immune cells, triggering immunostimulatory or immunosuppressive responses depending on their composition and surface chemistry [43].

Understanding nano–bio interactions is critical for minimizing adverse effects and optimizing therapeutic outcomes. Rational design strategies aim to control these interactions through surface engineering, biomimetic coatings, and careful material selection. As nanomedicine advances toward clinical translation, a comprehensive evaluation of nano–bio interactions remain a cornerstone of safe and effective nanotherapeutic development.

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic considerations form the backbone of nanostructured drug delivery system design. Nanocarriers can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy and minimize toxicity by modulating ADME processes and nano–bio interactions. A detailed understanding of these processes enables the development of rational, safe, and translatable nanomedicines tailored to specific clinical applications.

Types of nanostructured drug delivery systems

Nanostructured drug delivery systems comprise a diverse array of carrier platforms engineered at the nanoscale to enhance the therapeutic performance of pharmaceutical agents. These systems differ markedly in terms of their material composition, structural architecture, physicochemical characteristics, and biological behavior. The selection of an appropriate nanocarrier is influenced by multiple factors, including the physicochemical properties of the drug molecule, the intended route of administration, the pathological condition being treated, and the desired drug release kinetics. By tailoring these parameters, nanocarriers can improve drug solubility, stability, targeting efficiency, and safety profiles. This section provides a comprehensive overview of the major classes of nanostructured drug delivery systems, with emphasis on their design principles, advantages, limitations, and translational relevance.

Nanostructured drug delivery systems are mainly classified as organic (lipid-based, polymeric nanoparticles, and micelles) and inorganic, hybrid, or biomimetic in terms of structure and composition. Below is elaboration some important and mostly use form [45].

Lipid-based nanocarriers

Lipid-based nanocarriers represent one of the most extensively investigated and clinically validated classes of nanomedicine platforms. Their success is largely attributed to the use of physiologically compatible lipids that closely resemble biological membranes, thereby promoting favorable interactions with cells while minimizing immunogenic and toxic responses. These carriers are capable of encapsulating both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs and are suitable for a wide range of administration routes, including oral, parenteral, and topical delivery [46].

Liposomes

Liposomes are vesicular systems composed of one or more concentric phospholipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous core. This unique structural organization enables the simultaneous incorporation of hydrophilic drugs within the aqueous compartment and lipophilic drugs within the lipid bilayer. Liposomes have demonstrated considerable clinical success, particularly in cancer chemotherapy and infectious disease management, by enhancing drug stability, reducing systemic toxicity, and altering pharmacokinetic profiles. Advances in liposomal technology, such as surface modification with polyethylene glycol and targeting ligands, have further improved circulation time and facilitated site-specific drug delivery, thereby expanding their translational potential [47].

The advantages of this approach include high biocompatibility, clinical validation, reduced systemic toxicity, and a modifiable surface, such as through PEGylation or ligand attachment. However, the limitations include physical instability, such as fusion and leakage, as well as restricted long-term storage stability. Additionally, there is the potential for complement activation–related pseudo allergy (CARPA), and high production costs [47, 48].

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs)

SLNs are composed of solid lipids stabilized by surfactants, forming a rigid matrix that entraps drug molecules. Compared to conventional lipid emulsions, SLNs offer superior physical stability and enable controlled drug release over extended periods [76]. These systems are particularly suitable for delivering lipophilic drugs and protecting them from chemical degradation. However, challenges such as limited drug loading capacity and potential drug expulsion during storage due to lipid crystallization have restricted their broader application, prompting the development of improved lipid-based systems [49].

Pros: Offers regulated drug release; exhibits enhanced stability over emulsions; and is well-suited for lipophilic drugs. Cons: The drug loading capacity is restricted by the crystalline lattice structure; there is a possibility of drug expulsion during storage; and stability can be affected by polymorphic transformations. Clinical Translation as There are fewer clinical approvals than for liposomes, largely due to instability issues linked to crystallization [49, 50].

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs)

As shown in Fig. 3 Nanostructured lipid carriers have been developed as second-generation lipid nanoparticle systems to overcome the limitations associated with SLNs. By incorporating a mixture of solid and liquid lipids, NLCs form an imperfect crystalline lattice, which enhances drug accommodation and prevents drug expulsion during storage. This structural advantage results in a higher drug loading efficiency and improved release characteristics. NLCs are particularly attractive for chronic therapies and have demonstrated promising results in oral, dermal, and transdermal drug delivery applications [51].

Polymeric nanocarriers

Polymeric nanocarriers offer exceptional versatility because of the wide selection of natural and synthetic polymers available for formulation. These systems can be precisely engineered to achieve controlled drug release, biodegradation, and targeted delivery. Their tunable physicochemical properties make them suitable for delivering a broad range of therapeutic agents, including small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids [53].

The polydispersity index (PDI) is important to describe the polymeric nanocarrier formulation’s particle size distribution. The polydispersity index (PDI) is also done by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The polydispersity index is a dimensionless quantity that ranges from 0 to 1. As 0 indicates a monodisperse system. On the other hand, a higher value indicates a polydisperse system. For pharmaceutical nanocarriers, a PDI of less than 0.2 is taken to be ideal and indicates good uniformity and physical stability, while values between 0.2 and 0.3 may be acceptable, depending on the formulation. When PDI is greater than 0.3, it indicates miscalculation, possibly results in aggregation, sedimentation, or instability during storage. The PDI will not only help in particle characterisation, but it will also affect the drug loading, release and biodistribution. Similar particles will lead to reproducibility in their release profiles, whereas polydisperse systems will have unpredictable drug release and unreliable responses. Further, control of PDI is also important for scaling up production due to batch-to-batch reproducibility in particle size distribution. It is crucial to tailor formulation and processing parameters to achieve low PDI for polymeric nanocarrier systems to be stable, effective and clinically reliable [54].

Polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles are solid colloidal carriers fabricated from biodegradable polymers, such as poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid), chitosan, and alginate. Drugs may be encapsulated within the polymer matrix or adsorbed onto the surface, depending on the formulation strategy as shown in Fig. 4 [55]. These nanoparticles provide sustained drug release and protect encapsulated drugs from enzymatic degradation, making them particularly useful for long-term therapeutic applications. Their biodegradability and regulatory acceptance further enhance their translational appeal.

The advantages of polymer-based nano formulations include sustained release, high mechanical stability, and established regulatory familiarity, particularly with polymers such as PLGA. However, these systems also present certain limitations, including the production of acidic degradation products, complexities in scale-up processes, concerns regarding residual solvents, and challenges in achieving batch reproducibility. From a clinical perspective, while several polymer-based nano formulations are currently under clinical investigation, the number of approved formulations remains lower compared to lipid-based systems.

Polymeric micelles

Polymeric micelles are self-assembled nanosystems formed by amphiphilic block copolymers in aqueous environments. They possess a hydrophobic core capable of solubilizing poorly water-soluble drugs, and a hydrophilic corona that imparts steric stability and prolongs circulation. Polymeric micelles have gained significant attention for the delivery of hydrophobic anticancer agents, as they enhance solubility, reduce systemic toxicity, and improve therapeutic efficacy [101]

Self-assembled amphiphilic block copolymers, which form hydrophobic cores, present both benefits and challenges [56]. On the positive side, they excel at solubilizing hydrophobic drugs, possess a compact size of 10–100 nm, and enhance tumor penetration. However, they also have drawbacks, such as becoming unstable when diluted below the critical micelle concentration (CMC) and the potential for premature drug release in the bloodstream. A significant hurdle in clinical settings is the disassembly triggered by dilution, which continues to be a major obstacle as Fig. 4 show structure of polymeric micelles [57].

Dendrimers

Dendrimers, as shown in Fig. 4, are highly branched monodisperse macromolecules that possesses tree-like structure with functional groups on the surface. This unique structure allows for a high drug loading capacity and permits extensive surface modifications for delivery and imaging. Dendrimers have gained immense interest in gene delivery and targeted therapeutic systems due to their controlled size and architecture [58].

The effective nature of these particles is not only due to their accurate particle size but also because of the presence of many functional groups. Nonetheless, there are some hurdles limiting their practical applications. The dendrimers are usually synthesized from complex multi-step procedures which increase the production cost and decrease their ability to scale up. Moreover, some dendrimers, particularly those with cationic surface groups, can be cytotoxic, which can be a safety concern. These factors limit the wide clinical translation. Dendrimers are advanced and highly tunable nanocarrier systems. However, further optimization needs to address the issues of toxicity, cost as well as large scale manufacture so as to effectively be used in a clinical setting [59].

Inorganic nanocarriers

Inorganic nanocarriers are distinguished by their rigid structures and unique physicochemical properties, which extend their utility beyond drug delivery to diagnostic and theranostic applications. These systems often exhibit high surface area, structural stability, and responsiveness to external stimuli [60].

Gold nanoparticles

Gold nanoparticles have been widely explored owing to their chemical inertness, ease of surface functionalization, and unique optical properties. These characteristics have enabled their use as drug carriers, imaging agents, and photothermal therapeutic platforms. In oncology, gold nanoparticles have demonstrated significant potential for targeted drug delivery and image-guided therapy [61].

The material under consideration presents several notable advantages, such as its facile functionalization, photothermal capabilities, and compatibility with various imaging techniques. However, its limitations should be recognized, including the fact that it is non-biodegradable, raising concerns about potential long-term accumulation. Additionally, the material exhibits a limited capacity for drug loading, and its clinical application is predominantly clinical translation remains limited compared to lipid-based systems [61, 62].

Magnetic nanoparticles

Magnetic nanoparticles, commonly composed of iron oxide, enable magnetically guided drug delivery and localized therapy. External magnetic fields can be used to direct these nanoparticles to specific sites, thereby enhancing targeting efficiency. Additionally, magnetic nanoparticles are employed in diagnostic imaging and hyperthermia-based cancer therapy, highlighting their multifunctional therapeutic potential [63, 64].

Advantages include the capability for magnetic targeting, potential for MRI imaging, and applicability in hyperthermia treatments. Limitations encompass rapid uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), concerns regarding aggregation, and the potential to induce oxidative stress.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles possess a highly ordered porous framework that allows for high drug loading and controlled release. Their surface chemistry can be extensively modified to introduce targeting ligands or stimuli-responsive functionalities. These features make mesoporous silica nanoparticles attractive candidates for precision drug delivery and Combination therapies offer several notable advantages and face certain limitations [65].

The benefits include a high surface area, customizable pore size, and significant drug loading capacity. However, these therapies also encounter challenges, including slow rates of biodegradation, concerns regarding silica-associated toxicity, and uncertainties related to clearance processes [66].

Carbon-based nanomaterials

Carbon-based nanomaterials, including carbon nanotubes and graphene derivatives, exhibit exceptional mechanical strength, large surface areas, and unique electrical properties. These features facilitate high drug loading and efficient cellular internalization. Despite their promising delivery capabilities, concerns regarding long-term biocompatibility, biodegradability, and potential toxicity necessitate rigorous preclinical evaluation before clinical translation [66, 67]. This material offers several advantages, including a high surface area, robust mechanical properties, and effective cellular penetration. However, it is crucial to acknowledge its limitations, such as bio persistence, the risk of inducing inflammatory responses, and uncertainties regarding its long-term safety. These factors remain predominantly in the experimental stage [68].

Biological and biomimetic nanocarriers

Biological and biomimetic nanocarriers represent an emerging and highly innovative class of drug delivery systems designed to emulate natural biological components. Systems such as exosomes and cell membrane-coated nanoparticles exhibit inherent targeting capabilities, prolonged circulation, and immune evasion properties [69]. By leveraging natural biological pathways, these carriers offer significant advantages for personalized and precision medicine. Their application in cancer therapy, gene delivery, and immunotherapy highlights their growing translational significance [70].

Rational design and surface engineering strategies

The rational design of nanostructured drug delivery systems is a critical determinant of their therapeutic performance, safety, and translational success. Unlike conventional formulations, nano-system require a multidimensional design approach that integrates material science, pharmaceutical technology, and biological considerations. Design strategies focus on selecting appropriate carrier materials, optimizing drug encapsulation methods, and incorporating responsive features that enable controlled site-specific drug release. This section discusses the fundamental design principles that govern the development of efficient and clinically relevant nanocarriers [71].

Material selection for nanocarrier design

Material selection is the foundation of nanostructured drug delivery system development, as it directly influences biocompatibility, biodegradability, drug loading capacity, and in vivo behavior. Ideal nanocarrier materials should be non-toxic, chemically stable during formulation and storage, and capable of controlled degradation into safe by-products following administration [72].

Lipid-based materials are widely employed due to their physiological compatibility and ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. Phospholipids, triglycerides, and fatty acids are commonly used to formulate liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers. These materials exhibit favorable interactions with biological membranes and are particularly suited for systemic and oral delivery [73].

Polymeric materials offer greater flexibility in terms of molecular weight, functionalization, and degradation kinetics. Biodegradable polymers, such as poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid), chitosan, and alginate, enable sustained drug release and protect sensitive therapeutics. Synthetic polymers allow precise tuning of carrier properties, while natural polymers provide inherent biocompatibility and bio-adhesive characteristics [74].

Inorganic materials are selected for applications requiring structural rigidity, high surface area, or multifunctionality. Materials such as silica, gold, and iron oxide are often chosen for their diagnostic and imaging capabilities. However, careful material selection is essential to address long-term safety and clearance concerns associated with inorganic nanocarriers.

Drug encapsulation and loading techniques

Efficient drug encapsulation is essential for achieving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing the carrier burden. Encapsulation techniques are selected based on the physicochemical properties of the drug and the nature of the carrier system. Common approaches include physical entrapment, chemical conjugation, and adsorption onto the carrier surface [122].

Physical entrapment involves the incorporation of drug molecules within the core or matrix of the nanocarrier during formulation. This technique is widely used for lipid and polymeric nanoparticles and is particularly effective for hydrophobic drugs. Emulsion-based methods, nanoprecipitation, and solvent evaporation are commonly employed to achieve high encapsulation efficiency and uniform particle size distribution [123].

Chemical conjugation involves the covalent attachment of drug molecules to a carrier via cleavable linkers. This approach offers precise control over drug loading and release kinetics and is especially beneficial for delivering biologics and nucleic acids. The use of stimuli-cleavable bonds ensures drug release in response to specific physiological conditions at the target site [124].

Surface adsorption relies on electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions between the drug and nanocarrier surface. While this method is relatively simple, it may result in premature drug release and is often combined with additional stabilization strategies to enhance formulation robustness [125].

Stimuli-responsive design strategies

Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers represent an advanced design paradigm that enables controlled drug release in response to specific internal and external stimuli. These systems are engineered to remain stable under normal physiological conditions and release their payload selectively at the disease site, thereby improving spatial and temporal control of drug release and reducing systemic toxicity [126].

pH-responsive nanocarriers

pH-responsive systems exploit the differences in pH between healthy tissues and pathological environments, such as tumors or inflamed sites. Nanocarriers incorporating pH-sensitive polymers or linkers undergo structural changes under acidic conditions, thereby triggering drug release. This strategy is widely applied in cancer therapy and intracellular drug delivery [127].

Enzyme-responsive nanocarriers

Enzyme-responsive systems are designed to respond to disease-associated enzymes that are overexpressed under specific pathological conditions. Enzymatic cleavage of carrier components leads to targeted drug release, providing high specificity and minimizing off-target effects [128].

Redox-responsive nanocarriers

Redox-responsive nanocarriers utilize the differences in redox potential between the extracellular and intracellular environments. Incorporation of disulfide bonds enables drug release in the reductive intracellular milieu, making these systems particularly effective for cytosolic drug and gene delivery [129].

External stimuli-responsive systems

External stimuli, such as temperature, light, ultrasound, or magnetic fields, can be used to trigger drug release from nanocarriers. These systems offer precise temporal and spatial control and are increasingly explored in combination therapies and theranostic applications [130].

Surface functionalization and targeting strategies

An integral component of rational nanocarrier design is surface engineering, which governs biological identity, circulation behaviour, cellular interactions, and targeting specificity. While the core composition of nanocarriers determines drug loading and release characteristics, surface engineering dictates how these carriers interact with complex biological environments. By tailoring surface chemistry and architecture, nanocarriers can evade immune clearance, recognize disease-specific targets, and deliver therapeutic payloads with enhanced precision. This section critically discusses passive and active targeting approaches, ligand-based functionalization, stealth strategies, and the development of multifunctional nanocarriers [75].

Passive targeting strategies

Passive targeting exploits the intrinsic pathophysiological characteristics of diseased tissues to achieve preferential accumulation of nanocarriers without the need for specific targeting ligands. The most widely studied passive targeting mechanism is the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which arises from the leaky vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage commonly observed in tumors and inflamed tissues. Appropriate physicochemical optimization enhances in vivo performance [76].The success of passive targeting is highly dependent on nanoparticle size (20–200 nm), surface charge, and circulation time. Long-circulating nanocarriers are more likely to benefit from the EPR effect, emphasizing the importance of surface modifications that reduce rapid clearance. Although passive targeting has demonstrated clinical relevance, its effectiveness can vary significantly among patients and disease types, highlighting the need for complementary active targeting strategies. This highlights the necessity of patient stratification and the adoption of combination strategies to improve delivery efficiency [77].

To overcome limitation EPR strategies include variation of tumour vasculature, regularization of tumour stroma, usage of smaller nanoparticles (< 50 nm) for improved penetration, and integration of active targeting ligands. Imaging-based assessment of tumour perfusion and permeability has also been proposed to identify patients most likely to benefit from EPR-dependent therapies [78].

Active targeting approaches

Active targeting involves the functionalization of nanocarrier surfaces with ligands that selectively bind to receptors or biomarkers overexpressed on target cells or tissues. This strategy enhances cellular uptake and intracellular delivery through receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects [79].

Ligand selection is a critical design consideration and must account for receptor specificity, binding affinity, and expression levels. Active targeting does not replace passive targeting but rather builds upon it by improving cellular-level specificity after nanocarrier accumulation at the disease site. The combination of passive and active targeting strategies has emerged as a synergistic approach for achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes which is fully depicted in Fig. 5 [80].

Ligand-based surface functionalization

Antibody and antibody fragment functionalization

Antibodies and antibody fragments offer high specificity and strong binding affinity toward target antigens. When conjugated to nanocarrier surfaces, they enable precise recognition of disease-associated markers, particularly in oncology and immunotherapy. Antibody fragments such as Fab and single-chain variable fragments are often preferred due to reduced size and lower immunogenicity, which facilitate better tissue penetration and pharmacokinetic profiles [81].

Peptide-based targeting ligands

Peptides are widely used targeting ligands due to their small size, ease of synthesis, and favorable safety profile. Peptide ligands can be designed to recognize a variety of receptors involved in cancer, cardiovascular disease, and neurological disorders. Their flexibility allows high ligand density on nanocarrier surfaces, enhancing multivalent interactions and cellular uptake [82].

Aptamers and small molecule ligands

Aptamers are short nucleic acid sequences that bind to specific targets with high affinity and specificity. They offer advantages, such as chemical stability, low immunogenicity, and reproducibility. Small molecule ligands, including vitamins and sugars, are also employed for targeting purposes due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness. These ligands facilitate receptor-mediated uptake while maintaining favorable pharmacokinetic properties [84].

Stealth and long-circulating nanocarriers

One of the major challenges in nanomedicine is the rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Stealth strategies aim to mask nanocarriers from immune recognition, thereby extending their systemic circulation time. Surface coating with hydrophilic polymers creates a steric barrier that reduces protein adsorption and opsonization [85].

Hydrophilic polymer coatings are the most widely adopted stealth strategy and have been successfully applied in several clinically approved nanomedicines. Alternative approaches, including zwitterionic and biomimetic coatings, have emerged to address the limitations associated with repeated dosing and immune responses. These strategies enhance circulation longevity while maintaining targeting functionality [86].

Multifunctional and theranostic nanocarriers

Multifunctional nanocarriers integrate multiple functionalities, such as targeting, imaging, and therapy, into a single platform. These systems enable simultaneous diagnosis and treatment, often referred to as theranostics. By combining targeting ligands, imaging agents, and therapeutic payloads, multifunctional nanocarriers provide real-time monitoring of drug distribution and therapeutic response [87].

The design of multifunctional nanocarriers requires careful optimization to balance complexity with stability and scalability. Despite these challenges, theranostic platforms hold significant promise for personalized medicine by enabling tailored treatment strategies and dynamic therapeutic monitoring [88].

Cellular uptake and intracellular fate as determinants of pharmacokinetics

In addition to systemic distribution, the pharmacokinetic performance of nanocarriers is strongly influenced by cellular internalization and intracellular trafficking processes. Accordingly, mechanisms of uptake and subcellular transport are integral to nanocarrier biodistribution and therapeutic performance [90]. The therapeutic success of nanostructured drug delivery systems depends not only on their ability to reach the target tissue but also on efficient cellular internalization and precise intracellular trafficking. Once nanocarriers reach the vicinity of target cells, their interaction with cellular membranes and subsequent intracellular fate determine drug release efficiency, bioavailability at the subcellular level, and pharmacological response. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for the rational design of nanocarriers capable of overcoming biological barriers and achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes [91].

Clathrin-mediated endocytosis is one of the most extensively studied uptake pathways and is typically associated with nanoparticles functionalized with targeting ligands. This pathway involves receptor–ligand interactions followed by vesicle formation, leading to efficient and selective internalization. Caveolae-mediated endocytosis represents an alternative route, often associated with lipid-rich membrane domains, and is particularly relevant for nanocarriers designed to avoid lysosomal degradation [93].

Macropinocytosis enables the non-specific uptake of larger nanocarriers through membrane ruffling and vesicle formation. This pathway is frequently exploited by cancer cells, which exhibit elevated macro pinocytic activity. Phagocytosis, on the other hand, is primarily associated with immune cells such as macrophages and plays a significant role in nanoparticle clearance rather than therapeutic delivery [94].

Influence of nanocarrier properties on cellular internalization

The physicochemical properties of nanocarriers critically influence their cellular uptake efficiency. Particle size plays a decisive role, with nanosystems within an optimal size range demonstrating enhanced endocytosis. Extremely small particles may diffuse rapidly but risk rapid clearance, while larger particles may face steric hindrance during membrane internalization [95].

Surface charge also governs interactions with the negatively charged cellular membrane. Positively charged nanocarriers exhibit enhanced cellular uptake due to electrostatic attraction; however, excessive cationic charge may induce membrane disruption and cytotoxicity. Surface functionalization with targeting ligands further enhances uptake specificity by promoting receptor-mediated internalization, thereby increasing intracellular drug delivery efficiency.

Particle shape has emerged as an important determinant of uptake kinetics. Non-spherical nanocarriers may exhibit prolonged membrane interaction and altered internalization pathways compared to spherical particles, offering opportunities to optimize cellular entry through rational design [96].

Endosomal trafficking and intracellular transport

Following cellular internalization, nanocarriers are typically trafficked through the endosomal pathway. Early endosomes mature into late endosomes and ultimately fuse with lysosomes, where acidic pH and enzymatic activity may degrade both the carrier and the therapeutic payload. While lysosomal processing is advantageous for certain applications, such as lysosome-targeted therapies, it poses a significant barrier for drugs requiring cytosolic or nuclear localization.

Intracellular transport is mediated by cytoskeletal elements and motor proteins, which facilitate vesicle movement toward specific cellular compartments. The ability of nanocarriers to navigate this intracellular landscape and reach their intended site of action is a critical determinant of therapeutic success [97].

Endosomal escape strategies

Efficient endosomal escape is a major challenge in nanomedicine, particularly for gene and protein delivery applications. To address this limitation, nanocarriers are engineered with functionalities that promote disruption of the endosomal membrane, thereby enabling payload release into the cytoplasm before lysosomal degradation [98].

pH-sensitive nanocarriers exploit the acidic endosomal environment to induce structural changes that facilitate membrane destabilization. Nanocarriers incorporating membrane-active components can disrupt endosomal membranes through osmotic or mechanical effects. These strategies significantly enhance intracellular bioavailability and improve therapeutic efficacy [99].

Subcellular targeting and organelle-specific delivery

Advanced nanocarrier systems are increasingly designed for subcellular targeting, directing therapeutic agents to specific organelles, such as the nucleus, mitochondria, or lysosomes. This level of precision is particularly important for therapies involving gene regulation, apoptosis induction, and metabolic modulation [98].

Subcellular targeting is achieved through the incorporation of targeting signals or ligands that facilitate transport to specific organelles. Nuclear localization signals enable transport through nuclear pores, whereas mitochondria-targeting moieties promote accumulation within the mitochondrial matrix. These strategies enhance therapeutic specificity and reduce off-target effects at the intracellular level [100].

Applications of nanostructured drug delivery systems in targeted therapeutics

Nanostructured drug delivery systems have demonstrated transformative potential across a wide range of therapeutic areas by enabling precise targeting, improved pharmacokinetics, and reduced systemic toxicity. Their versatility allows adaptation to diverse disease pathologies, including cancer, neurological disorders, infectious diseases, and genetic diseases. This section discusses the major therapeutic applications of nanocarriers, emphasizing their mechanistic advantages and translational relevance in targeted therapy [102].

Cancer therapy

Cancer remains the most extensively explored application of nanostructured drug delivery systems because of the limitations of conventional chemotherapy, including nonspecific toxicity, poor tumor accumulation, and drug resistance. Nanostructured systems exploit both passive and active targeting mechanisms to deliver chemotherapeutic agents directly to tumor sites. By enhancing drug concentration within the tumor microenvironment, nanocarriers reduce exposure to healthy tissues and mitigate adverse effects, such as cardiotoxicity and myelosuppression. Furthermore, nanocarriers can be engineered to respond to tumor-specific stimuli, such as acidic pH or enzymatic activity, ensuring controlled drug release within malignant tissues [103].

In addition to conventional chemotherapy, nanocarriers play a critical role in cancer immunotherapy, gene therapy, and combination therapy as examples cover in Table 9. The co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents within a single nanocarrier allows synergistic treatment strategies that address tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance, positioning nanomedicine as a cornerstone of modern oncology. Mechanistically, synergistic effects are achieved through the simultaneous modulation of complementary oncogenic pathways. For example, the co-delivery of chemotherapeutic agents with siRNA targeting drug-resistance genes (e.g., P-glycoprotein or anti-apoptotic Bcl-2) enhances intracellular drug retention and promotes apoptosis. Similarly, nanoparticles combining cytotoxic drugs with immune checkpoint inhibitors or immunoadjuvants can potentiate antitumor immune responses by inducing immunogenic cell death while enhancing T-cell activation. Dual-drug formulations targeting parallel signalling pathways, such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, may suppress compensatory resistance mechanisms that commonly arise in heterogeneous tumour populations. By synchronizing pharmacokinetics and ensuring co-localization of therapeutic agents within tumour cells, nanocarriers facilitate coordinated pathway inhibition, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy beyond additive effects [104].

Cardiovascular diseases

The treatment of cardiovascular diseases presents unique challenges because it requires targeted drug delivery to specific vascular regions while minimizing systemic effects. Nanostructured drug delivery systems enable the localized delivery of therapeutic agents to atherosclerotic plaques, ischemic tissues, or inflamed vascular endothelium.

Enhancing the bioavailability and stability of cardiovascular drugs, enabling sustained release and improved therapeutic efficacy. Targeted delivery strategies reduce off-target interactions and lower the risk of adverse effects associated with systemic drug exposure. Additionally, nanocarriers have been explored for delivering genes and growth factors that promote angiogenesis and tissue regeneration following ischemic injury, highlighting their regenerative therapeutic potential [110]

Neurological disorders

Neurological disorders pose significant therapeutic challenges because of the presence of the blood–brain barrier, which restricts the entry of most drugs into the central nervous system. Nanostructured drug delivery systems offer promising solutions by facilitating drug transport across this barrier through receptor-mediated transcytosis or temporary modulation of barrier permeability.

Nanocarriers have been investigated for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, brain tumors, and central nervous system infections. By protecting drugs from degradation and enabling controlled release within the brain, nanocarriers can improve therapeutic outcomes, while reducing systemic toxicity. Their ability to deliver nucleic acids and proteins further expands their application in gene therapy and neuroregenerative medicine [111].

Infectious diseases

Nanostructured drug delivery systems have gained increasing attention for the treatment of infectious diseases, particularly for addressing issues related to drug resistance, poor bioavailability, and intracellular pathogen persistence. Nanocarriers enhance antimicrobial efficacy by improving drug penetration into infected tissues and facilitating intracellular delivery to pathogen reservoirs.

Targeted nanocarriers can deliver antimicrobial agents directly to infected cells, thereby reducing the required dose and limiting collateral damage to healthy microbiota. In viral infections, nanocarriers have been explored for the delivery of antiviral drugs and nucleic acids, offering controlled release and improved therapeutic index. These advantages are particularly relevant for chronic and drug-resistant infections [112].

Gene and nucleic acid delivery

Gene therapy and nucleic acid-based therapeutics are rapidly expanding areas of nanomedicine. Nanostructured delivery systems provide a non-viral alternative for delivering genetic material and overcome the challenges associated with viral vectors, such as immunogenicity and limited payload capacity.

Applications of nanocarriers include the delivery of small interfering RNA, messenger RNA, and gene-editing components for the treatment of genetic disorders, cancer, and infectious diseases. Therapeutic applications of nanostructured drug delivery systems

Translational and clinical perspectives

Despite remarkable advances in nanostructured drug delivery system design and preclinical validation, successful clinical translation remains a significant challenge. Bridging the gap between laboratory-scale innovation and clinical application requires a systematic evaluation of efficacy, safety, manufacturability, and regulatory compliance. This section critically examines the translational pathway of nanomedicines, encompassing preclinical assessment, clinical development, manufacturing considerations, and regulatory perspectives [116].

Preclinical evaluation of nanostructured drug delivery systems

Preclinical evaluation represents a critical step in the translational pipeline, providing essential data on safety, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic efficacy. Nanostructured drug delivery systems require more extensive characterization than conventional formulations due to their complex composition and dynamic nano–bio interactions. In vitro studies typically assess cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, drug release behavior, and targeting efficiency using disease-relevant cell models.

In vivo preclinical studies are indispensable for understanding biodistribution, organ accumulation, clearance mechanisms, and therapeutic outcomes. Animal models are used to evaluate pharmacokinetic parameters, dose–response relationships, and potential toxicity. However, interspecies differences in physiology and immune responses often limit the predictive accuracy of animal models, emphasizing the need for advanced in vitro systems and translationally relevant models to improve clinical predictability. Notably, despite promising preclinical efficacy, the clinical success rate of oncological drug candidates remains below 10% and nanoparticle-based systems are not exempt from this trend. Discrepancies between murine tumour models and human tumours, particularly regarding vascular permeability and immune complexity, often lead to overestimation of therapeutic benefit during early-stage evaluation [117].

Clinical development and current clinical landscape

The clinical development of nanomedicines follows conventional drug development pathways but involves additional complexity due to formulation-specific variables. Several nanostructured drug delivery systems have successfully progressed through clinical trials and achieved regulatory approval, particularly in oncology. These clinically approved nanomedicines have demonstrated improved safety profiles, reduced toxicity, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy compared to their conventional counterparts [118].

Ongoing clinical trials continue to explore nanocarriers for diverse therapeutic indications, including infectious diseases, neurological disorders, and gene therapy. However, clinical outcomes have occasionally fallen short of preclinical expectations, highlighting challenges such as variability in patient response, limited targeting efficiency in humans, and unpredictable nano–bio interactions. These observations underscore the importance of patient stratification and personalized approaches in clinical nanomedicine. For example, several actively targeted nanoparticle formulations that demonstrated robust tumour accumulation in preclinical models failed to show significant survival benefit in clinical trials, underscoring the challenges of translating EPR-dependent and receptor-mediated targeting strategies into heterogeneous human tumour environments [118].

Scale-up, manufacturing, and quality control

The challenges encountered in the transition of nanostructured drug delivery systems from laboratories to production scale are enormous. Nanocarrier production is difficult to replicate on a continual basis if developed in a controlled laboratory situation. Even small variations in particle size, drug loading, surface properties, and other parameters can significantly alter therapeutic performance and regulatory approval.

The manufacturing process must be robust and carefully controlled to produce the same quality of product in commercial scale repeatedly and over time. Nanomedicines differ from traditional medicines and require a more thorough characterisation. This includes the measurement of the nanoparticle size distribution, surface charge, morphology, and drug release. In order to satisfy these requirements, sophisticated analytical methods and strict process control is needed. The use of Quality-by-Design (QbD) principles is gaining importance for systematic identification and control of critical quality attributes (CQAs) such as particle size, zeta potential and encapsulation efficiency. A clearly defined design space will help assure product quality and regulatory compliance [119].

Microfluidics and continuous-flow processing technologies show potential as scalable nanocarrier production methods, making them attractive manufacturing options. Microfluidic systems allow precise control over fluid flow and mixing at the microscale to generate uniform particles reproducibly. The continuous-flow processes, often known as flow-based manufacturing, possesses several advantages including steady state production, better control over processing conditions, and enhanced heat and mass transfer compared to batch processes. These systems allow integration with real time monitoring tools for reducing variability and improving reliability. The advanced manufacturing processes are expected to lead to efficient scale-up, reproducible product quality and economical production of nanomedicine formulations [120].

Regulatory considerations and challenges

Regulatory evaluation of nanostructured drug delivery systems presents unique challenges due to the lack of universally harmonized guidelines specific to nanomedicine. Regulatory agencies assess nanomedicines within existing pharmaceutical frameworks while requiring additional characterization and safety data. Factors, such as nano-specific toxicity, immunogenicity, long-term accumulation, and environmental impact, must be carefully evaluated.

Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have issued reflection papers and guidance documents addressing liposomal formulations, iron-based nano colloids, and complex drug products. However, a universally harmonized nano-specific regulatory framework remains under development, creating variability in approval pathways across regions. Differences in regulatory requirements across regions further complicate global development and commercialization. Clearer regulatory definitions, standardized testing protocols, and international harmonization efforts are essential to facilitate the efficient approval of nanotherapeutics. Collaborative engagement between researchers, industry stakeholders, and regulatory authorities is crucial for addressing these challenges [121].

Commercialization and market translation

The commercialization of nanostructured drug delivery systems depends on demonstrating clear clinical benefits, economic viability, and manufacturability. High development costs, complex manufacturing processes, and uncertain reimbursement pathways can limit market adoption. Nevertheless, the growing demand for targeted and personalized therapies has created significant opportunities for nanomedicine-based products.

Strategic partnerships between academic institutions, pharmaceutical companies, and biotechnology firms play a pivotal role in accelerating commercialization. Continued investment in translational research and infrastructure development is expected to expand the clinical and commercial footprints of nanostructured drug delivery systems in the coming years.

From a critical perspective, the future success of nanostructured drug delivery systems will depend less on incremental formulation refinement and more on predictive translational modeling, patient stratification, and the integration of biomarker-driven clinical trial design. Bridging the nano–bio interface with quantitative systems pharmacology and real-world clinical data will be essential to overcoming the persistent gap between preclinical promise and clinical performance [122].

Safety, toxicity, ethical consideration

Although nanostructured drug delivery systems offer significant therapeutic advantages, their safe and ethical application remains a critical concern for clinical translation. The unique physicochemical properties that enable nanocarriers to improve drug delivery also introduce complex interactions with biological systems, which may result in unintended toxicological effects. Comprehensive safety evaluation and ethical oversight are therefore essential to ensure that the benefits of nanomedicine outweigh potential risks to patients and society [123, 124].

Nanotoxicity and mechanisms of adverse effects

Nanotoxicity refers to the potential harmful effects associated with the physicochemical characteristics of nanoscale materials. Factors, such as particle size, shape, surface charge, composition, and aggregation behavior, significantly influence biological responses. Due to their small size and large surface area, nanocarriers can intimately interact with cellular components, potentially inducing oxidative stress, inflammation, or membrane disruption.

One of the primary mechanisms of nanotoxicity is the generation of reactive oxygen species, which can lead to cellular damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of inflammatory pathways. Additionally, positively charged nanocarriers may disrupt cell membranes and alter intracellular signaling, while poorly biodegradable materials may accumulate in tissues, raising concerns regarding long-term toxicity. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for designing safer nanocarriers with minimized adverse effects.

Importantly, nanotoxicity is often dose-dependent and influenced by exposure duration, accumulation kinetics, and clearance efficiency. Subtoxic concentrations may induce adaptive cellular responses, whereas higher doses can trigger irreversible oxidative damage, apoptosis, or necrosis. Establishing threshold dose–response relationships and no-observed-adverse-effect levels (NOAEL) is therefore essential for safe clinical translation [125, 126].

Immunogenicity and biocompatibility

The interaction of nanocarriers with the immune system represents a major determinant of their safety profile. Nanostructured systems may be recognized as foreign entities that trigger immune activation or hypersensitivity reactions. Protein adsorption on nanoparticle surfaces can further alter immune recognition, leading to unpredictable biological responses.

Biocompatible materials and surface modification strategies are widely employed to reduce immunogenicity and enhance tolerance. However, repeated administration of certain nanocarriers may still result in immune sensitization or altered pharmacokinetics. Therefore, comprehensive immunotoxicity assessment is essential, particularly for chronic or long-term therapeutic applications.

In addition to general immune activation, certain nanocarriers, particularly liposomal and PEGylated systems, may trigger complement activation-related pseudo allergy (CARPA), leading to hypersensitivity reactions upon infusion. This phenomenon has been observed in some clinically approved liposomal formulations and underscores the importance of infusion protocols and premedication strategies [127].

Long-term safety and biodistribution concerns

Long-term safety evaluation is particularly important for nanostructured drug delivery systems that exhibit prolonged circulation or slow degradation. Persistent accumulation in organs, such as the liver, spleen, or lungs, may pose risks of chronic toxicity and organ dysfunction. Accumulation kinetics are influenced by nanoparticle size, surface chemistry, and biodegradability, with non-degradable inorganic systems exhibiting slower clearance profiles. Chronic exposure studies are particularly important for nanoparticles with half-lives extending beyond several days, as prolonged tissue retention may increase the risk of inflammatory or fibrotic responses.

Inorganic nanocarriers, in particular, require rigorous assessment of clearance pathways and long-term tissue retention. Preclinical studies often fail to fully capture long-term effects because of limited study duration. Consequently, post-marketing surveillance and long-term clinical monitoring are critical components of nanomedicine safety evaluation. Designing nanocarriers with predictable degradation and elimination profiles is a key strategy for mitigating long-term safety concerns.

Regulatory toxicology assessment of nanomedicines increasingly follows a risk-based framework that incorporates physicochemical characterization, in vitro cytotoxicity, in vivo biodistribution, immunotoxicity, genotoxicity, and chronic exposure evaluation. International harmonization of nano-specific safety testing remains under development, highlighting the need for standardized protocols and long-term surveillance data [128].

Environmental and occupational safety considerations

Beyond patient safety, the environmental and occupational impact of nanomaterials must be considered. Manufacturing, handling, and disposal of nanostructured drug delivery systems may expose workers and the environment to nanoscale materials with unknown ecological consequences. Accidental release into the environment could potentially affect ecosystems and public health.

The implementation of appropriate safety protocols, waste management strategies, and environmental risk assessments is essential throughout the lifecycle of nanomedicine products. Regulatory frameworks should incorporate environmental safety considerations to ensure responsible development and use of nanotechnology in healthcare [129].

Ethical and societal implications

The ethical implications of nanomedicine extend beyond safety concerns to issues of equity, accessibility, and informed consent. Advanced nanotherapeutics are often associated with high development and manufacturing costs, raising concerns about equitable access to treatment. Ensuring that the benefits of nanomedicine are accessible to diverse patient populations is an important ethical consideration. Additionally, ethical considerations extend to equitable participation in clinical trials, particularly for emerging nanotherapeutics with uncertain long-term effects. Transparent risk–benefit communication and post-approval monitoring are essential to maintain public trust in nanomedicine innovation.

Informed consent processes must adequately communicate the potential risks and uncertainties associated with nanotechnology-based therapies. Transparency in clinical research, patient education, and post-marketing surveillance is essential to maintain public trust. Ethical oversight committees play a crucial role in guiding responsible research and clinical application of nanostructured drug delivery systems [130].

Emerging trends and future paradigms in nanostructured drug delivery systems

The field of nanostructured drug delivery systems is undergoing rapid evolution, driven by advances in materials science, biotechnology, computational tools, and systems biology. While earlier generations of nanocarriers primarily focused on improving drug solubility and pharmacokinetics, emerging paradigms emphasize precision, adaptability, and integration with personalized medicine. This section highlights key technological and conceptual trends shaping the future of nanomedicine and their potential impact on targeted therapeutics [132].

Artificial intelligence and machine learning in nanocarrier design

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are increasingly being applied to the rational design and optimization of nanostructured drug delivery systems. These computational tools enable the analysis of large multidimensional datasets generated during formulation development, physicochemical characterization, and biological evaluation. By identifying complex relationships between formulation variables and biological outcomes, AI-driven models can predict optimal nanocarrier compositions with greater accuracy and efficiency than traditional trial-and-error approaches.

Machine learning algorithms are also being employed to predict nanotoxicity, biodistribution, and drug release behavior, thereby reducing development time and minimizing experimental costs. The integration of AI into nanomedicine research is expected to accelerate innovation, improve reproducibility, and enhance the translational success of nanotherapeutics [132, 133].

Modified and precision nanomedicine

Modified medicines represent a major paradigm shift in healthcare, aiming to tailor therapeutic interventions to individual patient characteristics. Nanostructured drug delivery systems are uniquely positioned to support this approach because of their modular design and tunable properties. By customizing nanocarrier composition, targeting ligands, and release profiles, therapies can be adapted to patient-specific disease biomarkers, genetic profiles, and physiological conditions.

In addition, precision nanomedicine enables adaptive treatment strategies, in which drug delivery systems respond dynamically to disease progression or therapeutic response. Such approaches are particularly valuable in oncology and chronic diseases, in which interpatient variability significantly influences treatment outcomes. The convergence of nanotechnology with diagnostics and biomarker discovery is expected to further advance personalized therapeutic interventions [134].

Smart and stimuli-responsive nanocarriers

Smart nanocarriers represent an advanced generation of delivery systems capable of responding to specific biological or external stimuli. These systems are designed to remain stable under normal physiological conditions and release their payload selectively in response to disease-associated triggers such as pH changes, enzymatic activity, redox gradients, or temperature variations.

The development of multi-stimuli-responsive nanocarriers enables greater control over drug release kinetics and spatial targeting. By integrating multiple sensing mechanisms within a single carrier, smart nanocarriers can achieve enhanced specificity and reduced off-target effects. These systems hold significant promise for improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity [135].

Bioinspired and hybrid nanostructured systems

Bioinspired nanocarriers are inspired by natural biological systems to enhance compatibility, targeting efficiency, and immune evasion. Examples include cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, protein-based carriers, and extracellular vesicle-mimetic systems. These platforms leverage endogenous biological pathways to achieve prolonged circulation and improved tissue targeting.

Hybrid nanostructured systems combine multiple material classes, such as organic–inorganic composites or lipid–polymer hybrids, to integrate complementary functionalities. Hybrid designs offer enhanced stability, multifunctionality, and adaptability, enabling simultaneous drug delivery, imaging, and therapeutic monitoring. Such systems represent a promising direction for next-generation theranostic applications [136].

Integration with advanced therapeutic modalities

Emerging nanostructured drug delivery systems are increasingly being integrated with advanced therapeutic modalities, such as gene editing, immunotherapy, and regenerative medicine. Nanocarriers provide a versatile platform for delivering gene-editing components, immune modulators, and biologics with improved safety and targeting precision.

In regenerative medicine, nanocarriers are being explored for the controlled delivery of growth factors and stem cell modulators to promote tissue repair and regeneration. These integrative approaches highlight the expanding role of nanotechnology beyond traditional drug delivery and underscore its potential to reshape future therapeutic strategies.

Integration with gene editing and immunomodulatory therapies necessitates rigorous safety evaluation, particularly regarding off-target genetic modifications and immune overstimulation. The convergence of nanotechnology with advanced biologics increases therapeutic complexity, requiring coordinated regulatory and pharmacovigilance frameworks.

Conclusion

Nanostructured drug delivery systems have emerged as a transformative platform in modern pharmaceutical and biomedical sciences, offering innovative solutions to long-standing challenges associated with conventional drug delivery. Through precise control over particle size, surface properties, drug loading, and release kinetics, nanocarriers have demonstrated the ability to enhance bioavailability, improve therapeutic specificity, and reduce systemic toxicity across a broad range of disease indications. The convergence of material science, nanotechnology, and molecular biology has enabled the rational design of multifunctional delivery systems capable of addressing complex pathophysiological barriers.

This comprehensive review highlights the fundamental principles governing nanostructured drug delivery systems, including their classification, physicochemical behavior, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic considerations, and interactions with biological systems. Advances in surface functionalization and targeting strategies have significantly improved the precision of drug delivery, enabling both passive and active targeting of diseased tissues. Furthermore, insights into cellular uptake mechanisms and intracellular trafficking have provided a deeper understanding of how nanocarriers can be engineered to achieve efficient subcellular drug delivery.

The broad applicability of nanostructured drug delivery systems in targeted therapeutics—ranging from oncology and neurological disorders to infectious diseases, cardiovascular conditions, and gene therapy–underscores their clinical relevance and translational potential. Several nanomedicines have successfully transitioned from laboratory research to clinical use, validating the promise of this technology. However, the review also emphasizes that translational success is often hindered by challenges related to scalability, reproducibility, regulatory ambiguity, long-term safety, and patient-specific variability.

Emerging paradigms, such as artificial intelligence–assisted design, smart and stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, bioinspired systems, and personalized nanomedicine, represent critical directions for future research. These innovations have the potential to enhance predictability, improve clinical outcomes, and support precision therapeutics tailored to individual patient needs. At the same time, rigorous evaluation of safety, toxicity, ethical considerations, and environmental impact remains essential to ensure the responsible and sustainable advancement of nanomedicine.

In conclusion, nanostructured drug delivery systems represent a dynamic and rapidly evolving field with significant implications for the future of targeted therapeutics. Continued interdisciplinary collaboration among researchers, clinicians, industry stakeholders, and regulatory authorities will be pivotal in overcoming existing limitations and accelerating clinical translation. With sustained innovation and responsible development, nanostructured drug delivery systems are poised to play a central role in shaping the next generation of safe, effective, and personalized therapeutic strategies [137].
