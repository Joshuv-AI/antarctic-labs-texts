Spinal cord ischemia in patients on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a literature review
Authors: Berhane Worku, Monica Prario, Iosif Gulkarov, Ivancarmine Gambardella, Sandhya Balaram, Monika Tukacs, Yoshifumi Naka, Ankur Srivastava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveSeveral cases of spinal cord ischemia (SCI) on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA ECMO) support have been reported recently. We perform a review of the literature regarding this devastating complication with the goal of better understanding the relative distribution of these potential mechanisms and prognosis.MethodsA comprehensive search of the PubMed (MEDLINE) database for all articles in English language describing cases of SCI on VA ECMO support was performed. Introduction

Reports of spinal cord ischemia (SCI) on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA ECMO) support have emerged recently (1-13). Postulated risk factors include hypoperfusion and vasospasm, thromboembolism from the ECMO circuit, disruption of aortic wall thrombus or dissection from prior cardiac catheterization, aortic occlusion from intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) use, and stasis from the watershed phenomenon (1-13) (Table 1). We recently noted several cases of SCI in patients VA ECMO support, particularly in those with concomitant microaxial flow device (Impella) venting. We perform a review of the literature regarding this complication, its suggested mechanisms and potential solutions. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0619/rc).

Methods

A comprehensive search of the PubMed (MEDLINE) database for all articles in English language describing cases SCI on VA ECMO support was performed. Case reports and case series were included. Search terms included “spinal cord ischemia” or “paraplegia” or “paraparesis” and “extracorporeal membrane oxygenation”. No time limits were placed (Table 2). Reference lists of all reviewed articles were similarly searched for potential relevance.

Results

There were 13 reports with a total of 33 patients suffering from SCI on VA ECMO support (Table 3) (1-13). An overall incidence of 0.3–3% was noted in three studies (1-3). Average age was 49.1 years, and 12 (36%) were female. Thirteen (39%) had concomitant IABP support, and 1 (3%) had concomitant microaxial device support (Impella). Twelve (36%) had prior cardiac arrest, and 11 (42%) had prior cardiac catheterization. Twenty-eight (85%) had magnetic resonance imaging confirmation. Fifteen (68%) survived to discharge and 1 (8%) showed some neurologic recovery (Table 4).

Hypotension and cardiac arrest can be associated with SCI through hypoperfusion and vasospasm from vasopressor use, analogous to anoxic brain injury (14). In an autopsy study of 145 non-ECMO patients suffering a severe episode of hypotension or cardiac arrest, 66 (46%) demonstrated findings of SCI at autopsy, with 63 (96%) of these demonstrating involvement of the lumbosacral spinal cord (14). The authors speculate that despite the mid-thoracic spinal cord having the most precarious blood supply (hence its designation as the “watershed” zone), the higher density of neurons in the lumbosacral spinal cord may counterintuitively make it at higher risk for SCI (14). Episodes of severe hypotension and cardiac arrest are frequent in patients on VA ECMO support, which may explain the ubiquitous involvement of the lumbosacral spinal cord in VA ECMO-related SCI (1-7).

In the current literature review, 36% of patients with SCI on VA ECMO support had cardiac arrest prior to ECMO cannulation. In most cases, SCI was discovered in a delayed fashion due to sedation requirements and encephalopathy (1-2,4-9). In a few reports, SCI developed after an interval period of intact neurological status (3,10). Such lack of a temporal association thus calls into question hypoperfusion as the etiology of SCI in VA ECMO.

In the current literature review, 39% of patients with SCI on VA ECMO support had concomitant IABP use. SCI related to isolated IABP use (in non-ECMO patients) has been reported to occur with an incidence of 1.7% (3/178) (15). Postulated mechanisms include mechanical disruption of aortic wall atheroma with cholesterol embolization or aortic dissection (4,10,15). Samadi et al. reported three cases of SCI in young women undergoing VA ECMO support with concomitant IABP unloading. They hypothesized that small aortic caliber with complete occlusion by the IABP may have predisposed to SCI (7). Other similar reports include exist, although in one of these the IABP was removed in two of three patients more than a week before signs of SCI developed (2-3). Again, lack of temporal association calls into question IABP use as the causative agent of SCI in VA ECMO.

Other reports of SCI on VA ECMO support suggest an association with prior cardiac catheterization, again likely related to embolization of aortic wall atheroma or aortic dissection. In the current literature review, 46% of patients with SCI on VA ECMO support had prior cardiac catheterization. However, all patients who undergo transfemoral cardiac catheterization, IABP, or VA ECMO are at risk of aortic wall atheroma disruption or injury during wiring for arterial access and therefore significant overlap exists between these risk factors.

The vascular supply to the spinal cord is mainly through a single anterior spinal artery and paired posterior spinal arteries arising from the vertebral arteries. Additional paired segmental arteries arise from the descending aorta to supply the thoracic and lumbar spine. The largest of these, the artery of Adamkiewicz, has variable anatomy, usually originating around T9. Branches of the internal iliac artery (iliolumbar and lateral sacral arteries) provide arterial supply to the lower lumbar and sacral spine (16). Internal iliac artery coverage by the stent graft with occlusion of these branches is thought to be the cause of cases of SCI after endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm repair (17,18). Similarly, internal iliac artery occlusion by large bore femoral sheath/cannula placement may be another mechanism of SCI on VA ECMO support (3,4). This would be consistent with the association of SCI on VA ECMO support in patients with small caliber vessels in several reports. It has been recommended to insert the arterial ECMO cannula only a controlled distance (e.g., 5 cm) to prevent coverage of the internal iliac artery for this reason (3).

Pasrija et al. hypothesized that mixing zone hyperperfusion in the descending aorta from excessive combined ECMO and native cardiac blood flow may cause spinal cord edema and injury, similar to cerebral hyperperfusion after carotid endarterectomy or gastrointestinal edema after repair of long-standing aortic coarctation. They demonstrated a total flow (ECMO plus intrinsic cardiac output) of 8.5 L/min (indexed flow 4.1 L/min/m2) in seven patients with SCI on VA ECMO support, with some patients experiencing up to 12 L/min of flow and a median central venous oxygen saturation of 88%. An elevated opening lumbar pressure of 30 mmHg was noted in one patient in this series, likely secondary to hyperperfusion and spinal cord edema (3). They recommend adjusting ECMO flow to achieve a more physiologic total flow estimated by central venous saturation (3). It has also been suggested that obstruction of spinal cord venous drainage by the ECMO venous cannula may also contribute to hyperperfusion (3). This is very unlikely given the much larger size of the inferior vena cava, and that SCI is almost unheard of in patients on venovenous ECMO.

Femoral VA ECMO creates a unique physiologic condition known as the watershed phenomenon in which blood ejected antegrade from the heart mixes with blood pumped retrograde from the ECMO circuit. This mixing zone may occur anywhere from the ascending aorta to more distal points in the descending aorta as cardiac function recovers, and has been imaged with a variety of techniques including ultrasound, computed tomography, and computational flow dynamics (3,19). This mixing zone may be prone to stasis, turbulence, and thrombus formation (1,5,11,12). The extensive Impella CP catheter-associated thrombus seen in a report of SCI associated with microaxial flow device (Impella) usage lends strong support to the hypothesis of mixing zone stasis with subsequent thromboembolism and SCI (10).

The “north-south syndrome” phenomenon or differential cyanosis occurs when the blood ejected antegrade from the heart is relatively deoxygenated due to pulmonary dysfunction. Hypoxemia at the mixing zone may cause vasospasm and contribute to SCI if this occurs in the descending aorta (3). Suggested management options include conversion to venoarterial-venous ECMO to allow for return of oxygenated blood into the intracardiac circulation to be ejected antegrade (1). Furthermore, retrograde blood flow from the ECMO circuit tends to be hypocarbic as ventilator and ECMO settings are typically regulated to maintain normal upper extremity arterial blood gases values, which reflect blood ejected from the heart and lungs. Hypocarbia at the mixing zone may similarly cause vasospasm and contribute to SCI if this occurs in the descending aorta (3,5). Hypocarbia was even suggested as a mechanism for SCI on venovenous ECMO support in one case report (5). Management would require adjustment of ECMO settings to optimize both upper extremity and postoxygenator arterial blood gases.

Some have suggested that a solution for stasis related to the watershed phenomenon would be the creation of an artificial pulse on the ECMO circuit to reduce stasis (11,12). IABP use should therefore be protective against SCI on VA ECMO support as constant pulsation of the IABP should reduce stasis in the descending aorta. Furthermore, it has been suggested that left ventricular venting with microaxial flow devices (Impella) may reduce the risk of SCI associated with VA ECMO by moving the mixing cloud further downstream (11). We believe this is counterintuitive as nonpulsatile antegrade flow generated by a continuous-flow Impella device in combination with nonpulsatile retrograde flow generated by the continuous-flow ECMO pump would likely generate a more static mixing zone when compared to pulsatile antegrade flow generated by native cardiac ejection.

Singh et al. also questioned this in their recent report of a case of SCI on VA ECMO support with concomitant Impella CP. They noted descending aortic thrombus that they proposed was related to either disrupted aortic mural thrombus or stasis-induced catheter-associated thrombus (10). The latter theory implicates mixing zone stasis in the vicinity of the Impella CP with subsequent thromboembolization as a causative agent in SCI.

We agree that moving the static mixing zone distal (or proximal) to the most critical areas spinal cord blood supply through manipulation of ECMO flow may mitigate against the risk SCI. Ultrasound techniques that allow for bedside imaging of the mixing zone exist (19). Refinement of this technology in combination with an understanding of how mixing zone stasis impacts SCI on VA ECMO support may allow us to one day prevent this disastrous outcome.

VA ECMO is associated with significant bleeding and thrombotic complications. Bleeding events outnumber thrombotic events roughly fivefold, posing limits on the safety of aggressive anticoagulation. However, thrombotic complications tend to be more catastrophic. Furthermore, autopsy findings suggest that thromboembolic complications are significantly underestimated (20). Optimal anticoagulation goals are not clearly defined, as acknowledged in the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) anticoagulation guidelines (21). Most centers use a heparin-based anticoagulation protocol with adequacy of anticoagulation monitored by partial thromboplastin time, as well as by anti-Xa levels. However, significant practice variation exists, with some advocating for a multimodal approach incorporating activated clotting time, rotational thromboelastometry, and thromboelastography (22,23).

Several limitations of the current review exist. Given the rare nature of SCI on VA ECMO support, only case reports containing one to a few cases exist, precluding a true systematic review with inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, meta-analysis is not possible for the same reason. Any conclusions drawn from the literature with regards to etiologic agents and preventative strategies are therefore speculative at best as there is no comparative data or even large case series to draw from.

Conclusions

In summary, several reports of SCI on VA ECMO support have emerged recently. Proposed etiologies include aortic occlusion from IABP use, aortic dissection although this has never been confirmed, thromboembolization from prior cardiac catheterization or from the ECMO circuit, and hypotension or prior cardiac arrest with subsequent hypoperfusion. Some have implicated mixing zone hyperperfusion with subsequent spinal cord edema and mixing zone stasis with thromboembolism. Furthermore, hypocarbic arterial return from the ECMO circuit may lead to vasospasm of spinal branches from the descending aorta. Methods to image the mixing zone are currently available, and manipulation of the mixing zone through creation of an artificial pulse on the ECMO circuit or left ventricular venting with a microaxial flow device to move the mixing zone distally are potential solutions. Lumbar drainage has been attempted on selected patients with no improvement. Most authors advocate for decannulation if feasible. Further research is needed.
