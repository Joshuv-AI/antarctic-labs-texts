Innovative Electrosurgery Application in the Era of TAVR-In-TAVR
Authors: Leo Kar Lok Lai, Kent C.Y. So
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Zourane et al1 showcases a significant advancement in structural heart interventions by detailing the first use of transcatheter electrosurgery to perforate a neoskirt created after a transcatheter aortic valve–in–transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure. This innovation allows for percutaneous coronary intervention in a high-risk patient. As transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) extends to younger, lower-risk patient, the need for redo procedures and the presence of concurrent coronary artery disease are becoming more pressing concerns.

A computed tomography (CT) simulation study by Forrestal et al. found that up to 78% of TAV-in-TAV cases will have inaccessible coronary arteries.2 Zourane et al. describe a 74-year-old man with significant comorbidities, including a previous TAV-in-TAV (Evolut 29 mm followed by Sapien 26 mm), who experienced a non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction due to severe stenosis in the left main and proximal left anterior descending artery.

Despite likely the 2 skirts of the valves might not have overlapped, coronary access is not feasible as it is covered by the Evolut Frame despite coronary perfusion remains. The team used a SteelCore coronary guidewire insulated in a microcatheter, energized with brief 40W bursts, to successfully navigate through the neoskirt, dilate it with balloons, and perform bifurcation stenting, achieving excellent angiographic results. This technique, derived from established electrosurgical methods, offers an innovative solution to an emerging clinical challenge.

Coronary access after TAV-in-TAV procedures remains a major challenge. The formation of a neoskirt can lead to sinus sequestration and limited access, even when blood flow is maintained. By employing an electrosurgery technique, the authors present a minimally invasive solution for a patient without surgical options. They provide detailed procedural steps, from using an EBU 3.0 guide to sequential balloon dilatation (1.0 mm–2.5 mm) and advancing the guide extension, creating a reproducible approach.

The preprocedural CT identified a 3-mm valve-to-coronary distance and an absent valve-to-sinus junction, which contributed to the significant challenges in coronary access despite having coronary perfusion. Notably, the procedure was performed without complications, including aortic perforations or thermal injury, attributed to meticulous precautions: continuous fluoroscopic guidance, low-power settings, and the use of nonionic contrast media, as previously reported by Bruce et al.3

However, there are limitations to consider. This is a single-case report, which affects its broader applicability. The success of the procedure relied on the specific anatomical arrangement—where the first TAVR valve (Evolut) was positioned slightly more aortic, and the second valve (Sapien) was positioned very ventricular likely to treat aortic insufficiency. A planning CT scan might be able to identify different modes of failure for coronary access.4 Besides, the authors stress the importance of further experience to refine aspects like balloon sizing for dilating the skirt. In addition, long-term outcomes regarding neoskirt durability post-perforation or late stent complications remain uncertain.

According to the CAvEAT Registry, factors contributing to unfeasible coronary access include commissural misalignment, implantation depth, and the use of tall-frame transcatheter heart valves.4 Furthermore, Tarantini et al. demonstrated that coronary access could be feasible after TAV-in-TAV for type 1 (coronary ostia and access above the risk plane) and type 2a scenarios (ostia below and access above the risk plane with a valve-to-artery distance >2 mm).5 Conversely, type 2b scenarios (ostia and access below the risk plane with a distance <2 mm) present a high risk of coronary obstruction. Highlight of the case is that coronary perfusion is not equal coronary access in the future (Figure 1: Panel A). There could be coronary perfusion, but access is challenging. Moreover, complete misalignment of the first valve would likely render the electrosurgical technique ineffective. Therefore, lifetime planning, especially in the younger TAVR cohort, is important. In summary, Zourane et al.’s report addresses a significant gap in the TAVR field. By successfully restoring coronary access through neoskirt perforation, it demonstrates how transcatheter innovations can overcome procedural barriers in patients with limited options. Further studies, including bench tests and multicenter registries, are necessary to enhance accuracy and safety.
