Impact of corneal guttata severity on visual quality in early-stage Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy
Authors: Mengyu Zhang, Xin Li, Yan Liu, Fei Li, Ning Gao, Zhenying Shang, Ruibo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo quantitatively evaluate the relationship between corneal guttata and visual quality in early-stage Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) without corneal edema.MethodsTwenty eyes from twenty patients with FECD were enrolled at Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital. Guttata were classified into three grades, and objective visual quality parameters, including objective scatter index (OSI), modulation transfer function cutoff frequency (MTF cutoff), Strehl ratio, and predicted visual acuity at 100%, 20%, and 9% contrast levels (OV100%, OV20%, OV9%), were measured using Optical Quality Analysis System II (OQAS-II). Corneal densitometry, as an indicator of backward light scatter, was assessed using a corneal Scheimpflug tomography (Pentacam HR), including anterior and posterior corneal layers. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was recorded in logMAR. Differences among guttata grades were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction. Correlations between guttata and visual quality were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation.ResultsGuttata severity correlated positively with OSI(0.822, P<0.001) and negatively with MTF cutoff, Strehl ratio, OV100%, and OV20%(-0.750, -0.554, 0.744, 0.567, separately, P < 0.05), while correlation with OV9% was weak(0.479, P = 0.033). No significant correlations were observed between guttata severity and either anterior or posterior corneal densitometry. BCVA showed no correlation with guttata severity or OSI but correlated moderately with MTF cutoff and Strehl ratio (-0.520, -0.551, separately, P < 0.05).ConclusionIn early-stage FECD, patients’ visual quality begins to decline as the density and confluence of guttata increase despite the absence of visual acuity loss or corneal edema. Quantitative guttata assessment combined with objective visual quality inspection provides a more sensitive indicator for early functional impairment and may guide earlier clinical intervention.

Introduction

Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is a bilateral, progressive disorder characterized by abnormal accumulation of extracellular matrix (guttata), thickening of Descemet’s membrane, and progressive loss of corneal endothelial cells. The corneal endothelium plays a critical role in maintaining transparency and hydration through its barrier and pump functions. As endothelial function deteriorates and becomes decompensated, corneal edema develops, ultimately leading to irreversible visual impairment [1–3]. The visual symptoms caused by FECD—including visual decline, diurnal fluctuations in visual acuity, glare, and ocular discomfort—significantly affect patients’ quality of life and are important clinical indicators for determining the optimal timing of surgical intervention [4].

At present, Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) remains the standard treatment for FECD [5–7]. In recent years, novel therapies such as Descemetorhexis without endothelial keratoplasty (DWEK) [8], corneal endothelial cell injection [9], and topical Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor therapy [10] have been developed. These emerging strategies aim to reduce surgical risks and allow earlier intervention before irreversible corneal edema occurs.

Interestingly, recent clinical observations have shown that some patients with early-stage FECD experience visual disturbances even when visual acuity remains relatively preserved. Previous research has primarily focused on postoperative improvements in visual quality following surgical treatment [11–14], while less attention has been given to changes in visual quality during the early stage of the disease. In this stage, guttata accumulate as excrescences on Descemet’s membrane, which may cause light scattering and visual blurring [4]. Intraocular forward light scatter, optical aberrations and corneal backscatter have been regarded as major factors contributing to visual impairment in FECD [15–18]. Watanabe et al. reported that, in mild FECD, increased intraocular forward light scatter was associated with reduced corrected distance visual acuity [15]. In addition, other studies have demonstrated that anterior and posterior corneal higher-order aberrations and backscatter were elevated compared with normal levels, even in the early phase of the disease [16].

The Optical Quality Analysis System Ⅱ(OQAS-II) has emerged as a valuable tool for evaluating optical degradation in corneal and refractive disorders, including tear film instability, cataract, and corneal irregularities [19–21]. By quantitatively measuring intraocular forward light scatter, optical aberrations, and retinal image degradation, OQAS-II provides reproducible parameters such as the objective scatter index (OSI), modulation transfer function (MTF) cutoff, Strehl ratio, and optical quality values (OVs) under varying contrast conditions [20]. These metrics offer objective assessments of visual quality that are less dependent on patient cooperation and can detect subtle optical disturbances not captured by conventional visual acuity testing [22]. Given that forward light scatter and aberrations are key contributors to visual impairment in FECD, double-pass-based optical quality analysis may provide a sensitive means of identifying early functional deterioration prior to overt acuity loss.

Despite these observations, systematic and objective evaluations of the relationship between guttata severity and visual optical quality remain limited, particularly in early-stage FECD patients whose best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) has not yet significantly declined. To assess this gap, the present study classified the severity of guttata based on their morphological characteristics and density, and quantitatively assessed objective visual quality parameters to evaluate their association. The findings of this study are expected to provide a scientific basis for facilitating stage-based management and guiding treatment decisions in FECD before significant loss of visual acuity occurs.

Methods

Participants

This prospective, observational study recruited twenty eyes from twenty patients with FECD without corneal edema at Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital. All patients were examined and diagnosed by a corneal specialist using slit-lamp biomicroscopy to identify central or paracentral guttata or corneal edema. Central corneal thickness (CCT) measured using corneal Scheimpflug tomography (Pentacam HR; Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) was reviewed for each eye and interpreted in conjunction with slit-lamp biomicroscopy and in vivo confocal microscopy findings. Eyes with CCT exceeding the normal reference range, together with clinical or in vivo confocal microscopy findings suggestive of corneal edema, were excluded from the study cohort.

Patients with dry eye, keratitis, corneal scar, cataract, a history of corneal or intraocular surgery, or other intraocular diseases were excluded. Individuals over 65 years of age were also excluded to minimize the influence of age on visual quality parameters. When both eyes of a patient met the inclusion criteria, one eye was randomly selected using the random number method to avoid selection bias. The study was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (registration number: ChiCTR2500112515) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital (Protocol number:2025KY-71). All subjects provided informed consent after they received a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and anticipated benefits.

Guttata classification: density and morphology

Anterior segments examinations were performed using slit-lamp microscopy. Quantitative and morphological distribution maps of corneal endothelial guttata were obtained using laser confocal microscopy (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). Patients were asked to fixate on a red light, and the endothelial guttata distribution of the 3.0 mm central corneal area and peripheral margin adjacent to this area was recorded with a field of view of 400 × 400 µm2. Based on the modified Krachmer classification [23], FECD without corneal edema was categorized into three grades according to the density and degree of confluence of central guttata: Grade1: ≤12 central guttata, Grade2:>12 central, non-confluent guttata, and Grade3: central confluent guttata. Considering the sample size in the present study, Grade 3 and Grade 4 of the original modified Krachmer scale were merged into a single category (defined as Grade 3 in our analysis).

In this study, slit-lamp examination was performed by a single experienced corneal specialist. In contrast, in vivo confocal microscopy, OQAS and BCVA assessments, and Pentacam examinations were independently conducted by three experienced technicians. Guttata grading was based on confocal microscopy images and was performed by the technician responsible for the confocal examination. To minimize potential subjective bias, different components of the examination were allocated among trained personnel rather than being performed by a single examiner.

Objective visual quality and visual acuity assessment

OQAS-II (Visiometrics S.L., Terrassa, Spain) was used to objectively evaluate visual quality. The system measured the OSI, MTF cutoff, Strehl ratio, and predicted visual acuity (VA) under three contrast conditions: 100%, 20%, and 9% (OV100%, OV20%, and OV9%). OQAS-II is a double-pass system that records the retinal image of a point light source to obtain the point spread function (PSF), thereby quantifying the combined effects of intraocular light scatter and optical aberrations. Measurements were performed in a dark room after full correction of refractive errors. The OSI provides an objective index of intraocular forward light scatter with higher values indicating greater scatter. MTF is derived from the Point Spread Function (PSF) through Fourier Transform. The MTF cutoff frequency, defined at a value of 0.01, serves as an indicator of the optical system’s imaging quality. Higher values indicate superior image quality. The SR quantifies the ratio between the actual and ideal PSF, reflecting the integrated performance of the optical system, which is one step ahead of wavefront analyzer. Its value, ranging from 0 to 1, is positively correlated with the visual quality. The predicted visual acuity values at 100%, 20%, and 9% contrast levels (OV100%, OV20%, and OV9%) were used to assess visual performance under different contrast conditions.

Corneal densitometry, reflecting corneal light backscatter and transparency, was assessed using Scheimpflug tomography (Pentacam HR; Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). Densitometry values were automatically generated by the software and expressed in grayscale units from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater corneal opacity. The densitometry measurements were analyzed in predefined corneal zones, including the anterior layer (central 0–120 μm from the anterior corneal surface, anterior corneal densitometry) and the posterior layer (central 60 μm from the posterior corneal surface, posterior corneal densitometry).

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was determined using the best spectacle correction after manifest refraction and subsequently converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Baseline data among the three guttata grades were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. The associations between objective optical quality variables and either corneal guttata grade or BCVA were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Correlation between corneal guttata and objective visual quality parameters

The descriptive data of the objective visual quality parameters for each group are presented in Table 2. Results of the Kruskal-Wallis test show that there were statistically significant differences among the three grades in the OSI, MTF cutoff, Strehl ratio, OV100%, OV20%, and OV9%. In the Grade 1 group, OSI and MTF cutoff values were generally within or close to the reference range, whereas in the Grade 2 and Grade 3 groups, 41.67% of eyes exhibited OSI values outside the reference range, and 83.33% demonstrated MTF values below the reference range. Strehl ratio values in all three grades were notably higher than the reference value (Table 2). The relationships between guttata grades and visual quality are illustrated in Fig. 2 and detailed in Table 3. Significant intergroup differences were observed for all objective visual quality parameters, except for OV9% and corneal densitometry (both anterior and posterior). Pairwise Mann-Whitney tests with Bonferroni correction showed that grade3 differed significantly from grade1, while no significant differences were observed between grades1 and 2 or between grades2 and 3(Fig. 2). Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant correlations between guttata severity and potential confounding variables: age, sex, CCT and BCVA (Table 3). However, the severity of corneal guttata showed a strongly positive correlation with the OSI (Fig. 2A), and negative associations with MTF cutoff, Strehl ratio, OV100%, and OV20%. (Figure 2B and D, and 2E). A weak correlation was observed between guttata severity and OV9% (Fig. 2F). No significant correlation was observed between guttata grade and either anterior or posterior corneal densitometry(Table 3).

Discussion

The classification of FECD has traditionally been relied on the severity of corneal edema or the extent of guttata confluence through semi-quantitative slit-lamp biomicroscopy [23]. In early-stage FECD without clinically detectable corneal edema, previous studies have suggested that the guttata area ratio may serve as a more objective and sensitive indicator for disease severity [15]. In the present study, laser confocal microscopy was employed to obtain high-resolution density and morphological distribution maps of guttata of the central cornea. Based on the modified Krachmer classification [23], three grades of guttata—Grade1, Grade2, Grade3—were established in FECD. This stratification of guttata severity was useful clinically to evaluate the severity of the visual quality in early-stage FECD where conventional clinical signs may be subtle.

Using this grading system, the present study demonstrated that guttata grades were significantly positively correlated with OSI(ρ = 0.822), and negatively correlated with MTF cutoff, Strehl ratio, OV100%, OV20% through Spearman’s correlation analysis. These findings suggest that in mild FECD without corneal edema, guttata primarily lead to increased intraocular forward scatter which in turn impairing visual quality. Patients with mild guttata demonstrated slightly better optical quality at the high spatial frequency compared to those with severe guttata, suggesting that the severity of guttata also plays a crucial role in determining visual performance. In contrast, OV9% showed only a modest correlation with guttata grades, without a corresponding significant between-group difference, and was therefore not consistently supported across statistical comparisons.

Previous studies assessed the visual quality of patients with FECD primarily using subjective methods such as visual acuity and contrast sensitivity, which rely on patients’ subjective judgment and are limited by their inherent variability [24]. In contrast, the Optical Quality Analysis System II (OQAS Ⅱ), utilized in present study, provides an objective and comprehensive evaluation of visual quality by integrating aberrations, diffraction, and intraocular scatter analyses. Its high repeatability and reproducibility have been validated in previous studies [22]. Watanabe et al. reported that in eyes with mild FECD without severe corneal edema, corneal guttata increased subjective forward light scatter that negatively affected the visual quality [15]. This observation was consistent with our objective findings, where greater guttata confluence was associated with a significant increase in OSI, indicating elevated intraocular scatter and consequent deterioration of visual quality. Furthermore, the present study also observed that both the MTF cutoff and objective visual acuity under 100% and 20% contrast levels were negatively correlated with guttata grades, indicating that optical resolution and image clarity of the human eye decline once guttata become confluent. This finding has not been reported previously.

The lack of a significant association between guttata severity and anterior or posterior corneal densitometry may be related to differences in disease stage compared with previous studies. Earlier reports demonstrating increased corneal backscatter in FECD generally included a wider range of disease severity, particularly moderate to advanced cases, in which stromal edema and tissue remodeling can increase corneal light scatter [16, 25]. In contrast, our study was limited to early-stage FECD without clinically detectable edema, where pathological changes are mainly restricted to the endothelium and Descemet membrane. Under these conditions, progressive guttata may not substantially alter overall corneal densitometry. Furthermore, the relatively small number of Grade 3 eyes may have reduced the power to detect subtle differences, and this issue should be further investigated in future studies including a broader spectrum of disease severity and longitudinal follow-up.

Wacker et al. reported that aberrations were increased prior to the onset of clinically detectable edema, and were known to persist even after endothelial keratoplasty [16]. Irregularities of the anterior cornea associated with FECD have been shown to impair postoperative visual quality by increasing HOAs [11, 26]. In line with these findings, the present study used Strehl ratio to evaluate the impact of aberrations on the human eye in FECD. Our results showed that a decreasing trend in Strehl ratio was observed with increasing guttata confluence and reduced BCVA. This pattern suggests that eyes with more advanced guttata tend to show greater optical degradation, reflected in reduced retinal image quality. Taken together, these results suggest that the OQAS Ⅱ system may serve as an effective and objective clinical tool for early detection and monitoring of visual quality deterioration in FECD.

Interestingly, no significant correlation was found between guttata grades and BCVA in the current study. Previous studies have shown that corneal structural alterations—such as increased central thickness, posterior corneal curvature changes, and epithelial thickening—may be associated with reduced contrast sensitivity but not necessarily with BCVA [24, 26–27]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the limitations of conventional visual acuity testing, which primarily assesses high spatial frequency targets under maximum contrast, and thus fails to detect subtle impairments in visual function. As a result, such tests may not accurately reflect the actual visual performance experienced by patients in daily life. In the present study, simulated contrast visual acuity was employed to intuitively characterize the visual performance of the human eye under varying contrast levels. This approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the form perception function of the visual system than traditional visual acuity examinations, highlighting its potential value in complementing standard visual function evaluations.

Research limitations are inevitable. The relatively small sample size limited more detailed subgroup analyses, which was partly attributable to the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria applied to minimize potential confounding factors affecting visual quality, including corneal irregularities, corneal scarring, corneal edema, dry eye, cataract, intraocular disease, and a wide age range. As a cross-sectional study, this work could not adequately assess the impact of disease progression on visual quality. In addition, sex imbalance across groups should be acknowledged, as one subgroup consisted entirely of female patients, which may have introduced unrecognized bias. Accordingly, future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts, ideally incorporating longitudinal follow-up, are needed to allow more comprehensive stratified analyses, refine classification criteria, and further validate the relationship between guttata and visual function.

In the early stages of FECD, even in the absence of visual acuity loss or corneal edema, increasing guttata density and confluence is associated with poorer objective visual quality. These findings suggest that subclinical functional impairment of optical quality may occur earlier in the disease course than is typically detected by standard clinical examination, indicating the potential value of objective optical quality assessment in detecting early functional changes. Further larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings before stronger clinical management implications can be drawn.
