Microstructural remodeling and mechanical enhancement of expansive and clayey soils induced by magnetized water
Authors: Shen-long Wang, Bao-jun Wang, Pei-yang Cheng, Cheng-Cheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe stabilization of expansive and clayey soils remains a persistent challenge in geotechnical engineering. This study investigates the potential of magnetized water—a sustainable, physical treatment method—to enhance the engineering properties of such problematic soils. Through a comparative analysis using expansive and clayey soils from Nanjing, China, we evaluated changes in particle size distribution, Atterberg limits, permeability, compaction, and shear strength. The results indicate that magnetized water treatment induces the disintegration of coarse soil aggregates, shifting the particle size distribution toward finer fractions. This microstructural remodeling significantly improved mechanical performance: shear strength cohesion increased by 61.8%, the internal friction angle rose by 17.8%, and the maximum dry density improved. Hydrologically, the treatment reduced the plasticity index by up to 33.4% and decreased permeability due to the pore-filling effect of the dispersed fine particles. Scanning electron microscopy and porosity analyses support a microscale mechanism wherein the enhanced solubility and altered molecular cluster structure of magnetized water dissolve aggregate cementation, promoting a denser, more stable soil matrix. These findings suggest that magnetized water offers a viable, eco-friendly alternative to chemical binders for soil improvement applications.Keywords: Magnetized water, Expansive soil, Soil microstructure, Shear strength, Soil improvementSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The application of magnetized water technology has a long history, dating back to 1945 when Belgian engineer T. Vermeiren first utilized it for the efficient treatment of boiler scale. Since then, magnetized water has been widely adopted across various disciplines1. Extensive research has confirmed that, under appropriate magnetization parameters, this technology can effectively improve concrete strength, enhance agricultural irrigation efficiency, and facilitate industrial descaling2. In China, research into magnetized water applications peaked in the early 1980s, yielding significant results in fields such as concrete mixing, wastewater treatment, and medicine3. Notably, Wang et al. conducted experiments mixing magnetized water with sodium-based expanded clay, observing a significant reduction in slurry viscosity and shear force, which highlighted the notable impact of magnetized water on the properties of bentonite4.

While magnetized water is extensively and successfully applied in producing concrete components and the desalination of saline soils, a notable gap remains in the research regarding its specific application for enhancing soil engineering properties. Recent studies on magnetized water and soil have primarily focused on infiltration characteristics and salt transport within agricultural contexts. For example, Surendran et al. observed that differences in soil moisture levels 1–3 days after irrigation with magnetized water were less pronounced than those in control solutions5. Conversely, Khoshravesh-Miangoleh and Kian found that magnetized irrigation water significantly increased cumulative water infiltration rates compared to non-magnetized water6. Within geotechnical engineering, a limited number of studies have explored using magnetized water to improve soil strength. Al-Ani et al. found that magnetized water helped mitigate the expansion potential of expansive soils and increased unconfined compressive strength7, while Jawad and Karkush similarly demonstrated increased unconfined shear strength in treated clayey soils8.

Given these promising initial findings, this paper aims to systematically verify the feasibility of using magnetized water to improve the engineering properties of problematic soils through extensive laboratory experiments. Furthermore, we propose a microscopic mechanism to explain these interactions. Soil is a complex structural system consisting of particles of varying sizes and cementing agents that interact to form structural units. Water plays a critical role in defining the dynamic engineering properties of these units. Numerous studies indicate that treating water with a magnetic field alters its structure by disrupting hydrogen bonds9–12. This process imparts strong activation energy to the water molecules, influencing macroscopic physical and chemical properties such as surface tension, permeability, solubility, and electrical conductivity13–18. While the exact quantum mechanisms of magnetization remain a subject of active research in the physics community12, these established macroscopic alterations fundamentally affect how the pore fluid interacts with the charged surfaces of clay minerals, ultimately leading to changes in engineering properties. By analyzing these physical and mechanical changes, the effect of magnetized water on soil at the microscopic level can be robustly deduced.

Materials and methods

Soil and test apparatus

Two distinct soil samples were selected: an expansive soil sourced from Pukou District, Nanjing, and a general clayey soil obtained from Xianlin, Nanjing. The magnetized water was generated using a BST-type strong magnetic water processor (SZBME Co., Ltd.) with a magnetic induction strength of 8000 GS. The device functions as a flow-through magnetization unit (Fig. 1).

Grain size analysis test

Grain size analysis was conducted using the hydrometer method in strict accordance with the “Soil—Determination of particle size distribution—Pipette method and hydrometer method” (HJ 1068-2019) published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the PR China. Thirty grams of air-dried soil were divided into two conical flasks—one filled with 200 mL of ordinary water, and the other with 200 mL of magnetized water. Both samples were soaked overnight, and the resulting suspension was passed through a 0.075 mm sieve. The suspension passing through the sieve was boiled, cooled, transferred to cylinders, diluted to 1000 mL, and agitated. A hydrometer was used to record readings at intervals of 1, 2, 5, 15, 30, 60, 240, and 1440 min.

Hydrological experiments

All hydrological experiments were performed in accordance with the “Standard for Geotechnical Testing Method” (GB/T 50123-2019) published by the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the PR China. The Atterberg limits were determined using a GYS-2 digital liquid-plastic limit tester. Air-dried, sieved soil samples (0.5 mm) were mixed with either ordinary or magnetized water to achieve varying moisture contents, and the average cone penetration depth was recorded. Three parallel samples were tested for each soil type to ensure statistical reliability.

For the free expansion rate test, 10 mL of standardly prepared soil was poured into 100 mL measuring cylinders containing 30 mL of either ordinary or magnetized water, along with 5 mL of a 5% NaCl solution. After agitation and clarification, the soil volume was recorded every 2 h until stabilization was achieved. Permeability was assessed via a variable head infiltration test utilizing a TST-55 infiltrometer.

Mechanical experiments

Compaction tests to determine the relationship between dry density and water content were performed using a JZ-2D multifunctional compacting instrument. The soil was compacted in three layers within a lubricated cylinder according to standard specifications, then saturated and allowed to rest to ensure moisture equilibrium. Direct shear tests were conducted using straight shear instruments on samples prepared at a dry density of 1.81 g/cm³ and a water content of 13.8%. Tests were conducted at normal pressures of 100, 200, 300, and 400 kPa using a “fast shear” (unconsolidated-undrained) mode at a rate of 0.8 mm/min. Because clayey soils possess inherently low permeability, the fast shear mode is standard practice for evaluating short-term shear strength indicators (undrained cohesion and friction angle) under rapid loading conditions.

Results

Grain size analysis

The grain size analysis revealed distinct changes in particle distribution following magnetized water treatment (Table 2). For the clayey soil (Xianlin), the particle size was primarily concentrated between 0.005 mm and 0.05 mm. When comparing the ordinary water group (S1) to the magnetized water group (S2), the S2 group contained a significantly higher percentage of particles smaller than 0.005 mm—increasing from 0.3% in the control to 3.9%—while the silt fraction (0.05–0.005 mm) decreased by 3.4%. A similar trend was observed in the expansive soil (Pukou). Comparing the ordinary group (S3) and the magnetized group (S4), the content of particles larger than 0.05 mm decreased from 13.6 to 8.7%, shifting toward the finer 0.05–0.005 mm range. Both soil types demonstrated a clear transformation of larger aggregates into smaller particles under magnetized water treatment.

Hydrological Properties

The Atterberg limits test demonstrated that magnetized water treatment significantly altered the water content boundaries (Fig. 2). For the clayey soil, the average plastic limit increased by 10.2%, the liquid limit decreased by 2.9%, and the overall plasticity index decreased by 18.2%. The expansive soil showed even more pronounced changes: the plastic limit increasing by 12.2%, the liquid limit decreasing by 8.8%, and the plasticity index dropped drastically by 33.4%.

Comparison of Atterberg limits (plastic limit, liquid limit, and plasticity index) for soil samples treated with ordinary water versus magnetized water: (a) expansive soil samples; (b) clayey soil samples.

Regarding free swelling, significant volume changes were observed during the initial stage for all specimens, with stabilization occurring around 4 to 6 h (Table 3). The free swelling rate of the expansive soil treated with magnetized water increased by approximately 7.3% compared to the control, while the clayey soil showed a marked 20.3% increase. Notably, during the clarification stage, samples treated with magnetized water remained more suspended and settled to a greater final height, suggesting a strong dispersive effect.

Furthermore, permeability tests illustrated a clear reduction in flow through magnetized samples (Table 4). At a dry density of 1.7 g/cm³, the permeability coefficient of the magnetized water sample decreased by 30.4% compared to ordinary water. At a higher dry density of 1.8 g/cm³, the decrease was 22.0%. This reduction is attributed to the disintegration of soil aggregates into smaller particles that fill the original structural pores. shifting the pore characteristics from large structural voids to highly dispersed small pores, thereby increasing flow resistance.

Mechanical properties

Compaction curves revealed that magnetized water treatment improved the compaction characteristics of the soil (Fig. 3; Table 5). The maximum dry density increased slightly from 2.013 to 2.024 g/cm³, while the optimum moisture content decreased by 4.8%, dropping from 14.5 to 13.8%. This implies that magnetized water allows the soil to achieve higher density at lower water contents using the same compaction energy.

Shear strength tests further confirmed the mechanical benefits. The shear stress-shear strain curves showed that stress rose rapidly to a stable value without a distinct peak, which is typical for remodeled clayey soil (Figs. 4 and 5). Using the shear stress at 4 mm displacement as the failure criterion, the shear strength of magnetized water samples was consistently higher than that of ordinary water samples. Specifically, cohesion increased by an impressive 61.8%, rising from 42.4 to 68.3 kPa, and the internal friction angle increased by 17.8%, rising from 24.5° to 29.8°.

Discussion

The experimental data confirms that magnetized water positively influences the engineering properties of soil by remodeling its internal structure. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals that soil treated with ordinary water possesses a loose structure formed by large particle aggregates with significant porosity (Fig. 6). In contrast, soil treated with magnetized water exhibits a much denser packing composed of finer particles. This quantitative observation is objectively supported by our quantitative porosity analysis, which indicates that the porosity of compacted specimens decreased drastically from 26.21% (ordinary water) to 11.33% (magnetized water).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) micrographs of compacted soil specimens: (a) Ordinary water treatment (×300 magnification) showing loose structure; (b) Magnetized water treatment (×300 magnification) showing denser packing; (c) ordinary water treatment (×1000 magnification); (d) magnetized water treatment (×1000 magnification) revealing finer particle dispersion.

Recent geotechnical literature indicates that when fine clay content exceeds a critical threshold (e.g., 22%), the shear plane within a soil matrix becomes clay-dominated, leading to a smooth shear surface and a corresponding decrease in shear strength19. However, our grain size analysis demonstrates that while magnetized water treatment breaks down larger aggregates, it only increased the highly fine particle (< 0.005 mm) fraction from 0.3 to 3.9% in the clayey soil. Because this resulting fine fraction remains well below the critical failure threshold, these newly liberated fine particles act beneficially as pore-fillers. They increase inter-particle contact points and bulk density rather than forming a continuous, weak shear plane. This microstructural remodeling fundamentally explains why the shear strength significantly increased in our tests.

The mechanism driving this dissolution lies in the altered molecular structure of the treated water (Fig. 7). Ordinary liquid water forms large clusters via robust hydrogen bond networks20,21. Magnetic treatment disrupts these bonds, breaking the clusters into smaller, highly active molecules with increased activation energy22,23. These highly active magnetized water molecules exhibit enhanced solubility, allowing them to more easily penetrate the bound water membranes of soil aggregates. This penetration dissolves the cementing substances at the contact points of clay flakes, causing the aggregates to soften, collapse, and disperse. As these aggregates dissolve into discrete particles, they pack more densely under stress, reducing the void ratio and strongly enhancing inter-particle connections.

Conceptual mechanism of soil aggregate dissolution induced by magnetized water: (a) Large molecular clusters in ordinary water are unable to penetrate the bound water membrane of soil aggregates; (b) Magnetization breaks water clusters into smaller, highly active molecules that penetrate aggregates and dissolve cementation; (c) The dissolution of aggregates facilitates particle rearrangement and compaction under stress.

Conclusions

This study investigated the impact of magnetized water on the physical, hydrological, and mechanical properties of expansive and clayey soils, yielding the following key conclusions:

Magnetized water treatment causes the disintegration of coarse soil aggregates into finer particles, validating a strong microstructural dispersive effect.

The treatment effectively reduces the plasticity index (by up to 33.4%) and significantly decreases hydraulic conductivity due to the newly formed fine particles filling existing structural pores.

The breakdown of aggregates into a denser, closer-packed matrix significantly improves mechanical performance, increasing cohesion by 61.8% and raising the internal friction angle.

The fundamental mechanism driving these improvements is the enhanced solubility and smaller cluster size of magnetized water, which allows it to penetrate bound water layers and dissolve aggregate cementation.

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that warrant future investigation. First, the physical and chemical properties of magnetized water are influenced by multiple variables—such as magnetic field strength, fluid flow rate, and resting time post-treatment—which were kept constant in this study but require comprehensive parameterization. Second, due to equipment constraints, the dissolution dynamics of specific soluble salts within the clay during the microstructural remodeling process were not directly measured. Finally, the current findings are based strictly on laboratory testing. Pilot-scale field applications are necessary to validate the long-term engineering viability of this technique, particularly focusing on its behavior under consolidated-drained conditions in practical geotechnical environments.
