What is the clinical question being addressed?

Did traditional cardiovascular risk factors change in U.S. adults after the COVID-19 pandemic?

What is the main finding?

Most risk factors remained stable, while moderate and total recreational physical activity increased and vigorous activity decreased after the pandemic, suggesting beneficial lifestyle adaptation.

Understanding the changes in traditional cardiovascular risk factors before and after COVID-19 pandemic is crucial for evaluating the long-term impact on cardiovascular disease (CVD) burden. The pandemic introduced disruptions, reduced health care access, economic instability, and shifts in lifestyle, which may have either exacerbated or improved risk factors.1

However, nationally representative evidence after the pandemic in the general population remains limited. Given diverse experiences, subgroup differences by age, sex, and race/ethnicity also warrant further investigation.

This study examines changes in cardiovascular risk factors pre- and after-COVID-19 using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data. Findings may reveal population-level trends, informing targeted interventions, and prevention strategies to address long-term cardiovascular risks in the postpandemic era.

Methods

We used NHANES data from March 2017 to the pre-pandemic period in 2020 (pre–COVID-19) and from August 2021 to August 2023 (post–COVID-19), including adults aged ≥18 years.

CVD risk factors included sociodemographic, lifestyle, anthropometrics, biomarkers, and chronic disease and medication. Physical activity was defined per guideline as ≥150 min/week of moderate-intensity activity or equivalent, counting vigorous activity twice. Weight change was the difference between current and previous year’s weight. Other self-reported variables included insurance, health care access, medication use, and history of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

Anthropometrics were obtained at the Mobile Examination Center. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure were averaged from the last 2 of 3 readings. Hypertension was defined as SBP/diastolic blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg, antihypertensive medication use, or self-report. Obesity was body mass index ≥30 kg/m2 (non-Asian) or ≥27.5 kg/m2 (Asian). Central obesity was waist circumference ≥88 cm (females) or ≥102 cm (males) for non-Asians, and ≥80 cm (females) or ≥90 cm (males) for Asians.

Laboratory indicators included hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥6.5%, antidiabetic medications use, or self-report. Hyperlipidemia was total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL, lipid-lowering medications use, or self-report. Further details are available on the NHANES website.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using survey-weighted two-sample t-tests for normally distributed variables and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for non-normally distributed variables; categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Holm correction was applied to adjust P values for 28 comparisons, with significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses employed variable-wise exclusion of missing data and were conducted in R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing. https://www.r-project.org/), following NHANES analytic guidelines by incorporating the complex sampling design and Mobile Examination Center sample weights. Missingness appeared largely random based on comparison between missing and nonmissing groups. This study was exempt from review by the Yale University Institutional Review Board because NHANES data are publicly available and deidentified.

Results

The study included 15,302 adults (8,965 pre-COVID, 6,337 after-COVID), with similar age (47.5 vs 47.5 years), sex (male: 48.3% vs 48.5%), and race (White: 62.3% vs 59.5%; non-White: 37.7% vs 40.5%) across groups (all P > 0.10).

Most risk factors remained stable (body mass index, cholesterol, HbA1c, etc). While, after the pandemic, total recreational physical activity increased significantly (median [Q1-Q3]: 60 [0-330] vs 180 [39-420] min/week; P < 0.001), driven by moderate-intensity activity (median [Q1-Q3]: 0 [0-120] vs 120 [30-240] min/week; P < 0.001), whereas vigorous activity decreased with no change in (median [Q1-Q3]: 0 [0-80] vs 0 [0-60] min/week). Average sleep duration increased slightly but significantly (7.79 ± 1.39 vs 7.89 ± 1.43 h/d; P = 0.044). SBP (121.6 ± 17.4 vs 120.6 ± 17.1 mm Hg), uninsured rate (13.3% [11.1%-15.6%] vs 9.6% [7.8%-11.4%]), and limited health care access (16.7% [14.8%-18.5%] vs 13.8% [11.8%-15.8%]) showed slight declines but were not significant after Holm correction.

Subgroup analyses by sex, age (18-39, 40-59, ≥60 years), and race/ethnicity consistently showed significant increases in total and moderate-intensity activity across all groups (all P < 0.001). The proportion of adults not meeting physical activity guidelines remained largely consistent across subgroups, except for Asian adults (P = 0.48). In contrast, sleep duration did not differ significantly among subgroups (all P > 0.05).

Additionally, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol was significantly increased among males (133.9 ± 41.9 vs 135.6 ± 43.5 mg/dL; P < 0.001), Whites (132.9 ± 40.6 vs 135.0 ± 41.9 mg/dL; P = 0.005), and younger adults (18-39 years, 124.5 ± 38.9 vs 127.1 ± 37.0 mg/dL; P = 0.011).

Discussion

In this nationally representative analysis of U.S. adults, most traditional cardiovascular risk factors remained stable after the pandemic, whereas recreational physical activity increased significantly, driven by moderate-intensity exercise. These findings highlight the stability of CVD metrics and a potentially beneficial lifestyle shift.

Hummel et al reported minimal changes in cardiovascular biomarkers within 6 months of the initial lockdown in the Netherlands.2 Our findings are consistent, extending these observations to later pandemic years and a broader range of risk factors. Our previous studies also found that lipid-lowering therapy eligibility, hypertension prevalence and awareness, remained unchanged, whereas lipid-lowering therapy use modestly improved.3,4

The increase in leisure-time physical activity aligns with prior evidence. O’Neal et al found that moderate-intensity activity increased among U.S. adults from 2020 to 2022, following the pandemic.5 Stay-at-home orders likely shifted gym workouts to home-based exercise, supported by remote work policies and online fitness classes. Greater public awareness may also have encouraged more active lifestyles.

This study fills an important gap by characterizing national trends in cardiovascular risk factors over 2 pandemic years. While earlier investigations focused on infected patients or short-term lockdown effects, we used serial NHANES data from August 2021 through August 2023 to assess population-level changes. These findings provide critical evidence for CVD prevention strategies in the post-COVID era.

Selection bias may exist if severely affected individuals were underrepresented. Self-reported behaviors are subject to recall bias, and unmeasured policy or environmental factors may have influenced trends. COVID-19 infection status, variants, and vaccination were not accounted for.

In conclusion, cardiovascular risk profiles remained largely stable, but leisure activity increased significantly. Continued surveillance is warranted, and public health efforts should sustain access to flexible, home-based, and digital exercise options.
