Research in context

Evidence before this study

On May 7, 2026, we searched the Web of Science database for epidemiological studies projecting future heatwave-related mortality, using the search terms (“heatwave” OR “heat wave” OR “warm wave” OR “extreme heat” OR “heatwave event” OR “heat episode”) AND (“death” OR “mortality” OR “fatality”) AND (“project” OR “predict”) AND (“global” OR “worldwide” OR “world”). Searches were conducted in titles, abstracts, and keywords, with an asterisk truncation adopted to capture variant word forms of relevant terms and improve retrieval comprehensiveness. Of 558 records retrieved, over 90% focused on the meteorological projection of future heatwave events themselves rather than the epidemiological projection of health burdens. Although a subset of studies applied multi-stage analytical frameworks to project heat-related mortality, they were predominantly conducted at national or regional scales, and none were identified that employed a three-stage epidemiological modelling strategy on a global scale with the same degree of methodological harmonisation. The projections that do exist use widely varying heatwave definitions, derive baseline exposure–response relationships from disparate studies, and often omit or hold constant key socioeconomic drivers such as population age structure, urbanisation dynamics, and adaptive capacity. Consequently, globally comparable and comprehensive projections of future heatwave-attributable mortality that simultaneously integrate multiple climate, population, and adaptation scenarios under a consistent framework have remained absent from the literature.

Added value of this study

This study presents a globally consistent, grid-based, three-stage projection of heatwave-related mortality covering 176 countries and 21 regions. By integrating an ensemble of 20 CMIP6 climate models under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) with future population distributions, urbanisation trajectories, and explicit adaptation scenarios, we provide direct cross-regional comparisons and disentangle the individual contributions of climate change, demographic shifts, and adaptation to future mortality burdens. Our findings reveal that global heatwave-related deaths are projected to increase from approximately 1.0 million in the 2000s to between 5.3 million (SSP1-2.6) and 16.1 million (SSP5-8.5) by the 2090s, with Sub-Saharan Africa contributing the largest share. We further demonstrate that heatwave mortality projections are steeper under low urbanisation levels, high population growth (SSP3) scenarios, and in the absence of adaptation.

Implications of all the available evidence

The projected heatwave mortality burden underscores an urgent and escalating global health threat that will disproportionately concentrate in some of the world's most vulnerable regions. Even under the most optimistic low-emission pathway, heatwave-related deaths are expected to rise substantially, with Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and North Africa and the Middle East facing the greatest risks. Critically, our findings show that sustained investment in adaptive capacity—such as heat-health action plans, urban cooling infrastructure, and strengthened health systems—has the potential to avert millions of deaths, even under high warming scenarios. These results provide a quantitative basis for prioritising equitable adaptation finance, mainstreaming heat-health strategies into national climate and public health planning, and reinforcing the health co-benefits of stringent climate mitigation.

Introduction

Climate change is the defining global health threat of the 21st century, with heatwaves posing a particularly severe and escalating risk to lives and wellbeing worldwide.1 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) estimates that 3.3–3.6 billion people already live in regions highly vulnerable to climate change, where heatwave exposure is intensifying.2 As warming continues, heatwaves are projected to increase dramatically in frequency, intensity, duration, and spatial extent.3 In China, for instance, the frequency and intensity of regional heatwaves nearly doubled between 1960 and 2018,4 and climate models indicate that even a 0.5 °C rise in global average temperature could substantially amplify heatwaves.5

Heatwaves inflict a cascade of health impacts,6, 7, 8, 9 ranging from acute heat-related illnesses such as heatstroke and dehydration to the exacerbation of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases,8,10,11 a risk compounded by the poor air quality that accompanies the stagnant atmospheric conditions driving the heatwaves. The heatwave also disrupts sleep,12,13 and imposes a heavy mental health burden, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations.14,15 The three exceptionally hot summers of 2022–2024 were associated with over 181,000 heat-related deaths in Europe,16 in India, an average of 1116 deaths annually were attributed to heatwaves between 2008 and 20197; and in China, the average number of heatwave exposure days per person in 2023 reached 16, more than three times the historical average, contributing to a 1.9-fold rise in heatwave-related deaths.17

A growing body of research has quantified the historical mortality burden of heatwaves,18 reporting, for example, a 9% (95% CI: 5–13%) increase in all-cause mortality risk during heatwave days (defined as 3 or more consecutive days with daily mean temperature above 92.5th percentile) compared to non-heatwave days across 31 Chinese cities during 2007–2013, and a 5% increase in Queensland, Australia, over 2010–2019 using a similar relative-threshold definition.6,19 Moving forward, a critical challenge is to project how this burden will evolve. Projections for China suggest tens of thousands of excess deaths annually by the end of the century,20,21 while estimates for the Middle East and North Africa indicate that mortality risks from heatwaves could increase 8- to 20-fold by the end of the century compared to historical baseline levels.22 However, globally comparable and comprehensive projections remain scarce, largely due to critical methodological inconsistencies.23,24 The lack of a standardised analytical framework is a key barrier. Heatwave definitions vary markedly in their temperature thresholds and minimum duration criteria; baseline exposure–response relationships are derived from disparate studies, introducing heterogeneity; and, perhaps most critically, key socioeconomic drivers, including population age structure, urbanisation dynamics, and adaptive capacity, are often held constant, modelled under a single trajectory, or omitted entirely.18,25,26 These inconsistencies preclude direct cross-regional comparison, obscure global vulnerability hotspots, and hamper evidence synthesis for policy. A unified, multi-scenario framework that systematically harmonises climate, population, and risk data is therefore urgently needed.

In an effort to address these gaps, we present a comprehensive, consistent global projection of future heatwave-related mortality. Our framework integrates the largest multi-location meta-analysis of heatwave effects to date, encompassing 750 locations across 43 countries, with an ensemble of 20 CMIP6 climate models under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5). Crucially, we simultaneously incorporate scenarios of future population change, urbanisation, and varying adaptation levels. This enables us to quantify the potential mortality burden across global, regional, and national scales and to disentangle the individual contributions of climate change, demographic shifts, and adaptation. Our findings aim to more precisely identify populations and regions at greatest risk, thereby guiding targeted, long-term prevention and adaptation strategies in a warming world.

Methods

We adopted a global, grid-based, and three-stage framework to project heatwave-related mortality under varying climate, population, urbanisation, and adaptation scenarios. A heatwave was defined as a period of at least two consecutive days with daily mean temperatures exceeding the grid-specific 95th percentile. This relative, percentile-based threshold with a two-day duration was chosen to keep the exposure definition consistent with the metric used to estimate location-specific relative risks,27 a prerequisite for valid health impact projections, and to capture spatial heterogeneity in acclimatization by defining heatwaves relative to each grid cell's local climate, rather than an arbitrary absolute temperature. The study was prepared in accordance with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement, where applicable. A completed GATHER checklist is provided as the Supplementary File. Excess deaths were estimated by combining projected exposure with historical exposure–response relationships and the overall technical workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Study design and area

The baseline period was set as 2000–2009, aligning with the most recent available historical data. Future projections were made for three periods: the 2030s (2030–2039), the 2050s (2050–2059), and the 2090s (2090–2099). We used four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and their associated climate forcings from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. These scenarios were combined with the three future periods to assess the implications of different climate trajectories over time.

All analyses were conducted on a common 0.5° × 0.5° grid, covering 63,029 land grid cells globally. This resolution was chosen to directly match the native resolution of below exposure and population data, thereby avoiding additional statistical downscaling that could introduce unknown spatial biases and offering a practical compromise between computational tractability, spatial consistency with exposure and population data, and preservation of the heatwave signal relevant to health impact assessment. Results were aggregated across 176 countries, grouped into 21 regions following the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) classification based on epidemiological similarity and geographic proximity (Supplementary Table S1). For higher-level synthesis, the world was further divided into seven super-regions: Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia; High-income; Latin America and Caribbean; North Africa and Middle East; South Asia; Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania; and Sub-Saharan Africa (Supplementary Figure S2).

Data collection

Mortality and population data

We obtained baseline mortality data from the Wittgenstein Centre for Demography and Global Human Capital (WIC) Human Capital Data Explorer, which provides projections that integrate key socio-demographic drivers, including educational attainment, ensuring consistency with the SSP narratives.28 Gridded population scenario data were obtained from Murakami et al.,29 which provides decadal population estimates for 0.5° × 0.5° grid cells from 1980 to 2100. For 1980–2010, the data were downscaled from actual country-level populations, while for 2020–2100, they were downscaled from projected populations under SSP1, SSP2, and SSP3 (SSP1: globalisation; SSP2: business as usual; SSP3: fragmentation). We extracted population data for the 0.5° × 0.5° grid cells using zonal statistics and aligned historical data with the baseline period and projected data with the corresponding future periods. For the core analysis, population exposure under all four climate scenarios was based on the SSP2 gridded population projections. This provided a consistent demographic baseline, allowing the mortality impacts of differing climate forcings to be compared directly.

Urbanisation level data

Urbanisation dynamics for the core projections followed the default narratives of the respective SSP climate scenarios. For the stratified analyses, we used the urbanisation dataset from Chen et al.,30 which provides annual urbanisation rates (percentage of population living in urban areas) for 215 countries over 1960–2019. These historical rates were harmonised by the United Nations Population Division from World Bank estimates, based on the World Urbanisation Prospects: 2018 Revision. The same dataset also provides projections of urbanisation for 188 countries/regions from 2010 to 2100 under five SSP urbanisation speed assumptions: SSP1 (moderate), SSP2 (slow), SSP3 (slow/stagnant), SSP4 (moderate/slow), and SSP5 (fast).31

Projected daily mean temperature under climate change scenarios

Daily mean temperature data were obtained from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6) dataset.32 This dataset provides bias-corrected and spatially disaggregated CMIP6 projections at a 0.25° resolution from 1950 to 2100, using a daily variant of the monthly bias correction/spatial disaggregation (BCSD) method.33 We used near-surface air temperature from the historical experiment (2000–2009) and from four future scenarios: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5, each for three 10-year periods: 2030–2039, 2050–2059, and 2090–2099. To achieve a common 0.5° × 0.5° resolution, the 0.25° data were aggregated to the target grid using bilinear interpolation for each of the 20 GCMs for which complete data were available across all four SSPs throughout the 21st century (Supplementary Table S2). These daily temperature simulations were then used to identify heatwave days.

Statistics

Definition of heatwave event

Following a previous study,27 a heatwave was defined for each 0.5° grid cell as at least two consecutive days with daily mean temperature exceeding the grid-cell-specific 95th percentile of temperature during warm season, calculated from the baseline period (2000–2009). Analyses were restricted to the warm season (May–September in the Northern Hemisphere; November–March in the Southern Hemisphere), when heatwave-related health effects are most pronounced.34,35

Historical heatwave–mortality associations

The relative risk (RR) quantifies the elevated mortality risk on heatwave days compared with non-heatwave days. We adopted the location-specific heatwave–mortality RRs from Zhao et al.27 (Supplementary Table S3). Briefly, that study used a quasi-Poisson regression with a distributed lag nonlinear model for each of 750 locations, controlling for long-term trend (natural cubic spline with 1 df per decade), seasonality (natural cubic spline with 4 df for day of season, interacted with year), and day of the week. The lagged effect up to 10 days was modelled with a natural cubic spline with 4 df and two internal knots evenly spaced on the log scale. A meta-regression was then used to pool the location-specific estimates to the country and region levels.

Calculation of future heatwave-related excess deaths

Heatwave-related excess deaths (hereafter “heatwave-related deaths” or “excess deaths attributed to heatwaves”) were defined as the additional number of all-cause deaths occurring on heatwave days compared with the expected number of deaths that would have occurred if those days had not been heatwave days. This excess is quantified using the location-specific attributable fraction (AF) derived from the historical heatwave–mortality relative risk (RR). where EDit is the excess deaths attributed to heatwaves. N.Dit is the estimated baseline daily deaths, calculated as the product of the grid-cell population (N.POPit) and the daily mortality rate (MRit), with MRit set to the annual all-cause mortality rate for the country divided by 365, held constant at the 2000–2009 average. AFit is the attributable fraction derived from the relative risk (RRit). N.HWit is the number of heatwave days in year t.

Heatwave-related deaths were summed across grid cells to obtain national and regional totals. We also calculated excess deaths per 100,000 population for the warm season.

Stratified analyses

To evaluate the relative contributions of urbanisation, demographic change, and population adaptation to future heatwave-related mortality,36, 37, 38: we conducted a series of stratified analyses:

First, we stratified countries by their projected urbanisation level. Country-level urbanisation rates (proportion of the population living in urban areas) were obtained from SSP-consistent projections for each country and projection year. For each decade, countries were grouped into three categories based on their national urbanisation rate under the SSP2 scenario: low (below the 33rd percentile), medium (33rd to 66th percentile), and high (above the 66th percentile). Heatwave-related mortality was then compared across these categories. Second, the projections were repeated using the gridded population data for SSP1, SSP2, and SSP3 separately to isolate the effect of different demographic trajectories. Third, to examine the potential moderating effect of human adaptation to heat exposure over time, we modelled incremental reductions in population susceptibility by applying a decadal reduction to the log-RR. For an x% per decade adaptation scenario, the log-RR in a given future decade was multiplied by (1 – x/100)n, where n is the number of decades from the baseline period (n = 0 for 2000–2009). This parameterisation is a stylised sensitivity test and is not intended to capture the full complexity of adaptive processes. The ‘no adaptation’ (0%) scenario serves as the reference baseline in which the RR remains at its historical value; the 5% and 10% per decade scenarios represent progressively faster reductions in vulnerability.

Uncertainty assessment and sensitivity analysis

Uncertainty in the mortality projections stemmed primarily from two sources: inter-model climate variability and uncertainty in the exposure–response coefficients. We quantified these sources using a Monte Carlo framework that independently ran the health impact model for each of the 20 GCMs while sampling the RR from its reported distribution (using standard errors where available).27,39 Results were reported as the ensemble mean,40 with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles defining the 95% uncertainty interval (UI).

We further assessed the sensitivity of our projections to the statistical modelling choices underlying the RR estimates. Zhao et al.27 reported that their central RR estimates were robust across a range of alternative model configurations, including using non-external cause mortality, varying the maximum lag (extended to 15 days) and the degrees of freedom for lag and seasonal control, and adjusting for relative humidity. We repeated our projections using these alternative RRs to evaluate whether such modelling variations would materially alter our global burden estimates. In addition, we tested the sensitivity of our findings to the temporal window used for heatwave identification by dynamically defining the warm season as the five consecutive hottest months for each country, rather than using fixed calendar months. The resulting projections were compared with the primary estimates to confirm the overall robustness of our results.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1), primarily using the dlnm, metafor, geojsonio, and raster packages.

Results

Spatiotemporal distributions of heatwave days

Overall, during the baseline decade (2000–2009), the total number of warm-season heatwave days across regions ranged from approximately 46 to 63 days. Under the low-emission scenario (SSP1-2.6), heatwave frequency is projected to increase in the future but stabilise after the 2050s, reaching 264 days in the 2050s and 271 days in the 2090s. Conversely, under the intermediate-emission scenario (SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0) and high-emission scenario (SSP5-8.5), there will be a significant and sustained increase in the global heatwave frequency over time, with an increase to 475 days and 863 days, respectively, by the 2090s (Fig. 1A & Supplementary Table S4). Compared to the baseline, the global heatwave frequency in the 2090s is projected to increase by factors of approximately 4.6, 8.1, 10.0, and 14.6 under the SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, respectively.

Spatially, under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, higher heatwave frequency by the 2090s is projected to concentrate in the Caribbean, Oceania (islands), Southeast Asia, Andean Latin America, and Central Latin America. In addition, heatwave frequency will increase by 11.8–12.1 times compared to the baseline period in the aforementioned regions. Similarly, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, higher heatwave frequency is projected to mostly concentrate in the Caribbean, Andean Latin America, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and Oceania (islands). Concurrently, the most substantial rises in heatwave frequency are projected in these regions during the 2090s, with an increase of approximately 22.5–25.4 times (Fig. 1B–K).

Spatial distributions of the baseline heatwave-related deaths

Globally, heatwave-related deaths during the warm season in the 2000s were estimated at 1,018,776 (95% UI: 935,188–1,106,785). The five regions with the highest contributions in the 2000s include Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and central Asia (accounted for 30.98% of the total heatwave-related deaths), Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania (26.55%), Latin America and Caribbean (18.62%), North Africa and Middle East (16.26%), and High-income (16.35%) (Table 1). Specifically, Nigeria (253,531, 95% UI: 193,641–305,952), India (141,906, 95% UI: 132,339–156,985), China (48,378, 95% UI: 44,853–52,498), Russia (41,901, 95% UI: 39,267–44,915), Ukraine (17,339, 95% UI: 15,250–18,678), and United States (14,607, 95% UI: 13,532–15,392) experienced the highest numbers of heatwave-related deaths in the 2000s (Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 2B). The highest overall heatwave-related deaths per 100,000 people in the 2000s were observed in Central Asia, with 32 deaths per 100,000 people (95% UI: 30–35), followed by Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (24, 95% UI: 21–26), Eastern Europe (11, 95% UI: 7–12), and Central Europe (4, 95% UI: 3–5) (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S3B).

Projected heatwave-related deaths under future climate change scenarios

The total number of heatwave-related deaths varied across different emission scenarios. Under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, heatwave-related deaths are projected to increase. In contrast, under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, heatwave-related deaths are projected to increase until the 2050s, after which a relative stabilisation occurs (Fig. 2A). Even under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, heatwave-related deaths will rise to 4,194,417 (95% UI: 2,549,535–6,658,538) by the middle of the century, signifying a 7.14-fold increase relative to the baseline. By the end of the century, heatwave-related deaths are projected to surge to 5,289,925 (95% UI: 2,665,816–8,287,210), 8,814,669 (95% UI: 5,055,306–13,734,514), 13,690,238 (95% UI: 9,902,215–19,250,977), and 16,133,330 (95% UI: 11,559,920–21,300,978) in the 2090s under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, respectively, representing increases of 5.19, 8.65, 13.44, and 15.84 times over the baseline (Table 1).

Future heatwave-related death burdens are projected to exhibit pronounced spatial heterogeneity (Fig. 2C–K). By the 2030s, under the SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenario, deaths concentrated in Western Sub-Saharan Africa (accounted for 25.88% and 8.93% of the total heatwave-related deaths, respectively), South Asia (19.45% and 21.56%), East Asia (10.11% and 14.09%), Western Europe (7.24% and 10.09%), and North Africa and Middle East (6.87% and 10.32%). Over time, by the 2090s, under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, South Asia will bear the heaviest burden, accounting for 29.12% of total heatwave-related deaths, followed by North Africa and the Middle East (14.18%), Western Sub-Saharan Africa (11.32%), Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (6.26%), and Western Europe (5.15%). In contrast, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, the burden is projected to be most severe in South Asia by the 2090s, accounting for 27.24% of the global heatwave-related deaths, followed by Western Sub-Saharan Africa (14.12%), North Africa and Middle East (13.84%), Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (7.76%), and Western Europe (4.95%). All regions display a continuous upward trend, with particularly notable increases observed in Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania (islands), and North Africa and Middle East. By the end of the century, the heatwave-related deaths in above regions are projected to reach 45.12 to 104.65 times compared to the baseline (Supplementary Figure S4). Specifically, India (3,207,421 deaths, 95% UI: 2,056,581–5,076,622), China (1,356,400, 95% UI: 860,516–1,824,435), and Nigeria (1,269,831, 95% UI: 703,257–1,598,881) experienced the highest numbers of heatwave-related deaths by the 2090s under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Supplementary Table S5). By the 2090s, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, Eastern Europe is projected to experience the highest heatwave-related deaths per 100,000 people, with 28 deaths per 100,000 people (95% UI: 26–30), followed by Central Europe (18, 95% UI: 13–33) (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Figure S3N).

Impact of urbanisation, population growth and adaptation on heatwave-related health burden

The results stratified by urbanisation levels show that regions with lower urbanisation levels are projected to experience greater heatwave-related deaths per 100,000 people compared to those with moderate and high urbanisation level (Fig. 3A–C). Under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, heatwave-related deaths in regions with low urbanisation level are projected to exhibit a more pronounced increase. Specifically, heatwave-related deaths in these regions are projected to increase by 2.28 times in the 2090s compared with the 2030s, whereas those in regions with moderate and high urbanisation level are projected to increase by 1.23 and 1.24 times, respectively. Similarly, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, regions with low urbanisation level will see a more significant increase. By the end of the century, heatwave-related deaths in these regions are projected to increase by 5.32 times compared with the 2030s. In comparison, heatwave-related deaths in regions with moderate and higher urbanisation levels are projected to increase by 2.80 and 3.12 times, respectively.

For instance, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, when the adaptation ability increases by 5% or 10% per decade, the number of heatwave-related deaths would decrease from 16,133,330 to 10,319,925 or 6,405,610 in the 2090s, representing a reduction of approximately 38% or 62% (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses indicate that while these variations led to adjustments in the central (point) estimates of projected heatwave-attributable mortality, the magnitude of these adjustments was relatively limited (Supplementary Figure S5). The overall order of magnitude of the projected burdens remained consistent with our primary results.

Discussion

This study provides a spatially detailed assessment of future heatwave-related mortality by integrating multi-model climate projections, demographic scenarios, and a globally consistent exposure-response framework. Our analysis indicates a substantial and escalating global burden throughout the 21st century across all emission scenarios. Even under the low-emission SSP1-2.6 pathway, heatwave-related deaths are projected to increase significantly by the 2090s. As anticipated, the burden becomes markedly more severe under higher-emission scenarios, with projections under SSP5-8.5 exceeding those under SSP1-2.6 by a considerable margin by the end of the century. The substantial variations observed under alternative, fully consistent socio-economic pathways (e.g., higher burden under SSP3) in our stratified analyses further underscore that the realised future burden will be co-determined by development choices affecting population size, distribution, and adaptive capacity. These core projections are based on a constant demographic baseline (SSP2 population) across climate scenarios, isolating the direct mortality impact of different warming levels. These findings align with and extend previous regional and national studies, reinforcing the urgent public health imperative of climate mitigation.

Given the scarcity of comparable global-scale epidemiological projection studies, the following discussion centres on the scientific plausibility and practical implications of our results. Where possible, we relate our findings to existing evidence, but the emphasis remains on the internal consistency, regional patterns, and policy relevance of the projected burdens. The observed increasing trend is consistent with those reported in an earlier national study, indicating that under high-emission scenarios, the heatwave-related deaths burden will exhibit more pronounced and more rapid increases.20 To be specific, by the end of the century, heatwave-related deaths is projected to increase by factors of 5.19, 8.65, 13.44, and 15.84 under the SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, respectively. In light of these alarming projections, it is imperative for policymakers to implement robust climate adaptation strategies, including early warning systems, public health interventions, and enhanced infrastructure to alleviate heat exposure.10 Furthermore, future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of these strategies across diverse socio-economic contexts and identifying vulnerable populations to effectively target interventions.

There was significant regional heterogeneity in heatwave-related deaths. The global study estimated that 153,078 deaths were linked to heatwaves during the warm season from 1990 to 2019. Among these heatwave-related deaths, 48.95% occurred in Asia, 31.56% in Europe, 13.82% in Africa, 5.37% in the Americas, and 0.28% in Oceania.27 However, we further investigated the future regional heatwave-related deaths burden. Our findings indicated that, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, South Asia, Eastern Europe, and Western Europe would bear a higher heatwave-related deaths burden in the coming decades. However, by the 2090s, Western Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and North Africa and the Middle East are projected to face the most severe threat from heatwave-related deaths. A previous study in the Middle East and North Africa indicated that the highest mortality risk due to future heatwaves is found in the areas of western Africa below the Sahara.22 We speculate that this trend may stem from multiple factors, including rising temperatures and declining humidity, the latter suppressing evaporative cooling and amplifying heat extremes during heatwaves,41 rapid urbanisation, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure, all of which could amplify the burden of heatwave-related deaths in these regions.42

At the national level, India and China are projected to bear particularly high burdens of heatwave-related deaths under the SSP5-8.5 scenario in the 2090s, with projected deaths of 3,207,421 and 1,356,400, accounting for 19.88% and 8.41% of global heatwave-related deaths, respectively. Additionally, a previous study conducted in China estimated that under the RCP8.5 scenario, annual heatwave-related deaths could reach 72,260 by 2090.20 This disparity may stem from several methodological differences, including the climate scenarios employed (SSP-based scenarios in our study versus RCP8.5 in the China study), the data sources and time windows used to estimate the epidemiological associations, and the heatwave definition (e.g., the China study used a duration of three days and a threshold at the 92.5th percentile). In contrast, evidence regarding future heatwave-related death burden in India remains relatively scant. A comprehensive literature review on heatwave revealed that heatwave research has predominantly focused on mid-latitude temperate regions, particularly in North America and Southern Europe, followed by China and Australia. Additionally, limited research exists concerning South and Central America, Central and Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, and the far Northern Europe, Africa and the Middle East.43 Previous studies have often overlooked the risk of heatwave deaths in less developed regions, while our research highlights a severe future burden in Nigeria, Pakistan, and Ethiopia. Furthermore, our findings indicate that under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, the fastest increases in heatwave-related deaths by the 2090s are projected to occur in Africa, particularly in Liberia, Equatorial Guinea, Uganda, Qatar, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. This trend can be attributed to the rapid increase in both the frequency and intensity of heatwaves in this region, coupled with inadequate infrastructure, scarce resources, dwindling food supplies, economic inequality, and fragile political systems.44,45 Acknowledging the unequal exposure across different socioeconomic groups, identifying the vulnerable communities, and allocating sufficient resources for climate risk adaptation, thereby promoting environmental justice.46 Our study spans a significant portion of the globe to enhance awareness of equity concerns associated with regional climate change.

Our stratification analysis indicates that areas classified with low urbanisation levels are generally associated with higher projected heatwave-related mortality burden. This finding may appear counterintuitive, as many previous studies have highlighted the enhanced vulnerability of highly urbanised areas due to the urban heat island effect, high population density, and socioeconomic disparities.47,48 However, at the global scale, low urbanisation levels are often strongly correlated with lower GDP, limited access to healthcare, and weaker adaptive capacity—socioeconomic determinants of vulnerability that may outweigh the localised physical hazards associated with dense urban environments. In this context, the higher mortality burden observed in low-urbanisation regions in our study likely reflects a pre-existing deficit in adaptive resources rather than a direct protective effect of low urbanisation itself. By contrast, the amplified heat exposure in highly urbanised areas, driven by urban designs that trap heat, the loss of green spaces, reduced ventilation, and the heat-absorbing properties of building materials such as concrete and metal roofs, remains well documented.49, 50, 51 Nevertheless, even highly urbanised regions are not uniformly resilient; those with inadequate cooling infrastructure or healthcare access remain susceptible.52 Our findings highlight that adaptive measures must be tailored to context: in low-urbanisation regions, strengthening healthcare systems and expanding access to cooling are priorities, while in high-urbanisation areas, enhancing urban design with green-blue spaces and reflective materials is essential to mitigate the amplifying effect of the urban heat island.53,54

For the population growth scenarios, our results show that the highest heatwave-related deaths are projected under the SSP3 population scenario (fragmentation and unsustainable scenarios). Apart from the regional fragmentation, limited economic growth, and other secondary factors characterising the SSP3 population scenario, the primary driver of this phenomenon can be predominantly attributed to the higher population growth projected under SSP3 relative to other scenarios, thereby leading to an increase in heatwave-related deaths. For example, a previous study in China revealed that under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, heatwave-related excess deaths could reach approximately 165,500, 242,100 and 342,900 by the 2090s for low, medium, and high population patterns, respectively. Correspondingly, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, the deaths are projected to rise to 614,600, 900,400, and 1276,500 for the corresponding population patterns.55 Our findings highlight the urgent need for health-oriented and climate-orientated investments, especially in regions following the unsustainable pathway, to enhance food security, improve infrastructure, reforestation, community mobilisation, and healthcare services.1

Future heatwave burden depends not solely on the unmitigated health effects of heatwave exposure, but also on the interplay between the climate hazard and the pace of adaptation. Even a 5% per decade improvement in adaptive capacity yields a discernible reduction in heatwave-related mortality. However, without adaptation, even low-emission scenarios may not fully avert a rise in heatwave-related deaths, as previous studies indicate.26 In contrast, a Chinese study projected that heatwave-related deaths could decline from approximately 900,000 to 57,000 under SSP5-8.5 by the 2090s, assuming a certain level of adaptation.56 These contrasting outcomes underscore the necessity of prioritising adaptation strategies, such as adaptation planning and implementation, climate information services, and health risk early warning services.17

This study achieves near-global spatial coverage (176 countries, 0.5° × 0.5° grid). The primary NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 climate dataset and population data provide complete, gap-free projections.29,32 The main source of data sparsity lies in the underlying exposure–response meta-analysis: the RRs from previous study covering 750 locations across 43 countries concentrated in East Asia, North America, and Western Europe.27 For many low- and middle-income countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Latin America, RRs were assigned from meta-predicted estimates. This constitutes a critical data gap and a source of uncertainty, as the true exposure–response relationship in these under-studied, highly burdened regions may not be properly represented by the meta-prediction. Direct epidemiological studies from these locations are urgently needed to refine global projections.

Our findings translate into clear, interlocking imperatives for multi-sectoral action, structured around the distinct roles of mitigation, adaptation, and targeted vulnerability reduction. Stringent climate mitigation aligned with pathways such as SSP1-2.6 remains the most effective long-term strategy to limit the escalation of heat-related mortality, as it addresses the hazard at its source. However, given that substantial warming is already locked in, proactive adaptation is equally indispensable. At the national and regional level, this requires mainstreaming heat-health action plans into climate and public health strategies, with particular focus on identified high-burden regions such as Western Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. These plans must be underpinned by robust, socially inclusive early warning and response systems and strengthened healthcare preparedness for heat-related illnesses. At the city scale, where the urban heat island effect amplifies exposure, targeted urban design reforms, including expanded green and blue infrastructure, revised building codes for passive cooling, and reflective materials, can directly reduce the intensity of heat stress experienced by urban populations. Beneath these systemic interventions lie deeper structural vulnerabilities that shape differential susceptibility: our stratified analyses indicate that without concurrent investments in poverty reduction, education, healthcare access, and basic infrastructure in rural and low-urbanised areas, the benefits of adaptation will be unevenly distributed and may bypass those at greatest risk. Health professionals serve as essential intermediaries in this effort, translating evidence into risk communication and guiding preventive behaviours among susceptible groups, including the elderly, those with cardiorespiratory conditions, and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, while individual-level measures such as reducing outdoor activity during peak heat and maintaining hydration provide a vital last line of defence. Finally, the projected geographic concentration of mortality in some of the world's most resource-constrained regions elevates this from a national to a global equity challenge: without prioritised international cooperation and adaptation finance directed toward capacity building in the most vulnerable countries, the health benefits of adaptation will remain unrealised precisely where they are most urgently needed.

This study has several strengths. First, by encompassing a broad geographic scope, our study offers valuable insights into the projected global burden of heatwave-related deaths in the future, as well as the influence of demographic shifts and socioeconomic factors on heatwave-related death. Second, the global heatwave-related deaths were calculated in the form of grid cells to improve accuracy and reliability. Third, our study analysed the global burden of heatwave-related deaths across 21 regions and 176 countries, offering a crucial reference for preventive policies concerning heatwaves in various regions. Fourth, our study employs a transparent dual-scenario framework, allowing separate assessment of climate pathway impacts and integrated socio-climatic futures. Fifth, the projections are based on a robust ensemble of 20 climate models and incorporate dynamic, scenario-specific mortality baseline data from the Wittgenstein Centre, which accounts for key drivers like educational attainment, thereby providing more realistic future health denominators. Sixth, extensive sensitivity analyses confirmed that our central projections are robust to alternative statistical specifications of the exposure-response function and to different definitions of the warm season. By delivering an in-depth analysis of heatwave risks and vulnerabilities specific to each region, our study may be able to guide policymakers in implementing more targeted and effective measures to safeguard the health of susceptible populations.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the exposure-response relationships are assumed to remain constant over time in our baseline projections, representing a “no adaption” scenario. While we explicitly model alternative adaptation pathways, future physiological, behavioural, and infrastructural acclimatization could further modify these relationships in complex ways.57, 58, 59 Second, our central projections assume a constant baseline all-cause mortality rate that is applied to changing population totals. This isolates the mortality signal directly attributable to climate-driven heatwave changes. In reality, baseline mortality evolves under two countervailing forces. In rapidly ageing societies (e.g., East Asia, Western Europe), an older population would likely elevate heatwave-attributable deaths, because the elderly are more physiologically susceptible. Our estimates may therefore underestimate future burdens in these regions. Conversely, regions with significant public health improvements may experience declining age-specific mortality rates, which would lead our constant-rate assumption to overestimate future burdens. The net effect is region-specific and uncertain, as reflected in the divergent results under SSP1–3 population scenarios. Future work integrating dynamic, age-specific mortality rates and models that account for the long-term evolution of population health profiles is needed to better capture this interplay. Third, our parameterisation of adaptation as a uniform decadal percentage reduction in the log-RR is a simplified exploratory tool. It does not capture diminishing marginal returns, socioeconomic constraints on adaptive capacity, or region-specific biophysical limits. The adaptation scenarios therefore represent idealised reductions in vulnerability and serve to quantify potential benefits rather than predict exact trajectories of resilience. Fourth, the urbanisation-stratified analysis classifies entire countries based on a single nation-level metric. This captures a macro-level, cross-country association that confounds a country's urbanisation rate with economic development, healthcare infrastructure, and governance. Consequently, this finding cannot be directly translated into an individual-level ‘urban versus rural’ risk differential within a country. We present this result as a heuristic for identifying broad national typologies of risk, and validation with finer-scale data is an important direction for future work. Fifth, the analysis focuses on all-cause mortality attributable to heatwaves; future research quantifying cause-specific mortality (e.g., cardiovascular, respiratory56) and incorporating interacting environmental stressors (e.g., air pollution60,61) would provide deeper mechanistic insight. Finally, our projections are conditional on the 20-member CMIP6 ensemble, and individual models diverge in simulating the regional processes that drive heatwave. This leads to considerable inter-model spread in projected heatwave days and heatwave-related mortality at regional scales, particularly toward the end of the century. Such structural uncertainty means that the reported magnitudes should be interpreted as central estimates within a broad envelope of plausible outcomes, not as deterministic predictions. Importantly, even the lower bounds of this envelope under high-emission scenarios represent historically unprecedented burdens, confirming that the qualitative conclusion—that heatwave-related mortality will rise dramatically—is robust to model selection. Future work could refine regional precision through model weighting or emergent constraints.

In conclusion, this global projection study estimates that, without substantial mitigation, heatwave-related deaths could increase from approximately 1.0 million annually in the 2000s to between 5.3 and 16.1 million by the 2090s, depending on the emission pathway, with the steepest rises projected under SSP5-8.5 and SSP3-7.0. The geographic centre of this burden shifts progressively toward Western Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East, which together account for over 50% of global heatwave-related deaths by the end of the century. Stratified analyses further show that the mortality trajectory is amplified under scenarios of rapid population growth, lower urbanisation levels, and the absence of deliberate adaptation, whereas sustained vulnerability reductions of 5–10% per decade could avert several million deaths annually, particularly under high-emission scenarios. Taken together, these results underscore that limiting future heat-related mortality will depend on a combination of mitigation and adaptation. Ambitious emission reductions consistent with SSP1-2.6 remain essential to constrain the overall scale of risk, while adaptation efforts are likely to be most effective if prioritised in regions where projected burdens are highest, particularly Western Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East. At the same time, the measurable benefits observed even under modest adaptation scenarios support a stronger integration of heat-health considerations into national adaptation strategies and the allocation of international climate finance.
