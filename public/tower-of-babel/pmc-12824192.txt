Multi-criteria spatial assessment of urban open spaces for promoting physical activity and spatial justice: a case study of Tehran metropolitan
Authors: Majid Javid, Zahra Taheri, Samad Goodarzi, Ali Saberi, Saman Nadizadeh Shorabeh, Mohammad Karimi Firozjaei, Jamal Jokar Arsanjani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban open spaces play a crucial role in enhancing citizens’ quality of life and health, making the assessment of their quality essential for urban planning. This study aims to propose a framework for evaluating the spatial distribution of urban open space quality for physical activity based on a multi-criteria spatial approach. Tehran Metropolitan was selected as the case study area. Spatial data, including satellite imagery, digital elevation models, demographic information, urban infrastructure, and land use data, were collected for this purpose. The study generated maps of various effective spatial criteria, including ten environmental criteria and ten accessibility and infrastructure criteria. After normalizing the criteria using the min-max method, criterion weights were calculated using a hybrid approach combining the subjective best-worst method (BWM) and the objective criteria importance though intercriteria correlation method (CRITIC) method to enhance the accuracy and consistency of the weights. The weighted linear combination (WLC) model was used to integrate the spatial layers. For spatial analysis, Hot Spot Analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*) and Local Moran’s I methods were employed to identify clusters of high desirability and analyze spatial patterns. To examine spatial justice and inequality in the distribution of desirable open spaces relative to population, the Lorenz curve was plotted, and the Gini coefficient was calculated. Results indicated that environmental criteria, with a total weight of 0.6, and accessibility and infrastructure criteria, with a weight of 0.4, play a fundamental role in determining the quality of open spaces. Sub-criteria such as NDVI and air pollution in the environmental category and proximity to public transport stations and sports facilities in the accessibility and infrastructure category were the most influential. Regarding environmental quality, the majority of the city falls within medium and low classes, with less than one-third of the urban area possessing favorable environmental conditions, mainly concentrated in the northern, some western, and eastern parts of the city. In contrast, access to infrastructure and urban services is significantly better, with more than half of the city’s area falling within high and very high classes, reflecting the widespread distribution of transport and service infrastructure across the city. Spatial analysis using Hot Spot and Local Moran’s I indices revealed that hot spot clusters, covering approximately 21% of the city, are mainly concentrated in the northern half, while cold spot clusters, covering 27% of the city, are mostly located in the southern and central parts, indicating substantial spatial inequality. Furthermore, the Gini coefficient of 0.6035 and the Lorenz curve confirm that the distribution of desirable open spaces is highly uneven relative to the population, with around 60% of the population having access to only 10% of these spaces. The study suggests that policymakers design targeted and balanced interventions in areas with unfavorable conditions and limited open spaces to reduce spatial inequalities and improve citizens’ quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32486-w.Keywords: Urban open spaces, Physical activity, Multi-criteria assessment, Spatial justice, Spatial analysis, TehranSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography

Introduction

In recent decades, urbanization has been expanding at an unprecedented pace, a phenomenon that has not only had extensive impacts on the economic, social, and environmental structures of societies but has also significantly transformed individuals’ daily lifestyles1. The increasing population density in urban areas2, the development of industrial infrastructure3, and the growing reliance on technology4 have led to a reduction in opportunities for physical activity and an increase in sedentary behaviors in everyday life. According to United Nations projections, by 2050, approximately 68% of the world’s population will reside in urban areas, with 90% of this urban population growth occurring in Asia and Africa5. These developments, combined with the rising proportion of elderly populations and the widespread prevalence of chronic diseases across all age groups, underscore the urgent need for effective policies aimed at promoting public health6.

Physical inactivity is recognized as the fourth leading risk factor for global mortality7,8 and contributes to the onset of at least 35 chronic diseases, whereas regular physical activity has been shown to be effective in preventing and managing 26 chronic conditions9,10. Scientific evidence indicates that participation in physical activities, particularly among individuals facing physical or mental health challenges, can lead to reductions in pain, depression, and anxiety, while simultaneously improving mood, physical function, and overall quality of life11–14. Despite these benefits, only about 27.5% of adults adhere to the recommended level of physical activity, which is 150 min per week15, and this proportion drops to approximately 7% among individuals with multiple chronic conditions16. Various factors, such as lack of time, low motivation, financial constraints, preference for non-formal exercise environments, and, most importantly, insufficient equitable access to suitable open spaces, are among the main barriers to individuals’ participation in physical activity17,18.

Physical activity in open spaces refers to any bodily movement or exercise performed in outdoor environments, whether natural or urban, outside of enclosed spaces, with objectives such as promoting physical health, improving mental well-being, or leisure8. These activities not only enhance physical fitness but also play a significant role in reducing stress, improving social interactions, and enhancing overall quality of life. Common examples include walking, running, cycling, yoga, team sports such as football and volleyball, and recreational activities like picnicking or children’s play. Research findings indicate that engaging in activities in open spaces, compared to indoor environments, is associated with benefits such as a more positive perception of health, increased enjoyment, reduced depressive symptoms, and greater motivation to continue physical activity19–21. However, structural factors, such as unequal distribution and suboptimal design of open spaces in urban areas especially in underprivileged neighborhoods pose barriers to fully utilizing the potential of these spaces. In this context, assessing and identifying urban open spaces with emphasis on indicators such as safety, environmental quality, accessibility, and spatial justice represents an effective step toward promoting public health and achieving sustainable urban development.

Physical activity in urban open spaces, particularly in parks, walkways, and public green areas, has recently attracted considerable attention as an effective and evidence-based strategy for promoting citizens’ physical and mental health. Numerous studies have shown that access to high-quality and safe open spaces can significantly increase physical activity levels and, consequently, improve public health22. Findings by Cardinali, et al.23 in four European cities emphasize that the presence of green spaces near residential areas is significantly associated with increased physical activity and enhanced health, whereas inadequate greenery at more distant locations can have adverse effects. Additionally, studies such as Gao, et al.24 indicate that investments in improving the quality of urban open spaces in the United States have led to increased regular physical activity and reduced prevalence of chronic diseases. Research focusing on specific populations, such as the study by Yang, et al.25 in China, has observed that diverse physical activities, including Tai Chi, walking, and Qigong, are positively associated with older adults’ mental well-being, and suitable outdoor exercise environments serve as mediating and moderating factors in this relationship. Tao, et al.26, using multilevel analysis in suburban Shanghai, also confirmed the positive effects of recreational physical activities in open spaces, especially neighborhood green spaces, on physical health, whereas indoor environments demonstrated lower impact. Findings from Nigg, et al.27 in Germany and studies by Leese and Al-Zubaidi28 in the United Kingdom consistently highlight the importance of quality, diversity, and proximity of urban open spaces in promoting physical activity, reducing obesity, enhancing life satisfaction, and decreasing stress. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Li, et al.29 demonstrated that outdoor physical activity has significant positive effects on reducing anxiety and depression and improving mood.

Despite substantial evidence highlighting the role of urban open spaces in promoting physical activity, a significant portion of existing studies has primarily relied on individual-level data and questionnaire-based methods, paying limited attention to precise spatial analysis and the identification of urban areas suitable for outdoor physical activities. In other words, there is a clear lack of research utilizing advanced geographic technologies, such as geographic information systems (GIS) and multi-criteria decision-making approaches, to evaluate and prioritize urban open spaces based on environmental, social, and physical indicators. In response to this gap, the present study aims to identify and analyze areas suitable for outdoor physical activity by integrating advanced GIS techniques with multi-criteria decision-making models within the context of Tehran. Furthermore, previous research has not adequately addressed the equitable distribution of high-quality urban open spaces for physical activity. Tehran, as the political and economic capital of Iran, with a population exceeding nine million within the urban boundary and more than thirteen million in the metropolitan area, is one of the most densely populated and challenging megacities in the region. Over the past two decades, the prevalence of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, and obesity has been rising; this issue, combined with citizens’ sedentary lifestyles, air pollution, a shortage of standard public spaces, and inefficient urban infrastructure, has posed a serious threat to residents’ physical and mental health. Consequently, evaluating the spatial distribution of urban open space quality for physical activities and the degree of equity in this distribution relative to the population across different areas of Tehran is of critical importance, yet it has received little attention in previous studies.

The aim of this study is to develop a framework for assessing the quality of urban open spaces for physical activity based on a spatial multi-criteria approach. In the proposed framework, the spatial distribution of different quality classes of urban open spaces for physical activity is first evaluated, and then the degree of equity in this spatial distribution is quantified according to the population distribution across the city’s districts. The findings of this study can serve as a decision-support tool for urban managers and health planners and, by improving the design and development of open spaces, can enhance citizens’ participation in physical activity, thereby improving their overall quality of life.

Study area

Tehran, the capital of Iran, covers an area of approximately 730 km2 and is located between 51°8′ and 51°37′ east longitude and 35°34′ and 35°50′ north latitude (Fig. 1). The city is administratively divided into 22 municipal districts, 134 neighborhoods, and 376 local districts. With its unique climatic and geographic diversity, Tehran extends from the Alborz Mountains in the north to the southern plains, with a significant elevation difference of up to 800 m between the northern and southern parts. This variation in altitude, together with Tehran’s semi-arid climate, which features an average annual precipitation of about 230 mm and a mean annual temperature of approximately 17 degrees Celsius, has led to distinct patterns in land use distribution and the spatial arrangement of urban open spaces.

The total population of Tehran is approximately 8.68 million, residing in 4.315 million households. The average population density of the city is about 14,099 people per km2 (Table 1). The total per capita urban green space amounts to 362 m2 for the entire population. The average per capita green space in Tehran is estimated at 16.45 m2 per person, which is below the recommended standard proposed by the United Nations Environment Programme of 20 to 25 m2. However, the per capita green space varies significantly across different districts. In northern districts such as District 22, with a population of approximately 175,000, the per capita green space is very high at 58.67 m2 per person. Similarly, District 21, with a per capita green space of 36.74 m2, and District 1, with an approximate population of 493,000 and a per capita green space of 33.85 m2, are in favorable conditions. In contrast, densely populated central districts such as District 10, with over 326,000 residents and a very high population density of 39,935 people per km2, have only 2.58 m2 of green space per person. Districts 8 and 11 also exhibit the lowest access to green space, with per capita values of 4.01 and 4.96 m2, respectively, combined with high population levels. Other districts such as 7, 12, 13, and 14, which have high population densities, also have per capita green space below 10 m2. This imbalance in the distribution of urban green and open spaces negatively affects the quality of life and physical health of residents and exacerbates social and health inequalities across Tehran. Therefore, precise spatial analyses and targeted planning for the development and improvement of open spaces in underserved areas are essential to ensure equal access to opportunities for physical activity for all citizens and to enhance overall urban quality of life.

Materials and methods

Data

The primary data used in this study include remote sensing data, ground-based data, spatial databases, urban maps, and statistical information. Remote sensing data consist of spectral bands and land surface temperature products from Landsat 8 and 9 images, which were used to generate vegetation cover maps and upward long-wave radiation (ULR) (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov). The AW3D digital surface model was employed for modeling downward long-wave radiation (DLR), and downward short-wave radiation (DSR), and slope maps were derived from the same dataset (http://aw3d.jp/en). Land cover data were obtained from the ESA WorldCover product with a spatial resolution of 30 m (https://esa-worldcover.org/en/data-access), and the MCD19A2 product was used to extract aerosol optical depth (AOD) from the MODIS sensor. Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI, 0.09), upward long-wave radiation (ULR, 0.23), downward long-wave radiation (DLR, 0.30), and downward short-wave radiation (DSR, 0.25).

Ground-based data, spatial databases, urban maps, and statistical datasets included the locations of parks, public transport stations, police stations, parking lots, traffic plans and pollution sources, medical centers, sports facilities, industrial zones, and recreational and tourism centers provided by the Tehran Municipality (https://www.tehran.ir). Road networks, including highways and main roads, were obtained from the Ministry of Roads and Urban Development (https://www.mrud.ir). Water bodies were mapped using data from the National Cartographic Center of Iran (http://www.ncc.org.ir), and air pollution data were acquired from the Tehran Meteorological Organization (https://tehranmet.ir). Population shapefiles from the Statistical Center of Iran (https://amar.org.ir) were used to calculate population density, and building shapefiles from the Tehran Municipality (https://www.tehran.ir) were employed to derive building density. All datasets were prepared at a scale of 1:2000.

After data collection, spatial layers were created and verified, and maps corresponding to the study criteria were generated using various capabilities of ArcGIS throughout the processing stages.

Research methodology

This study was conducted in six main stages (Fig. 2). In the first stage, the criteria affecting the assessment of urban open space quality for physical activity were identified based on expert opinions and previous studies. In the second stage, the required data were collected, pre-processed, and organized into a GIS database. In the third stage, maps of the effective criteria were prepared using spatial analysis. The values of these criteria were standardized according to their type of influence using minimum and maximum standardization methods. In the fourth stage, the weights of the effective criteria were determined using a hybrid approach based on both objective and subjective weighting methods. In the fifth stage, a suitability map of urban open space quality for physical activity was produced using a weighted linear combination of environmental, accessibility, and infrastructure criteria. In the sixth stage, high-suitability spatial clusters across different areas of Tehran were identified using local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi* indices, and their spatial distribution across urban districts was evaluated. Finally, in the seventh stage, inequality in the distribution of suitable open space area for physical activity relative to the population in different districts of Tehran was assessed using the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient.

Identification and definition of Decision-Making criteria

In this study, based on expert opinions and a review of the literature and theoretical foundations, two sets of factors were identified as effective criteria for assessing the quality of urban open spaces for physical and recreational activities. These include environmental conditions and accessibility and infrastructure conditions.

Environmental criteria

Environmental criteria play a key role in determining the quality and attractiveness of urban open spaces for physical and recreational activities. The physical conditions of the environment directly affect individuals’ health, comfort, and motivation to use these spaces. Solar radiation with different wavelengths, along with natural factors such as land slope, vegetation cover, and presence of water bodies, shape the local microclimate. This microclimate influences parameters such as temperature, relative humidity, and heat stress, ultimately determining the suitability of the environment for physical activity.

DSR and DLR, along with ULR are important indicators for evaluating thermal conditions. High levels of these radiations can increase ambient temperature and create heat stress, which may limit physical activity, whereas lower levels contribute to cooler and more comfortable conditions for exercise and recreation. Land slope affects airflow and heat accumulation; gentler slopes provide more favorable conditions for physical activity30. Additionally, slope influences the energy required for movement steeper slopes demand more effort and make activity more challenging, while mild slopes reduce fatigue and enhance the possibility of engagement in physical activity31. Dense vegetation and water bodies play an important role in temperature regulation. Through shading and evapotranspiration, these elements reduce ambient temperature and create more favorable conditions for human presence32. Proximity to parks and green spaces is also recognized as a key motivator encouraging individuals to engage in physical activity33. The AOD, which indicates the concentration of airborne particles, together with air pollution, negatively affects air quality in open spaces and users’ health34. High building density restricts airflow and increases ambient temperature, creating unfavorable conditions that hinder outdoor physical activities35.

Accessibility and infrastructure criteria

Adequate accessibility and the presence of proper infrastructure are critical factors in attracting and facilitating physical and recreational activities in urban open spaces. Proximity to public transport stations, parking facilities, and main roads enhances access to urban open spaces by reducing physical and temporal barriers an aspect particularly important in large cities. Easy access to public transportation decreases travel time and cost, while the availability of sufficient and convenient parking near these spaces increases user comfort and motivation to visit36. The presence of police stations and healthcare centers around open spaces enhances the sense of security and confidence of users during physical activities37. Additionally, proximity to sports, recreational, and tourism facilities encourages greater use of open spaces, as these amenities complement physical activity and provide diverse recreational opportunities37. Conversely, distancing open spaces from industrial centers, which are primary sources of noise and environmental pollution, improves air quality and reduces health risks38. Traffic management schemes, such as restrictions and fines for vehicle entry in central areas, aim to reduce air pollution and control congestion39. While these measures improve environmental quality, they may also reduce accessibility by private vehicles, limiting opportunities for some citizens to use urban open spaces for physical activity.

Production of maps for effective criteria

For each of the identified criteria in the potential assessment process, spatial data layers were collected from diverse sources. These sources included high-resolution satellite imagery, urban data such as land-use maps, infrastructure, and accessibility, as well as environmental data including vegetation cover, topography, and climate. All spatial data used in this study were projected to the global coordinate system WGS84 and the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) system, zone 39 N. Using this standardized reference framework enabled precise spatial analyses and proper overlay of vector and raster data within the GIS environment, ensuring consistency and reliability throughout all stages of site evaluation. After the projection, each layer was converted into a standardized format compatible with GIS processing. This step ensured that the input data were spatially and format-wise uniform and ready for integration and use in subsequent analysis steps.

Standardization of maps for effective criteria

Given the diversity of criteria and the varying scales of spatial data, a standardization step is essential to unify the data ranges. In this study, all spatial data layers were standardized to a standard scale ranging from 0 to 1. Weighting of effective criteria

In this study, a hybrid approach combining subjective and objective weighting methods was adopted to ensure a more comprehensive determination of criteria weights. Specifically, the best-worst method (BWM) was applied as the subjective approach42,43, while the Intercriteria correlation method (CRITIC) method was used as the objective approach44,45. Weighting techniques in multi-criteria decision-making can generally be divided into two main categories: subjective methods, which rely on expert judgment (such as AHP, ANP, BWM, or Delphi), and objective methods, which are based on the statistical characteristics of data (such as Entropy, CRITIC, PCA, or DEA). A detailed comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of subjective and objective weighting methods is provided in Table S2 (Supplementary Materials). Subjective methods are particularly advantageous for incorporating qualitative factors and expert experience into the analysis. However, they are often challenged by issues of expert bias, inconsistency, and disagreement. On the other hand, objective methods are entirely data-driven and free from human bias, but they suffer from the limitation of ignoring valuable qualitative knowledge and experiential insights. This comparative understanding (summarized in Table S2) highlights the complementary nature of subjective and objective approaches, thereby justifying the adoption of a hybrid method that integrates their strengths.

Among the subjective approaches, BWM has clear advantages over AHP and ANP due to the smaller number of pairwise comparisons required, reduced cognitive burden on experts, and higher consistency of results46. Nevertheless, its main limitation is the inability to fully account for interrelationships or correlations among criteria, which may reduce the robustness of the weighting outcomes. CRITIC, in contrast, effectively addresses this limitation by considering both the contrast intensity (variation) of each criterion and the degree of conflict (correlation) between criteria, thereby assigning more balanced and objective weights than classical approaches such as entropy47.

By combining the simplicity and expert-driven accuracy of BWM with the objectivity and correlation-sensitive structure of CRITIC, the hybrid BWM–CRITIC framework offers a more robust, valid, and scientific weighting strategy. This integration reduces the weaknesses of each individual method while enhancing their strengths, resulting in final weights that are more stable, credible, and reliable. Accordingly, the hybrid approach used in this study provides a solid foundation for subsequent spatial analysis and ensures that both expert knowledge and objective data characteristics are properly integrated into the evaluation process.

BWM method

The BWM was first introduced by Rezaei43. This approach belongs to the family of multi-criteria decision-making methods and is designed to derive criterion weights with the fewest pairwise comparisons and the highest level of consistency. The core principle of this method lies in the decision-maker selecting the best and worst criteria, followed by relative comparisons of all other criteria with these two. The implementation of the BWM is formulated as a linear optimization programming model, and by solving it, the optimal weight for each criterion is obtained46. Due to its high accuracy, reduced comparison load, and improved consistency, this method has found widespread application in evaluation and decision-making studies. Preparation of the urban open space suitability map for physical activities

After identifying and collecting the criteria and determining their final weights, the weighted linear combination (WLC) method was employed to prepare the urban open space suitability map for physical activities. This method is one of the most commonly used approaches in multi-criteria evaluation and has been widely applied in various studies due to its accuracy and efficiency. This approach operates based on the principle of the weighted mean, where the analyst or decision-maker assigns a weight to each criterion according to its relative importance51. The value of each option is obtained by multiplying the relative weight of each criterion by its corresponding score and then summing the results. The output generated by the decision-making model in this study produces continuous numerical values ranging from 0 to 1, representing the suitability of each location based on the evaluation criteria. These values indicate the intensity of suitability and desirability of each spatial unit relative to others. To facilitate interpretation of the results and create a practically usable map for planning purposes, the WLC output was further processed. Using the equal interval classification method, the range of output values was divided into five distinct classes: very low (0–0.2), low (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), high (0.6–0.8), and very high (0.8–1). This classification enables the identification and differentiation of areas with varying levels of suitability and forms the basis for producing the final urban open space suitability map for physical activity.

In this study, suitability maps were generated under three scenarios: (1) based on effective environmental criteria, (2) based on effective accessibility and infrastructure criteria, and (3) based on all criteria combined. Subsequently, the status of different areas in Tehran regarding the coverage of urban open spaces suitable for physical activity was evaluated and compared.

Spatial equity assessment

The assessment of spatial equity in the distribution of urban open spaces was conducted to identify and analyze inequalities and patterns of spatial clustering. In this study, to examine equity in accessibility and suitability of open spaces for physical activity, three well-established statistical and spatial methods were employed: the Local Moran’s I spatial autocorrelation index, Hot Spot analysis using the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic, and inequality analysis through the Lorenz curve.

The Local Moran’s I index is a tool for measuring local spatial autocorrelation, capable of detecting spatial clusters with similar characteristics (such as areas with high or low suitability aggregated together). To identify local clusters and spatial autocorrelation patterns in the WLC suitability map, Getis–Ord Gi* and Local Moran’s I statistics were applied using a raster-based spatial weights structure. For both indices, a first-order queen contiguity model implemented through a 3 × 3 neighborhood kernel was used, in which each focal pixel was linked to its eight immediate surrounding neighbors. The spatial weights were defined as binary ( = 1 for valid neighbors and 0 otherwise) without row-standardization, while statistical normalization was analytically handled through global mean and variance terms. Z-scores and two-sided p-values for Gi* and Local Moran’s I were approximated using the standard normal distribution. This raster-based neighborhood configuration is well suited for gridded urban spatial analyses.

Spatial inequality analysis with the Lorenz curve

To examine equity and inequality in the distribution of desirable open spaces relative to the population, the Lorenz curve was used as a key analytical tool. This curve provides a graphical representation of the cumulative distribution of a resource, in this case urban open spaces, relative to the cumulative population. In a perfectly equitable distribution, the Lorenz curve lies exactly on the 45-degree line, representing perfect equality. Deviations of the Lorenz curve from this line indicate the degree of inequality in the distribution of resources56. The further the curve bends downward, the greater the inequality. To quantify this inequality, the Gini index was calculated, defined as the ratio of the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve to the total area under the line of equality.

Software and reproducibility

In this study, ArcGIS 10.8 and MATLAB 2021b were used for preparing and analyzing spatial data, as well as generating the final maps. While ArcGIS was employed in this study to prepare spatial data layers (including Euclidean distance, density analysis, zoning, raster calculator–based normalization, reprojection, and resampling), all these operations rely on standard geospatial procedures that are not unique to ArcGIS. Equivalent functionalities are fully available in open-source platforms such as QGIS, GRASS GIS, and GeoDa, as well as Python-based geospatial libraries (e.g., GDAL, rasterio, geopandas). Consequently, the entire workflow can be reproduced in any software environment supporting these geospatial operations. To further facilitate reproducibility and transparency, we provide all MATLAB scripts, detailed annotations, and step-by-step documentation (README file), together with the input and output datasets, openly on Zenodo [https://zenodo.org/records/17257219]. This approach ensures that researchers and practitioners can replicate, adapt, or extend our methodology without reliance on proprietary software.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that environmental criteria, with an overall weight of 0.6, play a more prominent role than accessibility and infrastructure criteria, which have a weight of 0.4, in assessing suitable open spaces for physical activity in Tehran. Among the environmental criteria, vegetation cover (NDVI), building density, and air pollution had the greatest impact. These findings align with similar studies in megacities such as Zurich57, Wuhan58 and Sheffield59 City, where environmental quality, particularly vegetation cover, was identified as a key factor in improving thermal comfort and reducing environmental stress for physical activity. This importance is attributable to the direct effect of environmental quality on thermal comfort, air pollution mitigation, and the creation of an attractive environment for citizens. In contrast, accessibility and infrastructure criteria, while having a lesser influence on the composite index, play a functional and compensatory role, particularly in areas with lower environmental quality.

To determine the importance of criteria, a combination of subjective (BWM) and objective (CRITIC) approaches was used. The BWM, based on pairwise comparisons and expert judgment, enables extraction of precise weights with minimal inconsistency. In contrast, the CRITIC method relies on data variance and correlations among criteria, operating without subjective input, and assigns weights based on the intrinsic information and discrimination power of the criteria. Combining these two approaches helps compensate for the weaknesses of each. For example, potential biases in BWM are mitigated by CRITIC’s reliance on real data, while CRITIC’s disregard for perceived importance is balanced by expert judgments in BWM. This hybrid approach has been recommended in studies such as Luo, et al.60 and Wu, et al.61 because it enhances the accuracy and validity of results. Compared to traditional methods such as AHP or Entropy, the combined approach used in this study showed greater stability and allowed for better control of uncertainties.

Spatial distribution analysis revealed that environmental quality and accessibility follow different patterns in Tehran. Environmental quality is concentrated in the northern districts and parts of the west and east, whereas central and southern districts generally fall into lower classes. This pattern is similar to findings in China62 and Ghana63, where high building density, limited green space, and air pollution reduce environmental quality in central urban cores. In contrast, accessibility and infrastructure criteria exhibited broader coverage, with more than half of the city’s area falling into high or very high classes. This contrast indicates that while the development of transport and urban services has been relatively successful in Tehran, environmental policies have not been implemented evenly across the city.

Analysis of the Gini coefficient, which was calculated as 0.6035, and the Lorenz curve revealed significant inequality in the distribution of suitable open spaces for physical activity. Approximately 60% of the population has access to only 10% of these spaces. This level of inequality is even higher compared to cities such as London or New York, where green space Gini indices are reported around 0.4. Hot Spot and Moran’s I analyses further confirmed that hot clusters are primarily located in affluent areas, while cold clusters are concentrated in underprivileged districts. These findings indicate that merely increasing the number of open spaces or improving infrastructure does not ensure spatial equity, and both the geographic distribution and quality of spaces must be considered simultaneously. While the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient were intentionally chosen for their interpretability, clear depiction of inequality, and widespread use in both economic and spatial equity studies, we acknowledge that these traditional metrics have limitations. Specifically, they may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of spatial inequity or account for spatial dependencies and patterns in resource distribution. Future research could complement these measures with newer approaches, such as the spatial Theil index or inequity decomposition methods, which are capable of addressing multidimensional and spatial aspects of inequality. Incorporating such methods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of spatial justice and strengthen the analytical framework for evaluating equity in urban contexts.

While the present study applied well-established spatial multi-criteria decision analysis (SMCDA) techniques, such as min–max normalization, WLC, and spatial autocorrelation measures (Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi*), we acknowledge that these methods primarily provide a descriptive and diagnostic perspective on spatial suitability. More advanced approaches, including machine learning–based suitability modeling, multi-objective optimization, and agent-based simulations, have recently been suggested in the literature to better capture the complex dynamics of urban systems. In our case, the main limitation to adopting machine learning models was the lack of high-quality training datasets, which are generally essential for model calibration and validation. Nevertheless, we suggest that future studies could explore hybrid approaches that integrate SMCDA with machine learning. For instance, one potential pathway would be to generate uncertainty maps of SMCDA outputs using bootstrapping or resampling methods. Highly suitable areas with consistently low uncertainty could serve as pseudo-training samples for machine learning models, thereby improving predictive performance while addressing the challenge of limited training data. This integration could enable more robust modeling of urban spatial suitability and provide stronger predictive insights for urban planning and policy-making.

This study has several key limitations. First, the use of only a single satellite image per season may not fully capture temporary variations in vegetation cover and environmental quality. Second, the selection of criteria was limited to twenty main indicators, which, while adequate, did not account for certain socio-cultural or economic factors influencing open space use. Third, Hot Spot and Moran’s I analyses rely on spatial correlation and do not necessarily reveal causal relationships. Future research could benefit from multi-temporal, high-resolution data, socio-economic indicators, and machine learning methods to model complex relationships.

The findings suggest that urban policymakers should simultaneously focus on improving environmental quality and strengthening infrastructure. Key actions include developing green spaces in central and southern districts, controlling building density, reducing air pollution, and enhancing recreational facilities. Resource allocation should be guided by spatial needs and equity rather than purely by physical development opportunities. Targeted interventions, such as creating local parks in underprivileged areas, improving public transport access to open spaces, and implementing green infrastructure in dense neighborhoods, could reduce inequalities and improve overall quality of life in Tehran.

Conclusion

Given the rapid growth and uneven expansion of Tehran, examining and analyzing the environmental quality, infrastructure of urban open spaces, and accessibility to them is essential for improving citizens’ quality of life and reducing spatial inequalities. The aim of this study was to provide a comprehensive analysis of the distribution of environmental and infrastructural quality across Tehran and to identify areas with suitable and unsuitable conditions for physical activity and urban use, thereby offering effective strategies for planning and sustainable development of open spaces. The analysis of Tehran’s urban area based on environmental criteria shows that a large portion of the city falls within medium and low environmental quality classes. Approximately 81 km2 of the city are classified as very low environmental quality, providing unfavorable conditions for physical activity. In contrast, less than one-third of the total urban area belongs to high and very high-quality classes, mainly concentrated in the northern districts and parts of the western and eastern regions.

The accessibility and infrastructure analysis reveals a different pattern. More than half of the city’s area falls into high and very high classes, indicating that the population has generally good access to public transportation and infrastructural facilities. Areas with limited access are primarily located on the city’s periphery. Spatial cluster analysis (Hot Spot) further highlights that northern Tehran, particularly districts 1, 3, and 5, forms hot clusters in terms of environmental, infrastructural, and social quality, while central and southern districts, including districts 9 to 12 and 17, form cold clusters, reflecting spatial inequality and a lack of suitable facilities in these areas. Statistical indicators such as Moran’s I and the Gini coefficient of 0.6035, along with the Lorenz curve, indicate unequal distribution of suitable open spaces for physical activity relative to the population. Approximately 60% of the city’s population has access to only 10% of high-quality open spaces. This significant gap in access to urban open spaces represents a major challenge for spatial justice and quality of life.

The results clearly demonstrate the importance of addressing both environmental quality and urban infrastructure simultaneously in the planning and development of urban open spaces. Infrastructure development alone cannot compensate for environmental deficiencies or spatial inequalities. Therefore, designing targeted policies and precise spatial interventions in cold and transitional areas to improve environmental and infrastructural quality can significantly enhance living conditions and spatial balance in Tehran. Finally, this study emphasizes the necessity of comprehensive and multidimensional approaches in urban planning to improve spatial equity and overall quality of life throughout the city. Policymakers and urban planners should pay special attention to areas with low quality and limited access and implement targeted spatial interventions, such as increasing green spaces and improving public transport infrastructure, to reduce inequalities. Integrating environmental and infrastructural factors is a key strategy for sustainable development of urban open spaces and achieving a high quality of urban life for all citizens.
