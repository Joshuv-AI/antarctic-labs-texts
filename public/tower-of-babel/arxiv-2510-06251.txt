Does Physics Knowledge Emerge in Frontier Models?
Ieva Bagdonaviciute, Vibhav Vineet

Abstract
Leading Vision-Language Models (VLMs) show strong results in visual perception and general reasoning, but their ability to understand and predict physical dynamics remains unclear. We benchmark six frontier VLMs on three physical simulation datasets—CLEVRER, Physion, and Physion++—where the evaluation tasks test whether a model can predict outcomes or hypothesize about alternative situations. To probe deeper, we design diagnostic subtests that isolate perception (objects, colors, occluders) from physics reasoning (motion prediction, spatial relations). Intuitively, stronger diagnostic performance should support higher evaluation accuracy. Yet our analysis reveals weak correlations: models that excel at perception or physics reasoning do not consistently perform better on predictive or counterfactual evaluation. This counterintuitive gap exposes a central limitation of current VLMs: perceptual and physics skills remain fragmented and fail to combine into causal understanding, underscoring the need for architectures that bind perception and reasoning more tightly.



1
Introduction

Despite rapid progress in multimodal and embodied AI, it remains uncertain whether current models genuinely understand physical dynamics. These dynamics involve properties such as mass, friction, gravity, collisions, and occlusion. Human cognition relies on a tight coupling between perception and reasoning: the more accurately we perceive these properties, the better we can predict future events and reason about alternative outcomes. In real-world settings, this ability underpins safe decision-making, planning, and causal reasoning—making physical understanding a core requirement for intelligent systems.
To ground this challenge, consider the following illustrative examples: given a short video of a cone rolling toward a wall with a hole, a model should answer whether the cone will pass through to reach its goal (Figure

, left). In another case, if the wall is solid instead, the correct reasoning is that the cone will be blocked (Figure
1
, right). Of course, correctly answering both cases also requires the model to reason about underlying physical properties such as the cone’s mass and elasticity.

Most existing benchmarks collapse perception and physics understanding into a single accuracy number, making it difficult to identify whether errors stem from failures to see what is happening or failures to understand the underlying dynamics. In contrast, we introduce diagnostic subtests that disentangle the two:
perception
(recognizing objects, colors, and latent elements) and
physics understanding
(motion prediction and spatial relationships). We apply this framework to six frontier VLMs across CLEVRER

Counterintuitively, our results show that the correlation is weak: models that recognize objects or anticipate motion more reliably do not consistently succeed on predictive or counterfactual evaluation tasks. This disconnect underscores a central limitation of current architectures and motivates approaches that bind perception and physics into integrated causal understanding.

Methodology

We evaluate six VLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-O1, VideoLLaVA-7B, VideoLLaVANext-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, and InternVL-4B) on two benchmark families:
Physion
/
Physion++

[
3
,
1
]
and
CLEVRER

[
2
]
.

Physion/Physion++.

These datasets involve predicting whether a moving object (e.g., a red cone) will reach its goal (e.g., a yellow mat). We follow two conditions:
with property (WP)
: The video is truncated, and the model predicts the outcome (e.g., a cone rolls toward a wall with a hole), and
fully observed (WOP)
: The full video is shown, and the model verifies the observed outcome (e.g., the cone–wall setup is shown until the end).

To probe beyond this evaluation, we augment Physion/Physion++ with diagnostic questions targeting
perception
(detecting objects, goals, latent occluders) and
physical reasoning
(anticipating motion, understanding spatial relationships).

CLEVRER.

This dataset consists of synthetic videos of colliding objects, paired with four categories of questions:
descriptive
,
explanatory
,
predictive
, and
counterfactual
. We use counterfactual questions as the main evaluation task, while the other categories serve as diagnostic subtests of perception (descriptive, explanatory) and physical reasoning (predictive).

Framework.

We structure our methodology as a two-step process: original benchmark evaluation followed by our diagnostic analysis (Figure
2
). The benchmark evaluation tests whether the model can answer the original benchmark questions (e.g., counterfactuals in CLEVRER or goal-reaching in Physion). The diagnostic analysis decomposes the model’s performance into two key components: perception (recognizing objects, colors, occluders) and physical understanding (motion prediction, causal/spatial relationships).

Implementation.

All experiments were implemented using our custom pipeline within the open-source EUREKA benchmark framework

,

]
, which supports modular evaluation and analysis. The dataset-specific construction of diagnostic questions and answers is detailed in Appendix

4
Discussion

Our findings reinforce long-standing critiques that current multimodal models act more like sophisticated pattern matchers than causal reasoners
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Rather than integrating perception and physics into a coherent model of the world, VLMs appear to treat these skills as independent shortcuts: they can recognize surface features or answer some probes correctly, but these abilities do not combine into reliable predictive or counterfactual inference. This behavior suggests that success on evaluation tasks often reflects statistical guesswork rather than grounded physical understanding. Models may arrive at the right answer without fully perceiving the scene or reasoning through the dynamics, a form of hallucination that underscores the gap between pattern recognition and causal inference.

5
Conclusion

We presented a diagnostic evaluation of six frontier VLMs on CLEVRER and Physion/Physion++. Despite reliably recognizing objects and surface-level features, models struggle on predictive and counterfactual evaluation, with accuracy close to chance. Unlike human cognition—where perceptual and physics competencies reinforce downstream prediction and counterfactual inference—these models exhibit only weak correlation between perception/physics diagnostic performance and overall evaluation outcomes. This gap underscores a key limitation in current multimodal systems: strengths in perception and basic physics do not reliably translate into success on evaluation tasks or genuine physical understanding. Bridging this divide will require architectures that bind visual recognition to structured, causal models of physical dynamics.

Appendix A
Experimental Setup

A.0.1
Physion and Physion++ Setup

All evaluation questions for the Physion and Physion++ dataset videos follow the original binary true/false format. We adopt this format consistently across all sections of our analysis for these two datasets.

Evaluation:
We used the original benchmark task predicting whether the target object (often a red object) will reach the goal object (often a yellow object).

Perception:
We categorized perception analysis into three components:
target
,
goal
, and
latent
object perception. Targets refer to main objects in motion, goals are the intended destinations of those targets, and latent objects are elements that influence the trajectory or outcome of the interaction. Based on these categories, we constructed templated prompts such as:

•

“Answer true or false: there is a red object in the video.” (Target perception)

•

“Answer true or false: there is a yellow object in the video.” (Goal perception)

•

“Answer true or false: the blocker wall has a hole in the video.” (Latent object perception)

The prompts used in perception test cases varied across instances and often included descriptors related to shape, color, or spatial positioning. To ensure linguistic diversity and reduce prompt bias, we employed multiple phrasings (e.g., “There is a red object” vs. “There is no red object”).

Physical Understanding:
Beyond perception, we designed prompts to assess physical reasoning through motion and spatial relationship understanding. We designed prompts such as:

•

“Answer true or false: the red cone will move towards the left side of the frame if the video continues.” (Motion understanding)

•

“Answer true or false: the hole in the blocker wall prevented the red cone from reaching the yellow mat.” (Spatial relationship understanding)

For motion analysis, we incorporated both directional terms (e.g., left, right, up, down) and relational expressions (e.g., “towards,” “away from”). For spatial reasoning, we focused on causal language such as “prevented,” “caused,” and “affected.”

Physion++ paper refers to a video cut for prediction as
”with property”
and full video as
”fully observed”
(see Sections 3 and 4 of
[
1
]
). We will use the same terms in our setup shown in Figure
6
.

A.0.2
CLEVRER Setup

The CLEVRER dataset comprises four categories of questions:
descriptive
,
explanatory
,
counterfactual
, and
predictive
(see Section 3 of
[
2
]
). For our experiments, we utilized their validation set and selected only those videos that included at least one question from each of the four categories. In cases where multiple questions were available within a category for a given video, one question was randomly selected. As a result, each video in our subset is associated with exactly one question per category. We aligned CLEVRER’s question categories with our own framework based on their functional alignment: each category reflects a distinct component of our pipeline–evaluation, perception, or physical understanding–allowing for a structured and interpretable analysis.

•

Evaluation:
We used CLEVRER’s “counterfactual” category as our evaluation metric.

•

Perception:
We used CLEVRER’s “descriptive” and “explanatory” categories to assess perception.

•

Physical Understanding:
We used CLEVRER’s ”predictive” category to assess physical understanding.

A visual flowchart of this setup is shown in Figure
7
. Note that within CLEVRER, the descriptive category is open-ended and evaluated using LLM-based grading (GPT-O1), while explanatory, predictive, and counterfactual categories are four-way multiple choice.
