Fairness Regularization in Federated Learning
Zahra Kharaghani, Ali Dadras, Tommy Löfstedt

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a vital paradigm in modern machine learning that enables collaborative training across decentralized data sources without exchanging raw data. This approach not only addresses privacy concerns but also allows access to overall substantially larger and potentially more diverse datasets, without the need for centralized storage or hardware resources. However, heterogeneity in client data may cause certain clients to have disproportionate impacts on the global model, leading to disparities in the clients’ performances. Fairness, therefore, becomes a crucial concern in FL and can be addressed in various ways. However, the effectiveness of existing fairness-aware methods, particularly in heterogeneous data settings, remains unclear, and the relationships between different approaches are not well understood. In this work, we focus on performance equitable fairness , which aims to minimize differences in performance across clients. We restrict our study to fairness-aware methods that explicitly regularize client losses, evaluating both existing and newly proposed approaches. We identify and theoretically explain connections between the investigated fairness methods, and empirically show that FairGrad (approximate) and FairGrad ∗ (exact) (two variants of a gradient variance regularization method introduced here for performance equitable fairness) improve both fairness and overall model performance in heterogeneous data settings.

Introduction

In federated learning (FL), it is assumed that individual clients have insufficient data to effectively train models on their own local datasets. By collaborating within the FL framework, these clients jointly train a global model, thereby enhancing the overall performance without the need to share their raw data.
However, due to the heterogeneity of client data, the global model may perform inconsistently across clients. This disparity poses a significant challenge, particularly for clients with substantially different local data distributions or smaller sample sizes. Such clients may experience poor performance when using the global model.
Consequently, such clients might be discouraged from participating in FL since the appearance is that the system is unfair to them and that their contributions are not being valued equally.

To address this issue, it is crucial to ensure that the training process in FL is fair. For clients to have an incentive to participate, they must benefit from the global model without being disadvantaged by disparities in their local data distribution. A fair training process keeps clients engaged and encourages them to continue participating.
In the context of FL, fairness is typically categorized into three main types, collaborative fairness, equitable fairness, and algorithmic fairness. These three notions of fairness address different aspects of how a global model is trained and evaluated in FL. Collaborative fairness and equitable fairness primarily focus on how the global model makes predictions across clients, aiming to ensure fair contribution and uniform performance. Meanwhile, algorithmic fairness targets bias in the data itself and aims to train a global model that does not discriminate against protected groups, regardless of which client the data originates from.

An equitable fair FL procedure minimizes disparities in client performances, leading to more equitable outcomes. In this context, fairness has been conceptualized in three distinct forms:
good-intent equitable fairness
(
Mohri et al. 2019
)
, Pareto-optimal equitable fairness
(
Cui et al. 2021
)
, and performance equitable fairness
(
Li et al. 2019
)
.
Among these research lines, performance equitable fairness has attracted much attention. This approach aims to ensure equal performance across all clients without sacrificing the overall model performance. The local loss functions are usually modified in an attempt to achieve performance equitable fairness.
It is, however, still unclear how different proposed loss functions support the goal of performance equitable fairness, making it challenging to determine which approaches are most effective in practical settings. Moreover, most existing methods lack a solid theoretical foundation, making it difficult to justify their use or apply them across diverse FL scenarios.

In this work, we analyzed and evaluated three methods for performance equitable fairness in FL. Two of these methods incorporate variance-based regularization, while the third is q-fair federated learning
(
Li et al. 2019
, q-FFL;)
, which, as we show later, also relates to variance regularization for a specific choice of the parameter

q
. The first method regularizes the variance of client losses and includes two variants: one using the average loss from the previous iteration, and the other using the average loss from the current iteration, where the latter requires two rounds of communication with the server. The second method regularizes the variance in client gradients, also with two variants based on whether the average gradient is computed from the previous or current iterations (the latter again incurring an extra communication round). The third method, q-FFL, reweights the client contributions based on their local losses, giving a higher weight to clients with higher losses to improve their performance faster and by that reduce disparities across clients. Additionally, we also include FedAvg
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
for the comparisons. Formal definitions of the variance-regularized
loss functions are presented in
Section

3
.

In this work, we focused on performance equitable fairness methods that explicitly regularize the client loss. We investigated how the performance differed of these different types of variance regularization methods’ across multiple experiments.
Specifically, we examined loss variance regularization
(
Ling et al. 2024
)
, gradient variance regularization (introduced here for the first time in the context of performance equitable fairness), q-FFL
(
Li et al. 2019
)
, and federated averaging
(
McMahan et al. 2017
, FedAvg;)
.
For completeness and to compare with a more recent method, we also included AAggFF
(
Hahn et al. 2024
)
as a baseline method in the experiments. AAggFF also aims for performance equitable fairness and uses the same local loss as FedAvg, but differs in that the server applies a fairness-aware aggregation strategy instead of regularization.

The analysis reveals that when the clients’ data distributions are homogeneous, their performances tend to align. We provide theoretical insights into the relationships between the two methods with regularized loss functions, q-FFL, and FedAvg in
Section

4
.
In more heterogeneous or complex data scenarios, the theoretical results indicate that variance regularization methods may converge to different stationary points than FedAvg. Empirical results further demonstrate that variance regularization methods generally outperform the baselines (FedAvg and AAggFF) in both fairness and overall performance, with the proposed methods (FairGrad and FairGrad
∗
) showing particularly strong results.

Related Work

Fairness in FL can be defined in different ways. These include: collaborative fairness
(
Song et al. 2019
;
Sim et al. 2020
;
Xu and Lyu 2020
;
Yu et al. 2020
;
Rashid et al. 2025
)
, which suggests that rewards and collaboration should be proportional to the quality and quantity of data provided by clients, encouraging those with high-quality data to participate more in FL, algorithmic fairness
(
Cui et al. 2021
;
Du et al. 2021
;
Ezzeldin et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Su et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2025
)
, which ensures that the trained model is not biased against any client’s dataset, and equitable fairness, where the aim is to train a global model that minimizes performance disparities across clients. This category includes three key fairness definitions: good-intent fairness
Mohri et al. 2019
, performance equitable fairness
Li et al. 2019
, and Pareto-optimal fairness
(
Cui et al. 2021
)
.
In this work, we focused on methods for performance equitable fairness.

Equitable fairness was introduced by
Mohri et al. 2019
, who presented Agnostic Federated Learning (AFL). They showed that AFL naturally leads to a notion of fairness they refer to as good-intent fairness. This method aims to find the best global model by focusing on the worst-case client performance, thereby improving outcomes for the most disadvantaged clients. However, significant performance disparities can still occur under this approach
(
Nguyen et al. 2024
)
, and AFL has been found to be most effective when applied to smaller sets of clients
(
Li et al. 2019
)
.

In contrast to AFL, the q-FFL approach by
Li et al. 2019
offers greater flexibility and can be fine-tuned to achieve the desired level of fairness. Performance equitable fairness in q-FFL is introduced through a modification of the loss function that is supposed to encourage equitable performance across clients in FL, while maintaining or even improving performance compared to FedAvg. The q-FFL utilizes a method called q-FedAvg, which focuses on fairness by dynamically re-weighting devices based on their performance. Devices with lower performances are given higher weights to ensure a more uniform performance distribution across clients.
The optimization problem for q-FFL adjusts the fairness level through a parameter
q
, where a higher
q
value means more emphasis on helping devices with higher losses (those performing worse), promoting a more balanced accuracy and fairness across the federation.
AFL and FedAvg can be considered special cases of q-FFL, where AFL corresponds to setting
q
to a sufficiently large value, and FedAvg corresponds to setting

q
to zero.
AAggFF
(
Hahn et al. 2024
)
builds on this line of work by unifying existing fairness-aware aggregation strategies within an online convex optimization framework and enhancing them through sequential decision-making at the server, with specialized variants for both cross-device and cross-silo FL settings.
Another direction has explored fairness in FL through bi-level optimization. FedFB
(
Zeng et al. 2021
)
adopts a bi-level approach in which the outer loop adaptively selects fair batch representations of the training data. An entropy-based aggregation method
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
also formulates fairness in FL as a bi-level optimization problem, integrating model and gradient alignment, with analytic aggregation probabilities computed in the inner loop and adaptive alignment updates in the outer loop.

Some works highlight that data heterogeneity can lead to different levels of irreducible errors among clients.
To address this, Pareto-optimal fairness
(
Cui et al. 2021
)
has been proposed, aiming to balance trade-offs between clients. This concept aims to select the model that minimizes the worst-case loss (the max loss) while ensuring the best overall performance across all clients. In this context, further improving one client’s performance would inevitably worsen another client’s performance.
Building on this idea, FedMGDA+
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
introduced a multi-objective optimization algorithm for FL that converges to Pareto-stationary solutions without sacrificing any client’s performance. In a related direction,
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
introduced Federated Multi-Objective Learning (FMOL), which generalizes classical multi-objective optimization (MOO) to the federated setting.
(
Pan et al. 2023
)
further enhanced fairness in FL by addressing unfair update directions and step sizes, using a fair-guided multi-objective approach and a communication-efficient step size adjustment method.
AdaFed
(
Hamidi and Yang 2024
)
is a method that uses Pareto-stationary solutions to find a common descent direction for all selected clients, while prioritizing greater reductions for those with larger losses by adaptively adjusting the update direction based on local gradients and loss values. Similarly, OTA-FFL
(
Hamidi et al. 2025
)
formulates FL as a multi-objective minimization problem to improve fairness.
However, the theoretical understanding of the connections, if any, between these methods remains unclear, and their performance under varying levels of data heterogeneity has not been thoroughly explored.

Variance-regularized methods have been explored in FL for different purposes. For instance,
Guo et al. 2023
proposed the FEDIIR method, which uses gradient variance regularization to enhance out-of-distribution generalization for non-participating clients. In contrast, our FairGrad and FairGrad
∗
methods leverage gradient variance regularization specifically to improve performance equitable fairness among participating clients in heterogeneous data settings, supported by both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation.

Statement of the Problem

The standard FL procedure,
aims to minimize a global average expected risk,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{F}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\big[\ell(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i})\big],

(1)

where
n
denotes the number of participants (clients) involved in the federation,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the parameters of the machine learning model,
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a sample data point from the
i
-th client’s data distribution,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, and
\ell(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i})
is the loss function, indicating the loss of the model with parameters
\mathbf{x}
on data sample
\xi_{i}
. As the true data distribution,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, is unknown, clients rely on an empirical set of samples,
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{\xi_{i}^{1},\ldots,\xi_{i}^{N_{i}}\}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}^{N_{i}}
, for
N_{i}
samples, and corresponding average empirical risk, as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\ell(\mathbf{x},\xi_{i}^{j}).

(2)

Solutions to
Equation

2
may result in significant performance discrepancies among participating clients, particularly when the number of samples,
N_{i}
, is small or the client data distributions are highly heterogeneous
(
Zhao et al. 2018
)
. To quantify these discrepancies, we outline a fairness criterion for FL, drawing on and slightly modifying the definitions introduced by
Li et al. 2019
and
Nguyen et al. 2024
.

Definition 3.1

(Performance equitable fairness in FL)
.

For a set of models, with weights
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{x}:|F(\mathbf{x})-\min_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon\}
, where
\varepsilon\geq 0
is a tolerance for performance degradation, a model
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
is said to achieve better
\varepsilon
-performance equitable fairness than model
\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
if the variance of its performance across clients is lower. That is, if
\text{var}((p_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))_{i=1}^{n})<\text{var}((p_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))_{i=1}^{n})
, where

p_{i}(\mathbf{x})
denotes a performance measure (
e.g.
, accuracy) for model
\mathbf{x}
on the
i
-th client’s local data and
\text{var}((p_{i}(\mathbf{x}))_{i=1}^{n})
denotes the sample variance across all clients.

When a machine learning model is trained on data from multiple clients, ensuring fairness as defined in
Definition

3.1
means that each client has an equal opportunity to benefit from the model. The goal is to achieve equitable performance across all clients, regardless of differences in the data they contribute with. The tolerance parameter
\varepsilon

ensures that only models with average loss
F(\mathbf{x})

close to the best model (within

\varepsilon
) are considered. This avoids favoring models that achieve low variance simply because they perform poorly on all clients. Instead, the comparison is restricted to high-performing models, among which the fairest distribution of performance is preferred.

3.1
Evaluated Methods

In this section, we present the specific objective functions of the methods selected for evaluation in this work to assess performance equitable fairness in FL. These methods aim to reduce disparities in client performance by addressing differences in loss or gradient across clients.
In what follows, we describe the methods that were evaluated.

The methods described here introduce tunable parameters that allow control over the trade-off between average performance and fairness. When these parameters are set to zero, the objectives reduce to the standard finite-sum formulation described in Equation (
2
). Otherwise, they promote a more uniform distribution of performance across clients, with the intention of improving outcomes for underperforming clients, potentially at the cost of reduced performance for others. By adjusting these tunable parameters, the balance between performance and fairness can be fine-tuned to adapt to the specific task requirements.

Ling et al. 2024
proposed to penalize models with high variance in the client performances, which can be implemented using a variance-regularized loss, as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=F(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x})\big)^{2},

(3)

where
\lambda
controls the degree of fairness and
F(\mathbf{x})
denotes the average loss, as defined in Equation (
2
). We introduce two methods to solve this objective, detailed in
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

3
.
We propose an approach aimed at promoting performance equity across participating clients in FL by minimizing gradient disparities.
This can be formulated as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=F(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\big\|^{2},

(4)

where
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the average gradient,
\gamma
controls the degree of fairness, and
F(\mathbf{x})
is defined in Equation (
2
), as before. We also introduce two algorithms to solve
Equation

4
, that are detailed in
Algorithm

2
and
Algorithm

4
.

Li et al. 2019
proposed q-FFL to improve fairness in FL by prioritizing clients with higher losses through a reweighting mechanism that gives higher weight to under-performing clients. This is formulated as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ H_{q}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_{i}}{q+1}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{q+1},

(5)

where
r_{i}
is the proportion of total data samples held by client
i
, and

q
is a parameter that controls the degree of fairness.

McMahan et al. 2017
introduced the FedAvg algorithm, which aims to minimize an average of local loss functions across clients. The objective, given in
Equation

2
, is considered here with uniform weights
1/n
for all
i
. FedAvg serves as one of the baseline algorithms in our experiments.

Equations (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
) define the objective functions of the fairness methods considered in this work, however, they are not directly solvable in a federated context because they are not separable with respect to each client’s objective. To address this, we propose both exact and approximate algorithms for the first two methods in
Section

5
. For q-FFL, we adopted the original optimization algorithm proposed by
Li et al. 2019
.

4
Analysis of Stationary Points

Homogeneous Data
:
In a homogeneous data environment, the datasets of all
n
clients are drawn from the same underlying probability distribution,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{j}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
As a result, each client’s local loss,
f_{i}
, is an unbiased estimator of the expected global loss,
\mathbb{E}[F]
, and each client’s gradient,
\nabla f_{i}
, is an unbiased estimator of the expected global gradient,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla F]
.

The following theorems establish that, under the assumption of data homogeneity, the stationary points of the regularized objective functions
L_{\lambda}
,
J_{\gamma}
, and also
H_{q}
, when
q=1
, coincide in expectation with those of the objective function,
F
. Specifically, if
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is a stationary point of
F
, then it is also a stationary point of the expected functions
\mathbb{E}[L_{\lambda}]
,
\mathbb{E}[J_{\gamma}]
, and
\mathbb{E}[H_{q}]
for
q=1
, and vice versa. This implies that, in homogeneous settings, introducing these regularizations does not alter the location of the stationary points, thereby preserving the optimal solutions of the original objective,
F
. Consequently, the regularizations are unnecessary in such settings, as they do not change the outcome.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that the functions
f_{i}
’s are smooth and that there exists
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
,
such that

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star})=0.

Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\right]=0,\quad\text{and similarly,}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star})\right]=0.

Discussion.

Theorem

4.1
shows that, in the homogeneous setup, the solution obtained by FedAvg is already fair in expectation, as the expected loss on a new data point is the same across all clients. Furthermore, clients benefit from the collaborative nature of FL by training on a shared model aggregated over a larger dataset.
Consequently, regularization techniques such as
L_{\lambda}
and
J_{\gamma}
that are designed to reduce variance of losses and gradients, does not offer significant advantages over the objective function
F
in this setting.

The following theorem shows that the expectation of function
H_{q}
, when
q=1
can be upper bounded by the expectation of function
L_{\lambda}
, for a particular value of
\lambda
. Specifically, if
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is a stationary point of
F
, then it is also a stationary point of
H_{q}
in expectation, when
q=1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
q=1
and
r_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[H_{1}(\mathbf{x})\big]=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\big]^{2}+\frac{n}{(n-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x})\big)^{2}\right].

When
\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})]\leq 1,\forall i
, we further have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[H_{1}(\mathbf{x})\big]\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\big]+\frac{n}{(n-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x})\big)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\big],

with
\lambda=\frac{n}{n-1}
. In particular, with this
\lambda
,
q=1
, and when
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla H_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big]=0.

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

4.2
are provided in
Section

A.1
.

Discussion.

Theorem

4.2
demonstrates that
H_{1}
,
i.e.
, q-FFL with
q=1
, can be expressed in a similar form as
L_{\lambda}
for a particular
\lambda
. Further, at the stationary points of
F
, the two functions
H_{q}
and
L_{\lambda}
(for
q=1
and
\lambda=\frac{n}{n-1}
) are close, as
F
attains its minimum value at these points, leading to a small difference between them.

Heterogeneous Data
:
In the case of heterogeneous data, the datasets of different clients are drawn from different distributions,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{D}_{i}\neq\mathcal{D}_{j}
for some
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, resulting in variability in local objectives and gradients. In this case, the variance terms in the regularized loss functions,
L_{\lambda}
,
J_{\gamma}
, and
H_{q}
for
q=1
, are important because they penalize this variability. The regularization encourages the optimization process to find solutions that balance performance across diverse clients, promoting models that generalize better.

While the expectation-based arguments in the homogeneous case rely on the unbiasedness of local losses, and local gradients with respect to the global objective,
such assumptions do not hold universally in the heterogeneous setting, where client data distributions differ. However, under certain conditions, specifically, when client losses or gradients are aligned at the optimum point, similar results can still be obtained.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and there exists

\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}H_{q}(\mathbf{x})
, such that

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star})=\nabla H_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\star})=0.

If either
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
or
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\nabla f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
holds for all
i,j
, then

\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\nabla H_{q}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=0.

Similarly, if either
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
or
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\nabla f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
for all
i,j
, then
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=0
. If the analogous condition holds at
\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star}
, and
\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\star}
, then
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\gamma}^{\star})=0
, and
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{q}^{\star})=0
.

The proof of
Theorem

4.3
is provided in
Section

A.2
.
The following theorem investigates conditions under which the stationary points of the objective function

F
are not preserved in the regularized objectives
L_{\lambda}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that the
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and that there exists

\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
, such that
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=0
. Furthermore, suppose there exist at least two indices
1\leq i,j\leq n
such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\neq f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
and
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\neq\nabla f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
.
Let
\mathcal{G}=\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the set of gradient vectors at
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
.
Then
\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\neq 0
if at least one of the following conditions holds:

1.

Subset of clients with distinct gradient directions, and the rest sharing the same loss value.

This means that there exist
m
linearly independent gradients in

\mathcal{G}
, where
1<m\leq n-1
, and the remaining

n-m
gradients correspond to loss functions that are equal at
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
. Specifically, if
I_{\text{ind}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
indexes the
m
linearly independent gradients, i.e.,

\mathcal{G}_{\text{ind}}=\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\mid i\in I_{\text{ind}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, and the remaining indices are given by
I_{\text{rem}}=\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I_{\text{ind}}
, then the corresponding loss values satisfy

f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=f_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
for all
j,k\in I_{\text{rem}}
.

2.

Subset of clients with non-zero gradients, all but one sharing the same loss value.

That is, there exist
m
non-zero gradient vectors where
1\leq m\leq n
, and the loss functions corresponding to these gradient vectors are all equal except for one.

Discussion.

The conditions in
Theorem

4.4
describe situations where fairness regularization changes the optimization landscape, so that the FedAvg stationary point

\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is no longer preserved. In practice, these cases arise naturally in heterogeneous FL. The first condition may occur when some clients have substantially different data distributions, resulting in gradient directions that are genuinely distinct, while the remaining clients have similar data and therefore report identical loss values at
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
. The second condition can happen when most clients experience similar performance levels, but one client’s unique data distribution leads to a noticeably different loss. In both scenarios, the disagreement in either gradient directions or loss values introduces an imbalance that fairness regularization attempts to correct, thereby shifting the stationary point away from the FedAvg solution.

The next theorem examines the converse situation: conditions under which a stationary point of the regularized objective

L_{\lambda}
is not a stationary point of the original objective
F
. In other words, it characterizes scenarios where introducing regularization alters the optimization landscape in such a way that the solutions of
L_{\lambda}
differ from those of
F
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and there exists
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, such that
\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=0
. Furthermore, suppose there exist at least two indices
1\leq i,j\leq n
such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
and
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq\nabla f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{\lambda}=\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the set of gradient vectors at
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}
.
Then
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq 0
if at least one of the following conditions holds:

1.

Subset of clients with distinct gradient directions, others with identical loss values.

That is, there exist
m
linearly independent vectors where
1<m\leq n-1
, and the remaining
n-m
gradient vectors correspond to loss functions that are equal at
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}
. Specifically, if
I_{\text{ind}}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
indexes the
m
linearly independent gradients, i.e.,

\mathcal{G}^{\lambda}_{\text{ind}}=\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\mid i\in I_{\text{ind}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\lambda}
, and the remaining indices are given by
I_{\text{rem}}=\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I_{\text{ind}}
, then the corresponding loss values satisfy

f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=f_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
for all
j,k\in I_{\text{rem}}
.

2.

Subset of clients with non-zero gradients, all but one sharing the same loss value.

That is, there exist

m
non-zero gradient vectors where
1\leq m\leq n
, and the loss functions corresponding to these gradient vectors are all equal except for one.

Discussion.

This theorem describes the converse of Theorem
4.4
: situations where the regularized objective
L_{\lambda}
has a stationary point
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}
that is not a stationary point of
F
. In practice, these cases occur when the regularization changes the optimization landscape so that the resulting solution no longer satisfies the optimality condition for
F
.
The first condition may occur when, under regularization, a subset of clients pushes the model in genuinely different update directions, while the remaining clients behave similarly in terms of loss values.
The second condition may arise when most clients achieve the same performance level, but one client deviates due to a distinct data distribution, creating an imbalance that is preserved by the regularized objective but would not correspond to a stationary point of
F
.

The following theorem demonstrates that even when all clients have identical loss values at the stationary point of
F
, introducing small, zero-mean perturbations to the client losses can cause the stationary point of the regularized objective function
L_{\lambda}
to differ from that of the
F
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that the
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and that there exists
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=f_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
for all indices
i,j
.
Additionally assume that,
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\neq 0
for at least one index
i
. Then, there exist non-zero values
o_{i}
’s such that
\bar{o}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}o_{i}=0
and the function
\hat{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
defined as

\hat{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}):=\hat{F}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\hat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\hat{F}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{2}
,
where
\hat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=f_{i}(\mathbf{x})+o_{i}
, and
\hat{F}(\mathbf{x})=F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
,
has a different stationary point than
F
.

We showed that, under certain conditions, the regularized functions can converge to different stationary points of
F
. The following theorem demonstrates that the stationary points of
L_{\lambda}
can reduce the variance of local loss functions, leading to more balanced performance across clients.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume that the
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and convex. If there exists
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)^{2}
.

Discussion.

Even if each
f_{i}
is convex, the regularized objective
L_{\lambda}
is not necessarily convex. However,
Theorem

4.7
shows that when
F
is convex, the variance of local losses at the minimizer of

L_{\lambda}
is less than that at the minimizer of
F
. This means that, under convexity,
L_{\lambda}
promotes a more balanced distribution of performance across clients, aligning with our fairness definition in
Definition

3.1
.

The proofs of
Theorem

4.4
,
Theorem

4.5
,
Theorem

4.6
, and
Theorem

4.7
are provided in
Section

A.2
.

5
Algorithms

In this work, we propose and evaluate several methods for achieving performance equitable fairness in FL. Two of these methods incorporate variance-based regularization, focusing on reducing disparities in client losses and gradients, respectively. The third is the q-FFL method, which reweights client contributions based on their local losses to promote uniform performance across clients. We further show that q-FFL admits a regularized formulation in the special case where
q=1
.
To minimise the objective function in
Equation

3
, which aims to minimize both the average and the variance of client loss functions, we evaluated two algorithms: an exact version (
Algorithm

1
), and an approximate version (
Algorithm

3
). Similarly, for the objective in
Equation

4
, which minimizes the average loss and reduces the variance of the gradients of client loss functions, we introduce two algorithms: an exact version (
Algorithm

2
) and an approximate version (
Algorithm

4
).

In the loss-alignment approximate algorithm (referred to as FairLoss; see
Algorithm

1
), clients perform local updates using information from the previous iteration, thereby reducing communication with the server.
In the loss-alignment exact algorithm (referred to as FairLoss
∗
; see
Algorithm

3
), clients perform local updates using information from the current iteration. Detailed descriptions of both the approximate and exact variants are provided in
Section

B.1
.

These two algorithms represent a trade-off between communication efficiency and the accuracy of the information used during updates. In the following theorem, we demonstrate that the convergence of the loss-alignment approximate algorithm implies the convergence of the loss-alignment exact algorithm. The proof of
Theorem

5.1
is provided in
Section

B.2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
and
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
be approximations of the function
L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
and its gradient at iteration
t
, as used in
Algorithm

1
. Assume that SGD is used for the updates. Then we have that

L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big)^{2}\leq 0.

Moreover, if we assume that each local loss function
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is
L
-smooth, and
\eta
denotes the learning rate, then

\|\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|\leq\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|+\lambda L\eta\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\||F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})|.

Discussion.

Algorithm

1
uses approximations of the objective function,
L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
, and its gradient,
\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
, denoted by
\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
and
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
, respectively.
Theorem

5.1
establishes a connection between the approximate and true functions, ensuring that the surrogate function provides a reliable estimate of the original objective. Leveraging
Theorem

5.1
, we can further show that if
\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
converges, then
L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
also converges,
i.e.

\mathbf{x}^{t}

and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|=0
, then
\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}
is a stationary point of the function
L_{\lambda}
.

As mentioned earlier, to minimize the objective function in
Equation

4
, we propose both an approximate and an exact algorithm. In the gradient-alignment approximate algorithm (referred to as FairGrad; see
Algorithm

2
), clients perform local updates using information from the previous iteration, thereby reducing communication with the server.
In the gradient-alignment exact algorithm (referred to as FairGrad
∗

Algorithm

), clients perform local updates using information from the current iteration. While the exact version provides more accurate updated information, it requires more communication between the clients and the server. Detailed descriptions of both the approximate and exact variants are provided in

.

In the theorem below, we establish that the convergence of the gradient-alignment approximate algorithm implies the convergence of the gradient-alignment exact algorithm. The proof of
Theorem

5.2
is provided in
Section

B.2
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\bar{a}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

Conclusion

In this paper, we focused exclusively on fairness-aware FL algorithms that explicitly reduce client disparities through variance-based regularization of client losses or gradients.
We evaluated methods in this category, including a gradient variance regularization approach, introduced here for the first time in the context of performance equitable fairness, and implemented in two variants:
an approximate version that uses the average loss or gradient computed at the previous iteration, and an exact version that uses the average from the current iteration across clients.
The theoretical analysis indicates that, under certain conditions, regularized objectives may have different stationary points from the unregularized ones. Empirical results confirm that, in heterogeneous settings with more clients and complex datasets, regularized methods improve both fairness and overall model performance, with the gradient alignment variants consistently delivering strong results.
In contrast, in homogeneous settings, the theoretical results suggest minimal to no differences between these methods, which aligns with the empirical observations.

While the exact algorithms require additional server communication and gradient alignment involves costly second-order derivative computations, these limitations suggest promising directions for future work, such as developing efficient Hessian approximation techniques to enable faster and more scalable implementations.

Overall, our findings highlight the potential of variance-based regularization, especially the proposed gradient alignment, as a principled and effective approach to improve fairness in FL.

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\big(C-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\big(f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}).

Now using the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=0
, and

\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})=-\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\big(C-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\big(f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\underbrace{\big(f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-C\big)}_{\neq 0}\underbrace{\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})}_{\neq 0}\neq 0.

∎

We now prove Theorem
4.5
. We recall the theorem:

Theorem
4.5
.

Assume that
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and there exists
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})

\mathcal{G}^{\lambda}=\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\}_{i=1}^{n}

\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq 0

1.

Subset of clients with distinct gradient directions, others with identical loss values.

That is, there exist
m
linearly independent vectors where

n-m

Subset of clients with non-zero gradients, all but one sharing the same loss value.

That is, there exist

m
non-zero gradient vectors where

Proof.

Based on the assumption, for some
1\leq i,j\leq n

f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
and
\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq 0

3

\displaystyle\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\big(1+\lambda(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}))\big)}_{=:\beta_{i}}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=0,

(19)

where

This is a contradiction with the fact that

This implies that the gradients of the local loss functions at
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}

\nabla f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}),\ldots,\nabla f_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})
are linearly dependent. Let
m=n-1

\nabla f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}),\ldots,\nabla f_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})

\nabla f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}),\ldots,\nabla f_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}),\nabla f_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})

A.2

. It follows that

Then

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{n}}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bigg(1-\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{n}}\bigg)\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}).

Since we have assumed that

are linearly independent and at least for one

\beta_{i}\neq\beta_{n}

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\neq 0
.
Here we show that

for all

Section

A.2
it would imply that

for all

. However, this is not the case we are considering.

Let there be
1<m<n-1
linearly independent gradient vectors. Without loss of generality, we assume that

are linearly dependent. By assumption

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg(1-\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta}\bigg)\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}).

Since we have assumed that

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})+\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(-\frac{\beta_{m}}{\beta}\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})+\nabla f_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\right)

Since

Theorem
4.7
.

Assume that the
f_{i}
’s are differentiable and convex. If there exists
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, then
L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\leq L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
.
We have

{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})={F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)^{2}\leq F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)^{2}.

(28)

This shows that

{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\leq F(\mathbf{x}^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)^{2}.

(29)

If

, then we have

\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}^{\star})\big)^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\big)^{2}.

if

, which is a contradiction with the fact that

, and

∎

Appendix B
Analysis of the Algorithms

B.1
Algorithm Descriptions

In this work, we propose and evaluate several methods for achieving performance equitable fairness in FL. Two of these methods incorporate variance-based regularization, focusing on reducing disparities in client losses and gradients, respectively. The third is the q-FFL method, which reweights client contributions based on their local losses to promote uniform performance across clients.
To minimize the objective function in
Equation

3
, which aims to minimize both the average and the variance of client loss functions, we evaluated two algorithms: an exact version (
Algorithm

3
), and an approximate version (
Algorithm

1
). Similarly, for the objective in
Equation

4
, which minimizes the average loss and reduces the variance of the gradients of client loss functions, we introduce two algorithms: an exact version (
Algorithm

2
) and an approximate version (
Algorithm

4
).

In the loss-alignment approximate algorithm (referred to as FairLoss; see
Algorithm

1
), at iteration
t
, the server sends the model parameters
\mathbf{x}^{t}
, along with the average of client loss functions,
a^{t}
, and the average client gradient,
\mathbf{g}^{t}
, from the previous iteration (iteration
t-1
) to all clients. Each client computes its local loss,
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
, and constructs a regularized approximate objective with a penalty term, such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{2}(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t})^{2}
using its own data. The model parameters are updated using one SGD step,
\mathbf{x}^{t+1}_{i}=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}
, where
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}
is defined based on the previous iteration’s information as
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}=\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\lambda(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a_{t})(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t})
. This method reduces communication frequency between server and clients because it eliminates the need for clients to send their current loss values back to the server and wait for the aggregated average before proceeding with updates. By relying on the previous iteration’s aggregated information, clients can perform local updates independently, thereby decreasing the number of communication rounds required per iteration.

In the loss-alignment exact algorithm (referred to as FairLoss
∗
; see
Algorithm

3
), each client first receives the current model parameters
\mathbf{x}^{t}
from the server, then computes its local loss and gradient, and sends these values back to the server. The server aggregates the received losses and gradients to compute the current average loss,
a^{t}
, and average gradient,
\mathbf{g}^{t}
, and then broadcasts this information back to the clients. Each client uses the received average loss to construct the regularized exact objective
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{2}(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t})^{2}
.
This objective integrates the global loss information from the current iteration with the client’s local data.
Subsequently, the clients update their model parameters by performing a SGD step using
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}=\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\lambda(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a_{t})(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t})
. While this algorithm provides the best available information for the updates, it requires more communication between clients and the server.

B.2
Theoretical Analysis of the Algorithms

We now prove Theorem
5.1
. We recall the theorem:

Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

From

(30)

and

\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t}):=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big).

(31)

Now using Equations (
30

3

\displaystyle{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{a}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

\displaystyle=F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)^{2}-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big((f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t}))^{2}-(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t})^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{2}-2f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+F(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{2}-f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{2}-(a^{t})^{2}+2f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})a^{t}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{2}-(a^{t})^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{2}+{\lambda}a^{t}F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\frac{\lambda}{2}(a^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big)^{2}\leq 0.

Now we show that

\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})=-\lambda\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big)\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big).

Using the gradients of Equations (
3
) and (
31
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\big)\big(\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(-f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\;\;+f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\mathbf{g}^{t}+a^{t}\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-a^{t}\mathbf{g}^{t}\Big)

\displaystyle=\lambda a^{t}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big)+\lambda F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big(\mathbf{g}^{t}-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big)\big(a^{t}-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big)\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big).

(32)

Using
Equation

32
, we get that
\|\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|\leq\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|+\lambda\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\||F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})|.

If each
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is
L
-smooth, then

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla L_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|+\lambda L\|\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\|\big|F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|+\lambda L\eta\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big\|\big|F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big|

We obtain the last equation from the SGD step,
\mathbf{x}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{t-1}-\eta\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
5.2
. We recall the theorem:

Theorem
5.2
.

Let
\bar{a}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
and
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
be approximations of the function
J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})
and its gradient at iteration
t
, as used in
Algorithm

2
. Then we have that

J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{a}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\leq-\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big\|^{2}\leq 0.

Moreover, if we assume that each local loss function,
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, is
L
-smooth, and
\eta
denotes the learning rate, then

\big\|\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|\leq\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|+\gamma L^{2}\eta\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big\|.

Proof.

From
Algorithm

2
, each client computes
f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\|^{2}
, where
\mathbf{g}^{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})}=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})
. We define

\bar{a}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t}):=F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big\|^{2},

(33)

Now using
Equation

33
, and the fact that for vectors
a,b,c
, we have
\|a-c\|^{2}-\|a-b\|^{2}=2a^{\top}(b-c)+\|c\|^{2}-\|b\|^{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle{J}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\bar{a}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})

\displaystyle=F(\mathbf{x}^{t})+\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-\big\|\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{g}^{t}\|^{2}+2\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{T}\big(\mathbf{g}^{t}-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\mathbf{g}^{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{T}\big(\mathbf{g}^{t}-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\mathbf{g}^{t}\|^{2}+{\gamma}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{T}\mathbf{g}^{t}-\gamma\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\mathbf{g}^{t}\|^{2}+{\gamma}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})^{T}\mathbf{g}^{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{g}^{t}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\big\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big\|^{2}\leq 0.

(34)

Using
Equation

34
, we get that
\|\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|\leq\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|+\gamma\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\|\|\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{t})\|.

If each
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is
L
-smooth, then

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|\leq\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|+\gamma L^{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\|\leq\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t})\big\|+\gamma L^{2}\eta\big\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})\big\|.

We obtain the last equation from the SGD step,
\mathbf{x}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{t-1}-\eta\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}^{t-1})
.
∎

Appendix C
Experiment Setup

Datasets

We evaluated the investigated methods on four standard image datasets, which were MNIST
[
LeCun et al. 1998
]
, CIFAR-10
[
Krizhevsky 2009
]
, CIFAR-100
[
Krizhevsky 2009
]
, and Tiny ImageNet
[
Le and Yang 2015
]
.

For MNIST, we selected a subset of fewer than
1000
images and distributed them across
10
clients to simulate data scarcity. A multinomial regression model was used.

For CIFAR-10, we used the full dataset distributed across
50
clients, training a CNN with two convolutional layers.

For CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, we used
50\%
of each dataset, distributed across
100
clients, with MobileNetV2 as the model architecture.

In all settings, client datasets were generated using a Dirichlet distribution with parameters
\alpha=0.05
,
0.1
, and
0.5
, where smaller
\alpha
values indicate more extreme heterogeneity (arising from both label and quantity skew). Each client maintained its own training, validation, and test datasets.
For each
\alpha
, we ran experiments with
5
different random initialization seeds. Results were averaged across runs at each epoch to ensure robustness, and we report the average test accuracy together with the variance of test accuracies across clients. Standard errors, shown in parentheses, were computed over the different initializations (see
Table

1
,
Table

2
,
Table

3
, and
Table

4
).

All experiments were run on NVIDIA GPUs: A100 40GB for CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, and T4 16GB for MNIST and CIFAR-10.

Hyperparameter search:

We performed hyperparameter tuning for all methods using a validation set, exploring a range of learning rates, batch sizes, regularization coefficients (
\lambda
,
\gamma
, and
q
), and the number of global epochs.
The best hyperparameter configuration for each method was selected based on validation performance and used consistently in all reported experiments.
Specifically, we aimed to balance accuracy and stability by selecting the configuration that maximized the criterion

\overline{\text{acc}}-t\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\text{var}}}{n}},

where
t=1.96
indicating a
95\%
confidence interval around the mean, with
\overline{\text{acc}}
denoting the mean, over seeds, of the average validation accuracy across clients for each seed,
\overline{\text{var}}
denoting the mean, over seeds, of the sample variance of validation accuracies across clients for each seed, and
n
is the number of independent experiments (
i.e.
, seeds). This selection strategy aligns with our theoretical fairness definition in
Definition

3.1
by restricting consideration to high-performing configurations and, among these, choosing those with the most equitable performance across clients (
i.e.
, lowest variance).
