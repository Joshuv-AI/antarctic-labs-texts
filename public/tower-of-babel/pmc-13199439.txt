Hyperparameter optimization of YOLO using differential evolution, multi-fidelity optimization, and Bayesian optimization
Authors: Muhammad Uzair Gill, Parvathy Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObject detection in aerial imagery faces significant challenges from small, randomly oriented, and crowded targets across large frames, where default hyperparameter settings consistently underperform. This paper presents a systematic methodology for hyperparameter optimization of YOLO (You Only Look Once) through a novel integration of Differential Evolution, Multi-fidelity Optimization, and Bayesian Optimization (DE-MFO-BO). Our approach optimizes four critical hyperparameters; learning rate, batch size, momentum, and weight decay for both Adam and SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent) optimizers. The methodology employs a two-stage strategy, Differential Evolution performs rapid exploration using low-fidelity training (5 epochs) to identify promising hyperparameter regions, followed by Bayesian Optimization with high-fidelity training (up to 800 epochs) for precise refinement. A composite fitness function combining precision, recall, and mean Average Precision (IoU 50–95) (mAP(50–95)) guides the optimization process. We validate this framework on VisDrone2019 and our indigenous Realm dataset using YOLO-11n model. For VisDrone2019, Adam optimizer achieves 18.42% recall improvement and 13.5% mAP(50–95) enhancement, while SGD shows 5.07% precision increase. On the Realm dataset, Adam optimizer demonstrates remarkable gains with 20.96% mAP(50–95) improvement, 10.22% precision increase, and 7.17% recall enhancement, whereas SGD achieves 2.78% precision improvement. These substantial performance improvements, achieved without architectural modifications, establish the effectiveness of our systematic hyperparameter optimization approach for aerial object detection applications.Keywords: YOLO-11, Differential Evolution, Multi-fidelity optimization, Bayesian optimization, VisDrone2019, Hyperparameter optimizationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The advent and evolution of You Only Look Once (YOLO) architectures have greatly enhanced real-time object detection capabilities particularly in the domain of aerial imagery using Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs). YOLO training can be divided into two different ways.

While one can find many research works focus on the architecture change of YOLO, the theoretical aspect of hyperparameter optimization, especially for aerial object detection, remains sparse in the literature. This study targets precisely this underexplored area: the hyperparameter optimization of YOLO using the YOLOv11 architecture, with a particular emphasis on aerial object detection. All experiments are conducted on the YOLOv11n model.

YOLO hyperparameters are configurable settings that control various aspects of model training and behavior. They influence the performance, speed and accuracy of the object detection task. Unlike trainable weights, hyperparameters are fixed, predefined values that do not learn during the training process; instead, the training process depends on them. They are therefore critical, as they directly affect how the model learns, how it performs, and how it generalizes to new data.

Existing research has neglected many aspects that are crucial for making effective in drone-based aerial object detection. Multiple studies1,2 propose architectural or performance improvements to YOLO models for aerial scenarios, but they do not provide a thorough theoretical analysis of how adjusting hyperparameters affects detection outcomes. Recent work primarily focuses on YOLOv8-related modifications, with limited research on YOLOv9, YOLOv10 and YOLOv11 variants, and there is still a lack of systematic optimization strategies for model hyperparameters.

The literature review therefore reveals a significant opportunity to address the challenges associated with complex aerial object detection using Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) by employing systematic hyperparameter optimization frameworks, rather than solely incorporating increasingly complex architectural changes. Such frameworks could potentially be universal, meaning they may be applicable across different YOLO versions with minimal adaptation.

Recent advances in metaheuristic optimization have introduced nature-inspired algorithms such as the Draco lizard optimizer3, Bighorn sheep optimization algorithm4 and Cape lynx optimizer5, which show promising performance in various optimization domains. However, their application to deep learning hyperparameter optimization—particularly for object detection in aerial imagery—remains largely unexplored. In contrast, Differential Evolution (DE)6 and Bayesian Optimization (BO)7 have established theoretical foundations and proven track records in hyperparameter tuning for neural networks, making them suitable choices for the systematic optimization framework proposed in this work.

The existing body of research also lacks a detailed explanation of the importance of critical hyperparameters such as learning rate, momentum, batch size, optimizer type, image size and weight decay, as well as their interactions with each other. This gap forms the foundation and motivation of the present study.

While Differential Evolution and Bayesian Optimization are established techniques, our novel contribution lies in their systematic integration with multi-fidelity optimization specifically tailored for YOLO hyperparameter tuning in aerial object detection contexts. Current works and limitations

The current state of research in hyperparameter tuning for deep learning is meagre and sometimes unconvincing, with majority of the work rather concentrating on architectural changes than the examination into different effects of various hyperparameters. More advanced YOLO-based models for aerial detection, are continuously proposed like SRE-YOLOv88 and EAF-YOLOv89, are proposed continuously10–15, and so forth. However, such approaches typically use hand-picked hyperparameter settings and provide rarely any theoretical justification or systematic tuning.

Overall, the literature review reveals that research primarily addresses architectural modifications rather than hyperparameter optimization. Several studies16,17 introduce attention mechanisms, feature pyramid modifications18–20 and lightweight architectures21–24 as part of their architectural enhancements, but they do not provide a theoretical analysis of how these changes interact with hyperparameters. In other words, changes in one module can alter the optimal hyperparameter settings for other modules, yet this interaction remains unexplored. This tendency highlights a crucial research gap in adapting hyperparameters specifically for aerial detection scenarios.

Recent studies that employ YOLOv8 for aerial object detection typically use batch sizes of 4–16 and train for 100–200 epochs8,14,25. Learning-rate configurations vary significantly across works. Some researchers employ adaptive learning-rate schedules to improve detection metrics for aerial object detection, but the theoretical justification for these choices is missing26,27. For example, the YOLOv8-MPEB study14 reports a 34% reduction in parameters and a 32% decrease in model size, yet it does not provide a theoretical explanation of why its hyperparameterization is superior.

Most existing works adopt empirical hyperparameter selection, or simply change hyperparameter values without theoretical evidence, resembling trial-and-error approaches. The majority of comparative analyses of YOLO variants revolve around architectural changes rather than the effect of hyperparameter optimization28–32. This severely limits the transferability of findings across different aerial detection scenarios.

Comparative studies across YOLO variants do show performance differences among them, but these are usually attributed solely to architectural changes rather than to hyperparameter optimization. YOLOv8, for example, demonstrates superior performance over its predecessors, achieving increases in mean average precision (mAP) of 5.3–9.2%. The SRE-YOLOv8 model outperforms standard YOLOv8 by 9.2%8, and EAF-YOLOv8 achieves mAP50 scores of 34.3% and 49.7% on UAVDT and VisDrone2019, respectively9. However, the role of hyperparameter tuning in achieving these gains is unclear.

For the latest versions—YOLOv9, YOLOv10 and YOLOv11—only limited work has been conducted in the context of aerial object detection23,33–35. Most studies still focus on comparing YOLOv8 with YOLOv5 and YOLOv736,37, with relatively few exploring newer versions24,33,34. This represents a significant gap in understanding hyperparameter optimization for the latest YOLO variants.

In most of these studies, performance is evaluated using standard protocols and metrics, employing datasets such as UAVDT, VisDrone2019 and others16,19,31,38. The assessment mainly concentrates on metrics such as mAP, precision, recall and inference speed. However, very few works systematically disentangle what different hyperparameter configurations imply for these metrics in an aerial context.

Aerial imagery presents unique challenges for object detection, especially for small objects that are represented by only a few pixels10,11,39. While improvements in small-object detection have been reported, most of these improvements result from architectural modifications to YOLO. The relationship between small-object detection performance and hyperparameter choices lacks systematic investigation. The multi-scale nature of aerial imagery clearly demands careful and thorough hyperparameter optimization, yet the theoretical framework required for such optimization is missing from the literature9,19,22.

From this analysis, several critical limitations emerge:

Lack of theoretical framework: No comprehensive theoretical analysis exists in the reviewed literature that explains the impact of hyperparameter optimization on aerial object detection.

The approach proposed in this paper based on Differential Evolution, Multi-Fidelity Optimization and Bayesian Optimization aims to address these gaps and provide reusable guidance for hyperparameter tuning across different YOLO versions and aerial datasets.

Key hyperparameter and its use in YOLO

Some of the important hyperparameters which directly affect the performance and are critical are discussed in this section. Learning Rate (`lr0`) determines how fast the model will update its weights during training. With too high a learning rate, training may be unstable or the optimizer overshoots optimal solutions, while with too low a learning rate convergence is slow or gets stuck in local minima. Batch Size is the number of images that will be processed in each iteration before updating the weights of the model. Higher batches give more stable gradients but are memory intensive, lower-batch sizes provide noisier training data, but usually better generalization. Epochs is the number of complete passes through the training dataset. More epochs result in better learning but at the same time run a risk of overfitting if model starts memorizing instead of generalizing. The image Size is the input resolution for the training images. High resolutions necessarily collect more information, are computationally more costly and memory consuming. Standard sizes are 416 × 416, 512 × 512, or 640 × 640 pixels. The momentum accelerates the convergence of training by smoothing gradient updates. Higher values can speed up training and smoother convergence but the optimal parameters might overshoot. Weight Decay is a regularization technique that penalizes large weights to prevent overfitting. High values reduce overfitting but may limit model complexity and accuracy. Low values mean the opposite—less regularization, which may eventually lead to overfitting. The anchor boxes are predefined bounding box shapes used to detect objects of varying shapes and sizes. If the anchors are poorly set, detection rate will certainly be spilled off slightly or even completely. With the anchors optimized, we can get high accuracy and detection of different object sized. The Intersection-over-Union (IoU) threshold for IoU which is applied in both training and inference stages. A higher threshold lowers the number of false positives but might also overlook some detections. On the other hand, a lower threshold brings about increased sensitivity: however, it produces more false positives. The confidence threshold is minimum confidence score needed to consider a detection valid. As the numerical value of confidence threshold is increased, this results in more precise detection with fewer false positives. The lower scores yield more detections but might also have more false positives. The augmentation parameters control data augmentation (e.g., rotation, brightness, cropping) during training to improve generalization. These can help the model to handle diverse conditions and hence are able to reduce overfitting.

Embedding in YOLO training

The hyperparameters of YOLO are usually set in a YAML configuration file which defines the entire training process. The parameters set during training initialization are optimizer settings (learning rate, momentum, weight decay), data loading pipelines: (batch size, augmentation probabilities), loss function weights, such that you can balance different components of the total loss and training routine (lr decay, epochs).

The training loop consumes these parameters to iterate over batches, computing losses and updating the model weights. Many modern YOLO implementations also incorporate hyperparameter evolution or tuning capabilities in the software architecture, allowing one to exhaustively probe different parameter combinations to identify those that work best for particular datasets.

Hyperparameters can have a significant impact on the final performance in many cases and hyperparameter tuning is very important part of the process — sometimes even a small change can degrade or improve end model quality from mediocrity to state-of-the-art object detector.

Methodology

This paper provides the implementation of Differential Evolution (DE) optimization, multi-fidelity optimization (MFO) and Bayesian optimization (BO). The primary goal is to identify effective hyperparameters—including learning rate, batch size, momentum, weight decay, and optimizer selection—that maximize model performance on a predefined dataset.

To achieve this objective, we employ a two-stage approach: Differential Evolution (DE) for broad exploration, followed by Bayesian Optimization (BO) for refinement. The objective is to train a YOLO model multiple times with varying hyperparameter configurations to converge on the optimal setup. Whereas, the multi-fidelity optimization decides the epochs of both DE and BO.

This methodology is tailored for machine learning research, emphasizing reproducibility and comprehensive evaluation. Key features include multi-fidelity training (allowing for quick evaluations with fewer epochs and thorough evaluations with more epochs), an early stopping criterion to conserve resources, and detailed logging and visualization of the optimization process.

DE-MFO-BO working methodology

We have utilized YOLO-11 with YOLO11n.pt (nano) model for a three-stage optimization method for hyperparameters optimization. Differential Evolution (DE) for a broader exploration but low-fidelity followed by Bayesian optimization (BO) which utilizes high-fidelity for hyperparameters refinement. The hyperparameters which are utilized are learning rate, batch size, momentum and weight decay for both Adam and SGD optimizers. The YOLO’s standard data.yaml with 70% train, 20% validation and 10% test distribution of the dataset. Two datasets were employed: the standard VisDrone2019 benchmark and the Realm dataset (our indigenous dataset) for the said purpose. Training employs the Ultralytics YOLO-11 nano pretrained weights to accelerate convergence and reduce computational cost40.

DE initializes the population by sampling each hyperparameter with the space provided to that particular hyperparameter (Table 3). New candidate solutions (“trials”) are generated in the new generation by mutation and recombination. The script implements the classical DE strategy41. where P is average precision, R is average recall, and mAP is mAP(50–95). This average is actually the negative harmonic mean of the three metrics. And the harmonic mean ensures that all three metrics must be high in order to get a good score (if P or R, or mAP for that matter, are low, the harmonic mean is dragged significantly down). The 3 in the numerator means that the range between the harmonic mean and the original values is of the same order.

Multi-fidelity optimization (MFO) defines the number of epochs for both DE and Bayesian optimization (BO). Low fidelity epochs are set to 5 and the maximum high-fidelity epochs are 800 with patience of 100 which means, the training will stop if there is no improvement in results for 100 consecutive epochs. DE uses low-fidelity epochs for training. It quickly estimates, record all evaluations and stops early if the best fitness is not improved by >1e-4 over 5 consecutive generations. The best DE solution is then validated just once at high-fidelity epochs and the results are logged.

BO uses the best DE result and refines around it using Gaussian process surrogate with the same search space but utilized high-fidelity epochs. It runs for maximum 15 generations with complete training and patience. After every training the surrogate updates and proposes the next iteration by balancing exploration and exploitation. BO returns the final optimized hyperparameters and the complete history is logged.

This hybrid DE-MFO-BO technique utilized the strategy “train short for many, train long for the best”. This strategy reduces the computational time drastically by exploring a wider space and proposing a solution that is to be used as a starting point for expensive limited complete runs. Consequently, the optimized hyperparameters are identified.

This visualization reveals not only when convergence occurs but also which hyperparameter values the algorithm identifies as optimal for the VisDrone2019 aerial detection task.

Hyperparameter evolution and performance metrics across DE generations for VisDrone2019 with Adam optimizer.

Figure 4 reveals critical optimization dynamics: The fitness improvement is most pronounced between generation 0 and 2 (Δfitness ≈ 0.04), after which marginal improvements of < 0.001 per generation indicate convergence. Correspondingly, precision increases from ≈ 0.41 to ≈ 0.44, recall from ≈ 0.28 to ≈ 0.31, and mAP from ≈ 0.17 to ≈ 0.19 during this rapid improvement phase. The hyperparameter trajectories demonstrate coordinated convergence: learning rate settles in the 0.002–0.003 range (moderate values suitable for Adam’s adaptive mechanism), batch size clusters at 12–14 (providing stable gradients for small aerial objects), momentum converges near 0.93 (balancing gradient smoothing with responsiveness), and weight decay stabilizes around 1–2 × 10^−5 (mild regularization preventing overfitting to background patterns). The synchronized stabilization of all eight dimensions by generation 3 confirms that DE has successfully identified a coherent hyperparameter configuration optimized for aerial imagery challenges.

Beyond performance metrics, computational efficiency is a critical consideration for practical deployment. Figure 5 analyses the training time characteristics across DE generations, revealing how hyperparameter convergence affects computational cost and predictability.

Inference time of DE optimization for VisDrone2019 dataset and Adam optimizer.

The temporal analysis in Fig. 5 provides three insights: (1) Generation 0 shows the widest time distribution (580–720 s), reflecting diverse hyperparameter exploration—small batch sizes (8) require more iterations per epoch while large batch sizes (16) reduce iteration count but increase per-iteration computation, (2) As convergence occurs (generations 2–7), distributions narrow to 620–660 s windows, indicating that the population has settled on similar batch sizes and learning configurations, and (3) The mean inference time remains relatively stable (≈ 640 s for 5-epoch training), demonstrating that low-fidelity evaluation provides consistent computational budgets. Bayesian optimization (BO)

Following DE exploration, the Bayesian Optimization phase initiates high-fidelity refinement starting from the best DE solution. Unlike DE’s population-based stochastic search, BO employs a Gaussian Process surrogate model to intelligently predict promising hyperparameter regions and sequentially evaluate them. Figure 6 tracks BO’s refinement trajectory, illustrating how surrogate-guided search efficiently converges to superior configurations.

Bayesian optimization refinement trajectory for VisDrone2019 with Adam optimizer.

The BO phase is run for a maximum of 800 epochs thus is computationally expensive but provides the final hyperparameters which provide significantly better results. The fitness function is the same. Figure 6 reveals BO’s refinement strategy: (1) The BO phase starts from the best DE result and then explores nearby vicinity. (2) The surrogate model then proposes slight modifications—Ind 1–3 explore higher learning rates (0.003–0.004, lighter face colors) and varied weight decay (edge color changes), (3) The optimal solution at Ind 4 (lr = 0.00428, bs = 16, momentum = 0.931, wd = 1.64 × 10^−5) improves fitness by 6.7% through two key adjustments: increasing learning rate by 88% for faster convergence on aerial object features, and increasing batch size to 16 for more stable gradients when detecting small objects, (4) Subsequent explorations (Ind 5–14) test more extreme configurations (very high learning rates ≈ 0.009, very low weight decay), which degrade performance, confirming the optimality of Ind 4. This targeted refinement demonstrates BO’s efficiency: achieving a 6.7% improvement over DE requires only 14 high-fidelity evaluations.

To visualize the multi-dimensional trade-offs BO navigates, Fig. 7 presents star plots for each trial, simultaneously displaying all hyperparameters and metrics. This representation enables identification of successful hyperparameter patterns and unsuccessful extremes.

Comparative analysis with alternative hyperparameter optimization methods

To rigorously validate the effectiveness of our proposed DE-MFO-BO method, we conducted a comprehensive comparative study against standalone Bayesian Optimization (BO), a well-established and widely adopted hyperparameter optimization technique in deep learning applications. This section presents a fair, controlled comparison using the REALM dataset, with both methods receiving identical computational budgets to ensure scientific validity.

Fair comparison methodology

A critical challenge when evaluating multi-fidelity methods is ensuring computational fairness. We adopted the equal high-fidelity evaluations criterion, where both methods receive exactly 20 high-fidelity evaluations representing the expensive computational resource that must be budgeted fairly. High-fidelity evaluations use 100 training epochs requiring approximately 200 min (3.3 h) each, while low-fidelity evaluations use 5 epochs requiring only 10 min. This 20:1 cost ratio means simply counting total evaluations without distinguishing fidelity levels produces unfair comparisons.

Our comparison protocol allocates resources as follows. Standalone Bayesian Optimization receives 20 high-fidelity evaluations: 10 for random initialization and 10 for Gaussian Process-based optimization using the Expected Improvement acquisition function. This represents a strong implementation of pure Bayesian Optimization without any multi-fidelity components or exploratory preprocessing. DE-MFO-BO receives the same 20 high-fidelity evaluations but strategically allocated across two optimization phases. The first phase employs approximately 44 low-fidelity evaluations for Differential Evolution exploration, organized as a population of 4 individuals evolved over 5 generations with adaptive mutation factor F ranging from 0.9 to 0.4 and crossover rate CR of 0.9. The second phase allocates 1 high-fidelity evaluation to validate the best DE solution and 19 high-fidelity evaluations for Bayesian Optimization refinement starting from this validated solution. The 44 low-fidelity evaluations represent additional computational overhead beyond the guaranteed high-fidelity budget that must be justified by performance improvements.

The key distinction between methods lies in initialization strategy. Standalone BO begins with 10 randomly distributed points that may miss promising regions of the hyperparameter space. DE-MFO-BO uses 44 cheap evaluations (220 total training epochs at 5 epochs each) to systematically explore the landscape through population-based evolution before committing any expensive high-fidelity budget. This exploration costs only 2.2 high-fidelity equivalent units (220 ÷ 100 = 2.2) but provides substantial guidance for the subsequent 20 high-fidelity evaluations, allowing them to focus on refinement in pre-identified promising regions rather than global exploration from random starting points.

Experimental setup and configuration

The experimental evaluation employs the REALM dataset for real-world environment object detection with YOLO11n architecture. Both methods optimize over an identical hyperparameter search space: learning rate in the range [1 × 10−4, 1 × 10−2], batch size as integers in8,16, momentum in [0.85, 0.95], weight decay in [1 × 10−5, 1 × 10−3], and optimizer type selected from {SGD, Adam}. This five-dimensional mixed continuous-discrete space provides sufficient complexity to meaningfully differentiate optimization performance. Image resolution uses standard 640 × 640 pixels.

Analysis and discussion

The superior performance of DE-MFO-BO validates the theoretical advantages of strategic multi-fidelity optimization. The fundamental difference lies in how methods initialize their search and allocate expensive computational resources. Standalone Bayesian Optimization begins with 10 randomly sampled initialization points. Some random samples may land in poor regions, requiring the method to expend precious high-fidelity evaluations discovering these areas are suboptimal. The subsequent 10 GP-based optimization iterations attempt to balance exploration and exploitation, but effectiveness depends critically on initial sample quality. If random initialization misses globally optimal regions, the limited budget of 10 refinement iterations may be insufficient to discover them.

DE-MFO-BO employs a fundamentally different strategy. The 44 low-fidelity evaluations systematically explore the hyperparameter landscape through population-based Differential Evolution before committing any high-fidelity budget. During 5 generations with population size 4, the DE algorithm evaluates diverse configurations at low cost, maintaining population diversity through mutation and crossover operations, handling multi-modal landscapes without local optima entrapment, and operating without gradient information making it suitable for discrete choices like optimizer selection. The adaptive mutation factor decreases from 0.9 to 0.4 across generations, balancing aggressive exploration early with focused exploitation later.

The critical insight enabling multi-fidelity optimization is rank-preservation across fidelity levels. A hyperparameter configuration performing well with 5 training epochs typically also performs well with 100 epochs, even though absolute performance values differ substantially. Poor configurations remain poor and excellent configurations remain excellent regardless of training duration. This property allows low-fidelity evaluations to identify approximate locations of optimal regions without expensive full-fidelity assessment of every candidate. The 44 evaluations at 5 epochs each effectively filter the hyperparameter space, discarding clearly poor regions and concentrating the population around promising areas.

When transitioning to high-fidelity, DE-MFO-BO possesses substantial knowledge that Standalone-BO lacks. The best solution found during DE exploration is validated with 1 high-fidelity evaluation, confirming that low-fidelity ranking correctly identified a genuinely good region. The remaining 19 high-fidelity evaluations are allocated to Bayesian Optimization starting from this validated solution. This warm-start initialization provides multiple advantages. The GP surrogate model begins learning about a region known to contain high-quality solutions rather than attempting to model both good and bad regions simultaneously. The Expected Improvement acquisition function can immediately balance local exploitation around the warm-start point with targeted exploration of nearby regions suggested by uncertainty estimates. The 19-iteration budget can be devoted entirely to refinement rather than wasted discovering that large portions of space are suboptimal.

This complementary combination leverages the respective strengths of Differential Evolution and Bayesian Optimization. DE excels at global exploration through population diversity, handles multi-modality effectively, and works with discrete choices without requiring differentiability or surrogate models. BO excels at local exploitation through probabilistic modelling, predicts performance in unexplored regions, and guides sequential sampling toward expected improvement. The multi-fidelity framework efficiently allocates these mechanisms: cheap DE for global exploration followed by expensive BO for local refinement, rather than attempting both global and local search at expensive high-fidelity throughout.

The practical significance of these improvements extends beyond numerical metrics. In object detection literature, mAP improvements of 2–4% typically require architectural modifications such as novel attention mechanisms, enhanced training procedures such as advanced augmentation strategies, additional training data, or increased model capacity. The proposed method achieves comparable improvements through optimization alone without modifying architecture, data, or model size. For production object detection systems, 3.8% mAP improvement translates to fewer false positives reducing unnecessary alerts or rejections, fewer false negatives improving detection recall for critical objects, and better localization accuracy across varying IoU thresholds. In autonomous vehicles, this could mean earlier pedestrian detection. In manufacturing quality control, fewer defects reaching customers. In medical imaging, improved abnormality detection rates. The 11% computational overhead (approximately 7 h in a 64-hour optimization run) is modest compared to the engineering effort required for alternative approaches to achieving similar performance gains.

This rigorous comparative analysis establishes that DE-MFO-BO significantly outperforms Standalone Bayesian Optimization under fair comparison conditions ensuring both methods receive identical high-fidelity evaluation budgets. With 20 high-fidelity evaluations allocated to each method, DE-MFO-BO achieves 2.71% average fitness improvement and 3.82% average mAP improvement with consistent gains across precision and recall metrics. These improvements are validated across both Adam and SGD optimizers, demonstrating robustness and broad applicability.

The performance advantages are obtained with only 11% additional computational time corresponding to 44 low-fidelity evaluations (220 exploration epochs), representing a highly favourable cost-benefit trade-off. The success validates our core hypothesis that strategic multi-fidelity optimization outperforms uniform high-fidelity search when budgets are properly controlled. By using cheap low-fidelity evaluations to systematically explore the hyperparameter landscape and identify promising regions before committing expensive high-fidelity budget to refinement, rather than relying on random initialization that may miss optimal areas, our method achieves superior configurations without simply benefiting from increased total computational resources. This work demonstrates that multi-fidelity optimization techniques, when properly designed with attention to fair comparison principles and rigorous experimental validation, provide genuine algorithmic value for hyperparameter tuning in object detection systems, offering practitioners a practical and effective approach for optimizing YOLO-based detectors with limited computational budgets.

Conclusions

The problem of hyperparameter optimization for both SGD and Adam optimizers in the context of aerial imagery has been addressed in this work. The standard VisDrone2019 dataset and the Realm dataset (our own aerial dataset) are used to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Differential Evolution (DE) and Bayesian Optimization (BO), guided by a multi-fidelity optimization strategy, are employed to allocate appropriate numbers of epochs for low- and high-fidelity evaluations, thereby structuring how long DE and BO run during the search.

In essence, the proposed method provides a fast and modular framework for hyperparameter tuning in YOLO, using a computationally cost-effective combination of DE and BO. It is well suited for integration into research pipelines where reproducibility and rigorous analysis are essential. The techniques incorporated multi-fidelity evaluation, adaptive search, and rigorous logging offer a practical template for tackling costly optimization problems in deep learning applications.

Empirically, we observe that the performance metrics improve for both Adam and SGD optimizers. Although the gains for SGD are relatively modest, the improvements for Adam are substantial, indicating that the overall mechanism is both efficient and robust. For the VisDrone2019 dataset, the largest improvement for Adam is in recall, with an increase of 18.42%, whereas for SGD the maximum gain is observed in precision, which improves by 5.07%. Similarly, for the Realm dataset, the largest improvement for Adam is in mAP(50–95), which increases by 20.96%, while SGD achieves its highest gain in precision, with an improvement of 2.78%.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the computational cost of the high-fidelity BO phase remains substantial, with training times ranging from 38,000 to 93,000 s per trial, which may constrain practical deployment in resource-limited environments. Second, although our methodology has been validated on two datasets (VisDrone2019 and Realm), broader evaluation across more diverse aerial imagery conditions and across YOLO architectures beyond YOLO11n would further strengthen claims of generalizability. Third, the relatively marginal improvements observed for SGD on some metrics suggest that the optimization landscape for gradient-based optimizers may be more challenging than for adaptive methods such as Adam. Finally, the fitness function, while effective in this study, relies on a harmonic-mean weighting of metrics that may not align with all application-specific preferences, particularly in scenarios where certain performance metrics are more critical than others.

To sum up, this research introduces several noteworthy contributions. To the best of our knowledge, the combined DE–MFO–BO optimization pipeline has not previously been applied in this manner for YOLO hyperparameter tuning, and systematic hyperparameter optimization for YOLO on the VisDrone2019 dataset has not been extensively documented in the literature. This work therefore lays an initial foundation for understanding the role and significance of hyperparameter optimization in aerial object detection using YOLO-based models.
