Static sagittal cervical alignment, pain, and jaw functional limitation across temporomandibular disorder subtypes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tolgahan Kara, Nursena Ünlü Kuzu, Elif Doğan, Dilan Bayram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare cervical sagittal alignment among temporomandibular disorder subtypes and to investigate the relationships between cervical alignment parameters, temporomandibular joint pain, neck pain, and jaw functional limitation.Materials and methodsA total of 118 participants, including patients diagnosed with temporomandibular disorder (TMD) and healthy controls, were included in the study. The study was conducted between January 2, 2025, and July 2, 2025. Based on the DC/TMD criteria, participants were classified into four groups: healthy controls, myalgia, disc displacement with reduction (DDwR), and disc displacement without reduction (DDwoR). TMJ pain and cervical pain intensities were assessed using VAS, while jaw functional limitation was evaluated using the jaw functional limitation scale-8 (JFLS-8). Cervical sagittal alignment was assessed on lateral cephalometric radiographs using the C2–C7 Cobb angle, POCA, and O–C2 angle. Multivariate analysis of variance and correlation analyses were performed.ResultsTMJ pain intensity and JFLS-8 scores were significantly higher in the DDwoR group compared with the control group and other TMD subtypes (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed among groups in terms of C2–C7 Cobb angle, POCA, or O–C2 angle (p > 0.05). Statiscally significant but weak positive correlations were found between TMJ pain and neck pain, as well as between TMJ pain and jaw functional limitation (p < 0.001). A weak positive correlation was initially observed between TMJ pain and the C2–C7 Cobb angle; however, this association did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons.ConclusionAlthough TMD subtypes differ significantly in TMJ pain severity and jaw functional limitation, cervical sagittal alignment parameters do not significantly distinguish these groups. These findings suggest that sagittal cervical alignment has a limited role in explaining TMD-related symptom severity and highlight the multifactorial nature of temporomandibular disorders.

Background

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) generally refer to conditions affecting the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joint, and related structures [1]. The clinical picture is typically characterized by pain and limited jaw function in most TMD types [2]. It is reported that the proportion of the population who exhibit at least one symptom of TMD can reach 60%, although only a subset of individuals seek treatment for these complaints [3].

Contemporary conceptual models of temporomandibular joint disorders, which are considered to have a multifactorial etiology, emphasize the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors in the development and persistence of symptoms [1]. This Biopsychosocial Model (BPM) suggests that there are several factors – structural, neuromuscular, behavioral, and physiological – that can influence the processing of pain and the manifestation of symptoms. No single structural factor has been identified that is consistently correlated to TMD intensity [4]. Consequently, no single structure has been identified as a common factor for all TMD patients.

Although TMD is a multi-factorial condition, clinicians have frequently reported the co-existence of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and cervical disorders, specifically neck pain [5, 6]. A number of reports suggest that the patients with TMD had more cervical symptoms and functional impairment than controls [7, 8]. Although the mechanisms behind the association between craniofacial and cervical pain are not fully understood, recent neuroanatomical studies have suggested that the convergence of trigeminal and cervical nociceptive input in the trigeminocervical complex (TCC) may underlie the clinical observations of the common comorbidities of these two types of pain conditions [9, 10].

In addition to shared neural mechanisms, the craniocervical and mandibular regions function as an integrated biomechanical and neuromuscular system [11]. Rather than investigating the reasons a TMD may have developed, some researchers have tried to determine if head posture or cervical spine alignment may change with the TMD. However, there is no consensus regarding the structural craniocervical posture that is associated with TMD [12, 13]. While certain studies have reported differences in cervical curvature or head posture among individuals with TMD [14, 15], other investigations have failed to demonstrate meaningful structural differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups [16, 17]. These inconsistencies may be related to variations in diagnostic criteria, patient selection, imaging techniques, and the specific outcome measures evaluated.

While several studies have examined the association between cervical and TMD-related structural findings, they generally regard TMD as a heterogeneous syndrome without taking into account its specific diagnostic subtypes. The orofacial area of interest to this study includes muscle dysfunction disorders classified as TMD, as well as intra-articular disorders including disc displacement with or without reduction. Due to the different aetiologies, symptomatology and effects on mandibular function in different TMD subtypes, investigating the specific structural and clinical characteristics of TMD subtypes in relation to cervical and TMD-related structural findings may help achieve a more accurate relationship with cervical and TMD symptoms [18, 19]. Sagittal cervical alignment parameters obtained from lateral cephalometric radiographs, such as the C2–C7 Cobb angle, posterior occipitocervical angle (POCA), and occiput–C2 (O–C2) angle, have been used in previous studies to describe craniocervical posture [20]. These measurements provide an objective quantification of cervical curvature and craniocervical relationships while the patient’s head is held in a standardised position [21]. However, data regarding how these parameters vary among different TMD diagnostic categories remain limited.

This study aimed to evaluate and compare the cervical sagittal alignment in patients with various subtypes of temporomandibular disorder (TMD) and in asymptomatic individuals, as well as to investigate the correlations between cervical sagittal parameters and the TMD-related pain and functional problems. Given the clinical coexistence of cervical and temporomandibular symptoms, it is reasonable to explore whether cervical sagittal alignment parameters differ between TMD subtypes and asymptomatic individuals. Therefore, we hypothesized that measurable differences in cervical sagittal alignment and clinical outcomes might be observed between TMD subtypes and controls.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University (Approval No: 24-MOBAEK-062). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients diagnosed with TMD who presented to the oral and maxillofacial surgery clinic between January 2nd 2025 and July 2nd 2025, as well as healthy individuals without TMD findings, were included in the study. The exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: history of traumatic TMJ or cervical spine injury, rheumatologic/inflammatory arthritis or systemic connective tissue disease, neuromuscular disease, previous TMJ or cervical spine surgery, severe skeletal malocclusion (advanced Class II or Class III requiring orthodontic or orthognathic treatment), pregnancy, or history of malignancy in the head and neck region. The study population consisted predominantly of adults, although participants aged 17 years were also included if they met the eligibility criteria. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants, whereas written parental consent and participant assent were obtained for participants younger than 18 years of age.

Clinical examination and questionnaire

All patients were examined clinically and radiologically in an oral and maxillofacial surgery clinic by an experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeon, and the clinical diagnosis was made based on the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) [18]. Accordingly, participants were classified into four groups: Group 1: healthy controls (no TMD findings); Group 2: myalgia; Group 3: disk displacement with reduction (DDwR); and Group 4: disk displacement without reduction (DDwoR). Diagnostic classification was established according to the DC/TMD examination protocol. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not routinely performed; therefore, disc displacement diagnoses were based on clinical criteria alone. Efforts were made to achieve comparable distributions of age and sex across groups. Demographic characteristics and group-specific sample sizes are summarized in Table 1.

TMJ-related pain intensity was assessed using a 0–10 cm visual analog scale (VAS-TMJ), where 0 indicated “no pain,” and 10 indicated “unbearable pain.” Participants were asked to mark the most severe TMJ pain experienced during the preceding week. Neck pain intensity was recorded using the same method on a 0–10 cm VAS (VAS-cervical). Jaw functional limitation was evaluated using the 8-item Jaw Functional Limitation Scale (JFLS-8), which was developed to assess limitations in chewing, speaking, and other jaw functions; higher scores indicate greater functional limitation [22].

Radiographical measurements

All lateral cephalograms were obtained by the same radiology technician using a Planmeca ProMax 3D unit (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) with an exposure time of 0.5 s at 80 kV and 10 mA. During imaging, participants were positioned upright in natural head position with the gaze directed forward, teeth in light intercuspation, and shoulders relaxed. Natural head position, rather than Frankfort horizontal plane parallelism, was used as the standardized reference for head posture during cephalometric acquisition. Radiographic parameters were standardized according to the routine protocol. Measurements were performed using Romexis software (version 4.0; Planmeca, Finland).

Radiographic evaluation was performed by a single examiner experienced in TMD and cervical anatomy. The examiner performing the radiographic measurements was blinded to the participants’ diagnostic group allocation and clinical questionnaire results throughout the measurement process. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median (minimum–maximum) for non-normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using histograms, Q–Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For multivariate analyses, multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis distances, and homogeneity of variance–covariance matrices was evaluated using Box’s M test.

A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted with diagnostic group (4 levels) and sex (2 levels) entered as fixed factors, and the following clinical and radiographic variables entered jointly as dependent variables: VAS-TMJ, VAS-cervical, JFLS-8, C2–C7 Cobb angle, POCA, and O–C2 angle. Sex was retained as a fixed factor to explore potential sex-related effects and group-by-sex interactions rather than as a covariate. Because Box’s M test is sensitive to violations of covariance homogeneity, Pillai’s Trace was selected as the primary multivariate test statistic. For follow-up effects, F statistics with corresponding degrees of freedom, P values, and effect sizes (partial eta squared, η²p) were reported. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to account for multiple testing in between-group comparisons.

Linear associations between clinical and radiographic variables were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on distributional characteristics. All tests were two-tailed, and the level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The intra-rater reliability of measurements taken by the same assessor 15 days apart was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). In the ICC analysis, a two-way mixed-effects model, the absolute agreement definition and the single-measure approach were used. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good and > 0.90 excellent reliability.

An a priori sample size calculation was performed based on the effect size reported by Gao et al. (2024) (d = 0.4054), with a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90%, yielding a target sample size of 210 participants. During the predefined recruitment period (2 January 2025–2 July 2025), 118 eligible participants were enrolled and included in the final analysis. To evaluate sample adequacy, a post hoc power analysis was additionally conducted using G*Power version 3.1. Based on the observed effect size for VAS-TMJ differences among the four diagnostic groups (partial η² = 0.252; Cohen’s f = 0.58), the achieved statistical power was 0.99 at α = 0.05. No missing data were identified; therefore, all included participants were analyzed.

In the overall sample, mean VAS-TMJ scores varied across groups, with the lowest in the control group (0.7 ± 1.1) and progressively higher in the myalgia (3.9 ± 3.3), DDwR (2.9 ± 2.5), and DDwoR (6.3 ± 3.5) groups. A comparable trend was observed for mandibular functional limitation, as measured by the JFLS-8, with the DDwoR group exhibiting the highest mean score (32.9 ± 18.7) and the control group the lowest (6.8 ± 11). VAS scores for cervical pain demonstrated minimal variation among groups, with substantial overlap in standard deviations. Similarly, sagittal cervical alignment parameters—including Cobb angle, POCA angle, and O–C2 angle—showed comparable mean values across all diagnostic categories. Gender-stratified analyses revealed consistent patterns in both female and male participants. In both sexes, higher VAS-TMJ and JFLS-8 scores were observed in the DDwoR group compared with the control group and other TMD subgroups. In contrast, cervical alignment parameters remained relatively stable across groups.

The effects of diagnostic group, gender, and group-by-gender interaction on outcome variables were assessed using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). Prior to MANOVA, multivariate assumptions were evaluated. Examination of Mahalanobis distances identified one potential multivariate outlier (Mahalanobis distance = 22.91), which only marginally exceeded the critical χ² value for six dependent variables (χ² = 22.46, p < 0.001). However, the observation only marginally exceeded the critical threshold and was retained. Box’s M test was significant (Box’s M = 150.853, F = 2.157, p < 0.001), indicating violation of the homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices assumption. Therefore, Pillai’s Trace was used as the primary multivariate test statistic.

The multivariate analysis revealed a significant overall effect of diagnostic group on the combined dependent variables (Pillai’s Trace = 0.538, F(18,321) = 3.894, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.179) (Table 3). No significant multivariate effects were found for gender (Pillai’s Trace = 0.076, F(6,105) = 1.446, p = 0.204, partial η² = 0.076) or for the group × gender interaction (Pillai’s Trace = 0.158, F(18,321) = 0.993, p = 0.467, partial η² = 0.053).

Follow-up univariate analyses are presented in Table 4. A significant main effect of group was identified for VAS-TMJ (F = 12.379, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.252) and JFLS-8 scores (F = 15.230, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.293). No statistically significant group effects were detected for VAS-cervical pain, Cobb angle, POCA angle, or O–C2 angle (p > 0.05). Post hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrated that VAS-TMJ scores were significantly higher in the DDwoR group than in the control and DDwR groups (all adjusted p < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed between the DDwoR and myalgia groups. In addition, the myalgia group exhibited significantly higher VAS-TMJ scores than the control group (adjusted p < 0.05). For JFLS-8 scores, the DDwoR group demonstrated significantly greater functional limitation than the control, myalgia, and DDwR groups (all adjusted p < 0.05), while no significant differences were found among the control, myalgia, and DDwR groups (Table 2). The main effect of gender was not significant for any of the assessed variables (p > 0.05). Likewise, no significant group-by-gender interactions were observed across the outcome measures. The coefficients of determination (R²) indicated that the models explained a moderate proportion of variance in VAS-TMJ and JFLS-8 scores. In contrast, the explained variance for cervical pain intensity and cervical alignment parameters was low.

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between VAS-TMJ pain and VAS-cervical pain (r = 0.358, 95% CI 0.182–0.518, p < 0.001), between VAS-TMJ pain and JFLS-8 scores (r = 0.634, 95% CI 0.497–0.741, p < 0.001), and between JFLS-8 scores and VAS-cervical pain (r = 0.473, 95% CI 0.308–0.616, p < 0.001). A weak positive correlation was also observed between VAS-TMJ pain and the C2–C7 Cobb angle (r = 0.218, 95% CI 0.053–0.375, p = 0.018); however, this association did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted significance threshold p < 0.0083). No significant correlations were observed between VAS-TMJ pain and the POCA or O–C2 angles (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study evaluated cervical sagittal alignment parameters and their potential associations with pain intensity and jaw functional limitation across different temporomandibular disorder (TMD) subtypes. The findings indicated that patients with disc displacement without reduction (DDwoR) exhibited greater TMJ pain intensity and more pronounced functional limitation compared with healthy controls. However, no significant differences were observed in the examined cervical sagittal alignment parameters between groups. Although a weak positive association was initially observed between TMJ pain severity and the C2–C7 Cobb angle, this relationship did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

The DDwoR group showed the highest TMJ pain and jaw function restriction scores, consistent with previous reports [27, 28]. DDwoR is often considered a more advanced form of TMJ internal disorder and may be associated with mechanical obstruction, inflammatory changes, and persistent functional impairment, particularly in treatment-seeking populations [28]. The present findings reinforce the notion that clinical severity in TMD is more closely related to intra-articular pathology and pain processing mechanisms than to static postural characteristics alone. However, as verification by MRI is not possible, the findings in this subgroup should be interpreted taking into account the potential for diagnostic overlap between clinical TMD categories.

The absence of significant differences in cervical sagittal alignment parameters among TMD subtypes is not entirely consistent with some earlier studies reporting altered cervical curvature or craniocervical posture in patients with TMD [14, 29, 30]. For instance, de Farias et al. reported reduced cervical lordosis in patients with temporomandibular dysfunction using radiographic measurements [29], while Gao et al. identified a decrease in C2–C7 Cobb angle and an increase in POCA within TMD patients [30]. However, other studies have not found reliable evidence of significant alterations in craniocervical alignment between symptomatic and asymptomatic TMD patients [12]. Thus, cervical alignment alterations may not be a consistent or universal feature of TMD. Discrepancies across studies may be attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria, sample characteristics, imaging protocols, and whether TMD subtypes were analyzed separately.

The unique aspect of this study is its inclusion of DC/TMD-based diagnostic subgroups and the simultaneous assessment of cervical alignment, pain intensity, and jaw functional limitations in the same sample. The lack of differences between groups in the measured craniocervical angles can be explained by the fact that, when evaluated in a standardized neutral head position, sagittal cervical alignment may lack distinguishing value among TMD subtypes. These findings may indicate that static postural characteristics alone are unlikely to fully explain the variability in TMD-related symptoms. Furthermore, static alignment measures may not adequately capture the functional complexity of the craniocervical–mandibular system [31].

Although no significant differences in craniocervical alignment were found between the groups, statistically significant but weak positive correlations were observed between TMJ pain and neck pain, and between TMJ pain and limited jaw function. These relationships are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the close clinical association between cervical and temporomandibular symptoms [32, 33]. Neuroanatomical convergence within the trigeminocervical complex may underlie this relationship. Through this mechanism, nociceptive input from cervical structures may influence orofacial pain perception, and vice versa [34]. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that variations in muscle activation patterns along the craniocervical–mandibular myofascial chain may contribute to the co-occurrence of symptoms without necessarily resulting in measurable changes in static skeletal alignment [35].

The weak positive correlation observed in this study between temporomandibular joint pain and the C2–C7 Cobb angle should be interpreted with caution, as it was not statistically significant following the Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Furthermore, the small effect size suggests limited clinical significance. If present, the observed association may reflect adaptive or compensatory responses within the craniocervical system rather than the primary aetiological mechanism underlying TMD symptoms. Conversely, the absence of significant correlations between POCA and O-C2 angles supports the notion that upper cervical sagittal relationships are not strongly associated with the severity of TMD pain or functional limitations in a cross-sectional context.

From a clinical perspective, these results highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation in TMD patients. Although TMJ pain and functional limitations are frequently accompanied by cervical symptoms such as neck pain, relying solely on sagittal cervical alignment parameters may not be sufficient to fully explain symptom severity or to guide clinical decision-making. Functional assessments, muscle performance evaluation, and psychosocial factors can provide more relevant information for personalized treatment planning [12, 36].

Clinical implications

Although cervical sagittal alignment is not significantly associated with TMD subtypes, cervical symptoms and neck pain are frequently observed alongside TMJ complaints. Clinicians should therefore also consider cervical assessment as part of a comprehensive TMD evaluation. However, static cervical radiographic parameters alone should not be used to guide diagnostic or treatment decisions.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. Firstly, its cross-sectional design precludes any inferences regarding causal relationships between cervical sagittal alignment and TMD-related symptoms. Secondly, the diagnostic subgroup classification is based on the DC/TMD clinical examination protocol without magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmation. Although the DC/TMD provides acceptable diagnostic validity in clinical settings, MRI remains the gold standard for assessing disc position; therefore, some diagnostic misclassification between DDwR, DDwoR and other TMD categories cannot be entirely ruled out. Thirdly, the marked predominance of female participants reflects the higher prevalence of TMD in women, but limits the generalisability of the findings to male populations. In particular, the small number of male participants in the DDwoR subgroup has reduced the statistical power to detect potential gender-specific effects and interactions. Furthermore, only static radiographic parameters were assessed; dynamic posture, cervical muscle activity and movement patterns were not evaluated and may have a greater influence on symptom development and functional impairment.

Finally, psychological and behavioural variables such as anxiety, stress, pain catastrophising, parafunctional habits (including bruxism), occlusal characteristics and duration of symptoms were not assessed and may have influenced pain perception, functional limitations and overall symptom severity. Future studies incorporating longitudinal designs, MRI-based diagnostic validation, functional postural assessments and comprehensive biopsychosocial evaluations may provide a more complete understanding of the role of the cervical spine in the pathophysiology of TMD.

Although the final sample size was lower than the target estimated in the a priori calculation, post hoc analysis demonstrated adequate statistical power for the primary clinical outcome.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although temporomandibular disorder subtypes differ significantly in pain intensity and limitations in jaw function, cervical sagittal alignment parameters, including the C2–C7 Cobb angle, POCA, and O–C2 angle, do not appear to differentiate these groups. These findings suggest that sagittal cervical alignment plays only a limited role in explaining TMD severity, highlighting the multifactorial nature of these disorders and supporting the need for a comprehensive, multidimensional clinical approach.
