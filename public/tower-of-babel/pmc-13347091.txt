Genetic diagnosis of hereditary kidney disease in pediatric patients through whole-exome sequencing and mitochondrial DNA analysis
Authors: Jiyoung Oh, Keumwha Lee, Dongju Won, Young-Mock Lee, Jae Il Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying the genetic cause of hereditary kidney disease is essential for appropriate management. However, pediatric patients often present with nonspecific renal symptoms such as proteinuria, hematuria, or both, making accurate diagnosis difficult. Mitochondrial diseases are also an important but underrecognized etiology of hereditary kidney disorders. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic utility of whole-exome sequencing (WES) combined with mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analysis and to describe the clinical and genetic features of pediatric patients presenting with isolated proteinuria, combined hematuria and proteinuria, or hematuria with a relevant family history. MethodsDNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes of 77 pediatric patients with hematuria and/or proteinuria with or without family history. WES and mtDNA analyses were conducted to identify underlying genetic etiologies. ResultsThe overall molecular diagnostic yield was 54.5% (42/77) with COL4A-related nephropathy (29 cases) being the most prevalent diagnosis. Notably, one patient with no systemic symptoms except for renal manifestations was identified with the m.3243A>G variant in MT-TL1, which is associated with the MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) syndrome. This patient’s initial and predominant presentation was gross and microscopic hematuria, diverging from typical MELAS-associated kidney disease. ConclusionWES proved valuable in diagnosing hereditary kidney diseases in pediatric patients with nonspecific urinalysis findings. Incorporating mtDNA analysis further improved diagnostic yield and enabled identification of atypical presentations of mitochondrial disorders, such as MELAS, even when renal symptoms were isolated.

Graphical abstract

Introduction

Pediatric nephropathies encompass a wide spectrum of diseases with various clinical manifestations and underlying etiologies. Among the various causes of pediatric nephropathies, hereditary kidney disease is a significant etiology, with approximately 30% of children with chronic kidney disease (CKD) developing a monogenic condition [1–3]. Elucidating the exact genetic cause of hereditary kidney disease is crucial for proper treatment and management. However, pediatric patients often present with nonspecific nephrology symptoms, with manifestations limited to proteinuria, hematuria, or a combination of both, thereby hindering diagnosis [4]. Although invasive diagnostic tools such as renal biopsy may yield pathological findings, they often do not reveal the precise underlying etiology [5].

Mitochondrial diseases result from impairments of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, leading to insufficient generation of adenosine triphosphate, and are caused by pathogenic variants (PVs) in either mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA related to mitochondrial function [6,7]. Mitochondrial diseases can present with multifaceted symptoms across various organs as clusters of grouped manifestations. Syndromic mitochondrial diseases can also involve the kidneys, manifesting as glomerular disease, tubulointerstitial nephritis, or cystic disease [8,9]. However, renal involvement in mitochondrial diseases is easily overlooked because of mild initial renal symptoms or overshadowing by severe neurological, cardiological, or endocrine symptoms [10,11]. A systematic study of 42 patients with mitochondrial disease found that 50% had renal tubular involvement, whereas clinically overt renal manifestations were observed in only a minority of patients, underscoring the frequently underrecognized nature of renal involvement in mitochondrial diseases [12].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is an innovative and powerful technology that can be used for massive parallel sequencing of DNA fragments, providing extensive genetic information at once. NGS is also being applied to the diagnosis of inherited renal diseases, demonstrating remarkable diagnostic accuracy [13–18]. Precise genetic diagnosis using NGS in hereditary kidney disease can guide personalized medical decisions, such as drug selection for patients, facilitate prognosis prediction, and provide information for donor selection for transplantation. In addition, screening for mtDNA has become more efficient with the advent of NGS technology, facilitating the accurate diagnosis of mitochondrial diseases, which are rare genetic etiologies, in patients with subtle renal symptoms.

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic significance of genetic testing (whole-exome sequencing [WES] combined with mitochondrial DNA [mtDNA] analysis) in pediatric patients presenting with proteinuria or hematuria. This study highlights the critical importance of incorporating mtDNA analysis into WES when evaluating hereditary kidney disease, even in cases wherein hematuria is the predominant renal manifestation.

Methods

Study participants

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Health System, Severance Hospital (No. 2024-1160-001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Between January 2020 and December 2023, DNA samples were obtained from 77 pediatric patients after obtaining informed consent. Medical history, clinical data, and family medical information were collected for each patient. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) hematuria and a related family medical history; 2) both hematuria and proteinuria; and 3) isolated proteinuria exceeding age-specific thresholds (urine protein/creatinine ratio [mg/mg] >0.2 for children over 2 years of age or >0.5 for children aged 6–24 months [19]), confirmed through at least two positive tests separated by 1–2 weeks, following established guidelines. The exclusion criteria were as follows: a history of structural kidney abnormalities, renal stones, concurrent infectious disease with abnormal urinalysis, vascular disorders, hematologic abnormalities, or confirmed orthostatic proteinuria.

Sequencing and variant analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the collected peripheral blood of patients using the QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen). DNA samples were fragmented, end-repaired, and ligated to adapters, followed by hybridization with probes for whole exome and mtDNA sequencing. All procedures were performed per the manufacturer’s instructions. Enriched DNA was amplified, and clusters were generated and sequenced on a NextSeq 550 System (Illumina) using 2 × 151 bp reads. The average depth was greater than 200×, with 30× coverage being over 99%. Mitochondrial DNA variants were called using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). All mitochondrial genome positions were covered at a minimum depth of 100×, achieving 100% coverage of the mitochondrial genome. Heteroplasmic variants were defined as those with a minimum variant allele frequency of 5%. Sequencing reads were subjected to standard quality control. Low-quality reads with Q-scores below 30 were removed, and additional filtering included the removal of polymerase chain reaction duplicates and low-complexity regions. For nuclear DNA, >98% of targeted exons were covered at a minimum read depth of 30×. Variants were only considered if they passed these quality control criteria.

Variant analysis and interpretation

The Burrows-Wheeler alignment was used to align reads to the human genomic reference sequence (GRCh37). The HaplotypeCaller in the GATK was used to identify single-nucleotide variants and insertions-deletions (indels). All variants were annotated using the ANNOVAR (Wang Lab, University of Pennsylvania) and Variant Effect Predictor software (European Bioinformatics Institute). Detected variants were further examined and verified using the Integrative Genomic Viewer (Broad Institute). Split-read-based detection of large structural variations was conducted using Pindel. Read-depth-based detection of copy number variants was conducted using the ExomeDepth R package, followed by visualization using the CANBANA algorithm [20].

The pathogenicity of variants was classified according to the criteria of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology [21]. To assess pathogenicity, evidence was collected using population frequency data from multiple databases, including the 1000 Genomes, Genome Aggregation Database, Exome Sequencing Project, and Korean Reference Genome Database. In silico analysis was conducted using SIFT (sorting intolerant from tolerant), MutationTaster, FATHMM, and MetaSVM. Literature and database search for the collection of evidence were conducted using ClinVar, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, and Human Gene Mutation Database, and relevant scientific publications. For variant interpretation, parental testing was performed when possible.

Results

Characteristics of patients

In total, 77 patients from 75 families with urinalysis abnormalities were enrolled in this study. Among them, 21 patients had isolated hematuria with a family history (27.3%), 12 had isolated proteinuria (15.6%), while 44 had both hematuria and proteinuria (57.1%). The average age of the enrolled pediatric patients was 7.15 years, with ages ranging from 5 months to 18 years (Table 1). Overall, 43 patients were male (55.8%) and 27 (35.1%) had a family history of renal-related disorders. Four patients (5.2%) presented with end-stage renal disease at the initial examination and three (3.9%) underwent kidney transplantation from a living donor.

Diagnostic yield and final molecular diagnosis

The final molecular diagnostic yield was 54.5% (42/77 patients), encompassing a range of conditions: COL4A-related nephropathy (29 patients), PAX2-associated focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) (2 patients), dent disease (2 patients), SLC4A1-associated distal renal tubular acidosis (1 patient), C3 glomerulopathy (1 patient), INF2-associated FSGS (1 patient), LMX1B-associated FSGS (1 patient), NUP107-associated nephrotic syndrome (NS) (1 patient), UMOD-associated tubulointerstitial nephropathy (1 patient), NPHP4-related nephronophthisis (1 patient), WT1-associated disorder (1 patient), and mitochondrial nephropathy (1 patient).

Within the group presenting with isolated hematuria and a family history, the final genetic diagnosis was achieved in 61.9% of patients (13/21), with all of them having COL4A-related nephropathy. Among the patients presenting with concurrent hematuria and proteinuria, genetic diagnosis was accomplished in 47.7% of patients (21/44), with COL4A-related nephropathy being the most prevalent (16/21), similar to that in the isolated hematuria group. Additionally, 66.7% of patients (8/12) presenting with proteinuria attained a final molecular diagnosis, with the most common being PAX2-associated FSGS (2/8) and dent disease (2/8). Notably, one patient with isolated proteinuria was found to have mitochondrial nephropathy due to a well-known PV in MT-TL1 of MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) syndrome (Fig. 1).

When categorizing the diseases identified in the diagnosed patient group, the majority were classified as hereditary glomerular diseases (39/42, 92.9%), followed by hereditary tubulointerstitial diseases (3/42, 7.1%). PAX2-related cases were classified as FSGS based on the predominant clinical phenotype. All three patients who exhibited bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis received a genetic diagnosis, in which PVs were identified in CLCN5, SLC4A1, and UMOD, genes known to be associated with hereditary tubulointerstitial kidney disease.

Clinical impact of genetic diagnosis

In nine patients, confirmation of a genetic diagnosis led to immediate modification of pharmacologic management. Six patients were diagnosed with COL4A-related nephropathy, while the remaining three were diagnosed with WT1-related nephropathy, LMX1B-associated FSGS, and MELAS syndrome, respectively. Prior to genetic confirmation, all nine patients had received immunosuppressive agents, including systemic corticosteroids, azathioprine, and/or cyclosporine, under an initial diagnosis of primary glomerular disease. Following molecular diagnosis, immunosuppressive therapies were discontinued in all cases, and management was modified to renin–angiotensin system blockade with surveillance-based follow-up.

Variant analysis

We detected 53 PVs, with the most prevalent being COL4A5 (15/53, 28.3%), COL4A4 (11/53, 20.8%), and COL4A3 (8/53, 15.1%) (Fig. 2). In addition, we identified PVs in SLC12A3 (c.2573T>A), NPHP4 (2304+1G>A), and MT-ND1 (c.88T>C) in each patient. However, we excluded these variants from the final diagnosis because they did not correspond to the phenotype of the patients or the inheritance pattern of known genetic diseases (Supplementary Table 1, available online). Notably, we detected 18 novel PVs (Table 2). Additionally, we identified 10 variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) in genes related to inherited kidney disease, including COL4A5 (3), COL4A4 (1), COL4A3 (3), COL4A2 (1), LAMB2 (1), and CFHR5 (1) (Supplementary Table 2, available online). Moreover, we detected four additional VUSs alongside known PVs in COL4A4 in four patients.

Trio-based segregation analysis was performed in 28 families. In 18 families, variants were inherited from an affected parent, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. De novo variants were identified in five families. In families with autosomal recessive disorders, segregation analysis demonstrated that in three families both parents were asymptomatic carriers and the affected individuals harbored compound heterozygous variants, while in one family the affected individual was homozygous for the pathogenic variant. Notably, in one patient with asymptomatic parents, segregation analysis confirmed digenic inheritance, with a pathogenic variant in COL4A5 inherited from one parent and a pathogenic variant in COL4A4 inherited from the other parent.

Patient diagnosed with mitochondrial nephropathy

A 14-year-old female patient with a 4-month history of gross/microscopic hematuria and proteinuria following bronchitis was referred to our hospital. Gross hematuria persisted, with waxing and waning. The patient reported no headaches or seizures and had no relevant medical history, including stroke-like episodes. Developmental and neurological examinations as well as hearing and visual function assessments were normal. Despite no family history of kidney disease, the mother and maternal uncle of the patient had adult-onset sensorineural deafness. On examination, the patient was 154.4 cm tall (25–50th percentile) and weighed 36 kg (<3rd percentile). Initial blood pressure was 103/69 mmHg, with a heart rate of 76 bpm. Urinalysis revealed orange-colored gross hematuria with proteinuria (2.69 g/dL). Blood laboratory test results were unremarkable. Diffuse cortical echogenicity was observed on renal ultrasonography. In this patient, there was no family history of kidney disease; however, a family history of adult-onset hearing impairment—an extra-renal manifestation known to be associated with Alport syndrome—prompted genetic testing. Furthermore, despite consideration of the broad phenotypic expressivity observed within families affected by Alport syndrome, discrepancies in the family medical history led us to extend the analysis to mitochondrial DNA. Genetic analysis identified the m.3243A>G variant in MT-TL1, a known PV of MELAS syndrome, with 53.8% heteroplasmy. A renal biopsy was not performed owing to the patient’s refusal. Brain magnetic resonance imaging and cardiac echocardiography were conducted to assess the involvement of other organs; however, both showed normal results. Audiometric evaluation revealed no notable hearing impairment. A cosegregation test performed on the mother and two younger sisters showed that each harbored 14.1%, 46.2%, and 60.3% heteroplasmy for the m.3243 variant in MT-TL1. Notably, microscopic hematuria, previously unrecognized, was detected only in the mother, whereas both siblings exhibited normal urinalysis findings.

Discussion

When WES was combined with mtDNA analyses, a diagnostic yield of 54.5% was achieved in a cohort of pediatric patients presenting with proteinuria/hematuria. The most common genetic disorder was COL4A-related nephropathy (29/42), while 11 more monogenic disorders were identified. Notably, COL4A-related nephropathy was the most diagnosed disease in patients presenting solely with hematuria and having a related family history and in those presenting with both hematuria and proteinuria. However, PAX2-associated FSGS and dent disease were more frequent in the group of patients presenting with proteinuria alone.

COL4A-related nephropathy is the second most common inherited kidney disease leading to CKD after autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, even in pediatric patients [4,22]. The COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 genes encode the collagen IV α3–5 chain, which forms a heterotrimer protein. This network serves as the basic framework for the basement membranes in the kidneys, eyes, and ears [22–24]. COL4A5-related nephropathy follows an X-linked inheritance pattern, whereas COL4A4- and COL4A3-related nephropathies can manifest as autosomal dominant or recessive conditions. Specifically, when heterozygous PVs occur in COL4A3 or COL4A4, a broad spectrum of manifestations can arise, ranging from familial hematuria to thin basement membrane disease, Alport syndrome, or end-stage renal disease. However, proteinuria or decline in kidney function has been reported to be less common in these cases than in typical Alport syndrome, which exhibits an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Among the patients included in this study, those harboring a single PV in COL4A3 or COL4A4 exhibited various symptoms, ranging from hematuria alone to the concurrent presentation of proteinuria and hematuria. In these patients, careful long-term monitoring is necessary, considering the possibility of progressive deterioration in kidney function over time [25–27].

This study identified two patients harboring PVs in a digenic pattern: one involving COL4A3 and COL4A4, and the other involving COL4A4 and COL4A5. Individuals with digenic PVs in COL4A3 and COL4A4 are predisposed to proteinuria and are reported to manifest an intermediate severity of renal dysfunction compared with patients with the autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive forms of Alport syndrome [28,29]. One of our patients, a 7-year-old boy carried a single PV in each gene and presented with gross/microscopic hematuria and mild levels of proteinuria, with no significant decline in kidney function yet; thus, continued observation was deemed necessary. Nephropathy related to COL4A5 located on the X chromosome is known to exhibit a milder clinical course in female patients than in male patients. However, the clinical course can vary based on X-chromosome inactivation patterns. Additionally, although reported cases are few, patients with PVs in both COL4A5 and other collagen nephropathy-related genes may also exhibit a diverse range of clinical outcomes. In such cases, proteinuria may occur at an earlier age compared with that in cases where only COL4A5 harbors the pathogenic variant, potentially increasing the likelihood of kidney failure [28,30,31]. Another of our patients, a 1-year-old girl, carried missense PVs in COL4A4 and COL4A5 and presented with hematuria and proteinuria despite her age and sex. While her kidney function was preserved, vigilant monitoring of the onset of proteinuria and any decline in kidney function was considered essential.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the genetically diagnosed and undiagnosed groups with respect to clinical characteristics. Notably, all patients presenting with bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis on ultrasonography were genetically diagnosed, with PVs identified in CLCN5, SLC4A1, and UMOD. These findings suggest that bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis may help identify a subgroup of patients in whom genetic testing is particularly informative, although this observation is limited by the small sample size. Interestingly, in our cohort, we identified a noteworthy case of a female patient who presented with proteinuria and gross hematuria and was found to harbor a pathogenic variant in mtDNA known to cause MELAS syndrome. MELAS syndrome can present with a wide range of clinical symptoms, including various forms of renal involvement [7,9,32-36]. However, the patient presented with kidney manifestations as the sole evident symptom, with no other concurrent symptoms related to MELAS syndrome, such as recurrent stroke, encephalopathy, diabetes mellitus, skeletal muscle weakness, or hearing impairment. Similar to our patient, the condition in some previous cases was characterized by the absence of prominent MELAS syndrome symptoms at the time of diagnosis, with renal symptoms being the main findings upon presentation (Table 3) [9,32,34,35]. Notably, our patient presented with gross and microscopic hematuria as the main manifestations, combined with proteinuria. In contrast, most cases of MELAS syndrome with renal symptoms predominantly feature proteinuria caused by tubulointerstitial nephropathy, typically without hematuria or with minimal microscopic hematuria. Some studies have reported an overlap in symptoms between MELAS nephropathy and Alport syndrome, which are both characterized by nephropathy and hearing impairment, with the presence of hematuria being a key distinguishing factor of Alport syndrome [33,36]. Therefore, based on our findings, mitochondrial disease should be considered a differential diagnosis when evaluating patients suspected of having a genetic renal disorder, even if hematuria is the main symptom and no symptoms typically associated with MELAS syndrome were noted. In addition, when using NGS to diagnose genetic renal disorders, sufficient coverage of relevant genes, including mtDNA, is crucial, as mtDNA analysis is often not part of the standard WES pipeline.

Molecular diagnosis in this cohort had direct clinical implications beyond etiologic classification. Several genetically defined nephropathies identified in this study, including COL4A-related nephropathy, WT1-associated disorder, LMX1B-associated FSGS, and mitochondrial nephropathy, are conditions in which immunosuppressive therapy has not demonstrated sustained benefit [33,37,38,39]. In these patients, genetic confirmation enabled timely discontinuation of immunosuppressive agents and a transition to renin–angiotensin system blockade–based management, thereby avoiding unnecessary treatment-related adverse effects.

In parallel, broad genetic testing approaches, including WES combined with mtDNA analysis, raise important considerations regarding cost, accessibility, and ethical and interpretative challenges, such as secondary findings and VUSs. These factors underscore the risk of overtesting when such approaches are applied indiscriminately. Therefore, careful patient selection and appropriate pre- and post-test counseling are essential, as no statistically significant clinical indicators could be clearly identified to distinguish between genetically diagnosed and undiagnosed groups in this cohort when using WES with mtDNA analysis.

There are some limitations in this study. First, the small sample size and single-center design limited the statistical power, precluding the identification of statistically significant clinical predictors of genetic diagnostic yield. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings and to better define patient subgroups in whom genetic testing is most informative. Second, because the WES-based analysis applied in this study was limited to the exome, screening of noncoding regions was not possible.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that advances in genetic testing have improved the feasibility of early and accurate diagnosis of hereditary renal diseases, enabling appropriate clinical management. When applied based on appropriate clinical selection, mitochondrial DNA analysis can be actively integrated with nuclear DNA testing to improve diagnostic yield.
