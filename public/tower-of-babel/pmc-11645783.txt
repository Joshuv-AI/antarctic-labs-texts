All I want for Christmas…is a precisely defined research question
Authors: Suzie Cro, Rachel Phillips
Published: 2024

Each year, Santa Claus and his reindeer have the monumental task of delivering billions of presents across the world. With an ever-increasing population [1] and number of presents to deliver, Santa must continually find new ways to optimise his present delivery. This includes reindeer performance. Over recent years, he had noticed reindeers who ate carrots before starting their 24-h Christmas Eve journey tired more easily than those that ate Mrs Claus’ homemade mince pies, and he started to wonder if this was related to their chosen pre-flight meal. Santa decided to put his theory to the test and as part of his delivery improvement research programme conducted a randomised controlled trial to investigate the impact of a reindeer’s pre-flight meal on performance. On Christmas Eve 2023, Santa gathered nine reindeers (Dasher, Dancer, Prancer, Vixen, Comet, Cupid, Donner, Blitzen and, of course, Rudolph [2]) from the population of flying reindeers and randomly assigned each one to a pre-flight meal of carrots or a mince pie. On Christmas morning, immediately after successfully delivering all the presents carefully made in his workshop, Santa collected different measures of performance from each reindeer. Afterwards, he shared the data he collected with his friendly statistical elf and asked them to answer the question: does the reindeer’s pre-flight meal impact performance? But this research question is too vague. To make an informed decision about which pre-flight meal performs best, the statistical elf reminds Santa that he needs to ask a more precise question in line with the International Conference on Harmonisation E9 Addendum on Estimands and Sensitivity Analysis in Clinical Trials (ICH E9 (R1)). Otherwise, the statistical elf might not give Santa the answer to the question he wants answered. This would result in a waste of Santa Claus’ research time, costs and efforts in doing the trial. To avoid any confusion and ensure Santa gets the answer to his question of interest, just like in any trial of a healthcare intervention, he needs to specify five key attributes [3–6]:

In other settings, for different outcome types, different summary measures may be more appropriate. Considering how these different intercurrent events are handled is important, since different ways of handling them can lead to quite different conclusions about the most beneficial pre-flight meal. See Table 1 for different strategies for handling intercurrent events. We note that if a reindeer were not to come back and provide their tiredness score resulting in missing data (i.e. loss to follow-up), this is not an intercurrent event. Missing data often occur alongside intercurrent events but is not by itself an intercurrent event [10]. It is the intercurrent event that affects the interpretation of the outcome and not the missing data. Missing data would be an issue that the statistical elf needs to handle as part of their statistical analysis. By specifying the five attributes (1–5 above), Santa Claus will clarify precisely what treatment effect he is interested in, referred to as an ‘estimand’ [10]. This ensures he will get the answer to the question of interest and correctly interpret the trial results to make an informed decision.

For example, Santa could be interested in:

Estimand 1: The average difference (summary measure) in tiredness score (outcome) between eating a mince pie and a bunch of five carrots (treatment conditions) for flying reindeers (population) regardless of whether all the carrots or mince pies are eaten or cheeky reindeers switching allocations or sneaky reindeers eating other food on route or bold reindeers indulging in Santa’s sherry (intercurrent events—treatment policy strategy).

Or he could be interested in:

Estimand 2: The average difference (summary measure) in tiredness score (outcome) between eating a mince pie and a bunch of five carrots (treatment conditions) for flying reindeers (population) if all flying reindeers adhere to the allocated pre-flight meal and do not receive any additional foods or sherry (intercurrent events—hypothetical strategy).

If Santa had asked his friendly statistical elf to investigate estimand 1, then his statistical elf could have proceeded to include all reindeer outcomes in the analysis regardless of the occurrence of any of the reindeer shenanigans (intercurrent events) and use a suitable analysis model that estimates the mean difference in outcome by allocated food allocation, for example, a linear regression model including the tiredness VAS score as outcome and the food allocation as the exposure. This aligns with a conventional intention-to-treat trial analysis including everyone randomised and all outcomes regardless of subsequent events. Santa’s elf would have reported that the mean difference in tiredness score for mince pies compared to carrots was -15, demonstrating a small beneficial effect of the mince pies. But this has not accounted for reindeers not tolerating all of their carrot meal, reindeers switching their pre-flight meal from carrots to a mince pie and some reindeers consuming contraband (cookies or sherry) on route.

If Santa had instead asked his friendly statistical elf to investigate estimand 2, his statistical elf could have proceeded to set the tiredness VAS score outcomes to missing for all reindeer who experienced an intercurrent event and then used a missing data method to attempt to recreate what the outcomes would have looked like, had the intercurrent events not occurred, for example, a method such as multiple imputation or inverse probability weighting or likelihood-based analyses to recreate outcomes in the hypothetical setting [11, 12]. Alternatively, causal inference methods could be used [13]. For this hypothetical estimand, Santa’s elf would have reported that the mean difference in tiredness score was -55. This demonstrates a much bigger effect of the mince pies when accounting for all of the reindeers’ shenanigans.

In reality, the statistical elf would have found the sample size was too small (nine reindeers) to estimate these estimands reliably using the discussed methods. We provide fictitious results for the purpose of illustration and would not advise employing the discussed hypothetical estimation approaches in practice with such a small sample size.

Like optimising performance for flying reindeer, in trials of healthcare interventions multiple different questions can be investigated and the answers to these questions may lead to different conclusions on treatment benefit [5]. In such trials, participants may similarly experience different types of intercurrent events which when handled in different ways can result in different impressions of treatment benefit. In a clinical trial setting, the above intercurrent events (i)–(iv) would be analogous to (i) treatment intolerance, (ii) treatment switching, (iii) using rescue therapy, and (iv) taking a contraindicated medication (Table 2). Many more are possible. It is therefore important to have a clear understanding of exactly what treatment effect a trial intends to investigate, i.e. what ‘estimand’ is of interest. Trial design, conduct and analysis can then be aligned to address this.

The use of estimands in trials was brought to the forefront by the publication of an addendum to the ICH E9 harmonised guideline on statistical principles for clinical trials on estimands and sensitivity analysis in clinical trials in November 2019 (ICH E9 (R1)) [10]. These guidelines are now adopted by regulators worldwide and require the use of estimands. However, research has identified slow uptake of the use of estimands and moreover, that without estimands, it is often unclear what precisely is being estimated in trials, potentially leading to misinterpretation or the use of inappropriate statistical methods [14]. Five years on from the publication of the ICH E9 (R1), we urge trialists to implement the estimand framework into their practice, clearly specifying their precise research question using estimands. For more details on using estimands in trials of healthcare interventions, see a primer by Kahan et al. [3]. Disclaimer: This is an entirely fictitious trial setting.
