Phytochemical-mediated variation in growth, feeding, and life table parameters of Phthorimaea operculella (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae)
Authors: Ali Golizadeh, Zahra Abedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The potato tuber moth, Phthorimaea operculella (Zeller) (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae), is a major constraint on commercial potato production worldwide. Larvae damage plants by mining the foliage and penetrating tubers, thereby rendering them unmarketable. In the present study, the effects of six potato cultivars (Agria, Fontane, Javid, Jelly, Marfona, and Sprit) on the life table parameters and feeding efficiency of P. operculella were evaluated under controlled laboratory conditions. Data were analyzed based on the age-stage, two-sex life table method. In parallel, we examined biochemical traits associated with host susceptibility and quantified protein, starch, flavonoid, and phenolic contents to evaluate their relationships with the ecological performance and nutritional indices of the pest. The results indicated that P. operculella exhibited the shortest developmental period when reared on Fontane, Marfona, and Sprit, whereas the longest development time was observed on Agria. The Javid cultivar supported the highest intrinsic rate (r) and finite rate of increase (λ), while Agria and Marfona resulted in the lowest values for these parameters. The highest relative growth rate (RGR) was recorded on Fontane, Javid, and Sprit, whereas Agria yielded the lowest RGR. Correlation analyses revealed a positive association between tuber starch content and both developmental duration and net reproductive rate (R₀). In contrast, phenolic content was negatively correlated with R₀ and r. Cluster analysis classified Javid as a relatively susceptible cultivar, while Fontane, Sprit, and Marfona were identified as comparatively more unsuitable to P. operculella population growth. Overall, these findings provide valuable insights into host plant-pest interactions and can contribute to the development and refinement of integrated pest management (IPM) strategies against the potato tuber moth.

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.; Solanaceae) is one of the most widely cultivated commercial crops and holds substantial global importance1–3. It ranks among the world’s principal staple crops, alongside rice, wheat, and maize4–7. Owing to its broad adaptability and high productivity, potato plays a critical role in global food security.

Potato tubers are a rich source of essential nutrients, including carbohydrates, proteins, vitamin C, B-complex vitamins, magnesium, potassium, and dietary fiber8,9. These nutritional constituents also contribute to the plant’s capacity to tolerate a wide range of biotic and abiotic stresses10. Among the bioactive compounds present in potatoes, secondary metabolites constitute a particularly important group11,12. Although these metabolites are not directly involved in primary growth or development, they play crucial roles in plant defense and environmental adaptation. Many plant-derived foods contain diverse classes of secondary metabolites that exhibit significant biological activity13. Potato tubers, in particular, are recognized as an important source of secondary metabolites, especially phenolic compounds14,15. Consequently, potatoes and potato-based products have attracted increasing interest from both consumers and researchers6.

The potato tuber moth, Phthorimaea operculella (Zeller) (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae), is considered one of the most destructive pests of potato worldwide due to its close association with the host plant, high reproductive potential, strong adaptability to environmental fluctuations, and substantial economic impact16–18. This species is widely distributed and infests various solanaceous crops and weeds, with potato (S. tuberosum) being its primary host19,20,1,21. Although foliar mining by larvae typically results in limited yield loss, infestation of tubers severely reduces marketability, with damage intensifying during storage, particularly under non-refrigerated conditions22. Field infestations may reach 20–30%, while storage losses can approach 100% in severe cases4,24. Given that the most significant economic losses arise from tuber damage, effective management strategies should primarily focus on controlling P. operculella during tuber formation and throughout the storage period24.

Currently, chemical insecticides remain the primary means of managing P. operculella populations in potato fields25,7. However, the rapid development of insecticide resistance has progressively reduced the efficacy of chemical control strategies26,27. Moreover, effective suppression of this pest is hindered by its high reproductive potential, cryptic and burrowing larval behavior, adaptability to fluctuating environmental conditions, and multivoltine life cycle28,29.

The deployment of less susceptible potato cultivars can substantially enhance the effectiveness of cultural and biological control measures while decreasing reliance on chemical pesticides, thereby constituting a cornerstone of integrated pest management (IPM) programs30,22,31,23. The use of resistant cultivars represents an effective and sustainable strategy for mitigating damage caused by the potato tuber moth21,32,33. The findings of some studies indicate that diverse wild potato species exhibit robust and mechanistically distinct tuber resistance to P. operculella, mediated by both periderm- and cortex-based defenses, highlighting their considerable potential as genetic resources for resistance breeding programs34–36. Food availability and nutritional quality play a critical role in the establishment, development, and population dynamics of P. operculella23. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the pest’s biological performance on different host plants is essential for the design of sustainable management strategies24. Golizadeh et al.31 investigated the life history and fecundity of P. operculella on both leaves and tubers of several potato cultivars and demonstrated substantial variation in insect performance among cultivars. Such findings underscore the importance of elucidating fundamental insect-plant interactions to improve pest management practices. Accordingly, the identification of effective and environmentally sound control strategies for P. operculella has become increasingly important for ensuring the stable and sustainable growth of the global potato industry24.

The present study aimed to assess the responses of life history traits and nutritional performance of P. operculella to different potato cultivars used as larval food sources. In addition, we examined whether variation in the biochemical composition and nutritional quality of these cultivars influences key biological characteristics of the pest. To this end, relationships between major biochemical attributes of potato tubers and multiple biological parameters of P. operculella were analyzed. To our knowledge, no previous study has quantified correlations between the biological traits of P. operculella and the biochemical composition of potato tubers. The integration of demographic parameters with nutritional indices provides robust metrics for evaluating host plant susceptibility. Consequently, the findings of this study offer valuable insights for enhancing integrated pest management (IPM) programs targeting P. operculella in potato production systems.

Materials and methods

Potato cultivars

Tubers of six potato cultivars including Agria, Fontane, Javid, Jelly, Marfona, and Sprit were obtained from the Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Center of Ardabil, Iran. These cultivars represent some of the most widely cultivated potato varieties in the country and are commonly used in commercial production systems.

Rearing of Phthorimaea operculella

The initial population of potato tuberworm used in this study was collected from potato stores infested with P. operculella in the Ardabil province, north western Iran. The stock culture of P. operculella was established on potato tubers (cv. Agria) and subsequently maintained in a rearing cage under controlled conditions in a growth chamber at the Department of Plant Protection laboratory, University of Mohaghegh Ardabili, Ardabil, Iran. The stock colony was maintained in transparent cylindrical rearing cages (40 cm in diameter × 80 cm in height) covered with fine mesh and equipped with two zipper openings to facilitate handling. Adult moths were provided with a 10% (w/v) honey-water solution supplied via cotton wicks. For experimental rearing, tubers from each potato cultivar were used as larval food sources. Approximately 50 newly emerged male-female pairs from the stock colony were transferred to transparent Plexiglas containers (30 × 20 × 10 cm) and reared separately on each cultivar. The breeding cage contained potato tubers as larval food and sand as a pupation medium. To minimize maternal and pre-adaptation effects, moth populations were maintained on each cultivar for two successive generations prior to experimentation. All experiments were conducted using individuals from the third generation. All rearing and experimental procedures were performed in a controlled-environment growth chamber set at 25 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 14:10 h (L: D) photoperiod, following the methodology described by Golizadeh & Esmaeili21 and Golizadeh et al.31.

Pre-adult life history of Phthorimaea operculella

To obtain age-synchronized eggs, 20 pairs of newly emerged male and female moths were placed in oviposition chambers consisting of transparent cylindrical Plexiglas containers (15 cm in diameter × 20 cm in height) covered with fine mesh. A sheet of filter paper was affixed to the inner surface of the mesh to serve as an oviposition substrate. Females deposited eggs on the underside of the filter paper, which was subsequently removed and used for further experiments. To evaluate egg developmental duration and survival on each potato cultivar, sections of filter paper containing 60 eggs were cut and placed individually in 9-cm Petri dishes. Each dish was covered with a lid fitted with fine mesh to ensure adequate ventilation and maintained under the previously described growth-chamber conditions. Egg hatch was monitored daily, and the number of hatched and unhatched eggs was recorded until all eggs had either hatched or collapsed.

For larval and pupal development assessments, each newly hatched larva was carefully transferred using a fine hair brush into a clear plastic cup (15 cm in diameter × 8 cm in height) containing a piece of potato tuber from the corresponding cultivar and a layer of sterilized sand to facilitate pupation. Cup lids were modified with fine mesh gauze to allow aeration. Because larvae typically exit the tuber prior to pupation, each cup was inspected daily to record larval development time, pupation, survival, and adult emergence.

Adult life history Variables of Phthorimaea operculella

Upon adult emergence, each male-female pair was transferred to a clear plastic cup (15 cm in diameter × 8 cm in height) covered with fine mesh gauze to ensure adequate ventilation. Adults were sexed based on external morphological characteristics: males are generally smaller than females and possess two to three dark spots on each forewing, whereas females exhibit more extensive spotting, often forming an X-shaped pattern37. A sheet of filter paper was placed on the mesh surface to serve as an oviposition substrate. To stimulate natural oviposition behavior, a fresh slice of potato tuber from the corresponding cultivar was positioned on the filter paper. The number of eggs laid on the filter paper was recorded daily, after which the paper was replaced with a new one. Eggs deposited on the inner walls of the cup were also counted and removed. Following each daily count, the adult pair was transferred to a clean cup to continue observations. Monitoring was continued until the death of both adults. All collected data were subsequently analyzed using the age-stage, two-sex life table method described by Chi & Liu38 and Chi39.

Nutritional indices of Phthorimaea operculella

The nutritional performance of third-instar P. operculella feeding on the six potato cultivars was evaluated using the gravimetric method of40. Larvae were randomly assigned to seven replicate groups, each consisting of 10 individuals, and all nutritional measurements were expressed on a dry-weight basis. Prior to the experiment, individual third-instar larvae were weighed and then transferred to Petri dishes containing fresh tuber slices from the corresponding potato cultivar. Larval body weight was recorded daily, both before and after feeding, over a 7-day period. Fresh tuber tissue was supplied daily, and the remaining food was collected and weighed at the end of each 24-h interval. Food consumption was calculated as the difference between the initial fresh weight of the tuber slice and the remaining weight after feeding. To convert fresh weights to dry weights, 20 samples of larvae and tuber tissue from each cultivar were weighed, oven-dried at 60 °C for 48 h, and reweighed to determine fresh-to-dry weight conversion ratios. Standard nutritional indices for P. operculella larvae were subsequently calculated using the formulas described by Waldbauer40.

Biochemical traits of potato cultivars

Phytochemical characteristics of the potato cultivars, including tuber protein content, starch content, total phenolic content, and total flavonoid content, were quantified to examine their relationships with the demographic parameters and nutritional indices of P. operculella. All biochemical assays were performed in triplicate for each cultivar.

Protein content was determined using the Bradford assay41, with bovine serum albumin serving as the calibration standard. For each cultivar, 200 mg of fresh tuber tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of distilled water. An aliquot of 100 µL of the homogenate was mixed with 3 mL of Bradford reagent, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Starch content was quantified following the method of Bernfeld42, using potato starch as the reference standard. For each cultivar, 200 mg of tuber tissue was homogenized in 35 mL of distilled water and heated to boiling. Subsequently, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of iodine reagent (0.2% KI and 0.02% I₂), and absorbance was recorded at 580 nm.

Total phenolic content was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method43. Briefly, 200 mg of tuber tissue was homogenized in 1.5 mL of 99% methanol. After centrifugation, the supernatant was evaporated to dryness and the residue was resuspended in 5 mL of distilled water. A 2.5-mL aliquot of the resulting extract was mixed with 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 5% Na₂CO₃ solution, and absorbance was measured at 725 nm. Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay44 and expressed as milligrams of catechin equivalents per milliliter of extract.

Data analysis

Life table parameters, including gross reproductive rate (GRR), net reproductive rate (R₀), intrinsic rate of increase (r), mean generation time (T), and finite rate of increase (λ), were estimated using the age-stage, two-sex life table approach implemented in TWOSEX-MSChart45. The variability of life table estimates was assessed using a bootstrap resampling procedure, and differences among the six potato cultivars were compared using paired bootstrap tests at a significance level of P < 0.0545. Developmental duration, immature survival, and fecundity data were used to calculate age-specific survivorship (lx) and age-specific fecundity (mx)46.

Prior to statistical analysis, nutritional indices and plant biochemicals datasets were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test in SPSS version 16.047. Nutritional indices of P. operculella and biochemical traits of the potato cultivars were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at the 5% significance level48. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate relationships among demographic parameters, nutritional indices of P. operculella, and biochemical characteristics of the potato cultivars. In addition, hierarchical cluster analysis was performed using Ward’s method in SPSS47 to generate a dendrogram illustrating the grouping of potato cultivars based on life table traits and nutritional indices of the pest.

Results

Pre-adult life history of Phthorimaea operculella

Potato cultivar significantly affected the developmental duration of immature stages of P. operculella (P < 0.05; Table 1). The egg incubation period was longest on the Fontane and Sprit (3.98 days) cultivars, both of which differed significantly from the remaining cultivars. Larval development was most prolonged on Jelly and Agria, whereas the shortest larval period was recorded on Fontane and Sprit. Among all cultivars, Agria supported the longest total pre-adult developmental duration (26.90 days). In contrast, immature survival did not differ significantly among cultivars and remained statistically similar across treatments (P > 0.05; Table 1).

Adult life history variables of Phthorimaea operculella

Significant differences among cultivars were detected in total pre-oviposition period (TPOP), oviposition period, fecundity, and adult longevity of both female and male P. operculella (P < 0.05; Table 2). The longest TPOP was observed on the Agria cultivar. Oviposition period varied markedly among treatments, with the longest duration recorded on Javid and the shortest on Agria. Fecundity was highest on Javid, Jelly, and Agria. The moths reared on Agria exhibited the shortest female and male longevity compared with those reared on the other cultivars (Table 2). Figure 1 presents the age-stage–specific survival rate (lx) and age-specific fecundity (lx) of P. operculella on the different potato cultivars. The lx curve was more extended on the Javid cultivar, indicating prolonged adult survival on this host. In contrast, the lx curve for Marfona was shorter, reflecting reduced adult longevity. Likewise, the mx peak on Marfona was narrower, indicating a shorter fecundity period for P. operculella on this cultivar (Fig. 1).

Nutritional indices of Phthorimaea operculella

Mean nutritional indices of P. operculella larvae feeding on the evaluated potato cultivars are presented in Table 4. The consumption index (CI) differed significantly among cultivars, with the highest CI recorded on Fontane and the lowest on Marfona (F5,36 = 4.64; P = 0.0023). Similarly, the relative consumption rate (RCR) was greatest on Fontane and lowest on Marfona (F5,36 = 4.64; P = 0.0023). The efficiency of conversion of ingested food (ECI) did not differ significantly among cultivars (F5,36 = 1.90; P = 0.1178), although the lowest ECI value was observed on Agria and the highest on Sprit. Relative growth rate (RGR) varied significantly among cultivars, with the highest values recorded on Fontane, Javid, and Sprit, and a significantly lower value on Agria (F5,36 = 4.50; P = 0.0027) (Table 4).

Biochemical features of potato cultivars

The protein, starch, total phenolic, and total flavonoid contents of the potato cultivars are summarized in Table 5. Significant differences among cultivars were detected for protein (F5,12 = 135.82; P < 0.0001), starch (F5,12 = 74.15; P < 0.0001), flavonoid (F5,12 = 9.73; P = 0.0007), and phenolic contents (F5,12 = 4.67; P = 0.0134). Among the cultivars, Marfona exhibited the highest protein content. The lowest starch content was also measured in the Fontane cultivar. Correlation and cluster analysis

The results of the correlation analysis between demographic parameters and nutritional indices of P. operculella and the biochemical traits of the potato cultivars are presented in Table 6. Developmental time was significantly and negatively correlated with protein content, while showing a significant positive correlation with starch content (P < 0.01). The net reproductive rate (R0) exhibited significant negative correlations with both protein and total phenolic contents and a significant positive correlation with starch content (P < 0.01). Similarly, the intrinsic rate of increase (r) was significantly and negatively associated with protein and phenolic contents of the cultivars (P < 0.01). The efficiency of conversion of ingested food (ECI) showed a significant positive correlation with protein content and a significant negative correlation with starch content (P < 0.01). In contrast, no significant correlations were detected between the relative growth rate (RGR) and any of the measured biochemical traits (P > 0.05). Likewise, flavonoid content was not significantly associated with any of the evaluated demographic or nutritional parameters of P. operculella (P > 0.05) (Table 6).

Discussion

Marked differences in the demographic parameters and nutritional performance of P. operculella were observed among the tested potato cultivars, reflecting substantial variation in host quality and suitability. These findings corroborate previous studies demonstrating that potato cultivars can differentially influence population growth, feeding efficiency, and developmental characteristics of the potato tuber moth31,49–52,24. Evaluating the susceptibility of potato cultivars to P. operculella thus provides critical insight into their relative host suitability and yields valuable information for the development of effective pest management strategies.

The present results indicated that larval development was significantly prolonged on the Agria and Jelly cultivars. Extended developmental duration is often indicative of suboptimal host quality and may be attributed to the presence of phytochemicals with antibiotic or antifeedant properties, or to a deficiency of essential nutrients required for optimal insect growth. In contrast, the shorter developmental periods recorded on Fontane, Marfona, and Sprit suggest that these cultivars provided more favorable nutritional conditions for larval development. Consistent with this interpretation, phytochemical analyses revealed that the Marfona cultivar contained the highest protein content, which may partially explain its suitability for supporting larval growth and development.

In general, adverse host plant traits, such as the presence of feeding deterrents, growth-inhibitory compounds, or imbalanced nutrient composition, can negatively affect insect feeding efficiency and growth rate. In addition, environmental factors, including temperature and plant growing conditions, may further modulate insect performance53. The interaction of these biotic and abiotic factors likely contributed to the observed variation in developmental duration and nutritional performance of P. operculella across the tested potato cultivars.

Correlation analyses indicated that the biochemical properties of potato cultivars, particularly protein and starch contents, significantly influenced the developmental performance of P. operculella. Macronutrients such as proteins and starch are widely recognized as key determinants of growth, development, and fitness in herbivorous insects54. Consistent with our findings, Mansouri et al.51 reported that essential nutritional components of potato, including starch and mineral content, were closely associated with variation in larval development time and reproductive output of P. operculella. Similarly, Zhang et al.24 demonstrated a positive relationship between larval survival of the potato tuber moth and the availability of essential nutrients in potato tubers. A comprehensive understanding of how nutritional factors and defensive metabolites interact to shape larval survival and performance can therefore provide deeper insight into host-insect interactions and the extent of crop damage caused by P. operculella24.

Host plant suitability plays a critical role in mitigating pest pressure. Potato cultivars with lower suitability can be recommended for cultivation in regions experiencing high P. operculella infestation, as they may reduce or delay population establishment and subsequent damage30. The present study demonstrates that potato cultivars influence not only the developmental duration of P. operculella but also its reproductive capacity. Although the moth successfully completed its life cycle on all tested cultivars, its biological performance varied markedly among host types. These results clearly indicate significant differences in cultivar susceptibility to attack by the potato tuber moth.

Life table parameters, particularly the intrinsic rate of increase (r) and the finite rate of increase (λ), are among the most informative indicators for evaluating host plant suitability for insect herbivores55. Higher r values generally reflect lower mortality, higher fecundity, and shorter developmental periods. In the present study, this pattern was most evident in P. operculella reared on the Javid cultivar, indicating that this cultivar provides the most favorable conditions for rapid population growth. In contrast, cultivars such as Agria and Marfona consistently reduced key demographic parameters, suggesting that they may serve as relatively stable sources of host plant lower suitability to P. operculella.

Correlation analyses further revealed that the intrinsic rate of increase (r) of P. operculella was negatively associated with the total phenolic content of potato cultivars. The relatively low phenolic concentration observed in the Javid cultivar may therefore partly explain the shorter developmental duration and higher fecundity of the moth on this host, which ultimately resulted in the highest r values. Nevertheless, a more detailed characterization of both primary and secondary metabolites in Javid tubers would be necessary to elucidate the specific biochemical mechanisms underlying these effects. In addition, the correlation results indicated a negative relationship between tuber protein content and both the net reproductive rate (R₀) and intrinsic rate of increase (r). This finding suggests that elevated protein levels in potato tubers may adversely affect the reproductive performance and population growth potential of P. operculella. Comparable results have been reported by Rahimi Namin et al.56, Majd-Marani et al.57, and Abedi et al.58, who demonstrated that protein-based defensive compounds particularly proteinase inhibitors can significantly impair pest growth, digestive efficiency, and overall nutritional performance. Such nutritional imbalances and defensive metabolites are known to disrupt larval development and reduce reproductive output59, thereby reducing the suitability of host plants for herbivorous insects60.

Several previous studies have evaluated life table parameters as indicators of resistance to the potato tuber moth across different potato cultivars. Golizadeh & Razmjou2 reported Burren as the most susceptible cultivar, whereas Agria exhibited the lowest level of suitability. Similarly, Golizadeh et al.31 compared the life history traits of P. operculella reared on leaves and tubers of multiple cultivars and identified Marfona as a relatively less susceptible host based on population growth metrics. Nouri Ganbalani et al.32 further demonstrated that Jelly sustained the lowest number of larval entry holes under greenhouse and field conditions, indicating its potential value for managing potato tuber moth infestations. Additional studies have consistently documented pronounced variation in the behavioral, developmental, and reproductive performance of P. operculella among potato cultivars30,21,51,24,61, supporting the conclusion that cultivar selection is a critical component of pest management.

The present study further indicates that less suitability to P. operculella in potato is not incidental but is functionally associated with specific cultivar traits, particularly primary and secondary metabolites. These biochemical characteristics significantly impaired pest development, fecundity, and nutrient utilization, as reflected by reduced intrinsic rate of increase (r), efficiency of conversion of ingested food (ECI), and relative growth rate (RGR)59,62. Host plant nutritional quality is therefore a key factor influencing the growth and population dynamics of P. operculella53,20.

Marked differences in larval nutritional responses among the six potato cultivars further demonstrate that tuber quality directly shaped larval performance. The highest RGR values were observed for larvae reared on Fontane, Javid, and Sprit, indicating greater biomass accumulation, likely driven by superior nutritional quality. In contrast, the lowest RGR value was recorded on Agria, potentially reflecting increased metabolic costs associated with detoxification and excretion of inhibitory compounds63. The particularly high nutritional value of Javid, characterized by elevated starch content and low total phenolic concentration, likely contributed to the enhanced RGR observed in larvae feeding on this cultivar. Moreover, larvae reared on Agria exhibited the lowest ECI values, suggesting a reduced ability to convert ingested food into body biomass. Reductions in nutritional indices generally constrain insect growth by limiting available energy and consequently reducing fecundity and longevity64.

Hierarchical cluster analysis grouped the six potato cultivars into two major clusters based on population growth parameters and feeding performance of P. operculella. Cultivars in cluster A (Fontane, Spirit, and Marfona) exhibited the lowest suitability for the pest, based on both demographic traits and nutritional indices. Sub-cluster B1, consisting of Agria and Jelly, included cultivars with intermediate suitability, whereas Sub-cluster B2 contained only Javid, which was identified as the most susceptible cultivar. These results suggest that Fontane, Sprit, and Marfona may possess traits associated with host plant unsuitability, such as specific secondary metabolites or enhanced defensive signaling pathways that adversely affect pest performance and could serve as valuable sources of reduced host suitability against P. operculella.

In conclusion, although P. operculella was able to complete its life cycle and reproduce on all six evaluated potato cultivars, its growth, survival, and reproductive performance varied significantly among hosts. The Javid cultivar was identified as the most susceptible, providing optimal nutritional conditions that supported rapid population growth of the potato tuber moth. Based on the present findings, Fontane, Sprit, and Marfona appear to be promising candidates for cultivation in regions with high P. operculella pressure; however, this hypothesis requires further validation under storage and field conditions using intact plants. Future research should further investigate cultivar-specific effects on the pest’s digestive enzyme activity and detoxification mechanisms. In addition, detailed assessments of structural traits, secondary metabolite profiles, host nutritional quality, and plant-derived inhibitors will be essential for developing effective cultivar-based strategies to minimize P. operculella infestations, particularly during storage.
