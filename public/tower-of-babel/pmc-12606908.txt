Clear cell renal cell carcinoma with collecting duct carcinoma transformation: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Mingwei Liang, Luona Li, Yalei Cao, Keqin Zhang, Qiang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Collecting duct carcinoma (CDC), a rare and aggressive renal malignancy originating from the medullary collecting ducts, often presents with nonspecific symptoms and rapid metastatic progression, yielding a dismal prognosis. The occurrence of a composite CDC and conventional renal cell carcinoma is rare in the literature. We report a 55-year-old female with clear cell renal cell carcinoma with collecting duct carcinoma transformation in the same kidney. Seven months postoperatively, the patient developed metastasis to the lungs. A literature review of 18 mixed CDC with other tumors highlights metastatic propensity (52% cases) and poor outcomes, emphasizing the need for molecular profiling to guide targeted therapies. This report contributes to the growing body of literature regarding the complexity of composite renal malignancies, furthering our understanding of clinical, imaging, and histological findings of this rare phenomenon.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) represents a heterogeneous group of malignancies with distinct histological subtypes, among which clear cell RCC (ccRCC) is the most prevalent, accounting for 70–80% of cases. In contrast, collecting duct carcinoma (CDC) is an exceedingly rare and aggressive subtype, constituting less than 2% of renal malignancies [1]. The simultaneous occurrence of two histologically distinct renal tumors within the same kidney is a relatively uncommon phenomenon. Such cases predominantly involve combinations of RCC with urothelial carcinoma (UC) or other rare subtypes, while CDC-associated composite tumors are even rarer, with fewer than 20 cases documented in the literature to date.

The pathogenesis of synchronous tumors remains speculative, encompassing theories of clonal divergence from a common progenitor cell, independent carcinogenic events, or collision tumors arising from adjacent microenvironments [2]. Clinically, these cases pose significant diagnostic challenges due to overlapping imaging features and histopathological complexity, often necessitating extensive immunohistochemical and molecular analyses for accurate classification. Furthermore, the co-existence of CDC with other malignancies may exacerbate therapeutic dilemmas and worsen clinical outcomes. This underscores the imperative for systematic reporting of such cases to refine diagnostic algorithms and explore tailored therapeutic strategies. Herein, we report a rare case of clear cell renal cell carcinoma with collecting duct carcinoma transformation in the same kidney and summarize 18 reported cases of CDC combined with other renal tumors, focusing on radiological and histopathological findings, clinical management, and prognostic implications.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old female patient was admitted to the hospital due to painless hematuria persisting for over five months. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a mass lesion with relatively an approximate size of 6.2 × 5.7 cm in the left kidney, with T1-weight imaging (T1WI) findings of hypointense signal and contrast-enhanced MRI of hyperintense signal, suggestive of renal carcinoma (Fig. 1). She had no dysuria and urinary tract irritation symptoms. There was no percussion pain and no pressure pain in both kidneys. The patient subsequently underwent a left radical nephrectomy under general anesthesia.

The resected left kidney tissue was longitudinally sectioned along the renal hilum. A solid, round mass measuring 5.5 × 4.5 cm was observed in the mid-to-lower pole of the kidney (Fig. 2). The cut surface exhibited a variegated appearance with gray-white, gray-yellow, and red areas. The mass had a medium texture with internal fibrous septations forming nodular patterns. The majority of the mass was well-demarcated with a fibrous pseudocapsule. However, the side adjacent to the renal sinus lacked a capsule and was indistinctly demarcated from the surrounding tissue. After sectioning and staining the specimen, histopathological analysis under light microscopy revealed high-grade renal cell carcinoma. Histologically, some areas (30%) exhibited features consistent with clear cell carcinoma, while other areas (70%) displayed with high grade transformation (the high-grade transformation being the collecting duct carcinoma component (Fig. 3). These two tumor components were intermixed without clear boundaries. The tumor was adjacent to the renal capsule, with local necrosis and involvement of the renal sinus fat, but did not extend beyond the perirenal fascia (T3a). No carcinoma was identified in the renal pelvic cells, ureteral margin, renal hilum vasculature, or left renal hilum lymph nodes. The case has been taken into routine follow up while pulmonary metastasis was radiographically identified 7 months after surgery. The patient was started on adjuvant pazopanib (800 mg orally once a day). Approximately 2 months after adjuvant pazopanib, the patient was switched to axitinib (5 mg orally twice a day) due to the progression of pulmonary metastasis. The patient has been treated with axitinib for 2 months and there is no further progression of the disease, 11 months after surgery. The patient provided informed consent for the use of their case data in this research.

Discussion

Renal collecting duct carcinoma (CDC), also known as Bellini duct carcinoma, is a rare but highly aggressive renal cancer, defined as a malignant epithelial tumor originating from the distal principal cells of the bellini collecting ducts in the renal medulla, which accounts for approximately 0.4%−2% of all renal cell carcinomas [3]. The characteristics of CDC, including its high malignancy, low incidence, propensity for metastasis, poor prognosis, and lack of effective treatment [3], distinguish it from other RCC subtypes, rendering it a challenging disease to manage. The coexistence of multiple synchronous primary neoplasms in the same kidney is exceedingly rare in the literature. The most frequently reported type is the combination of RCC and UC. When CDC coexists with other renal cancers in the same kidney, the imaging and pathological diagnosis of CDC becomes more challenging. To the best of our knowledge, 18 cases have been reported to date as coexistence of CDC and other tumors in the same kidney (Table 1). A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the PubMed database to identify all reported cases of synchronous renal collecting duct carcinoma (CDC) and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) up to February 2025. The search strategy utilized keywords including “collecting duct carcinoma” OR “Bellini duct carcinoma” AND “clear cell renal cell carcinoma” AND (“synchronous” OR “collision” OR “coexistence” OR " high grade transformation”), with a filter for “Case Reports.” The reference lists of retrieved publications were also manually screened for additional relevant studies. There were no restrictions on the publication date. Articles were excluded if they were not in English, did not involve CDC, or did not report a concurrent ccRCC, as they were deemed irrelevant to the focus of this study.

The clinical manifestations of CDC are nonspecific and primarily include back pain, palpable masses, hematuria, and symptoms related to tumor metastasis [19, 20]. CDC is characterized by rapid metastatic progression with extensive dissemination. Approximately 80% of patients eventually develop metastatic symptoms involving lymph nodes, lungs, liver, brain, and bones [20]. In our analysis of 18 cases of concurrent CDC and other renal tumors, metastatic signs were identified in only 9 cases, which is lower than the expected statistical value. This discrepancy may be attributed to insufficient follow-up data or the fact that some patients died shortly thereafter due to disease progression.

Histological, immunohistochemical, and genetic studies have confirmed significant differences in the origins of RCC and CDC. While most RCCs originate from the proximal tubules of the renal cortex, CDC shares a similar medullary origin with urothelial carcinoma and renal medullary carcinoma [18]. Embryologically, CDC and other RCC subtypes arise from the mesonephric blastema and metanephric blastema, respectively [15]. The simultaneous occurrence of CDC and RCC in the same kidney is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the synchronous occurrence of renal tumors: (1) Two distinct cell lines proliferate simultaneously, resulting in two phenotypically distinct tumors; (2) Two unrelated tumors arise from a common precursor stem cell; (3) Two separate tumors coincidentally occur at the same anatomical site [2]. However, the precise mechanism underlying the simultaneous occurrence of primary renal tumors remains unclear. In this case, no clear boundary was observed between ccRCC and CDC, which may align with the first two hypotheses. However, in most reported cases of composite renal carcinomas, the two tumors are typically located in different regions of the kidney with distinct boundaries, possibly corresponding to the third scenario. This observation suggests that when two tumors are in close proximity within the same kidney, the more aggressive tumor component may invade the other, resulting in a histological transformation of collision tumors.

Imaging features supportive of a CDC diagnosis include: CT findings of a medullary location and infiltrative growth pattern with weak and heterogeneous enhancement; ultrasound findings of hyperechogenicity; T2-weighted MRI findings of hypointensity; urography findings of distortion of the renal collecting system; and angiography findings of low vascularity. Lymph node involvement and metastasis are often present at diagnosis [21]. In large tumors, the imaging characteristics of CDC may be obscured by exophytic or expansive components, making differentiation from other RCCs challenging [21]. Macroscopically, CDC typically presents as a solid gray-white or gray-yellow mass, often accompanied by hemorrhage, necrosis, or cystic changes [20]. Microscopically, CDC tumor cells are arranged in irregular acinar, tubular, and papillary structures. The tumor cells are cuboidal, columnar, or hobnail-shaped, with pale eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm and distinct cell membranes [8]. Immunohistochemical markers specific to CDC include ulex europaeus-1 lectin (UEA-1), peanut agglutinin (PNA), and high molecular weight cytokeratins [18, 20, 22]. However, the reliability of immunohistochemical results in diagnosing CDC is limited. Immunohistochemistry also helps exclude other malignancies that may mimic CDC. For instance, an immunoprofile of PAX8(+)/p63(-) and/or GATA3(-) is highly suggestive of CDC, whereas PAX8(-)/p63(+) and/or GATA3(+) is indicative of urothelial carcinoma [20].

Composite malignant tumors may require combined treatment strategies effective against each histological component or targeting the most aggressive component. In most reported cases of composite tumors, CDC is the most malignant and aggressive component, necessitating focused therapeutic measures. However, due to the rarity and high malignancy of CDC, it remains challenging to establish a universally recognized and effective treatment strategy. According to the Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®) for Kidney Cancer, Version 3.2022, in previous cases of localized renal cell carcinoma (including CDC), the treatment for early-stage localized tumors has predominantly been partial or radical nephrectomy, selected on a case-by-case basis [23], but for CDC, the prognosis is dismal [24, 25]. For tumor metastasis and postoperative recurrence, the first-line strategy is participation in clinical trials. When this is not feasible, chemotherapy regimens may be adopted in conjunction with symptomatic and supportive care [23]. Tokuda et al. found that patients with metastatic CDC may exhibit partial responses to treatment regimens combining gemcitabine with cisplatin or carboplatin [26]. Based on a literature review, it has been suggested that sunitinib and sorafenib may exhibit a partial response in patients with poor performance [13]. Furthermore, CDC and urothelial carcinoma exhibit similar histological and clinical characteristics. Consequently, some researchers have suggested that chemotherapeutic agents effective against urothelial carcinoma may also benefit patients with metastatic CDC [27]. Based on the cases and reviews we reviewed, early surgical intervention and comprehensive treatment may improve survival rates to some extent, but the overall treatment efficacy remains suboptimal. Advanced technologies, such as array comparative genomic hybridization and next-generation sequencing, have enabled the extraction of genetic and molecular data from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. The research team led by professor Tan Jing first elucidated the molecular characteristics of CDC in the Asian population, demonstrating that the CDK9 inhibitor LDC000067 can specifically inhibit the growth of CDC tumor cells [28], offering a potential targeted treatment strategy for CDC patients.

Little literatures were found related with RCC combined with collecting duct carcinoma transformation. Reporting sarcomatoid change is recommended since this is an indicator of poor prognosis with potential treatment implications. And sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma has a dismal prognosis with a median survival of 4 to 9 months after diagnosis, and a 15% to 22% 5-year cancer specific survival [29]. Systemic therapies including VEGF-targeted therapies, have shown a modest benefit. Furthermore, interleukin-2 and interferon-alpha therapy has yielded better outcomes [30, 31]. Recent advances in molecular signatures are expected to help the development of effective treatment strategies. Higher expression of PD-1 and PD-L1 in RCC with sarcomatoid transformation suggests potential utility of a therapeutic blockade with PD-1/PD-L1 targeted immunotherapy [32]. Collecting duct carcinoma is a high-grade RCC accounting for less than 2% of renal tumors with poor prognosis [33]. The median survival of collecting duct carcinoma ranges from 10 to 13 months. Currently, the use of gemcitabine and either cisplatin or carboplatin, paclitaxel with carboplatin, or other platinum-based chemotherapies are considered first-line therapy for collecting duct carcinoma; which are also the basis for urothelial carcinomas [34]. Other studies suggest a potential benefit of immune check-point inhibitors, including nivolumab and ipilimumab, in patients with metastatic collecting duct carcinoma [35–37].

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. The single-case design inherently restricts the generalizability of our findings. The relatively short postoperative follow-up period (11 months) precludes a comprehensive assessment of long-term oncological outcomes. Additionally, the unavailability of extensive molecular profiling data limits our exploration into the potential pathogenic mechanisms underlying this rare tumor coexistence. Multi-center studies are warranted to validate and extend our observations.

Conclusion

In summary, the simultaneous presence of CDC and ccRCC in the same kidney is exceedingly rare. CDC is characterized by its rarity, high metastatic potential, and poor prognosis, with no effective treatment currently available. Despite differences in tissue origin and common sites of occurrence, the coexistence of CDC and RCC remains possible. Therefore, conducting detailed preoperative imaging and pathological examinations in patients with atypical renal malignancies can improve the detection rate of CDC, facilitate targeted clinical interventions for metastasis, and provide more cases for further research into effective adjuvant treatments for CDC.
