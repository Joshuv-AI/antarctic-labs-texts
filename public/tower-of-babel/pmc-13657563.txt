Frailty and sleep quality in elderly living in old age homes of Kathmandu Valley: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ronit Zoowa, Dipana Pradhan, Ravi Shah, Sabin Bahadur Zoowa, Naresh Manandhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Frailty is a clinical syndrome characterized by age-related decline in strength, endurance, and physiological function that increases vulnerability to adverse health outcomes. Sleep disturbances are also common among older adults, particularly in long-term care settings, and may contribute to worsening frailty and reduced quality of life. However, limited evidence exists regarding the relationship between frailty and sleep quality among institutionalized elderly populations in Nepal.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 219 individuals aged ≥60 years residing in old-age homes in Kathmandu Valley. Participants were selected through convenience sampling. Frailty was assessed using the Groningen Frailty Indicator, and sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.Results:The mean age of participants was 73.81 years (range 60–99 years), and 62.6% were female. Frailty was present in 75.3% (n = 163) of participants, with prevalence increasing from 63.5% in the 60–69 age group to 95.2% among those aged 90–99 years. Poor sleep quality was observed in 85.8% (n = 188) of participants. Among frail individuals, 95.8% had poor sleep quality. A significant association was found between frailty and poor sleep quality (r = 0.497, P < 0.001) and in adjusted analysis poor sleep quality was strongly associated with frailty (AOR = 22.58, 95% CI: 8.10–62.94, P < 0.001) .Conclusion:These findings highlight a strong association between frailty and poor sleep quality among elderly living in old age homes in Kathmandu valley, underscoring the need for integrated screening and management of sleep disturbances within frailty care.

Introduction

Frailty is recognized as a clinical syndrome in which age-related declines in strength, endurance, and physiological function make an individual more vulnerable to adverse health outcomes[1]. It affects elderly populations in both community-dwelling and long-term care settings and is driven by the cumulative effects of chronic diseases, reduced physical activity, and multisystem deterioration[2]. Frailty in older adults has been linked to a variety of negative outcomes, including declines in daily functioning, a higher risk of falls, more frequent hospital visits, greater mortality, and poorer quality of life[3].

Sleep disturbances, including poor sleep quality and shorter sleep duration, are common among older adults in long-term care settings; one study reported that up to 95.8% of residents experience poor sleep, which has been associated with increased frailty and reduced quality of life[4]. With the global population of adults aged 60 and over projected to reach 1.4 billion by 2030, and with most of the increase occurring in low- and middle-income countries, health and social systems face growing challenges in addressing and supporting aging populations[5].

Despite the adverse health consequences of inadequate restorative sleep, sleep problems in older adults are frequently undiagnosed and untreated, with surveys suggesting that many older adults perceive poor sleep as a normal part of aging[6]. Similarly, many healthcare providers lack awareness of frailty, including its screening, assessment, and management[7]. Although sleep disturbances and frailty have been linked globally among older adults, few studies have examined this relationship in Nepal. Understanding frailty and sleep disturbances among older adults residing in Nepalese old-age homes can improve health outcomes, inform care strategies, and provide regional data to complement global evidence. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of frailty and sleep disturbances and examine their association among elderly residents of old-age homes in the Kathmandu Valley. This cross-sectional study has been reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines[8].

Methodology

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among individuals aged 60 years and above residing in various old-age homes within the Kathmandu Valley, following ethical approval from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of Nepalese Army Institute of Health Sciences (Reg. No. 856). Verbal permission was initially obtained from the administration of the old-age homes by telephone, followed by written consent obtained in person. Data were collected from November 2023 to February 2024. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Participants and sample size

A sample size of 219 individuals was determined using the formula n = Z2pq/e2, where p is the prevalence of frailty (0.71) among residents of old age homes in Kathmandu Valley, as per a previous study[9]; q is 1 − p; e is the margin of error (0.06); and Z is the standard normal deviate corresponding to a 95% confidence level (1.96). Participants were recruited from old-age homes in Kathmandu Valley that granted permission for data collection. Following approval from facility administrators, residents were approached individually, and those who met the eligibility criteria and provided written informed consent were included consecutively until the required sample size was achieved. Convenience sampling was used because a comprehensive sampling frame of institutionalized older adults could not be established prior to recruitment, and recruitment depended on administrative approval from participating facilities and on the willingness of residents to participate. Individuals with major disabilities (e.g., being bedridden or having speech impairments that precluded effective communication), inability to comprehend the questionnaire or provide meaningful responses, or severe acute illness that interfered with participation were identified during the consent process and excluded from the study.

Variables and assessment tools

Frailty was measured using the Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), which comprises 15 questions covering eight components: mobility, vision, hearing, nutrition, co-morbidity, cognition, psychosocial status, and physical fitness, in which responses were recorded as “Yes/Sometimes” with a score of 1 or “No” with a score of 0, with scores reversed for items 1 to 4; for the cognition-related question, “Yes” was scored as 1 and “No/Sometimes” as 0; self-rated physical fitness was scored as 1 for responses ranging from 0–6 and 0 for responses ranging from 7 to 10; a total score of 4 or more was considered frail, while a score of less than 4 was considered non-frail[9,10].

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a self-rated questionnaire evaluating sleep disturbances over the previous month, which consists of 19 items grouped into seven components – subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction – with a global score obtained by summing the component scores, where a score of 5 or more indicates poor sleep quality and a score of less than 5 indicates good sleep quality; it has a diagnostic sensitivity of 89.6% and a specificity of 86.5%, with a kappa value of 0.75 (P ≤ 0.001)[11,12].

The GFI and PSQI were selected for their brief, multidimensional, and internationally validated nature, making them suitable for the assessment of frailty and sleep quality among institutionalized older adults.

Quality control and outcome analysis

A pre-test was conducted among 22 elderly participants (10% of the calculated sample size) in one old-age home. Following the pre-test, the study team reviewed challenges encountered during questionnaire administration, particularly those related to language and participant comprehension, and agreed on a uniform approach to administering the questionnaire, which was followed throughout the data collection process. Regular debriefing meetings were held after data collection at each old-age home to discuss challenges encountered and ensure consistency in questionnaire administration. The pre-test participants were excluded from the final analysis. As many participants had limited literacy, questionnaires were administered in an interview format, and responses were recorded. All questionnaires were reviewed immediately upon completion, and any incomplete responses were clarified during the interview. All participants completed both assessment tools; therefore, a complete-case analysis was performed.

The collected data were entered and analyzed using SPSS software, version 16. The data obtained were presented as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to assess the association between frailty and sleep quality, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Binary logistic regression was performed with frailty status as the dependent variable and sleep quality, age, and sex as independent variables. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and reported.

Frailty

Among the surveyed participants, 75.3% (163) were found to be frail (GFI score ≥ 4), and the remaining 24.7% (56) were non-frail. The prevalence of frailty increased with age, rising from 63.5% in those aged 60–69 years to 95.2% in participants aged 90–99 years. Frailty was more prevalent among females, with 83.2% of sampled participants being frail, which is higher than among male participants, with a prevalence of 62.2%. Furthermore, 59.4% of frail participants reported consistent loneliness, 50.3% reported feeling persistently down or depressed, and 48.5% regularly felt anxious or nervous during the past month. Additionally, 46.1% reported difficulties in daily life due to impaired hearing and 67.9% reported difficulties due to impaired vision.

Sleep quality

Poor sleep quality was found in 85.8% (188) of the total respondents (PSQI score ≥ 5). Among the participants, 47% reported taking more than 60 minutes to fall asleep, while 41.1% reported sleeping less than 5 hours in the past month. The most common cause of nocturnal awakening was the need to use the bathroom, which was reported by 70.3% of participants three or more times a week, followed by having bad dreams (47.5%), experiencing pain (47.5%), coughing (42.5%), feeling too hot (37%), feeling too cold (31.5%), and breathing difficulties while asleep (35.1%). However, 90.9% of respondents did not use medication to aid sleep. Daytime disturbances were also prevalent: 56.2% of respondents reported difficulty staying awake during the day, and 63.9% reported a lack of motivation to complete regular tasks. 84.1% of male participants and 86.9% of female participants experienced poor sleep quality. Across age groups, the majority of participants reported poor sleep quality, ranging from 82.7% in individuals aged 70–79 years to 95.2% in individuals aged 90–99 years. Association between sleep quality and frailty

Among the participants identified as frail (GFI score ≥ 4), 95.8% (158) had poor sleep quality (PSQI score ≥ 5). Nearly half of them (47.9%) slept fewer than 5 hours per night, and 53.9% took more than 1 hour to fall asleep. The majority (82.4%) woke up between 4:00 and 6:00 a.m., and only 33.3% managed to stay awake during the day without difficulty in the past month. Night awakenings were also common among the frail participants, with 57.6% experiencing them at least 3 times per week. Sleep quality among frail participants, compared with non-frail participants, is shown in Figure 3. A significant association was found between frailty and sleep quality (P < 0.001), with a moderate positive correlation (r = 0.497), indicating that higher frailty scores tended to be associated with poorer sleep quality. Binary logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age and sex, demonstrated that poorer sleep quality was independently associated with frailty. Participants with poor sleep quality had 22.58 times the odds of being frail compared with those with good sleep quality (AOR = 22.58, 95% CI: 8.10–62.94, P < 0.001). Age was also significantly associated with frailty, with each additional year increasing the odds of frailty by 6% (AOR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.11, P = 0.008). In addition, male participants had 67.9% lower odds of frailty compared with female participants (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.15–0.70, P = 0.004).

Discussion

As populations age worldwide, the health and well-being of older adults have become a growing public health concern, with frailty and poor sleep emerging as contributors to morbidity and reduced quality of life[4,13]., This study demonstrates a high prevalence of frailty (75.3%) and poor sleep quality (85.8%) among elderly residents of old-age homes in the Kathmandu Valley, with a significant positive association between frailty and poor sleep quality. These findings align with global evidence indicating that frailty and sleep disturbances frequently co-occur in older adults, particularly those in long-term care settings such as old-age homes. A study by Xue-Hui Sun et al in community-dwelling elders demonstrated that poor sleep quality and long sleep duration were significantly associated with increased odds of frailty and pre-frailty, with stepwise increases in sleep problems across frailty severity[14]. A cross-sectional study by Siyue Liu et al among nursing home residents in Hunan, China, found that 60.9% of residents reported poor sleep quality and that poor sleep and longer nap duration were associated with significantly higher odds of cognitive frailty (OR: 2.53 and OR: 1.77, respectively)[15].

Disturbances in circadian rhythms in older adults may increase the risk of frailty by impairing metabolic function and promoting inflammation, both of which are key contributors to the development of frailty [16]. Sleep disturbances may also exacerbate fatigue, depression, and cognitive impairment, thereby accelerating the progression of frailty in older adults[17]. Poor sleep quality has been linked to chronic inflammation, hormonal dysregulation, impaired immune function, and reduced physical performance, all of which are key components of frailty[18]. Frailty itself may adversely affect sleep quality, as increasing frailty has been shown to disrupt sleep rhythms and exacerbate fatigue and reduced activity levels, and worsen adverse living conditions that contribute to poorer sleep in older adults[19].

Older adults in nursing homes often face limited social interactions, prolonged separation from family, and higher levels of loneliness and depression, which may contribute to increased frailty compared with community dwellers. Institutionalized older adults are more prone to frailty because of physical inactivity, chronic disease burden, poor sleep quality, and psychosocial stressors, which likely contribute to the higher prevalence of frailty in nursing home settings compared with community-dwelling older adults[2]. A study by Shah et al showed that the elderly residing in old-age homes in Nepal demonstrated higher frailty scores compared with community-dwelling peers, primarily driven by an increased prevalence of chronic diseases, polypharmacy, suboptimal lifestyle behaviors, and lower satisfaction with the residential environment[9]. A similar study by Lorber et al showed that the prevalence of frailty was higher among older adults in nursing homes (58.5%) compared with those living in the community (36.5%), and poor sleep quality was reported by 95.8% of nursing home residents and 76.0% of community-dwelling older adults, with significant positive correlations between poor sleep quality and frailty in both settings[4].

While the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and precludes determination of the temporal direction between frailty and sleep quality, and convenience sampling may restrict generalizability and potentiate selection bias, several additional limitations should be considered. Individuals who were unable to participate meaningfully in the interview because of major disabilities, inability to comprehend the questionnaire, or severe acute illness were excluded from the study. This may have led to an underestimation of frailty prevalence, particularly among residents with greater functional or communication limitations. Sleep quality was assessed solely using the self-reported PSQI; objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography were not used, which may have introduced reporting bias. Although internationally validated assessment tools were used, cultural adaptation in institutionalized Nepalese elderly populations remains limited. Addressing frailty and sleep disturbances in tandem is a critical avenue for enhancing healthy aging in older adults. Multidomain educational programs that incorporate physical activity, lifestyle education, and cognitive training have been shown to improve sleep efficiency and reduce sleep disturbances in frail older adults[20]. However, despite the strong link between poor sleep quality and frailty, most geriatric care programs currently lack sleep-related screening and interventions, underscoring the need to integrate sleep assessment and improvement strategies into routine care for frail older adults[21].

Conclusion

This study highlights a strong link between frailty and poor sleep quality among elderly residents of old age homes in the Kathmandu Valley, with 75.3% identified as frail and 85.8% experiencing sleep disturbances, highlighting the need to include sleep assessment and management as part of routine frailty care, particularly in settings where psychosocial factors such as loneliness and anxiety may exacerbate vulnerability. As the elderly population continues to grow, meeting the health needs of institutionalized older adults is increasingly important. Future research should examine causal relationships and develop targeted interventions that address both frailty and sleep disturbances concurrently. Addressing these interconnected issues has the potential to improve health outcomes, enhance quality of life, and support healthy aging.
