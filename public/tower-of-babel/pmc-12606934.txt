The role of AI and personalized medicine in healthcare: balancing technological advancements and the art of medicine
Authors: Anette Lykke Hindhede, Vibeke Harms Andersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, global political efforts have increasingly focused on the transformative potential of personalized medicine to prevent disease, improve health outcomes, and lower treatment costs. This has created at pressing demand for healthcare professionals to deepen their understanding and utilizing health data and its application in clinical decision-making. In response, a new master’s program in personalized medicine has been launched across five universities in Denmark. This study, drawing on the sociology of expertise, investigates how educators and master’s students in the program perceive and engage with the knowledge necessary for integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into patient treatment and care. Our findings suggest a prevailing belief among educators in the potential of AI and big data to revolutionize healthcare by personalizing treatment options and improving diagnostic processes. Course participants generally acknowledged the ethical responsibility to leverage the vast amounts of data provided by patients to enhance treatment outcomes. However, some feared it might undermine the clinical insight they had gained through years of experience – insights they described as ‘the art of medicine.’ They navigated their agency and autonomy in the human-algorithm collaboration by framing algorithms as supportive additions to human judgment rather than replacements for it. In conclusion, this paper advocates for a more integrated approach that combines big data analysis with practical experience in healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07771-x.Keywords: Expertise, AI, Knowing, Personalized medicine, Precision medicine, Professionalism, Diagnostics, Clinical decision-making, Clinical judgment

Introduction

Diagnostics have witnessed a significant transformation over time, progressing from qualitative assessments to binary and, more recently, quantitative measurements [1]. This transformation has been greatly influenced by the inclusion of artificial intelligence (AI) into various clinical contexts, making it a significant aspect of modern medicine [2–4]. Although considerable knowledge exists regarding population-level risk factors for common diseases, identifying the specific factors that contributes to an individual’s disease remains challenging. Current diagnostic criteria and treatment evaluations, which are predominantly symptom-based, can hinder accurate disease characterization and lead to errors in medical decisions and health recommendations [5].

These challenges have led to the emergence of personalized medicine; also called precision medicine, which aims to improve accuracy and reduce errors in medical decisions and health recommendations. By utilizing data-driven algorithms, personalized medicine seeks to automate cognitive, discretionary tasks traditionally performed by humans [6]. This automation includes disease diagnosis, prediction, prognosis, and the selection of the most suitable treatments tailored to individual patient characteristics [7]. The term ‘diagnosis’, derived from the Greek ‘diagignoskein,’ means to distinguish or discern, highlighting the importance of careful examination [8]. However, cognitive biases and decision fatigue can contribute to suboptimal clinical decision, potentially leading to misdiagnoses or delayed diagnoses. For instance, studies have shown that surgeons tend to perform fewer operations towards the end of their shifts, and office-based physicians order fewer tests later in the day [9–11]. Algorithms analyse data consistently, employing standardised and structured readings without experiencing reader fatigue. As a result, using AI to replace or support humans to minimize biases and decrease the likelihood of errors in decision-making appears to be a sensible choice. However, for which tasks is this appropriate? Should AI be limited to simple, low-risk, high-volume tasks? Furthermore, what happens if the system itself makes mistakes?

Historically, in the late 1950s, AI systems employed rule-based approaches to capture expert reasoning and match patient traits with standard disease profiles [12]. Over time, the focus has transitioned from data management to providing tailored recommendations bases on the analysis of large datasets [13–16]. While current AI advancements aim to support personalized medicine by tailoring interventions to the unique patient characteristics [17, 18], they also raise concerns about the diminishing role of healthcare providers and the erosion of clinical decision-making autonomy. Some experts perceive AI systems as a threat to medical expertise [19, 20]. Additionally, experts may struggle to decide when to rely on or disregard algorithmic guidance, which could be systematically biased in ways that are not apparent to them [21]. A variety of examples have emerged demonstrating biases related to race, ethnicity, gender, disability, and other biases in the application of AI healthcare [21].

Despite the growing implementation of AI in healthcare, there is a limited understanding of how these technologies are transforming knowledge acquisition and its dissemination among professional groups [22].

Our research has long focused on the implications of new technologies for work practices [23], for the organization of work, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the relationships between professionals, technologies, and their current context [24–26]. We recently had the opportunity to observe a master’s program in Personalized Medicine, which is offered by five Danish universities and is targeted toward professionals with extensive experience in healthcare, sciences, and public sectors. Our focus was to investigate how instructors and course participants engage in discussions about AI and personalized medicine. We were particularly interested in exploring their reflections regarding algorithmic guidance in decision-making and how they articulated their understanding of integrating this guidance with their clinical judgment. What thoughts and considerations did they express, both individually and in group discussions during the course? This inquiry has led us to formulate the following research question:

Which opportunities and challenges does the application of AI present for healthcare professionals in clinical decision-making processes, particularly in relation to their scientific domain and understanding of clinical expertise?

Theorizing professions and expertise

Profession theories by e.g. Abbott [27] and Friedson [28] view expertise as a trait that actors leverage in their quest for recognition and jurisdiction. The STS perspective contrasts by emphasizing the evolving nature of expertise and the material and discursive conditions that shape its creation and dissemination within networks. In the work of Collins and Evans [29], ‘Rethinking Expertise’, the authors suggest two categories of expertise: contributory expertise and interactional expertise relative to a scientific domain. Contributory expertise is grounded in practical, embodied knowledge that is gained through socialization and hands-on experience within a specific field. It requires immersive socialization, which involves not only learning explicit knowledge but also acquiring tacit knowledge—skills, know-how, and intuitions that are not easily verbalized. This socialization process requires direct, often bodily, engagement within a community of practice [30]. To achieve contributory expertise, individuals must engage in the activity itself and thus develop the ability to contribute to the field through practice. Collins argues that the real challenge lies not in tacit knowledge but in how certain forms of knowledge are made explicit. For instance, we expect experts to be able to clearly explain and justify their diagnoses and clinical decisions.

Interactional (or participatory) expertise, on the other hand, does not require hands-on experience in the field. Instead, it can be obtained through extensive reading, listening, and intellectual engagement with the community of practitioners, without necessarily participating in the actual practice. This expertise allows individuals to understand the language and concepts of the field well enough to discuss it knowledgeably, even if they cannot perform the tasks themselves. Collins and Evans suggest that interactional expertise is valuable for individuals who need to communicate or mediate between expert practitioners and other audiences, such as policymakers or patients.

In his argument for a Sociology of Expertise, Eyal [31] stresses the importance of distinguishing between expertise and individuals identified as experts, as this distinction sheds light on the collaborative processes through which expertise is generated within knowledge networks. Even individuals without credentials can play vital roles within these networks as when they integrate amateur or non-traditional contributors this can bolster their credibility because the collaborative nature of expertise is enhanced rather than diminished by their involvement.

Drawing on the work of Collins and Evans, Eyal [32] argues that decision-making in healthcare should primarily involve a core set of experts, including ones without formal credentials. However, professional movements like Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) prioritize explicit, standardised procedures and objective, numerical decision rules, often without enhancing public involvement in defining these roles. This creates a dichotomy between expert systems and a core group of experienced experts, leading to a paradox of medicine of being both an ‘art’ and a ‘science’ [33].

This paradox reflects the complexities involved in medical practice and suggests a need for a more nuanced understanding of clinical decision-making. Various authors draw on Aristotle’s concept of phronesis, or ‘practical wisdom’, to navigate the morally complex situations that arise in medicine [33–35]. Montgomery [33] defines practical wisdom as the ability of doctors to choose the most appropriate actions based on the contextual nature of knowledge. In this context, Bleakley et al. [36] refer to this quality as ‘connoisseurship’, highlighting the aesthetic sensibility that shapes clinical practices. Chin-Yee and Upshur [37] label this the’ art of medicine’, emphasising the skill and intuition physicians bring to their clinical practice. Central to this discussion is the concept of clinical judgment, defined as ‘the ability to form diagnoses, make prognoses, and choose treatments that benefit the patient’ [38]. Montgomery [33] highlights its complexity, noting that effective clinical judgment combines clinical experience, analytical thinking, awareness of biases, and effective communication - all rooted in an understanding the patient’s unique context. Experienced clinicians often rely on intuition or ‘gut feelings’, developed through years of practice and a deep understanding of clinical patterns [39]. Furthermore, Eyal [31] argues that effective clinical judgment requires integrating the patient’s perspective by listening to their values and preferences and incorporating this information into the decision-making process.

Methods

The empirical context for this study is Denmark, recognised as one of the most data-intensive healthcare systems globally [40]. The country has a longstanding tradition of collecting extensive information on its citizens, facilitated by a civil registration system that links data from birth to death. Established in 2019, The Danish National Genome Center is a government agency that acts as an authority within the Danish healthcare system. Its primary objective is to enhance diagnostics and enable more targeted treatments for patients through the use of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) [41]. In addition to this, the newly introduced master’s program in personalized medicine is designed for professionals in the healthcare sector. This continuing education program aims to equip course participants with a comprehensive understanding of how various types of complex health data are generated and utilized in the development of personalized medicine. The program’s homepage states that students ‘will acquire essential tools for structuring, analysing, and interpreting these data’ [42]. The curriculum covers the role of health data in the clinical decision-making process, including applications in prevention, early disease detection, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up care, while also addressing the ethical, legal, and organizational aspects of a data-driven healthcare system.

This study explores the ways in which course organizers, teachers, and course participants articulate their understanding of the integration of AI in their decision-making processes.

To answer our research question, we conducted a document analysis of official documents related to the programme’s establishment, study regulations, and the educational platform of the host university. This analysis helped establish the intended curricular framings and objectives regarding AI and personalized medicine.

A total of 24 qualitative interviews were then conducted with course participants, teachers, and a course organiser. This included nine interviews lasting approximately one hour each, conducted based on a semi-structured interview guide at the workplace of the respondent, as well as 15 in situ interviews lasting 5 to 15 min each. The interview guides used in this study were developed for this study (see supplementary file).

The course participants came from a diverse range of professional backgrounds in healthcare, including physicians, pharmacists, chemists, molecular biologists, and data analysts; some worked at various hospitals across different regions of Denmark, while others were employed in the medical industry. The group predominantly consisted of physicians specializing in oncology. Specifically, the seven in-depth interviews conducted with course participants included two bioanalysts, one chemist, one pharmacist, two senior consultants, and one specialist consultant. Both the organizer and the teachers were also senior consultants.

Their participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to their involvement.

Declaration of Helsinki

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [43]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and measures were taken to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of their responses. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (Journal number P-2022-100).

The interviews aimed to elicit detailed reflections on their experiences and insights regarding the role of AI in clinical decision-making. Further, we engaged in direct observations by participating in two modules of the master’s program, totaling 28 h. During this time, we interacted with both instructors and course participants, allowing us to gain insights into the dynamics of the classroom discussions and the educational methods employed. The observations provided an opportunity to witness firsthand how course content was delivered, how course participants engaged with the material and with each other, and how teachers facilitated discussions on personalized medicine and AI.

Additionally, in situ interviews based on an ultra-short, partially structured interview guide were conducted during breaks in the teaching sessions. This allowed us to capture reflections on the topics covered in the lectures. This approach gave participants the opportunity to articulate their thoughts and insights in real-time, enriching our understanding of the ongoing discourses.

The collected data from document analysis, interviews, observations, and in situ interviews were coded following Timmermans and Tavory’ s argument of being ‘an informed theoretical agnostic’ [44]. Anchored in the sociology of expertise as outlined earlier [29, 31, 45], we employed a thematic analysis strategy to identify meaningful themes in our material. We began by reading through the material identify recurring themes relevant our research question. Next, we coded the material and organized the preliminary themes into a series of overarching categories, which we then refined and labelled with meaningful headings that structured our analysis. The final analysis themes we used to answer our research question were as follows: ‘Understanding expertise in the context of AI,’ ‘The art of medicine meets AI: addressing the trust issue’, and ‘Ethical implications of AI integration: who is accountable?’

Thematic analysis

Understanding expertise in the context of AI

The course webpage emphasizes that the master’s programme in personalized medicine is for individuals who are fully educated and possess years of professional experience healthcare, sciences, public sectors. With a focus on enhancing competencies in health utilization and personalized medicine, in the program it is stated that it specifically targets those looking to improve their professional communication and collaboration skills within their respective fields. The webpage’s messaging also highlights the practical value of the course content, stating that participants will gain competences in ‘applying complex health data to real-world clinical scenarios’. By emphasising the program’s collaborative nature and networking opportunities, it suggests the potential development of interactional expertise among participants, as interactional experts can contribute to scientific areas where they lack contributory expertise- for example, a physician collaborating with a programmer to develop algorithms. At the same time, the structured curriculum, which includes both mandatory and elective courses alongside a master’s project, appears designed to comprehensively equip participants with contributory knowledge and skills.

During our interviews with teachers, as well as through the course description and our observations of lectures, we found an overall strong belief in the transformative potential of integrating AI into medical practice. One teacher described AI advancements as ‘powerful tools capable of revolutionizing healthcare’ by providing tailored treatment options and improving diagnostics. This teacher clearly illustrates the role of interactional expertise in advocating for the value of a new form of contributory expertise [29, 73].

In the interview with the course organizer, he noted that the core idea behind predictive algorithms is that understanding the likelihood of a future outcome can positively influence present decisions. He explained, ‘Algorithms can quickly screen a large number of blood test results, allowing clinicians to focus solely on atypical results.’ In discussing how AI could reshape the Danish healthcare system, two key arguments were prominent: the immediate potential for computers to solve problems typically addressed by healthcare professionals, and the growing complexity and volume of health data generated by new technologies. Throughout the interview, he consistently portrayed AI as a critical decision-support system that empowers medical professionals to optimize diagnoses and treatment. In his opinion, this could alleviate the burden on healthcare providers, as ‘automation enhances their capabilities and allows them to allocate time more effectively’.

One of the course participants, an oncologist, mentioned during a break in the course ‘Evidence-Based Clinical Application of Personalized Medicine’ that, as the teacher stated, ‘The cognitive bandwidth required to process vast amounts of information exceeds human capacity,’ highlighting the need for AI to help daily clinical decision-making practices. In one of the lectures the teacher emphasized the necessity of integrating automated decision support tools into healthcare, advocating for a unified data collection approach to avoid ‘data silos.’ The teacher stressed the importance of having all relevant information accessible through clinical applications to ease decision-making by consolidating critical data. According to Busnatu et al. [46], clinical applications refer to the practical implementation of medical knowledge in healthcare settings for diagnosing and managing patient conditions. This consistent emphasis on AI and personalized medicine as supportive tools for healthcare professionals during the diagnostic process, with ultimate decision-making authority retained by doctors aligns with the concept of contributory expertise, where healthcare professionals rely on their specialized knowledge and judgment rather than fully deferring to automated systems. One teacher articulated this point by stating, ‘. The computer simply tells us what we have agreed upon in advance; if this situation occurs, then this is what should be done,’ highlighting that the medical professional’s expertise remains central to the decision-making process, even when AI is involved. By framing the algorithm as ‘just a tool’, clinicians aim to distance themselves from the discomfort associated with losing epistemic authority and the accompanying uncertainty about ‘who is really making the decisions’.

A course participant, an oncologist, expressed a desire for more individualized treatment options in her daily work, noting that current practices involve routinely screening all patients every three months. She highlighted the potential for algorithms that analyse specific patient data to inform her clinical decisions. Her concern points to a cognitive competence originally held by her as an expert but now being distributed among a constellation of humans and instrumentation. According to Eyal [31, 32], this shift should not be viewed as a de-skilling but rather a redistribution of expertise.

Another course participant, a chemist, further added to this discussion by distinguishing between machine learning and deep-web algorithms. He emphasized the importance of understanding the reasoning behind algorithmic outputs, stating, ‘Whether we completely trust blindly, or whether we stick to algorithms that can tell us a bit more about why it is what it is.’ This concern addresses unpredictability, fear of unknown errors and the ‘black box’ nature of AI algorithms, which can foster mistrust among health professionals. He noted the difference between using algorithms as assisted medical advice versus relying on them as standalone devices for tasks like patient triaging without immediate clinical supervision.

This recognition points to the need for interactional expertise, where healthcare professionals are equipped to interpret and critically assess the decisions made by AI systems. Such expertise is essential for maintaining oversight of technological tools and integrating them meaningfully into clinical practice. Additionally, it highlights Collin’s and Evan’s distinction between participation in decision-making and participation in knowledge creation. This underscores the role of healthcare providers not only in interpreting AI outputs but also in contributing to a broader understanding of the technology’s implications in their field.

The art of medicine meets AI: addressing the trust issue

Through our interviews, the challenge of reducing epistemic uncertainty in clinical decision-making emerged as a key topic. Participants discussed difficulties in applying evidence to individual patients, navigating conflicting information, and determining their level of confidence in medical conclusions. Many course participants expressed insecurities regarding their ability to effectively use AI in the practice. They referred to their professional knowledge as ‘the Art of Medicine’, emphasising that it rooted in years of clinical experience and peer discussions. A common concern was that increasing reliance on AI could undermine their clinical judgment, potentially leading to a decline in the maintenance of their skills.

Participants worried that algorithms might challenge or even override their expertise, especially given the limited time available for patient interactions. One participant remarked, ‘Today, the doctor functions as a sort of algorithm, monitoring complex data and providing an answer, yet often has a few minutes with each patient.’ This scenario raises significant issues about contributory expertise, as clinicians feel pressured to synthesize information quickly, which may hinder their ability to apply specialized knowledge effectively.

During a class discussion on the trustworthiness of algorithm conclusion, a participant remarked, ‘But you can challenge the algorithms.’ This illustrates a critical engagement with the technology, revealing an aspect of interactional expertise where physicians must possess the ability to evaluate and question AI outputs. Importantly, the notion of ‘challenging the algorithms’ suggest that physicians should not accept these tools as definitive decision-makers; instead, they must be prepared to substantiate their reasoning, highlighting the need of maintaining critical thinking as part of their contributory expertise. It also shows how an introduction of automated algorithmic support for data analysis and prediction work necessitates new roles and a redistribution of existing expertise to augment and improve the algorithm’s accuracy and effectiveness.

A young physician recounted the complexities involved in using algorithms to manage patients with multiple coexisting diseases. He noted that in his cardiac department, the algorithms often generate inaccurate recommendations - such as proposing a diagnosis of stroke when it is not applicable. Nonetheless, he acknowledged that these algorithms can be useful in emergency settings, where they may suggest alternative diagnoses that are not immediately apparent. This underscores the importance of contributory expertise, particularly in complex cases where clinical judgment must outweigh algorithmic recommendations.

The physician also pointed out situations where patients present with normal results yet appear very ill, illustrating that data alone is insufficient for diagnosis. In such cases, reliance on clinical intuition and experience—key elements of the ‘art of medicine’—becomes essential. His narrative underscores the relational aspect of expertise: effective treatment relies on interactions with patients and his ability to respond to their needs. Ultimately, his capacity to address medical issues is fundamentally linked to these exchanges, reinforcing the interactive nature of effective healthcare delivery.

While the algorithm is seen as a tool that allows clinicians to share judgment and responsibility—easing the burden of difficult decisions—it also creates a clear demarcation between human and algorithmic agency. Respondents express the importance of maintaining ‘pure’ human self-determination. When cognitive tasks are delegated to an algorithm, decision-making authority becomes distributed within the system. Consequently, sharing responsibility with AI alters what constitutes a clinician’s ‘own’ decisions. As a result, autonomy and self-determination are not inherent qualities of cardiologists; instead, they are situational achievements that must be continuously renegotiated considering evolving technologies and artificial reasoning.

Ethical implications of AI integration: who is accountable?

In the module ‘Ethics, Law, and Organization of Personalized Medicine,’ participants addressed critical questions on the use of AI in clinical settings such as, ‘What if the algorithm makes a mistake? Who bears responsibility?’ This highlights the tension between integrating AI tools and maintaining personal patient assessment skills. Participants expressed concern that an over-reliance on algorithms could compromise their clinical judgment and hinder the ongoing development of their contributory expertise.

A senior consultant in an oncology department discussed her daily dilemmas regarding targeted treatments that benefit only for a small number of patients. She stressed the need to balance technical data interpretation with interpersonal skills, remarking, ‘(…) doctors need time and the ability to empathize with the person in front of you.’ This highlights her struggle to allocate time effectively – whether to focus on providing optimal care for individual patients or to engage with the broader health policy goal of implementing personalized medicine.

Similarly, the course organizer emphasized the importance of involving clinical departments in decision-making about AI implementation. He stated that, ‘the clinical departments should decide which current work processes can be advantageously replaced by a computer’, ensuring that those closest to patient care influence such decisions increasing the chances of successful integration and acceptance. This suggests that personalized medicine is perceived as stretched between caring practices and the creation of possibilities for health prevention and improved treatment.

One participant, an AI implementation consultant for a network of hospitals in Denmark, posited that as technology becomes entrenched in medical practices, there is a corresponding need for healthcare professionals to manage not just the technology but also the social implications of its use. The participant states, ‘As soon as we use this data and artificial intelligence, we rely on this black box where we can’t explain how the computer arrives at its conclusions,’ however, she did not sense the discomfort around the opacity of AI systems. She further explained: ’In the most recent course, we worked with a circular algorithm designed to assess the risk of infection in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia. Given that doctors often struggle to predict which patients might develop infections months in advance, this algorithm can serve as a valuable tool for guiding patient monitoring. Preliminary studies suggest that the algorithm performs better than doctors in these assessments, so why shouldn’t healthcare professionals rely on it?’

Concerns about potential biases in AI were also raised, particularly regarding underrepresented demographics in algorithmic data pools. One of the participants noted, ‘Some studies found that algorithms often replicate the same biases present in the real world, essentially recreating them. This raises the risk that human judgments may inadvertently influence the algorithms’. This aligns with Eyal’s focus on the social dynamics of knowledge production and data representation in healthcare. Another participant noted, ‘There may be a group that is not represented in the dataset,’ emphasizing the ethical responsibilities healthcare professionals must navigate to ensure equitable care.

Other dilemmas faced by healthcare professionals in their interactions with patients and patient data—particularly concerning AI-generated diagnoses—were discussed. Questions such as, ‘What happens to me if just rely on the algorithm? Will I still be held responsible?’ emerged. A participant raised a concern, asking, ‘What do I do if the power goes out?’ Several participants nodded in agreement when someone mentioned, ‘We risk losing our skills in reading patients,’ implying that an over-reliance on algorithms could hinder the development of clinical judgment, especially given the limited time we have with each patient. Another question arises from the group: ‘Who truly owns patient data, when it comes down to it?’ This discussion highlights not only ethical concerns but also, as Eyal [32] emphasizes, the importance of increasingly involving patients in the decision-making processes regarding research direction and medical intervention goals. In this context, patients’ knowledge and insights are seen as co-produced within the realm of medical expertise.

In discussing the involvement of senior doctors in complex cases, one participant asserted, ‘We should use their expertise for decisions in situations where there is no clear solution.’ This reinforces a collaborative model where AI serves as a tool to complement, rather than replace, the extensive contributory expertise of seasoned practitioners.

One of the respondents, an oncologist, referred to the size of one’s mental ‘card index,’ developed through exposure and repetition, as crucial in medical decision-making. This notion aligns with the principles laid out by Eyal [32], which stress the significance of experiential knowledge in shaping clinical judgment. The respondent remarked, ‘It’s about the size of the card index you have in your mind and how many times you’ve seen that before.’ This remark signifies a scepticism toward blindly trusting AI outputs, particularly when they do not conform to established biological knowledge, underscoring the need for critical thinking in the face of technological advancements. However, this perspective is situated within a rationalist tradition that prioritizes definitive answers and solutions [22].

Ethical concerns surrounding AI are also prevalent in the discussions. Another participant addresses the complexities of informed consent and the challenges in explaining the decision-making processes of algorithms to patients. They highlight issues of explainability, fairness, and representativeness, noting that certain patient groups may be underrepresented in algorithm training data. Eyal’s work on the social dimensions of knowledge production underscores how these biases can shape healthcare delivery. The participant states, ‘We rely on this black box where we can’t explain how the computer arrives at its conclusions,’ calling attention to the ethical imperative for transparency and effective communication with patients regarding the limitations of AI technologies. Furthermore, other participants express concerns about the appropriate use of patient data. One oncologist noted, ‘In my lifetime, I cannot see 500 million patients, but if this algorithm has data from 500 million patients, would it be unethical not to use the data?’ This statement highlights the ethical tension between leveraging comprehensive datasets to improve patient outcomes and ensuring that such practices remain ethically sound. She added, ‘the use of artificial intelligence in our field can be challenging because, with a new scanning method, it feels impersonal; it’s not me, it’s the scan. But when I explain to patients that I’m using an algorithm instead of relying solely on my judgment…there are many factors to consider. The algorithm may not incorporate all relevant data, raising issues of fairness. For example, certain groups may be underrepresented in the dataset, or there could be a bias towards male patients. So, it can’t be generalized’.

Overall, discussions underscored the necessity for transparency and critical evaluation of AI outputs to ensure accountability and maintain commitment to ethical patient care.

Discussion

The responses from participants illustrate a complex interplay between clinical judgment, expertise, and the use of AI tools in healthcare. There is a shared recognition of the importance of maintaining contributory expertise to preserve decision-making authority while also developing interactional expertise to navigate the complexities introduced by AI technologies and engage critically with algorithms.

Expertise is generated, applied, and recognised within collaborative interactions. This aligns with the findings of Pakarinen & Huising [47], which indicate that AI technologies designed to augment or replace professional expertise face challenges. Health professionals must verify and interrogate automated outcomes, as they remain accountable for diagnoses and clinical advice. Consequently, inputs must be adjusted and supplemented with the relational expertise inherent to the profession. However, a significant challenge is the black-box nature of these systems, which renders their decision-making processes opaque. In response to concerns about using such systems, participants expressed a demand for explainable AI models - a set of processes that enables human users to comprehend and trust the output created by AI. Some participants raised concern about the training of the AI tools, the data used and the extent to which their own professional judgment might be overshadowed by AI assessments. This ambiguity can lead clinicians to second-guess their expertise, which may be considered particularly troubling in healthcare settings, where professionals bear legal, ethical, and professional responsibilities. However, as noted by Engebretsen et al. [48], uncertainty should not be seen merely as a regrettable aspect of decision-making but a productive component of clinical reasoning.

On a broader scale, political initiatives are working to realize the promise of personalized medicine to enhance disease prevention, improve health outcomes, and reduce treatment costs. Achieving personalized healthcare relies heavily on systems that allow clinicians and researchers to access and repurpose diverse population health data for clinical and research purposes. However, the use of health data for new purposes raises significant concerns regarding public opinions on data access, the types of data that should be accessible, their intended uses, and the regulatory frameworks governing these practices. Addressing these dynamics is critical for patient well-being and compliance with regulatory bodies such as The Danish Patient Safety Authority.

Viewed through the lens of the sociology of expertise, there exists a complex interplay among physicians’ experiences, data assessment, algorithmic tools, collaboration with other healthcare professionals, departmental dynamics, patient care norms (e.g., the typical 10-minute consultation), and patient and family expectations, including demands for specific treatments.

Professional boundaries, which define the scope of practice and expertise for healthcare professionals, complicate the acceptance of personalized medicine and genomics. Overcoming these barriers demands the acquisition of new knowledge and skills, and resistance may arise from concerns regarding professional identity, competence, and the disruption of established hierarchies. According to Bryan and Lammers [49], professionals are interconnected and influenced by external forces, which leads to the emergence of new forms of collaboration and roles, ultimately fostering expertise and agency. Yet, the struggles to embracing personalized medicine arise from conventional professional boundaries, which dictate the expertise and scope of practice for healthcare professionals.

The introduction of a master’s program represents a structural shift within the Danish health sector, aiming to restore medical treatments, workflows, and inter-professional collaboration to enhance healthcare effectiveness. Guided by the principles of interdisciplinary collaboration [50], this program seeks to bridge gaps between healthcare professionals and those generating data for the benefit of patients, colleagues, and society. According to the standards of EBM, a doctor’s fundamental responsibility is to integrate medical knowledge - including their experience - with the best available information from clinical studies [51]. However, in practice, a pragmatic diversity of biological, clinical, and principled knowledge are incorporated to support guidelines, adapt RCT findings to different subpopulations, and define what qualifies as evidence [52]. Nonetheless, EBM has prompted clinicians to reconsider their reliance on practical reasoning and clinical judgment. The emphasis on standardization and control fosters scepticism toward the roles of intuition, creativity, and uncertainty in medical practice, as EBM does not offer tools for addressing the complexity of real-world situations [48].

With the introduction of AI support, the process of data evaluation is transformed in a manner that is increasingly difficult for health professionals to comprehend. This new approach operates alongside existing methods, such as medical guidelines and theoretical expertise, to generate actionable knowledge. Consequently, when confronted with conflicting or divergent actionable knowledge, decision-making becomes challenging for clinicians without access to underlying reasoning [53] which is a central barrier to the use of AI support [54, 55].

The implications of AI extend beyond task automation; they raise questions about which responsibilities can be redistributed among various groups of healthcare professionals. If AI assumes portions of medical judgment, it may result in semi-professional healthcare workers (such as nurses, midwives, and biomedical scientists) taking on roles traditionally held by physicians. Personalized medicine necessitates that clinicians remain informed about the latest genetic research, technological innovations, and treatment options. This continual learning process complicates their professional identity as they collaborate within multidisciplinary teams and expand their expertise into new domains. Additionally, personalized medicine may pose ethical challenges for physicians [56], particularly as it often requires collaboration with diverse specialties, potentially disrupting existing hierarchies in medicine. Thus, understanding healthcare professionals’ experiences with working alongside bioinformaticians, genetic counsellors, and other specialists becomes essential.

The course organizer expressed the belief that AI will revolutionize the healthcare sector by enhancing efficiency through automation of tasks such as analysing blood test results this would allow health professionals to focus on their expertise and artistry. However, this optimistic outlook clashes with the realities posed by an aging population with multiple health conditions, increasing diagnostic demands across various age groups, and rising patient expectations for treatment options as new therapies and medications emerge.

Our data also reveal varied perspectives on clinical decision-making and personal judgment concerning personalized medicine’s implementation. What transpires when the art of apprenticeship transfers to algorithms, leading to a diminished role for clinical judgment? As Grundmann [57] posits, the interplay between human expertise and technology is critical in knowledge societies where decision-making is informed by a combination of expert knowledge and practical experience. In Collins‘ analysis of computers [58], he argues that they can be considered as artificial experts provided that they are continuously supported by humans that– at a minimum– digitize their input and repair their output.

According to the course leader, AI will not undermine the medical profession or its authority. However, aligning with Eyal’s perspective, it is crucial to recognize that AI occupies an active role in society that often transcends its traditional perception as merely a supportive tool. By applying the insights from Collins and Evans’ work to personalized medicine, it becomes evident that expertise in the field is not based solely on individual knowledge and skills but is significantly shaped by interdisciplinary collaborations, patient interactions, and societal contexts. Clinicians practicing personalized medicine engage in contributory expertise by actively participating in the development and validation of personalized medicine practices while developing interactional expertise to effectively communicate this knowledge and collaborate with patients and interdisciplinary colleagues. This approach will help ensure the integration of AI technologies into routine practice while preserving essential human elements in patient care.

Reflection on methodology and research design

Our decision to conduct qualitative interviews with a relatively small sample of course participants and a limited number of teachers presents both opportunities and challenges. While the diversity of our participants—drawn from various hospitals across Denmark—enriches the breadth of perspectives represented, it may not fully capture the experiences of all healthcare professionals engaged in personalized medicine in their daily practice. Future research would benefit from a larger, more diverse sample to validate and expand upon the patterns identified in our analysis.

Moreover, the inclusion of observational data collected during the teaching modules added substantial depth to our study. This firsthand observation of classroom interactions and pedagogical approaches enhanced our understanding of the educational dynamics at play. However, it is important to note that we only observed two modules, each organized differently; one primarily involved traditional lecturing, while the other emphasized dialogue and discussion. This variability in teaching methods may influence the learning experiences of participants and consequently affect their reflections.

Additionally, our use of in situ interviews conducted during breaks facilitated the capture of spontaneous reflections. While this approach offered immediate insights into participants’ thoughts and feelings, it also raises questions about the depth and focus of their responses in a less formal setting. Although these spontaneous reflections can yield valuable information, they may lack the comprehensive depth typically associated with more structured interview formats.

To sum up, while our methodological choices allowed for rich qualitative insights, acknowledging these limitations is crucial. Future studies should aim to incorporate a more extensive and varied participant base, as well as consider the context in which data is collected, to enhance the robustness of findings in this evolving field of personalized medicine.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the complex interplay between AI and personalized medicine within the realm of healthcare, revealing both opportunities and challenges that arise from their integration. Participants emphasized a shared optimism about AI’s transformative potential, particularly its ability to enhance clinical judgment and improve diagnostic accuracy. However, a crucial aspect of our findings highlights the need to critically balance this technological advancement with the art of medicine—a balance that underlines the importance of preserving healthcare professionals’ decision-making power.

AI is seen as a powerful tool that can support clinicians in executing their duties more efficiently, enabling them to focus on intricate cases that require a nuanced understanding of patient needs. Yet, there remains a strong belief among participants that AI should complement, rather than supplant, the uniquely human aspects of medical practice. The art of medicine involves not just clinical knowledge but also empathy, understanding, and the capacity for interpersonal communication—qualities that AI technology cannot replicate. Furthermore, our findings underscore a critical concern: healthcare professionals often struggle to possess sufficient knowledge to appropriately assess the explainability, fairness, and representativeness of AI algorithms. Moreover, the challenges posed by AI’s reliance on data highlight other ethical concerns, particularly around the implications of decision-making when algorithms are involved.

Questions about accountability and the potential risk of diminishing clinical skills due to dependence on AI tools require careful consideration by healthcare professionals. Establishing robust frameworks for integrating these technologies is essential to foster a collaborative environment in healthcare, where the interactional expertise of professionals remains central to patient care and decision-making. Qualitative studies that examine how algorithms are used, accepted, and challenged in clinical practice are crucial for understanding their implications for clinicians, patients, and healthcare provision.
