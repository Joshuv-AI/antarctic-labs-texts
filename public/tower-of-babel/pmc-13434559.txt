Comparative risk of pneumonia and acute exacerbation between P-CAB and PPI use in patients with COPD and GERD
Authors: SooBeen Park, Seoung Ju Park, Hyuk Soon Choi, Hankil Lee, Jae Hyun Kim, Ha-Lim Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs) are newer gastric acid suppressants used to treat gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), but evidence regarding their respiratory safety compared with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains limited. We conducted a target trial emulation study using the Korean National Health Insurance database. Patients aged ≥40 years with COPD who initiated P-CAB or PPI for GERD between 2019 and 2022 were included. Follow-up continued from treatment initiation until the earliest occurrence of the outcome, treatment discontinuation, switching to another drug class, death, or the end of the study period. The outcome was the occurrence of pneumonia or moderate–to–severe COPD exacerbations. Propensity score matching (1:3) was applied, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Among the 560,987 eligible patients, 4671 P-CAB users and 14,013 PPI users were included after matching. P-CAB was not significantly associated with the risk of respiratory events compared with PPI, but a trend toward lower risks for pneumonia (aHR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.54–1.07) and severe exacerbations (aHR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.32–1.14) was observed. In patients with a Charlson Comorbidity Index ≤1, the risk of severe exacerbations was significantly lower (aHR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.11–0.88). P-CABs showed lower risks of pneumonia and severe exacerbations compared with PPIs, although the differences were not statistically significant. Further long-term studies across diverse patient subgroups are warranted.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition characterized by persistent symptoms such as dyspnea, chronic cough, and excessive sputum production. The Global Burden of Disease Study ranked COPD as the third leading cause of death in 20191, and its prevalence is expected to increase due to the aging population. Effective management of COPD is essential not only to control the chronic symptoms but also to prevent complications such as acute exacerbations and pneumonia to reduce mortality. The comprehensive management strategy of COPD includes the identification and reduction of risk factors, addressing comorbid diseases, and optimizing pharmacotherapy.

One of the common comorbidities of COPD is gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Studies using self-reported questionnaires or endoscopic procedures have reported GERD prevalences ranging from 29 to 50% among COPD patients2,3. Previous research has identified GERD as an independent risk factor for COPD exacerbations2,4. Although GERD treatment with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) was anticipated to lower the risk of exacerbations, subsequent findings have been inconsistent. For instance, a self-controlled case series demonstrated reduced COPD exacerbations during PPI treatment5, whereas a cohort study reported PPI use was associated with a shorter time to severe exacerbations (hazard ratio [HR] 2.05; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.37–3.08)6. Moreover, PPI use has also been linked to an elevated risk of pneumonia in a Swedish study with self-controlled case series design7. The relationships among the use of PPI, acid suppression, COPD exacerbations, and pneumonia are not fully understood, and current guidelines do not yet provide clear recommendations on the use of PPIs to prevent COPD exacerbations in patients with comorbid GERD8. GERD may contribute to COPD exacerbations through several pathophysiological mechanisms, including microaspiration of gastric contents and acid-induced airway inflammation. Although acid suppression may help reduce GERD-related respiratory complications, it has also been suggested that acid-suppressive therapy could increase susceptibility to respiratory infections, which may partly explain the inconsistent findings observed in previous studies.

Potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs) are a newer class of GERD treatments that achieve a faster onset of action and more stable acid suppression than PPIs9,10. They have been approved in multiple countries, including the United States, Japan, and Korea, and their global use is expected to rise10. Meta-analyses comparing the efficacy and safety of P-CABs and PPIs have shown considerable efficacy regarding Helicobacter pylori eradication or erosive esophagitis while reporting comparable results in treating gastric acid-related disorders11,12. Clinical guidelines also recommend the use of P-CABs in patients with heartburn symptoms, erosive esophagitis or GERD, although the strength of recommendation varies across guidelines, depending on the local availability and cost-effectiveness of P-CAB therapy13,14.

However, it is still unclear whether stronger and more consistent acid suppression with P-CABs translates into a higher or lower risk of COPD exacerbations and the development of pneumonia. Because P-CABs have a different mechanism of action and acid suppression profile compared to PPIs, the clinical relevance of using P-CABs in COPD patients with GERD is yet to be determined. This study aimed to compare the risk of moderate–to–severe COPD exacerbations and pneumonia between P-CAB and PPI users in patients with COPD and GERD, to explore whether differences in the degree of acid suppression may influence respiratory outcomes.

Methods

Study design and data source

We applied a target trial emulation (TTE), which mimics the design of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) using observational data. This method involves defining a hypothetical RCT protocol and applying it to observational data15,16. To construct our target trial, we specified key elements, including eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, treatment assignment methods, follow-up, outcomes of interest, causal contrasts, and analysis. A structured comparison between the hypothetical target trial and its emulation using real-world data is presented in Table 1, which outlines how each component was adapted to fit the observational study design. We used the National Health Insurance (NHI) data from the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) for the period from January 1, 2018, to October 31, 2023. This database, derived from South Korea’s mandatory national health insurance system that covers almost all the Korean population (≥97%), provides comprehensive information, such as demographic data (age, sex, and region of residence), diagnostic codes (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] codes), prescriptions (drug names, dosages, and durations), medical procedures, and healthcare utilization (outpatient visits and hospitalizations).

Study population

This study included patients aged ≥40 years who initiated P-CABs (tegoprazan, fexuprazan, revaprazan) or PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, rabeprazole, esomeprazole, ilaprazole, dexlansoprazole, and s-pantoprazole) for the first time during the study period (January 1, 2019, to April 30, 2023) for the treatment of GERD (ICD-10: K21) (eTable 1). The index date was defined as the first prescription of either drug during the study period.

To include only new users and minimize confounding, we applied several exclusion criteria. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 40 years; had a history of pneumonia, acute exacerbation of COPD (defined using the same operational definitions as the study outcomes), or cancer (registration code for patients with severe disease: V193) within 1 year before the index date; had a prescription record of P-CABs or PPIs during the 1 year prior to the index date; or had missing demographic data such as age or sex.

Drug exposure

Per-protocol (PP) analysis was adopted in a phase 3 trial of P-CABs18. However, in observational studies, treatment adherence cannot be strictly controlled, rendering PP analysis impractical. Thus, we used an as-treated approach, which is conceptually similar to PP analysis but better suited to real-world data19. This approach accounts for treatment discontinuation and switching, considering only periods of active drug exposure to improve effect estimation20.

If a subsequent prescription for drugs of the same class was issued within a predefined grace period, the treatment was considered continuous. Treatment discontinuation was defined as the absence of a prescription renewal within 30 days after the end of the prior prescription.

Outcomes and follow-up

Respiratory events of interest were defined as the occurrence of pneumonia or COPD exacerbation. Pneumonia was defined as a recorded diagnosis with ICD-10 codes J12–J18. Moderate exacerbation was defined as an outpatient visit for COPD (ICD-10 codes J43–J44) accompanied by a prescription for either corticosteroids, systemic antibiotics, or both. A severe exacerbation was defined as a COPD-related hospitalization or emergency department visit with a prescription for corticosteroids (eTable 2)5. The risk of pneumonia and moderate–to–severe acute exacerbations was examined separately as secondary outcomes. Follow-up began on the index date and ended at the earliest occurrence of one of the following events: an outcome event, 30 days after treatment discontinuation, a switch to another drug class, in-hospital death, or the end of the study period (October 31, 2023). In the NHI data from HIRA, death information was limited to in-hospital death.

Potential confounders

We evaluated potential confounders, including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, comedications, index year, and healthcare utilization. Baseline characteristics were collected at the index date and included age, sex, and insurance type (medical aid, health insurance, and veterans’ benefits). During the 1-year baseline period, we assessed the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, asthma, and liver disease were examined (eTable 3). Comedications prescribed during the 1 year before the index date were identified. These included steroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antibiotics, beta-2 agonists (short-acting [SABA] and long-acting [LABA]), muscarinic antagonists (short-acting [SAMA] and long-acting [LAMA]), LAMA/LABA and ICS/LABA combinations, methylxanthines, and phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors (PDE-4 inhibitors) (eTable 4). Healthcare utilization was captured as the number of outpatient and inpatient visits with a COPD diagnosis during the baseline period. A summary of the covariates assessed and the study design is presented in eFigure 1.

Statistical analysis

A 1:3 propensity score (PS) matching approach was applied to balance baseline characteristics between the two treatment groups. A 1:4 matching ratio was also evaluated, but it resulted in the exclusion of a substantially larger number of treated patients with no meaningful improvement in covariate balance; therefore, 1:3 matching was selected. The propensity score, defined as the conditional probability of receiving P-CAB or PPI given the observed covariates, was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model21. Matching was performed using a nearest-neighbor greedy algorithm without replacement. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using absolute standardized differences (aSD), and values greater than 0.1 were considered indicative of meaningful imbalance22.

In the matched cohort, the incidence of pneumonia and moderate–to–severe acute exacerbations were compared between P-CAB and PPI users. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these outcomes. Across all analyses, adjusted models included the same set of baseline covariates. In addition, the absolute rate differences (aRDs) between the P-CAB and PPI groups were estimated. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05.

Subgroup analysis

For each outcome, subgroup analyses were conducted by age, sex, CCI, and COPD-related visits. These subgroup analyses were pre-specified based on clinical plausibility. Age was categorized into 40–64 and ≥65 years, CCI into ≤1 and >1, and COPD-related visits into ≤2 and >2. Interaction tests were performed to assess potential effect modification between the treatment group and each subgroup variable.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary analysis results. First, we varied the grace period for treatment discontinuation from 30 days (main analysis) to 15 and 45 days. Second, Intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were conducted with follow-up periods of 90, 180, and 365 days, as well as the full study period. Finally, alternative statistical methods, including inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and PS adjustment, were applied to evaluate consistency across analytical techniques.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Jeonbuk National University (Approval No. JBNU202404019-UE002). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized claims data.

Regarding sex distribution, the proportion of male patients was 74.1% in the P-CAB group and 74.9% in the PPI group (aSD=0.018). The CCI was also similar between the groups, with a mean of 1.57 (standard deviation [std]=1.1) in the P-CAB group and 1.56 (std=1.09) in the PPI group. The prevalence of comorbid conditions was generally higher in the P-CAB group for cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes. In contrast, asthma was slightly more common in the PPI group (P-CAB: 46.3 vs. PPI: 47.8%). Comedications most commonly prescribed included NSAIDs (P-CAB: 79.2%, PPI: 81.4%), antibiotics (69.8, 72.1%), and glucocorticoids (56.7, 58.8%). Among bronchodilators, methylxanthines (24.4, 26.1%), LAMA/LABA combinations (24.6, 23.4%), and ICS/LABA (24.2, 23.4%) were the most frequently prescribed.

Following 1:3 PS matching, 4671 P-CAB users and 14,013 matched PPI users were included in the matched cohort. After matching, all baseline covariates were well balanced, with aSD reduced to below 0.1 for all variables (eFig. 2). The mean follow-up duration was relatively short, with P-CAB users followed for 1.2 months (std=2.81) on average, and PPI users followed for 1.6 months (std=4.33) (eTable 5).

The incidence rate and risk of pneumonia and exacerbation

After PS matching, the overall incidence rate (IR) was 12.5 per 100 person-years (PY) (95% CI, 9.24–15.72) in P-CAB users and 13.9 per 100 PY (95% CI, 12.25–15.60) in PPI users (Table 3). Discussion

This target trial emulation study assessed the risk of pneumonia and moderate–to–severe acute COPD exacerbations associated with P-CABs compared with PPIs in COPD patients. The risk of respiratory events appeared lower but was not statistically significant in P-CAB users. P-CAB use tended to be associated with lower risks of pneumonia and severe exacerbations, whereas a higher risk trend was observed for the risk of moderate exacerbations. Subgroup analyses generally aligned with these findings, and a significant reduction in the risk of severe exacerbations was observed in patients with a CCI of 1 or lower. Sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main results, demonstrating statistically significant reductions in respiratory event risk under specific analytic conditions, including alternative grace periods and the use of the IPTW method.

The use of P-CABs demonstrated a trend toward a lower risk of overall events, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. GERD has been suggested as a potential risk factor for respiratory events, mainly through mechanisms such as microaspiration of gastric contents and acid-induced airway inflammation23,24. Compared to PPIs, P-CABs provide faster and more sustained acid suppression9,10, which may more effectively mitigate these mechanisms. A previous study examining the association between GERD and COPD exacerbation differentiated GERD symptoms into daytime and night-time patterns and evaluated their interaction with acid-suppressive treatment4. The highest risk of COPD exacerbation was observed in patients experiencing both daytime and nighttime GERD symptoms, while those with symptoms limited to one period showed a comparatively lower risk. Given these pharmacodynamic characteristics of P-CABs, more consistent acid suppression may partially explain the observed trend toward a reduced risk of respiratory events. In light of the non-significant primary results and small absolute differences in event rates, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Among the individual outcomes, the trend toward a lower risk of pneumonia in the P-CAB group is of interest given the controversy surrounding acid-suppressive therapy, particularly PPIs, and pneumonia risk. While microaspiration and airway inflammation are key mechanisms linking GERD to pneumonia, previous studies have paradoxically reported that PPI use may increase pneumonia risk25. Subsequent investigations suggested that these findings might have been influenced by protopathic bias, confounding, or publication bias26,27, indicating the need for further research to clarify the association between acid suppressant use in GERD and pneumonia. In this context, the favorable trend observed with P-CABs warrants further investigation to determine whether their more potent acid suppression translates into a clinically meaningful reduction in pneumonia risk.

Results for individual outcomes revealed that P-CAB use was associated with a trend toward lower risks of pneumonia and severe exacerbations, although these associations were not statistically significant. The observed reduction in severe exacerbations and pneumonia may suggest a potential protective effect of P-CAB therapy in preventing clinically significant events requiring hospitalization. Notably, a statistically significant reduction in respiratory events was observed only when the as-treated analysis was combined with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). This may be explained by increased precision resulting from the reweighting process, which can lead to narrower confidence intervals despite similar effect sizes; however, it may also reflect sensitivity of the results to the analytic approach. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate the relationships among these clinical events.

In the subgroup analysis, a trend toward a lower risk of pneumonia and severe exacerbations was observed in male patients. Regarding sex, male patients tended to show a more favorable risk profile across all outcomes. Sex-based differences in COPD pathophysiology have been increasingly recognized. Females typically have smaller airways, and hormonal factors may further contribute to distinct COPD phenotypes compared to males28,29. Additionally, a retrospective cohort study in Taiwan found that male sex was associated with increased risks of exacerbation and mortality in COPD patients with GERD, whereas PPI use was linked to reduced risks in this subgroup30. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects observed in male patients remain unclear, it is plausible that more consistent acid suppression contributes to mitigating the heightened risk of overall events in males. However, because the interaction test was not statistically significant, the observed sex difference should be interpreted with caution.

A favorable trend with P-CAB in moderate–to–severe exacerbations was observed in patients with a lower comorbidity burden (CCI ≤ 1), with this pattern being more pronounced for severe exacerbations. Several factors have been proposed to contribute to diminished responses to PPIs in patients with GERD, including poor compliance, inter-individual variability in drug metabolism, and delayed gastric emptying31. Patients with a lower CCI might have fewer complicating factors, potentially facilitating better adherence and responsiveness to acid-suppressive therapy. Therefore, the potent and rapid acid inhibition provided by P-CABs may contribute to clinical benefits among these less-comorbid patients compared to PPIs. However, given the exploratory nature of the subgroup analyses and the lack of statistically significant interaction, the observed difference according to comorbidity burden should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare P-CAB and PPI use in patients with COPD. Given their relatively recent introduction into clinical practice, limited evidence exists regarding the impact of P-CABs in this population. Our study contributes valuable insights into the potential respiratory effects of these two acid-suppressive therapies in COPD patients. Second, this study applied TTE to strengthen causal inference in an observational setting. By structuring the design to approximate a hypothetical randomized trial, TTE helped reduce biases related to treatment assignment and improve the validity of comparisons between P-CAB and PPI users.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the absence of key clinical parameters, such as body mass index (BMI), smoking status, pulmonary function, and other lifestyle factors, limits the ability to adjust for important confounders. These variables are closely associated with both the risk of COPD exacerbations and pneumonia, and their omission may lead to residual confounding and affect the validity of the findings. Second, this study relied on administrative claims data, which are based on diagnostic codes rather than detailed clinical assessments. Although we applied previously validated coding algorithms, the possibility of misclassification bias cannot be completely ruled out. Third, because P-CABs were introduced to the market in 2019, the number of users was relatively small compared to PPI users, which may have limited the statistical power. Although the IPTW approach helped address this limitation and showed significant associations under certain conditions, further studies with larger cohorts and more detailed clinical data are warranted to confirm and expand these findings. Fourth, in the as-treated analysis, follow-up was censored at treatment discontinuation or switching. Because treatment discontinuation or switching may be related to patients’ clinical status or prognosis, informative censoring cannot be ruled out completely. However, the direction of the results was generally consistent with those from the ITT analyses, suggesting that the findings were robust to potential informative censoring. Fifth, death may have acted as a competing event in this older population. However, because the NHI data from HIRA do not comprehensively capture all-cause mortality and only in-hospital death is partially identifiable, we were unable to fully account for competing risk. This may have affected the estimated associations. Sixth, the relatively short follow-up period in the as-treated analysis may not have been sufficient to capture longer-term treatment effects. However, pneumonia and COPD exacerbations are clinically relevant events that may occur relatively early after treatment initiation. In this context, the observed associations are likely to reflect early effects of treatment rather than longer-term risks. Nevertheless, because follow-up was limited, caution is warranted when interpreting these findings in relation to longer-term outcomes. Lastly, the exclusion of patients with pneumonia or COPD exacerbation within 1 year prior to the index date may have led to the selection of a relatively more clinically stable COPD population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to higher-risk populations such as frequent exacerbators. Furthermore, this restriction may have reduced the overall event rate and statistical power. Because prior exacerbations are associated with both treatment selection and future risk, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded. Future studies using alternative approaches, such as shorter washout periods or adjustment for prior exacerbation history, may help further clarify these associations.

Conclusion

This study found no significant difference in the risk of pneumonia or acute COPD exacerbations between P-CAB and PPI use among patients with COPD. However, P-CAB use showed a favorable trend toward reduced risks of pneumonia and severe exacerbations. Although the trends for moderate and severe exacerbations were inconsistent, a lower risk of severe exacerbations was observed in patients with a lower comorbidity burden. Further studies involving more diverse populations and longer follow-up periods are warranted.
