Social support and depressive symptoms among migrant elderly following children: the chain mediating role of resilience and loneliness
Authors: Haixia Liu, Xiaoyu Chen, Yinggang Yao, Wenqi He, Qi Zhang, Hui Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigrant elderly following children (MEFC) is an emerging special elderly mobile migrant population in China. MEFC face multiple dilemmas of distress, which makes their depressive symptoms more prominent. To explore ways to alleviate depressive symptoms, this study aimed to investigate the chain mediating effects of resilience and loneliness on the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using purposive sampling from Changsha, Hunan Province, China, and 298 MEFC were included. The 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale, and Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale were used to measure depressive symptoms, social support, loneliness, and resilience, respectively. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to clarify the association between the variables. The PROCESS macro model 6 was applied to analyze the multiple mediating effects.ResultsThe prevalence of depressive symptoms among MEFC was 29.86%. Social support (r = -0.354, P < 0.001), resilience (r = -0.373, P < 0.001), and loneliness (r = 0.483, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with depressive symptoms. Social support not only had a direct negative effect (Effect= -0.086, 95% CI: -0.149 to -0.023) on depressive symptoms, but also had an indirect effect on depressive symptoms through resilience (Effect= -0.045, 95% CI: -0.076 to -0.015), loneliness (Effect= -0.053, 95% CI: -0.091 to -0.020), and the chain mediating effects of resilience and loneliness (Effect= -0.019, 95% CI: -0.037 to -0.007), respectively.ConclusionsThe prevalence of depressive symptoms was relatively high. Resilience and loneliness partially mediated the association between social support and depressive symptoms. In the future, interventions could focus on increasing social support to enhance resilience and reduce loneliness, thereby reducing depressive symptoms in MEFC.Keywords: Migrant elderly following children, Depressive symptoms, Social support, Resilience, Loneliness

Introduction

The migrant population in China is rapidly increasing. According to the Seventh National Population Census, 490 million people are separated from their households, with an 88.52% growth rate [1]. Over time, China’s migration pattern has gradually changed from individual to family migration [2]. Under this pattern, a group of elderly migrants who follow their children to support them and take care of their younger generation has gradually emerged, known as the migrant elderly following children (MEFC) [3]. They belong to a unique cohort arising from the Chinese household paradigm and are undergoing swift societal transformations. In fact, their situation in the new environment is not optimistic. They are facing many challenges, such as a lack of social integration, intergenerational conflicts, and barriers to receiving welfare benefits [4, 5], and these dilemmas may induce depressive symptoms [6]. It is important to focus on and try to reduce the occurrence of depressive symptoms among MEFC in order to promote their physical and mental health.

Social support is a multifaceted concept that broadly relates to emotional, instrumental, or informational assistance provided by others [7]. Gable & Bedrov [8] emphasized that social support could amplify the benefits of positive events and achievements for individuals and help minimize setbacks. It is widely recognized that social support is a crucial protective factor against depressive symptoms [9, 10]. Older adults without social support were more likely to experience depressive symptoms [11]. Elderly depressed individuals with less social support performed worse in rehabilitation and social functioning [12] and were more likely to develop coronary heart disease [13]. One of the current challenges facing the MEFC is the lack of social support, which is significantly lower than that available to and utilized by local elderly people [14]. Thus, it is imperative to investigate the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms to provide practical suggestions for enhancing the psychological situation of MEFC.

Resilience is defined as an individual’s ability to recover and cope successfully in the face of adversity [15]. It is crucial for preserving elderly persons’ mental health [16] and organismic functioning [17]. Resilience is a dynamic process that allows individuals to grow and develop through adversity [18]. Social support was positively related to resilience [19], and resilience was negatively associated with depressive symptoms [20]. According to earlier studies, resilience mediated the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms in Chinese rural-to-urban migrants [21] and Chinese elderly adults in Singapore [22]. Hence, resilience may mediate the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC.

Loneliness refers to the subjective perception of the discrepancy between an individual’s actual and desired levels of social interaction [23]. This discrepancy may result in negative experiences of loneliness and/or feelings of distress and irritability from social isolation [24]. Loneliness is more prevalent in contemporary society [25]. Lee et al. [26] suggested that loneliness was an important factor influencing depressive symptoms in older adults. Specifically, loneliness was positively associated with depressive symptoms[27]. Furthermore, a prior meta-analysis revealed a strong correlation between loneliness and social support [28]. According to a previous study among individuals older than 50 years, loneliness mediated the association between social support and major depression [29]. Zhang et al. found a mediating role for loneliness between social support and depression among family caregivers of rural individuals with severe mental illness [30]. Thus, loneliness may be a mediator of the association between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC.

According to the above review, both resilience and loneliness may be mediators between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC. Jakobsen et al. [31] found that resilience had a strong negative relationship with loneliness. Individuals’ resilience affects their loneliness, with less resilient individuals likely to result in higher levels of loneliness [32]. These findings suggest that resilience not only directly affects an individual’s loneliness, but may also indirectly affect depressive symptoms through loneliness. Thus, in addition to these two single mediating effects, there may be a chain of mediating effects of resilience and loneliness influencing the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms.

In summary, resilience and loneliness may mediate the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms. However, to date, the above mechanisms have been unclear in the MEFC. The present study aimed to elucidate the multiple mediating roles of loneliness and resilience in the association between social support and depressive symptoms in MEFC. Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from April to July 2023 in Changsha, Hunan Province, China. As a result of the MEFC’s domicile still being registered in their places of origin, the community’s understanding of their comprehensive circumstances has remained incomplete. Therefore, a purposive sampling method was adopted. Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) were older than 50 years [33]; (2) left their household registration to live in their children’s city for at least six months; (3) supported offspring and took care of their younger generation; (4) had household registration still in their hometowns. Participants with serious physical or mental health problems were excluded from the study.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University (No. E202336), and informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study was implemented. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical principles set out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement

Sociodemographic characteristics

The sociodemographic demographic characteristics included age (50–59, 60–69, 70 and above), gender (male, female), household registration (rural, urban), migration type (cross-provincial, cross-city), and willingness to migrate (I willingly embraced my children’s invitation to accompany them; I independently sought to be with my children; I passively accepted my children’s request to be with them).

Depressive symptoms

The 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) was used to assess participants’ depressive symptoms [34]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.793, and the test-retest reliability was 0.728 [35]. It has been validated to have satisfactory reliability among Chinese elderly adults. The scale consists of 15 items with yes / no answers (0 = “no”, 1 = “yes”) and scores ranging from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A critical score > 5 predicts a tendency toward depressive symptoms [36]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.712.

Social support

Social support was evaluated using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS). The scale was developed in China by Xiao [37]. The scale comprises 10 items, including three dimensions: subjective support, objective support and utilization of support. An individual with a higher total score is likely to have more social support, with possible scores ranging from 12 to 66. The SSRS is widely used in China and has been shown to have good reliability and validity [38]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.896. The correlation coefficients between the three subscales and the total scale ranged from 0.724 to 0.835. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.763.

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured by the University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA), which is a self-evaluation of subjective perceptions of the gap between actual and desired social relationships [39]. The full scale consists of 20 items rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = “never” to 4 = “often”), with a total score of 20 to 80. Higher loneliness scores indicate higher levels of loneliness. The UCLA has been found to be a reliable measure for assessing loneliness in Chinese elderly adults [40]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.89, and the test-retest reliability was 0.73. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.895 in this study.

Resilience

Resilience was measured utilizing the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC). It was originally developed by Connor and Davidson [41] and translated into Chinese by Yu and Zhang [42]. The scale consists of 25 items on a five-point Likert scale (0 = “never” to 4 = “always”), and total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater levels of resilience. The CD-RISC is known to have good reliability and validity among elderly people [43]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the scale ranged from 0.64 to 0.88, with a consistency reliability of 0.91. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.849.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Depressive symptoms of MEFC with different socio-demographic characteristics were analyzed using t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson correlation analysis was used to illustrate the relationship between depressive symptoms and social support, loneliness, and resilience. the PROCESS macro model 6 was applied to test the mediating role of resilience and loneliness between social support and depressive symptoms. The bias-corrected 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated with 5000 bootstrapping resamples. If the 95% CI of the indirect effect did not contain 0, it indicated that the mediating effect was significant. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05 (two-tailed). The model controlled for covariates such as gender, age, household registration, migration type, and willingness to migrate.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the chain-mediated role of resilience and loneliness on the association between social support and depressive symptoms in MEFC. The results indicated that social support was not only directly associated with depressive symptoms, but also indirectly influenced depressive symptoms through resilience and loneliness. The total mediating effect of resilience and loneliness was 57.64%, suggesting that these two mediators play an important role in explaining the relationship of social support on depressive symptoms.

The study indicated that 29.86% of MEFC reported having depressive symptoms. This result was significantly higher than that of middle-aged and elderly individuals aged over 50 in China [44](n = 17.4%, χ2 = 30.440, P < 0.001), as well as studies of MEFC in China’s Guangdong Province and Wuhan city in Hubei Province [45](n = 15.77%, χ2 = 17.439, P < 0.001) and Weifang city in Shandong Province (n = 16.5%, χ2 = 14.927, P < 0.001) [46]. It may be because the process of migration and relocation is often accompanied by social determinants such as social, economic, cultural and communication barriers, which have been shown to have a significant impact on the development of depressive symptoms in older migrants [47, 48]. In addition, negative interactions with children also shaped depression among elderly Chinese migrants [49]. Furthermore, variations in the measurement instruments and the diversity of policies, economies, and cultures among regions may lead to different results. Overall, the prevalence of depressive symptoms was higher among MEFC, and more attention should be given to this topic.

This study revealed a significant negative correlation between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC, which was consistent with earlier research [9]. After moving from their hometowns to another place, they have to adapt to an unfamiliar environment, take on the responsibility of busy household chores, and care for the younger generation. These difficulties make it more difficult for them to access social networks and other forms of support, which may increase the risk of depressive symptoms. Social support is an effective way to protect against depressive symptoms, especially among elderly adults with limited social networks [50]. A systematic review of elderly individuals within Asian communities revealed that robust social networks, particularly unwavering support from familial ties, were associated with reduced depression among elderly adults [51]. Therefore, the involvement of children, such as dedicating more time to positive interactions with elderly people and assuming certain household responsibilities, is imperative as an initiative.

Additionally, the study showed that resilience was negatively associated with depression, and loneliness was positively associated with depressive symptoms. According to Kumpfer’s resilience framework [52], resilience is a process of dynamic interaction between the environment and the individual. For MEFC, environmental changes resulting from migration may lead to changes in their resilience. Previous research has found that compared to that of community-dwelling elderly individuals, the resilience of MEFC is substantially lower [53]. Lower resilience means that older adults lack adequate bounce-back and coping skills in the face of adversity, which increases their risk of developing depressive symptoms [54]. Robertson and Cooper [55] pointed out that an individual’s resilience was not fixed, but could be enhanced through specialized training. A recent systematic review indicated that interventions utilizing resilience factors, including changing negative beliefs, accepting psychotherapy, improving social engagement and cognitive factors, could reduce the likelihood of depression in old age [56]. On the other hand, MEFC has fewer social connections and experience more obvious loneliness after being far from familiar social networks, which may induce depressive symptoms. Community-based “neighborhoods in solidarity” [57] and the formation of migrant associations [58] can distract from loneliness by counteracting social losses in the migration process.

The results of the present study showed that solid social support could alleviate depressive symptoms by enhancing resilience, which was consistent with the findings of Li et al. [22]. Social support is an important resource for individuals in coping with adversity and can enhance their resilience [59]. In turn, resilience can assist individuals to effectively cope with stressful events in adversity, thus helping to reduce negative emotions in older adults [18]. A study of Chinese community-dwelling older adults [60] revealed that resilience played a crucial role in reducing the negative impact of stressful life events on depressive symptoms in older adults. Therefore, improving resilience of MEFC by increasing social support will not only help them to better cope with life’s stresses and challenges, but also effectively reduce depressive symptoms.

Meanwhile, the present study further demonstrated that loneliness mediated the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms. Loneliness is shaped by the discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships [61]. Loneliness is related not only to the number of contacts an individual has but also to their quality and whether the existing contacts fulfill their needs [62]. It has been shown that the MEFC face challenges in accessing social resources and social support [63]. This unsatisfactory societal support has strengthened intergenerational support relationships. In fact, parents provide far more emotional support to their children than they receive on their own[64]. According to Gabarrell-Pascuet et al.‘s study [29], factors that impacted social relationships, such as intergenerational conflict and shrinking sources of social support, increased the risk of experiencing loneliness, which could cause a relapse of major depression in elderly adults. This finding supports the idea that loneliness is a mediator between social support and depressive symptoms.

In addition, social support also had a negative effect on depressive symptoms through the chain-mediated effects of resilience and loneliness. Possibly because, MEFC face a lack of social support when they migrate to new environments, and lower social support can further lead to lower levels of resilience. There was a negative correlation between individual resilience and loneliness, suggesting that people with lower levels of resilience were more likely to experience stronger feelings of loneliness [65]. And higher levels of loneliness increased an individual’s risk of experiencing depressive symptoms [66]. Therefore, enhancing social support, improving resilience, and reducing loneliness are important for reducing the occurrence of depressive symptoms among MEFC.

Limitations and implications

The study had some limitations that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, as a cross-sectional survey, it was not possible to reveal causal relationships between social support, resilience, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. Secondly, the data for this study were collected only in Changsha, Hunan Province. Thirdly, this study did not consider other factors that may have had an impact on the MEFC’s depressive symptoms, such as the duration of migration and economic status. In addition, this study focused only on depressive symptoms and ignored other aspects of mental health.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important theoretical and practical implications. Firstly, the relationship between social support, resilience, loneliness, and depressive symptoms was explored, which helps to uncover the mediating mechanisms of how social support affects depressive symptoms; Secondly, the results of the study provide an empirical basis for raising social awareness of depressive symptoms among MEFC. Additionally, this study highlights the potential role of resilience and loneliness in the relationship between social support and depressive symptoms, which provides new perspectives and directions for future intervention strategies.

Conclusions

In the present study, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among MEFC was relatively high. Significant correlations were found between depressive symptoms and social support, loneliness, and resilience. There were chain-mediated effects of resilience and loneliness in the association between social support and depressive symptoms among MEFC. In the future, interventions could focus on increasing social support to enhance resilience and reduce loneliness, thereby reducing depressive symptoms among MEFC.
