Haplotype-resolved genome assembly of a cultivated variety of Zizania latifolia (Zhejiao7)
Authors: Jun-cheng Wu, Fei Xu, Wan-long Jiang, Hui-shan Shao, Jing-tian Tang, Ya-fen Zhang, Zi-hong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-term reliance on vegetative propagation combined with continuous infections by Ustilago esculenta, has resulted in substantial genetic alteration in cultivated varieties of Zizania latifolia. In this study, we focus on the ‘Zhejiao7’ variety, with its characteristic of early maturation in both the summer and autumn seasons. We present the first haplotype-resolved genome assembly of the Jiaobai variety ‘Zhejiao7’, with the majority of chromosomes sequenced to the T2T level. With the mixed application of PacBio HiFi, Nanopore, Illumina, and Hi-C technologies, we assembled two haplotypes (Hap1: 610.06 Mb, Hap2: 585.52 Mb), each with 17 chromosomes. Functional annotation identified ~78% of the protein-coding genes. This high-quality assembly provides insights into domestication, agronomic traits, and stem expansion, facilitating research on Jiaobai’s bioactive compounds and medicinal properties and supporting advancements in agriculture and biomedicine.Subject terms: Data publication and archiving, Haplotypes

Background & Summary

Zizania latifolia (Griseb.) Turcz. ex Stapf, a member of the Poaceae family, is a traditional cereal grain with literature records in ancient China from more than two thousand years ago, closely related to Oryza sativa and is mainly distributed across East Asia1,2. However, with the widespread dominance of O. sativa and the challenges posed by seed shattering, low germination rates, and pronounced dormancy, Z. latifolia has become less common as grain in modern diets2–5. Now its edible swollen stem, induced by infection of the smut fungus Ustilago esculenta, known as Jiaobai in China and Makomotake in Japan, has been a valuable vegetable and is preferred by consumers for its delicious taste and nutritional value in East Asia2,5–7. While this fungal infection prevents flowering and seed formation, Jiaobai is propagated through asexual reproduction via underground rhizomes, a practice that has persisted for over 2,000 years in China8.

In China, Jiaobai has been an important economic crop, covering more than 60,000 hectares with an annual production value exceeding 13 billion RMB, making it the second-largest aquatic vegetable crop after Nelumbin Rhizomatis9. According to historical records, Jiaobai has been cultivated on a large scale for at least 400 years, and its domestication history maybe even longer10,11. Due to Z. latifolia- U. esculenta interactions, Jiaobai varieties show significant diversification in ripeness. To date, Jiaobai includes more than 100 varieties, which are categorized into two ecological types based on the harvesting season and frequency: single-season Jiaobai (harvested once in autumn) and double-season Jiaobai (harvested twice, in summer and autumn)12. ‘Zhejiao7’ is the first double-season variety recognized so far, that is particularly early-maturing in summer and autumn13. This variety is characterized by its smooth, white skin, tender flesh, minimal gray discoloration, and excellent commercial quality (Fig. 1).

Various views of a Jiaobai (‘Zhejiao7’) showing Shell state, after peeling, transverse section, longitudinal section.

In cultivated varieties, extensive genetic changes have arisen as a consequence of sustained vegetative propagation and chronic fungal infection by Ustilago esculenta8,12,14,15. The practice of asexual reproduction in Jiaobai has maintained desirable agronomic traits but has also led to a reduction in genetic diversity9,16. To better understand species differentiation, the process of domestication, and the functional roles of key genes, a complete reference genome is essential17.

The first genome of Z. latifolia was published in 2015, which, combined with resequencing, provided insights into the genome alterations induced by U. esculenta infection8. The subsequently release of genome sequences for Z. latifolia, include the Chinese wild rice (as grain crop)18 and a wild variety (as vegetable crop)19, have achieved chromosome-level. These genomic resources are invaluable for advancing our understanding of the crop’s genetic makeup and its potential for improvement. However, more accurate characterization of complex genomic regions is crucial for crops with reduced heterozygosity due to asexual reproduction such as Jiaobai20,21. Recent advances in sequencing technologies and bioinformatics algorithms, particularly haplotype-resolved assembly methods, offer promising solutions for overcoming these challenges22.

In this study, we present a haplotype-resolved genome assembly of ‘Zhejiao7’, a variety of Jiaobai known for its high commercial value and desirable traits, including early maturity, uniform harvesting. we employed a combination of sequencing technologies, including PacBio and Nanopore long-read sequencing, Illumina short-read sequencing, and Hi-C (high-throughput chromosome conformation capture) sequencing, to assemble the genome at the chromosome level. By using the haplotype-resolved assembler Hifiasm23, we generated two high-quality haplotype genomes—Hap1 and Hap2—and refined chromosome boundaries and gaps using advanced tools like Juicebox and Minimap2. The resulting genome assemblies are nearly telomere-to-telomere (T2T), providing a comprehensive reference for genetic studies and breeding applications. This work not only fills critical gaps in the genomic resources available for Z. latifolia but also showcases the potential of haplotype-resolved assembly techniques for resolving complex plant genomes.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

All samples were derived from ‘Zhejiao7’, a new Jiaobai variety breed by China Jiliang University in 2014. The variety was obtained from the university’s field gene bank located in Tongxiang, Zhejiang (30.69°N, 120.52°E), and is recognized as a high-quality, double-season Jiaobai cultivar. Genomic DNA was extracted from the stem tip tissue using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method24. Sequencing was performed on several platforms, including PacBio and Oxford Nanopore (ONT) long-read technologies, Illumina short-read sequencing, and Hi-C sequencing, all carried out at Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). RNA sequencing was performed on stem tips and fresh leaves of ‘Zhejiao7’ at various stages: tube seedling period, unexpanded stage, and early, middle, late, gray, and male Jiaobai stages. RNA samples from these various stages were pooled together for sequencing. Illumina short-read sequencing technology was used for this process, also conducted at Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd.

For ONT sequencing25, libraries were size-selected for approximately 50Kb segment length and sequenced on a PromethION 48 machine with three FLO-PRO114M chips using the SQK-LSK114 kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Oxford Nanopore). 68.53 Gb raw data was produced with about 112.32× coverage. For Pacbio HiFi sequencing26, high-quality DNAs were fragmented using g-TUBE (Covaris) to construct the SMRTbell libraries with the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit (PacBio), and sequencing was performed on a PacBio Sequel II machine. 54.6 Gb raw HiFi (high-fidelity) data was produced with about 89.60× coverage. Illumina sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit, with a library insertion length of 500 bp. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System, using the PE150 strategy. 92.61 Gb raw data with ~151.67× coverage. Hi-C libraries were constructed following Belton’s protocol and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform27, producing 228.08 Gb of clean reads with ~373.86× coverage. RNA-seq libraries were prepared from stem tips and leaves at various stages, sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System, and yielded 80.91 Gb of clean reads with ~132.62× coverage (Table 1).

Genomic characterization assessment

K-mer analysis was performed using Jellyfish (v2.2.10) with a k-mer size of 21 in count mode28. Default parameters were used if not explicated stated in the following contents. Genome characteristics, including heterozygosity and genome size, were estimated from the k-mer distribution using GenomeScope2 (http://qb.cshl.edu/genomescope/)29. The estimated genome size of Jiaobai was around 640.0 Mb, with an estimated heterozygosity of 0.45% and an error rate below 0.024% (Fig. 2A).

The X-axis is the k-mer depth, and the Y-axis represents the k-mer frequency for a given depth. (B) Chromosomes of 'Zhejiao7' were observed from a five-micron perspective, pair and arranged according to their morphology.

For cytogenetic analysis, young and healthy shoots of ‘Zhejiao7’ were collected and placed on moist soil for rooting. Shoot tips measuring 1-2 cm were excised and treated with 0.2 mol L-1 colchicine for 4 hours at 4 °C. The roots were subsequently fixed in Carnoy’s solution (glacial acetic acid: anhydrous ethanol = 1:3) for 4-24 hours. Following fixation, the samples were rinsed and treated with 1 M HCl for 45 minutes. The tissues were then digested with a solution of 6% cellulase and 4% pectinase in a 2:1 ratio for 5-6 hours, washed and incubated in distilled water at 37 °C for 30 minutes. The samples were stained with Carbol-Fuchsin solution, mounted on glass slides, and squashed under a cover slip for microscopic observation30. The results confirmed that ‘Zhejiao7’ has a chromosome number of 2n = 34, with no evidence of chromosomal deletions or duplications, corresponding to 17 chromosome pairs suitable for genome assembly (Fig. 2B).

Genome assembly

Genome assembly and haplotype phasing were performed using the Hi-C mode of hifiasm v0.19.823 by integrating HiFi reads, ONT reads, and Hi-C reads, resulting in two haplotype contig files: Hap1 and Hap2. Chromosome boundaries were manually adjusted and scaffolded. Using Illumina reads, Hap1 and Hap2 underwent 3 to 4 correction rounds with Pilon v1.2431, yielding assemblies of 610.06 Mb (Hap1) and 585.52 Mb (Hap2), with Scaffold N50 values of 33.27 Mb and 33.34 Mb, respectively (Table 2). Minimap2 v2.2632 was used to compare large segment sequences between the two haplotypes, with the contiguous haplotypes plotted on horizontal and vertical axes for structural observation. Discontinuous segment mapping facilitated motif searches at linear map ends, allowing manual connection of fragmented chromosome sequences to achieve chromosome-scale assembly33. Contig joining was performed using CLC Genomics Workbench 23 (QIAGEN)34. To validate the manually created chromosome-level assembly, cleaned Hi-C reads were mapped to the final dataset using fanc v0.9.28 and visualized with Juicebox v1.11.0835 (Fig. 3). Finally, we successfully anchored the 572.93 Mb (Hap1) and 556.67 Mb (Hap2) sequences to 17 pseudochromosomes for the assembly of the two haplotype genomes, accounting for 93.91% and 95.07% of the total genome sizes of Hap1 and Hap2, respectively (Fig. 4). In addition to the sequences mapped to chromosomes, Hap1 contains 601 non-anchored sequences totaling 37,130,400 bp, and Hap2 contains 376 non-anchored sequences totaling 28,841,179 bp (Table 2).

Genome annotation

RepeatModeler v2.0.4 was employed to annotate the repeats. Consensus sequences for each identified family were generated using additional LTR structural analysis conducted with related tools, including GenomeTools v1.6.236. Two haplotypes were indexed separately using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 in its default mode to identify different types of repetitive sequence markers in the genome37. The tagged repetitive sequences and genomes were compared and statistically analyzed using BLAST v3.0. The queries were aligned against the classified sequences in the consensus sequences for each identified family dataset. 393.03 Mb (64.43%) and 369.47 Mb (63.10%) repetitive sequences were annotated in the Hap1 and Hap2 genomes, respectively (Table 3).

Genome annotation of repetitive sequences and protein-coding genes.

Protein-coding gene prediction and functional annotation were subsequently carried out. To minimize false-positive predictions of gene structures in duplicated and low-complexity regions, genome duplicates were identified and soft-masked using RepeatMasker v4.1.537. Gene prediction methods primarily include de novo prediction, RNA-seq-based prediction, and homology-based prediction using protein data38. The prediction workflow is mainly carried out using BRAKER339. First, Trinity v2.15.1 was used for RNA-Seq assembly. The assembled transcripts, genome sequence, and a protein dataset from the OrthoDB v9 Viridiplantae kingdom, were inputted into the pipeline. The software configuration was thoroughly checked to ensure the smooth operation of the entire process. samtools v1.18, GeneMark-ETP, and ProtHint v2.6.0 were used to process the inputs. The gene prediction was done in GeneMark’s ETP mode (integrating protein and transcript information) using BRAKER v3.0.3. AUGUSTUS v3.5.0 and TSEBRA v1.1.1 were used to integrate and optimize the gene predictions, filtering, and selecting high-quality gene models. 45,205 and 48,284 protein-coding genes were predicted in the Hap1 and Hap2 genomes, respectively (Table 3).

The functional annotation of the two haplotypes was done using the Blast2GO40 Comprehensive Suite by integrating the results from Blast against NCBI-NR and Interproscan against Enzyme Commission (EC: https://enzyme.expasy.org/EC/) and Gene Ontology (GO: http://www.geneontology.org/page/go-browser). A total of 35,493 genes (78.52%) in Hap1 and 37,697 genes (78.07%) in Hap2 were successfully annotated (Table 3).

Comparison between haplotype assemblies

Using SyRI v1.6.3441 to compare the two haplotypes, we identified 1,465,571 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and detected several prominent hotspots of structural variation. Chromosome 1 contained the largest structural variant in the genome, characterized by a ~6.6 Mb inversion. Although only two inversions were detected on this chromosome, their combined length (~13.01 Mb) exceeded that of any other chromosome, suggesting extensive local structural remodeling. Chromosome 16 exhibited a more complex inversion pattern, comprising five discrete inverted segments that together form a continuous inversion block, indicative of multiple independent rearrangement events. Regarding duplication-related variation, chromosome 7 showed the highest density, with 88 DUP events and 115 DUPAL regions. The insertion–deletion (InDel) analysis revealed 151,531 InDels spanning 9.83 Mb, including 75,766 insertions and 75,765 deletions. Large InDels (>50 bp) were enriched on chromosomes 4 (415 events), 1 (413 events), and 2 (398 events) (Fig. 5).

Technical Validation

Genome assembly completeness was assessed using BUSCO v5.3.2 with the ‘auto-lineage’ parameter, comparing the assembled genomes and gene annotations to conserved single-copy orthologs in the poales_odb10 database49. The analysis showed that the completeness of Hap1 and Hap2 was 97.7% and 97.8%, respectively (Table 4). The hexamer of “CCCTAA” was manually observed and used as a motif to check the telomere completeness in the chromosome assemblies using the ‘locate’ module of the seqkit suite50,51. Results showed that 24 chromosomes (70.59%) in both haplotypes reached the T2T level (Table 2).

By comparing the assembly statistics of the two haplotype genomes with those of three recently published Z. latifolia genomes, we found that the completeness and continuity of our assemblies were comparable to those of the two cultivated Z. latifolia genomes and higher than those of the wild genome. These results indicate that the two haplotype assemblies of the cultivated variety (zhejiao7) are of high quality and reliability. Furthermore, this study represents the first haplotype-resolved assembly for this species, providing new insights into the characterization of its complex genome structure (Table 5).
