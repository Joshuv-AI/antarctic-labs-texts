Nodal T-cell lymphoma with eosinophilic infiltration and sclerosing fibroplasia in a cat with eosinophilia
Authors: Tamami SUZUKI, Jumpei YAMAZAKI, Kouta YAMAGUCHI, Keisuke AOSHIMA, Takashi KIMURA
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 8-year-old castrated male mixed-breed cat presented with an abdominal mass of unknown origin, accompanied by eosinophilia. Autopsy revealed mild-to-severe enlargement of lymph nodes throughout the body and multiple nodules in the lungs. Histopathologically, the lymph nodes showed severe fibroplasia and infiltration by a large number of eosinophils and fewer tumor cells, exhibiting large-sized lymphoid cell morphology. Metastatic lesions of tumor cells with eosinophilic infiltration and fibrosis were observed in the lungs, liver, kidneys, stomach, and intestines. Immunohistochemistry revealed that the tumor cells were positive for CD3 and negative for B cell and mast cell markers. Thus, T-cell lymphoma with eosinophilic infiltration and sclerosing fibroplasia was diagnosed.Keywords: eosinophilia, feline, lymph node, sclerosing fibroplasia, T-cell lymphoma

Eosinophilic diseases are common in cats, including eosinophilic ulcer, eosinophilic plaque, linear granuloma (eosinophilic complex) [5], and feline gastrointestinal eosinophilic sclerosing hyperplasia (FGESF). In addition, eosinophilic infiltration is observed in several types of tumors such as mast cell tumor [7] and intestinal T-cell lymphoma [13, 14]. FGESF is a distinctive disorder in cats observed in stomach, intestines and/or lymph nodes. Bacteria were detected in 32–56% of FGESF cases, but its etiology remains unknown [2, 4]. The histopathological features of those eosinophilic diseases and tumors with eosinophilic infiltration are similar, hindering accurate diagnoses. Here, we describe a case of nodal feline T-cell lymphoma with severe eosinophilic infiltration and sclerosing fibroplasia.

An 8-year-old castrated male mixed-breed cat presented with an abdominal mass of unknown origin. The number of peripheral eosinophils was markedly increased (43,151 cells/µL; reference range 0–1,000 cells/µL [3]). Computed tomography revealed an enlarged mesenteric lymph node (90 mm in diameter) and increased lung density. A punch biopsy of the mesenteric lymph node was performed, and T-cell lymphoma was suspected based on positive immunohistochemical (IHC) results for CD3. Prednisolone was administered for 11 days, but the cat developed hematemesis and collapsed, so the prognosis was considered poor. The patient was euthanized, and autopsy was performed two days after death.

Autopsy revealed pale mucosa of the conjunctiva and gingiva. The pale green to white, enlarged mesenteric lymph node measured 9.5 × 4.0 × 3.5 cm (Fig. 1A), with a uniformly white cut surface and extensive central necrosis (Fig. 1B). The other lymph nodes including pancreaticoduodenal, axillary, submandibular, mediastinal, renal hilar, and caudal mesenteric lymph nodes also showed mild to moderate enlargement and similar cut surfaces. The stomach contained abundant blood clots, and multifocal to coalescing areas of its mucosa were detached. Multiple white to yellow-tan nodules (1.5 × 1.0 cm in maximum) were observed in the lungs. The heart, lungs, stomach, intestines, liver, spleen, kidneys, and submandibular, renal hilar, mesenteric, and pancreaticoduodenal lymph nodes were collected and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathological examination. The bone marrow of the left femur was decalcified and fixed with 10% formic acid/10% formalin and neutralized with 5% sodium sulfate solution.

(A) Severely enlarged mesenteric lymph node. (B) Cut surface of the mesenteric lymph node showing extensive necrosis within the lymph nodes surrounded by a capsule, and growth of white tissue outside the lymph nodes.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 2 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Masson’s trichrome, toluidine blue, and Luna staining were applied to several sections. For IHC, endogenous peroxidase was blocked in 3% H2O2, and antigens were retrieved using a steam cooker and 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with 10% normal rabbit or goat serum, sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted with phosphate-buffered saline: CD3 (FLEX Polyclonal Rabbit Anti-Human CD3, Ready-to-Use; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) 1:1, CD20 (CD20 Polyclonal Antibody; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) 1:500, Granzyme B (Anti-Granzyme B Antibody; Abcam, Cambridge, England) 1:500, CD56 (PathPlusTM Monoclonal Mouse anti-Human NCAM/CD56 Antibody; LSBio, Lynnwood, WA, USA) 1:150, MUM1 (Monoclonal Mouse Anti-Human MUM1 Protein, Clone MUM1p; Agilent) 1:200, c-kit (Polyclonal Rabbit Anti-Human CD117, c-kit; Agilent) 1:100. After incubation with secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (Histofine® Simple Stain MAX PO; Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan), sections were visualized with diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride and counterstained with hematoxylin. To accurately assess mitotic activity, double labeling for CD3 and Ki67 (FLEX Monoclonal Mouse Anti-Human Ki-67 Antigen, Clone MIB-1, Ready-to-Use; Agilent) at a 1:1 ratio was performed as previously described [9].

To detect genetic clonality in antigen receptor rearrangement, DNA from the lung, spleen, stomach, ileum, bone marrow, mesenteric, pancreaticoduodenal, and punched-biopsy lymph nodes was extracted from FFPE tissues using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and then subjected to polymerase chain reaction using the primers and protocols as previously described (Immunoglobulin heavy chain [IgH] [10]; T-cell receptor [TCR] γ [11]).

Microscopically, the structures of all lymph nodes sampled were completely disrupted by diffuse, severe fibroplasia and infiltration of a large number of eosinophils and fewer tumor cells exhibiting large-sized lymphoid cell morphology (Fig. 2A). The average number of infiltrating eosinophils and tumor cells were 76 and 9 cells in the mesenteric lymph nodes and 59 and 13 cells in the pancreaticoduodenal lymph nodes in 0.1 mm2, respectively. The diameter of the tumor cells was 2–3 times the diameter of the red blood cells, and their nuclei were large, round-to-oval, and euchromatic (Fig. 2B, 2C). Anisocytosis and anisokaryosis were moderate. Mitotic neoplastic cells were 4 per 10 high power fields at 400× magnification in the mesenteric lymph node. Mild eosinophilic and moderate tumor cell infiltration and fibrosis were observed around the small arteries in the lungs (Fig. 2D). Severe eosinophilic and mild tumor cell infiltration and moderate fibrosis were observed in the hepatic portal areas. Similar lesions of eosinophilic and tumor cell infiltration and fibroplasia were observed in the renal interstitium and around the small arteries and veins in the gastrointestinal muscular layers. In the stomach, there were a severe multifocal transmural tumor cell infiltration, accompanied by mild eosinophilic infiltration with rare lymphocytes and macrophages and minimal fibrosis (Fig. 2E), and diffuse mucosal necrosis and ulceration. Trilineage hematopoiesis was observed in the bone marrow; however, the granulocyte lineage was moderately increased with mean myeloid/erythroid ratio of 11.1:1.0 (Fig. 2F). Masson’s trichrome staining of the fibrotic lesions revealed severe fibroplasia (Fig. 2G). Mast cell tumor was excluded because IHC for c-kit was negative in tumor cells, and toluidine blue staining did not reveal metachromasia of the tumor cells. The intracytoplasmic granules of eosinophils were stained red using Luna staining (Fig. 2H). IHC demonstrated that the majority of tumor cells in all tissues were positive for CD3 (Fig. 3A) and negative for Granzyme B (Fig. 3B), CD20 (Fig. 3C), CD56, MUM-1, and c-kit, suggesting an immunophenotype of T-cell without cytotoxicity. The percentage of Ki-67-positive tumor cells among CD3-positive cells was approximately 20% in both the lymph nodes and other organs (Fig. 3D). Lymphocyte infiltration in the ileum mucosa was positive for both CD3 and Granzyme B, and negative for CD20, indicating an immunophenotype of cytotoxic T-cell. No genetic clonalities were detected for IgH or TCR in any of the samples, including punch-biopsied samples that showed relatively low eosinophilic infiltration.

Based on the clinical and histological findings, the cat was diagnosed with T-cell lymphoma and paraneoplastic eosinophilia. Although clonality analysis was negative for both IgH and TCR, T-cell lymphoma was diagnosed based on the invasive growth of CD3-positive atypical tumor cells and evident distant metastases. Since genetic clonality was not detected in the biopsy lymph node, in which the population of tumor cells versus eosinophils was higher than in tissues sampled after death, we speculate that the primers might not have matched the TCRγ sequence of this case [11], or formalin fixation might have damaged DNA structures in the TCRγ regions.

Severe fibrosis and eosinophilic infiltration were observed in many organs, along with a small population of CD3-positive tumor cells. Although it is desirable to confirm whether tumor cells are CD4-positive or CD8-positive, they might belong to the helper T2 cell population because of the Granzyme B-negative expression. Interleukin-5, a helper T2 cytokine, stimulates the bone marrow to produce eosinophils that migrate into the peripheral blood [1]. The activation of granulopoiesis in the bone marrow in our patient is consistent with this hypothesis, and similar changes were observed in the bone marrow in a previous study by Takeuchi et al. [14]. In the present case, eosinophils in the tumor tissue may have induced activation of fibroblasts and sclerosis via interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [6].

As differential diagnoses of this case, sclerosing mast cell tumor and FGESF were considered. Paucity of positive cells in IHC for c-kit and no metachromatic cytoplasmic staining of tumor cells exclude the possibility of sclerosing mast cell tumor [7]. In FGESF, not only eosinophiles but also various types of inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells are observed in the lesions. In this case, however, almost all of the infiltrating inflammatory cells were eosinophils. Furthermore, in 32–56% of FGESF cases, bacteria have been detected at the affected sites by PCR, bacterial culture or histopathological analyses [2, 4]. In the present case, although no infectious agents were observed in the lesion, some of the histopathological findings, including severe fibroplasia with eosinophilic infiltration, may overlap with the findings in FGESF.

This case presented a feline nodal T-cell lymphoma with severe eosinophilic infiltration and sclerosing fibroplasia, contrasting to previous reports with intestinal presentation. We also noted histopathological characteristics of eosinophilic and tumor cell infiltration with fibroplasia in the metastatic organs. Additionally, this case exhibits pulmonary metastasis which has not been reported in previous cases [13, 14]. To date, few studies have investigated the immunological features of this malignant T cell [8, 12]. Further case studies are required to understand and define the immunophenotypes of feline T-cell lymphomas with eosinophilic infiltration and fibroplasia.
