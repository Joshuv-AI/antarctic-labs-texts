Performative Risk Control: Calibrating Models for Reliable Deployment under Performativity
Victor Li, Baiting Chen, Yuzhen Mao, Qi Lei, Zhun Deng

Abstract
Calibrating blackbox machine learning models to achieve risk control is crucial to ensure reliable decision-making. A rich line of literature has been studying how to calibrate a model so that its predictions satisfy explicit finite-sample statistical guarantees under a fixed , static , and unknown data-generating distribution. However, prediction-supported decisions may influence the outcome they aim to predict, a phenomenon named performativity of predictions, which is commonly seen in social science and economics. In this paper, we introduce Performative Risk Control , a framework to calibrate models to achieve risk control under performativity with provable theoretical guarantees. Specifically, we provide an iteratively refined calibration process, where we ensure the predictions are improved and risk-controlled throughout the process. We also study different types of risk measures and choices of tail bounds. Lastly, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework by numerical experiments on the task of predicting credit default risk. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first one to study statistically rigorous risk control under performativity, which will serve as an important safeguard against a wide range of strategic manipulation in decision-making processes.

Introduction

We have entered an era characterized by the ubiquitous deployment of increasingly complex models, such as large language models (LLMs) with billions of parameters
(
Achiam et al.,, 2023
;
Team et al.,, 2023
;
Team et al.,, 2024
)
. These models play important roles in our daily lives, informing us, shaping our opinions, and deciding allocation of societal resources. However, in most scenarios, these models can only be treated as a blackbox, either because they are too complex to understand or simply because their details are kept private by companies. Given their influence in today’s society and their blackbox nature, it is urgent to derive new tools to ensure reliable deployment.

A recent line of work
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2022a,
;
Bates et al.,, 2021
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2022b,
)
has been investigating methods to calibrate the predictions of blackbox machine learning models, generally known as (conformal) risk control. The goal is to ensure reliable deployment by satisfying explicit, finite-sample statistical guarantees (either with high probability or marginally) for controlling risk. The introduced frameworks are lightweight, agnostic to the data-generating distribution, and do not require model refitting. Specifically, in (conformal) risk control, a blackbox model
f
is given to us, and we need to post-process it using calibration data in order to make our final predictions. The calibration process is governed by a low-dimensional parameter
\lambda
(e.g., a threshold). For example,
Bates et al., (2021)
and
Angelopoulos et al., 2022b ()
study false negative rate (FNR) control in tumor segmentation, where
f:{\cal X}\mapsto[0,1]^{d\times d}
takes an image
x
as input and outputs scores for all the
d\times d
pixels in image
x
. For a given
\lambda
, one can produce
{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x):=\{i:f(x)_{i}\geqslant 1-\lambda\}
to include all the pixels with "high" scores. The aim is to control the expected loss:

\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}}\left[1-\frac{|y\cap{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x)|}{|y|}\right],

where label
y
is the set of pixels truly containing poly segments,
f(x)_{i}
is the
i
-th coordinate of
f(x)
, and
{\cal D}
is the underlying data-generating distribution.

Nevertheless, in many applications, predictions of machine learning models impact the outcome to predict, a phenomenon known as
performativity

(
Perdomo et al.,, 2020
)
. For instance, a bank’s loan policies may impact the purchasing patterns of the population, which in turn change the features used by the bank in predicting default risk, thus completing a cyclical relationship where predictions and data evolve together. So far, the important topic of risk control on blackbox models under performativity eludes the literature. In light of this, we
initate
the study of
Performative Risk Control
(PRC).

Formalizing our goal.
We post-process the outputs of the pre-trained model
f(x)
in
{\cal Y}
to generate predictions
{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x)\in{\cal Y}^{\prime}
indexed by a scalar threshold
\lambda
. Here,
{\cal T}_{\lambda}
could either be standard predictions, i.e.,
{\cal Y}^{\prime}={\cal Y}
, or prediction sets, i.e.,
{\cal Y}^{\prime}=2^{{\cal Y}}
. In this paper, we mainly study the expected risk. However, since we further consider the performativity of predictions, the measure of our interest is mainly the
performative expected risk
defined as

R(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim{\cal D}(\lambda)}\left[\ell(y,{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x))\right],

where
{\cal D}(\lambda)
is the distribution induced by the decision threshold
\lambda
and is typically unknown. In Sec.
4
, we also discuss a broader class of measures beyond expected risk. By carefully setting the parameter
\lambda
, we control a user-chosen error rate, regardless of the quality of

f
. Specifically, our goal is to calibrate
f
on a
calibration
dataset
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}
(specified later in Sec.
3.1
) and achieve the following:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\widehat{\lambda}\in\Lambda
be the threshold obtained by calibrating on
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}
. We say that risk
R(\widehat{\lambda})
is
(\alpha,\delta)
-performative-risk-controlled if

\mathbb{P}(R(\widehat{\lambda})\leqslant\alpha)\geqslant 1-\delta,

where the randomness is taken on
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}
. Here,
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
are both specified by the user.

Our contribution.
Our central contribution is to introduce an iteratively refined procedure to select risk-controlling decision thresholds in a performative environment, as illustrated informally in Fig.
1
. Our framework greatly generalizes the previous line of work on risk control for a static distribution to a dynamic and model-dependent distribution. In particular, our work can handle strategic manipulation of input distributions in decision-processes. Our framework satisfies explicit finite-sample statistical guarantees, and we present experimental results that highlight its practical utility. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to study risk control under performativity, and we believe our work will serve as an important safeguard in a wide range of applications in social science and economics.

1.1
Related Work

Performative prediction.
Follow-up works on performative prediction, first pioneered by
Perdomo et al., (2020)
, have extended the framework to various settings.
Miller et al., (2021)
provides conditions for which performatively optimal model parameters can be found, while
Brown et al., (2022)
considers the history of model deployments when modeling performative distribution shifts.
Jagadeesan et al., (2022)
and
Chen et al., (2023)
cast performativity into a bandit problem, with the assumption of additional knowledge through access to the samples. In these settings, the goal is to find a setting of parameters that in some way minimizes an expected loss and obtain a performative optimal point
\theta_{\text{PO}}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\,\ell(z;\theta).
However, due to the unknown
{\cal D}(\theta)
, this task is intractable, so much focus has been devoted to studying performative stability, i.e., finding
\theta_{\text{PS}}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{\text{PS}})}\,\ell(z;\theta).
In our setting, our objective is instead to
control
the expected loss, i.e., find the most aggressive setting of the threshold
\widehat{\lambda}
while guaranteeing
R(\widehat{\lambda})
is risk controlled. To the best of our knowledge, risk control in the performative setting has yet to be explored. For an overview on performative prediction,
Hardt and Mendler-Dünner, (2023)
serves as a solid reference.

Distribution-free uncertainty quantification.
Conformal prediction aims to endow black-box models with rigorous, finite-sample statistical guarantees
(
Vovk et al.,, 2005
;
Shafer and Vovk,, 2008
;
Angelopoulos and Bates,, 2021
)
. Conformal risk control is an extension to this framework that handles a more general class of loss functions
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2022a,
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2022b,
)
. These works are concerned with controlling the expected risk at some threshold
\alpha
; further work by
Snell et al., (2022)
extends this framework to more general quantile risks.

Risk control under distribution shift.
The canonical conformal prediction procedure relies on the assumption that the calibration and test data are i.i.d., or more generally,
exchangeable
. Because this assumption often does not hold in real-world environments, statistical extensions of conformal prediction have been developed to incorporate distribution shift.
Tibshirani et al., (2019)
deals with covariate shift, while
Gibbs and Candes, (2021)
handles distribution shift in the online setting by continuously re-estimating a single parameter to achieve exact coverage.
Barber et al., (2023)
generalizes conformal prediction to account for nonexchangeability through a procedure that weighs points in the calibration data higher if they are closer in distribution to the test data. Other works focus on making coverage robust to a set of possible test distributions, e.g., those in an
f
-divergence ball around the training distribution
(
Cauchois et al.,, 2024
)
and those due to feature alterations by strategic agents after model deployment
(
Csillag et al.,, 2024
)
.

Setup

2.1
Setting and Notation

For
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we use
[K]
to denote
\{1,2,\cdots,K\}
. For simplicity, we denote
{\cal Z}={\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
and
z=(x,y)
. Recall that we have a prediction function indexed by a threshold
\lambda
, where for a fixed
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
{\cal T}_{\lambda}
is a function mapping
{\cal X}
to
{\cal Y}^{\prime}
. Here,
{\cal Y}^{\prime}
could either be
{\cal Y}
or
2^{\cal Y}
. We further use
\ell(z,\lambda)
short for
\ell(y,{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x))
.

Monotonicity.
Throughout the paper, we consider
\ell(z,\lambda)
to be
non-increasing
with respect to
\lambda
for any
z\in{\cal Z}
. This is mainly following and motivated by previous work in standard risk control
(
Bates et al.,, 2021
)
. On one hand, one can think of the case that
{\cal T}_{\lambda}
is a set-valued function as in
Bates et al., (2021)
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2022b ()
, that

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\Rightarrow{\cal T}_{\lambda_{1}}(x)\subseteq{\cal T}_{\lambda_{2}}(x).

A larger set includes more candidates and provides a larger tolerance region that will decrease the loss values of loss functions such as the classical one corresponding to tolerance region, i.e.,
\ell(y,{\cal S})=\bm{1}\{y\notin{\cal S}\}
, which satisfies
{\cal S}\subset{\cal S}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\ell(y,{\cal S})\geqslant\ell(y,{\cal S}^{\prime}).
On the other hand, when
{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x)
is a standard prediction, larger
\lambda
indicates a more conservative but safer decision. For the example of credit loans,
{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x)=\bm{1}\{f(x)\leqslant 1-\lambda\}
may indicate whether an applicant gets credit (
1
means they got it). With increasing
\lambda
, the credit firm narrows the set of credit users, reducing the firm’s risk and leading to a decreasing loss function
\ell(z,\lambda)
w.r.t the threshold
\lambda
.

Setup for
\Lambda
.
We consider the case where there exists a
safe
threshold
\lambda_{\text{safe}}
such that
\ell(z,\lambda_{\text{safe}})=0
, and we consider
\Lambda=[\lambda_{\text{min}},\lambda_{\text{safe}}]
for
\lambda_{\text{min}}\in{\mathbb R}

1
1

1

Our setting follows
Bates et al., (2021)
, in which the loss value becomes trivially too large at
\lambda_{\text{min}}
and too small at
\lambda_{\text{safe}}
. This holds generally in risk control literature.
. For the example of
{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x)=\bm{1}\{f(x)\leqslant 1-\lambda\}
above, we can choose
\lambda_{\text{safe}}=1
and in the example of detecting tumor segmentation in
Bates et al., (2021)
, we can choose
\lambda_{\text{safe}}
large enough to make
{\cal S}_{\lambda_{\text{safe}}}
include all possible labels, so that
\ell(y,{\cal S}_{\lambda_{\text{safe}}})=\bm{1}\{y\notin{\cal S}_{\lambda_{\text{safe}}}\}=0
.

2.2
Our Desiderata and Significance

As mentioned in the introduction, our main goal is to select an
appropriate
threshold
\widehat{\lambda}
by using the calibration dataset
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}
, such that

\mathbb{P}(R(\widehat{\lambda})\leqslant\alpha)\geqslant 1-\delta.

However, there might be multiple choices for
\widehat{\lambda}
and how to define “appropriate" under performativity is nuanced. We further illustrate our extra desiderata here.

Achieve user-specified conservativeness.
Our setting prescribes that a larger
\lambda
leads to being more conservative. As an extreme case, choosing a
\lambda
large enough to include all the pixels in the tumor segmentation example
(
Bates et al.,, 2021
)
can guarantee zero loss, thus satisfying
(\alpha,\delta)
-performative-risk-controlled for all
\alpha\geqslant 0
. But this safe-guard choice of
\lambda
is not useful in practice since it sacrifices too much utility in providing useful class information. This illustration demonstrates that a user of this framework may further desire to choose a
\lambda
that is less conservative. To formalize this, we wish the final choice of
\widehat{\lambda}
also satisfies

R(\widehat{\lambda})\geqslant\alpha-\Delta\alpha

for a small user-specified
\Delta\alpha
. In particular, we show that our framework allows users to choose
\Delta\alpha
such that
\Delta\alpha\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This means that our framework can provide a
\widehat{\lambda}
also satisfying a tight lower bound guarantee for the performative risk, i.e.,
\widehat{\lambda}
is the optimal and least conservative in an
asymptotic
sense.

Safe at anytime.
Similar to the literature of performative prediction
(
Perdomo et al.,, 2020
)
, our algorithm is an iterative algorithm (see Sec.
3
for details). On a high-level summary, our algorithm guarantees that the
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
updated in each iteration is improving, i.e.,
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}<\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}
, until we find our final
\widehat{\lambda}
that satisfies the user-specified conservativeness. However, we don’t want any of the
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
’s to be chosen too aggressively, resulting in a threshold too small to be risk-controlled. This is especially important in policy-making, where a policy’s risk must be closely monitored as its usage is incrementally increased. Thus, we further require that the trajectory of the
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
’s is safe for all
t\in[T]
:

R(\widehat{\lambda}_{t})\leqslant\alpha,

where
T
is the iteration of return in our algorithm.

3
Performative Risk Control

In this section, we describe main results for PRC. We provide an iterative procedure to compute a series of safe thresholds. Specifically, we demonstrate that our framework provides statistical guarantees on the ability to maintain risk control at user-specified level
\alpha
throughout the iterative process, as well as obtain
\widehat{\lambda}_{T}
achieving user-specified tightness at
\alpha-\Delta\alpha
.

3.1
Our Procedure

To give a high-level summary, we provide an iterative procedure to obtain less conservative thresholds at every iteration. Specifically, not until the process is terminated, at time
t\geqslant 1
, we make progress by choosing a less conservative threshold
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
such that
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}<\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}
. Meanwhile, we ensure that
R(\widehat{\lambda}_{t})\leqslant\alpha
for all
t\in[T]
with high probability. Eventually, our procedure will end up with a
\widehat{\lambda}_{T}
that achieves user-specified tight risk control.

For user-specified
\delta,\alpha,\Delta\alpha
, the PRC procedure is outlined below. We start with setting
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{\text{safe}}
and then do the following process:

Performative Risk Control (PRC)

For
t\geqslant 1
, we sample
{\cal I}^{t}_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{t-1,i},y_{t-1,i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{t-1}}\in{\cal X}^{n_{t-1}}\times{\cal Y}^{n_{t-1}}
i.i.d. drawn from
{\cal D}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1})
(here,
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=\lambda_{0}
) and calculate

\widehat{\lambda}_{t}=\min\Big\{\inf\{\lambda\in\Lambda\mid V(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda,\delta)\leqslant\alpha\},\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}\Big\}.

If
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}<\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}-\Delta\lambda
for a progress measure
\Delta\lambda
, which means
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
makes big enough progress over
\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}
, then repeat the process with
t+1
. Otherwise, return
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
.

We specify details of
V
and
\Delta\lambda
later. Notice that the calibration dataset
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}
in Def.
1.1
is the union of calibration sets throughout the process, i.e.,
{\cal I}_{\text{cal}}=\cup_{t}{\cal I}^{t}_{\text{cal}}
. The formal and complete algorithm is listed in Alg.
1
.

3.2
Technical Details

In this subsection, we spell out details regarding various parameters and functions in our procedure. Before that, we need to state our key assumption. Intuitively, if the distribution shift of
{\cal D}(\lambda)
between implementing different
\lambda
’s is too dramatic, it will be hard to achieve performative risk control. Thus, we impose our key distributional assumptions below, which is similar to the one proposed in classical performative prediction
(
Perdomo et al.,, 2020
)
but more general.

Definition 3.1

(
(\gamma,p,g)
-sensitivity)
.

Let
g:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a deterministic function, and denote
\mathcal{D}_{g}(\lambda)
as the induced distribution of
g(z)
where
z\sim\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
. A distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
(\gamma,p,g)
-sensitive if for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda
,

W_{p}(\mathcal{D}_{g}(\lambda_{1}),\mathcal{D}_{g}(\lambda_{2}))\leqslant\gamma|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|

where
W_{p}
denotes the
p
-Wasserstein distance.

The following assumption applies
(\gamma,p,g)
-sensitivity to our setting. Intuitively, it means that the distribution of loss functions arising from samples drawn from similar thresholds are also similar.

Assumption 3.2

(Lipschitz Distribution Mapping)
.

We assume that
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
(\gamma,p,\ell(\cdot,\lambda))
-sensitive for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

We remark here that the distributional assumption in
Perdomo et al., (2020)
is a special case of our assumption, since it could be viewed as assuming
{\cal D}(\cdot)
is
(\tilde{\gamma},1,\ell(\cdot,\lambda))
-sensitive for a Lipchitz loss function
\ell(z,\lambda)
w.r.t
z
and an appropriately chosen
\tilde{\gamma}
in our setting. A more detailed discussion is deferred to App.
A.1
.

Now, we are ready to state further technical details. Without loss of generality, we can consider
n_{t}=n
for all
t\geqslant 0
since we can denote
n
as the minimum of
\{n_{t}\}_{t}
presented in our procedure, and all the results below still hold.

Choice of
V
.
We denote
\widehat{R}_{n}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{n}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\ell(z_{i}(\lambda),\lambda^{\prime})
to be the empirical mean on the loss of samples
\{z_{i}(\lambda)\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
when evaluated against the threshold
\lambda^{\prime}
. Thus,
{\cal I}^{t}_{\text{cal}}
could also be denoted as
\{z_{i}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{n}
. We further denote
R(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim{\cal D}(\lambda)}\ell(z,\lambda^{\prime})
.

To construct
V
, we require knowledge of a
confidence width

c(n,\delta^{\prime})
defining pointwise confidence bounds
\widehat{R}^{\pm}_{n}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}):=\widehat{R}_{n}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\pm c(n,\delta^{\prime})
that satisfy the following property for
\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}
and
\lambda^{\prime}\geqslant\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 1
encountered in the procedure:

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{R}_{n}^{-}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\leqslant R(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{R}^{+}_{n}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\right)\geqslant 1-\delta^{\prime}.

(1)

We discuss choices for
c(n,\delta^{\prime})
–including derivations from Hoeffding’s inequality, Bernstein’s inequality, Hoeffding-Bentkus’s inequality, and the central limit theorem–in App.
A.2
. We are further often interested in the risk profile of
R(\widehat{\lambda}_{t},\cdot)
, whose difference from
R(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\cdot)
can be bounded via sensitivity. We call this difference the
performative error
, or the error that arises from evaluating on two different distributions. In a nutshell, we hope to update
\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}\rightarrow\widehat{\lambda}_{t}
by accounting for increases in the risk due to generalization and performative error, which leads to the following form:

V(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda,\delta):=\underbrace{\widehat{R}_{n}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda)}_{\text{empirical loss}}+\underbrace{c(n,\delta/\tilde{T})}_{\text{confidence width}}+\underbrace{\tau(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}-\lambda)}_{\text{performativity guard}}.

Here,
\tilde{T}
is an upper bound on the number of iterations required in our procedure, not to be confused with
T
and discussed in (c). Meanwhile, the user-chosen parameter
\tau>0
guards against performative effects; we require
\tau\geqslant\gamma
. While
\gamma
is typically unknown, one can estimate its potential range or magnitude based on domain knowledge of the problem at hand to then choose a high enough
\tau
.

Choice of
\Delta\lambda
.
We choose

\Delta\lambda=\frac{1}{2\tau}\left(\Delta\alpha-2c(n,\delta/\tilde{T})\right).

\Delta\lambda
represents the minimum decrease in
\lambda
required for the procedure to continue iterating. We require
\Delta\lambda>0
to ensure that our procedure returns in a finite number of iterations.

Number of iterations
T
vs. guaranteed convergence
\tilde{T}
.
Because we require
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}<\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}-\Delta\lambda
and the interval
[\lambda_{\text{min}},\lambda_{\text{safe}}]
is finite, our algorithm terminates in at most
\lceil(\lambda_{\text{safe}}-\lambda_{\text{min}})/\Delta\lambda\rceil
iterations. Hence, by choosing
\tilde{T}\geqslant\lceil(\lambda_{\text{safe}}-\lambda_{\text{min}})/\Delta\lambda\rceil
, we can guarantee convergence, i.e.
T\leqslant\tilde{T}
always. Further, note the cyclical dependence between
\tilde{T}
and
\Delta\lambda
. We can find solutions
(\tilde{T},\Delta\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb R}
as follows: for a given
\tilde{T}
, we solve
\Delta\lambda=\frac{\Delta\alpha-2c(n,\delta/\tilde{T})}{2\tau}
and check if
\Delta\lambda\geqslant(\lambda_{\text{safe}}-\lambda_{\text{min}})/\tilde{T}
. If there are no such solutions
(\tilde{T},\Delta\lambda)
, the algorithm cannot guarantee that its sequentially produced solutions are safe; in this case, we just return
\lambda_{\text{safe}}
.

Details about
\Delta\alpha
.

Suppose we let
\tilde{T},n\rightarrow\infty
with
\tilde{T}=Cn^{r}
(for some constant

. We leave this derivation to App.
A.3
.

3.3
Theoretical Guarantees

In this subsection, we state our main theoretical result. By the following theorem, we demonstrate how our procedure can realize the promise of achieving user-specified conservativeness and being safe and reliable at anytime.

Theorem 3.3

(Risk control of PRC)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, where the distribution mapping
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
(\gamma,1,\ell(\cdot,\lambda))
-sensitive for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, if the loss function
\ell(z,\lambda)

for all

.
(ii)

for

.
(iii)

4
Extension

In this section, we discuss how PRC can be extended to handle quantile-based risk measures, which is commonly used in quantify tail risk and widely used in mathematical finance. In particular, both conditional variance-at-risk (CVaR) and expected risk belong to quantile-based risk measures. Denote the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of losses for

(Quantile-based risk measure)

6
Conclusion

We have introduced a theoretically sound and empirically effective framework for risk control under performativity, a previously unexplored topic in the risk control literature. To mitigate performativity, our framework iteratively refines a series of decision thresholds, careful to control risk. As a concrete example, we examined the deployment of a credit scoring system for loan approvals and found that PRC successfully controls risk, even as credit applicants seek to strategically manipulate their outcome. One limitation of our current framework is inherited from the line of work on risk control. Since risk control focuses on calibrating models via a simple postprocessing, it may not always be as effective as finetuning the entire model. For instance, this simple postprocessing may sometimes only output
\lambda_{\text{safe}}
. Nevertheless, given the growing role of blackbox models in shaping policies of societal significance, PRC offers an effective and proactive strategy for their safe deployment.

\left|R_{\psi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda)-R_{\psi}(\lambda_{2},\lambda)\right|\leqslant\gamma M|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|.

Proof.

\displaystyle|R_{\psi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda)-R_{\psi}(\lambda_{2},\lambda)|

\displaystyle=\left|\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\psi(p)F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{1},\lambda)dp-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\psi(p)F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{2},\lambda)dp\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\psi(p)\left[F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{1},\lambda)-F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{2},\lambda)\right]dp\right|

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\psi(p)\left|F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{1},\lambda)-F^{-1}(\cdot\mathbin{;}\lambda_{2},\lambda)\right|dp

\displaystyle=\left(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\psi(p)|^{u}dp\right)^{1/u}\left(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|F^{-1}(p\mathbin{;}\lambda_{1},\lambda)-F^{-1}(p\mathbin{;}\lambda_{2},\lambda)\right|^{v}dp\right)^{1/v}

\displaystyle\leqslant M\left(\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|F^{-1}(p\mathbin{;}\lambda_{1},\lambda)-F^{-1}(p\mathbin{;}\lambda_{2},\lambda)\right|^{v}dp\right)^{1/v}

\displaystyle=MW_{v}(\mathcal{D}(\lambda_{1}),\mathcal{D}(\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle\leqslant M\gamma|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|

∎

As a note on notation, from now on until the rest of this proof section, we write the risk measure as

R_{\psi}
(where the subscript

In the following proposition, we restate Theorem A.1 in

Proposition A.4

(Validity of UCB Calibration)
.

Assume we have deployed some
\lambda\in\Lambda
to obtain samples from the distribution
\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
. Let the true risk function
R(\lambda,\cdot):\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
and its
1-\delta^{\prime}
upper confidence bound (UCB)
\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\cdot;\delta^{\prime}):\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and non-increasing functions. Suppose there exists some
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda
such that both

R(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\leqslant\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime};\delta^{\prime})\leqslant\alpha.

Consider the smallest
\lambda_{*}^{+}
whose UCB falls below the risk level:

\lambda_{*}^{+}:=\inf\{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda:\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})<\alpha\}

Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(R(\lambda,\lambda_{*}^{+})\leqslant\alpha\right)\geqslant 1-\delta^{\prime}

Proof.

First, we claim that
\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\cdot,\delta^{\prime}):=\widehat{R}_{n}(\lambda,\cdot)+c(n,\delta^{\prime})
is non-increasing and continuous. By the property of the loss function,
\widehat{R}_{n}(\lambda,\cdot)
is non-increasing and continuous. Further,
c(n,\delta^{\prime})
is constant by construction, proving the claim.

Assume
R(\lambda,\lambda_{*}^{+})>\alpha
. Consider the smallest
\lambda_{*}
that controls the risk

\quad\lambda_{*}:=\inf\{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda:R(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\leqslant\alpha\}

Because
R(\lambda,\cdot)
is non-increasing and continuous,
\lambda_{*}^{+}<\lambda_{*}
. Further, since
\lambda_{*}^{+}
satisfies
\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\lambda_{*}^{+},\delta^{\prime})<\alpha
and
\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\cdot,\delta^{\prime})
is also non-increasing and continuous,
\widehat{R}_{n}^{+}(\lambda,\lambda_{*},\delta^{\prime})<\alpha
. However, since
R(\lambda,\lambda_{*})=\alpha
(by continuity), the pointwise confidence bounds in Equation
1
ensures that this occurs with probability at most
1-\delta^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem A.5

(Safety of Each Deployment)
.

Take the setting of Theorem
3.3

in

\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=\lambda_{0}

Proof.

We establish this theorem by verifying the following inductive statement: on iteration

for all

with probability
1-\delta t/\tilde{T}

To establish the base case, recall the definition of
\lambda_{\text{safe}}

\ell(Z,\lambda_{0})=\ell(Z,\lambda_{\text{safe}})=0<\alpha

Case 1:

\widehat{\lambda}_{t}=\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}

1-\delta t/\tilde{T}<1-\delta(t-1)/\tilde{T}

Case 2:

\widehat{\lambda}_{t}<\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}

\widehat{\lambda}_{t}=\inf\{\lambda\in\Lambda\mid V(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda,\delta)\leqslant\alpha\}

\displaystyle R\bigl(\widehat{\lambda}_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle\;=\;R\bigl(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{t}\bigr)\;+\;R\bigl(\widehat{\lambda}_{t},\widehat{\lambda}_{t}\bigr)\;-\;R\bigl(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle\;=\;\alpha\;-\;\bigl(\tau-\gamma M\bigr)\,\bigl|\widehat{\lambda}_{t}-\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1}\bigr|

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\alpha

A.3

in

Since by induction

s
in
0\leqslant s\leqslant t

∎

Proof of part (ii).

Proof of part (iii).

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\lambda}_{T})

\displaystyle=R(\widehat{\lambda}_{T-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{T})+\left[R(\widehat{\lambda}_{T},\widehat{\lambda}_{T})-R(\widehat{\lambda}_{T-1},\widehat{\lambda}_{T})\right]

A.5
Quantile extension proofs

The risk measure’s lower bound corresponds to the CDF upper bound.

Assume we have access to lower and upper CDF confidence bounds such that for all
\lambda\in\{\widehat{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
and
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{F}_{L,n}(w\mathbin{;}\lambda,\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\leqslant F(w\mathbin{;}\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{F}_{U,n}(w\mathbin{;}\lambda,\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\right)\geqslant 1-\delta^{\prime}\quad\forall w

We rewrite the above as
\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{F}_{L,n}\preceq F\preceq\widehat{F}_{U,n}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta^{\prime}
, dropping the
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
for the rest of this section. The following property establishes that
\widehat{F}_{L,n}\preceq F\preceq\widehat{F}_{U,n}
implies
\widehat{F}^{-1}_{U,n}\preceq F^{-1}\preceq\widehat{F}^{-1}_{L,n}
.

Proposition A.6
.

If
F\preceq G
, then
F^{-1}\succeq G^{-1}
.

Proof.

Recall the generalized inverse of a CDF
H
is

H^{-1}(p)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb R:H(x)\geqslant p\},\quad p\in(0,1).

Since
F(x)\leqslant G(x)
for all
x
, we have
\{x:G(x)\geqslant u\}\;\subseteq\;\{x:F(x)\geqslant u\}
, so

F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geqslant u\}\;\geqslant\;\inf\{x:G(x)\geqslant u\}=G^{-1}(u).

Hence
F^{-1}\succeq G^{-1}
.
∎

Applying a weighting function does not change this relationship. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{R}^{-}_{\psi,n}\psi(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\leqslant R_{\psi}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime})\leqslant\widehat{R}^{+}_{\psi,n}(\widehat{\lambda}_{t-1},\lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\right)\geqslant 1-\delta^{\prime}

as desired.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

This follows as a consequence of the proofs in App.
A.4
.
∎

B.2
Quantile Risk Control in Credit Scoring

Set up,
\gamma
-sensitivity, and the confidence width

For this experiment, we adopt the same setting as in expected risk and use the same shift technique. However, after training the logistic classifier,
we avoid any subsampling,
using the rest of the dataset. Hence, while

remains the same,

drops from

to
\gamma=pC\leqslant 0.205

To calculate the confidence width, we follow a recipe based on a version of CLT that is applicable to quantile-based risk measures. The recipe calls for knowledge of the loss CDF. Recall that our loss is given by
\ell(y,{\cal T}_{\lambda}(x))=yU[0,1](1-{\cal T}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x))
; we make a minor approximation to this loss function by taking
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, so that the loss is binary. Denote by
p^{\prime}
the proportion of the population that has
y(1-{\cal T}_{\lambda,0}(x))=1
. Because at most
p
of the population has
y=1
, we know that
p^{\prime}\leqslant p
, which holds for any given distribution-inducing
\lambda
and threshold
\lambda^{\prime}
. Knowing
p^{\prime}
allows us to derive the CDF and its inverse, which are piecewise linear because of the uniform distribution.

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{when }w<0\\
1-p^{\prime}+p^{\prime}w&\text{when }0\leqslant w\leqslant 1\\
1&\text{when }w>1\end{cases}

\displaystyle F^{-1}(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{when }0<r\leqslant 1-p\\
1-\frac{1-r}{p}&\text{when }1-p<r\leqslant 1\end{cases}

By Theorem 22.3 in
Vaart, (1998)
, we have

\sqrt{n}(R_{\psi}(\widehat{F}_{n})-R_{\psi}(F))\to_{D}N(0,\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)),

where

\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)=\iintop\nolimits(F(r\wedge\tilde{r})-F(r)F(\tilde{r}))\psi(F(r))\psi(F(\tilde{r}))\,dr\,d\tilde{r}.

Since the
\beta
-CVaR metric is bounded everywhere and has one discontinuous point, we can apply this theorem (appealing to dominated convergence). We split the calculation of
\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)
into two cases.

Case I:
\beta\leqslant 1-p^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}F(r\wedge\tilde{r})-F(r)F(\tilde{r})drd\tilde{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}F(r\wedge\tilde{r})-F(r)F(\tilde{r})drd\tilde{r}

Using
F(r)=1-p^{\prime}+p^{\prime}r
gives us

\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\frac{1}{12}(4-3p^{\prime})p^{\prime}

Case II:
\beta>1-p^{\prime}

We require
F(r)=1-p^{\prime}+p^{\prime}r\geqslant\beta
, or
r\geqslant(\beta-1+p)/p
for the weighting function to be nonzero. Similarly to above, the upper bound can be changed from
\infty
to
1
because
F(r)=1
for
r\geqslant 1
. The lower bound is then changed to
a=1-\frac{1-\beta}{p}
.

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\psi,F)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\intop\nolimits_{a}^{1}\intop\nolimits_{a}^{1}F(r\wedge\tilde{r})-F(r)F(\tilde{r})drd\tilde{r}
