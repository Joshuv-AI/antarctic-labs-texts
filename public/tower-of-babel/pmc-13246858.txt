Unveiling alternate pathways for SARS-CoV-2 infection via extracellular vesicle-mediated transfer of ACE2 and TMPRSS2
Authors: Martha Rea-Moreno, Lu Tian, Tara N Tavakol, Min-Chi Yang, Nicole M Pek, Shubham Gulati, Helena Bugacov, Cristina Cusmai, Gbalekan Dawodu, Remi V Klotz, Irving M Garcia, Hsu-Yu Chen, Chennan C Zhang, Heng Pan, Xisheng Li, Andrea S Wolf, Huachao Huang, Diana H Yu, Justin K Ichida, Susmita Sahoo, Paula M Cannon, Jianwen Que, Scott A Scheinin, Daniel Laskey, Alfin G Vicencio, Megan N Januska, Brandon A Guenthart, Jeffrey Johnson, Min Yu, Mingxia Gu, Caterina Tiozzo, Ya-Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, has underscored the urgency of understanding viral entry mechanisms to develop effective therapeutic strategies. SARS-CoV-2 primarily exploits angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as its entry receptor and relies on the serine protease TMPRSS2 to prime its spike protein, enabling membrane fusion and infection. Traditionally, TMPRSS2 has been described as a cell surface protein, but our study reveals that in human lung epithelial cells, TMPRSS2 is largely absent from the plasma membrane and instead resides intracellularly. We show that TMPRSS2 is secreted together with ACE2 in extracellular vesicles (EVs) from lung epithelial cells, which are subsequently taken up by non-epithelial cells, specifically alveolar macrophages, endothelial cells, and pericytes, that do not express TMPRSS2 or ACE2 mRNAs under homeostatic conditions. This EV uptake deposits ACE2 and TMPRSS2 protein onto recipient cells, equipping them for SARS-CoV-2 entry. By transferring these viral entry proteins, EVs expand the spectrum of susceptible cell types in the lung, offering a new explanation for how the virus can infect diverse cell populations and cause widespread tissue damage. Identifying EVs as vehicles for delivering functional ACE2 and TMPRSS2 across cell types reveals a previously unrecognized pathway of viral entry with important implications for not only COVID-19 pathogenesis but also for other viral infections that exploit similar entry mechanisms. These findings open new avenues for therapeutic intervention aimed at disrupting EV-mediated protein transfer, potentially limiting viral dissemination and severity, and may also represent a generalizable mechanism exploited by other viral pathogens, highlighting the potential relevance of EV-mediated protein transfer beyond SARS-CoV-2.Subject terms: Mechanisms of disease, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

The global upheaval caused by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, triggered by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has significantly impacted public health, economic, and social systems worldwide. Despite the widespread rollback of pandemic restrictions, the emergence of new virus variants continues to pose a persistent threat. This highlights the critical need for ongoing research into the mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2 infectivity, transmissibility, and pathogenicity. The virus’s rapid mutation rate, which is up to a million times faster than that of its hosts1,2, underscores the necessity of advanced research to keep pace with the evolving virus, thereby enhancing our preparedness for future healthcare challenges. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing effective interventions to prevent and mitigate not only current but also future viral outbreaks and emerging infectious disease threats. Moreover, the persistence of long COVID symptoms among many survivors underscores the importance of studying SARS-CoV-2 pathogenicity to address ongoing health issues3,4. This aspect of research is essential for developing targeted interventions that alleviate the prolonged health complications faced by some COVID-19 survivors.

SARS-CoV-2 primarily targets the respiratory system, with severe cases leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multi-organ failure, and death. The virus exploits specific host proteins, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) and transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2), to facilitate entry into host cells. SARS-CoV-2, like severe acute respiratory syndrome-related coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and Middle East respiratory syndrome-related coronavirus (MERS-CoV), depends on TMPRSS2 to enter cells5. This dependence makes TMPRSS2 a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of COVID-19, and since TMPRSS2 is a host factor, it is less likely that SARS-CoV-2 could mutate to evade this therapeutic target. The precise mechanism by which TMPRSS2 participates in SARS-CoV-2 infection has not been well elucidated, however. In addition, while much research has focused on respiratory epithelial cells in the context of COVID-19, how alveolar macrophages (AMs), endothelial cells (ECs), and pericytes (PCs) are infected and participate in disease progression is crucial yet underexplored. These cell types are integral to the lung’s immune response and vascular function, and their interaction with SARS-CoV-2 can significantly impact the severity of COVID-19.

AMs play a pivotal role in the lung’s immune response. Located within the alveoli where gas exchange occurs, these cells are among the first to encounter respiratory pathogens. In the context of COVID-19, AMs contribute to the innate immune response and are implicated in the cytokine storm often observed in severe cases6. These cells can either help clear the infection or exacerbate lung injury through the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines7. ECs line the entire circulatory system and are critical for maintaining vascular health and function. COVID-19 has been shown to induce endothelial dysfunction, contributing to widespread vascular inflammation, thrombosis, and microangiopathy—conditions that are now recognized as hallmarks of severe COVID-198,9. The virus’s interaction with ECs highlights the systemic nature of the disease and its capacity to affect multiple organs and tissues beyond the lungs10. PCs are located on the microvascular walls and are essential not only for blood vessel stability and blood-brain barrier integrity, but also for maintaining the blood-lung barrier which is particularly critical during COVID-19-associated microvascular injury and dysfunction11,12. Damage to PCs can lead to capillary destabilization, contributing to the vascular leak and pulmonary edema often seen in acute COVID-19 cases13. Understanding how SARS-CoV-2 infects AMs, ECs, and PCs offers profound insights into the virus’s sophisticated strategies for invading the human body.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound particles released by cells that facilitate intracellular communication and the transfer of biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. EVs have been implicated in a range of lung diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and lung cancer14–23. They have also been shown to play a role in immune regulation and in lung tissue repair and regeneration. In addition, EVs have been implicated in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis. Recent studies have shown that SARS-CoV-2 can infect and replicate in various cell types in the lung, including epithelial cells, macrophages, and ECs5–7,24–27, which can then release EVs containing viral components26,28,29. These EVs can potentially facilitate the spread of the virus to other cells in the lung, as well as to other organs in the body. Much of the research on the roles of EVs in SARS-CoV-2 infection and transmission has focused on the viral components that can be detected in EVs, rather than on a primary role of EVs in the infection and transmission of the virus30,31. Our research shows that EVs containing TMPRSS2 and ACE2 proteins can facilitate viral transmission to cells. This novel mechanism underscores the potential of targeting EV-mediated pathways to develop new therapeutic strategies against COVID-19.

In this study, we show that TMPRSS2 is located intracellularly and is secreted in EVs with ACE2 from lung epithelial cells. These EVs containing TMPRSS2 and ACE2 enhance SARS-CoV-2 infection and potentially promote transmission in the lungs. Additionally, EVs derived from lung epithelial cells deliver TMPRSS2 and ACE2 to non-epithelial cells, such as AMs, ECs, and PCs, facilitating viral entry and infection. This finding is crucial not only for the effective management of COVID-19 but also for enhancing our preparedness against future viral epidemics that exhibit similar systemic behaviors.

Results

High-efficiency SARS-CoV-2 infection in lung organoids

We previously developed a lung organoid model, primarily containing alveolar type II epithelial (AT2) cells, from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs)32,33. Expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 increased as the organoids matured in culture at both the mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, the amounts of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 were higher in lung organoids compared to conventional cell lines used for SARS-CoV-2 studies (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Comparable to cell lines, lung organoids were readily susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 Spike pseudotyped vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) vectors with efficient vector entry (about 25%) observed (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). This pseudoviral entry primarily depended on TMPRSS2 activity rather than cathepsin L (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). We further tested SARS-CoV-2 live virus infection in lung organoids. Unlike other infection methods where organoid cultures were either disrupted mechanically or digested enzymatically into single cells34,35, we exposed the cultures to virus by simply replacing the culture media with virus-containing media at corresponding multiplicity of infections (MOIs). This allowed us to preserve the structure and physiological location of cells in the organoids. SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid proteins (NPs) were readily detected one day post infection (dpi) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The majority of cells remained viable at 4 dpi but sustained viral damage (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Both quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR, Supplementary Fig. 2c) and plaque assay (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e) indicated that live SARS-CoV-2 virus can infect and replicate in lung organoids. These results demonstrate that lung organoids provide an authentic model for respiratory viral pathogenesis, recapitulating respiratory viral infection in the host and serving as a valuable tool to study COVID-1932,36.

Subcellular locations of TMPRSS2 in the lung

TMPRSS2 has long been classified as a transmembrane protease located on the cell surface37. However, we found that it resides in intracellular vesicles, while also being secreted in EVs. In lung organoids, immunofluorescent staining of TMPRSS2 showed intracellular localization close to the plasma membrane (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The punctate staining pattern suggested that TMPRSS2 was inside vesicles (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 3a, right panels). To further investigate the subcellular location of TMPRSS2, we labeled the cell surface proteins with biotins and subsequently separated the cell surface and cytoplasmic portions by streptavidin beads. TMPRSS2 was readily detected in the total lysates and cytoplasmic portions but not the cell surface portion (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3b). This result was supported by cell surface (no fixation and no permeabilization) and intracellular (fixation and permeabilization) analysis of TMPRSS2 using flow cytometry. Strikingly, no TMPRSS2 expression was observed on the cell surface; its expression was exclusively intracellular (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3c–e). Given that the antibodies we used are designed to recognize the extracellular domain of TMPRSS238, these results further supported the intracellular location of TMPRSS2. Since antibody binding can potentially induce shedding of the targeted protein, we incubated antibodies with cells on ice, then collected the supernatant for mass spectrometry analysis and fixed the cells for subsequent flow cytometry analysis. Incubation at low temperatures (0 °C–4 °C) is known to reduce membrane dynamics and protein shedding. Mass spectrometry revealed none to negligible levels of TMPRSS2 in the supernatant, indicating that antibody binding did not result in significant shedding (n = 9, Supplementary Fig. 3f). To further control for potential shedding during flow cytometry preparation, we fixed the cells immediately after antibody incubation on ice to preserve any surface-bound TMPRSS2. Despite fixation, TMPRSS2 was not detected on the cell surface (Supplementary Fig. 3g). These findings suggested that the absence of surface TMPRSS2 was not due to antibody-induced shedding. To further confirm our observation, we performed immuno-electron microscopy (immuno-EM) using normal and COVID-19 lungs. TMPRSS2 was detected in the cytoplasm or right beneath the plasma membrane (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Consistent with immunofluorescence staining, TMPRSS2 was also observed within vesicle-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 4b, e). In addition, we found that the majority of SARS-CoV-2 NPs were located in close proximity to TMPRSS2 intracellularly (Fig. 1e). NPs were frequently surrounded by TMPRSS2 (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 4b, d–g) with 87% of NPs associated with TMPRSS2 (Fig. 1e), including 12.91% with one, 15.06% with two, 19.07% with three, and 37.13% with more than three TMPRSS2 molecules nearby (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Furthermore, we detected intracellular co-localization of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 4h). These findings provide direct ultrastructural evidence of TMPRSS2 localization within the cytoplasm and vesicle-like structures, often in proximity to SARS-CoV-2 NPs and ACE2.

Extracellular vesicles containing TMPRSS2 and ACE2 enhance SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus transduction

Given that immunofluorescent imaging revealed TMPRSS2 localized in intracellular punctate structures, we next investigated whether TMPRSS2 co-localized with canonical EV markers within cells. To address this, we performed confocal imaging with co-staining for TMPRSS2 and the EV markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 (Fig. 2a), followed by quantification (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Our results showed that 52.45% of TMPRSS2 co-localized with CD9, 36.79% with CD63, and 38.20% with CD81 (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Conversely, 25.51% of CD9 co-localized with TMPRSS2, 16.54% of CD63 with TMPRSS2, and 35.96% of CD81 with TMPRSS2 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results indicate that a substantial proportion of TMPRSS2 is associated with EVs marked by these canonical markers. To further support these findings, we performed immune-EM in lung organoids and found TMPRSS2 was located in close proximity to CD63 (Supplementary Fig. 5c) and TSG101 (Supplementary Fig. 5d), reinforcing its association with EV structures. To examine if TMPRSS2 is indeed secreted in EVs, EVs were isolated from lung organoid conditioned media (LOCM), and the purity was confirmed by the EV markers TSG101 and CD63 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6a). TMPRSS2 was detectable in EVs and total cell lysates but not in conditioned media without EVs (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6a). In contrast, ACE2 was detected in all three portions, suggesting that ACE2 can be secreted in EVs and as free forms (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6a). To determine the orientation of TMPRSS2 on EVs, we performed immune-EM on purified EVs using antibodies against the extracellular domain of TMPRSS2 under either permeabilized or non-permeabilized conditions. The data showed that, unlike ACE2 whose extracellular domain was predominantly oriented outside EVs (Fig. 2c, no perm), the majority of the extracellular domain of TMPRSS2 was oriented inside EVs (Fig. 2c, perm). The presence of both TMPRSS2 and ACE2 in EVs suggests that these vesicles participate in SARS-CoV-2 infection and transmission in host lungs. To test this, we incubated purified EVs (Supplementary Fig. 6b) with SARS-CoV-2-GFP pseudovirus and then exposed them to lung organoids. The SARS-CoV-2 transduction rate increased in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d), suggesting EVs could enhance SARS-CoV-2 infection and potentially promote transmission. To further clarify whether EVs act directly on viruses or on recipient cells to enhance transduction, we conducted complementary pseudovirus entry assay (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). When EVs were preincubated with SARS-CoV-2-GFP (S18) pseudovirus prior to transduction, transduction efficiency was significantly increased relative to pseudovirus-only controls, indicating a potential hitchhiking mechanism mediated by ACE2 and TMPRSS2 on EVs. In contrast, EV preincubation did not alter transduction by VSV-G or bald pseudoviruses, confirming spike-specific effects. When EVs were preincubated with cells before viral exposure, no enhancement of transduction was observed, suggesting that brief contact was insufficient for functional ACE2 and TMPRSS2 transfer.

Aberrant localization of TMPRSS2 in diseased lung

We further confirmed our observations using flow cytometry analysis of human specimens. As expected, none or minimal TMPRSS2 was detected on the cell surface of the normal lung epithelium (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Interestingly, we noted that the expression pattern of TMPRSS2 changed in diseased patient samples. We detected TMPRSS2 on the cell surface of ~1–10% of lung epithelial cells in patients with lung diseases, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), COVID-19 induced fibrosis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and others (Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). This observation is consistent with reports that patients with underlying pulmonary pathology are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection39–41. Furthermore, we observed a non-epithelial cell population that expressed TMPRSS2 on the cell surface (EPCAM-TMPRSS2surf+) in both healthy and diseased human lungs. From the analysis of two published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) lung datasets containing healthy, ILD, COPD, and IPF samples42,43, TMPRSS2 and ACE2 are predominantly expressed in lung epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 8). TMPRSS2 expression was minimal or undetectable in non-epithelial (EPCAM-) cells. No significant differences were observed in the proportion of TMPRSS2+ cells across non-epithelial cell populations between control and disease groups. Since TMPRSS2 is an epithelial gene, we did not expect to see EPCAM-TMPRSS2+ cells. It is possible that these cells receive EVs and deposit TMPRSS2 on the cell surface, even though they do not express TMPRSS2 mRNA.

Cell surface TMPRSS2 expression on AMs and ECs

To identify the non-epithelial cells that express TMPRSS2 on their cell surface, we performed fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) to sort different cell populations from an IPF lung using antibodies against EPCAM and TMPRSS2 and then performed scRNA-seq. As expected, the EPCAM+TMPRSS2surf- population expressed TMPRSS2 and ACE2 mRNAs (Fig. 4a, b, P7). However, the EPCAM-TMPRSS2surf+ population showed no detectable TMPRSS2 or ACE2 mRNAs (Fig. 4a, b, P6). After assigning cell types, we found that the EPCAM-TMPRSS2surf+ population contained AMs, ECs, and PCs (Fig. 4c, arrows and 4d). Excitingly, we found that approximately 90.28 ± 1.58% of ECs (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 9a) and 90.11 ± 1.16% of AMs (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 9b) in explanted lungs and about 93.38 ± 1.23% of AMs in adult and pediatric bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF, Fig. 5c) expressed TMPRSS2 on the cell surface, further supporting the possibility that EVs deposit TMPRSS2 protein to cells that do not transcribe the TMPRSS2 gene.

Lung epithelial cell-derived EVs deliver TMPRSS2 and ACE2 proteins to macrophages and ECs

To further validate our hypothesis, we employed hPSC-derived macrophages (hPSC-Ms) and hPSC-derived vessel organoids (hPSC-VOs), the latter containing ECs and PCs, that lack inherent TMPRSS2 and ACE2 proteins and had not been exposed to EVs generated by epithelial cells (Fig. 6a, b, d, -EVs). Following exposure to LOCM-purified EVs (LOCM-EVs), TMPRSS2 and ACE2 were readily detectible in both the hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs (Fig. 6b, d, +EVs). There is evidence that EVs can carry damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and Toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands, which are capable of triggering transcriptional upregulation of TMPRSS2 and ACE244. To determine whether such mechanisms contributed to TMPRSS2 and ACE2 surface expression in our system, we analyzed mRNA expression levels in LOCM-EV exposed hPSC-Ms. We found no significant upregulation of TMPRSS2 or ACE2 transcripts at 24- or 48-hours post-exposure (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Similarly, treatment with the TLR4 agonist lipopolysaccharide (LPS) failed to induce their transcription (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). To further confirm the localization of TMPRSS2 on the cell surface, we incubated hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs with and without LOCM-EVs and conducted flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 6c, e, and supplementary Fig. 11). Results from flow cytometry revealed that, post-incubation with LOCM-EVs, nearly all macrophages and ECs exhibited TMPRSS2 on their cell surface (Fig. 6c, e), emphasizing the role of EVs in protein transfer and further supporting the efficacy of EV-mediated protein transfer across different cell types. Consistent with this, pharmacological inhibition experiments revealed that disruption of actin polymerization with cytochalasin D and depletion of membrane cholesterol with methyl-β-cyclodextrin strongly impaired EV uptake, while inhibitors targeting clathrin-mediated endocytosis (pitstop 2, dynasore), caveolae (filipin, nystatin), and macropinocytosis (wortmannin) only partially inhibited EV uptake (Supplementary Fig. 10c). These results suggest that EV internalization in these cells is largely reliant on actin- and cholesterol-dependent pathways, which may facilitate the acquisition of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 by recipient cells. Together, these findings support that the observed surface localization of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 results from direct EV-mediated protein transfer rather than de novo transcriptional activation.

Enhanced susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 in cells primed with EVs and the critical role of TMPRSS2 in EV-mediated infection

We next sought to determine whether hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs primed with EVs would exhibit increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. To test this, hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs were incubated with LOCM-EVs for 24 h. After removing excess EVs, these cells were exposed to SARS-CoV-2, followed by a plaque assay to measure viral titers (Fig. 7a). Both hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs primed with LOCM-EVs had significantly higher viral titers compared to their unprimed counterparts (Fig. 7b, c). This increase in viral susceptibility suggests that the transfer of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 proteins is via EVs, facilitates more efficient viral entry, and highlights the potential for EVs to serve as a key factor in the enhancement of viral infection dynamics in previously resistant cell types. To further explore the role of TMPRSS2 in EV-mediated SARS-CoV-2 infection, we generated a TMPRSS2 knockout (TMPRSS2-KO) RUES2 line (Fig. 7d). One of the key challenges in dissecting the contribution of individual EV components is that knocking out such components in donor cells can alter the release or composition of generated EVs. To address this, we performed mass spectrometry of EVs derived from WT and TMPRSS2-KO lung organoids and found that the overall protein composition was largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 12a–d). We then primed hPSC-Ms with TMPRSS2-KO EVs and confirmed that these cells acquired ACE2 but not TMPRSS2 (Fig. 7e, Supplementary Fig. 12e). We further assessed whether TMPRSS2-KO EVs induced endogenous TMPRSS2 or ACE2 expression in recipient cells and found no significant increase in TMPRSS2 or ACE2 mRNA levels upon incubation with either WT or TMPRSS2-KO EVs, suggesting that the observed acquisition is due to EV-mediated protein transfer rather than transcriptional activation (Supplementary Fig. 12f and 11g). Functionally, priming with TMPRSS2-KO EVs led to a reduction in viral titers in hPSC-Ms and hPSC-VOs by approximately 70% and 60%, respectively, compared with cells primed with WT EVs (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 12h–j), confirming the critical role of TMPRSS2 in EV-mediated viral enhancement.

Discussion

There is increasing interest in treating COVID-19 by targeting TMPRSS2, given its key role in viral entry and spread between cells5,45. Previous studies have shown that targeting TMPRSS2 can inhibit SARS-CoV-2 viral entry and spread46,47. Here, we show that hPSC-derived lung organoids can faithfully recapitulate SARS-CoV-2 infection and that the key protease TMPRSS2 is located intracellularly and secreted in EVs from lung epithelial cells. In addition, our study reveals a critical mechanism by which EVs carrying ACE2 and TMPRSS2 facilitate the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into AMs, ECs, and PCs. Similar observations have been reported in other systems, including work showing that ACE2- and TMPRSS2-bearing EVs can mediate SARS-CoV-2 entry into recipient cells lacking these proteins48. This discovery expands our understanding of SARS-CoV-2’s ability to infect diverse cell types beyond those traditionally known to express the entry receptor ACE2 and the protease TMPRSS2.

TMPRSS2 has long been classified and described as a transmembrane serine protease located on the cell surface due to its protein structure. However, our studies demonstrate the intracellular location of TMPRSS2 and its presence in secreted EVs from lung epithelial cells. TMPRSS2 has been reported as an essential protease for priming the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to fuse the viral envelope with the host cell membrane, enabling the virus to enter host cells5. Whether this priming actually occurs on the cell membrane has not been fully investigated. Our discovery of the co-localization of TMPRSS2 and SARS-CoV-2 NPs in intracellular vesicles suggests that spike protein priming might happen within the cell. In addition, changes in the location of TMPRSS2 expression – shifting from the intracellular space to the cell surface in diseased lung epithelial cells – changes SARS-CoV-2’s access to this key protease, thereby enhancing infection. This is consistent with the fact that patients with underlying pulmonary pathology are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection39–41. Furthermore, we show that TMPRSS2 is secreted in EVs along with ACE2 and that EVs significantly enhance the SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus transduction rate. These data suggest that SARS-CoV-2 binds to and travels with EVs, providing a potential mechanism for viral transmission in the lungs and to cell types that do not inherently express TMPRSS2 or ACE249. Alternatively, our immune-EM suggests that viruses might be secreted and travel alongside TMPRSS2 and ACE2 in EVs to infect other cells. Interestingly, a recent study also found that SARS-CoV-2 was more likely to exit the cell in small secretory vesicles in Vero cells50.

Although preincubation of EVs with pseudovirus supports a hitchhiking model in which virions associate with ACE2- and TMPRSS2-containing vesicles, we cannot exclude the possibility that receptor transfer to recipient cells also contributes during the full transduction period when EVs and pseudovirus are present concurrently. In our assay design, EVs remained in contact with target cells throughout viral exposure, and prolonged interaction may allow sufficient time for ACE2 and/or TMPRSS2 delivery to enhance susceptibility prior to or during viral entry. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may operate in parallel within the lung microenvironment. Future temporally resolved experiments will be required to dissect the relative contribution of direct virus–EV association versus EV-mediated receptor transfer to recipient cells.

Our data show that the EV-mediated transfer of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 occurs constitutively in healthy lung epithelial cells, independent of viral infection and other overt pathological stimuli. This suggests that the presence of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 on recipient cell types, such as AMs and ECs, may not be a virus-induced phenomenon but rather a baseline feature of lung epithelial EV biology. Although the physiological purpose of this constitutive protein transfer remains unclear, its existence may predispose non-epithelial lung cell types to SARS-CoV-2 infection by equipping them with the necessary viral entry proteins even before infection occurs. This mechanism may help explain why cells that do not transcriptionally express TMPRSS2 or ACE2 can nonetheless be infected in vivo, and it underscores a potentially critical role for homeostatic EV signaling in shaping viral tropism and pathogenesis in the lung. Future studies using infected epithelial organoids may help define how this process is modulated by viral exposure and other inflammatory cues.

The findings from our study provide compelling evidence for a novel and previously underappreciated mechanism of SARS-CoV-2 entry into AMs, ECs, and PCs via EV-mediated delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2. Understanding the potential routes through which these EVs deliver ACE2 and TMPRSS2 to these cells is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. One of the primary mechanisms by which EVs can deliver their cargo to recipient cells is through direct fusion with the plasma membrane51. This process involves the EV membrane merging with the host cell membrane, releasing the EV contents directly into the cytoplasm. In the context of our findings, EVs containing ACE2 and TMPRSS2 could fuse with the membranes of AMs and ECs, depositing these proteins on the cell surface or into the cytoplasmic compartment. This would effectively equip these cells with the necessary machinery for SARS-CoV-2 entry, which would otherwise be absent. Another possible route for EV uptake is endocytosis, a process whereby cells engulf EVs, incorporating them into endosomes. Once inside the endosomes, the EVs can release their cargo either into the endosomal lumen or, following endosomal maturation and fusion with lysosomes, directly into the cytoplasm52. This pathway is particularly relevant for AMs and ECs, which are known to actively engage in endocytosis for various physiological functions. Upon internalization, ACE2 and TMPRSS2 could be trafficked to the cell surface or other relevant cellular compartments, thus facilitating viral entry. Given that we observed EVs carrying ACE2 and TMPRSS2 being delivered specifically to AMs, ECs, and PCs in the lung, it is highly possible that receptor-mediated uptake is a key route for EV internalization in these cell types. This targeted uptake involves specific interactions between proteins on the surface of EVs and receptors on the recipient cell surface, triggering EV internalization. In our study, these interactions likely facilitate the selective and efficient delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 to AMs, ECs, and PCs. Identifying these specific receptors and their ligands could offer new therapeutic targets to block this pathway and prevent the spread of infection. AMs and ECs might also internalize EVs through macropinocytosis, a form of endocytosis involving the non-specific uptake of extracellular fluid and its contents into large vesicles called macropinosomes53. This pathway is particularly active in immune cells, including macrophages, and might be exploited by EVs to deliver their cargo. Once inside the cell, the contents of the EVs, including ACE2 and TMPRSS2, could be processed and presented on the cell surface or other relevant intracellular locations.

Importantly, analysis of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 mRNA expression levels in EV-exposed hPSC-Ms showed no significant upregulation at 24 or 48 hours, and treatment with LPS, a TLR4 agonist, similarly did not increase TMPRSS2 or ACE2 mRNA expression. These findings further support that the observed surface localization of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 is due to direct EV-mediated protein transfer rather than de novo transcriptional activation. Recently findings by Yao et al.44 suggest that TLR stimulation of circulating monocytes leads to surface translocation of ACE2 and TMPRSS2, a mechanism that may help resolve discrepancies between protein and mRNA expression. However, this mechanism is specific to circulating monocytes and does not fully explain how tissue-resident macrophages in the lung acquire these proteins in the absence of detectable mRNA. Unlike Yao’s model, our study directly shows that EVs from lung epithelial cells deliver ACE2 and TMPRSS2 proteins to macrophages. This mechanism differs fundamentally from transcriptional upregulation or intracellular recycling and provides an alternative explanation for how lung-resident macrophages acquire these proteins in the absence of detectable mRNA. Our findings complement prior observations by Sefik et al.54, who reported ACE2 protein expression in lung macrophages, further supporting EV-mediated delivery as a physiologically relevant mechanism.

Consistent with these proposed uptake routes, our inhibitor experiments revealed that blocking actin polymerization with cytochalasin D or depleting membrane cholesterol with methyl-β-cyclodextrin produced the strongest inhibition of EV internalization, indicating that EV uptake in these cells depends heavily on actin- and cholesterol-dependent pathways. Other inhibitors, including dynasore, filipin, nystatin, pitstop 2, and wortmannin, resulted in more moderate reductions in EV entry, suggesting that multiple endocytic routes may contribute but that actin- and cholesterol-rich membrane structures play dominant roles. These findings provide mechanistic support for the involvement of specific uptake pathways in the delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 to recipient cells. While EV uptake appears to be a dynamic process, the exact molecular steps by which ACE2 and TMPRSS2 are subsequently transported and displayed on the cell surface remain beyond the scope of this study.

An additional mechanistic consideration concerns the membrane topology of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 following EV-mediated transfer. In our study, flow cytometric analysis using antibodies directed against the extracellular domains of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 under non-permeabilizing conditions confirmed their surface exposure on recipient cells after EV treatment, supporting an orientation compatible with viral engagement. However, while these data establish extracellular accessibility, the precise membrane remodeling and trafficking events that enable this topology remain incompletely defined. EV populations are highly heterogeneous in origin and composition, arising from distinct biogenesis pathways including exosomes and microvesicles55,56. It is therefore possible that ACE2 and TMPRSS2 reside in distinct vesicle subtypes or are delivered through partially distinct routes. In addition, we cannot exclude the possibility that ACE2 and TMPRSS2 are present in separate vesicles rather co-packaged within the same individual EV, as our analyses were performed on EV-enriched preparations without single-vesicle resolution. Moreover, EV–cell interactions may involve endocytic uptake, recycling pathways, transient membrane mixing, or regulated fusion processes rather than simple one-step plasma membrane fusion57. Importantly, functional intercellular transfer of transmembrane receptors via EVs has been previously demonstrated in other systems, including EGFRvIII and CCR5, where recipient cell responsiveness was altered despite incomplete resolution of post-transfer membrane topology58,59. Together, these precedents support the biological plausibility of EV-mediated receptor delivery while underscoring that the detailed molecular mechanisms governing ACE2 and TMPRSS2 membrane insertion and orientation warrant further investigation.

Understanding the potential routes of EV entry into AMs and ECs provides critical insights for developing interventions to prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection. For instance, therapies designed to inhibit EV uptake pathways could effectively block the delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 to non-typical host cells, thus preventing viral entry. This could involve the use of small molecule inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, or other agents that specifically target and block the receptors or cellular machinery involved in EV internalization. Moreover, elucidating the exact molecular mechanisms and pathways of EV-mediated delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 could lead to the development of highly targeted antiviral therapies. By interfering with these pathways, it may be possible to significantly reduce the ability of SARS-CoV-2 to infect and propagate within the host, thereby mitigating the severity of COVID-19.

The critical finding of our study is the demonstration that EVs carrying ACE2 and TMPRSS2 facilitate the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into AMs, ECs, and PCs. This novel mechanism of viral entry is central to understanding the pathogenesis of COVID-19 and highlights the potential for developing targeted therapeutic interventions. This discovery is significant for several reasons. Traditionally, the understanding of SARS-CoV-2 entry has focused on epithelial cells expressing ACE2 and TMPRSS2. However, our findings reveal that EVs can deliver these entry proteins to other critical cell types in the lung that do not naturally express them. This broadens the scope of SARS-CoV-2 target cells, explaining the virus’s ability to infect diverse cell types and contributing to the severity and complexity of COVID-19. The delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 via EVs enhances the susceptibility of AMs, ECs, and PCs to SARS-CoV-2 infection. By equipping these cells with the necessary entry proteins, EVs facilitate more efficient viral entry and propagation within the lung microenvironment. This mechanism may contribute to the extensive lung damage and systemic involvement observed in severe COVID-19 cases. In addition, AMs play a crucial role in the lung’s immune response, while ECs are essential for vascular health and PCs maintain blood-brain barrier integrity. The infection of these cells by SARS-CoV-2 can lead to immune dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, thrombotic complications, and vascular leakage, respectively, exacerbating the disease’s progression. Understanding the role of EVs in this process provides valuable insights into the multifaceted nature of COVID-19 pathogenesis. Targeting the pathways involved in EV-mediated delivery of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 presents a novel therapeutic strategy. By inhibiting EV formation, release, or uptake, it may be possible to prevent the transfer of these entry proteins to non-epithelial cells, thereby reducing the spread and severity of infection. This approach could complement existing therapies and enhance their efficacy.

While previous studies60,61 have suggested that ACE2+ and TMPRSS2+ EVs may function as antiviral decoys by binding SARS-CoV-2 and preventing cell entry, other work48,49 has reported a proviral effect of ACE2/TMPRSS2-containing EVs, consistent with our findings. Those studies relied on overexpression-based systems60 and concentrated EV preparations, which may not reflect endogenous EV behavior. In contrast, we examined EVs secreted by human lung epithelial cells under physiological conditions, without genetic manipulation. We found that these EVs naturally carry ACE2 and TMPRSS2 and are selectively taken up by ACE2/TMPRSS2-negative cell types, including AMs, ECs, and PCs, facilitating viral entry. Our data suggests that, rather than neutralizing viruses, these endogenous EVs enhance viral susceptibility by delivering functional viral entry proteins to previously non-permissive cells. This mechanism is supported by our knockout and inhibitor experiments, in which TMPRSS2-deficient EVs or camostat-treated cells failed to promote infection. Additionally, our model demonstrates targeted delivery of EV cargo to relevant lung cell types, which was not observed in prior overexpression-based studies. Notably, Cocozza et al.60 demonstrated that EVs containing ACE2 could bind SARS-CoV-2 and reduce infection in vitro, suggesting a potential antiviral decoy effect under certain conditions. Such effects may occur when EVs lack TMPRSS2 or in non-lung epithelial cell contexts.

In addition to overexpression-based systems, several recent reports have shown that EVs derived from physiological or primary epithelial sources also exhibit antiviral decoy activity. El-Shennawy et al.61 demonstrated that circulating ACE2+ EVs from SARS-CoV-2 infected individuals directly bound the viral spike protein, blocked RBD-ACE2 interaction, and reduced viral infection in vitro and in vivo61. Similarly, Wang et al.62 reported that ACE2⁺ EVs from human lung spheroid cells inhibited infection in cultured cells and protected mice and hamsters against SARS-CoV-2 challenge, underscoring their protective capacity under physiological conditions62. Schürz et al.63 further performed quantitative single-vesicle analyses showing that ACE2⁺ EVs from primary or engineered sources inhibit viral entry in a concentration- (1 × 108 to 1 × 1011 particles per mL as determined by NTA) and variant-dependent manner63. Together, these studies indicate that EVs released from primary or circulating sources can act as natural decoys and data suggest that their neutralizing potency increases with higher EV doses, supporting that the functional outcome of EV-virus interactions depends on both vesicle abundance and biological context.

Our results reveal a complementary, and not contradictory, mechanism. Using lung organoids and primary-cell-derived EVs, we showed that ACE2⁺/TMPRSS2⁺ EVs also deliver functional entry factors to non-epithelial cells, including AMs, ECs, and PCs, rendering them susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection. This finding suggests that while some EV subsets bind and neutralize virions extracellularly, others facilitate intercellular transfer of viral entry machinery, thereby broadening the range of permissive cell types within the lung. These distinct outcomes likely reflect the heterogeneity of EV populations and the influence of the tissue microenvironment: at high vesicle-to-virus ratios or in circulation, decoy activity may dominate, whereas within complex tissue settings, receptor transfer and infection enhancement may prevail. Recognizing this duality provides a framework for understanding how EVs contribute both to antiviral defense and to viral dissemination within the host. By delineating these context-dependent roles, our work bridges previously conflicting models and emphasizes that EVs are dynamic mediators of host-virus interactions rather than uniformly protective or pathogenic entities.

In conclusion, our study highlights the pivotal role of EVs in transporting ACE2 and TMPRSS2 to AMs, ECs, and PCs, thereby facilitating SARS-CoV-2 entry. This novel mechanism of viral entry is central to understanding the pathogenesis of COVID-19 and underscores the potential for developing targeted therapeutic interventions. Further research is necessary to fully elucidate these pathways and their implications, but the insights gained here provide a promising foundation for the development of novel strategies against COVID-19 and other viral infections exploiting similar mechanisms.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research involving hPSC-derived cells was reviewed and approved by the Embryonic Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee (ESCRO) of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (protocol 22-003) and was conducted in accordance with the International Society for Stem Cell Research Guidelines for Stem Cell Research and Clinical Applications of Stem Cells64. No experiments involving human embryos were performed in this study.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSC) lines and maintenance

hPSC maintenance (H+) media consisted of DMEM/F12 (1:1) supplemented with 20% knockout serum replacement (KSR; Gibco, 10828028), 1X GlutaMAXTM Supplement (Gibco, 35050061), 1X NEAA (Gibco, 11140050), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME; Sigma-Aldrich, M6250), 100 μg/ml Primocin (InvivoGen, ant-pm-2), and 20 ng/mL human basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF2; R&D Systems, 233-FB/CF). The Rockefeller University Embryonic Stem Cell Line 2 (RUES2; NIH approval number NIHhESC-09-0013, registration number 0013, passage 17–28) was obtained from Dr. Ali Brivanlou (Rockefeller University), Sendai Virus (SV) and modified mRNA (mRNA) generated hiPSC lines from healthy human dermal fibroblasts (passage 16–25) were obtained from the Mount Sinai Stem Cell Core facility. RUES2, SV, and mRNA lines were maintained on irradiated CF1 mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs; Gibco, A34181) plated at a density of 15,000–18,000 cells/cm2. Cells were cultured in hPSC medium with daily media changes. hPSCs were passaged using Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies, AT104), washed, and replated at a dilution of 1:48. The cultures were maintained in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Karyotyping and mycoplasma contamination checks using MycoProbe Mycoplasma Detection Kit (R&D Systems, CUL001B) were performed every six months.

hPSC-derived lung organoids

Lung organoids were generated from hPSCs and validated as previously described32. On day 0, hPSC differentiation into endoderm was performed in basal serum-free differentiation (SFD) medium (IMDM/Ham’s F-12 (3:1) (Corning, 10-016-CV, 10-080-CV) supplemented with the following: 0.5X N-2 Supplement (Gibco, 17502048), 0.5X B-27 Supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid (Sigma Aldrich, A4544), 1X GlutaMAXTM Supplement, 0.45 mM monothioglycerol (MTG; Sigma Aldrich, M6145), 0.056% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Gibco, 15260037)) with 10 µM Y-27632 (Tocris Bioscience, 1254), and 3 ng/mL human bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4; R&D Systems 314-BP/CF) in a 5% CO2/5% O2 atmosphere at 37 °C. After 12–16 h, replace the medium with Definitive Endoderm induction medium, which is SFD medium with 10 µM Y-27632, 0.5 ng/mL BMP4, 2.5 ng/mL FGF2, and 100 ng/mL human Activin A (R&D Systems, 338-AC/CF), in a 5% CO2/5% O2 atmosphere at 37 °C for 72–76 h.

On day 4, flow cytometry determined endoderm yield by the expressions of C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) and c-KIT. Only cells with an endoderm yield greater than 90% were used for further experiments. For induction of anterior foregut endoderm, embryonic bodies were dissociated into single cells using 0.05% Trypsin/EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25300120) and plated onto fibronectin-coated six-well tissue culture plates (80,000–105,000 cells/cm2). Cells were incubated in SFD medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL human NOGGIN (R&D Systems, 6057-NG/CF) and 10 μM SB431542 (Tocris Bioscience, 1614) for 24 h followed by switching to SFD media supplemented with 10 μM SB431542 and 1 μM IWP2 (Tocris Bioscience, 3533) for an additional 24 h. For induction of early-stage lung progenitor cells (after day 8), at the end of anterior foregut endoderm induction, cells were switched to CFKBRA medium, which consists of SFD media supplemented with 3 μM CHIR99021 (CHIR; Tocris Bioscience, 4423), 10 ng/mL human fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10; R&D Systems, 345-FG), 10 ng/mL human keratinocyte growth factor (KGF; R&D Systems, 251-KG/CF), 10 ng/mL human BMP4 and 50 nM all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA; Tocris Bioscience, 0695) for 48 hours to promote the formation of three-dimensional cell clumps. These clumps were then gently suspended by pipetting and maintained in ultra-low attachment plates (Corning, 3471) under suspension culture conditions to form lung organoids. The cultures were fed every other day. Lung organoids were either kept in low attachment plates or embedded in 100% Matrigel in 24-well cell culture inserts (Falcon, 353095) to induce branching morphogenesis on day 20–25. Media were changed every other day.

hPSC-derived macrophages

Macrophages were derived from hPSC line mRNA and adapted based on previously reported protocols65. For macrophage differentiation, hPSCs were digested into a single-cell suspension using Accutase on day -1 and plated onto Matrigel-coated culture dishes at a density of 6000 cells/cm² in H+ medium. On day 0, the cells were induced to differentiate into mesoderm by culturing them in SFD medium supplemented with 25 ng/mL BMP4, 15 ng/mL Activin A, and 1.5 μM CHIR for 48 h. Subsequently, the medium was changed to SFD medium containing 50 ng/mL human vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF; R&D Systems, 293-VE/CF), 50 ng/mL FGF2, 10 μM SB431542 and 50 ng/mL human stem cell factor (SCF; R&D Systems, BT-SCF) for 72 h to induce hemogenic endothelium. On day 5, the medium was replaced with SFD medium containing 50 ng/mL VEGF, 50 ng/mL FGF2, 50 ng/mL SCF, 10 ng/mL human interleukin 3 (IL-3; R&D Systems, 203-IL/CF), 50 ng/mL human interleukin 6 (IL-6; R&D Systems, 7270-IL/CF) and 50 ng/mL human thrombopoietin protein (THPO; R&D Systems, 288-TPN/CF) for hematopoietic induction. On day 11, the cells were dissociated using Accutase and replated in ultra-low attachment plates in SFD medium with 10 ng/mL IL-3, 50 ng/mL IL-6, and 80 ng/mL macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF; R&D Systems, 216-MC/CF) to induce monocytes. On day 14-15, floating single cells were collected and plated onto fibronectin-coated plates in SFD medium with 80 ng/mL M-CSF for 4 days to induce macrophages. All differentiation steps were cultured under normoxic conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

hPSC-derived blood vessel organoids (VOs)

VOs were derived from hPSC lines and adapted based on previously reported protocols66. hiPSCs were dissociated into single cells using 0.05% Trypsin/EDTA and seeded in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates at the density of 1000 cells/well in Aggregation Medium (DMEM (Corning, 10-013-CV)/Ham’s F12 (1:1) supplemented with 20% KSR, 1X GlutaMAXTM Supplement, 1X NEAA, 0.1 mM β-ME, and 20 μM Y-27632). On day 0, the cell aggregates were treated with Mesoderm differentiation medium (DMEM/Ham’s F12/NeurobasalTM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21103049) (1:1:2) supplemented with 1X GlutaMAXTM Supplement, 1X N-2 supplement, 1X B-27 supplement, 0.1 mM β-ME, 12 μM CHIR and 30 ng/mL BMP4 for 72 h. On day 3, the medium was switched to one containing 100 ng/mL VEGF and 2 μM forskolin (R&D Systems, 1099). On day 5, the aggregates were embedded in a 1:2 mixture of Matrigel and collagen I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1048301) and cultured in StemProTM-34 SFM (Gibco, 10639011) with StemProTM-34 nutrient supplement, 1X GlutaMAXTM Supplement, 15% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, R&D System, S11150H), 100 ng/mL VEGF, and 100 ng/mL FGF2. By day 7 or 8, endothelial cells had sprouted, forming vascular networks. On day 10, individual VOs were isolated from the gel and maintained in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates until day 15.

Immortalized cell cultures

Human lung cancer cell line A549 (ATCC CCL-185) was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Human lung cancer cell line Calu-3 (ATCC HTB-55) was maintained in EMEM (Corning, 10-009-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS. Human prostate cancer cell line LNCaP (ATCC CRL-1740) was maintained in RPMI 1640 (Corning, 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Adult BALF samples were obtained as de-identified medical waste from the Department of General Thoracic Surgery. Pediatric BALF samples were obtained as de-identified medical waste from the Department of Pediatrics, Division of Pediatric Pulmonology. All studies involving human samples were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Surface protein isolation

The isolation of cell surface proteins was conducted using Pierce™ Cell Surface Protein Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89881) with slight modifications. Cells were plated on 10 cm tissue culture dish to reach 90-95% confluence. Cells were washed three times with ice cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by a 30-min incubation with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin at 4 °C. The non-reacted biotin was blocked with quenching solution and washed twice with tris-buffered saline (TBS) before cell lysis. Lysed cells were sonicated 5 times followed by incubation on ice on a shaker for 30 min. The cell extracts were centrifuged for 10 min (20,800 × g, at 4 °C) to pellet the insoluble material. 50 µl of the supernatant was kept for further analysis and the rest was used for the cell surface protein isolation by incubating with NeutrAvadinTM Agarose for one hour at room temperature. The beads were washed four times with wash buffer and the flow-through was kept for further analysis. The surface protein was eluted with sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) sample buffer with dithiothreitol (DTT; Sigma Aldrich, 43816) by incubating for one hour at room temperature.

Western blot

Proteins for Western blot analyses were prepared from organoids and cell cultures by lysis with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, X100) in PBS. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation, and the protein concentration was determined using BCA protein assay reagents (Pierce, 23227) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell lysates were diluted with 5X sample buffer in the absence or presence of reducing agent and incubated either at room temperature or 95 °C (boiled) for 5 min before SDS-PAGE. The proteins were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to nitrocellulose western blotting membranes (GE Healthcare, 10600003), and probed with the desired primary antibodies: TMPRSS2 (2 µg/ml, clone AL20), TMPRSS2 (2 µg/ml, clone P5H9-A3, EMD Millipore, MABF2158, lot 3584853) ACE2 (1 µg/ml, R&D Systems, AF933, lot HOK0320061), TSG101 (1.8 μg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA1-23296, lot WL3453886G), CD63 (2 µg/mL, clone TS63, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10628D, lot 00998136), HSP90 (0.2 µg/ml, clone 341320, R&D Systems, MAB3286, lot XKB0221011), ZO-1 (0.1 µg/ml, clone D6L1E, Cell Signaling Technology, 13663S, lot D6L1E), calnexin (4 ng/ml, Cell Signaling Technology, 2433, lot 5) and β-actin (40 ng/ml, clone C4; Santa Cruz, sc-47778 HRP, lot G03919). The binding of primary antibody was detected using an anti-mouse-HRP-conjugated antibody (100 ng/ml, SeraCare, 5220-0341), anti-rabbit-HRP-conjugated antibody (100 ng/ml, SeraCare, 5220-0336, lot 10440068), or anti-goat-HRP-conjugated antibody (100 ng/ml, SeraCare, 5220-0362, lot 10422112), followed by SuperSignal West Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34095) or Western Lightning Plus-ECL, Enhanced Chemiluminescence Substrate (PerkinElmer, NEL105001EA). Uncropped and unprocessed scans of the blots are provided in the Supplementary Information.

SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus production and transduction

The SARS-CoV-2 Spike pseudotyped VSV vector production and titration were described previously67. In brief, to generate Spike pseudotyped VSV vectors, 4 ×106 293 T cells were seeded in DMEM plus 10% FBS in a 10 cm plate and transfected with 15 μg of Spike expression plasmid 24 h later, using the calcium-phosphate transfection method. Media was replaced 16 h later with 10 ml fresh media, and after a further 8 hours, 5 ml was removed and 2 × 108 vector genomes of VSVΔG particles containing GFP in place of the VSV G protein were added for one hour at 37 °C. Following this incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated for an additional 24 h before harvesting supernatants. Vector supernatants were harvested and filtered through 0.45 μm syringe filters and stored at −80 °C in aliquots. To titer VSV-GFP vectors, HeLa-ACE2 cells were seeded as 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates, and the following day, 50 μl of serially diluted unconcentrated vector stocks were added. The final dilutions in the cultures were 1:2, 1:6, 1:18, and 1:27. Transduction efficiency was determined by GFP expression 16–24 h after transduction using flow cytometry (Guava easyCyte, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA) and transducing units (TU) per ml calculated from the dilutions showing a linear relationship between the dilution factor and the number of GFP-positive cells. To transduce cell lines, tissue culture-treated, 96-well half-area white plates (Corning, 3688) were seeded with various cells lines to achieve 50% to 75% confluence at the time of transduction. Different cells were transduced with VSV-GFP vectors at doses of 0.7 to 1 × 103 TU. Transduction efficiency was determined by GFP expression 16–24 h after transduction using flow cytometry (Guava easyCyte). To transduce lung organoids in suspension culture, 10–20 organoids at days 35–40 were picked manually and transferred to 96-well U-bottom plates and transduced with 8.5 × 102 TU of GFP-expressing VSV vectors. Transduction efficiency was examined by GFP expression 24 h later by fluorescence microscopy. To quantify the transduction efficiency, organoids were digested by TrypLETM Express Enzyme (Gibco, 12604013) and the % of GFP was quantified by flow cytometry.

For SARS2-Spike-pseudotype lentiviral vector production, HEK293T cells (2 × 106) were plated in a 10 cm tissue culture dish and transfected the next day when they were about 75% confluent with a combination of the following plasmids: 6.67 µg of pHAGE-GFP (Addgene, 106281), 6 µg of psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260), and 0.667 µg of 526 pCAGGS-SARS2-S-cflag wt. Polyethylenimine (PEI) reagent (2 mg/ml, Millipore Sigma, 913375) was used for transfection following manufacturer’s protocols, with the ratio of DNA: PEI = 1:4. The following day, the cells were checked for transfection efficiency under a fluorescent microscope for GFP expression and the media was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The supernatant from cell culture at 48 hours and 72 hours were harvested and combined for each sample. The combined supernatants were spun in a tabletop centrifuge for 5 min at 2000 × g to pellet the residual cells and then passed through a 0.45 µm syringe filter (VWR, 28145-505). Lenti-X concentrator (Takara Bio, 631232) was used to concentrate the virus following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The concentrated aliquots were frozen at −80 °C and used for EV and pseudovirus co-transduction of lung organoids.

SARS-CoV-2 production

Vero E6 cells overexpressing ACE2 (VeroE6-hACE2) were obtained from Dr. Jae Jung (University of Southern California, USC) and maintained in DMEM high glucose, supplemented with 10% FBS, 2.5 μg/ml puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere in the Hastings Foundation and The Wright Foundation Laboratories biosafety level 3 (BSL3) facility at USC and the BSL3 facility in the Emerging Pathogens Facility at Mount Sinai. SARS-CoV-2 virus (BEI resources, Manassas, VA) was cultured and passaged 4 times in VeroE6-hACE2 cells and harvested every 48 h post-inoculation. Plaque forming units (PFU) were determined using a plaque assay by infecting a monolayer of VeroE6-hACE2 cells with serial dilutions of virus stocks and layering semi-solid agar. Plaques were counted at day 3 post-infection to determine PFU. Virus stocks were stored at −80 °C. All work with SARS-CoV-2 was performed in the BSL3 facility following approved SOPs.

SARS-CoV-2 infection of lung organoids

Lung organoid cultures embedded in Matrigel were infected with purified SARS-CoV-2 stock at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI). From our previous experiments, number of cells in each organoid used for experiments was estimated at 10,000 cells/organoid and this was used in MOI calculation in infection. 100 μl inoculum was prepared in CFKBRA medium (or mock-infected with medium-only). Inoculum was added to each cell culture insert and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After the adsorption period, the inoculum was removed, and fresh media was added. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 or 4 days. At the time of harvest, the insert media was collected for further analysis. Infected and mock-infected lung organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and used for immunofluorescence analysis. Alternatively, organoids were lysed in 500 μl TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10296010) for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis as described below.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR of infected organoids

RNA was collected in 500 μl of TRIzol per insert and incubated for 15 mins at room temperature. Cell isolates were gently mixed by pipetting up and down. Cell isolates were collected and stored at −80 °C until required. RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s protocol for the Monarch Total RNA mini prep kit (New England Biolabs, T2010S). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using Prime Time Gene Expression Master Mix (IDT, 1055771) following the manufacturer’s protocol (cycle 5 min @ 25 degree, 20 min @ 46 degrees, 1 min @ 95 degrees, 4 degrees forever). Predesigned TaqMan probes were from Applied Biosystems or IDT. qRT-PCR was performed in a 96-well plate in triplicate using primers for SARS-CoV2 N1 protein. qRT-PCR reaction used the iScript RT supermix on the Bio-Rad CFX96 under the following conditions (95 for 3 min, [15 sec @ 50 degrees, 2 min @ 95 degree [3 sec @ 95 degrees, 30 sec @ 55 degrees] x 45 cycles]) Relative gene expression was calculated based on a standard curve. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) Control RNA from Heat-Inactivated SARS-Related Coronavirus 2, Isolate USA-WA1/2020 (BEI #NR-52347) cDNA was made and used as a standard within the qPCR. Three dilutions with known genomic equivalent (GE) were used for the standard curve. The GE from the experimental samples were derived from this curve using the Maestro analysis software. Primers used for qPCR: SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) CDC RUO Primers and Probes catalog # 10006713 (Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT)).

Plaque assay

SARS-CoV-2 plaque assay was performed following the protocol outlined in a previous protocol68. Briefly, confluent Vero E6 cell monolayers were infected at 37 °C for 1 h with 10-fold serial dilutions of the viral supernatant. After infection, the inoculum was replaced with an agar overlay composed of equal parts 2 × MEM and 1.4% low-melting agarose. Post-plaque formation, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, the overlay was removed, and plaques were stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Viral titer (pfu/ml) = plaque count x virus dilution factor ÷ volume plated (ml).

Electron microscopy

Healthy and COVID-19 lung biopsy tissues were directly fixed with freshly made fixative containing 3% paraformaldehyde and 0.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.03 M potassium-phosphate buffer with 4% sucrose (pH 7.4). After washing with 0.03 M potassium-phosphate buffer with 4% sucrose, 0.1 M glycine was added to quench the unbonded aldehyde group. The tissues were dehydrated in graded series of ethanol on ice and embedded in LR White (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA). The samples were polymerized under UV light (360 nm) at −10 °C for 48 h followed by 12 h at room temperature. Ultrathin sections were cut, mounted on formvar-carbon coated 200 mesh nickel grids. After incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, gold conjugated secondary antibodies (6 nm, or 18 nm colloidal gold-affini pure anti-goat, anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG (H + L), Jackson Immuno Reasearch Laboratories, Inc., West Grove, PA; 15 nm Protein A Gold, Cell Microscopy Center, University Medical Center Utrecht, 35584 CX Utrecht, The Netherlands) were applied. For double immunolabeling, the grids were stained with either anti-SARS or ACE2, fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde then continue with anti-TMPRSS2 antibody. The grids were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate by standard methods, imaged with Talos120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, OR) and recorded using Gatan (4k x 4k) OneView Camera with software Digital Micrograph (Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Lung organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Organoids were washed twice with PBS and then immersed in 15% sucrose solution followed by 30% sucrose solution for 1 h each at 4 °C. Organoids were then immersed in 1:1 30% sucrose: O.C.T. solution overnight, embedded in O.C.T compound (Sakura, 4583) and sectioned with a cryostat (Leica) at 5-8 µm thickness unless otherwise specified. For immunostaining, cryosectioned slides were washed with PBS before permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS for 30 min followed by blocking in 5% donkey serum (EMD Millipore, S30-100ML) for 1 hour. The following primary antibodies were used for immunostaining: EPCAM (0.5 μg/ml, clone D4K8R, Cell Signaling Technology, 36746S, lot 1), Purified anti-human CD326 (EPCAM) (0.5 μg/ml, clone W18060B, BioLegend, 312502, lot B383643), Sars nucleocapsid protein (10 µg/ml, Rockland, 200-401-A50, lot 44838), TMPRSS2 (2 µg/ml, AL20). The following biotin antibodies were used for staining slides for confocal imaging: 0.5 ug/mL Biotin anti-human CD9 (clone HI9a, Biolegend, 312112, lot B428718), 2.5 ug/mL Biotin anti-human CD63 (clone H5C6, Biolegend, 353017, lot B437774), and 0.5 ug/mL Biotin anti-human CD81 (TAPA-1) (clone 5A6, Biolegend, 349514, lot B439207) and incubated at 1 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS three times, secondary antibodies diluted in blocking solution were applied to the sections for 1–2 h at room temperature. Slides with biotin antibodies were counterstained with 1 ug/mL Streptavidin (Alexa Fluor 555) (Invitrogen, S21381, lot 2652988) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, sections were washed with PBS three times before staining with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific, D1306) and mounting (GBI Labs, E18-18). Secondary antibodies were: Alexa Fluor 488- (anti-mouse, A21202, anti-rat, A21208), 555- (anti-mouse, A31570), 594- (anti-rat, A21209), or 647- (anti-rabbit, A31573) conjugated donkey antibodies (Invitrogen) used at 1/500 dilution. Samples were imaged using motorized Leica DMi8 (Leica Microsystems) inverted microscopes or a two-photon confocal laser scanning microscope Leica TCS SP8 (Leica Microsystems).

Image analysis

Fluorescent images were analyzed using LAS X (Version 3.10.2.30243) and ImageJ (V1.54 d) software. Image threshold for each of the markers was standardized to determine the area of each marker present. This was followed by merging of individual standardized channels into one image. Color threshold was used to determine area of colocalization, indicated by an overlay of red and green resulting in yellow.

Sample preparation for proteomics analyses

Samples were incubated in a buffer containing 2 M urea, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 1 mM DTT at 37 °C for 30 min. Iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 3 mM and samples were incubated at room temperature for 45 min in the dark. DTT was added to a final concentration of 3 mM and 750 ng of trypsin (trypsin gold, Promega) was added to each sample, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C with shaking. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and an additional 500 ng trypsin added to the supernatant, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Digested samples were desalted on BioPureSPN Mini C18 SPE columns (Nest Group) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were dried by vacuum centrifugation and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA) for mass spectrometry analysis.

Mass spectrometry data acquisition

All samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometry system equipped with an Easy nLC 1200 ultra-high pressure liquid chromatography system interfaced via a Nanospray Flex nanoelectrospray source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were injected onto a fritted fused silica capillary (30 cm × 75 μm inner diameter with a 15 μm tip, CoAnn Technologies) packed with ReprosilPur C18-AQ 1.9 μm particles (Dr. Maisch GmbH). Buffer A consisted of 0.1% Formic Acid (FA), and buffer B consisted of 0.1% FA/80% Acetonitrile (ACN). Peptides were separated by an organic gradient from 5 to 35% mobile buffer B over 120 min followed by an increase to 100% B over 10 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Analytical columns were equilibrated with 3 μL of buffer A.

The mass spectrometer acquired data in a data-independent analysis (DIA) manner. A full scan was collected at 60,000 resolving power over a scan range of 390–1010 m/z, an instrument controlled AGC target, an RF lens setting of 30%, and an instrument controlled maximum injection time, followed by DIA scans using 8 m/z isolation windows over 400–1000 m/z at a normalized HCD collision energy of 28%.

The DIA-NN algorithm was used identify peptides/proteins and to extract intensity information from DIA data69. Data were searched against the Homo sapiens reference proteome sequences in the UniProt database (one protein sequence per gene, downloaded on August 23, 2023). Search parameters included a fixed modification for carbamidomethyl cysteine and variable modifications for N-terminal protein acetylation and methionine oxidation. All other search parameters were DIA-NN factory defaults.

Statistical analysis of proteomics data was conducted utilizing the MSstats package in R70. All data were normalized by equalizing median intensities, the summary method was Tukey’s median polish, and the maximum quantile for deciding censored missing values was 0.999. For protein abundance analyses, only the top 20 peptide features per protein were considered.

Flow cytometry

Lung organoids and cell lines were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme or 0.05% Trypsin/EDTA. Human lung tissue cells were digested using Dissociation Buffer (containing 1.6 U/mL Dispase (Corning, 354235), 100 U/mL Collagenase type I (Gibco, 17100017), and 40 U/mL DNase I (Bioland, EPD01-01) in PBS). For intracellular staining, the cells were fixed using the Fixation Buffer (BioLegend, 420801) and permeabilized with an Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend, 421002) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For surface staining, cells were skipped from the fixation and permeabilization steps. After resuspension in FACS buffer (0.1% Sodium Azide (Acros Organics, 190381000), 0.1% BSA, 1 mM EDTA (ThermoFisher Scientific, 15575020), and 25 µg/mL DNase I in Hanks’ Balanced Salt solution (HBSS; ThermoFisher Scientific, 14185052)), cells were stained with desired anti-human antibodies. The antibodies used in this study were: APC/FireTM 750-conjugated EPCAM (2 μg/ml, clone 9C4, BioLegend, 324234, lot B308895), APC-conjugated CD31 (1 μg/ml, clone WM59, BioLegend, 303116, lot B380800), BV605-conjugated CD31 (1 μg/ml, clone WM59, BioLegend, 303122, lot B381683), APC/Cy7-conjugated CD11b (2 μg/ml, clone ICRF44, BioLegend, 301342, lot B374482), PE/Cy7-conjugated CD206 (2 μg/ml, clone 15-2, BioLegend, 321124, lot B388206), AF488-conjugated HLA-DR (1 μg/ml, clone L243, BioLegend, 307620, lot B394852), AF700-conjugated CD15 (2 μg/ml, clone W6D3, BioLegend, 323026, lot B360514), APC-conjugated CD169 (5 μg/ml, clone 7-239, BioLegend, 346008, lot B364487), PerCP/Cyanine5.5-conjugated CD45 (1 μg/ml, clone HI30, BioLegend, 304028, B368649), APC-conjugated CD184 (2 μg/ml, clone 12G5, Biolegend, 306510, lot B261520), PE-conjugated CD117 (1 μg/ml, clone 104D2, Biolegend, 313204, lot B257667), APC-conjugated EPCAM (0.2 μg/ml, clone EPR20532-225, Abcam, ab237398, lot GR3281794-1), and unconjugated TMPRSS2 (2 μg/ml, clone AL20). Data acquisition was performed using a BD LSRFortessa and a Northern Lights™ Flow Cytometry (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed with FCS Express (De Novo Software Version 7.28.0035) and SpectroFlo (Cytek Biosciences Version 3.4.0).

EV isolation, quantification, and preparation of conditioned media without EVs

Ultracentrifugation method

EVs were isolated from the conditioned media of lung organoids. Organoids were cultured in CFKBRA medium under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After 48–72 h, the conditioned media were collected, and EVs were isolated using differential centrifugation. The conditioned media were first centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 min, followed by 2000 × g for 10 min, and then at 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove dead cells and debris. The resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min using an SW32 Ti swinging bucket rotor (Beckman Colter Optima XPN-90) to pellet the EVs. The EVs were washed by resuspension in sterile PBS and pelleted again by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The EV pellet was resuspended in PBS at approximately 500-fold concentration, aliquoted at 50 μL per tube, stored at −80 °C until use. EV protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. The particle size and concentration were determined by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) using Particle Metrix ZetaView Nanoparticle using the software ZetaView (Version 8.05.14 SP7). EV samples were diluted in filtered PBS to achieve an optimal particle concentration and recorded for 3 cycles with 11 positions at 25 °C. EVs purified from lung organoids derived from different hPSC lines exhibited a mean diameter of approximately 180-200 nm, with particle concentration ranging from 0.6 × 109 to 1.7 × 109 particles/mL (Supplementary Fig. 6b and Supplementary table 1). Conditioned media without EVs refers to the remaining supernatant after ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min, which is depleted of EVs. This EV-depleted media was collected and used immediately for experiments or stored at −80 °C until use.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) method

ZsGreen EVs were isolated from conditioned media of ZsGreen lung organoids. Organoids were cultured in CFKBRA medium under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After 48-72 hours, the conditioned media were collected, and EVs were isolated using differential centrifugation followed by PEG isolation. The conditioned media were first centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 min, followed by 2000 × g for 10 min, and then at 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove dead cells and debris. The resulting supernatant was mixed at a 1:1 ratio with 16% PEG (Sigma-Aldrich, 81260-1KG) for a final concentration of 8% PEG. The PEG supernatant solution was placed on a platform rotator at low speed for a minimum of 12 h. The following day, the PEG supernatant solution was centrifuged at 3000 xg for 60 min to pellet the EVs. The EVs were washed by resuspension in SFD- medium, aliquoted, and stored at 4 °C for no more than 1-2 days or used fresh. Batch to batch quality control was performed via flow cytometry to determine EV concentration prior to use.

EVs and pseudovirus co-transduction

EVs were isolated as described above. Lung organoids were dissociated into single cell suspension using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme. Cells were seeded at 20,000–50,000 cells per well on 16-well Lab-Tek® Chamber Slide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 178599PK) coated with Matrigel. The following day, 5 µl, 15 µl and 25 µl of EVs or PBS were pre-incubated with 9 µl of SARS2 Spike-Pseudotyped Lentiviral vector for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by addition the mixtures to the replated organoid cultures in a final volume of 100 µl. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 16–24 h before replacing the media with vector-free CFKBRA media. Pseudovirus transduction was quantified at 72 h later by percentage of GFP+ area over the total cell area using ImageJ71.

EV inhibition

Inhibitors were reconstituted in DMSO according to manufacturer’s instructions. The inhibitors were diluted in SFD- medium with 80 ng/mL M-CSF to a working stock of: Pitstop (30 uM, Sigma-Aldrich, SML1169-5mg), Dynasore (50 uM, Tocris, 2897), Filipin III (5 ug/ml, Tocris, 6250), Methyl-beta-cyclodextrin (2 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, C4555-1G), Nystatin (30 U/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, N9150-20ML), Wortmannin (1 uM, Tocris, 1232), Cytochalasin D (2.5 uM, Tocris, 1233). Macrophages were incubated with this EV inhibitor-containing medium for 1 h at 37 °C under normoxic conditions. ZsGreen EVs were then added to the macrophages containing the EV inhibitors and placed back into the 37 °C incubator under normoxic conditions for 20 h. The following day, the macrophage media was aspirated to remove any free EVs, followed by cell dissociation for flow cytometric analysis.

scRNA-seq analysis

Raw read count, cell barcode, and cell metadata were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE135893 and GSE136831)42,43 and analyzed using Seurat version 4. Expression matrix was normalized using ‘NormalizeData’ function and the expression for the genes TMPRSS2, EPCAM and ACE2 was evaluated in control lung and pulmonary fibrosis lung (GSE135893) or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease lung (GSE136831). Positivity for TMPRSS2, EPCAM and ACE2 was defined by an expression level higher than 0 and determined using ‘WhichCells’ function. Sorted cells from IPF lung were loaded onto the 10x Chromium (Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ kit, 10X Genomics) to generate single cell gene expression libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Preprocessing of raw data was performed using CellRanger v7. Data were next normalized, scaled using Seurat v4. Low quality cells were filtered-out using miQC72 and data were integrated using Harmony73. To identify cell types, we used the reference dataset of human lung GSE135893 and GSE136831 to guide cell type annotation with the package SingleR74. Visualization of gene expression was performed with the VlnPlot function in Seurat or the package Nebulosa.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t tests were used to compare quantitative analyses comprising two groups of n ≥ 3 samples. Further specifics about the replicates used in each experiment are available in the figure legends. The p value threshold to determine significance was set at p = 0.05. p value annotations on graphs are as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001. Data for quantitative experiments is typically represented as the mean with error bars representing the standard error of the mean.
