Combined water exchange and cap-assisted colonoscopy reduces cecal intubation time and patient discomfort in patients with unsedated colonoscopy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dongchu Wang, Aixiang Wang, Xiangwu Ding, Rong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimColonoscopic insertion remains technically challenging. While cap-assisted colonoscopy (CAC) reduces the cecal intubation time (CIT), sigmoid colon water exchange (SWE) enhances linear scope advancement. We hypothesized that combined water exchange and cap-assisted colonoscopy (SWC) would optimize the CIT and improve patient tolerance during unsedated colonoscopy.Patients and methodsThis prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) included 200 subjects scheduled for unsedated colonoscopy at a tertiary endoscopy center in Wuhan, China. The participants were randomly assigned to receive either SWC (intervention, n=100) or CAC (control, n=100). The primary outcome was CIT (minutes). The secondary outcomes included pain scores, the polyp detection rate (PDR), bowel preparation quality (Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS)), and patient willingness to repeat the procedure.ResultsCompared with CAC, SWC significantly reduced the median CIT (5.0 [IQR 4.0–7.0] min vs. 7.0 [6.0–10.0] min; P < 0.001). The left colon pain scores were lower with SWC (1.0 [1.0–2.0] vs. 2.0 [1.0–3.0]; P < 0.001), and the willingness to repeat was greater (96.0% vs. 85.9%; P = 0.024). The PDRs (61.6% vs. 58.6%; P = 0.663) and BBPS scores (P = 0.213) were comparable. Multivariate analysis revealed that SWC, better bowel preparation, younger age, no prior abdominal surgery, and the absence of constipation were independent predictors of a shorter CIT.ConclusionSWC shortens the CIT, reduces discomfort, and improves patient acceptance without compromising procedural quality—advantages conducive to promoting and popularizing unsedated colonoscopy.Trial registrationThe registry titled ”Transparent cap combined water exchange enteroscopy versus transparent cap method enteroscopy in time to cecal intubation: a prospective study”was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 14/05/2025 (clinical trial registration number: ChiCTR2500102387).Retrospectively registered.Keywords: Water exchange colonoscopy, Cap-assisted colonoscopy, Cecal intubation time, Patient comfort, Unsedated colonoscopy

Introduction

Colonoscopy serves as the definitive modality for diagnosing, treating, and monitoring colorectal diseases, underscoring its essential clinical implementation [1–3].

Nevertheless, this procedure demands significant technical expertise [4], with proficient colonoscope insertion representing the primary learning challenge. Inadequate operator competency may delay diagnosis, increase patient discomfort, and precipitate serious complications.

Proficient colonoscope intubation necessitates extensive procedural repetition, with even expert endoscopists facing 3–5% failure rates [5]. Novices’ challenges in acquiring this competency constitute a barrier to wider adoption. Thus, identifying techniques that reliably achieve cecal intubation while reducing procedural complexity remains imperative.

Anatomically, the rectum and distal colon demonstrate retroperitoneal fixation. The intervening sigmoid colon displays dynamic mobility with variable configurations [6]. Endoscopically, this segment poses the greatest navigational challenge because of frequent acute angulations and looping formations [3, 4, 7, 8].

Conventional colonoscopy relies on air insufflation; however, this approach exacerbates colonic tortuosity, complicates scope advancement, and induces abdominal discomfort [9].

The evidence indicates that cap-assisted colonoscopy (CAC) maintains the optimal lens-to-lesion distance and reduces the insertion time when a 4-mm transparent distal attachment is used [10, 11].

Sigmoid colon water exchange colonoscopy (SWE) is an endoscopic method that employs clear water infusion into the sigmoid lumen to evacuate fecal debris [3]. The generated hydrostatic pressure reduces sigmoid angulations while preserving a shortened endoscopic axis, promoting linear colonoscope progression [12].

We hypothesized that combined sigmoid water exchange and cap-assisted colonoscopy (SWC) would shorten the cecal intubation time (CIT), reduce the degree of technical difficulty, and increase the efficiency of unsedated colonoscopy compared with cap-assisted colonoscopy (CAC) alone. This randomized controlled trial evaluated whether SWC reduces the CIT compared with CAC—addressing procedural feasibility while improving patient tolerance.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-center, patient-blinded RCT adhered to CONSORT guidelines. The completed CONSORT checklist is provided as Additional File 1.The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Fourth Hospital (KY2024-110-01) on 28 April 2024 and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ID: ChiCTR2500102387). The study recruited adults aged 18–80 years scheduled for unsedated diagnostic or screening colonoscopy at Wuhan Fourth Hospital between June 2024 and June 2025.All participants provided prior written informed consent. The inclusion criteria required no endoscopic contraindications: (1) acute gastrointestinal perforation; (2) severe cardiac, pulmonary, renal, or cerebral insufficiency/multisystem organ failure; and (3) psychiatric conditions or impaired consciousness precluding cooperation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) inadequate bowel preparation (Boston Bowel Preparation Scale < 6); (2) known abdominal fistula; (3) preprocedure diagnosis of obstructing tumor; and (4) refusal to provide informed consent.

Two board-certified endoscopists performed all procedures. Each operator performed approximately 1,000 CAC procedures and 100 SWC procedures before the study commenced.

Preparation and procedure

All patients initiated a standardized low-residue diet 72 h before the procedure. Bowel cleansing was performed according to the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines via a split-dose polyethylene glycol regimen: 1 L was administered the preceding evening, and 2 L was taken on the morning of the colonoscopy.

A standard adult variable-stiffness colonoscope (CF-H260Q, Olympus) was used for examination. The procedures were performed uniformly with patients in the left lateral decubitus position.

Randomization and allocation

The participants were allocated to SWC or CAC groups via computer-generated block randomization. Sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes ensured allocation concealment; these envelopes were revealed to the endoscopist immediately prior to colonoscope insertion. Patient blinding was implemented by positioning the endoscopic monitor behind the patient’s head with an opaque barrier, preventing method visualization. The procedural staff (endoscopists and nurses) remained aware of the group assignments. An independent coordinator, uninvolved in assessments or analysis, disclosed assignments immediately before insertion.

Both study groups employed identical 4-mm transparent caps (Model D-201-14304; Olympus) affixed to the colonoscope tip throughout the procedures. In the CAC group, luminal distension was achieved through conventional air insufflation followed by standard advancement techniques. For the SWC cohort, a foot-pedal-controlled irrigation system (OFP Endoscopic Flushing Pump; Olympus) was used to administer warm water infusion via the biopsy channel, with continuous intraprocedural suction maintaining luminal collapse by evacuating air, debris, and fluid. Advancement beyond the descending colon utilized identical CAC protocols in both groups. Although patients were blinded to the visual aspects of the procedure, the audible noise from the water pump during SWC may have partially unblinded some participants. However, no patient reported awareness of the technique used upon post-procedure inquiry. Standardized adjunctive maneuvers, including positional changes and abdominal pressure application, were implemented as clinically indicated across all procedures. Consistent with protocol requirements, mucosal cleansing and therapeutic interventions such as biopsy or polypectomy were exclusively performed during the withdrawal phase.

Outcome measurement

CIT served as the primary endpoint and was defined as the interval between rectal insertion and confirmed cecal intubation (evidenced by appendiceal orifice visualization) according to established criteria [3]. Prolonged intubation was operationalized as CIT durations beyond the cohort’s 90th percentile, per Burak et al.‘s criteria [13]. All examinations included a standardized ≥ 6-minute withdrawal phase for mucosal inspection.

The secondary outcomes included procedural success rates, polyp detection frequency, bowel preparation quality objectively evaluated during withdrawal via the validated Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) [14] after cleansing, and the incidence of iatrogenic mucosal injury. The systematically recorded technical metrics included the frequency of abdominal compression, positional changes during insertion, and withdrawal duration, excluding therapeutic interventions. Patient-reported outcomes featured segmental maximum pain scores (left, transverse, right colon) during insertion assessed using an established visual analog scale (VAS; 0–10) [15], alongside willingness to repeat unsedated procedures. Patients received standardized VAS instructions before reporting.

Trained personnel prospectively documented demographic variables (sex, age, BMI, surgical history, constipation status, chronic laxative use, and indications) and safety outcomes, including endoscopically observed suction-induced mucosal marks and traumatic lesions.

Statistical analysis

Sample size determination was based on pilot data indicating a 2-minute mean CIT difference (SWC: 5 min vs. CAC: 7 min; SD ≈ 4 min). Power analysis revealed that 91 participants per arm were required to achieve 90% power at two-tailed α = 0.05. To compensate for potential dropouts and protocol violations, enrollment targets were increased by 10% to 100 per group, aligning with superiority trial recommendations.

Continuous variables predominantly exhibited nonnormal distributions and were consequently summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). For between-group comparisons, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for continuous measures, whereas for categorical variables, the chi‒square test or Fisher’s exact test was used (latter applied for expected frequencies < 5). Kaplan-Meier curves were used to compare cumulative cecal intubation rates over time between groups, with log-rank tests to assess differences in time-to-event.

Multivariate logistic regression identified predictors of prolonged insertion time via stepwise variable selection (retention threshold: P < 0.05). The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All computations utilized SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Patient recruitment and baseline characteristics

Between June 2024 and June 2025, 242 patients were assessed for eligibility at Wuhan Fourth Hospital. After exclusions for inadequate bowel preparation, known abdominal fistula, preprocedure diagnosis of obstructing tumor, or declined consent, 200 participants were randomized equally to the SWC (n=100) or CAC (n༝100) groups (Fig. 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics demonstrated excellent intergroup balance (Table 1). The median age was 53.5 years (IQR 46–62) in CACs vs. 52 years (IQR 37–63) in SWCs (P = 0.247), with a nearly identical sex distribution (49% male in CACs vs. 51% in SWCs; P = 0.777). No statistically significant differences existed in BMI, prior abdominal surgery (24% vs. 30%), constipation history (12% vs. 13%), or colonoscopy indications (screening, abdominal pain, bleeding; all P > 0.4). There was also no significant difference between the two groups in terms of the number of procedures performed by the two endoscopists (P = 0.479).

Cecal intubation efficiency

The SWC technique demonstrated profound advantages in procedural efficiency. The median CIT was significantly reduced by 2 min compared with that of the CAC group (5.0 [IQR 4.0–7.0] min vs. 7.0 [6.0–10.0] min; P < 0.001), representing a 28.6% reduction (Fig. 2; Table 2). This acceleration was consistently observed across both endoscopists (Fig. 3). Time-to-event analysis revealed substantially superior cumulative intubation success for SWCs at all critical intervals: 64.6% of SWC procedures reached the cecum within 5 min versus only 20.2% for CACs (P < 0.001, Fig. 4). Despite this marked time reduction, the cecal intubation success rates remained equally high in both cohorts (99.0% each), with one failure per group attributed to refractory looping.

Patient experience and tolerability

Beyond procedural efficiency, SWC significantly enhanced patient comfort during unsedated examinations. Participants receiving SWC reported markedly lower left-colon pain scores during insertion (median VAS score of 1.0 [IQR 1.0–2.0] vs. 2.0 [1.0–3.0] for CAC; P < 0.001), indicating a clinically relevant 50% reduction in median discomfort (Table 2). This improvement directly correlated with patient acceptance: the willingness to repeat unsedated colonoscopy was 96.0% in the SWC group versus 85.9% in the CAC group (P = 0.024). The transverse and right colon pain scores showed no significant intergroup differences, underscoring the technique’s specific benefit in navigating the challenging sigmoid segment.

Bowel Preparation and diagnostic yield

Bowel cleansing quality, objectively assessed via the BBPS, was comparable across the groups (median 8.0 [IQR7.0–8.0] for both; P = 0.213) (Table 2). Notably, SWC demonstrated superior cleansing, especially in the left colon (median BBPS 3.0 [2.0–3.0] vs. 2.0 [2.0–3.0] for CAC; P < 0.001), likely reflecting the synergistic effect of water exchange on debris clearance. The polyp detection rate (PDR), a critical quality indicator, did not significantly differ between SWCs and CACs (61.6% vs. 58.6%; P = 0.663). This equivalence extended to anatomical subsites (left colon: 53.6% vs. 47.3%; transverse: 24.2% vs. 25.3%; right: 18.2% vs. 19.2%; all P > 0.3), confirming diagnostic integrity despite accelerated insertion.

Safety and technical performance

In terms of mucosal safety, the incidence of endoscopically detected trauma was comparable between the groups (8.1% for SWC vs. 9.1% for CAC; P = 0.800) (Table 2). However, suction-induced mucosal markings and minor injuries from forceful debris clearance tended to be lower in the SWC cohort (7.1% vs. 13.1%; P = 0.157), suggesting enhanced safety during the suction phase. Both groups required similar rates of adjunctive maneuvers: abdominal compression (8.1% vs. 10.1%; P = 0.621) and positional changes (4.0% vs. 6.1%; P = 0.747). The median withdrawal times for mucosal inspection were equivalent (9.0 min [8.0–9.0] for SWC vs. 9.0 min [8.0–9.0] for CAC; P = 0.218).

Multivariate predictors of prolonged intubation

Multivariate logistic regression identified five independent factors significantly associated with prolonged cecal intubation time (CIT ≥ 12 min) (Table 3). Employing the SWC technique emerged as the strongest protective factor, reducing the odds of prolonged CIT by 95.3% (OR 0.047, 95% CI 0.007–0.304; P = 0.001). Superior bowel preparation (BBPS > 6) similarly lowered the odds ratio by 79.2% (OR 0.208, 95% CI 0.051–0.849; P = 0.029). Patient-specific characteristics, including younger age (< 60 years) (OR 0.250, 95% CI 0.069–0.914; P = 0.036), absence of prior abdominal surgery (OR 0.209, 95% CI 0.057–0.765; P = 0.018), and no history of chronic constipation (OR 0.122, 95% CI 0.026–0.571; P = 0.008), were additional protective predictors, collectively highlighting the interplay between technical innovation, procedural preparation, and patient physiology in optimizing insertion efficiency.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC), the third most prevalent malignancy worldwide, imposes a substantial global burden projected to increase to 3.2 million new cases by 2040 [16, 17]. Colonoscopy represents the cornerstone of CRC prevention, reducing incidence and mortality through early detection and resection of precancerous lesions [1–3].

Colonoscopic proficiency remains challenging to acquire [5]. Reducing procedural complexity facilitates broader implementation [7], while optimizing CIT improves patient satisfaction, lessens operator burden, and enhances endoscopy unit throughput [18].

The marked anatomical curvature of the sigmoid colon constitutes the primary navigational obstacle during advancement [4, 6, 8]. Effective shortening techniques maintain their length at ~ 10 cm, whereas looping (N-/α-configurations) can extend it beyond 1 m, causing most procedural failures and perforation risks. Successful sigmoid traversal determines procedural completion and duration [4].

Conventional air insufflation induces colonic distension, promoting looping and impairing maneuverability [9]. CAC utilizes a 4-mm distal attachment to compress folds, optimize the focal distance, and maintain a shortened configuration [19]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that CAC reduces the CIT compared with conventional colonoscopy [20, 21]. Water navigation colonoscopy, having evolved from water immersion (WI) to water exchange (WE) techniques, employs hydrostatic pressure to straighten the sigmoid folds [22–26]. Cadoni et al. distinguished WE from WI by emphasizing suction during insertion, which further reduced real-time pain [24]. Asai et al. described that WI with a cap attachment significantly increased the rate of straight passage through the sigmoid colon without loop formation, reducing patient pain [25]. Wang et al. reported on a left colon water exchange technique, which limited water exchange to the left colon, thus achieving a balance between efficiency and comfort [26]. Rutter et al. and Jiao et al. both found that SWE employs hydrostatic pressure to straighten sigmoid folds; furthermore, they discovered that water infusion through non-distended luminal pathways facilitates antegrade flow, thereby minimizing looping and expediting advancement [3, 12]. Unlike these predecessors, our approach combined SWE with cap assistance to synergize the benefits of both techniques.

This prospective RCT demonstrated that, compared with CAC alone, the SWC technique significantly enhances procedural efficiency and patient comfort during unsedated colonoscopy. The key findings are threefold: (1) SWC reduced the median CIT by 28.6% (2 min) compared with CAC; (2) SWC significantly lowered patient-reported left-colon pain during insertion; and (3) SWC improved patient willingness to repeat unsedated colonoscopy without compromising procedural success rates, bowel preparation quality, or PDR.

The substantial CIT reduction observed with SWC underscores the synergistic potential of integrating SWE and CAC. While CAC facilitates fold retraction and maintains optimal visualization, its efficacy can be hampered by fecal debris obstructing the cap during sigmoid navigation [19]. Conversely, SWE promotes loop reduction via hydrostatic pressure but risks visual field loss during suction [12]. SWC effectively mitigates these limitations: antegrade water infusion clears debris, preserving cap functionality, while the cap optimizes the lens-to-mucosa distance during suction [19]. This complementary interaction likely explains the accelerated advancement through the tortuous sigmoid colon—the primary bottleneck in colonoscope insertion [4, 6]—which was observed in the SWC group. Our time-to-event analysis further highlights the efficiency of SWC, with over 64% of procedures achieving cecal intubation within 5 min versus only 20% with CAC.

Beyond efficiency, SWC markedly improved patient comfort, specifically in the left colon. Reduced luminal distension from continuous air evacuation, combined with water-mediated antispasmodic effects and diminished sigmoid colon looping [3, 9], likely contributed to the 50% reduction in median pain scores in the left colon. This increased comfort directly translated into significantly greater patient acceptance (96.0% vs. 85.9%; P = 0.024). The technique’s safety profile was comparable to that of CAC, although the trend toward fewer suction-induced mucosal markings with SWCs may reflect hydrodynamic turbulence, reducing direct mucosal contact [19, 27].

Critically, SWC preserves procedural quality. While superior left-colon cleansing—attributable to insertional water exchange—overall BBPS scores remained equivalent. This equivalence potentially reflects type II error from the restricted cohort size. The adenoma detection rate (ADR) is a validated predictor of colorectal cancer morbidity and mortality [28]. When pathological sampling is limited, the polyp detection rate (PDR) serves as a reliable surrogate metric for the ADR in screening colonoscopies [29, 30]. In our study, the PDR remained comparable between groups across all the colonic segments (61.6% vs. 58.6%; P = 0.663), which aligns with reports that sigmoid water exchange alone failed to significantly increase the ADR versus air insufflation [3]. This nonsignificant finding may represent a type II error, where inadequate power from the restricted cohort limited the detection of small effects. While adequately powered ADR trials comparing SWC and CAC are warranted [28–30], our findings suggest that the efficiency gains of SWC occur without sacrificing detection capability.

Multivariate analysis revealed that SWC was the strongest independent predictor of avoiding prolonged CIT (≥ 12 min), reducing its odds by 95.3% (OR 0.047; P = 0.001). Other significant predictors included better bowel preparation (BBPS >6; OR 0.208), younger age (< 60 years; OR 0.250), absence of prior abdominal surgery (OR 0.209), and no history of chronic constipation (OR 0.122) [13, 30–32]. This emphasizes that optimizing the insertion efficiency requires addressing both technical innovations and patient-specific factors.

Study limitations include potential performance bias from unblinded operators, generalizability constraints of a single-center design, and a cohort focused on diagnostic indications-warranting validation in screening populations. Future research should assess adenoma detection rates in larger cohorts.

In summary, SWC significantly reduces the cecal intubation time, alleviates patient discomfort, and enhances procedural acceptance while maintaining examination quality. These advantages support broader implementation of unsedated colonoscopy.
