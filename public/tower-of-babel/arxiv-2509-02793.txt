Title: Scalar Curvature and Transfer Maps in
{\operatorname{Spin}}
and
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
Bordism

In 1992, Stolz proved that, among simply connected
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-manifolds of dimension
5
or greater, the vanishing of a particular invariant
\alpha
is necessary and sufficient for the existence of a metric of positive scalar curvature. More precisely, there is a map
\alpha\colon\Omega_{*}^{\operatorname{Spin}}\to\operatorname{ko}
(which may be realized as the index of a Dirac operator) which Hitchin established vanishes on bordism classes containing a manifold with a metric of positive scalar curvature. Stolz showed
\ker\alpha
is the image of a transfer map
\Omega_{*-8}^{\operatorname{Spin}}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)\to\Omega_{*}^{\operatorname{Spin}}
. In this paper we prove an analogous result for
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-manifolds and a related invariant
{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}\colon\Omega_{*}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}\to\operatorname{ku}
. We show that
\ker{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}
is the sum of the image of Stolz’s transfer
\Omega_{*-8}^{\operatorname{Spin}}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)\to\Omega_{*}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
and an analogous map
\Omega_{*-4}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}{\operatorname{BSU}}(3)\to\Omega_{*}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
. Finally, we expand on some details in Stolz’s original paper and provide alternate proofs for some parts.

Chapter 1
Introduction

A major topic of differential geometry today is the study of Riemannian curvatures on a smooth manifold, and how this relates to topological invariants. Perhaps the simplest notion of curvature on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is the scalar curvature
s_{g}
. One way to define curvature is by comparing the volume of a geodesic ball
B_{\varepsilon}
around a point
x\in M
with a ball
B_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
of radius
\varepsilon
in Euclidean space. More precisely,
s_{g}(x)
is such that

\frac{{\operatorname{Vol}}(B_{\varepsilon})}{{\operatorname{Vol}}(B_{\varepsilon}^{(0)})}=\left(1-\frac{s_{g}(x)}{6(n+2)}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{4})\right).

In this document we address a fundamental question about Riemannian metrics of positive scalar curvature (psc metrics).

Under which conditions does a manifold admit a psc metric?

(1)

The case for
2
-dimensional manifolds is unique: here the scalar curvature is simply twice the Gauss curvature
K_{g}
. The Gauss-Bonnet theorem states

\int_{M}s_{g}(x)d\sigma_{g}=4\pi\chi(M),

where
\sigma_{g}
is the volume element corresponding to
g
and
\chi(M)
is the Euler characteristic of
M
. Hence, in
2
dimensions, the existence of metrics of positive scalar curvature is a matter of which surfaces have positive Euler characteristic – namely,
S^{2}
and
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
.

The existence problem
1
has also been resolved for all manifolds of dimension five or greater which are simply connected. Under these conditions, Gromov and Lawson proved a crucial result of surgery theory. We recall some core definitions and facts of this field. By default, all manifolds we mention in this paper are compact.

A surgery on a manifold is defined as follows: let
S^{p}\times D^{q+1}\to M
be an embedding of a sphere
S^{p}
with a tubular neighborhood (a “handle”) around it, where
p+q+1
is the dimension of
M
. The surgery on
M
given by this embedding produces a manifold
M^{\prime}
obtained by removing the interior of
S^{p}\times D^{p+1}\subset M
and gluing in the handle
D^{p+1}\times S^{q}
:

M^{\prime}=\left(M\setminus{\operatorname{int}}\left(S^{p}\times D^{q+1}\right)\right)\cup_{S^{p}\times S^{q}}\left(D^{p+q}\times S^{q}\right).

In short, the effect of this surgery is to “suture up” the
p
-dimensional hole given by a topologically framed embedding of
S^{p}
. We say that the codimension of this surgery is
q+1
, since this is the codimension of
S^{p}
in
M
. Gromov and Lawson established an important result about
{\operatorname{Spin}}
manifolds and metrics of positive scalar curvature:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold with positive scalar curvature (a psc metric). If
M^{\prime}
is obtained from
M
by a surgery of codimension
\geq 3
, then
M^{\prime}
carries a psc metric.

Theorem
1
has the following important corollary and related result which, for some classes of manifolds, allows us to only consider bordism classes when discussing the existence of psc metrics. For a review of
X
-bordism, see
[
19
,
\lx@sectionsign 12.12
]
.

Corollary 1.2

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
M
be a simply connected
{\operatorname{Spin}}
manifold of dimension
\geq 5
. If
M
is
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-bordant to a manifold
M^{\prime}
with a psc metric, then
M
also has a psc metric.

For manifolds which are not
{\operatorname{Spin}}
, Gromov and Lawson proved the following.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
M
be a simply connected manifold of dimension
\geq 5
which is not
{\operatorname{Spin}}
. Then
M
admits a psc metric.

Remark
.

There is inconsistency in the literature on the usage of “bordism” versus “cobordism.” Following Rudyak (
[
15
,
7.19
]
), we tend to use “bordism” ring and say that two manifolds are “bordant.” This terminology may be more common today since it allows authors to reserve “cobordism” for the dual cohomology theory (as in
[
15
,
7.30
]
and
[
19
,
\lx@sectionsign 12
]
). Historically the term “cobordism” was more common for the homology theory (as in
[
18
]
). This is presumably since “cobordant” has a literal interpretation of two manifolds which together bound another manifold (the French verb “
bordant
” means “to bound”).

We can summarize corollary
1
and theorem
1
and rephrase them as follows: for
M
a simply connected manifold of dimension
n\geq 5
,

\bullet

if
M
is
{\operatorname{Spin}}
, then the existence of a psc metric on
M
depends only on the
{\operatorname{Spin}}
bordism class
[M]\in\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}
;

\bullet

if
M
is
not

{\operatorname{Spin}}
, then the existence of a psc metric on
M
depends only on the oriented bordism class
[M]\in\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{SO}}
.

Remark
.

In particular, for simply connected manifolds of dimension
\geq 5
, the existence of a psc metric is determined by purely topological data. Similarly, a manifold
M
is
{\operatorname{Spin}}
if and only if
w_{2}(M)=0
and simply connected if and only if
w_{1}(M)=0
(assuming
M
is connected). The equivalence classes of
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-structures on a manifold are in bijection with
H^{1}(M)
(with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
), hence, if a simply connected manifold admits a
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-structure, the
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-structure is unique.

Since at least the early 1960s, it was known that the presence of a
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-structure on a manifold was had an intimate relationship with that of a psc metric. A Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
which is
{\operatorname{Spin}}
has a Dirac operator
D_{g}
acting on the bundle of spinors over
M
, and in 1963 Lichnerowicz (
[
12
]
) proved the formula

D_{g}^{2}=\nabla^{*}\nabla+\frac{1}{4}s_{g},

(2)

where
\nabla
is a covariant derivative and
\nabla^{*}
is the formal adjoint. Equation (
2
) implies that if
s_{g}
is positive,
D_{g}
is invertible (i.e., there are no harmonic spinors on
M
). If
M
is a
{\operatorname{Spin}}
manifold of dimension
n
with a psc metric, the Lichnerowicz formula implies that
[M]\in\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}
lies in the kernel of the index

\alpha\colon\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}\to\operatorname{ko}_{n}.

(3)

Following Stolz’s work, we had a complete answer to the existence problem
1
for simply connected manifolds of dimension
\geq 5
. Under these conditions,

(i)

if
M
is not
{\operatorname{Spin}}
, then
M
admits a psc metric (
[
8
]
);

(ii)

if
M
is
{\operatorname{Spin}}
, then
M
has a psc metric if and only if
\alpha([M])=0
(
[
16
]
).

Remark
.

In
[
8
]
, Gromov and Lawson proved the rational version of Stolz’s result, which can be stated as follows: for a simply connected
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-manifold of dimension
n\geq 5
, if
\alpha([M])=0
, then some multiple
M\#M\#\cdots\#M
admits a psc metric.

The techniques used by Stolz in his proof are central to the results in this paper. We now describe the geometric idea behind Stolz work. Let
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
be the quaternionic projective space with the standard metric
g_{0}
. It is well known that the group of isometries of
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
(with respect to
g_{0}
) is the projective group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
(which is the quotient of
{\operatorname{Sp}}(3)
by
\{\pm 1\}
). We now make an elementary observation.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
E
be the total space of a smooth bundle over a manifold
M
with fiber
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
and structure group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
. Then
E
carries a psc metric.

Proof.

Cover
M
by open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
over which
E
is locally (diffeomorphic to)
D_{i}\times{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
. Let
g_{M}
be the the metric on
M
and
g_{0}
be the standard metric on
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
. Since
M
is compact,
s_{g_{M}}
bounded, whence there exists sufficiently small
\lambda>0
such that
g\coloneqq g_{M}\times\lambda g_{0}
has positive scalar curvature on each neighborhood:
s_{g}=s_{g_{M}}+\lambda^{-1}s_{g_{0}}
. Again for
\lambda
sufficiently small, this holds over each of the
U_{i}
. Finally, since
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
acts isometrically on
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
, these local metrics glue to form a global psc metric.
∎

Remark
.

It is an elementary fact that, for any
\lambda>0
, the scaled metric
\lambda g
is a Riemannian metric with scalar curvature
s_{\lambda g}=\lambda^{-1}s_{g}
. In fact, since scalar curvature can be defined as a sum of sectional curvature, one’s geometric intuition for surfaces (roughly) shows this for higher dimensions.

Of course
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
is a compact Lie group, so we have a classifying space
{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)
with a universal
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
-bundle and the associated
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
-bundle. We now define a transfer map

\Psi\colon\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)\to\Omega_{n+8}^{\operatorname{Spin}}

(4)

as follows: a class in
\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)
is represented by a pair
(M,f)
, where
M
is an
n
-dimensional
{\operatorname{Spin}}
manifold and
f
is a map
M\to{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)
. Let
\widehat{M}
be the total space of the pullback via
f
of the
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
-bundle. Note that
\widehat{M}\to M
is an
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
-bundle which also has the structure group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
acting isometrically on the fibers. Now we have the following main result from
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1.5

(Stolz)
.

For the transfer map
\Psi\colon\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)\to\Omega_{n+8}^{\operatorname{Spin}}
and index map
\alpha\colon\Omega_{n}^{\operatorname{Spin}}\to\operatorname{ko}_{n}
, we have
\operatorname{im}\Psi=\ker\alpha
.

Since Lichnerowicz’s formula implies
\operatorname{im}\Psi\subseteq\ker\alpha
, Stolz had to show
\ker\alpha\subseteq\operatorname{im}\Psi
. He achieved this by reducing to a homotopy-theoretic problem: to begin with, the transfer
\Psi
may be considered as the map induced on homotopy of a map of spectra

T\colon\operatorname{MSpin}\wedge\Sigma^{8}{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)_{+}\to\operatorname{MSpin}.

(5)

The index map
\alpha
is similarly induced by a map

D\colon\operatorname{MSpin}\to\operatorname{ko}.

(6)

The algebraic fact that
\alpha([M])=0
if
M
has a psc metric, for example, translates to statement that
DT
is nullhomotopic. This means that
T
factors through the homotopy fiber
\widehat{\operatorname{MSpin}}
of
D
. We write
\widehat{T}
for this lift and
i\colon\widehat{\operatorname{MSpin}}\to\operatorname{MSpin}
for the inclusion of
\widehat{\operatorname{MSpin}}
. Theorem
1
then follows from showing
\widehat{T}
induces a surjection of homotopy groups.

It is worth emphasizing here that, prior to Stolz’s result in
[
16
]
, the same result had been proven rationally in
[
8
]
. As was well known by that time, the map
\operatorname{MSpin}\to\operatorname{MSO}
is a homotopy equivalence for any coefficient ring containing
1/2
. Hence the most difficult component is the
2
-primary data. Hence, in this paper, we work entirely with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\mathbb{Z}/(2)
for homology and cohomology. We may also abuse notation and write
\pi_{*}X
to mean
\pi_{*}(X)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
.

Appendix A
Twisted scalar curvature for
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
manifolds

As we have seen, existence of a
{\operatorname{Spin}}
structure (and consequently of the Dirac operator) plays a fundamental role in the existence of a psc metric. More generally, a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-structure implies the existence of a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
Dirac operator.

Let
M
be a simply-connected manifold with

w_{2}(M)\neq 0
(i.e.
M
is not a spin manifold). Then
M
has a

{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-structure if there is a class
c\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
which maps
to
w_{2}(M)
under the mod-2 reduction map
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. The class
c
gives a map
c:M\to{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{\infty}
and,
consequently, a complex line bundle
L\to M
. We use the notation

(M,L)
for a manifold with a choice of
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-structure. We
notice that a
{\operatorname{Spin}}
-manifold
M
has a canonical
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
corresponding to the trivial complex line bundle over
M
.

Assume
(M,L)
is a non-
{\operatorname{Spin}}

{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
manifold. We
choose a Riemannian metric
g
on
M
, a Hermitian metric
h
on

L
, and a unitary connection
A_{L}
on the line bundle
L
. These data
give the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
Dirac operator
D_{(M,L)}
. Then we have the Lichnerowicz formula
[
12
]

D_{(M,L)}^{2}=\nabla\nabla^{*}+\frac{1}{4}s_{g}+\mathcal{R}_{L}

(7)

here

\mathcal{R}_{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j<k}F_{L}(e_{j},e_{k})\cdot e_{j}\cdot e_{k},

where one sums over an orthonormal frame, and where
F_{L}
is the
curvature of the connection
A_{L}
on the line bundle
L
. We denote

s^{L}_{g}:=s_{g}+4\mathcal{R}_{L},

and say that
s^{L}_{g}
is the
L
-twisted scalar curvature
. Notice that
s^{L}_{g}
is, in fact, a zeroth order operator on spinors, and it depends on a choice of the hermitian metric
h
on
L
and on the connection
A_{L}
.

Appendix B
Existence of positive
L
-twisted
scalar curvature

One of our primary goals is to resolve the following existence question:
under which conditions on a non-
{\operatorname{Spin}}

{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
manifold
(M,L)
does there exist a Riemannian metric
g
, a Hermitian metric
h
on
L
, and a connection
A_{L}
such that the
L
-twisted scalar curvature
s_{g}^{L}
is positive?

It turns out that the positivity of the
L
-twisted
scalar curvature
s_{g}^{L}
is also invariant under surgeries of
codimension at least three. In particular, this implies that for a
simply-connected manifold
M
the existence of the data
(g,h,A_{L})

as above such that
s_{g}^{L}
is a positive operator, depends only on
the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-cobordism class
[(M,L)]\in\Omega^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}
.

Next, we recall that the index of the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
Dirac
operator
D_{(M,L)}
takes values in
\operatorname{ku}_{*}
, where
\operatorname{ku}
is the connective cover of the complex
K
-theory spectrum
\operatorname{KU}
. In particular, we have a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-version of the
\alpha
invariant

{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}:\Omega^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}_{n}\to\operatorname{ku}_{n}.

(8)

The formula (
7
) shows that, if the
L
-twisted scalar
curvature
s_{g}^{L}
is positive, then the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-bordism class

[(M,L)]\in\Omega^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}_{n}
is in the kernel of the index map

{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}
. This leads to the following existence result due to
Botvinnik and Rosenberg.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
5
,
4
]
)
.

Let
(M,L)
be a simply connected non-
{\operatorname{Spin}}

{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
manifold of
dimension
n\geq 5
. Then
M
admits a Riemannian psc metric
g
, a
hermitian metric
h
and a connection
A_{L}
such that
s_{g}^{L}>0
if
and only if the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
index
{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}[(M,L)]
vanishes in
\operatorname{ku}_{n}
.

A proof of Theorem
B
is given in
[
5
]
Theorem 3.8
and
[
4
]
Corollary 32 which relies on deep results in
cobordism and homotopy theory. A main goal of this thesis is to give a
direct proof of Theorem
B
using the same technology developed by Stolz in
[
16
]
.

As with the
{\operatorname{Spin}}
case, we will ignore odd-primary data. The proof for the case of odd primes also appears in
[
5
]
; in this paper we give a direct, constructive proof for the
2
primary case.

Appendix C
New transfer map and main result

We notice that the complex projective spaces
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2k}
are non-spin manifolds. In
particular,
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
has a standard
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-structure given by a
complex line bundle
L_{0}\to{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
with
c_{1}(L_{0})=x
, where
x
is a
generator of
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2};\mathbb{Z})
. A calculation of

{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}[({\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2k},L_{0})]
was worked out by Hattori
[
9
]
,
in particular, he shows that
{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}[({\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2},L_{0})]=0
.

The projective plane
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
has a remarkable property – namely, the group
G={\operatorname{SU}}(3)
acts transitively on
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
, and
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}=G/H
, where the subgroup
H\coloneq{\operatorname{SU}}(2,1)
is the subgroup of elements in
{\operatorname{U}}(2)\times{\operatorname{U}}(1)\subset{\operatorname{U}}(3)
with determinant
1
. We obtain a fiber bundle
p:{\operatorname{BH}}\to{\operatorname{BG}}
with fiber
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
and structure group
{\operatorname{SU}}(3)
. Thus given a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-manifold
(M,L)
and a map
f:M\to BG
, we can form the associated
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
-bundle
\hat{p}:E\to M
as a pull-back

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{BH}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\operatorname{BG}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{p}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

where
E=M\times_{f}{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
has dimension
n+4
and has
a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
structure inherited from the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
structure on
M

defined by
L
and the
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
structure on
{\mathbb{C}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
defined by the
bundle
L
. This construction defines a transfer map

{{\mathcal{T}}^{\operatorname{c}}}:\Omega_{n-4}^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}({\operatorname{BSU}}(3))\to\Omega_{n}^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}.

(9)

We also have a
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
-version of the Stolz transfer map:

\mathcal{T}:\Omega_{n-8}^{{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}}({\operatorname{BPSp}}(3))\to\Omega_{n}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}}.

(10)

Here is the main result of this thesis:

Theorem 1.7
.

The transfer maps
\mathcal{T}
and
{{\mathcal{T}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
are such that

\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})+\operatorname{im}({{\mathcal{T}}^{\operatorname{c}}})=\ker{{\alpha}^{\operatorname{c}}}

as abelian groups.

Appendix D
Proof of main result (theorem
C
)

We summarize and expand on Stolz’s original proof and give an analogous result for
{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
bordism involving the transfer map
{\mathcal{T}}^{\operatorname{c}}\colon\Omega_{*-4}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}{\operatorname{BSU}}(3)\to\Omega_{*}^{{\operatorname{Spin}}^{\operatorname{c}}}
of (
9
). The Steenrod algebra
\mathcal{A}
and basic theory of modules and comodules over
\mathcal{A}
(and its dual) are needed to describe the transfer maps, so we begin with some algebraic preliminaries in chapter
2
. In chapter
3
we give more details about the transfer maps
\mathcal{T}
and
{\mathcal{T}}^{\operatorname{c}}
and compute their induced maps on cohomology. Important lemmas and other results used in the proof of theorem
C
appear in chapter
3
, with some necessary computations included in the appendices.

The compact Lie group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
acts transitively on
S^{11}\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
and this descends to a transitive action on
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
. The stabilizer of
[0:0:1]
is
{\operatorname{PSp}}(2,1)\coloneqq{\operatorname{P}}({\operatorname{Sp}}(2)\times{\operatorname{Sp}}(1))
, giving a fiber bundle
{\operatorname{PSp}}(2,1)\to{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)\to{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
. In turn this yields a bundle
\pi\colon{\operatorname{BPSp}}(2,1)\to{\operatorname{BPSp}}(3)
with fiber
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
and structure group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)
. Note that the standard metric on
{\mathbb{H}\!\operatorname{P}}^{2}
has positive scalar curvature and the structure group
{\operatorname{PSp}}(3)

\operatorname{Sq}=1+\operatorname{Sq}^{1}+\operatorname{Sq}^{2}+\cdots
.

As we mentioned, third property is also called the Cartan formula. Note that
\operatorname{Sq}(x)
is always a finite sum due to the second property. An instructive application of the properties of the
\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
is when
x
is the generator of
H^{*}\mathbb{RP}^{\infty}
. In this case
\operatorname{Sq}(x)=1+x+x^{2}
, and this completely determines the action of
\mathcal{A}
on
H^{*}\mathbb{RP}^{\infty}
. Via the splitting principle, one can use this to determine the action on
{\operatorname{BO}}
.

As an algebra,
\mathcal{A}
is generated by the classes
\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
for
n\geq 0
, but this is not a minimal generating set: for example,
\operatorname{Sq}^{3}=\operatorname{Sq}^{1}\operatorname{Sq}^{2}
. We demonstrate now that
\mathcal{A}
is generated (as an algebra) by only the classes
\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{n}}
for all
n
. First we need a useful fact of arithmetic modulo
2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given positive integers
a,b
with base-
2
expansions
a=a_{0}+2a_{1}+\cdots+2^{n}a_{n}
and
b=b_{0}+2b_{1}+\cdots+2^{n}b_{n}
, the modulo-
2
binomial coefficient
\binom{a}{b}
factors as

\binom{a_{0}+2a_{1}+2^{2}a_{2}+\cdots+2^{n}a_{n}}{b_{0}+2b_{1}+2^{2}b_{2}+\cdots+2^{n}b_{n}}=\binom{a_{0}}{b_{0}}\binom{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\binom{a_{2}}{b_{2}}\cdots\binom{a_{n}}{b_{n}}.

Proof.

By definition or otherwise,
\binom{a}{b}
is the coefficient of
x^{b}
in
(1+x)^{a}
. Now the Frobenius map gives

\displaystyle(1+x)^{a}

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{a_{0}}(1+x)^{2a_{2}}(1+x)^{2^{2}a_{2}}\cdots(1+x)^{2^{n}a_{n}}

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{a_{0}}\big(1+x^{2}\big)^{a_{2}}\big(1+x^{2^{2}}\big)^{a_{2}}\cdots\big(1+x^{2^{n}}\big)^{a_{n}}.

It follows from the Euclidean division algorithm that the only way to obtain a
x^{b}
term is if each factor
(1+x^{2^{k}})^{a_{k}}
contributes
x^{2^{k}b_{k}}
. Hence
\binom{a}{b}
is nonzero precisely if every factor
\binom{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
is nonzero.
∎

Notice that a factor
\binom{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
is
0
if and only if
a_{i}=0
and
b_{i}=1
. Hence a useful interpretation of lemma
E
is that
\binom{a}{b}=1
if and only if every digit in the base-
2
expansion of
a
is greater than or equal to the corresponding digit in the base-
2
expansion of
b
.

Theorem 2.2
.

As an algebra,
\mathcal{A}
is generated by the classes
\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{n}}
for all
n
.

Proof.

If
n
is not a power of
2
, pick the largest integer
k
such that
2^{k}<n
. Let
b=2^{k}
and
a=n-b
. Then

\operatorname{Sq}^{a}\operatorname{Sq}^{b}=\sum_{c}\binom{b-c-1}{a-2c}\operatorname{Sq}^{a+b-c}\operatorname{Sq}^{c}.

When
c=0
, we have the term
\binom{b-1}{a}\operatorname{Sq}^{a+b}\operatorname{Sq}^{0}=\binom{b-1}{a}\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
. Now
b-1=2^{k}-1
has the base
2
expansion
1+2+2^{2}+\cdots+2^{k-1}
, and, by construction,
a=n-b
can only contain summands
2^{i}
with
i\leq k-1
. By corollary (
E
),
\binom{b-1}{a}=1
, and thus

\operatorname{Sq}^{a}\operatorname{Sq}^{b}=\operatorname{Sq}^{n}+\sum_{c>0}\binom{b-c-1}{a-2c}\operatorname{Sq}^{a+b-c}\operatorname{Sq}^{c}.

That is,

\operatorname{Sq}^{n}=\operatorname{Sq}^{n-2^{k}}\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{k}}+\sum_{c>0}\binom{2^{k}-c-1}{n-2^{k}-2c}\operatorname{Sq}^{n-c}\operatorname{Sq}^{c}.

Thus
\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
can be written in terms of
\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{k}}
and
\operatorname{Sq}^{i}
with
i<n
. To avoid infinite descent, we see that
\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
can be written using only
\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{j}}
for various
j
.
∎

Given a sequence
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{r})
, we use the shorthand
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}=\operatorname{Sq}^{i_{1}}\operatorname{Sq}^{i_{2}}\cdots\operatorname{Sq}^{i_{r}}
. The sequence
I
is called
admissible
if
i_{j}\geq 2i_{j+1}
for all
j
; hence the monomials
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
are precisely those with no nontrivial Adém relations. It follows from the Adém relations that
\mathcal{A}
is generated as a vector space by the monomials
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
for all admissible sequences
I
. Serre established the linear independence of these classes, hence they form a basis known as the Serre-Cartan basis for
\mathcal{A}
.

The coproduct also has a particularly elegant description using the
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
notation: given any sequence
I=\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\right)
, we have

\Delta\left(\operatorname{Sq}^{I}\right)=\sum_{I_{1}+I_{2}=I}\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{2}},

(13)

where
I_{1}+I_{2}
denotes component-wise addition. This formula becomes clear upon examination: applying
\Delta
to each factor, the terms in
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{I})
correspond to a choice of one term from each
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{i_{j}})
. These correspond to a choice of number
a_{j}
between
0
and
i_{j}
which indicates the term
\operatorname{Sq}^{a_{j}}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{b_{j}}
in
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{i_{j}}
(where
a_{j}+b_{j}=i_{j}
). Hence the terms of
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
correspond to any and all choices of sequences which are component-wise between
(0,0,\ldots,0)
and
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n})
. Each such sequence of course has a complimentary sequence such that their sum is
I
.

Remark 2.3
.

In the coproduct formula (
13
), it is worth emphasizing that none of
I
,
I_{1}
, or
I_{2}
are required to be admissible. Also, we are
adding
sequences together rather than concatenating: for example,
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{3,5,2})
will a priori have
72
terms including
\operatorname{Sq}^{3,5,2}\otimes 1
,
\operatorname{Sq}^{1,3,2}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{2,2,0}
, and
\operatorname{Sq}^{1,1,1}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{2,4,1}
. Of course, many of these terms vanish due to nontrivial relations. The second and third terms mentioned vanish since
\operatorname{Sq}^{1,3,2}=0
and
\operatorname{Sq}^{1,1,1}=0
.

Appendix F
A closer look at
\mathcal{A}_{*}

One must be careful when taking the dual of an infinite dimensional space. Here,
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is the subspace of the
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{Z}_{2})
generated by functionals with finite-dimensional support. Since
\mathcal{A}
is infinite dimensional, the true linear dual has strictly larger dimension, but we nonetheless refer to
\mathcal{A}_{*}
as simply “dual” to
\mathcal{A}
and vice versa. This is possible since, by only allowing functionals with finite dimensional support, we ensure that
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
as a graded vector space.

Define
I_{n}=\left(2^{n-1},2^{n-2},\ldots,4,2,1\right)
for
n>0
and
I_{0}=(0)
(that is,
\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{0}}=\operatorname{Sq}^{0}=1
). For all
n\geq 0
, one can define
\xi_{n}
as the linear dual to
\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{n}}
with respect to the Serre-Cartan basis for
\mathcal{A}
. That is,
\langle\xi_{n},\operatorname{Sq}^{I}\rangle
is
1
if
I=I_{n}
and is
0
if
I
is any other admissible sequence. Of course, this also defines
\langle\xi_{n},\operatorname{Sq}^{I}\rangle
for any
I
after applying the Adem relations. We will write
\xi_{0}=1
. We noted earlier that cocommutativity of
\mathcal{A}
ensures that
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is commutative. Some relatively simple combinatorics will show that, for every
n
, the number of admissible sequences of degree
n
corresponds bijectively to the number of partitions of
n
using the numbers
2^{k}-1=\deg(I_{k})
. This turns out to not give false hope: Serre showed that, as an algebra,
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots]
. Note that
\deg(\xi_{n})=2^{n}-1
, and as with the notation
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
, we write
\xi^{J}=\xi_{1}^{j_{1}}\xi_{2}^{j_{2}}\cdots\xi_{r}^{j_{r}}
for any a sequence
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{r})
of nonnegative integers. Note that using admissible sequences correspond to Serre-Cartan basis elements for
\mathcal{A}
, but for
\mathcal{A}_{*}
it is more convenient to use arbitrary sequences (with finite length and nonnegative integer entries).

The comultiplication on
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is given by

\mu^{*}(\xi_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\xi_{n-i}^{2^{i}}\otimes\xi_{i}=\xi_{n}\otimes 1+\xi_{n-1}^{2}\otimes\xi_{1}+\xi_{n-2}^{4}\otimes\xi_{2}+\cdots+\xi_{1}^{2^{n-1}}\otimes\xi_{n-1}+1\otimes\xi_{n}.

Sometimes it is convenient to use the Hopf conjugates
\zeta_{n}\coloneqq\chi(\xi_{n})
. Then the coproduct is

\mu^{*}(\zeta_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\zeta_{i}\otimes\zeta_{n-i}^{2^{i}}=1\otimes\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{1}\otimes\zeta_{n-1}^{2}+\zeta_{2}\otimes\zeta_{n-2}^{4}+\cdots+\zeta_{n-1}\otimes\zeta_{1}^{2^{n-1}}+\zeta_{n}\otimes 1.

In theory, one can compute the coproduct directly by dualizing from the product on
\mathcal{A}
. Consider
\mu^{*}(\xi_{2})
, for example. By definition,
\xi_{2}
is dual to
\operatorname{Sq}^{2,1}
. There are only two vector space generators in degree
3
, and we have

\left\langle\xi_{2},\operatorname{Sq}^{2,1}\right\rangle=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\langle\xi_{2},\operatorname{Sq}^{3}\right\rangle=0.

The basis elements which may be summands of
\mu^{*}(\xi_{2})
are

\xi_{2}\otimes 1,\quad\xi_{1}^{3}\otimes 1,\quad\xi_{1}^{2}\otimes\xi_{1},\quad\xi_{1}\otimes\xi_{1}^{2},\quad 1\otimes\xi_{1}^{3},\quad\text{and}\quad 1\otimes\xi_{2},

and we can simply check which of these are summands in by evaluating against basis elements in the Serre-Cartan basis elements
\operatorname{Sq}^{3}\otimes 1
,
\operatorname{Sq}^{2,1}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
, etc. For example,

\left\langle\mu^{*}\left(\xi_{2}\right),\operatorname{Sq}^{3}\otimes 1\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{2},\mu\left(\operatorname{Sq}^{3}\otimes 1\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{2},\operatorname{Sq}^{3}\right\rangle=0.

In this fashion we see that
\mu^{*}(\xi_{2})
evaluates to
1
on precisely the generators
\operatorname{Sq}^{2,1}\otimes 1
,
\operatorname{Sq}^{2}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
and
1\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{2,1}
. By examining the lower dimensions one finds
\mu^{*}(\xi_{2})=\xi_{2}\otimes 1+\xi_{1}^{2}\otimes\xi_{1}+1\otimes\xi_{2}
.

We defined
\xi_{n}
to be dual to
\operatorname{Sq}^{n}
in the basis consisting of monomials
\operatorname{Sq}^{I}
with
I
admissible; now we set
\operatorname{Sq}(I)=\operatorname{Sq}(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n})
to be dual to
\xi^{I}=\xi_{1}^{i_{1}}\xi_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\xi_{n}^{i_{n}}
with respect to the basis consisting of all monomials
\xi^{J}
for any sequence
J
. Note that the degree of
\xi^{I}
is
\sum_{j}i_{j}(2^{j}-1)
, and we can obtain an element of this degree in the original basis of
\mathcal{A}
as follows: create an admissible sequence
I^{\prime}
such that the excess
i_{j}^{\prime}-2i_{j+1}^{\prime}
in position
j
is equal to
i_{j}
. One might hope that
\operatorname{Sq}^{I^{\prime}}
is equal to
\operatorname{Sq}(I)
, but this is not true in general. However, we can order these bases to obtain a bilinear pairing which recovers
\operatorname{Sq}^{I^{\prime}}
from
\operatorname{Sq}(I)
and vice versa. This ordering on both the sets of admissible sequences and the sets of sequences is lexicographic from the right. For example,

(100,21,3)<(42,22,3)<(16,8,4)<(8,4,2,1).

Appendix G
Pairing between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{*}

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the set of finite sequences of nonnegative integers and let
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{I}
be the set of such sequences which are admissible. We give
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
total orders using the lexicographic ordering from the right. That is, longer sequences have higher order, and, if two sequences have the same length, we compare the rightmost entries where they differ (larger means higher order). For example,

(5)<(3,1)<(4,1)<(0,2)<(0,0,1).

Note that this order applies to both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
. We also have an order-preserving bijection
\sigma\colon J\to I
given by
\sigma(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})=(j_{1}-2j_{2},j_{2}-2j_{3},\ldots,j_{n-1}-2j_{n},j_{n})
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
I
and
J
be admissible sequences. If
I\geq J
, then

\left\langle\xi^{\sigma(I)},\operatorname{Sq}^{J}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }I=J\\
0,&\text{if }I>J.\end{cases}

Proof.

Assuming
I
is nontrivial, we can write
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
with
i_{n}>0
. Then we can subtract
1
from the last entry of
\sigma(I)
to get
\widetilde{I}\coloneqq(i_{1}-2i_{2},i_{2}-2i_{3},\ldots,i_{n-1}-2i_{n},i_{n}-1)
, and we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi^{\sigma(I)},\operatorname{Sq}^{J}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Delta^{*}\left(\xi^{\widetilde{I}}\otimes\xi_{n}\right),\operatorname{Sq}^{J}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\xi^{\widetilde{I}},\Delta\left(\operatorname{Sq}^{J}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{J_{1}+J_{2}=J}\left\langle\xi^{\widetilde{I}}\otimes\xi_{n},\operatorname{Sq}^{J_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{J_{2}}\right\rangle.

Since
\xi_{n}
is dual to
I_{n}\coloneqq\left(2^{n-1},2^{n-2},\ldots,4,2,1\right)
, the only possible nonzero term here is when
J_{2}=I_{n}
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{J_{1}+J_{2}=J}\left\langle\xi^{\widetilde{I}}\otimes\xi_{n},\operatorname{Sq}^{J_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{J_{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\xi^{\widetilde{I}},\operatorname{Sq}^{J-I_{n}}\right\rangle\left\langle\xi_{n},\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\xi^{\widetilde{I}},\operatorname{Sq}^{J-I_{n}}\right\rangle.

At this point, notice that
J
must have at least length
n
for
J-I_{n}
to be nonnegative. In addition, the assumption
I\geq J
means the length of
J
is at most
n
, so we can write
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})
(note that
I\geq J
also implies
i_{n}\geq j_{n}
). If
I=J
, then we can repeat this process until reaching
\langle\xi_{k},\operatorname{Sq}^{I_{k}}\rangle=1
for some minimal
k
. We now assume
I>J
. It’s easy to see that
J-I_{n}=(j_{1}-2^{n-1},j_{2}-2^{n-2},\ldots,j_{n-1}-2,j_{n}-1)
is admissible, and since
i_{n}\geq j_{n}
, we can repeat this process
j_{n}-1
more times. If
i_{n}=j_{n}
, we start from the beginning, replacing
\widetilde{I}
with
(i_{1}-2i_{2},\ldots,i_{n-1}-2i_{n})
and
J
with
(j_{1}-2^{n-1}j_{n},j_{2}-2^{n-2}j_{n},\ldots,j_{n-2}-4j_{n},j_{n-1}-2j_{n})
. In this case,
i_{n-1}-2i_{n}\geq j_{n-1}-2j_{n}
, and we continue as long as possible. Eventually, we reach some value
\langle\xi^{I^{\prime}},\operatorname{Sq}^{J^{\prime}}\rangle
where the length of
I^{\prime}
is strictly larger than the length of
J^{\prime}
. As we saw, in this case
\langle\xi^{I^{\prime}},\operatorname{Sq}^{J^{\prime}}\rangle=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix H
The Hopf algebra antipode map and Hopf subalgebras

Since
\mathcal{A}_{*}
is also a Hopf algebra, it has an antipode map
\chi\colon\mathcal{A}_{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{*}
characterized by the commutativity of the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{*}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1\otimes\chi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi\otimes 1}

The diagonal maps are the obvious product and coproduct maps, and the unlabeled horizontal maps are the augmentation (counit) and unit maps. For
n\geq 0
, the middle row takes
\xi_{n}
to
0
. Comparison with the top row shows
\sum_{i+j=n}\xi_{i}^{2^{j}}\chi(\xi_{j})=0
. In particular
\chi(\xi_{n})
is determined inductively via

\chi(\xi_{n})=\xi_{1}^{2^{n-1}}\chi(\xi_{n-1})+\xi_{2}^{2^{n-2}}\chi(\xi_{n-2})+\cdots+\xi_{n-2}^{4}\chi(\xi_{2})+\xi_{n-1}^{2}\chi(\xi_{1})+\xi_{n}.

Adopting the semi-standard convention
\zeta_{n}\coloneqq\chi(\xi_{n})
, we equivalently have

\zeta_{n}=\xi_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\xi_{i}^{2^{n-i}}\zeta_{n-i}=\xi_{n}+\xi_{n-1}^{2}\zeta_{1}+\xi_{n-2}^{4}\zeta_{2}+\cdots+\xi_{1}^{2^{n-1}}\zeta_{n-1}.

For
x\in\mathcal{A}
, we write
\Delta(x)=\sum_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}\otimes x^{\prime\prime}_{i}
. The fact that
\mathcal{A}
is connected means that, if
x\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
, then the conjugate satisfies
\sum_{i}\chi(x^{\prime}_{i})x^{\prime\prime}_{i}=0=\sum_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}\chi(x^{\prime\prime}_{i})
, where
\mathcal{A}^{+}
denotes the positively graded part of
\mathcal{A}
. In this case,
\Delta(x)
is the sum of
1\otimes x+x\otimes 1
plus terms in
\mathcal{A}^{+}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{+}
, hence one can inductively determine
\chi(x)
by this property. For example, with the Steenrod algebra we have
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{1})=\operatorname{Sq}^{1}\otimes 1+1\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
, hence
\mu(\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{1})\otimes 1)+\mu(\chi(1)\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{1})=0
and
\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{1})=\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
. Next
\Delta(\operatorname{Sq}^{2})=\operatorname{Sq}^{2}\otimes 1+\operatorname{Sq}^{1}\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{1}+1\otimes\operatorname{Sq}^{2}
and so
\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{2})=\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{1})\operatorname{Sq}^{1}+\operatorname{Sq}^{2}=\operatorname{Sq}^{2}
. For
\operatorname{Sq}^{3}
the antipode is nontrivial:
\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{3})=\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{2})\operatorname{Sq}^{1}+\chi(\operatorname{Sq}^{1})\operatorname{Sq}^{2}+\operatorname{Sq}^{3}=\operatorname{Sq}^{2}\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
.

We say
E\subseteq\mathcal{A}
is a sub-Hopf algebra of
\mathcal{A}
if
E
is a Hopf algebra whose structure maps are restrictions of those for
\mathcal{A}
. In particular,
E
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
, the dual
E_{*}
is a sub-coalgebra of
\mathcal{A}_{*}
, and the conjugation maps preserve
E
and
E_{*}
as subspaces.

The correct notion of the quotient of
\mathcal{A}
by a Hopf subalgebra
E
is a bit subtle: since
E
is unital, the quotient by the left or right ideal generated by
E
gives the zero ring. Instead, we use the semi-standard notation
\mathcal{A}{/\!\!/}E
to denote the Hopf algebra quotient
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\otimes_{E}\mathcal{A}
. In light of this, the dual notion involves the cotensor product. That is, we say
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\otimes_{E}\mathcal{A}
is dual to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\square_{E}\mathcal{A}_{*}
.

Appendix I
A brief review of comodule theory

We now include a superficial review of comodules and cotensor products. Given an algebra
A
over a base ring
k
, a right
A
-module
M
has the structure of a map
\mu_{M}\colon M\otimes_{k}A\to M
; a left
A
-module has
\mu_{N}\colon R\otimes_{k}N\to R
. Completely dual to this, a right
A
-comodule
C
has coaction map
\Delta_{C}\colon C\to C\otimes_{k}A
, and a left
A
-comodule
D
similarly has
\Delta_{D}\colon D\to A\otimes_{k}D
. Now the tensor product
M\otimes_{A}N
may be defined as the cokernel of

M\otimes A\otimes N\xrightarrow{\mu_{M}\otimes 1-1\otimes\mu_{N}}M\otimes N,

where the unadorned tensor
\otimes
denotes
\otimes_{k}
. Dually, the cotensor product
C\square_{R}D
is the kernel of

C\otimes D\xrightarrow{\Delta_{C}\otimes 1-1\otimes\Delta_{D}}C\otimes A\otimes D.

As is consistent with the literature, we will refer to elements
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
defined as the linear dual to
\xi_{i}
with respect to the monomial basis. The following facts can be found in
[
14
]

15.5
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
n
,
\mathcal{A}(n)\coloneqq\langle\operatorname{Sq}^{1},\operatorname{Sq}^{2},\operatorname{Sq}^{4},\ldots,\operatorname{Sq}^{2^{n}}\rangle
and
E(n)\coloneqq\langle Q_{0},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}\rangle
are sub-Hopf algebras of
\mathcal{A}
. Moreover,
E(n)
is the exterior algebra on
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}
. The quotient
\mathcal{A}{/\!\!/}\mathcal{A}(n)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\otimes_{\mathcal{A}(n)}\mathcal{A}
is dual to

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\square_{\mathcal{A}(n)_{*}}\mathcal{A}_{*}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\left[\xi_{1}^{2^{n+1}},\xi_{2}^{2^{n}},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{4},\xi_{n+1}^{2},\xi_{n+2},\xi_{n+3},\xi_{n+4},\cdots\right].

The quotient
\mathcal{A}{/\!\!/}E(n)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\otimes_{E(1)}\mathcal{A}
is dual to

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\square_{E(n)_{*}}\mathcal{A}_{*}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}[\xi_{1}^{2},\xi_{2}^{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{2},\xi_{n+1}^{2},\xi_{n+2},\xi_{n+3},\xi_{n+4},\ldots].

This lemma is overpowered for our purposes, but it shows

is dual to

and
\mathcal{A}{/\!\!/}E(1)
is dual to

Appendix N
Virtual vector bundles

Define a virtual vector bundle over
X
to be a pair
(X,f)
, where
f\colon X\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}
is an unbased map. For convenience, we sometimes omit the word “vector” and say
(X,f)
is a “virtual bundle.” Homotopy classes of maps
X\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}
form the (unreduced) real
\operatorname{K}
-theory of
X
; i.e.,
\operatorname{KO}(X)\coloneqq[X,{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}]
. (Note that
\operatorname{KO}(X)
can equivalently be defined as
based
homotopy classes of maps
X_{+}\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}
.) Given a virtual bundle
\xi=(X,f)
, we call
f
the classifying map of
\xi
. Projection
{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
defines the rank of
\xi
. When
X
is connected, the rank may be considered an integer
{\operatorname{rk}}(\xi)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Two virtual bundles
(X,f)
and
(X,g)
are equivalent if they correspond to the same class of
\operatorname{KO}(X)
(i.e., if
f
and
g
are homotopic).

By contrast, a genuine vector bundle
\eta
over a connected space
X
is classified by a map
X\to{\operatorname{BO}}(n)
, where
n
is the rank of the bundle. Now
\eta
yields a virtual bundle of rank
n
via the composition

X\to{\operatorname{BO}}(n)\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\{n\}\hookrightarrow{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}.

Generally we abuse notation and write
\eta
for both the vector bundle and virtual bundle. However, it is important to note that a virtual bundle does not always arise from a genuine vector bundle in this way. For example, virtual bundles can have negative rank, and, if
X
is not compact, a map
X\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}
may not factor through
{\operatorname{BO}}(n)\times\mathbb{Z}
for any
n
.

While there are virtual bundles which do not arise from vector bundles, there is also some loss of information in considering vector bundles as virtual bundles. For example, a stably trivial vector bundle yields a trivial virtual bundle of the same rank: suppose
\xi\oplus\mathbb{k}\cong\mathbb{n}\oplus\mathbb{k}
, where
n
is the rank of
\xi
and
\mathbb{k}
is the trivial vector bundle of rank
k
. Then
X\xrightarrow{f}{\operatorname{BO}}(n)\hookrightarrow{{\operatorname{BO}}(n+k)}
is nullhomotopic, so the resulting composition
X\to{\operatorname{BO}}
is also nullhomotopic. This means that
\xi
, considered as a virtual bundle, is equivalent to the trivial virtual bundle of rank
n
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is immediate from the definitions that trivial virtual bundles are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank. It follows that vector bundles
\xi
and
\eta
over a connected base space
X
yield equivalent virtual bundles if and only if
{\operatorname{rk}}(\xi)={\operatorname{rk}}(\eta)
and
\xi\oplus\mathbb{k}\cong\eta\oplus\mathbb{k}
for some
k
.

Suppose we have a genuine vector bundle
\xi
of rank
n
over a CW complex
X
. In particular,
X
is paracompact, so we can embed
\xi
into a trivial vector bundle
\mathbb{k}
for some
k
. The complementary bundle
\xi^{\perp}
(which can be defined topologically as the quotient bundle of
\overline{k}
by
\xi
) can be used to form an additive inverse of
\xi
as classes in
\operatorname{KO}(X)
. That is,
\operatorname{KO}(X)
is an additive group, and, since
\xi+\xi^{\perp}=k
, we have
-\xi=\xi^{\perp}-k
. Hence
-\xi
can be considered as the formal difference
\xi^{\perp}-k
(which is a virtual bundle of rank
-n
). The (virtual) bundle
-\xi
is called the
stable complement
of
\xi
. In particular, when
X
is a manifold, we define the
stable normal bundle
of
X
as the stable complement to the tangent bundle (considered as a virtual bundle).

Now suppose
\xi
is stably trivial. Without loss of generality,
\xi\oplus\mathbb{1}\cong\mathbb{n+1}
. Then
X\xrightarrow{f}{\operatorname{BO}}(n)\hookrightarrow{{\operatorname{BO}}(n+1)}
is nullhomotopic, so
\widetilde{f}\colon X_{+}\to{\operatorname{BO}}\times\mathbb{Z}
is homotopic to the constant map with image
\{b_{0}\}\times\{n\}
and
\widetilde{\xi}\equiv\widetilde{\mathbb{n}}
. This shows that
stably
trivial vector bundles become trivial virtual bundles of the same rank. Similar logic shows that
stably equivalent
bundles of equal rank represent equivalent virtual bundles (of the same rank). It is important to not overlook the requirement that the genuine bundles had the same rank to begin with. For example, trivial virtual bundles of distinct ranks are
not
equivalent.

Example 3.5

(Thom spectrum of a virtual bundle represented by a genuine bundle)
.

Suppose now that

Example 3.6

Theorem D.6
.

We have
