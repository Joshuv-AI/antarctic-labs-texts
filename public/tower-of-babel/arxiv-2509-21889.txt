QoNext: Towards Next-generation QoE for Foundation Models
Yijin Guo, Ye Shen, Farong Wen, Junying Wang, Zicheng Zhang, Qi Jia, Guangtao Zhai

Abstract
Existing evaluations of foundation models, including recent human-centric approaches, fail to capture what truly matters: user’s experience during interaction. Current methods treat evaluation as a matter of output correctness alone, overlooking that user satisfaction emerges from the interplay between response quality and interaction, which limits their ability to account for the mechanisms underlying user experience. To address this gap, we introduce QoNext , the first framework that adapts Quality of Experience (QoE) principles from networking and multimedia to the assessment of foundation models. QoNext identifies experiential factors that shape user experience and incorporates them into controlled experiments, where human ratings are collected under varied configurations. From these studies we construct a QoE–oriented database and train predictive models that estimate perceived user experience from measurable system parameters. Our results demonstrate that QoNext not only enables proactive and fine-grained evaluation but also provides actionable guidance for productized services of optimizing foundation models in practice.

As services mature, expectations now extend beyond mere correctness to interactions that are contextually reliable and responsive to human needs. A human-centric evaluation methodology that captures this perceived experience is therefore essential.

However, existing human-centric approaches have their limitations that they fail to provide a unified and fine-grained analytical framework that effectively connects output quality with interactive experience, making it difficult to uncover the underlying mechanisms that shape human perception. In many other fields, such as networking, videos and multimedia, a closely related concept already exists: Quality of Experience (QoE)

. QoE research has clearly established that, after sufficient training, subjective user perceptions can be predicted from measurable system parameters. For example, network latency, buffering time, and frame rate can serve as reliable predictors of the perceived smoothness or enjoyment of the multimedia content. Therefore, we introduce QoNext (Towards Next-Generation QoE for Foundation Models), the first framework to systematically integrate QoE principles into the evaluation of foundation models. The relevant factors are identified through a concise review of QoE research and a short questionnaire with users of large-model services. Specifically, we study five fine-grained dimensions: information density, content accuracy, output speed, latency duration and position. We then design controlled experiments that systematically vary these factors and collect detailed user ratings under matched settings. Making use of the resulting data, we manage to construct a QoE-oriented database and train regression models that predict user-perceived experience for different configurations of foundation models.

Through a comprehensive analysis of the collected database, we find that content quality outweighs service quality in shaping overall user experience. Among the five examined dimensions, content accuracy emerges as the most critical factor, followed by output speed as the second. We also conduct a personalized analysis based on MBTI personality framework. The results reveal that while different personality types exhibit consistent overall structures, they differ in their sensitivities to specific factors.

Building on this insight, QoNext models achieve strong predictive consistency with human judgments, reaching an

Our main contributions are as follows:

We present QoNext, the first framework introducing QoE into the evaluation of foundation models, thereby establishing a human-centric assessment paradigm that moves beyond the limitations of traditional benchmarks.

•

Guided by the principles of QoE, we construct a dedicated database that links model parameters with subjective user ratings,
focusing on identifying the factors that most significantly influence the overall user experience.

•

Based on the constructed database, we develop a predictive tool that evaluates and optimizes human experience with foundation models, paving the way for next-generation user-centric evaluation paradigms.

Related Work

2.1
QoE in Multi Media and Networks

QoE, as an inherently user-centric concept, first emerged and gained prominance in multimedia and communication networks. Its core idea is to integrate objective system-level indicators (e.g., bandwidth, latency, packet loss) with users’ subjective perceptions to construct a holistic assessment of service quality—one that more closely reflects real user experience than approaches dependent solely on technical parameters. This paradigm relies on standardized subjective evaluation methodologies
bt500
, which obtain Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) from human raters and ensure comparability and reliability of results.

In practice, QoE research typically adheres to a two-stage pipeline consisting of database construction and predictive modeling. Researchers first collect MOS through laboratory studies or crowdsourcing experiments, thereby building subjective databases that cover a wide range of degradation conditions (e.g., LIVE-Netflix QoE
duanmu2016quality
, the Waterloo SQoE series
duanmu2018quality
). These databases then serve as the foundation for developing and validating predictive models that infer user experience from observable parameters. A large body of work has approached this challenge from different perspectives, including network-level Quality of Service (QoS) modeling
hossfeld2013internet
,
yin2015control
, no-reference content quality assessment
mittal2012no
,
mittal2015completely
, and multi-factor fusion approaches that integrate both
bentaleb2016sdndash
,
zhang2024quality
, thereby characterizing experiential variations from transmission conditions, perceptual quality, or their combination.

This database-driven, hybrid modeling paradigm—grounded in both subjective and objective indicators—offers direct inspiration for designing evaluation frameworks in human–AI interaction and foundation model assessment.

2.2
Evaluation for Foundation Models

Current evaluation of foundation models primarily relies on a range of objective benchmarks. For instance, MMLU
hendrycks2020mmlu
and BIG-Bench
srivastava2023bigbench
target multidisciplinary knowledge and reasoning. GSM8K
cobbe2021gsm8k
and MATH
hendrycks2021measuring
focus on mathematical reasoning, while HumanEval
chen2021humaneval
and SWE-bench
jimenez2023swe
evaluate code generation. These benchmarks have been instrumental in advancing objective performance assessment by providing clear, quantifiable measures of models’ capabilities in areas such as knowledge coverage, logical reasoning, and task completion. However, they primarily emphasize “task accomplishment”, offering limited insight into users’ subjective experience during real interactions.

As models are increasingly deployed in practical scenarios, researchers have begun to explore human-centric evaluation methods. One line of work examines models in subjective and socially situated contexts, emphasizing alignment with human cognition and value systems. For example, Humanibench
raza2025humanibench
and HermBench
li2024herm
probe models’ sensitivity to ethics and value judgments. UniAA
zhou2024uniaa
and HumanBeauty
liao2025humanbeauty
assess performance in artistic and aesthetic domains. Hateful Memes
kiela2020hateful
and Social-IQA
sap2019socialiqa
focus on social reasoning and commonsense understanding, while EmpathicStories++
shen2024empathicstories++
evaluates empathetic abilities in multimodal narrative settings. Nevertheless, these studies continue to rely primarily on predefined objective benchmarks, lacking direct integration of subjective human engagement. As a result, they provide only a limited and indirect reflection on the actual user experience in real-world application scenarios.

Another strand of research introduces direct human ratings as an evaluation signal. Chatbot Arena
chiang2024chatbot
compares models through blind dialogue assessments, whereas OpenAssistant Conversations
kopf2023openassistant
collects feedback from volunteer-driven crowdsourced interactions. They are closer to real-world usage but are costly and often rely on entangled evaluation dimensions, obscuring the causes of experience differences.

In summary, existing methods face clear limitations: on one hand, a large subset of mainstream methods remain overly dependent on objective task completion metrics. On the other hand, while some advanced methods do involve limited forms of human feedback, they still lack a unified, fine-grained framework that systematically connects output quality with interaction experience. Building on this body of work, our study proposes an evaluation approach that jointly considers model outputs and interactive feedback, offering decomposable and interpretable analyses of user experience to address these gaps.

QoNext Framework

3.1
Literature Review

In the development of QoE research, the earliest focus was on QoS, which is defined by objective network and system-level indicators such as bandwidth, latency, packet loss, and jitter
singh2018quality
. However, relying solely on QoS is insufficient to capture users’ perceptions in their entirety
floris2016qosapproach
.
Consequently, Content Quality was gradually incorporated into the framework
zhang2024quality
, addressing factors such as visual clarity and semantic completeness. The integration of these two dimensions has been widely adopted in video quality assessment, multimedia transmission, and networking applications. This paradigm effectively explains the dual nature of user experience: it is shaped not only by transmission conditions but also by the intrinsic properties of the delivered content.

Methodologically, existing QoE research generally follows a standardized paradigm
barakovic2013survey
: first, subjective ratings are collected through laboratory tests or crowdsourcing to construct databases covering diverse degradation conditions; then, predictive models are trained on these databases to approximate human judgments. This framework not only quantifies the relative contributions of different factors to overall user experience but also provides actionable guidance for system optimization.

3.2
Questionnaire Investigation

To examine the applicability of this conceptual framework to large-model interactions, we design and distribute a questionnaire, inviting users to recall and report the key factors that most strongly influence their experience when engaging with large models. To analyze the collected responses, we apply word cloud analysis and K-means clustering, which together enable us to extract recurring themes from user feedback.

Through these approaches, we identify the core factors most frequently emphasized by users and classify them along the dual dimensions of content quality and service quality. Within this framework, five representative factors emerge: content accuracy, information density, output speed, latency position, and latency duration. This finding not only aligns with the theoretical underpinnings of traditional QoE but also provides empirical support for subjective evaluation in the context of large-model interactions. The detailed questionnaire design and results analysis are provided in the Appendix
A
.

3.3
QoNext Overview

Building on the comprehensive literature review and the preliminary user survey we conducted, we formally propose the
QoNext framework
as a novel evaluation methodology. Within this framework, we first construct a human-labeled dataset: by systematically varying the five key factors (content accuracy, information density, output speed, latency position, and latency duration), we simulate diverse response patterns that large models may exhibit in real interaction scenarios and collect subjective ratings from participants.

The collected ratings are then aggregated into a database, upon which we perform statistical and modeling analyses to uncover the mechanisms through which different factors shape human experience. Finally, regression models are employed to fit and predict user ratings, thereby testing the feasibility of using key parameters to evaluate subjective experience.
This complete process not only extends the methodological tradition of QoE but also, for the first time, systematically links content quality and interaction experience in large-model evaluation, offering practical guidance for future model development and optimization. 4.1
QoS Design

Based on the preliminary investigation in Sec
3.2
, we focus on output speed, latency position and latency duration of output for QoS design. LLM applications experience unstable output speeds and occasional pauses as reported by users. This is due to a mix of complex technical factors and intentional design choices, which are summarized as follows:

•

Generation and Transmission:
The model generates tokens sequentially in an autoregressive manner, while the server typically streams these tokens to the client in real time; depending on implementation and network conditions, the output may appear as a continuous stream or as small chunks.

•

Resource and Network Fluctuations:
Fluctuations in system resource scheduling, combined with instabilities in network transmission, constitute significant factors leading to speed variations, particularly under high concurrency.

•

Filtering and Check:
Some platforms also insert real-time content filtering and safety checks during streaming, which introduces further latency.

•

Deliberate Design:
For a more natural and engaging interaction, some products deliberately decelerate the output speed to mimic human typing rhythms, thereby enhancing the sense of immersion during the interaction.

Output Speed

We aim to cover a range of experiences from
instant responses
to
noticeable delays
, so as to systematically examine the effect of output speed on user perception. Prior research
nielsen1993response
,
google_rail_model_2020
indicates that when a user receives a response in under 100 milliseconds, they perceive the interaction as instantaneous. If the response takes up to 1 second, users will notice the delay, but they can generally maintain their train of thought. However, if the delay extends beyond this threshold, it becomes disruptive, and additional feedback, such as a loading indicator, is necessary to prevent users from losing attention and forming a negative impression. Therefore, we design three levels of output speed to model a spectrum of interactive conditions: 0.5 tokens/s, simulating near-instantaneous and fluent interaction; 0.1 tokens/s, representing a middle range where delay is perceptible but not disruptive; and 0.01 tokens/s, deliberately slow, intended to test the boundary conditions under which extended delays affect satisfaction and anthropomorphic perceptions.

Latency Position

To understand how latency position shapes user perception, our research design focuses on the most critical temporal windows, to systematically examine how they affect the user experience and satisfaction. At the very beginning, the Time to First Token (TTFT) is a critical indicator of responsiveness; a fast start makes users feel the system is processing their request immediately and sets a positive tone for the entire interaction
bentoml_llm_benchmark_2023
,
vllm_metrics_2024
. This is due to the primacy effect, where initial hesitations can disproportionately degrade a user’s overall impression, even if the rest of the response is delivered smoothly
weis2024primacy
,
pilat2021primacy
. Pauses in the middle of the output are also interpreted by users—short pauses might be seen as the model ”thinking,” while longer gaps are more likely to be perceived as outright hesitation or a failure
clark2002using
,
levinson2015timing
. Finally, the end of the output carries a heavy weight, following the peak-end rule. A delay or stall right before the response is complete can feel disproportionately long, heightening user impatience and significantly lowering their satisfaction with the experience
do2008evaluations
,
harrison2010faster
. Based on these findings, we divide the latency position into 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75, corresponding to startup feedback, early and mid-turn signals of planning and uncertainty, and the heavily weighted final segment, thereby capturing the full range of potential effects of latency on user experience.

Latency Duration

In order to explore the full range of user tolerance, our study was designed to configure latency duration that moves from simply noticeable to overtly frustrating. Prior research shows that while waits of around 2–3 seconds are generally endurable, they are readily perceived and already begin to influence attention
nah2004study
. As delays stretch toward 5 seconds, the patience of users erodes; studies in search and mobile environments report that response times beyond this point significantly increase abandonment rates and reduce satisfaction
brutlag2008user
,
google_mobile_abandon_2016
. Ultimately, prolonged waits of 7 seconds or more are consistently associated with heightened negative emotions like stress, irritation, and disengagement
arapakis2021impact
,
dwyer2016streaming_delays
. Drawing on these insights, we made choice of three distinct intervals, 3, 5, and 7 seconds, to represent the threshold of a tolerable delay, the tipping point of impatience, and a duration that provokes a marked emotional response, respectively. This scheme enables a structured exploration of how varying pause lengths make influence on both subjective user evaluations and observable behavior.

Content Design

In our work, “content” refers to question-answering pairs. We manage its creation and quality systematically through a three-step process. First, we define
Quality Classification

Next, we adopt

Finally, a rigorous

4.2.1
Quality Classification

We define content quality as the human-perceived quality of a model’s response, which serves as a core metric for evaluating its performance. Informed by prior research
park2023density
,
li2025information
,
fadahunsi2019protocol
and our own preliminary study, we specifically evaluate this quality along two key dimensions:
Information Density
and
Content Accuracy
.

Information Density is a critical metric in measuring how effectively a response conveys targeted information
jaeger2010redundancy
. A high-density response is characterized by conciseness and focus with the users’ query, including only the core content required to address the question, without unnecessary elaboration, redundant restatements, or tangential information. Conversely, a low-density score indicates a response that is overly verbose, bloated with excessive explanations, repeated points, or content unrelated to the original query. In short, a high Information Density score reflects a response that is user-centric, precise and relevant-delivering exactly what the user needs, while a low score signifies a response that is unfocused, inefficient, and potentially unhelpful-failing to prioritize the user’s need for clear.

Content Accuracy measures the correctness and logical coherence of the information provided in a response
bailey1983development
. A response is considered to be accurate if it is factually correct, logically consistent, and aligns with established knowledge. A high Content Accuracy score indicates a response free from factual errors or inconsistencies, while a low score corresponds to responses that contain substantial factual errors or logical fallacies. In essence, this metric evaluates the truthfulness and internal consistency of the output.

(\rho,\alpha)

\rho

\alpha
represents information accuracy. To simplify the experiments and amplify human perception of content differences, both
\rho
and
\alpha
take binary values, allowing for the classification of content quality into four distinct categories. Then we get the configuration of content quality of a model response as a tuple:

Dialogs Generation

This section outlines how to generate the dialogues used in our experiments.

Dialog Formats

Given the complexities inherent in multi-turn dialogues, where contextual dependencies can make influence on the quality and relevance of responses, we make a decision to simplify the experimental design to focus on single-turn interactions. 4.3
Experiment Setup

4.3.1
Format

The experiment comprised 54 questions, each paired with answers of varying quality. All procedures were conducted online through a self-constructed platform based on Gradio
1
1

1

https://www.gradio.app
, ensuring a consistent experimental environment. In each session, participants were required to evaluate all 54 question–answer pairs. For each pair, the presentation of the LLM’s response began only after the participant had fully understood the specific user question. Each pair was presented under pre-defined QoS conditions—controlling output speed, latency position, and latency duration—to emulate diverse response patterns that may occur in real-world interactions. To minimize systematic bias, both the answer content and the QoS settings are randomized across sessions.
Furthermore, for each question, the different QoS settings are evenly distributed across participants to collect a balanced number of evaluations for each condition. More details can be found in Appendix
C
.

4.3.2
Participants

We recruited a total of more than 70 participants, including both Chinese and English native speakers. Each participant conducted the experiment in their own language. To account for individual preferences, a short pre-experiment questionnaire was administered to collect personality-related information such as patience and MBTI type
myers1962myers
. Each participant could take part in up to four sessions, with each session involving ratings for 54 question–answer pairs. While the set of questions remained constant, the model responses and QoS configurations varied across sessions, thereby avoiding repeated ratings of identical items. Consequently, each participant evaluated any given question–answer pair at most once, ensuring independence and fairness in the data.
Following this complete experimental procedure, we successfully conducted a total of 292 distinct sessions, which collectively yielded 15,768 individual subjective ratings for subsequent analysis.

4.3.3
Metrics

Participants were tasked to rate the model outputs on a
1–5 integer scale
across three dimensions:
Overall Impression
,
Content Quality
, and
Perceived Responsiveness
. Our primary metric, Overall Impression, focused on the user’s holistic experience by jointly considering both content quality and response speed. As a secondary dimension, Content Quality focused exclusively on the accuracy, relevance, and value of the response itself. The third dimension, Perceived Responsiveness, measured only the speed and fluency of the generation, including any delays or interruptions.
This decoupled evaluation framework enables us to disentangle and precisely quantify the two distinct dimensions of content quality and response performance to the overall user experience.

4.4
Human Label Processing

After gathering subjective ratings from multiple individuals, it’s essential to process the data to extract valid group preferences and quantify the impact of various model parameters on user experience. Given the inherent individual differences, subjective fluctuations, and occasional anomalies in human ratings, we adopted a strict data processing workflow based on traditional QoE methods. By first cleaning the data to address outliers and then standardizing ratings to account for individual scale usage, we ensure the reliability and validity of our analysis. Ultimately, we calculate the Mean Opinion Scores (MOS), a metric that quantifies the average group preference and allows us to precisely measure how different settings influence the overall user experience.

4.4.1
Score Normalization and Logic Filtering

To eliminate differences in how raters use the scoring scale, we normalize all rating data using the
Z-score
method. This statistical technique standardizes each rater’s score by measuring its deviation from their own average score. The standardization formula is:

By the means of applying this method, we can compare scores across different raters on a common, standardized scale, making sure that individual rating styles don’t skew the overall results.

After standardizing the data and removing outliers, we removed 594 outlier data points, resulting in 15174 valid data points, and calculated the MOS for each experimental condition.
MOS is a standardized metric which quantifies the overall user perception of a model’s performance across various settings. The MOS calculation formula is as follows:

MOS=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}

(7)

where
Z_{i}
is the standardized score of each rater and
N
is the number of valid raters under the given setting. To ensure that MOS retains an understandable scale after standardization, we apply a linear transformation to scale the Z-scores to the
[0,5]
range, providing a straightforward assessment of user experience for each model setting.

Through this process, we obtain MOS scores for each experimental setting, providing a reliable data foundation for subsequent analysis of model performance and optimization. The MOS calculation reveals how different settings influence human subjective perception, offering valuable support for the design and optimization of foundation models.

6.3
Ablation Study

To further investigate the contribution of individual input parameters to model performance, we conducted an ablation study. The experimental design and training setup were kept consistent with the main predictive modeling experiments described earlier, with the only difference being the systematic removal of one parameter at a time before training the regression models. The retrained models were then evaluated on the same test set, and their performance was averaged across all model families to obtain representative results.

The results are summarized in Table
2
. The original setting with all parameters included achieves a stronge overall performance, with an average SRCC of 0.7842, PLCC of 0.8295, KRCC of 0.6396, and RMSE of 0.8746. By contrast, removing Content Accuracy causes a drastic performance degradation across all metrics, leading to the lowest SRCC (0.5907), PLCC (0.6392), and KRCC (0.4693), along with the highest RMSE (1.1663). This indicates that content accuracy plays a central role in shaping user judgments, and its absence severely limits the model’s predictive capability.

Other factors show more moderate effects. Excluding Information Density or Latency-related features yields only slight decreases compared to the full setting, suggesting that these features contribute to performance but are not indispensable for maintaining rank-level consistency. The removal of Speed, however, results in a notable performance drop (SRCC reduced to 0.7362, KRCC to 0.5816), indicating that perceived response speed has a meaningful but secondary influence on subjective evaluations.

Overall, the ablation study provides a key finding: although multiple parameters collectively make measurable contributions to the final model accuracy, it is the content-related factors—with Content Accuracy being particularly dominant—that play the decisive role in determining predictive performance. This result strongly aligns with the intuitive understanding that users primarily base their subjective judgments on the core informational quality of a response, while system-level factors such as latency position, duration, and output speed function as less decisive, secondary moderating signals.

7
Further Work

The proposed QoNext framework and its accompanying dataset provide the first practical implementation for incorporating QoE principles into the evaluation and optimization of interaction experiences with foundation models. As an initial exploration in this direction, our work not only verifies the feasibility of predicting users’ subjective perceptions from controllable model parameters but also highlights the potential value of embedding human-experience signals into model development workflows.
To quantitatively validate the predictive capability of the proposed QoNext regression model, we conducted a targeted small-scale experiment. The results demonstrate that it can accurately predict user ratings across different parameter configurations, thereby offering a reliable guidance signal for model tuning (see Appendix
F
for details). This line of research still offers ample room for expansion and warrants deeper investigation.

On the application side, QoNext can be integrated into model training, inference scheduling, and resource allocation strategies, serving as a feedback signal to guide large models in dynamically balancing content richness and response speed.
In particular, within complex multi-model service scenarios, QoNext can support intelligent scheduling and dynamic resource allocation between large and small models, enabling adaptive load balancing and response strategies that jointly optimize both cost and experience.

For metric and experimental extensions, future work could incorporate additional interaction dimensions—such as response tone, textual confidence, emotional consistency, and contextual coherence—to further enrich QoE factors and explore their impact on subjective experience.
Fine-grained experiments can also be strategically designed to identify key bottlenecks that currently constrain model speed optimization. Guided by product requirements, test interaction mechanisms such as staged output and dynamic feedback to alleviate perceived latency and provide practical guidance for system design.

In addition, the MBTI-based personalized analysis opens up new avenues for personality-aware perception and experience modeling. Future research can explore personality-driven optimization, such as adjusting response rhythm or information density for different personality types, to achieve more fine-grained human–AI interaction customization.

Overall, QoNext demonstrates promise not only as a tool for evaluating user experience with foundation models but also as an effective guiding mechanism for facilitating iterative model improvement and human–AI interaction design, driving large models toward greater user-friendliness and closer alignment with human preferences.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we propose QoNext, a QoE-inspired framework for evaluating foundation models that integrates both content quality and service quality. By systematically varying five key factors—content accuracy, information density, output speed, latency position, and latency duration—we construct a human-annotated database and analyze their respective contributions to user perception.
Our results demonstrate that content quality is the dominant determinant of overall experience, while interaction efficiency plays a secondary yet independent role. Personalized analysis based on MBTI denotes that while different personality types exhibit consistent overall structures, they differ in their sensitivities to specific factors.
Furthermore, regression modeling shows that human ratings can be effectively predicted from these factors. Taken together, QoNext provides a unified and interpretable methodology for linking model parameters to user experience, offering practical guidance for designing and optimizing human-centered AI systems.

Explain quantum entanglement in simple terms and how it differs from classical physics.

Model Response:

Quantum entanglement is a very peculiar and important phenomenon in quantum mechanics. To understand it, we can start by discussing the differences between classical physics and quantum physics. In classical physics, the state of an object is definite; for example, the position and velocity of a ball can be measured precisely at the same time. However, in quantum physics, the state of a particle is described by a probabilistic wave function, which ”collapses” to a definite state only when measured.

Quantum entanglement refers to a special correlated state formed between two or more particles at the quantum level. This correlation persists even if the particles are far apart, or even at opposite ends of the universe. Information between entangled particles is transmitted instantaneously, a phenomenon Einstein referred to as ”spooky action at a distance.” For instance, consider two entangled electrons with opposite spin states. If you measure the spin direction of one electron, the spin direction of the other electron is immediately determined, regardless of the distance between them.

This phenomenon differs from classical physics, which follows the principle of locality, meaning objects can only influence each other through contact or some medium. Quantum entanglement breaks this principle, exhibiting non-locality. In classical physics, the speed of information transfer cannot exceed the speed of light, but quantum entanglement seems to be free from this limitation.

Another difference of quantum entanglement is its inseparability. In classical physics, we can decompose a system into multiple independent parts for study, but in quantum entanglement, the wholeness of the entangled state prevents us from simply breaking it down into independent parts. Particles in an entangled state are no longer independent entities but part of an overall system.

Quantum entanglement has important applications in quantum computing and quantum communication. In quantum computing, entangled states can be used to achieve superdense coding of qubits and quantum gate operations, significantly improving computational efficiency. In quantum communication, entangled states can be used for quantum key distribution, ensuring the security of information transmission.

In summary, quantum entanglement is a fascinating phenomenon in quantum mechanics that challenges our intuitive understanding of the real world. Unlike the determinism and locality of classical physics, quantum entanglement exhibits features of non-locality and wholeness, revealing a more complex and wondrous microscopic world.

Appendix D
Topic Analysis of QoNext Database

To further examine how user ratings vary across different dialogue topics, we conducted a topic-level MOS analysis based on the QoNext database.

First, we calculated the overall MOS for each dialogue record and divided all samples into three quality tiers: a high-MOS group (MOS above 4.0), a mid-MOS group (MOS between 2.0 and 4.0), and a low-MOS group (MOS below 2.0). This grouping reflects different levels of user experience from poor to excellent. Within each quality tier, we then computed the average MOS for five predefined dialogue categories to evaluate how topic performance varies within the same quality level. Finally, the mean scores of the five topics were visualized in a radar chart to intuitively present the overall distribution and topic differences across the three quality tiers.

As shown in Figure
9
, the three quality tiers exhibit clear hierarchical patterns. In the low-MOS group, noticeable variations emerge across topics: Lifestyle & Entertainment and Empathy & Personal Growth achieve slightly higher averages, whereas Knowledge & Reasoning and Creative Tasks are relatively lower. This indicates that when the overall output quality is poor, users display different levels of tolerance across topics, giving more lenient evaluations to open-ended or emotionally engaging conversations. In contrast, the mid-MOS and high-MOS groups show uniformly high and closely aligned scores, producing smoother radar curves without marked separations, which suggests that once model outputs reach moderate or high quality, user ratings primarily reflect the overall experience rather than topic-specific differences. Taken together, this analysis shows that QoNext ratings capture a certain degree of topic sensitivity at low quality levels, but topic effects diminish considerably when quality reaches medium or high levels, indicating that users focus more on overall content and interaction quality. This finding provides valuable insight for future model improvements targeting topic-specific enhancements in lower-quality scenarios.

Appendix F
Predictive Experiments for Regression Models

To validate the predictive performance of the QoNext models, we designed and conducted a small-scale verification experiment to examine whether the QoNext models can accurately predict users’ subjective ratings of large-model interaction experiences from controllable parameters.
The experiment was based on the QoNext database and incorporated both content quality and QoS parameters to test the model’s generalization ability under parameter combinations that differed from those used in the dataset construction stage.

F.1
Setup

A single question was randomly selected from the dialogue content used in the main study, and responses were generated under new QoS settings. Three key QoS parameters were manipulated with values distinct from those in the original dataset: output speed was set to 0.03 or 0.06 seconds per word, latency position to 0.3 or 0.6, and latency duration to 1 or 9 seconds, thereby testing the model’s ability to generalize to entirely new parameter configurations.
To avoid potential bias from the original annotators, an independent group of participants—none of whom were involved in dataset construction—was recruited to provide subjective ratings.
Participant profiles, rating metrics, and evaluation procedures followed the same protocol as in the main data collection. Each parameter combination was evaluated by three different raters, resulting in 96 annotated dialogues completed by 24 participants, ensuring both independence and sufficient coverage of the evaluation data.

F.2
Results

The already trained QoNext regression models described in the main text were applied to predict user ratings under these new parameter combinations, and their predictions were compared against the independently collected human ratings. Evaluation metrics remained consistent with those reported in the main text. The results are shown in Table
5
.

The results show that the QoNext models maintains stable predictive performance even when tested on parameter configurations entirely different from those in the training data. All dimensions achieve moderate to high correlation coefficients, with the content dimension performing best, indicating that the model can effectively capture user ratings of content and interaction quality under new conditions and thus demonstrates strong generalization ability.
