Primary large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the submandibular gland arising from a long-standing benign tumor: case report
Authors: Dorra Chiboub, Ameni Amri, Nadia Romdhane, Imen Zoghlami, Ehsen Ben Brahim, Chiraz Mbarek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is an aggressive malignancy that commonly arises in the lungs. Primary salivary gland involvement is exceptional, presenting a major diagnostic challenge in differentiating it from metastatic pulmonary disease.Case presentation:A 62-year-old man presented with a right submandibular mass that had been stable for 16 years before rapidly expanding. Imaging revealed a massive 11 cm necrotic submandibular tumor with disseminated pulmonary and hepatic metastases. Following partial debulking, pathology revealed a high-grade neuroendocrine tumor (Ki-67 index 85%) arising alongside a benign mesenchymal component. It underscores the importance of including high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma in the differential diagnosis of rapidly expanding salivary lesions.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms typically arise in the gastrointestinal tract or lungs. Primary head and neck neuroendocrine carcinomas are exceptional, accounting for less than 1% of malignancies in this region. The WHO classifies large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) as a poorly differentiated, high-grade carcinoma distinct from well-differentiated “carcinoids.” It exhibits aggressive biological behavior and expresses neuroendocrine markers like Synaptophysin, Chromogranin, and CD56. Because native salivary tissue lacks neuroendocrine cells, LCNEC pathogenesis is debated; the prevailing theory suggests that it arises via dedifferentiation of a pre-existing neoplasm or from pluripotent stem cells, rather than de novo[3,4].

Diagnosing primary salivary LCNEC is challenging because metastatic disease must be rigorously excluded. A neck mass co-existing with lung nodules typically suggests a pulmonary primary metastasizing to the neck. Therefore, definitively distinguishing a salivary primary from a metastasis requires careful correlation of clinical history, radiological features, and immunohistochemistry[5].

We report a rare primary submandibular LCNEC in a 62-year-old man. The clinical evolution suggests the rapid dedifferentiation of a long-standing benign mass, which presented with “cannonball” lung and liver metastases. We highlight the diagnostic challenges of confirming a salivary origin and the implications of this high-grade malignant transformation. This report follows the SCARE criteria[6].

Case presentation

A healthy 62-year-old man presented with a hard, fixed right latero-cervical mass (Fig. 1) that had been stable for 16 years. One month prior to presentation, the lesion exhibited sudden, rapid expansion accompanied by asthenia and a 17-kg weight loss. Figure 1.Voluminous mass occupying the right submandibular region.

Voluminous mass occupying the right submandibular region.

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a 13.6 × 7.2 cm heterogeneously enhancing, necrotic, and calcified mass centered in the right submandibular space. The tumor exhibited aggressive local invasion of the parotid gland, pterygoid, and floor-of-mouth muscles, along with osteolysis of the mandibular ramus.

Staging CT demonstrated a “cannonball” pattern of bilateral pulmonary metastases (up to 21 mm), 30 mm subcarinal lymphadenopathy, multiple hepatic metastases (up to 49 mm), and a pathologic D12 vertebral fracture. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was negative for a primary digestive neoplasm. To further evaluate the diagnosis, the patient underwent surgical exploration of the cervical mass. The lesion corresponded to an enormous, whitish, encapsulated mass infiltrating the floor of the mouth. Because of the extent of invasion into the floor of the mouth, only a partial excision was performed, primarily intended for diagnostic and palliative debulking (Fig. 2). Histopathology revealed a dual-component tumor. The malignant component comprised large, necrotic cells with vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli (Fig. 3A). It was confirmed as a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma (Ki-67: 85%), staining diffusely positive for CD56 (Fig. 3B) and Synaptophysin (Fig. 3C), but negative for Chromogranin. This was associated with a distinct, unclassified benign mesenchymal proliferation of well-vascularized, non-atypical fusiform cells (Vimentin-positive; PS100, GFAP, and CD34-negative). While direct tissue transition was not clearly observed, these combined features strongly suggest the probable malignant transformation of a pre-existing benign lesion. A liver biopsy confirmed a metastasis identical to the cervical mass.

Based on morphology, immunohistochemistry, and clinical evolution, the final diagnosis was primary submandibular LCNEC – arising from a long-standing benign mesenchymal tumor – with lung, liver, and bone metastases. The patient’s Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status performance status at diagnosis was 3. Following a multidisciplinary team discussion, the recommended plan was a referral to a specialized oncology department for palliative systemic chemotherapy. The patient was discharged home to recover from surgery while awaiting his scheduled oncology consultation prior to initiating systemic treatment. Unfortunately, due to the rapid and aggressive biological behavior of the disease, his condition acutely deteriorated, and he passed away 1 week after hospital discharge before systemic therapy could be administered.

Discussion

While common in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, neuroendocrine neoplasms account for less than 1% of major salivary gland malignancies. Because small cell carcinoma is the most frequent subtype, primary salivary LCNEC is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 20 submandibular cases reported in the English-language literature. The latest WHO classification categorizes them as poorly differentiated, high-grade carcinomas, distinguishing them from well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (carcinoids) occasionally seen in the head and neck[3].

Confirming primary salivary LCNEC is a diagnosis of exclusion, as metastasis from a pulmonary primary is much more common. Because normal salivary glands lack native neuroendocrine cells, the tumor’s cellular origin is debated. The literature suggests two main pathogenetic theories: first, de novo development from pluripotent excretory duct stem cells differentiating along a neuroendocrine pathway; second, dedifferentiation or transdifferentiation of a pre-existing benign or malignant epithelial neoplasm. This latter theory is supported by reports of LCNEC components arising within old benign lesions or alongside non-neuroendocrine carcinomas, a phenomenon similar to the malignant transformation seen in carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma[7].

The pathogenesis of this tumor likely involves dedifferentiation or transdifferentiation of a pre-existing epithelial or benign mesenchymal neoplasm into a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma. While our histological findings showed LCNEC components alongside benign tissue, the precise classification of the benign component remains limited by extensive necrosis and massive malignant overgrowth. Therefore, the “tumor-within-tumor” malignant transformation remains a highly probable yet ultimately hypothetical pathogenesis in this case.

Excluding a metastasis from a primary lung tumor is the most critical step in managing head and neck LCNEC. Diagnosis relies on specific morphology (large cells, low nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, high mitotic rate) combined with positive neuroendocrine markers (Synaptophysin, Chromogranin A, and CD56). Furthermore, although TTF-1 is a key discriminator that classically indicates a pulmonary or thyroid primary, it presents a diagnostic pitfall: a small subset of extrapulmonary head and neck neuroendocrine carcinomas can aberrantly express it[8,9].

Confirming a primary salivary LCNEC rather than a pulmonary metastasis remains a diagnostic challenge, and our conclusion of a salivary primary represents the most clinically probable scenario rather than an absolute certainty. While the immunohistochemical profile demonstrated TTF-1 negativity, this does not definitively rule out a pulmonary origin, as aberrant expression profiles are documented in extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinomas. Furthermore, the absence of a PET-CT or bronchoscopy in our diagnostic workup presents a limitation in definitively excluding a microscopic primary lung lesion. However, the presence of a stable cervical mass for 16 years followed by acute expansion, coupled with the “cannonball” pattern of the lung lesions – which is classic for hematogenous metastasis – strongly supports the submandibular gland as the primary site.

Salivary LCNEC is a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis, driven by rapid growth (Ki-67 > 50–80%) and early lymphatic and hematogenous dissemination (lungs, liver, bone). Unlike typical head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, which initially remain loco-regional, LCNEC frequently presents with synchronous distant metastases at diagnosis[10].

When it comes to treating salivary LCNEC, there are no established therapeutic plans, given the rarity of this entity. However, it is possible to extrapolate from protocols developed for pulmonary LCNEC and small cell carcinomas.

For resectable non-metastatic disease, the gold standard treatment is radical surgical excision followed by adjuvant radiotherapy. For locally advanced or metastatic disease, the first-line treatment relies on platinum-based chemotherapy, typically cisplatin or carboplatin combined with etoposide. Despite high initial response rates, rapid relapse and chemoresistance are universally documented[11–13].

Targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors for extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinomas are being explored in recent studies. A genomic profiling study conducted in 2021 revealed that salivary neuroendocrine carcinomas may exhibit mutations in TP53, RB1, and PIK3CA. Furthermore, to determine eligibility for immunotherapy, it is recommended to evaluate Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) and PD-L1 expression. However, the literature still lacks robust data specific to salivary LCNEC[14,15].

The prognosis for primary salivary LCNEC is generally guarded. However, metastatic disease classifies it as Stage IV, with a low survival rate of only a few months. The presence of hepatic metastasis is a particularly poor prognostic factor that often dictates the rate of decline[8].

A major strength of this report is the detailed documentation of the chronological and clinical evolution of a very rare salivary malignancy, supplemented by robust immunohistochemical profiling. However, this report is limited by the lack of definitive molecular sequencing of the tumor, the absence of PET/CT imaging to unequivocally exclude other primary sites, and the inability to assess response to systemic therapies due to the patient’s rapid clinical decline.

Conclusions

Primary submandibular LCNEC is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy. This case highlights a unique presentation, likely arising from the malignant transformation of a long-standing benign mesenchymal tumor. Differentiating it from metastatic disease is critical; in this case, the clinical timeline, “cannonball” pulmonary metastases, and TTF-1 negativity strongly supported a salivary origin. Due to its rapid systemic dissemination, high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma must be included in the differential diagnosis of any chronic salivary mass exhibiting sudden expansion, emphasizing the critical need for prompt, multimodal management.
