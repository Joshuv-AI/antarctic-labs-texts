Migrant Background Youth and Hazardous Drinking: Drinking to Fit Into Australian Society?
Authors: Caroline Ng Tseung‐Wong, Justine Dandy, Amanda M George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIn this study, we proposed that migrant youth might have acculturation reasons to drink alcohol and explored the role of acculturative gap conflict and perceived drinking culture in predicting hazardous drinking. We examined proximal acculturation reasons for drinking in terms of drinking to fit into Australian culture and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations.MethodsWe conducted an online survey with 470 participants (age range 18–25 years). Data from those who reported having tried alcohol (n = 383; 65% female, 71% born in Australia) were analysed. We grouped participants under those who came from similar alcohol consumption environments to Australia and those who did not.ResultsUsing hierarchical logistic regression, we found that predictors of hazardous alcohol use included motives for drinking (coping—OR = 2.28, 95% CI [1.57, 3.32], p < 0.001; enhancement—OR = 2.22, 95% CI [1.50, 3.29], p < 0.001) and drinking to fit in (OR = 1.47, 95% CI [1.04, 2.08], p = 0.028). Those who came from similar drinking environments as Australia had increased odds of drinking at hazardous levels compared to those who came from dissimilar drinking environments (OR = 0.44, 95% CI [0.23, 0.83], p = 0.011). The model correctly classified three‐quarters of both non‐hazardous and hazardous drinkers and demonstrated good discriminant validity (AUC = 0.845).Discussion and ConclusionsBesides coping and enhancement drinking motives, drinking to fit in was a significant predictor of hazardous drinking. This highlights the importance of looking at proximal acculturation reasons for drinking in migrant youth.Keywords: acculturative gap conflict, drinking motives, drinking to fit in, migrant youth

Introduction

Alcohol use is one of the leading public health concerns in Western countries [1]. However, little is known about the alcohol attitudes and use among migrant youth in these countries, especially outside of the United States. Early research regarding acculturation and alcohol use in migrant populations has mostly used proxy measures of acculturation such as language fluency, generation and years of residency [2]. Acculturation, which is the process of cultural change and adaptation that results from ongoing contact between two or more cultural groups [3], is more nuanced than static proxy measures. For many migrants to WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic) countries, there may be a mismatch between alcohol‐related norms in their countries of origin and those of the new country of settlement. Adoption of alcohol norms of the settlement country can reflect acculturation. Therefore, the current study investigates the influences of acculturation on alcohol use in migrant youth by going beyond proxy measures to look at: (i) perceived acculturation gap conflict and perceived drinking mainstream culture; and (ii) acculturation‐specific reasons for drinking. We extend the empirical base of acculturation and drinking research by examining the above in a settler country (Australia) other than the US. This is important given that 32% of Australian residents are born overseas [4] and according to the 2021 Census, 48.2% of Australians have at least one parent born overseas.

Research on alcohol patterns in migrant background individuals is primarily from the US, specifically focussed on one ethnic group at a time (see [2, 5, 6, 7, 8] for reviews and meta‐analyses). Studies are mostly within the Hispanic/Latine American communities [9] and, to a lesser extent, Asian American communities [10]. Evidence supports a small but positive association between acculturation to White American norms and alcohol use among Hispanic/Latine samples, but this is also moderated by factors such as alcohol patterns (e.g., drinking game participation [11]), acculturation domains (e.g., language use [12]), dimensions (ethnic identification versus US identification) and gender [13, 14, 15]. Similar findings have been observed with Asian Americans (e.g., [16, 17]) although some studies have not found a significant link between acculturation to Americans norms and measures of alcohol use among Asian Americans [18].

In Australia, it is commonly accepted that drinking alcohol is a cultural norm [19]. The National Drug Strategy Household Survey (2022–2023) identified that of all age groups, young adults aged 18–24 years were the most likely to consume alcohol at risky levels [20]. Moreover, according to the 2011 Census, approximately 25% of Australian youth aged 12–24 years were from a migrant background. While national data regarding alcohol and other drug (AOD) use among adult migrants in Australia are limited [21], findings suggest lower AOD use among (a) Australians who speak English as a second language at home [21] and (b) ethnic minority adults [22]. However, this can change with longer residency and over successive generations [23], perhaps due in part to acculturation to perceived Australian drinking culture. There is a substantial proportion of Australian youth growing up in bicultural (or multicultural) contexts who may have to navigate a range of potentially conflicting cultural and social norms, including for alcohol use.

Of the limited Australian research, findings suggest that alcohol consumption is generally lower in youth from non‐English backgrounds compared with Australian born and/or English‐speaking young people [23, 24]. In a large‐scale study with 10,273 adolescent participants from high school Grades 7, 9 and 11 [23], adolescents born in Asia and Africa were less likely to report drinking alcohol (in the past 30 days) than Australian born adolescents. They also reported higher levels of parental monitoring and disapproval of alcohol use. Recent studies have employed focus groups or in‐depth interviews with migrant and/or ethnic minority youth from a range of ethnicities [25] and with refugee background South Sudanese youth [26]. Findings demonstrated that AOD use was perceived as normative for young people in Australia and there was negative ethnic community stigma related to AOD and fears of consequences within their families/communities [25]. Neither study assessed the role of acculturation on AOD use.

A recent focus group study with 55 migrant youth in Perth examined acculturation and AOD use. Three themes were constructed: bicultural conflict and acculturative stress, freedom and constraints, and gender × culture [27]. Findings showed the ubiquity of alcohol in Australian culture and drinking as one way to fit into Australian culture (herein referred to as ‘drinking to fit in’). Participants reported drinking to cope in response to feeling conflicted between their parents' expectations and the mainstream norms. These themes align with Lui et al.'s [28] identified perspectives on how individual acculturation can influence drinking. The socialisation‐and‐learning perspective proposes that individuals' drinking attitudes and beliefs are formed by the norms and practices dominant in the mainstream culture. Migrant and minoritised individuals may adopt the attitudes of the mainstream to increase their social adjustment. For instance, mainstream peers and popular media have been found to significantly increase drinking behaviours (e.g., [29]). The stress‐and‐coping perspective suggests that the challenges of adapting to a new culture can lead to drinking as a coping strategy to relieve migration and minority stress. Acculturative stress can take the form of intergenerational conflict due to the incongruence in cultural practices between parents and children (e.g., [30]). Finally, the access‐and‐opportunity perspective proposes that individuals who have more purchasing power would have easier access to frequent drinking.

In the current study we investigate whether acculturation gap conflict (acculturative stress) and perceived Australian drinking culture (socialisation) influence hazardous alcohol use measured with the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) [31]. It is expected that greater conflict between parental expectations and that of the mainstream society (acculturation gap conflict) would be associated with increased odds ratio for hazardous alcohol use. From a socialisation perspective, the mainstream drinking culture prevalent in the society may influence the drinking behaviours of migrant youth. We therefore explore if perceived Australian drinking culture plays a role in hazardous alcohol use of migrant youth.

Furthermore, measures of acculturation in current research tend to lack predictive specificity. What is needed are acculturative measures targeted for specific behaviours such as hazardous drinking. Lui et al. [28] pertinently noted that the acculturative literature on drinking and the general conceptual models of drinking in terms of motives and beliefs (e.g., [32, 33]) work in silo. This is unfortunate because there could well be drinking motives that are specific to migrant individuals associated with acculturation to their new home. Motives have been described as ‘the final pathway to alcohol use’ ([34], p. 178) and can be organised by source and valence [32]. Valence considers whether the desired outcome is positive (e.g., pleasurable) or negative (e.g., to reduce distress) and the source can also be internal or external (e.g., drinking to enhance a positive mood or drinking to fit in, respectively). With this framework, Cooper [32] proposed four general drinking motives: enhancement (positive/internal), social (positive, external), coping (negative/internal) and conformity (negative/external). Based on the recommendation of Lui et al. [28] and acknowledging that context‐specific drinking motives have also been examined (e.g., [35, 36]), we test two possible reasons for drinking that are specific to individuals growing up from a migrant background: drinking to fit in and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations. While similar to conformity and coping motives, respectively, they reflect specific acculturation reasons for alcohol use. Indeed, migrant youth reported drinking to conform with the Australian culture and suggested that the challenge of reconciling heritage and mainstream cultures was associated with problematic drinking [27]. We therefore assessed these two specific acculturation reasons for drinking and predict that they would be associated with higher alcohol intake.

Finally, as highlighted by Dandy et al.'s [27] focus group study, there are gender and culture interactions at play in alcohol use. Across the cultural groups, participants reported that drinking alcohol was less acceptable for women compared to men. This suggests that for migrants coming from lower alcohol baseline environments, acculturation effects might be stronger for women compared to men [2]. For instance, high acculturation is associated with more drinking in Latina women (e.g., [37]) but the findings are mixed for Latino men (e.g., [38, 39]). In the current study, we explore the interaction of gender with: (i) acculturation gap conflict; and (ii) perceived drinking culture. We do not make specific predictions given that the literature does not point to a clear trend although the recent qualitative work with Australian migrant youth suggests higher heritage culture pressure when it comes to drinking for women.

Methods

Participants

The data were collected by Online Research Unit a panel research company and the study was focussed on emerging adults (18–25 years old). Participants receive points for completing surveys which can be cashed out as cash gift vouchers sent to their postal addresses. In the current survey, the participants received between AUD 5‐AUD 10. Five hundred and three participants initially completed the online survey via Qualtrics. There were 33 incomplete cases. Out of the 470 participants who completed the survey, most were female (62%), 37% were male and 2% identified as Trans and/or gender diverse. Participants answered the following screening question: ‘Do you consider that you belong to another cultural group other than Australian’? If they answer ‘yes’ to the question, they could proceed with the survey. A total of 68.72% of the participants were born in Australia and 84.47% were Australian citizens. The percentage of participants who reported that their mother was not born in Australia was 62.98% (64.88% reported their father was not born in Australia). Many participants reported living with their parent(s) and/or extended family (61.49%). For those who were not born in Australia (31.28%), there were 52 countries of birth reported with the most common ones being New Zealand (4.26%), China (2.13%), UK (2.13) and India (1.7%). Most participants reported speaking English at home (75.53%) with Chinese (e.g., Mandarin, Cantonese) the second most reported (4.47%). In terms of religion, the five most common denomination participants reported were no religious affiliation (39.79%), Christian (33.62%), Muslim (7.87%), Buddhist (5.32%) and Hindu (5.11%). There is considerable heterogeneity in terms of the ethnic/cultural background reported by participants with the highest being Chinese (9.57%), followed by Indian (7.23%) and Italian (6.81%) (see Supporting Information). The data analytic sample for the current study was restricted to participants who answered ‘yes’ to the question ‘have you ever tried alcohol’ (n = 383). Of these, 65% were female, 71% were born in Australia, 61.4% still lived with their parents and/or extended family. The mean age was 22.05 years (SD = 2.37). Given the heterogeneity of the sample in terms of ethnic background, we created a demographic variable (see Table 1) differentiating participants on whether they came from geographical environments which were similar to Australia on alcohol consumption (e.g., US, UK, Europe) or dissimilar to Australia on alcohol consumption (e.g., Southeast Asia, East Asia, South Asia) based on the World Health Organization alcohol data [1].

Measures

Cultural Preference Conflict is a sub‐dimension of the Acculturation Gap Conflict Inventory [40]. There are seven items measured on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) with higher scores representing more cultural preference conflict. One example of an item is ‘My parents complain that I act too Australian’ (Cronbach α = 0.90).

Perceived Drinking Culture

This dimension is made up of three items and was constructed for the current study. It measures participants' perceived drinking culture in the country. Items were assessed on a five‐point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were: ‘Drinking alcohol is part of the Australian culture’, ‘Heavy drinking is normalised in Australia’ and ‘To be fully Australian, is to drink alcohol at social gatherings’ (Cronbach α = 0.73).

Drinking Motives

The Drinking Motives Questionnaire‐Revised‐Short‐Form [33] measures four drinking motives: enhancement (α = 0.76), social (α = 0.81), coping (α = 0.84) and conformity (α = 0.84). Participants respond on a scale from 1 (almost never/never) to 5 (almost always/always). Higher scores are indicative of greater endorsement of each drinking motive.

Acculturation Drinking

We devised three items for drinking to fit in assessed on the same five‐point scale as drinking motives (1 = almost never/never to 5 = almost always/always) and with the same instructions. Items were: ‘To fit in Australian society’, ‘To be accepted as an Australian’ and ‘To be part of the Australian culture’ (α = 0.91). Three items assessed drinking to cope with parental/community expectations (α = 0.895). These items were: ‘To escape family conflicts due to growing up in a different context to parents’, ‘To deal with family expectations of the good/successful migrant’ and ‘To defy parental and cultural community expectations’.

Given construction of items and potential similarity with coping and conformity drinking motives, a confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS 29 ascertained if drinking to fit in and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations were distinct dimensions from each other and the drinking motives. We specified a six‐factor model where each dimension (coping, enhancement, conformity, sociability, drinking to fit in, coping with parental/community expectation) was made up of three items. We allowed error terms to correlate if modification indices suggested so. The six‐factor model, with error terms correlations, had acceptable fit, χ 2 (115) = 286.11, p < 0.001; Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.962; Standardised Root Mean Squared Error (SRMR) = 0.051, Root Mean Squared Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.062, 90% confidence interval (CI) [0.053, 0.072]. A six‐factor model was better than a four‐factor model where the three items from the drinking to fit in dimension were placed under ‘conformity’ and the three items assessing drinking to cope with parental/community expectations were placed under ‘coping’. Error terms were again allowed to correlate but the resulting model still had poor fit, χ 2 (123) = 533.304, CFI = 0.908, SRMR = 0.085, RMSEA = 0.093, 90% CI [0.085, 0.102]. Goodness of fit statistics show a statistically significant improvement in model fit of the six‐factor model compared to four‐factor model, Δχ 2 (2) = 247.19, p = 0.01 and substantial differences in the CFI (0.962 vs. 0.908) and RMSEA (0.062 vs. 0.093). We also tested a model whereby the two acculturation drinking motives (drinking to fit in and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations) were combined as one acculturative drinking motive with six items. Error terms were also allowed to correlate, and the resulting model was adequate, χ 2 (114) = 297.137, CFI = 0.959, SRMR = 0.0546, RMSEA = 0.065, 90% CI [0.056, 0.074]. Moreover, the six‐factor model was significantly better than this five‐factor model, Δχ 2 (1) = 11.027, p = 0.01 and there were differences in the CFI (0.962 vs. 0.959) and RMSEA (0.062 vs. 0.065). We therefore treated Cooper's (1994) drinking motives as four dimensions and the acculturation reasons as two dimensions.

Hazardous Alcohol Use

The 10‐item Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test [31] measures alcohol consumption, dependence symptoms and alcohol‐related problems [41]. Possible scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores reflecting greater likelihood of hazardous/harmful drinking and possible dependence. An AUDIT score of 8 or more is indicative of hazardous and harmful alcohol use [41] and was used to classify ‘hazardous’ alcohol use in the current study.

Analysis Strategy

A hierarchical logistic regression examined the odds of being classified as a hazardous drinker versus a non‐hazardous one (0 = no, 1 = yes). All continuous variables were centred. The rationale for the sequence of the blocks is that we are interested in the unique contributions of acculturation and drinking motives dimensions, after controlling for demographic variables. Demographic variables, that is, gender, generation (first vs. later generations), living at parental home, drinking environment (dissimilar to Australia vs. similar) were first entered to control for their potential influence on hazardous drinking. In Block 2, we then examined the unique contributions of acculturation variables (acculturation gap conflict and perceived drinking norms) together with each of these variables' interactions with gender, after accounting for demographic variables. In Block 3, we added the influence of acculturation drinking reasons (drinking to fit in and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations), and finally in Block 4, we added the four general drinking motives. Models were evaluated and compared across blocks using the Nagelkerke R 2, model chi‐square and change in χ 2 (Δχ 2). The discriminative ability of the final model was further evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and sensitivity and specificity values. Finally, in Block 4, we evaluate the role of general drinking motives.

Assumption checks were carried out for multicollinearity (variance inflation factor within acceptable range of 1.09–2.20) as well as the Box‐Tidwell test to check for linearity assumptions. Interaction terms were created between each continuous predictor and its natural logarithm and were then added to a logistic regression. All interaction terms were non‐significant, except for general drinking motives coping (p < 0.001) and enhancement (p < 0.001), suggesting that the linearity assumption was partially met. We reran the logistic regression with log transformation applied to these two drinking motives, and the pattern of results was similar to the non‐transformed dimensions with the same predictors reaching significance in the same direction. We therefore report results using untransformed variables for ease of interpretation. Syntax code for carrying out the Box‐Tidwell test was generated using Claude 4.5. Sonnet (accessed May 2026). All AI‐generated syntax code was reviewed, tested and revised by the first author, who confirmed accuracy and reproducibility of results.

Predictors of Hazardous and Non‐Hazardous Drinking

A hierarchical (sequential) logistic regression was performed to assess hazardous drinking (0 = no, 1 = yes). The model fit at Block 1 was non‐significant, χ 2(4, N = 311) = 9.23, p = 0.056, Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.039 and drinking environment was a predictor of hazardous drinking (odds ratio [OR] = 0.61, 95% CI [0.38, 0.992], p = 0.046). Individuals from a dissimilar drinking environment to Australia had 0.61 times reduced odds of hazardous drinking compared to those from a similar environment. After addition of the two acculturation predictors in Block 2, there was significant improvement in model fit, χ 2(6, N = 311) = 34.68, p < 0.001, Δχ 2(2, N = 311) = 25.45, p < 0.001, Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.141. Drinking environment, OR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.30, 0.87], p = 0.013 and acculturation gap conflict, OR = 1.75, 95% CI [1.34, 2.28], p < 0.001 were significant predictors. 2 The third model with the two acculturation reasons for drinking led to a significant improvement in fit, χ 2(8, N = 311) = 69.98, p < 0.001, Δχ 2(2, N = 311) = 35.3, p < 0.001, Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.269. The significant predictors were: generation, with those born in Australia having decreased odds of hazardous drinking compared to those born outside of Australia (OR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.29, 0.89], p = 0.018); drinking to fit in, (OR = 1.43, 95% CI [1.1, 1.8], p = 0.013) and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations (OR = 1.55, 95% CI [1.15, 2.09], p = 0.004). These indicate that each unit increase in drinking to fit in was associated with 1.43 times the odds of being classified as a hazardous drinking so stronger endorsement was associated with higher odds of hazardous drinking which represent a small to medium effect size. The same association was found for drinking to cope with parental/community expectations whereby this was associated with 1.55 times the odds of hazardous drinking which represent a small to medium effect size. These results suggest that both acculturative drinking reasons are significant predictors of classification in the hazardous drinking group. In the final/fourth Block (see Table 3), the four general drinking motives were added. This led to a significant improvement χ 2(12, N = 311) = 130.21, p < 0.001, Δχ 2(4, N = 311) = 60.23, p < 0.001, Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.456. Coping motives (OR = 2.28, 95% CI [1.57, 3.32], p < 0.001) and enhancement motives (OR = 2.22, 95% CI [1.50, 3.29], p < 0.001) were significant predictors, with each unit increase in coping and enhancement motives associated with 2.28 and 2.22 times the odds of being classified as a hazardous drinker respectively. Drinking to fit in (OR = 1.47, 95% CI [1.04, 2.08], p = 0.028) also remained a significant predictor as did drinking environment (OR = 0.44, 95% CI [0.23, 0.83], p = 0.011), with individuals from a similar drinking context to Australia having 2.27 times the odds of hazardous drinking compared to those from dissimilar environments. This final model demonstrated good discriminant ability (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.845). At the optimal classification threshold (cut‐off = 0.50), the model achieved 76.8% sensitivity and 74.4% specificity, correctly classifying approximately three‐quarters of both the non‐hazardous and hazardous drinkers.

Discussion

We investigated predictors of hazardous drinking in Australian migrant youth. We considered the role of acculturation stress in the form of acculturation gap conflict and perceived Australian drinking culture (socialisation) in alcohol use. We proposed that there may be reasons for drinking that are specific to migrant background youth, that is, acculturation reasons such as drinking to fit in with Australian culture and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations. Our final model demonstrated that coping and enhancement general drinking motives, as well as the constructed acculturation reason drinking to fit in, were associated with higher odds of hazardous drinking.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to quantitatively measure acculturation reasons for drinking. We found that drinking to fit into Australia (‘drinking to fit in’) and drinking to cope with parental/community expectations were related but distinct dimensions from general drinking motives, including coping and conformity motives. This suggests that it is important to consider specific acculturation reasons for drinking for migrant background individuals. Future research should consider acculturation reasons that are specific to the population under study and specific to the context of study. Indeed, in Australia where drinking is typically viewed as normative, it is telling that, whilst coping and enhancement were the significant general drinking motives as identified in the general literature on alcohol use [45], we identified that for youth of migrant background, drinking to fit in Australian culture is also a significant influence on hazardous alcohol use. This highlights that over and above the general drinking motives, migrant youth may have additional drinking influences because of their adaptation to their new home.

Interestingly, once the general drinking motives were considered, acculturative gap conflict was no longer associated with higher odds of hazardous drinking. As Lui et al. [28] argued, it is important to assess the proximal antecedents of alcohol use and our study shows that drinking to fit in, a proximal acculturative drinking reason, was a significant predictor. It may well be that drinking to fit in is significant because it relates to the drinking culture in Australia and other contexts of reception might not have the same drinking norms and therefore different acculturation reasons for drinking. However, we tentatively suggest that drinking to fit in might also be significant for migrant background youth in contexts of reception with a similar drinking culture to Australia.

Despite the novelty of our study, there are some limitations. First, our participants came from a wide array of cultural backgrounds which made it challenging to analyse the data by ethnic group. We created a dichotomous classification of drinking environment background to differentiate between those who came from environments where alcohol consumption was similar or approximate to the Australian mainstream society and those who came from environments with dissimilar alcohol use to Australia. We are mindful that this classification masks heterogeneity that likely exists within countries and within regions. Second, the sample was mostly second‐born or later generation individuals. Past research has shown that with time and generations further from immigration, migrant background individuals drinking patterns come to mirror that of the mainstream (e.g., [2]). While the significance of drinking to fit in within a second and later generation sample is supportive of the role of acculturation reasons for hazardous alcohol use, future studies could employ the acculturation reasons for drinking of the current study to first‐generation migrant youth in one specific ethnic group to further investigate the role of acculturation reasons for drinking.

The current study applied the latest recommendations on acculturation and drinking by investigating the role of acculturation drinking reasons in migrant youth alcohol use. It seems that for migrant youth who drink at hazardous levels, this is associated with drinking to cope and to enhance positive feelings but also to fit into Australian society. Drinking at high levels could be viewed as normative in Australia and one way via which migrant youth can feel they belong.
