Bye-bye, Bluebook? Automating Legal Procedure with Large Language Models
Matthew Dahl

Abstract
Legal practice requires careful adherence to procedural rules. In the United States, few are more complex than those found in The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation. Compliance with this system’s 500+ pages of byzantine formatting instructions is the raison d’être of thousands of student law review editors and the bête noire of lawyers everywhere. To evaluate whether large language models (LLMs) are able to adhere to the procedures of such a complicated system, we construct an original dataset of 866 Bluebook tasks and test flagship LLMs from OpenAI , Anthropic, Google, Meta, and DeepSeek. We show (1) that these models produce fully compliant Bluebook citations only 69%-74% of the time and (2) that in-context learning on the Bluebook’s underlying system of rules raises accuracy only to 77%. These results caution against using off-the-shelf LLMs to automate aspects of the law where fidelity to procedure is paramount. 1 1 1 Work in progress. This version: August 8, 2026.



1
Introduction

Can new forms of artificial intelligence (AI) automate legal procedure? Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) promise to radically reshape the practice of law—from speeding up legal research, to reviewing discovery productions, to even drafting contracts, judicial filings, and other legal documents. Yet, before lawyers can rely on AI to perform these ambitious
substantive
tasks, they will need assurance that AI can consistently comply with the complex
procedural
rules that structure them.

In law, adherence to procedure is critical. Even minor deviations from intricate timing, notice, and filing requirements can automatically disqualify claims that would otherwise be meritorious. For example, in
Bowles v. Russell
, 551 U.S. 205 (
Bow 2007
), the U.S. Supreme Court held that a habeas petition that was filed just two days late—in conformity with a lower court order but in violation of the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure—was untimely and could not be heard. The consequences of this technicality were devastating: because the defendant’s claim of racial imbalance in the jury was time-barred from federal reconsideration, he was eventually sentenced to fifteen years in prison.

(

(

In this paper, we begin to fill this gap by evaluating LLMs’ abilities to adhere to the requirements of one notoriously complicated source of American legal procedure—The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation

Law is a discipline predicated on citation to authority
(
Raz 1979
;
Schauer 2008
)
, and this 500+ page text serves as the definitive manual for how such citations are supposed to be structured. Its rules cover almost everything from the proper abbreviation of party names in case titles, to the precise use of interpretive “signals,” to a host of idiosyncrasies regarding almost any possible secondary source. Indeed, its sheer length and complexity has long bedeviled the thousands of law students, paralegals, and clerks that are tasked with ensuring compliance with its myriad instructions and exceptions.

The same features of the Bluebook that make it challenging for humans, however, also make it an ideal benchmark for testing LLMs. Doing so is worthwhile for two reasons. First, if LLMs could automate compliance with the Bluebook, they could finally liberate legal practice from a form of drudgery that has generated substantial criticism over the years
(
Posner 1986
;
Posner 2011
;
Posner 2016
)
, freeing up lawyers to dedicate resources to more involved forms of legal reasoning. Second, compliance with the Bluebook is a useful—albeit narrow—first approximation of LLMs’ abilities to comply with other forms of legal procedure as well, many of which are comparable in length and have not yet been empirically evaluated (Table
1
).

This paper makes three main contributions:

1.

Bluebook task dataset.
We aggregate an original dataset of 866 challenging Bluebook formatting tasks, complete with ground-truth answers provided by experts.

2.

Legal procedure abilities.
We present new evidence quantifying LLMs’ legal procedure-following abilities, isolated from their substantive legal reasoning abilities.

3.

Long-context challenges.
We contribute to a growing literature about LLMs’ true long-context learning abilities, which may be more limited than advertised.

Legal systems comprise both a substantive and a procedural dimension
(
Risinger 1982
)
. Although the line between the two is often blurry
(
Stuntz 1995
;
Main 2010
)
, substantive law is the law of crimes, torts, contracts, property, and so on—the concrete rights and remedies available to claimants. By contrast, procedural law is often said to be
trans
-substantive
(
Cover 1975
)
—procedure is the law that applies to all legal proceedings as such, irrespective of their underlying causes of action.

What is this law for? On one classic account, procedure primarily serves an information-forcing function
(
Posner 1973
;
Kaplow 1994
)
. Requiring litigants to disclose key facts early in a case, for example, promotes meaningful settlement negotiations and avoids long discovery battles, thereby saving the time and resources of both parties and the courts
(
Boyd and Hoffman 2013
;
Cooter and Rubinfeld 1989
;
Priest 1989
)
. Indeed, the entire structure of pleadings, discovery, and motion practice might be seen as a transaction cost reduction device—one that compels the timely and accurate exchange of information to promote the fair and efficient resolution of disputes.

2.2
Legal citation as legal procedure

With this background in mind, legal citation can be understood as one form of legal procedure (cf. Table
1
). When courts, attorneys, and scholars cite legal authority, they engage in a highly codified practice that structures how arguments are exchanged, evaluated, and verified—a quintessentially procedural function.

A failure to comply with citation rules can lead to confusion and even judicial sanction
(
Fischer 1997
)
. As the Seventh Circuit has remarked, “[j]udges are not like pigs, hunting for truffles …” Courts will “strike any of the parties’ factual assertions, in any section of their briefs, that lack direct citation to easily identifiable support in the record.”
Gross v. Town of Cicero, Ill.
, 619 F.3d 697, 702 (7th Cir.
Gro 2010
) (cleaned up). Likewise, in another case, where “[d]efendants’ failure to provide a full date of publication prevented the Court from finding the cited authority in Westlaw,” the court “ADMONISHED [counsel] to provide pinpoint citations in future filings,” stating that “[i]n future rulings, the Court will DISREGARD citations to caselaw that are not supported by pinpoint citations.”
SASC, LLC v. Sch. Supply Connection, Inc.
, No. 3:23-CV-083, 2024 WL 3849424, at *8 (S.D. Ohio Aug. 15,
SAS 2024
).

The advent of LLMs has introduced a new dimension to this problem: citations to sources that do not even exist in the first place
(
Dahl et al. 2024
;
Weiser 2023
)
. As one court has explained, “many harms flow from such deception—including wasting the opposing party’s time and money, the Court’s time and resources, and reputational harms to the legal system (to name a few).”
Morgan v. Cmty. Against Violence
, No. 23-CV-353-WPJ/JMR, 2023 WL 6976510, at *8 (D.N.M. Oct. 23,
Mor 2023
). “Courts therefore do not, and should not, make allowances for a party who cites to fake, nonexistent, misleading authorities.”
Kohls v. Ellison
, No. 24-CV-3754 (LMP/DLM), 2025 WL 66514, at *5 (D. Minn. Jan. 10,
Koh 2025
) (cleaned up).

Judicial comments like these reinforce the idea that legal citation, like legal procedure in general, is essentially a set of rules designed to optimize the exchange of information in the legal system. More specifically, legal citation exhibits the following procedural properties:

•

Universality.
Like other procedural rules, citation requirements are trans-substantive, applying regardless of whether a litigant is advancing a constitutional claim, a contract dispute, a personal injury action, or any other kind of legal argument.

•

Intricacy.
Like other procedural rules, citation requirements can be surprisingly and frustratingly convoluted, requiring synthesis of multiple provisions from different sections of long, detailed documents containing the rules and their exceptions.

•

Inflexibility.
Like other procedural rules, citation requirements demand strict adherence to their numerous specifications, permitting little deviation from the rules as written.

To be sure, citation is only one narrow instance of legal procedure, and each of the procedural systems listed in Table
1
has its own distinctive elements that will require separate evaluation. Still, legal citation offers an important starting point for legal procedure research. If LLMs can faithfully implement universal, intricate, and inflexible rules in this context, they may be able to do so in others as well.

2.3
The Bluebook

Today, the rules of American legal citation are organized in The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation
(
Columbia Law Review et al. 2020
)
. Known as simply the Bluebook for short, this 500+ page manual dates back to 1920 and describes, in great detail, the precise way in which almost every conceivable document is to be cited in official legal writing
(
Shapiro and Krishnaswami 2016
)
. For example, a full case law citation must contain any signal glossing the nature of the citation, the names of the parties, the page and volume of the reporter publishing the opinion, any pincite page, the court and year of the decision, and any subsequent history noting if the case was later vacated or abrogated (Figure
2
). Abstruse styling rules for italicization, underlining, and the use of small caps must be respected as well. For instance, when the signal “
e.g.
” is used in conjunction with another signal, it must be preceded by an italicized comma but must be followed—for whatever reason—by an
unitalicized
comma. Even more complicated rules govern the citation of statutes, legislative history, dictionaries, court filings, and numerous other categories of authority.

Over the years, the extraordinary complexity of the Bluebook has garnered much criticism. Judge Richard Posner, in particular, has written a series of diatribes against the system
(
Posner 1986
;
Posner 2011
;
Posner 2016
)
. He describes it as “560 pages of rubbish, a terrible time waster for law clerks” and recommends that readers “burn all copies”
(
Posner 2016
)
. Others argue that “Bluebook skills simply signal membership in an elite club”
(
Salmon 2016
)
; that, “[a]s a semiotic code, The Bluebook effectively hides ideological roots and assumptions behind a mask of necessity and naturalness”
(
Bacchus 2002
)
; and that “[t]he Bluebook hammers law students with the notion that the law is simply an intricate set of rules that, although tedious to learn, contains determinate answers decipherable by anyone willing to spend sufficient time staring at all of the possibly relevant rules”
(
Heifetz 1999
)
. At the same time, still others have spoken out in favor of the Bluebook, arguing that it serves a useful pedagogical and information-retrieval purpose
(
Asbury and Cole 2011
;
Shawler 1992
;
Whisner 2008
)
.

In this paper, we are agnostic on this debate. For better or worse, the Bluebook exists and is followed religiously by large numbers of law students, clerks, judges, and lawyers. Indeed, “[f]or over seventy years, law students have begun their journeys into the legal profession with The Bluebook in hand”
(
Heifetz 1999
)
. Given that this is the reality, we take compliance with the Bluebook as a natural benchmark for gauging whether AI systems can truly master the kind of hyper-detailed procedures that structure everyday legal practice.

3
Related literature

Since the introduction of ChatGPT in late 2022, there has been an explosion of work exploring AI’s ability to perform substantive legal tasks. Attention to its fluency in legal procedure, however, has been lacking.

One line of research has focused on LLMs’ internal knowledge of the law, which is generally poor and unevenly distributed
(
Dahl et al. 2024
)
. Even when equipped with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) techniques, LLM systems struggle to accurately answer questions about the law and frequently hallucinate
Hou et al. 2024
;
Doyle and Tucker 2025
;
Magesh et al. 2025
;
Ouellette et al. 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2025
. Tailoring smaller models to legal data via self-supervised pre-training
(
Chalkidis et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2021
)
or fine-tuning
(
Colombo et al. 2024
;
Dominguez-Olmedo et al. 2025
;
Niklaus et al. 2025
)
can substantially improve performance, but not always to conventionally acceptable levels of accuracy. Serious biases
(
Salinas et al. 2025
;
Schwartz and Wang 2024
)
and deviations from human behavior
(
Engel and McAdams 2024
;
Posner and Saran 2025
)
remain.

LLMs are more useful when they are combined with human oversight and guidance. For example, recent experimental research has shown that both law students
(
Choi and Schwarcz 2024
;
Choi et al. 2024
;
Nielsen et al. 2024
;
Schwarcz et al. 2025
)
and actual legal professionals
(
Chien and Kim 2025
)
perform better on legal tasks when given access to flagship, general-purpose LLMs like GPT-4. Additionally, there are ethical
(
Aidid 2024
;
Cyphert 2021
;
Yamane 2020
)
and practical
(
Markovic 2019
;
Mik 2024
)
considerations that counsel in favor of using LLMs only to augment, and not to replace, human lawyers.

Little of this existing work, however, examines LLMs’ abilities to perform
procedural
legal tasks. Benchmarking suites like LegalBench
(
Guha et al. 2024
;
Pipitone and Alami 2024
)
and LawBench
(
Fei et al. 2024
)
contain a handful of rule-application evaluations, but they do not test LLMs’ ability to follow procedural law as such, which is composed of rules that are orders of magnitude longer than those assessed in those benchmarks (cf. Table
1
). Yet, because compliance with procedural law is essentially an exercise in step-by-step rule-following, it could be a prime application for LLMs, given their amenability to instruction-tuning
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Peng et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, while accuracy on such tasks is just as important as it is in substantive legal drafting, analysis, and question-answering assignments, there may be fewer normative objections to automating aspects of legal practice that are already largely mechanical.

The research that comes closest to the present study is
Bongard et al. 2022
, which develops a multiple-choice question-answering dataset based on the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure; the authors describe the task as “far from solved”
(
Held and Habernal 2024
)
. Also related is research examining LLMs’ capacity for basic legal text handling, such as quoting from documents, returning lines of deposition transcripts, or finding sections of the U.S. Code; the authors find that off-the-shelf LLMs behave like “sloppy paralegals” and require fine-tuning
(
Blair-Stanek et al. 2024
)
.

Finally, while some previous work has attempted to systematize the parsing and formatting of legal citations
(
Cushman et al. 2021
)
, these efforts are not model-based and are limited by manually curated lists of regular expressions. No technical solution for complete, automatic Bluebook compliance currently exists; accordingly, if LLMs could reliably reproduce the patterns called for in the Bluebook, they could replace a large amount of human labor currently dedicated to completing this task.

4
Data

We devise 16 distinct Bluebook tasks, composed of a total of 866 queries in a mix of cloze-style and open-ended formats. All focus on the Bluebook style prescribed in the “Bluepages,” i.e., the portion of the Bluebook dealing with rules for practitioners.

For exposition, we group the tasks into three categories:

1.

Case law tasks.
Tasks related to the appropriate abbreviation of party names; reporter abbreviations; court abbreviations and opinion dates; subsequent case history; case short forms; and parentheticals.

2.

Enacted law tasks.
Tasks related to constitutional provisions; federal statutes (both annotated and un-annotated); state statutes (both printed and electronic); statute short forms; and administrative law.

3.

Other tasks.
Tasks related to other miscellaneous legal sources, including legislative resources (e.g., floor statements, committee reports, resolutions); court filings (e.g., depositions, appellate records, exhibits); secondary sources (e.g., law review articles, books, dictionaries); and signals.

Each case law task consists of information from a case caption—as might be retrieved from an opinion found in a legal research database like Westlaw or Lexis—and instructions about what kind of citation to construct. Where possible, we structure the queries in a cloze format, providing the model with a Bluebook-compliant citation and masking only the portion that is specifically being tested (Figure
3
). Because case law citations are composed of multiple parts, each requiring adherence to different rules (Figure
2
), a cloze structure allows separate evaluation of each.

The prompt structure of the enacted law tasks and other tasks is similar; however, the more integrated nature of these citations makes cloze formatting more challenging for them, so in these tasks we simply prompt the model to provide the complete citation altogether.

The underlying content and ground-truth answers for each task are sourced from two legal writing resources: the Interactive Citation Workbook for The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation
(
Norton 2024
)
and Understanding and Mastering The Bluebook: A Guide for Students and Practitioners
(
Barris 2020
)
. Both contain expert-verified answers to Bluebook exercises that were originally designed for law students, which we substantially transform for the purposes of this paper.

LLMs cannot fully implement the Bluebook in a zero-shot setting

6.2
LLMs’ case law performance is partly driven by memorization

LLMs are best at producing Bluebook-compliant citations for case law. They are especially strong at generating volume, reporter, and page combinations (pooled average = 96%) and court and date abbreviations (pooled average = 90%). Importantly, these are the most critical components of a case law citation: with this core information, a reader should be able to deterministically locate the case in a database or library (cf. Figure
2
). The models are also very strong at generating the proper parenthetical indicating whether a citation is to a plurality, per curiam, en banc, dissenting, or concurring opinion (pooled average = 98%).

However, because these case law tasks are structured as cloze queries, it could be that the LLMs are able to succeed at them by simply regurgitating memorized parts of citations instead of actually applying the rules of the Bluebook. Test leakage in LLM evaluations is a growing concern
(
Li and Flanigan 2024
)
, and, while the tasks developed in this paper are synthesized from sources unlikely to be in the models’ training corpora, LLMs are known to have internalized the citations of many common cases
(
Dahl et al. 2024
)
. To explore whether this is driving their Bluebook performance, we restructure the case reporter and court and date tasks into an open format, asking the LLM to generate the entire citation from a given caption with no portions already completed. We then create two versions of each prompt: one containing the real caption information and one containing the same information but with a random page number.
7
7

7

By randomizing the page only, we ensure that the given caption remains otherwise valid. E.g., the volume of the given reporter still corresponds to the given year, and so forth.

Figure
5
depicts the results of this supplementary analysis. Troublingly, most of the LLMs are better at creating citations to the real cases than to the synthetic ones, suggesting that their strong performance on these tasks is driven by simple memorization of the real cases’ citations. Only Gemini 2.5 Flash and DeepSeek V3 0324 do not show evidence of this behavior. The gap is greatest in Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the model that performs the best overall. Thus, while it is encouraging that LLMs seem to know enough about certain real cases to insist on producing the correct citation for them, these gaps call into question LLMs’ abilities to handle the formatting of cases whose citations they have never seen before.

Finally, examining the remainder of the case law tasks, the LLMs’ otherwise strong performance begins to decline on tasks relating to the abbreviation of party names (pooled average = 78%), inclusion of subsequent appellate history (pooled average = 78%), short form citations (pooled average = 53%), and parallel reporters (pooled average = 42%). We discuss the implications of these inaccuracies in more detail in Section
6.5
below.

6.3
LLMs struggle with statutes, regulations, and secondary sources

Moving beyond case law, LLMs perform significantly worse on the tasks in the enacted law and other categories (Figure
4
). These tasks include citations to other primary sources like print federal (pooled average = 57%) and state (pooled average = 36%) statutes, administrative regulations (pooled average = 48%), legislative resources (pooled average = 65%), and court filings (pooled average = 37%), as well as citations to secondary sources like books, journal articles, newspapers, and dictionaries (pooled average = 29%).

Citations to these kinds of authority are extremely common in legal writing, yet the LLMs show much less familiarity with them than the case law citations. This is likely because these citation formats are considerably more varied—different states all have subtly different structures for their state statutes, some of which are published by government offices and some of which are published by private vendors. Even federal law can appear in multiple formats, such as the official United States Code (U.S.C.), the United States Code Service (U.S.C.S.), or the United States Code Annotated (U.S.C.A.). Regulations, too, can be located in various official and unofficial compendiums—for instance, the Code of Federal Regulations (C.F.R.) at the federal level or analogous state-based collections such as the California Code of Regulations. Under the Bluebook, each of these materials triggers its own carefully tailored citation format.

By far, however, the models struggle the most with crafting citations to
electronic
versions of statutes (pooled average = 10%). This format is particularly arcane, requiring many abbreviations referring to the date and legislative session through which the electronic source is current.

Rounding out the zero-shot results, the LLMs’ difficulty with the signals task is also noteworthy (pooled average = 34%). A signal is a special term, appended to the beginning of a citation, that is used to indicate the degree of support that the citation is intended to offer the stated proposition (Figure
2
). For example, if the cited authority directly supports the proposition, no signal should be used, but if an inferential leap is required between the cited authority and the proposition, a “
see
” signal should be used.

Signals therefore involve an element of legal reasoning that distinguishes them from the other tasks in this paper. The Bluebook contains guidelines for using each signal, but even humans do not always agree about which situations call for which signal
(
Robbins 1999
)
. Accordingly, the LLMs’ difficulty with this task reinforces research suggesting that LLMs are limited when clear-cut answers do not exist, as in the case in many legal applications
(
Balagopalan et al. 2023
;
Thalken et al. 2023
)
.

6.5
LLMs’ Bluebook errors are non-trivial

We now discuss the nature of the LLMs’ Bluebook mistakes in more depth, both quantitatively and qualitatively. When the LLMs do make errors, how serious are those errors?

Figure
6
gives a numerical answer to this question, counting the number of Levenshtein edit operations (insertion, deletion, substitution) that are required to perfectly conform the LLMs’ incorrect citations to the requirements of the Bluebook. These metrics suggest that when the LLMs are generating non-compliant citations, they are not merely forgetting a comma or a hyphen—around 14 characters are out of place in each incorrect citation. Notably, however, Claude 3.5 Sonnet performs the best on this evaluation—meaning that even when it is wrong, it is less wrong than the other models.

Table
3
adds more texture to these results by reporting a qualitative selection of some of the LLMs’ specific failures across the different tasks. These examples are chosen to highlight the kind of substantive mistakes that LLMs can sometimes make. As the table shows, misstating the parties, the deciding court, the location in the case reporter, the subsequent history, the publisher, the identity of the author, or a citation’s signal of support are all non-trivial errors that could have serious consequences for readers in need of accurate reference information.

To be clear, however, semantic errors of this magnitude are relatively rare. For the vast majority of LLM-generated citations evaluated in this paper, a reader would be able to understand and locate the referenced source, even when the citation is technically incorrect. But comprehension is not the standard being benchmarked in this paper. The Bluebook is not designed to produce citations that are merely intelligible; this could be achieved with a much shorter citation manual
(
Posner 1986
;
Posner 2011
)
. Rather, the outrageous complexity of the Bluebook reflects the legal profession’s obsession with detail as such. Accordingly, any failure to meet the Bluebook’s exacting standards—whether through a minor textual error or a more substantive misstatement—is informative about a LLM’s procedure-following abilities.

6.6
In-context learning on the Bluebook rules offers only moderate gains

Finally, we investigate the ability of LLMs to perform in-context learning on the text of the Bluebook rules themselves. The models studied in this paper all offer context windows of at least 128k tokens, enabling them to ingest lengthy documents and ground responses in their content. In theory, this feature could be ideal for legal applications: if LLMs could simply be given the text of relevant procedural rules, their responses could directly drawn upon them for task completion. However, whether LLMs can truly utilize the full length of their context windows remains a topic of ongoing debate
(
An et al. 2024
;
Hsieh et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Bertsch et al. 2025
)
, and long-context learning has not yet been deeply studied in the legal setting (but see
Lee and Lee 2025
). Therefore, we use the rules of the Bluebook---which run 90k tokens,

)—as an initial test of this potential approach.

Although in-context learning on the Bluebook rules does improve performance on certain tasks—especially on the statutes, secondary sources, and signals tasks—its effect on overall performance is minor. Average accuracy increases only six percentage points, from 71% in the zero-shot setup to 77% in the in-context learning setup.

These results call into question the efficacy of LLMs’ stated context window lengths for legal tasks. If legal rules are sufficiently complex—as the Bluebook rules certainly are—in-context learning alone may not be enough to ensure that LLMs adhere to them. Ultimately, this limitation highlights a broader challenge in deploying LLMs for legal tasks: their difficulty in faithfully executing complicated rule-based processes, even when all necessary information is directly available to them.

7
Limitations

While this study provides important and original evidence about LLMs’ procedure-following abilities, it comes with certain limitations.

First
, this evaluation necessarily only captures the abilities of LLMs at the time of writing. Improvements—and regressions
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
—on this paper’s set of Bluebook tasks are likely to occur as new model versions are released. All experiments in this paper were run in April 2025 at temperature zero.

Second
, while there are reasons to believe that legal citation is a form of legal procedure (Section
2
), the results presented in this paper about LLMs’ Bluebook abilities are only a first approximation of their general facility for other procedural tasks.

Third
, this paper focuses only on the citation style mandated by the Bluebook for legal practitioners (the “Bluepages” of the Bluebook) rather than the style required for legal academics (the “Whitepages” of the Bluebook). While they are often similar, the latter entails certain conventions—like the use of small caps—that we do not investigate in this paper.

Fourth
, this paper does not attempt to evaluate LLMs’ abilities to perform “source checking,” another labor-intensive task often undertaken by law students, clerks, and paralegals. In legal practice, verifying a citation’s referenced material for its existence and content is of course crucial
(
Dahl et al. 2024
)
, but this paper does not delve into this complicated retrieval task and only examines the technical formatting of Bluebook citations themselves.

Fifth
, for the in-context learning evaluation, for copyright reasons this paper is forced to rely on the citation rules provided in the Indigo Book rather than the Bluebook itself. While the authors of the Indigo Book represent that “anyone using The Indigo Book will produce briefs, memoranda, law review articles, and other legal documents with citations that are compatible with the [Bluebook]”
(
Sprigman and Romig 2021
)
, the two texts may not be perfectly aligned in all instances.

Last
, this paper focuses only on zero-shot question-answering and in-context learning. Although techniques like few-shot prompting and fine-tuning would surely produce performance gains
(
Blair-Stanek et al. 2024
;
Dominguez-Olmedo et al. 2025
)
, a huge number of examples—greater than the test data developed in this paper—would be required to fully capture all of the Bluebook’s interlocking instructions and exceptions. Additionally, this paper is primarily concerned with studying LLMs’ abilities to
follow
complicated procedural rules, rather than to
memorize
patterns from a curated example set. While few-shot prompting or fine-tuning might improve citation accuracy in specific instances, such approaches would not necessarily show that LLMs can independently parse and apply a complicated procedural document like the Bluebook to an out-of-sample question. We leave efforts to build a bespoke Bluebook formatting model to future work.

8
Conclusion

With the wide proliferation of LLMs, is it time for lawyers to finally say “bye-bye” to the Bluebook
(
Posner 1986
;
Lysaght and Tonner 2000
)
? For years, “[i]n light of recent and rapidly evolving developments in technology,” observers have been heralding the “imminent obsolescence” of human implementation of this citation manual
(
Salmon 2016
)
. However, the findings of this paper suggest that such predictions remain premature. Despite the impressive capabilities of LLMs in certain legal applications, they continue to struggle with the complex and highly structured procedural rules that govern legal citation.

These results stand in some dissonance with the prevailing mood in the legal profession. On the one hand, a culture of AI hype has taken over the legal technology industry
(
Mignanelli 2024
)
, with some envisioning a future of robot lawyers and AI-assisted judges, as is already being seen in China
(
Liu and Li 2024
)
. On the other hand, skepticism persists among academics
(
Markovic 2019
;
Kapoor et al. 2024
)
, and for good reason. If LLMs cannot yet even conquer the Bluebook, it remains dubious that they can be safely and responsibly integrated into higher-stakes legal settings.

Of course, LLMs can still be very useful for certain aspects of legal practice, and researchers should continue to map their jagged frontier
(
Dell’Acqua et al. 2023
)
. To that end, further research into the
procedural
abilities of LLMs is warranted. Existing scholarship has generally overlooked this more quotidian dimension of legal practice in its enthusiasm for applying LLMs to
substantive
tasks instead. Studying those applications is important, but before generative AI can be entrusted with mission-critical assignments—like sentencing, adjudication, or even legal drafting—it should first be able to demonstrate reliability in following the formal rules that constitute the structure of the legal system in the first place. Future research should continue to evaluate LLMs’ performance along this dimension, moving beyond Bluebook tasks into other forms of procedure that underpin legal practice, such as adhering to filing deadlines, following jurisdiction-specific rules, and producing judicially acceptable documents and forms.
