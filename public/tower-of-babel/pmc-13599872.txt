AI-based applications supporting informal caregivers in childhood caries prevention and management: protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Pani Matin Anugrah, Elham Emami, Melissa Adiatman, Risqa Rina Rina Darwita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDental caries remains highly prevalent among children despite being preventable. Limited access to dental services, workforce shortages and low oral health literacy among informal caregivers contribute to its delayed detection and poor oral health outcomes for children. As informal caregivers play a vital role in children’s daily oral health practices, innovative and scalable approaches are needed to support them. Artificial intelligence (AI)-based mobile health applications could potentially help engage these caregivers in education, early detection, and other preventive activities. However, the evidence is still limited. The objective of this review is to map and characterise the existing evidence on caregiver-centred AI-based applications supporting the management and prevention of childhood dental caries.Methods and analysisThe scoping review will adhere to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology guideline, using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) for reporting structure and PRISMA-S (PRISMA Search Reporting Extension) for a comprehensive search strategy. The search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an expert librarian. An initial search will be conducted in Embase (Ovid) and adapted for other databases (MEDLINE (Ovid), PubMed and Scopus). Two reviewers will independently screen the titles, abstracts and full-text articles, with a third resolving any conflicts. The included studies will be selected according to the participants, concept and context (PCC) framework, in any setting and any country, that addresses AI-based applications facilitated by informal caregivers for caries prevention and management in children. Articles published in English and primary studies will be included, with no restrictions on publication date. Data will be charted and synthesised using descriptive statistics and qualitative thematic mapping.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this review. These findings will be disseminated in a peer-reviewed publication and at academic conferences.Trial registration details(https://osf.io/7akw5).

Introduction

One of the most common oral health problems in children is dental caries.1 2 Although dental caries is preventable, its prevalence remains high. Dental caries has affected nearly 50% of children under the age of 12 worldwide.3 Untreated caries can lead to a higher risk of systemic diseases and lower quality of life.4 Risk factors for caries in children are multiple, including high sugar dietary intake, poor oral hygiene, lack of parental knowledge about the importance of caries control,5 6 as well as the shortage of dentists to address the prevention and management needs of caries in children in both high-income countries (HIC) and low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). Globally, there are 2 542 593 dental practitioners, with an average of 3.3 dental practitioners per 10 000 population.7 8 In fact, more than 68% of the WHO Member States have fewer than five dental practitioners, and 37% of the nations have fewer than one dental practitioner per 10 000 population.7 8 This shortage is exacerbated by the uneven distribution of dentists in rural and remote areas. A significant proportion of the population resides in remote locations, facing financial, transportation and time constraints that hinder access to preventive and curative services.9 Such barriers often lead to delayed early detection in oral health issues in children, resulting in more complex and extensive curative treatments.

In this resource-constrained situation, community empowerment emerges as a pivotal strategic priority, particularly through informal caregivers such as parents (particularly mothers), grandparents, siblings or guardians. They serve as the main actors, providing unpaid care or assistance in children’s oral healthcare, including instruction on oral hygiene, healthy diet control and identifying the need for dental visits and treatment. However, the capacity of informal caregivers in children’s caries control is limited. The most important barriers are oral health illiteracy, as well as limited knowledge and attitudes.10 According to the literature, there is a significant correlation between parental knowledge, behaviour and lifestyle and children’s oral health.11 Hammouri et al reported that a high prevalence of dental plaque (89.95) among children whose parents (83.3%) had infrequent toothbrushing practices.12 These gaps highlight the need for behaviour change frameworks, such as Protection Motivation Theory and Behaviour Change Wheel, to better understand informal caregivers’ perceptions and practices in childhood caries prevention and management.

Conventional educational interventions toward oral health literacy are often unsustainable and difficult to implement, especially in rural and remote areas, necessitating innovative and practical approaches such as artificial intelligence (AI).13 AI is defined as systems that include automated decision-making components (eg, machine learning, natural language processing (NLP), computer vision), distinguishing them from conventional digital health tools. In dentistry, AI-based applications have shown potential for oral health education, early detection, risk factor prediction, oral disease diagnosis, treatment planning and patient management.14 Additionally, AI, when integrated with teledentistry, exhibits high scalability, enabling access without restrictions of time and location, aligning with the need to support dental health programmes in areas with limited access.15 The integration of AI into mobile oral health (m-OralHealth) applications allows informal caregivers to obtain accurate, personalised and easily accessible information at their convenience in maintaining children’s oral health.

Despite an increasing number of reviews on AI in the clinical diagnostic aspects of paediatric dentistry for clinicians or formal healthcare providers, existing reviews have not comprehensively examined the use of AI by informal caregivers as primary agents in the prevention of childhood dental caries.16 17 Furthermore, a preliminary search of Open Science Framework, JBI Evidence Synthesis, PubMed and Scopus was conducted in February 2026, which did not identify any current or ongoing scoping or systematic review on this topic. In addition, evidence about how AI applications support knowledge and behavioural changes in informal caregivers is still dispersed and has not been systematically mapped.

Objectives

This scoping review aims to systematically map the literature and synthesise how informal caregivers can better prevent and manage childhood dental caries using AI-based applications. More specifically, the objectives are to examine the types of AI-based interventions used by informal caregivers, explore their relationship to behaviour change theory, identify barriers and facilitators and highlight the research gaps.

Methods

Protocol registration

This review will be guided by the JBI methodology for scoping review.18 The structure of this scoping review is informed by The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA ScR) checklist.19 In addition, this review complies with the PRISMA-S (PRISMA Search Reporting Extension) checklist for a comprehensive search strategy.20 This scoping review protocol has been registered in Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7akw5)

Eligibility criteria

Participants

This scoping review will involve, as participants, informal caregivers of children aged 6 months to 12 years who have an unpaid responsibility and play a vital, non-professional role in the prevention, diagnosis, screening and management of dental caries in children. These caregivers include family members (parents, grandparents, siblings) and legal guardians.21 Participants can be from various socioeconomic backgrounds and residential areas, and there will be no restrictions on gender. On the other hand, this review will exclude formal caregivers, such as paid, medically trained professionals who provide care, education and support related to children’s oral health (dental hygienists, dental therapists, dentists and school health workers). Studies involving both informal caregivers and formal caregivers (ie, mixed participants) are acceptable and will be included if informal caregivers are part of the study population.

The selected age group for children is 6 months to 12 years, as primary teeth begin to erupt at 6 months and are at risk of dental caries.22 Untreated caries in primary teeth can have a detrimental impact on the development and health of the underlying permanent teeth.23 Meanwhile, 12 years is considered the transition phase from primary to permanent dentition due to the last exfoliation of primary teeth at this age, making permanent teeth more vulnerable to dental caries.22 24 This age is also regarded as an important age for international comparisons and global monitoring of disease trends, including dental caries, as recommended by the WHO.25 Furthermore, informal caregiver supervision in the prevention and management of dental caries in children, including toothbrushing supervision, is considered essential and is recommended until at least 8–12 years of age.26 Studies with mixed age groups (eg, ages <6 months or >12 years) will be included if data relevant to children aged 6 months to 12 years can be extracted separately.

Concept

The main concept of this study is the use of AI-based applications that facilitate informal caregivers in dental caries prevention and management for children aged 6 months to 12 years. AI-based applications are digital systems that use automated decision-making, machine learning, natural language processing or computer vision without direct clinician interpretation. These applications can take various forms, such as mobile applications, web platforms, chatbots, camera-based systems, gamification and other relevant AI systems.

Within the framework of dental caries prevention, AI-based applications can play an important role in supporting interventions that inhibit cavity formation and encourage early treatment of existing decay to prevent further damage.16 27 These supports can include providing oral health education, oral hygiene guidance, diet counselling, caries risk assessment and prediction, caries screening and recommendations for the use of preventive agents such as fluoride and calcium-phosphate compounds.28 Meanwhile, dental caries management refers to efforts aimed at reducing the burden of dental caries and maintaining or restoring the function of affected teeth. This approach can encompass various forms of therapeutic interventions, ranging from extensive restorations to tooth extraction to teeth with lesions involving the pulp or lesions that are non-restorable.

This review will exclude studies focusing on conventional interventions, such as didactic, in-person lectures using dental aids and printed materials, as well as the use of non-AI-based digital tools to ensure conceptual clarity and avoid dilution of AI-specific evidence, such as education websites, reminders via WhatsApp or SMS and manual teleconsultations that rely entirely on the response of human healthcare workers. Furthermore, studies that focus only on the technical development of AI from a computer science perspective, such as algorithm design, model architecture or code implementation, will also be excluded if they do not report the application or testing of the technology involving informal caregivers as users.

Context

This review will be conducted using studies across varying contexts, including home, school, clinic, laboratory or community. There will be no geographical restrictions (eg, studies from developed or developing countries).

Types of sources

This scoping review will include primary studies (quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods). Secondary sources such as systematic reviews and opinion papers will be used to support the background and discussion, but will not be included in the data extraction. Additionally, grey literature, such as theses and dissertations, news and conference proceedings, will be included. The language of the AI application will not be a restriction if the published article is in English, and there will be no limitation on the years of publication. The English-language restriction is applied due to feasibility and resource constraints.

Information sources and search strategy

An extensive search strategy will be developed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords, in collaboration with an expert librarian at McGill University. According to JBI guidelines, the search strategy will follow a three-phase approach. First, a preliminary search will be conducted in one appropriate database (Embase via Ovid) to identify relevant keywords from the titles, abstracts and index terms of the identified articles, thereby refining the comprehensive search strategy for the next full search across the other databases. This phase is also intended to ensure the sensitivity and accuracy of the search strategy. The preliminary search strategy is available in online supplementary 1. After that, a second search will be adapted to other databases, including CINAHL via EBSCOhost, Scopus and Medline via Ovid. Third, citation searching will be employed in Google Scholar and the Science Citation Index, with results limited to the first 20 entries sorted by relevance. This phase will involve inspecting the reference lists of included studies and identifying the articles that cite them. Grey literature will be searched in ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global for theses and dissertations. Official websites of universities and international organisations (WHO, World Dental Federation (FDI), and American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD)) will be reviewed for reports, policy documents and working papers. Finally, Web of Science will be used to identify conference proceedings. The article will be filtered for English-language, human, peer-reviewed articles and no year restrictions. Should further information be required, the corresponding author of the primary article or organisations will be contacted.

Data selection

Two independent reviewers (PMA and MA) will be involved in selecting the studies. This process consists of three distinct phases using Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) to manage citation screening and collaboration. First, duplicate studies among all databases will be identified. Second, the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles will be screened according to PCC, independently by each reviewer, after a pilot screening test of 20% random articles to ensure inter-reviewer reliability.29 Third, the reviewers will conduct a full-text screening of the included articles. Any discrepancies between reviewers in article selection will be mediated through discussion with the third reviewer (EE). The number of records identified, duplicates, included and excluded articles, along with the reason for exclusion, will be reported in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.30

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

This review will not conduct a critical appraisal of the selected studies’ methodological quality, as this aligns with scoping review guidelines.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (PMA and MA) will independently extract selected studies and record all data using a data extraction template in Microsoft Excel, following the JBI template source of evidence details, characteristics and results extraction instrument (online supplementary 2), on completing pilot testing using 10% of included studies. The data extraction will allocate data items into specific details, such as author, year of publication, country of origin, World Bank income classification (HIC/LMIC), purpose of study, participants and sample size, study design, implementation setting, details of AI-based oral health applications, behaviour change theory, role of informal caregivers, digital literacy requirement, outcomes measured, barriers and facilitators. If necessary, corresponding authors will be contacted up to two times to obtain missing or additional data. The details of data extraction will be updated as needed during the extraction process to accommodate the specific characteristics of each included study. Any disagreement will be resolved by discussion with the third reviewer (EE).

Collating, summarising and reporting the results

Data synthesis in this scoping review will employ descriptive statistics and a qualitative approach to map the frameworks of AI-based applications, informal caregiver roles, behaviour change theory, as well as barriers and facilitators, using a thematic analysis. The characteristics of AI-based applications will include the type and platform of AI used, as well as the intervention’s primary function. The informal caregiver’s empowerment component will be synthesised as a core part of this review. Consequently, the synthesis will examine not only the functional specification of AI for children’s caries but also the role of informal caregivers in managing this oral disease through this technology. Outcomes reported in the studies will be systematically categorised into several domains encompassing clinical, knowledge, behavioural and implementation outcomes for both children and informal caregivers. Furthermore, the narrative synthesis will highlight barriers and facilitators to the use of AI-based applications by informal caregivers. At the end, this scoping review will produce a research gap map based on the distribution of existing findings, which can serve as a basis for recommendations for further research and for the development of more effective, contextually relevant and sustainable AI-based applications to support caries prevention in children through informal caregiver empowerment. These findings will be presented on the evidence map and frequency table. The evidence maps will present the results of AI-based applications, informal caregiver roles and reported outcomes across included studies. At the same time, the frequency table will provide an overview of the characteristics of the included studies.

Discussion

This scoping review protocol outlines a comprehensive strategy to evaluate the use of AI-based applications to support informal caregivers in the prevention and management of childhood caries. Given the high prevalence of childhood dental caries worldwide and the limited access to preventive dental health education and services, AI-based applications have the potential to serve as a relevant approach to enhance health promotion, early detection and behaviour change related to oral health at the family level. Through the JBI methodology framework and the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, this review aims to systematically map the existing evidence on caregiver-centred AI-based applications supporting the management and prevention of childhood dental caries. It will describe study characteristics, including the need for informal caregivers’ digital literacy, the inclusion of behaviour change frameworks, the role of informal caregivers, AI details, reported outcomes and barriers and facilitators. By integrating these elements, the review will highlight the role of informal caregivers in using AI applications and accurately interpret the outcomes.

This review is also expected to benefit several stakeholder groups. For researchers, the review’s findings can serve as a foundation for developing AI interventions that are more relevant, inclusive and user-centred. For healthcare professionals and service providers, the study’s findings can help in identifying potential digital interventions to integrate into community-based oral health promotion. Meanwhile, for policymakers, the review’s results can inform the development of digital health policies, particularly those related to children’s health and the use of family-based preventive technologies. From the public’s perspective, especially that of caregivers, this mapping can provide an overview of available digital oral health technologies, along with their potential benefits and challenges in daily life.

Based on existing literature, this review will be the first to map the role of informal caregivers in the use of AI-based applications in childhood caries prevention and management. Unlike previous reviews that tended to focus on the accuracy of AI models or their use in clinical settings by professionals, this scoping review will highlight the role of AI in daily parenting and non-clinical settings, which are crucial yet relatively underexplored areas.16 27 31 As such, this study provides a new perspective on how AI can contribute to the prevention and management of caries in a more sustainable and family-based manner through the role of informal caregivers.

This study’s strength lies in its timeliness, given the rapid AI advancements since the COVID-19 pandemic. By focusing on informal caregivers’ roles in using AI-based childhood caries interventions, this review will fill a noted gap that prior reviews have overlooked. Another strength is the study’s robust methodology, which will follow the JBI framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Furthermore, including both peer-reviewed and grey literature will help capture new and practical approaches that might not yet appear in academic journals.

However, several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Restricting eligibility criteria to English publications may introduce language bias, as data extraction accuracy is prioritised over the potential misinterpretation of foreign-language articles. This scoping review does not evaluate the clinical effectiveness of interventions or assess the methodological quality of the primary studies. Rather, it identifies patterns, areas of research focus and gaps in the available evidence regarding the use of AI by informal caregivers.

Despite these limitations, this scoping review is expected to make an initial contribution to understanding the development and use of AI applications in caregiver-based paediatric dental health. The findings of this study can also serve as a foundation for future research, particularly in evaluating the effectiveness of AI interventions, user acceptance, the ethical aspects of using children’s digital data and the implementation of health technologies that are more adaptable to communities’ social and cultural contexts. Ultimately, this work will contribute to advancing promotive and preventive efforts in children’s oral health and help reduce disparities in access to health education in the digital age.
