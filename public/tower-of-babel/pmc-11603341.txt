The extracellular vesicle tetraspanin CD63 journey from the testis through the epididymis to mature bull sperm
Authors: Jana Jankovičová, Katarína Michalková, Petra Sečová, Ľubica Horovská, Jana Antalíková
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe important role of extracellular vesicles, which are considered key mediators of intercellular communication under physiological and pathological conditions, in various cellular processes, including those crucial for mammalian reproduction, has been increasingly studied. Tetraspanins, including CD63, are widely used as markers of extracellular vesicles, but they may also play a role in their biogenesis, cargo selection, cell targeting, and uptake. This study aimed to map the journey of the extracellular vesicle protein tetraspanin CD63 from the testis through the epididymis into mature bull sperm via an approach that included immunohistochemistry (immunofluorescence and immunoperoxidase staining), Western blot analysis, and immunoprecipitation analysis. We described the presence of CD63 in bull testicular and epididymal tissues, extracellular vesicles produced in these organs and spermatozoa during epididymal transit and after ejaculation. In addition, we revealed the nonuniform distribution of potential CD63 partners, such as CD9, integrin αV and syntenin-1, in the sperm head and tail and in extracellular vesicles. These findings contribute to understanding the complex mechanisms underlying sperm maturation and point to the possible involvement of tetraspanins and their associated partners, either as part of extracellular vesicles or sperm membranes, in these processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81021-w.Keywords: Exosomes, Spermatozoa, Reproduction, Maturation, Fertility, ProteinsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Physiology

Introduction

Intensive research on extracellular vesicles (EVs) provides increasing evidence of their important role in many cellular processes, including those related to mammalian reproduction. EVs are membrane-derived structures with different sizes (30–5,000 nm), cargo (proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids), and surface markers that are secreted into the extracellular environment1. Mammalian cells release several types of EVs, such as exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, according to their subcellular origin2,3. In addition, cells are likely to release subpopulations of EVs with unique functions that can be further influenced by secondary conditions3. Extracellular vesicles are thought to be important mediators of intercellular communication in various physiological and pathological processes. Furthermore, their potential as disease biomarkers, therapeutic agents, and drug carriers is increasingly being explored4–8. Several tetraspanins (CD63, CD9, CD81, and CD82) are widely used as EV markers9,10. Nevertheless, they are also thought to be involved in the biogenesis, cargo selection, cell targeting, and cell uptake of EVs in various mammalian body systems, including reproductive systems10. Extracellular vesicles produced by the male reproductive tract are involved in the processes associated with the ability of sperm to fertilise oocytes.

Sperm released from the testicle must pass through the epididymis, where they mature and gain motility. However, sperm only acquire their fertilising capacity in the female genital tract during the process of capacitation. Since sperm entering the epididymis are believed to be transcriptionally and translationally inactive, the key modulator of the sperm surface, i.e., the plasma membrane, is derived from the epididymal environment11,12. Furthermore, as sperm modification continues during ejaculation, additional EVs, such as prostasomes and seminal plasma vesicles, are undoubtedly a part of this process13–16; however, the mechanism of EV interaction with spermatozoa remains unresolved.

Decreased fertility is a complex and growing problem that affects not only the human population but also many species of mammals. In farm animals, the factors that most affect fertility include environmental pollution, endocrine disruptors, disease and infection, husbandry management and stress. Therefore, it is necessary to identify new diagnostic markers of male fertility/infertility and new approaches in assisted reproductive technologies. Several studies have indicated that one promising tool could be extracellular vesicles15,17–21. Furthermore, although the processes of development and maturation of the male mammalian gamete, the sperm, are now generally understood, not all the molecular pathways that lead to the ability of sperm to fertilise the egg have been elucidated, including those involving extracellular vesicles.

This study focused on one of the major exosome markers22, CD63, which is a protein belonging to the tetraspanin family and was the first characterised member23. CD63 is commonly referred to as a lysosomal membrane protein but is also found on the cell surface23,24. Although the tetraspanin family encompasses a variety of proteins, all of these proteins are thought to be principally involved in the organisation of plasma membrane proteins into molecular complexes known as the tetraspanin web, within the specific membrane domains but distinct from lipid rafts. These domains are known as tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs). Owing to the ability of tetraspanins to interact with various partner proteins, including tetraspanins (identical or different)25–31 and lipids (cholesterol)32,33, they regulate cellular processes such as cell adhesion, cell morphology, protein trafficking, and signal transduction34. To better understand the functions of tetraspanin complexes in the processes underlying gamete development, maturation, and fertilisation, several studies have investigated tetraspanins, particularly CD9, CD81, and CD151, in the female and male reproductive systems, including gametes10,35–41. There is some evidence that another tetraspanin, CD63, may interact directly or indirectly with at least CD9 and with other putative members of TEMs, such as integrins, to form a functional complex, the tetraspanin web, on cell membranes. CD63 has been shown to associate with the cytoskeleton only through interactions with the CD9-αIIβ3 integrin complex in platelets42. Our previous study reported the coimmunoprecipitation of CD63 and CD9 with integrin αV (ITG αV) in cow ovarian tissue lysates41. In addition, syntenin-1 (SYN-1), which has been shown to interact directly with CD63, coimmunoprecipitates with several tetraspanins, including CD9 and CD63, in MDA-MB-231 cell lysates24. Since the expression and distribution of CD63 in the bull testis, epididymis, and sperm have not yet been described, the aim of this study was to analyse the following: (1) CD63 in bull testicular and epididymal tissues; (2) CD63 in extracellular vesicles isolated from epididymal fluid and seminal plasma; (3) CD63 in epididymal and ejaculated bull sperm; and (4) potential partners of CD63, such as tetraspanin CD9, integrin αV, and syntenin-1.

Materials and methods

All chemical reagents were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise noted.

The following antibodies were used in the experiments: mouse monoclonal anti-CD63 antibody, clone CC25 (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) at 1 mg/mL; rabbit polyclonal anti-syntenin-1 antibody (PA5-42592) (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 3.19 mg/mL; rabbit polyclonal anti-integrin αV antibody (AB1930) (Sigma‒Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA); rabbit polyclonal anti-CD9 antibody (ABIN741015) (Bioss Antibodies, Inc., Woburn, MA, USA) at 1 mg/mL; mouse monoclonal anti-CD9 antibody (IVA50) (EXBIO, Vestec, Czech Republic) at 1 mg/mL; and mouse monoclonal anti-α-tubulin antibody (DM1A; Sigma‒Aldrich) at 1 mg/mL. The specificity of the reaction was verified via appropriate control staining. Mouse IgG1 (EXBIO, Vestec, Czech Republic) was used as an isotype control for monoclonal antibodies; rabbit IgG was used as an isotype control for polyclonal antibodies (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at the corresponding concentration. The secondary antibodies used were as follows: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 488 (A-11029), donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate (A-21206), anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA), and anti-mouse IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA).

In silico analysis of the expression of the gene encoding CD63 in the bull testis and epididymis (Bos taurus)

In silico analysis was performed via the following data sources: genes for the CD63 tetraspanin were retrieved from the BGEE database (release 15.1) (http://bgee.org/)43. The genes are listed in the Ensembl ID format (https://www.ensembl.org/)44. UniProt matches refer to the UniProt identifier of the protein (https://www.uniprot.org/)45 corresponding to the gene. The expression data (expression scores) were obtained from the BGEE database (Release 15.1). The expression scores of expression calls use the minimum and maximum values. Rank of the species is used to normalise the expression to a value between 0 and 100. A low score indicates that the gene is expressed at low levels.

Tissue section preparation

The testes and epididymides were obtained from at least three different young bulls (15–17 months old, Slovak Spotted Cattle breed) at a local slaughterhouse (Malá Mača, Slovak Republic). The epididymides were dissected into three main segments: caput, corpus and cauda. The tissues were preserved with TissueTek (Sakura Finetek, Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherland), frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Subsequently, 5 μm frozen sections were cut via a Leica Cryocut 1800 cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), placed on glass slides, air-dried and fixed for 5 min with a cold mixture of acetone and methanol (1:1)46.

Bull sperm collection

Ejaculated spermatozoa were obtained from bulls (Bos taurus) from Slovak Breeding Services, Inc., Lužianky, Slovak Republic. The sperm donors were Holstein bulls (aged 4–6 years; average body condition score of 3.5). The sperm were separated from the seminal plasma by centrifugation at 200 × g for 10 min at room temperature (RT), washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in PBS.

Epididymal sperm were obtained from the organs of at least three bulls (15–17 months old, Slovak Spotted Cattle breed) at the local slaughterhouse (Malá Mača, Slovak Republic). Epididymides were dissected into three segments. The caput and corpus epididymides were cut into small pieces and incubated in 10 mL of PBS for 15 min at 37 °C. The cloudy suspension was centrifuged at 50 × g for 10 min at RT to remove pieces of tissue debris from the sperm suspension. From the caudal part of the epididymis, spermatozoa were isolated by retrograde flushing by applying air pressure with a syringe inserted in the scrotal part of the vas deferens by aspiration via needles and a syringe (modified from Frenette et al.47). Pellets containing spermatozoa from all three parts of the epididymis were washed twice in PBS at 200 × g for 10 min at RT and resuspended in PBS. The sperm suspensions were further used to separate the sperm heads and tails46, and immunofluorescent labelling and Western blot analyses (WB) were performed.

Immunolabelling of tissues

An immunofluorescence assay was performed on testicular and epididymal tissue sections after blocking with Super Block® Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C. All the treatments were applied in a humid chamber to prevent the tissue sections from drying out. The tissue sections were treated with primary anti-CD63 antibody (1:100) or mouse IgG1 as an isotype control (1:100) in saline overnight at 4 °C. The secondary goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 at a 1:500 dilution in saline was applied for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The nuclear DNA of the cells was stained with mounting medium with DAPI-Aqueous Fluoroshield (Abcam, Cambridge, UK)46. The acrosomes of round, elongated spermatids and differentiated spermatozoa within the seminiferous tubules and spermatozoa in a particular part of the epididymis were stained with rhodamine-labelled peanut agglutinin (PNA-TRITC) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), a lectin that predominantly binds the carbohydrate sequence Gal-β(1–3)-GalNAc of the outer acrosomal membrane proteins of bull spermatozoa48,49. The sperm and spermatids with positive PNA-TRITC signals in the acrosomal region were considered those with present acrosomes. Immunostaining was evaluated under a Leica DM5500B epifluorescence microscope at 400× magnification. The fluorescence images were recorded via a Leica DFC340 FX digital camera and processed via Leica Advanced Fluorescence software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The settings for the epifluorescence microscope were fixed for every image. The staining procedure was repeated three times. Representative results are shown.

Immunoperoxidase staining of tissues

All the treatments were applied in a humid chamber to prevent the tissue sections from drying out. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, frozen testicular and epididymal sections were treated with 0.6% H2O2 in PBS. After blocking with Super Block® Blocking Buffer for 1 h at 37 °C, the sections were treated with an anti-CD63 antibody or an isotype control for 1 h at 37 °C, incubated with anti-mouse IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP), stained with 0.06% (wt/vol) 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride in PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature and slightly counterstained with Harris’s haematoxylin. The samples were evaluated under a light microscope at 400x magnification. The staining procedure was repeated three times. Representative results are shown.

Immunolabelling of isolated spermatozoa

The suspensions of epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa were smeared on slides, fixed with cold acetone-methanol (1:1) for 5 min and dried. The treatments were applied in a humid chamber to prevent the cell smears from drying out. All smears were blocked with Super Block® Blocking Buffer for 1 h at 37 °C and treated with primary antibodies (anti-CD63 and anti-CD9) (1:100) or an isotype control overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibodies in saline (1:500) were applied for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The nuclear DNA of the sperm was stained with mounting medium with DAPI-Aqueous Fluoroshield. A minimum of 200 spermatozoa with acrosomes (positive PNA-TRITC staining) were evaluated for each assay. PNA labeling spermatozoa showed the presence of acrosome in more than 96% of the sperm. Immunostaining was evaluated under a Leica DM5500 B epifluorescence microscope at 400× magnification. The fluorescence images were recorded via a Leica DFC340 FX digital camera and processed via Leica Advanced Fluorescence software. The settings for the epifluorescence microscope were fixed for all images. The staining procedure was repeated three times46. Representative results are shown.

The percentage of CD9 reaction patterns was normalised and is presented as the relative proportion of CD9 sperm patterns (mean ± SEM of three replicates) in individual parts of the epididymis (caput, corpus, and cauda). The statistical significance of differences was evaluated by Student’s t test via SigmaPlot 11.0 statistical software.

Separation of the sperm head and tail

The epididymal and ejaculated sperm heads and tails were separated according to Nagdas et al.50, with slight modifications. The washed sperm pellets were resuspended in PBS supplemented with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail and sonicated (Soniprep 150, 30 amplitude microns of power) at 5 × 10 s intervals with continuous cooling on ice. The sperm suspension was layered on a gradient composed of 60%, 70% and 75% sucrose (1:1:1) and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C (Beckman Optima™ L-100 XP, rotor SW30Ti, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The sperm heads remained in the pellet, and the tail fraction was at the interface between the 60% and 70% sucrose. Both fractions were collected, diluted in PBS supplemented with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail, pelleted by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C for sperm heads and 120,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C for sperm tails, and used for Western blot analysis.

Isolation of extracellular vesicles

The extracellular vesicles were isolated from caput, corpus and cauda epididymal fluid and seminal plasma. The epididymal fluid was obtained after isolation of the epididymal sperm via centrifugation at 250 × g for 10 min at RT. The seminal plasma was obtained by centrifugation of the fresh bull ejaculate at 250 × g for 10 min at RT to pellet the sperm. The supernatants were subsequently used for EV isolation via a Total Exosome Isolation Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Pellets of the EVs were used for Western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

The bull testicular and epididymal tissues were homogenised in 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.8) with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail by a homogeniser (UltraTurrax T18 IKA-Labortechnik, Germany). The homogenates were left on ice for 1 h to extract the proteins and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The whole epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa, as well as separated sperm heads, were solubilised in 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 detergent solution with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 1 h, subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and boiled with the Laemmli reducing/nonreducing sample solutions for 5 min at 100 °C. The sperm tails were treated with the Laemmli reducing/nonreducing sample solutions with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail and boiled for 5 min at 100 °C. The extracellular vesicles were resuspended in nonreducing sample solutions with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail and boiled for 5 min at 100 °C. The protein extracts were subsequently separated by 12% SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Advantec Toyo Kaisha, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The molecular weights of the proteins were estimated via PageRuler Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) in 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against anti-CD63 (1:500), anti-syntenin-1 (1:800), anti-CD9 (1:500), anti-integrin αV (1:500), and anti-α-tubulin (1:5,000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with anti-rabbit IgG-HRP conjugate (1:10,000) or anti-mouse IgG-HRP conjugate (1:5,000) for 1 h. α-Tubulin was used as a loading control. The membranes with protein extracts from the sperm heads and tails and EVs were stripped using BlueClear SB (SERVA, Heidelberg, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and reused for further immunodetection. Antibody binding was visualised via SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). HRP chemiluminescence was monitored with a VWR® Imager CHEMI Premium (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA) detection system and analysed with VWR® image capture software.

Coimmunoprecipitation analysis

Sperm isolated from caput, corpus, and cauda epididymides and ejaculated sperm were solubilised in 1% (w/v) Brij-97 in PBS with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail for 1 h on ice and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with the anti-CD63 monoclonal antibody (2 µg/mL) for 4 h at RT on a rotator. As a control, mouse IgG1 at the same concentration was used. Protein G agarose was added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotator. Precipitates bound to Protein G agarose were washed 5 × in PBS with a 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail for 5 min at 4 °C and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Coimmunoprecipitated complexes obtained by incubation with the Laemmli sample mixture for 5 min at 100 °C were separated via 12% SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk in 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS, the membranes with CD63 and control immunoprecipitates were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (anti-CD9, anti-integrin αV, and anti-syntenin-1) at 4 °C, followed by incubation with anti-rabbit/mouse IgG-HRP conjugates for 1 h. The antibody reaction was visualised via the SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate. HRP chemiluminescence was monitored with VWR R Imager CHEMI Premium detection systems and analysed via VWR® Image Capture software.

Detection of CD63 in bull testicular and epididymal tissues and extracellular vesicles

In the testis, the indirect immunofluorescence assay revealed that CD63 was distributed in clusters located close to the germ cells and the sperm in the lumen of the seminiferous tubules. A clear signal was observed, especially around the spermatogonia resting on the basement membrane (Fig. 1a). CD63-positive staining was observed in the epididymal secretory epithelium as a distinctive line of clusters towards the lumen (Fig. 1a) in all three parts of the epididymis (caput, corpus, and cauda). Additional immunoperoxidase staining confirmed the same localisation of CD63 in testicular and epididymal tissues and, more clearly, in the sperm tails within the lumen, predominantly in the corpus and cauda (Fig. 1a). The negative controls showed no immunoreactivity (Supplementary Fig. S1a, b). Western blot analysis of protein extracts of testicular and epididymal tissues confirmed the presence of CD63 as a protein band of ∼33 kDa in the corpus and a wider band of ∼34–42 kDa in the cauda (Fig. 1b). In addition, EVs isolated from caput, corpus and cauda epididymal fluid and seminal plasma were analysed. The bands corresponding to CD63 were detected at ∼32 kDa in the corpus and ∼32–45 kDa in the cauda (Fig. 1c).

CD63 in epididymal and ejaculated bull spermatozoa

The clustered reaction pattern of CD63 was also detected by immunofluorescence in the tails of sperm isolated from all three parts of the bull epididymis (Fig. 2a). In spermatozoa after ejaculation, CD63 clusters were located along the entire length of the sperm tail and partially in the postequatorial region of the sperm head (Fig. 2a). The negative controls showed no immunoreactivity (Supplementary Fig. S1c). Western blot analysis of protein extracts from whole epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa revealed CD63 only in the sperm isolated from the corpus epididymis (Fig. 2b). After the WB analysis of the protein extracts obtained from the sperm heads and tails separately, the anti-CD63 antibody recognised ∼65 and ∼70 kDa bands predominantly in the sperm tails from the caput, corpus and cauda epididymis, as well as in the ejaculated spermatozoa (Fig. 2c). Weak bands of ∼65 kDa were also observed in the corpus and cauda epididymis sperm head protein extracts (Fig. 2c). The negative controls showed no immunoreactivity (Fig. S2a).

Potential partners of CD63

To construct the protein‒protein interaction network associated with CD63_BOVIN (UniProt identifier: Q9XSK2), the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins database (STRING v12.0) was used (Fig. 3). STRING is a database of known and predicted protein‒protein interactions. The interactions include direct (physical) and indirect (functional) associations; they originate from computational prediction, knowledge transfer between organisms, and interactions aggregated from other (primary) databases51.

On the basis of the STRING analysis, we supplemented experiments concerning the detection of tetraspanin CD9, integrin αV, and syntenin-1 on bull tissues, extracellular vesicles, and sperm. In addition, coimmunoprecipitation analysis of sperm was performed.

Tetraspanin CD9

Immunofluorescence analysis of epididymal spermatozoa revealed a changing reaction pattern of anti-CD9 antibody towards the cauda (Fig. 4). In the sperm from the caput, the signal was predominantly observed in the equatorial region of the sperm head (blue arrows, Fig. 4a). In the corpus epididymis, the labelling gradually spread over the acrosome (orange arrows, Fig. 4a). Most of the sperm from the cauda epididymis were CD9 positive throughout the entire acrosome (orange arrows, Fig. 4a). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in the proportions of both reaction patterns (equatorial and acrosomal) between the caput and cauda epididymis (Fig. 4b).

Our previous work revealed the presence of CD9 predominantly in the sperm head plasma membrane fraction of bull ejaculated spermatozoa (∼24 and ∼37 kDa)35,36. Here, WB analysis of separated sperm heads and tails via an anti-CD9 antibody revealed positive bands of ∼30 and ∼37 kDa in the epididymal sperm head protein extracts from the caput. The ∼37 kDa band disappeared, and the ∼30 kDa band became more intense towards the cauda epididymis. Additionally, weak bands of ∼22, 24, 30, and ∼50 kDa were visible in the tail extracts of epididymal sperm. Only the ∼30 kDa band in the sperm head extracts and the ∼24, 30, and 50 kDa bands in the sperm tail extracts were detectable in the ejaculated spermatozoa (Fig. 5a). The anti-CD9 antibody also recognised ∼20 and ∼24 kDa protein bands in protein extracts of extracellular vesicles isolated from corpus and cauda epididymal fluid and seminal plasma (Fig. 5b). The negative controls showed no immunoreactivity (Fig. S2b). The possible partnership of CD9 with CD63 was demonstrated by coimmunoprecipitation experiments. Here, tetraspanin CD9 was detected as a band of ∼50 kDa in CD63 precipitates from caput, corpus and cauda epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

Mammalian spermatozoa are produced by spermatogenesis, a very complex and highly regulated process that takes place in the testis. Spermatogenesis begins with spermatogonia located in the basal compartment of the seminiferous tubules, which undergo mitotic and meiotic division to form spermatids, resulting in differentiated spermatozoa. The spermatozoa then transit through the epididymis, where they functionally mature. Although these processes are generally known, their specific actors, including tetraspanins, and molecular pathways are not fully understood, partly owing to species specificity. One of the potentially important players involved in these processes is CD63, a tetraspanin usually referred to as a marker of EVs, especially exosomes22. However, CD63 is also abundantly expressed on the cell surface and thus can be incorporated into microvesicles, which are EVs produced by direct budding from the plasma membrane53,54. The only available information concerning CD63 in bulls is from the Bgee database, which lists the gene expression of bull CD63 (ENSBTAG00000011931) in spermatocytes and spermatids, semen, testis, caput, and corpus of the epididymis.

This study provides a detailed distribution of CD63 in the reproductive tissues of bulls, specifically the testis and epididymis, as well as the epididymal and ejaculated sperm. Our immunohistochemical analysis showed that CD63 was already present in the testis, in clusters between the developmental stages of spermatozoa, around spermatogonia and the spermatozoa in the lumen of the seminiferous tubules. As tetraspanin CD63 is routinely used as a marker of exosomes and the observation of a clustered CD63 signal, we assume the presence of CD63-positive exosomes in bull testis. The occurrence of testicular EVs (testisomes) has been previously reported in mice55–57, rats, rabbits, and humans57 and more recently in cattle46. Similarly to the present observation, testisomes have been described close to the basement membrane of the seminiferous tubules46,57. In mice, spermatogonia have been shown to secrete large numbers of testisomes. As 83% of testicular EVs physically interacted with spermatogonia, the authors hypothesised that these EVs may play a role in negatively regulating spermatogonial stem cell proliferation57. In addition, the specific uptake of testisomes by somatic and germ cells, including spermatozoa, and their mutual communication have been documented in the same species56. Yun et al. assumed that EV-mediated communication between Leydig cells and spermatogonia likely regulates testosterone production55. Taken together, although the involvement of testisomes has been suggested in several vital functions in the testis (including spermatogonial stem cell proliferation and differentiation, Leydig cell survival and steroidogenesis, aberrant protein removal, antigen transfer and testicular protection58), most of these functions have not been fully elucidated.

Bull spermatozoa that develop in the testis are released to the epididymis, a long tubular organ comprising morphologically and functionally distinct anatomical segments: the caput, the corpus, and the cauda. The epididymal epithelium consists of different cell types (principal, narrow, clear and basal cells) and generates a luminal microenvironment appropriate for sperm maturation. During epididymal transit, spermatozoa, which are unable to synthesise their own proteins, undergo significant molecular changes, including the acquisition of new proteins and the addition, removal, and modification of sugars and lipids. In these modifications, an important role is attributed to EVs called epididymosomes, which are shed from the principal cells into the lumen of the epididymal duct12,59. The variation in the quantitative and qualitative profiles of luminal elements (inorganic ions, proteins and small noncoding RNA transcripts), including epididymosomes, reflects the different functions of individual epididymal segments60–62.

Our immunohistochemical analysis of the bull epididymis revealed CD63 in clusters projecting from the secretory epithelium towards the lumen of the epididymal ducts in all segments. These clusters were also observed around spermatozoa and even in the tails of the epididymal spermatozoa in the lumen. While the fusion or budding of vesicles with/from sperm tails has been previously observed by cryo-electron microscopy in humans63,64, the question that arises here is whether sperm can release EVs. Mature sperm cells lack the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, cytosolic ribosomes, and lysosomes and have silenced transcription and translation as well as membrane recycling; neither endocytosis nor exocytosis can occur63. In addition, bovine spermatozoa in particular lack endocytic pathways for internalisation65. Given these findings, we hypothesise that CD63 is a component of epididymal EVs produced by the stereocilia of principal cells. Epididymosomes are among the important mechanisms involved in the transfer of epididymal secretions to spermatozoa66,67. Our assumption of CD63-positive EVs was supported by the detection of CD63 as ~32–45 kDa bands in the protein extracts of epididymal tissues (corpus and cauda) and EVs isolated from the epididymal fluid of the corpus and cauda. We hypothesised that the differences in the detection of CD63 towards the cauda (regarding the presence and width of the bands) probably reflect the glycosylation status of this highly glycosylated molecule68. Changes in sperm proteins, including glycoproteins, that occur during epididymal transport have been described in mice69,70, pigs and bulls71,72 and may result from the activity of glycosidases and glycosyltransferases secreted into the lumen72 or from the addition of new glycoproteins to the sperm73–77.

In contrast to immunofluorescence, we did not detect CD63 in the spermatozoa of all epididymal segments by WB analysis. Because we observed CD63 preferentially in the tails of spermatozoa (epididymal and ejaculated), we assumed that the lysates of whole spermatozoa contained low levels of this protein and therefore further analysed the sperm heads and tails separately. WB confirmed the presence of CD63 predominantly in the tails of sperm from all three segments of the epididymis and ejaculated spermatozoa, with additional occurrence in the heads of sperm from the corpus and cauda. Proteins carried by epididymosomes can be transported to different subcompartments of the sperm plasma membrane (head or tail)73,74,76 and even to intracellular parts of spermatozoa78. Therefore, we speculate that the differences found in the protein profile could reflect specific interactions of spermatozoa with components of the epididymal lumen, including epididymosomes, depending on the segment of the epididymis or the nature of the interaction with vesicles.

Since CD63 belongs to the family of tetraspanins known for their formation and activity within multimolecular complexes, we studied the potential partners of CD63 in sperm. The findings of some authors24,79 suggested the cooperation of CD63 with another tetraspanin, CD9, in somatic cells. This assumption is also supported by our results in cow ovarian tissue41. The involvement of CD9-positive epididymosomes in the transfer of molecules to spermatozoa has been shown in bulls80. Similarly, Barranco et al., who described the association of CD63 with CD9 in exosomes from porcine seminal plasma, suggested a possible synergistic effect of these tetraspanins81.

An interesting dynamic in the distribution of CD9, which we observed in the heads of the epididymal sperm passing through the epididymis, suggested the transport of additional CD9 (to that carried by bull sperm from the testis46) associated with epididymosomes. The transfer of specific proteins by CD9-positive epididymosomes to bull sperm preferentially incorporated into the acrosome and the midpiece region of the tail was previously documented80. This finding is consistent with our WB results, which also confirmed the presence of CD9 in the tails of the spermatozoa. Our coimmunoprecipitation experiments indicated possible cooperation of CD9 with CD63 in the sperm tail, since a band with a molecular mass of ~ 50 kDa, detected in the sperm tail protein extracts, was confirmed in CD63 precipitates from epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa. The higher molecular weight suggests the formation of CD9 dimers82,] as we previously detected in ejaculated sperm36 and ovarian tissue extracts41,] or the covalent linkage of CD9 to another protein40. The STRING database also predicted both direct and CD9-mediated associations of CD63 with integrin αV. A previous coimmunoprecipitation analysis showing the possible interaction of tetraspanins CD63 and CD9 with integrin αV in cow ovarian tissue41 was confirmed in sperm. Our recent immunofluorescent analysis revealed the presence of ITG αV in bull epididymal and ejaculated spermatozoa (in the head and tail), and WB detection revealed a band corresponding to integrin αV (~ 130 kDa) in the fraction of the sperm plasma membrane46. The additional WB analysis of the tail protein extracts revealed several bands of lower molecular weight, which could be attributed to fragments of ITG αV produced by sonication during sample preparation. The sonochemical and sonomechanical effects of ultrasonic waves on proteins, including the breakage of chains and the modification of 3D structures, have already been reported83. Integrin αV has also been detected in sperm from other mammalian species, such as mice, pigs, and humans84,85. According to Al-Dossary et al.84 in mouse spermatozoa, ITG αV was localised together with CD9 over the acrosome and in the midpiece and principal part of the tail. The same authors reported the colocalisation of EVs (oviductosomes) with integrin αV and CD9 at the sperm head and tail and suggested the involvement of CD9 and integrin αVβ3 in the delivery of proteins to sperm in the oviduct84,86. Since we detected both ITG αV and CD9 in epididymosomes, we hypothesised that both molecules could participate in a similar mechanism in the epididymis. When studying extracellular vesicles in the male reproductive system, we should be aware that EV production may be affected by pathological changes or diseases. Modified secretion, heterogeneity, size, and small RNA cargo in CD9-positive EVs regulated by dihydrotestosterone were found in prostate cancer87,88. Interestingly, the αVβ3 integrin has been identified as a receptor for dihydrotestosterone89. In addition, increased levels of CD9 and CD63 double-positive exosomes in urine have also been detected in patients with this disease90,] and CD9-positive EVs measured in blood-based biofluids have even been suggested as alternative biomarkers87. For prostate cancer therapy, understanding the role of androgens and their receptors in controlling cancer cell proliferation and survival through their role in EV secretion and cargo is crucial. A similar regulatory mechanism may need to be considered for other diseases.

Additionally, CD63 was confirmed to directly interact with syntenin-1, a double PDZ domain-containing protein involved in pathways that control cytoskeletal dynamics, vesicular trafficking, cell adhesion, and cell polarity24. Moreover, SYN-1 is an abundant protein in exosomes from distinct cell lines of different species and biofluids91. Accordingly, our analysis indicated a potential partnership between CD63 and SYN-1 in the sperm tails, probably as a part of the population of epididymosomes (most likely exosomes). We suggest that the sources of CD63-positive epididymosomes are the principal cells of the epididymal epithelium, which secrete extracellular vesicles that interact with the plasma membrane of the sperm during passage through the male genital tract. The population of bovine epididymal EVs is not homogenous76,92,93,] and caput epididymal spermatozoa exhibit a higher fusion rate with epididymosomes than those from the cauda94. These findings support our WB detection of CD63 and its partners in protein extracts of epididymosomes and epididymal tissues.

The results revealed the presence of CD63, CD9, and ITG αV on the sperm head and the same molecules and SYN-1 on the sperm tail. These molecules may be part of the sperm membrane and/or the extracellular vesicles associated with it. We propose that CD63, together with its partners, CD9, ITG αV, and SYN-1, may be a part of the particular populations of extracellular vesicles associated with the head and/or the tail of epididymal and ejaculated bull spermatozoa. Different localisations of these molecules in distinct sperm subcompartments highlight the importance of a methodological approach in which sperm heads and tails are analysed separately.

Additionally, we detected EVs positive for these molecules in seminal plasma. However, it should be considered that they may also originate from accessory sex glands. The positive effects of CD9- and CD63-positive EVs from boar seminal plasma on motility, plasma membrane integrity, antioxidant capacity and prevention of premature capacitation have been shown95. Interestingly, CD63-positive exosomes secreted by sperm storage tubules influence sperm viability in the hen oviduct96. In humans, the use of extracellular vesicles from seminal plasma can significantly increase the success rate of IVF programs, especially in spermatogenesis disorders. Extracellular vesicles from the seminal plasma of normozoospermic but not asthenozoospermic men were found to increase sperm motility and induce capacitation97,98. In addition, the STRING database predicted a direct association of all investigated molecules, CD63, CD9, ITG αV, and SYN-1, with integrin β1 (ITGB1) (Fig. 3), and integrin α6β1 was identified as a potential clinical marker for assessing sperm quality (lower expression was observed in subfertile men99). As suggested by Roca et al.21, similar studies have not yet been carried out on farm animals, probably because sires with satisfactory sperm quantity and quality are selected, whereas those with poorer sperm quality are discarded. In this context, we anticipate that our results could be relevant for future clinical studies in humans. However, in farm animals, EVs have the potential to improve the freezability of sperm100, which would address the problem of significant differences in sperm freezing ability with the inclusion of some males in sperm cryobanks20. Sperm cryotolerance could be improved by supplementing freezing media with EVs from males with relatively greater sperm freezing ability or EVs artificially enriched with specific molecules21.

Considering the biological relevance of our findings, we established two hypotheses. The first one is, that tetraspanins CD63 and CD9 and SYN-1 and ITG αV are present on EVs as ‘remnants’ of their biogenesis and remain as part of EVs after their release from the cell. These molecules may simply indicate their presence on EVs, which either associate with or interact with the sperm plasma membrane. However, they can also have a specific role in the context of the interaction partners, as tetraspanins in EVs are likely to be involved in the different stages of extracellular vesicle biogenesis, cargo sorting, release, and interactions with target cells. Several studies have shown that CD63 and SYN-1 are directly involved in exosome biogenesis. Syntenin likely promotes exosome formation by inhibiting CD63 endocytosis, which allows CD63 to accumulate at the plasma membrane, the primary site of exosome biogenesis101. Syntenin-1 acts as an adaptor protein, playing a key role in coordinating the molecular interactions necessary for exosome formation and transport. It interacts with syndecans and ALIX102,103. Studies using knockout models have demonstrated CD63 as a key player in the exosome secretion104. On the other hand, another recent study revealed that CD63 and CD9 are not involved in the regulation of EV uptake and cargo delivery in several cell models (HeLa, MDA-MB-231 and HEK293)105. Different situations can exist in the sperm or reproductive tract, as outlined in a study on humans, in which the authors reported the overexpression of CD63 in the seminal plasma of men with primary infertility and suggested the involvement of CD63 in the vesicle-mediated transport of molecules106. Furthermore, it was suggested that CD9 generates fusion-competent sites on the membranes of both the oviductosomes and the sperm and that integrins ανβ3 and α5β1 facilitate adhesion by bringing these two membranes close together84.

Our study revealed that CD63 accompanies bull sperm during testicular development, epididymal maturation and ejaculation, together with partners such as CD9, integrin αV and syntenin-1 as a part of EVs. In western blot experiments, we analysed the population of EVs probably enriched in exosomes, but we could not exclude the presence of vesicles released by budding directly from the plasma membrane. The detailed characterisation of EVs in the bull reproductive tract is very difficult because they are released by several reproductive organs (testis, epididymis, prostate, seminal vesicles, and bulbourethral glands) and are also tissue segment-specific (caput, corpus and cauda epididymis). In addition, our results revealed that subsets of different EV populations are associated with sperm heads and tails, suggesting their potential influence on specific sperm competencies (motility, capacitation, sperm‒egg adhesion, and the acrosomal reaction). Our results contribute to the characterisation of the vesicle population, which is necessary for future studies and potential applications in assisted reproductive technologies. Particularly in the case of farm animals, expanding the knowledge of specific markers suitable for distinguishing between different EV populations or using EVs as carriers for other specific molecules will increase the possibility of their use, for example, to improve the freezability of sperm in cryobanks.

In conclusion, our study contributes significantly to a better understanding of specific molecular pathways involving CD63, CD9, integrin αV, and syntenin-1 in the development and maturation of bull (mammalian) spermatozoa. Our findings offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes, which are crucial for improving assisted reproductive technologies. In addition, we provide valuable data on the composition of bull genital tract vesicles, a crucial step towards the potential engineering of EVs for therapeutic purposes. More importantly, our results highlight the urgent need to investigate not only the molecular composition and cargo of EVs, but also their precise sublocalization in sperm. These findings have direct implications for the functionality and future use of EVs in reproductive technologies and reveal new opportunities to improve fertility treatments.
