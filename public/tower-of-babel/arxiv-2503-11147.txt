Asynchronous Sharpness-Aware Minimization for Fast and Accurate Deep Learning
Junhyuk Jo, Jihyun Lim, Vincent-Daniel Yun, Sunwoo Lee

Abstract
. Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) is an optimization method that improves the generalization performance of machine learning models. Despite its effectiveness, it has not been widely adopted in real-world applications due to its high computational cost. Existing approaches reduce this overhead by approximating or reusing gradients in the perturbation step, but still assume sequential perturbation and update steps. In this study, we introduce AsyncSAM , a variant of SAM that enables concurrent model perturbation and parameter updates across multiple compute resources. By adjusting the perturbation batch size in a system-aware manner, our asynchronous scheme fully overlaps perturbation with updates, eliminating the overhead and allowing even CPUs to perform perturbations without extra time. We also provide theoretical analysis showing that AsyncSAM achieves the same order of convergence as conventional SAM. Extensive experiments demonstrate that AsyncSAM delivers superior generalization performance and the highest training throughput among state-of-the-art SAM variants. For example, in ViT fine-tuning on CIFAR-100, it achieves accuracy comparable to SAM while maintaining training time nearly identical to SGD.

1.
Introduction

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) is an optimization method that improves generalization performance of machine learning models
(
Foret et al., 2021
)
.
Although SAM improves generalization, its high computational cost has limited its adoption in real-world applications.
Specifically, it calculates gradients twice for a single training step – one for model perturbation (gradient ascent) and another for model update (gradient descent).
To make SAM a more compelling option for deep learning applications, thus, addressing this high computational cost is crucial.

While several previous works have studied ways to mitigate the high computational cost of model perturbation in SAM, a recent trend is to partially discard perturbation gradients.
For example, LookSAM
(
Liu et al., 2022b
)
periodically computes perturbation gradients and reuses them across multiple iterations.
AE-SAM
(
Jiang et al., 2023
)
alternates between SGD and SAM depending on the loss landscape geometry.
ESAM
(
Du et al., 2022a
)
perturbs only a randomly selected subset of parameters.
Although these methods effectively reduce the computational cost, they inevitably lead to a non-negligible drop in accuracy due to the poor quality of the perturbation directions.

), indicating that the parameter space with respect to a given dataset does not change drastically during training.

In this paper, we design a novel asynchronous model perturbation scheme which leverages a critical observation on gradient stability during neural network training.
Figure
1

The similarity remains consistently high (greater than 0.8), indicating that the gradient direction in parameter space tends to be stable over short training intervals.
Similar observations have been reported in different contexts
(
Azam et al., 2021
;
Liu et al., 2022b
)
.
This observation naturally motivates us to develop an asynchronous-parallel model perturbation scheme.

We propose
AsyncSAM
, an Asynchronous model perturbation-based Sharpness-Aware Minimization algorithm.

AsyncSAM
decouples the model perturbation and update steps and hides the perturbation time with gradient descent time in SAM.
That is, we introduce a controlled degree of asynchrony so that the perturbation and update steps can run concurrently.
Instead of computing the perturbation gradient using the most recent model parameters, we employ slightly stale parameters to break the data dependency.
Our empirical results show that, with a system-aware choice of batch size, asynchronous perturbation can be performed even on relatively slow CPU cores, effectively eliminating the perturbation overhead.

Our study provides two key insights as follows.
First, gradients in large-scale deep learning tend to remain stable throughout training. As a result, a limited degree of staleness in model perturbation does not considerably harm the quality of the perturbation direction in parameter space, which in turn preserves the gradient norm penalization effect of SAM.
Second, although reducing the batch size for gradient ascent introduces stronger noise into the model perturbation, the resulting penalty is offset by the shorter training time made possible by asynchronous–parallel perturbation.
Based on these findings, we argue that SAM can be effectively applied to many real-world deep learning applications without significantly increasing training time.

2.
Related Work

Problem Statement
–
We consider the following standard minimization problem:

(1)

\displaystyle L(w):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}\left[l(w,\xi)\right],

where
\xi
is a data sample randomly drawn from the distribution
D
, and
l(\cdot)
is a non-convex and smooth objective function.
For convenience, we abbreviate
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}[\cdot]
as
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM)
– SAM is an optimization method that improves the model’s generalization performance
(
Foret et al., 2021
)
.
It first computes the gradient of the loss function and uses it to perturb the model.
Next, the gradient is computed again with the perturbed model parameters.
Finally, the model is reverted to its original parameters and updated using the gradient calculated from the perturbed model.
The parameter update rule is as follows.

(2)

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla L\left(w_{t}+r\frac{\nabla L(w_{t})}{\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|}\right),

where
w_{t}
is the model parameters at iteration
t
,
\eta
is the learning rate, and the
r
is a hyper-parameter that determines how strongly the model is perturbed.
Recently, it has been shown that SAM improves generalization performance by penalizing the gradient norm
(
Zhao et al., 2022
)
.

System-Efficient Variants of SAM
– There are several previous works that tackle the expensive model perturbation cost in SAM.

LookSAM
reuses gradients for multiple perturbations
(
Liu et al., 2022b
)
, but its generalization deteriorates with smaller batch sizes.
While
AE-SAM
reduces the overall training time by adaptively alternating between SGD and SAM based on the loss landscape geometry
(
Jiang et al., 2023
)
, it does not fundamentally reduce the perturbation cost.

ESAM
perturbs a random subset of parameters
(
Du et al., 2022a
)
, which may introduce bias due to selective data updates.

SAMPa
perturbs and updates the model simultaneously using an auxiliary sequence
(
Xie et al., 2024
)
.

SAM-ON
perturbs only batch normalization layers
(
Mueller et al., 2024
)
, limiting its applicability to architectures without them.

MESA
employs trajectory-based gradient approximation
(
Du et al., 2022b
)
, whose effectiveness depends on data complexity.
A layer-wise SAM has been also proposed, which perturbs only a subset of network layers making a trade-off between system efficiency and generalization performance
(
Lee, 2024
)
.
SAM has also been extended to federated learning, showing that local perturbation can improve global generalization
(
Qu et al., 2022
)
.
While these methods reduce the perturbation overhead, they do not completely eliminate it, still requiring a non-negligible amount of additional time.

Neural Network Training on Heterogeneous Systems
–
Several recent studies have explored opportunities for utilizing heterogeneous systems in neural network training.

DistReal

(
Rapp et al., 2022
)
is a system-aware federated learning method designed for heterogeneous environments.

EmbracingFL

(
Lee et al., 2024
)
proposes a partial model training strategy for heterogeneous federated learning.

InclusiveFL

(
Liu et al., 2022a
)
is a federated learning framework that adapts the model width based on device resource capabilities.
However, these works primarily focus on distributed learning settings with resource-constrained edge devices.
To the best of our knowledge, relatively few studies have investigated efficient neural network training that exploits in-node heterogeneous resources.
Zheng et al.
(
Zheng et al., 2022
)
proposed a distributed training method for hybrid (CPU–GPU) systems.
While this work considers in-node heterogeneous resources, it focuses specifically on graph neural networks.
Li et al.
(
Li et al., 2023
)
studied how to leverage the large memory capacity of CPUs to support neural network training on GPU servers.
Their work focuses on scheduling CPU and GPU workloads to maximize parallelism.

3.
Method

In this section, we first define an asynchronous-parallel model perturbation method, and then propose
AsyncSAM
, a general SAM optimizer built upon this method.
Finally, we provide a theoretical analysis of its convergence properties to demonstrate its efficacy.

3.1.
Asynchronous-Parallel Model Perturbation with Fixed Staleness

The first step of SAM is to perturb the model parameters via gradient ascent
(
Foret et al., 2021
)
.
After the perturbation, the gradient is computed, and the model is finally updated using this gradient.
Our key idea is to introduce a fixed degree of staleness into the model perturbation, enabling the perturbation and update steps to be performed concurrently.
This controlled staleness breaks the data dependency between the two steps, allowing the perturbation to be executed independently in a separate process.
The asynchronous model perturbation can be defined as follows.

(3)

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla L_{t}\left(w_{t}+r\frac{\nabla L_{t-\tau}(w_{t-\tau})}{\|\nabla L_{t-\tau}(w_{t-\tau})\|}\right).

The main difference between (
3
) and the parameter update rule of the original SAM lies in the asynchrony term,
\tau
, on the right-hand side.
When
\tau=0
, the method reduces to the original SAM, which updates the model only after perturbation.
A positive
\tau
indicates that the model is perturbed using slightly outdated gradients.
Ideally, by employing separate processes for the asynchronous perturbation and update steps, the overall training time can match that of SGD, without incurring additional overhead for model perturbation.

Discussion on Gradient Staleness
–
While asynchronous model perturbation (
3
) improves parallelism by removing data dependencies, it may slow down convergence if the gradient ascent step becomes too inaccurate.
Specifically, when
\tau
is large, the stale gradient
\nabla L_{t-\tau}(w_{t-\tau})
can deviate significantly from the current gradient
\nabla L_{t}(w_{t})
, thereby reducing the effectiveness of gradient norm penalization.
Therefore,
\tau
should be kept sufficiently small to ensure that the stale gradient remains close to the current one.
Azam et al., investigated how long stochastic gradients remain similar across SGD iterations
(
Azam et al., 2021
)
.
Their results suggest that first-order gradients remain similar over several iterations, supporting the effectiveness of our approach.

Comparison to Representative SOTA Methods
–
LookSAM
(
Liu et al., 2022b
)
exploits the stability of stochastic gradients by reusing a gradient for
k
iterations, where
k
is a hyperparameter.
This stability, however, depends on the training data and the learning rate, making it difficult to determine an appropriate
k
in advance.
For instance, with a large learning rate, the gradients can change substantially due to rapid movement in the loss landscape.
Consequently, reducing the degree of gradient staleness increases the likelihood of maintaining stable gradient dynamics, thereby improving the effectiveness of gradient-norm penalization.

AsyncSAM
introduces a similar hyperparameter
\tau
, however, we find that
\tau=1
effectively hides the perturbation overhead while minimizing staleness.

SAMPa
(
Xie et al., 2024
)
also parallelizes the gradient computations for perturbation and update.
However, it computes the perturbation gradient from an auxiliary sequence and assumes a homogeneous system with two similarly capable compute devices.
In contrast,
AsyncSAM
perturbs the model using the gradient computed at the previous model parameters, allowing the perturbation and update steps to overlap.
In addition, our study explores how to adjust the perturbation batch size in a system-aware manner, making a practical trade-off between system efficiency and generalization improvement.

3.2.
Asynchronous-Parallel Model Perturbation on Heterogeneous Systems

In modern systems for machine learning, it is common to use accelerators such as GPUs to train machine learning models.
Without loss of generality, we consider a system with at least two compute resources of possibly different capabilities (e.g., a CPU and a GPU).
To allow the SAM algorithm to fully exploit such heterogeneous resources while preserving its convergence properties, we propose assigning gradient descent to the faster resource and gradient ascent to the slower one.
Under this general setting, the parameter update rule of SAM can be written as follows.

(4)

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\eta\nabla L_{t}^{b}\left(w_{t}+r\frac{\nabla L_{t-1}^{b^{\prime}}(w_{t-1})}{\|\nabla L_{t-1}^{b^{\prime}}(w_{t-1})\|}\right),b^{\prime}\leq b,

where
\nabla L_{t}^{b}(w_{t})
indicates the mini-batch gradient computed from
b
random training samples at iteration
t
and
\nabla L_{t-1}^{b^{\prime}}(w_{t-1})
is the mini-batch gradient computed from
b^{\prime}
random samples at the previous iteration.

The model
w_{t}
and
w_{t+1}
are perturbed using the lastest gradients,
\nabla l(w_{t})
and
\nabla l(w_{t+1})
, respectively.

b
) A schematic illustration of the proposed
AsyncSAM
. Instead of using the latest gradient for the model perturbation, one-iteration-stale gradients,
\nabla l(w_{t-1})
and
\nabla l(w_{t})
are used to perturb
w_{t}
and
w_{t+1}
, respectively.
When the staleness
\tau
is sufficiently small, the stale gradients are close to the latest gradients, and the model is expected to move toward the same flat minimum.

The batch size
b^{\prime}
directly determines the time required for the gradient ascent.
In the original SAM,
b^{\prime}
is assumed to be equal to
b
.
We propose adjusting
b^{\prime}
in a system-aware manner such that
b^{\prime}=(T_{f}/T_{s})\times b
.
Here,
T_{s}
is the single data gradient calculation time on the slow compute resource and
T_{f}
is that on the fast resource.
In this way, we can automatically determine the maximum batch size
b^{\prime}
that equalizes the gradient ascent and descent times.
With a dedicated process, the gradient ascent computation can be fully overlapped with the gradient descent, effectively hiding the model perturbation time.
Note that
b^{\prime}
decreases as the performance gap between the two resources widens.

In (
4
), we intentionally fix
\tau=1
.
One might think the
\tau
could be increased instead of reducing
b^{\prime}
to match the gradient descent time and the ascent time.
Previous studies already have shown that they have the same complexity of the adverse impact on the convergence rate.
The degree of asynchrony
\tau
has the impact of
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
on the convergence rate
(
Koloskova et al., 2022
)
.
In the meantime, it has also been known that
\textbf{var}(\nabla L^{b})\propto b^{-1}

(
Gower et al., 2019
;
Smith and Le, 2018
)
.
However, reducing the batch size is more beneficial in terms of system efficiency.
A smaller perturbation batch size
b^{\prime}
reduces both computation time and memory footprint, whereas greater asynchrony only increases the gradient descent time that can overlap with the ascent time.
Therefore, we fix
\tau=1
and adjust
b^{\prime}
accordingly.

3.3.
Asynchronous SAM Algorithm

We construct
AsyncSAM
, an asynchronous-parallel model perturbation-based SAM, by combining the two aforementioned methods.
Figure

In Figure

The two gradient ascent steps perturb the model in nearly the same direction, consistent with our observation in Figure
1
.
Figure
2

Instead of the latest gradients (red arrows), our method uses
\tau=1
iteration-old gradients (blue arrows) for the model perturbation.
Although the gradients are staled, they are still similar to the latest gradients.
Thus, the model is expected to move toward a similar direction resulting in reaching the same flat minimum.

The algorithm assumes two threads (or processes) running concurrently.
Thread

\nabla L_{t}^{b^{\prime}}(w_{t})
at line 5, while thread
1
simultaneously computes
\nabla L_{t}^{b}(\hat{w}_{t})

Note that Algorithm

\tau=1

b^{\prime}<b

AsyncSAM

This mini-batch mismatch does not reduce its effectiveness.
Under uniform random sampling, each mini-batch gradient serves as an unbiased estimator of the full gradient.
That is,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla L_{t}^{b}(w_{t})]=\nabla L(w_{t})

\mathbb{E}[\nabla L_{t-1}^{b^{\prime}}(w_{t-1})]=\nabla L(w_{t-1})

As shown in Figure

3.4.
Convergence Analysis

We analyze the convergence behavior of
AsyncSAM
.
Our goal is to show that
AsyncSAM
guarantees a minimum level of performance, making it safely applicable to real-world applications.
The analysis assumes the followings.

Assumption 1.
(Smoothness): There exists a constant
\beta\geq 0
such that
\|\nabla l(u)-\nabla l(v)\|\leq\beta\|u-v\|\mathrm{,}\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 2.
(Unbiased Gradient): The gradient estimator is unbiased such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}\left[\nabla l(w,\xi)\right]=\nabla L(w)
.

Assumption 3.
(Bounded Variance): There exists a constant
\sigma\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}[\|\nabla l(w,\xi)-\nabla L(w)\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.

The following lemma provides a bound on the loss reduction per update.
All proofs are given in the Appendix.

Lemma 1.

Under Assumption 1–3, Asynchronous SAM guarantees the following if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}
and
r\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\frac{\eta}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta r\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta\beta\left(\eta+\frac{r}{2}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b}+2\eta^{2}r\beta^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}.

Based on the above result, we analyze the convergence rate of Algorithm
1
as follows.

Theorem 2.

Under assumption 1–3, if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}
and
r<\frac{1}{4\beta}
, asynchronous SAM satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\eta(1-4r\beta)T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4r\beta\tau}{(1-4r\beta)T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4\beta(\eta+\frac{r}{2})}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b}+\frac{8\eta r\beta^{2}}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}.

Remark 1 (Convergence guarantee).

For non-convex and smooth problems, Algorithm
1
guarantees a convergence regardless of the values of gradient ascent batch size
b^{\prime}
.
The first and second terms on the right-hand side are
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\eta T})
and
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau}{T})
, respectively, and thus vanish as
T\rightarrow\infty
.
While the noise terms do not depend on
T
, they also vanish as
\eta\rightarrow 0
, which is standard under a learning rate decay schedule.
This convergence rate has the same complexity with that of the conventional SAM with stochastic model perturbation
(
Andriushchenko and Flammarion, 2022
)
.

Remark 2 (Impact of asynchronous model perturbation).

The second constant term on the right-hand side grows linearly with the degree of asynchrony
\tau
.
However, this term vanishes as
T\rightarrow\infty
, it has no effect on the asymptotic convergence rate.

Remark 3 (Impact of perturbation batch size
b^{\prime}\leq b
).

As
b^{\prime}
decreases, the variance term on the right-hand side increases making the training loss converge more slowly.
That is, if the compute resources used for model perturbation and model update differ significantly in computational power, the convergence will become slower.
The last term on the right-hand side denotes the penalty from asynchronous gradient ascent, where gradient staleness slows convergence.
Nonetheless, the proposed method hides the gradient ascent time behind the gradient descent, yielding a practical trade-off between slower convergence and shorter wall-clock time.

Generalized SAM is a variant of SAM which updates the model using both the gradient ascent and descent
(
Zhao et al., 2022
)
.
ESAM is a variant of SAM that takes advantage of the partial model perturbation and a biased gradient estimator
(
Du et al., 2022a
)
.
LookSAM is another variant of SAM that reuses the gradient ascent
(
Liu et al., 2022b
)
.
MESA is a memory-efficient version of SAF (Sharpness-Aware Minimization for Free) that perturbs the model using a trajectory loss
(
Du et al., 2022b
)
.
AE-SAM runs SGD and SAM interchangeably based on the gradient norm
(
Jiang et al., 2023
)
.
SAMPa perturbs the model using the previous model that is updated using SGD
(
Xie et al., 2024
)
.

Overall,
AsyncSAM
achieves significantly higher accuracy than SGD.
Across all benchmarks, its accuracy is comparable to that of Generalized SAM, which attains the best validation accuracy among all SOTA SAM variants.
Several SOTA methods perform notably worse than the original SAM or even SGD (e.g., MESA on CIFAR-100 and LookSAM on Oxford_Flowers102 and Google Speech), whereas
AsyncSAM
consistently outperforms them by a large margin.
These results empirically demonstrate that the proposed asynchronous-parallel model perturbation preserves SAM’s superior generalization performance, even when the data dependency between model perturbation and model update is removed.
We also confirm that
AsyncSAM
is effective not only for moderately sized ResNet architectures but also for a large-scale Vision Transformer (ViT-b16).

5.
Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that SAM’s perturbation cost can be effectively hidden by introducing limited asynchrony in gradient ascent and appropriately adjusting the batch size based on the available system capability.
Using slightly stale parameters still yields sufficiently accurate perturbation gradients, preserving SAM’s gradient-norm regularization.
This enables efficient utilization of heterogeneous resources such as CPUs and GPUs, accelerating training while maintaining strong generalization.
We believe the proposed asynchronous perturbation scheme will make SAM significantly more practical for real-world applications on modern GPU servers.

Limitations and Future Work
–
This study focuses only on full-model perturbation, ignoring the potential of partial model perturbation approach. Harmonizing asynchronous perturbation and partial model SAM is a promising direction of future work.

A.2.
Theoretical Analysis

To make this appendix self-contained, we present the assumptions used in our analysis as follows:

Assumption 1.
(Smoothness): There exists a constant
\beta\geq 0
such that
\|\nabla l(u)-\nabla l(v)\|\leq\beta\|u-v\|\mathrm{,}\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 2.
(Unbiased Gradient): The gradient estimator is unbiased such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}\left[\nabla l(w,\xi)\right]=\nabla L(w)
.

Assumption 3.
(Bounded Variance): There exists a constant
\sigma\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim D}[\|\nabla l(w,\xi)-\nabla L(w)\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Then, we present a couple of useful lemmas here.
Note that our analysis borrows the proof structure used in
(
Andriushchenko and Flammarion, 2022
)
.

Lemma 1.

Given a
\beta
-smooth loss function
L(x)
, we have the following bound for any
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})),\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\geq\left(1-\frac{\beta r}{4}\right)\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}-r\beta\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\|^{2}

Proof.

\displaystyle\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(w_{t}),\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle+\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle\geq-\left\|\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|+\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle\geq-\beta\left\|r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|+\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\beta r\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|+\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle\geq-\beta r\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}-\frac{\beta r}{4}\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}+\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\beta r\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{\beta r}{4}\right)\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}

where (
6
) holds based on Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
7
) is based on
\beta
-smoothness, and (
8
) holds due to the fact
ab\leq\lambda a^{2}/2+b^{2}/\lambda 2
, where
\lambda=2
.
∎

Lemma 2.

We consider the classical SAM which uses the same mini-batch when calculating the gradient ascent and the gradient descent.
Then, given a
\beta
-smooth loss function
L(x)
, we have the following bound for any
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]-r\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\|^{2}\right]-\frac{\beta^{2}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2b}.

Proof.

We first define the asynchronous gradient ascent step
\tilde{w}_{t}=w_{t}+r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})),\,\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})),\,\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t})+\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t}),\,\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{E_{1}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t}),\,\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]}}+\underset{E_{2}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t}),\,\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]}}.

Then, we will bound
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
separately.
First,
E_{1}
is lower-bounded as follows.

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t}),\nabla L(w_{t})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau}))-\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})-\tilde{w}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})-r\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]

(11)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\beta^{2}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2b^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]

where (
10
) is based on the smoothness assumption. The final equality, (
11
), is based on the bounded variance assumption.
Then,
E_{2}
is lower-bounded directly based on Lemma
1
as follows.

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(\tilde{w}_{t}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]\geq\left(1-\frac{r\beta}{4}\right)\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}]-r\beta\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\|^{2}].

Summing up these bounds, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=E_{1}+E_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}\right]-r\beta\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\|^{2}]-\frac{\beta^{2}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2b^{\prime}}

∎

Lemma 3.

Under the assumption of
\beta
smoothness and the bounded variance, our asynchronous SAM guarantees the following if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}
and
r\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\frac{\eta}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\eta r\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\left(2\eta^{2}r\beta^{2}+\frac{\eta r\beta}{2}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}.

Proof.

We first define the perturbed model parameter at round
t
as
w_{t+1/2}
.

Then, from the smoothness assumption, we begin with the following condition:

\displaystyle L(w_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq L(w_{t})-\eta\langle\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta}{2}\left\|\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t+1/2})\right\|^{2}.

By taking expectation and based on the variance assumption, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t+1/2})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L_{t+1}(w_{t+1/2})-\nabla L(w_{t+1/2})\right\|^{2}\right]+\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t+1/2})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}

(12)

\displaystyle\quad+\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t+1/2})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}

\displaystyle\quad+\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t+1/2})-\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}

\displaystyle\quad+\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t+1/2})-\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

(13)

\displaystyle\quad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right].

Now, based on the smoothness assumption, (
13
) can be re-written as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|w_{t+1/2}-w_{t}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta^{2}\beta^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|w_{t}+r\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})-w_{t}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L_{t-\tau+1}(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla L(w_{t+1/2}),\nabla L(w_{t})\rangle\right].

Then, if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}
, based on Lemma
2
, the final term on the right-hand side can be replaced and rearranged as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta^{2}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta(1-2\eta\beta)r\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta(1-2\eta\beta)\frac{\beta^{2}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]

\displaystyle\qquad-\eta\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}+\frac{\eta r\beta^{2}}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta r\beta\left(1-2\eta\beta\left(1-r\beta\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\left(2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}+\frac{\eta\beta^{2}r^{2}(1-2\eta\beta)}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}

If
r\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
, the coefficient of
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(w_{t})\|^{2}]
term can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}+\frac{\eta r\beta^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\eta\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r\beta}{4}\right)\geq\frac{\eta}{4}.

Under the same condition, the coefficient of
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\|^{2}]
term can be also simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\eta r\beta\left(1-2\eta\beta\left(1-r\beta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\eta r\beta.

Since
\eta,\beta>0
,
(1-2\eta\beta)<1
, and the coefficients of the noise terms can also be simplified under the same condition
r\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\eta\beta^{2}r^{2}(1-2\eta\beta)}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta r^{2}\beta^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{\eta r\beta}{2}

\displaystyle 2\eta^{2}\beta^{3}r^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta^{2}r\beta^{2}.

Finally, plugging in the above coefficients, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t})]-\frac{\eta}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\eta r\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\left(2\eta^{2}r\beta^{2}+\frac{\eta r\beta}{2}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}.

∎

Theorem 4.

Under assumption 1–3, if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}
and
r<\frac{1}{4\beta}
, asynchronous SAM satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\eta(1-4r\beta)T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]\right)+\frac{4r\beta\tau}{(1-4r\beta)T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4\eta\beta}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b}+\frac{2r\beta(1+4\eta\beta)}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}.

Proof.

First, for the sake of simplicity, we define
c_{1}=\frac{\eta}{4}
,
c_{2}=\eta r\beta
, and
c_{3}=\frac{\eta^{2}\beta\sigma^{2}}{b}+\left(2\eta^{2}r\beta^{2}+\frac{\eta r\beta}{2}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}}
.
Then, based on Lemma
3
, we begin with:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{t+1})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq-c_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{3}.

By summing up across
T
steps, we have a telescoping sum as follows:

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\leq-c_{1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{3}T.

By replacing
t-\tau
with
s
, the second term on the right-hand side can be re-written as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t-\tau})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=-\tau}^{T-1-\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{s})\right\|^{2}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+\tau\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right].

By plugging in (
16
) to (
15
), we have:

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\leq-(c_{1}-c_{2})\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{2}\tau\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]+c_{3}T.

By rearranging the terms and dividing both sides by
T(c_{1}-c_{2})
:

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T(c_{1}-c_{2})}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]\right)+\frac{c_{2}\tau}{T(c_{1}-c_{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}-c_{2}}.

Finally, if
r\beta<\frac{1}{4}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\eta(1-4r\beta)T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{0})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(w_{T})\right]\right)+\frac{4r\beta\tau}{(1-4r\beta)T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla L(w_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]

(19)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4\eta\beta}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b}+\frac{2r\beta(1+4\eta\beta)}{1-4r\beta}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b^{\prime}},

where (
19
) holds since
c_{1}-c_{2}=\frac{\eta(1-4r\beta)}{4}
.
∎

.
