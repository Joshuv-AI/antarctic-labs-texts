On the structure of complex spectra and eigenfunctions of transfer and Koopman operators
Matheus M. Castro and Gary Froyland

Abstract
. Complex eigenspectra of transfer and Koopman operators describe rotational motion in dynamical systems. A particularly relevant situation in applications is when the rotation speed depends on the state-space position of the dynamics. We consider a canonical model of such dynamics in the presence of small noise, and provide precise characterisations of the eigenspectrum and eigenfunctions of the corresponding transfer operators. Further, we study the limiting behaviour of the eigenspectrum and eigenfunctions in the zero-noise limit, including their quadratic and linear response. Our results clarify the structure of transfer and Koopman operator eigenspectra, and provide new interpretations relevant to applications. Our theorems on support localisation of the eigenfunctions yield simple algorithms to detect the existence and state-space location of approximately cyclic motion with distinct periods. Our numerical results verify that information on the cycle periods and their locations determined by the operator eigendata is insensitive to noise level in the linear response regime. We believe that the dynamic mechanisms underlying the eigendata and their properties apply rather broadly and enhance our understanding of approximate cycle detection in dynamical systems with operator methods.



1.
Introduction

Over the last three decades, operator-theoretic techniques have proven to be highly successful methods of analysis for complex dynamical systems.
The basis of the analysis is usually the dominant eigenspectrum of a transfer operator or Koopman operator and the corresponding eigenfunctions.
In the autonomous setting, where the underlying dynamics does not change in time, large real spectral values correspond to almost-invariant sets (large regions of state space that have no collective state-space motion under the dynamics)
[
7
,
6
]
.
This concept has been applied extensively in several fields, including molecular dynamics
[
27
]
to find stable molecular conformations, space mission design
[
5
]
to identify orbital trapping regions, and physical oceanography
[
13
,
15
,
25
]
to map ocean gyres and garbage patches.
Regarding
complex
eigenvalues, the Halmos–von Neumann Theorem (see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 17.11]
) states that each ergodic measure-preserving system with discrete spectrum is isomorphic to an ergodic rotation system, for example, an aperiodic rotation on a
n
-torus.
This concept has been used extensively in the context of Koopman operators, e.g.
[
24
,
26
]
.

The current paper takes the underlying Halmos–von Neumann idea and extends it in two ways.
Firstly,
we allow the rotation to depend on the location in the state space
, rather than being fixed along a particular coordinate throughout the state space. Secondly,
we make a detailed study of the effect of noise
on the complex eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.
The motivation for this study is that complex dynamical systems often possess many co-existing cycles or approximate cycles.
For example, in the Earth system, climate drivers such as the El-Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO)
and the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO) are approximate co-existing cycles that influence the weather and climate.
On even longer timescales, changing glacial cycles describe how northern-hemisphere continental ice sheets repeatedly accumulate and melt over millions of years.

Recent work
[
10
,
11
]
has demonstrated that transfer operator methods are a flexible and reliable way of extracting cycles from models, observations, and even time series of spatial fields; for example extracting the ENSO cycle from a time series of observed sea-surface temperature fields.
Long-lived persistent cycles typically give rise to complex eigenvalues in the dominant spectrum of the transfer operator.

The magnitude of the eigenvalue quantifies the rate of temporal decay of the cycle, while the argument of the eigenvalue directly provides an estimate of the cycle’s period.
Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions may be used to represent the cycle in terms of the original spatiotemporal time series
[
10
]
.

These ideas may be illustrated with the Lorenz equations, where an estimate of the transfer operator is constructed and the complex eigenvalue with largest magnitude is computed.
The left panel of Figure

displays the magnitude of the complex entries of the corresponding eigenvector on a blue colourscale, and the right panel shows the argument of the complex entries on a periodic colourscale.

Our focus in this work is to provide a formal explanation for the behaviour of the spectrum and eigenfunctions of the transfer operator in the presence of coexisting cycles with different periods.
Roughly speaking,
eigenfunctions corresponding to cycles with distinct periods should have supports that are largely disjoint
, because large overlaps would need to simultaneously rotate at two distinct speeds.
Our formal analysis concentrates on a mathematical model with multiple coexisting cycles, acting on a discrete collection of circles, between which trajectories may randomly jump under the influence of noise.
Our state space
M
is the union of
N
circles:
M:=\{1,\ldots,N\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which we view as a cylinder that has been discretised along its central axis.
The dynamics on the
j^{\rm th}
circular band of the discretised cylinder is a rotation with speed
\alpha_{j}
; see Figure

.

The addition of noise is a crucial selection procedure that promotes important eigenfunctions of the transfer/Koopman operator by associating them with large-magnitude eigenvalues.
This heuristic is classical for the eigenvalue 1 where the eigenfunction corresponds to the invariant measure
[
21
,
20
,
30
,
1
]
, which describes the state-space distribution of long trajectories, and for large positive real eigenvalues in connection with almost-invariant sets and metastability
[
7
]
.
Moreover, the addition of noise may also be useful for proving convergence of numerical schemes
[
7
,
19
,
4
]
.
It has also been applied in the context of thermodynamic formalism, where noise perturbations identify equilibrium states by associating them with large-magnitude eigenvalues of suitable operators
[
3
,
2
]
.
Further motivations for considering the addition of noise are:

(1)

models and observations are imperfect and often subject to small errors;

(2)

many numerical approaches to estimating transfer and Koopman operators introduce artificial noise or diffusion through reduction to a finite-rank representation of the operator;

(3)

the addition of noise enables formal discussion of spectra and eigenfunctions of transfer operators acting on simple spaces such as
L^{2}
;

(4)

zero-noise limits are difficult limits of singular type, yet remain relevant and are the type observed in numerical experiments.

This work builds fundamental theory to support the heuristic notion that the addition of noise
promotes eigenfunctions connected with dominant cycles toward the largest-magnitude complex eigenvalues
.
We achieve this by conducting a formal analysis of a model system that possesses the relevant cyclic properties.
We perturb this model with noise and carefully study the zero-noise limit, fully characterising the limiting eigenvalues and the form of the limiting eigenfunctions.
Moreover, we prove quadratic response of the eigenvalues and linear response of the eigenfunctions, the form of which demonstrates a
strong robustness of the support-localisation of the eigenfunctions to a range of small noise
.

To introduce noise to our model we use a discrete-time Markov process between the circular fibres governed by an
N\times N
stochastic transition matrix

W_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
that converges to the identity matrix as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
For example, if we have
N=33
fibres as in Figure
3
, the matrix

(1)

W_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{pmatrix}1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&&&&\\
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&1-\varepsilon&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&&&\\
&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&1-\varepsilon&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&1-\varepsilon&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\
&&&&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}&1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

will be suitable for our model.

Additionally, on each circular fibre we apply i.i.d. additive noise.
In summary, the dynamics
T:M\to M
is given by

T(j,x)=(j,x+\alpha_{j})+(\gamma,\eta)
, where
\gamma
is selected according to the Markov chain
W_{\epsilon}
conditioned on being in state
j
, and
\eta
is an i.i.d. random variable with values uniformly distributed in
[-\delta,\delta]
.
Equivalently,
for each
\varepsilon>0
we consider a Markov chain
X_{n}^{\varepsilon,\delta}=(I_{n}^{\varepsilon},Y_{n}^{\varepsilon,\delta})
on
M
, where
I_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is a
Markov chain on
\{1,\ldots,N\}
with transition matrix
W_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

W_{\varepsilon,i,j}=\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1}^{\varepsilon}=j\,\middle|\,I_{0}^{\varepsilon}=i\right)
.
The second component evolves according to

Y_{n+1}^{\varepsilon,\delta}:=Y_{n}^{\varepsilon,\delta}+\alpha_{I_{n}^{\varepsilon}}+\eta_{n}^{\delta},

where
(\eta_{n}^{\delta})_{n\geq 0}
are i.i.d. random variables, each uniformly distributed on
[-\delta,\delta]
as above.

For this model, we demonstrate a separable structure of the eigenfunctions of the transfer operator (Proposition
2.3
) and use this structure to generate a series of precise results regarding zero-noise limits.
We prove that as the noise
\varepsilon
approaches zero, the eigenvalues converge to a complex exponential given by one of the rotation speeds, linearly in
\varepsilon
with an explicit bound for the distance from the limit (Proposition
2.3
).
When there are bands of neighbouring circular fibres that share a rotation speed,
clusters
of eigenvalues approach a single complex exponential (Theorem
2.8
and Figure
4
(left)).
We prove that in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit, the corresponding eigenfunctions approach an orthonormal basis supported on the band (Theorem
2.8
and Figure
5
).
Despite having multiple eigenvalues converging to a single eigenvalue, we develop a quadratic response formula for the eigenvalues and linear response formulae for the eigenfunctions (Theorem
2.10
).
The supports of the latter are also restricted to their associated band, resulting in this support localisation property being particularly robust for small noise levels.
Proposition
2.13
covers the response of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the transfer operator with respect to perturbations of the rotation speeds
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.

2.
Model and statements of main results

2.1.
The model

The Perron–Frobenius operator corresponding to the Markov chain on the fibres
\{1,...,N\}
is given by

(2)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{base},\varepsilon}f(j^{\prime}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{\varepsilon,j,j^{\prime}}f(j),

On the
j^{th}
band, the annealed transfer operator

\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}:L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is given by

(3)

\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}h(x):=\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{[-\delta,\delta]}h(x-\alpha_{j}-\eta)\ d\eta.

The product of the discrete uniform probability measure on
\{1,\ldots,N\}
and normalised Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is denoted by
m
.
Using the form of
T
, and letting
F\in L^{1}(M)
, the annealed transfer operator with respect
m
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\delta}F(j^{\prime},x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{\varepsilon,j,j^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{[-\delta,\delta]}F(j,x-\alpha_{j}-\eta)\ d\eta.

We now write
F(j,x)=f(j)h_{j}(x)
to highlight the skew-product nature of
T
and
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\delta}F(j^{\prime},x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{\varepsilon,j,j^{\prime}}f(j)\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{[-\delta,\delta]}h_{j}(x-\alpha_{j}-\eta)\ d\eta

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{\varepsilon,j,j^{\prime}}f(j)\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}h_{j}(x)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{base},\varepsilon}f(j)\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}h_{j}(x).

Because each
\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}
is the transfer operator for a rotation on the
j^{th}
circle by
\alpha_{j}
(plus uniform bounded noise), the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}
are complex exponentials of increasing order,
independent of
j
.
Thus, as we shall shortly see, the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
are
separable in the variables
j
and
x
.

2.2.
General behaviour of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

2.2.1.
The case of
\varepsilon=0
.

Before we begin our main analysis, it is informative to consider the simple situation where
\varepsilon=0
and
\delta\geq 0
.
This corresponds to no noise between the circular fibres, and
\delta
-noise along fibres.
With no noise connecting the distinct copies of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the rotations on each circle all individually contribute to generate the full eigenspectrum of
\mathcal{P}_{0,\delta}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The spectrum of
\mathcal{P}_{0,\delta}
consists of the
N
-fold union of the eigenvalues
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\sigma(\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j})
, where

(5)

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j})=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\frac{\sin(2\pi k\delta)}{2\pi k\delta}e^{-2\pi ik\alpha_{j}}\right\}\cup\{0\}.

Proof.

In this case
W_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{Id}
, therefore (
4
) implies that given
F(j,x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{k}(j)e^{2\pi ikx}\in L^{2}(M)

Using (
3
) one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0,\delta}F(j,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(j)\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j}(e^{2\pi ikx})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(j)\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}e^{2\pi ik(x-\alpha_{j}-\eta)}\operatorname{d}\!\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{\sin(2\pi k\delta)}{2\pi k\delta}e^{-2\pi ik\alpha_{j}}\right)f(j)e^{2\pi ikx},

where
\sin(0)/0
should be interpreted as
1
in the above equation. Thus, by comparing the Fourier modes of
\mathcal{P}_{0,\delta}F
and
\lambda F
we obtain (
5
).
∎

The modes
k=\pm 1
are the primary modes of oscillation corresponding to a single oscillation around the circle, as shown by the
e^{2\pi ikx}
term in the final display equation above. In practice, selecting
k=\pm 1
makes the extraction of the
\alpha_{j}
from an eigenvalue straightforward because of the argument term of
e^{-2\pi ik\alpha_{j}}
.
For positive
\delta
the damping factor
\frac{\sin(2\pi k\delta)}{2\pi k\delta}
suppresses higher-order Fourier modes.
In particular, the
k=\pm 1

making the identification of the

Remark 2.2
.

All of the results in Section
2
can be easily generalized from the state space
M=\{1,\ldots,N\}\times S^{1}
to a state space
M=\{1,\ldots,N\}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
where:

(1)

the
\alpha_{j}
-rotations on the circle
S^{1}=\mathbb{T}^{1}
are replaced with
(\alpha_{j,1},\ldots,\alpha_{j,d})
-translations on the
d
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

(2)

the discrete fibres
\{1,\ldots,N\}
are no longer discretely filling an interval, but discretely fill a connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
.

Such a situation allows one to create models of rather general rotational dynamics.
For example, a simple extension to
d=1
and
d^{\prime}=2
would model fluid rotation around a solid three-dimensional torus, with the
N
fibres discretely filling out the non-periodic two-dimensional cross-section of the solid torus.
Subsets

of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
called

(see Definition
2.4
) represent solid “tubes”
B_{s}\times S^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
of three-dimensional fluid that rotate at a common angular speed inside a solid three-dimensional torus.
Alternatively – remaining in a solid three-dimensional torus – we may set
d=2
and
d^{\prime}=1
to model translations on two-dimensional nested toral shells within the solid torus.
Bands (subsets of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
) represent nested families of solid tori

(like Matryoshka dolls) with common translations on each member of the family.

In this general setting, the spectrum of
\mathcal{P}_{0,\delta}
is

\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\sigma(\mathcal{P}_{\delta\restriction j})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left\{\prod_{q=1}^{d}\frac{\sin(2\pi k_{q}\delta)}{2\pi k_{q}\delta}e^{-2\pi ik_{q}\alpha_{j,q}}\right\}\cup\{0\}

where
\alpha_{j,q}
is the rotation speed on fibre
j
along coordinate direction
q
.
Concerning the extension from
S^{1}
to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the same proofs of all of our results follow with minor adaptations.
For the extension of the fibres
\{1,\ldots,N\}
filling out sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, one only needs to appropriately adjust the matrices

.
For example, in
d^{\prime}
dimensions, a natural choice would be to construct

from a finite-difference stencil for the Laplace operator on a regular grid in
d^{\prime}
dimensions (see (
1
) for such a choice for

For the sake of simplicity of exposition, we restrict the statements and proofs of all results to the case

2.3.
The case of multiple fibres with common rotation speed

A particularly interesting situation arises when some
\alpha_{j}
are identical for neighbouring
j
s.
This corresponds to a band of fluid rotating at the same speed, and handling this physically relevant situation requires a more careful analysis.
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.4

(
S
-banded)
.

A
N
-vector
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to be
S
-banded
if there exist

(i)

a natural number
1\leq S\leq N
,

(ii)

distinct real numbers
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{S}\in\mathbb{R}
, and

(iii)

a vector of positive integer numbers
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{S})
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{N}L_{i}=N,

such that

\alpha_{j}=\beta_{s}\quad\text{if }N_{s-1}<j\leq N_{s}\quad\text{for some }s\in\{1,\ldots,S\},

where
N_{0}<N_{1}<\ldots<N_{S}=N
is a sequence of recursively defined natural numbers

N_{0}=0\ \text{and }N_{i}=L_{i}+N_{i-1}\ \text{if }i\in\{1,\dots,S\}

In the above context, the vector
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{S})
is referred to as a
band vector
, and
L
is called the
band-width vector
. Moreover, given
s\in\{1,\ldots,S\}
we denote the
band members of
\beta_{s}
as
B_{s}=\{j\in\mathbb{N}:N_{s-1}<j\leq N_{s}\}.
Observe that from the convention adopted in (
10
) we have that

\{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:\lambda^{(\ell)}_{k,\varepsilon}\to e^{-2\pi ik\beta_{s}}\mbox{ as $\varepsilon\to 0$}\}=B_{s}.

2.4.
Quadratic and linear response

Proposition
2.3
tells us that for each Fourier index
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,N
of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
each converge to
e^{-2\pi ik\alpha_{\ell}}
, while Theorem
2.8
says that the eigenprojections
\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
associated with the eigenfunctions
F^{(\ell)}_{k,\varepsilon}
of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
converge to
L^{2}
-orthogonal projections
\Pi_{k}^{(\ell)}
onto
\operatorname{span}\{F_{k}^{(\ell)}\}
which have explicit eigenfunction characterisations and support localisations.
A fundamental question arising from these results is the rate of convergence of
\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
and
\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
If all
\alpha_{\ell}
are distinct, one may hope that the corresponding one-dimensional eigenprojections
\Pi^{(\ell)}_{k,\varepsilon}
are regular functions of
\varepsilon
at
\varepsilon=0
.
On the other hand, if we have nontrivial bands – i.e.
S<N
in Definition
2.4
– then we would expect at best regular dependence of the various
L_{s}
-dimensional eigenspaces, corresponding to the
L_{s}
repeated eigenvalues
e^{-2\pi ik\beta_{s}}
, at
\varepsilon=0
.
Surprisingly, in the nontrivially banded situation, we can in fact demonstrate quadratic response of
all individual eigenvalues

\varepsilon\mapsto\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}
at
\varepsilon=0
, and linear response of
all individual eigenprojections

\varepsilon\mapsto\Pi^{(\ell)}_{k,\varepsilon}
at
\varepsilon=0
.
To further illustrate why this is surprising, we recall that the standard approach to establishing linear response of the eigenprojections is to differentiate the equation
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}=\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}

with respect to
\varepsilon
, where we use the notation
\dot{}
to denote
\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}
. This differentiation yields the identity

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\dot{\Pi}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}+\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}=\dot{\lambda}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}+\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\dot{\Pi}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)},

which can be rewritten as

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}=-\left(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\right)^{-1}\left(\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}-\dot{\lambda}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\right)\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}.

A common strategy is to evaluate equation (
14
) at
\varepsilon=0
. However, in our setting, this approach fails because the range of the operator

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left.\left(\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}-\dot{\lambda}_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\right)\Pi_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}

lies outside the domain of
(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{k,\varepsilon}^{(\ell)})^{-1}
. To overcome this difficulty, we use a non-trivial cancellation mechanism specific to our framework.
This allows us to obtain explicit expressions for the response terms for both the eigenvalues and eigenprojections.

Further, we observe numerically (for example as in Figure
6
(right)) that the form of the response of the eigenfunctions
\dot{F}^{(\ell)}_{k}
is such that the overall shape of the envelope of
F^{(\ell)}_{k,\varepsilon}
is relatively unchanged by modestly increasing
\varepsilon
from 0.
Practically, this observation helps to explain the robustness of dominant complex eigenfunctions to various types of noise, whether they arise from models, data, or numerical techniques.

The following theorem refines Theorem
2.8
by stating the linear response of the eigenprojections, in addition to the linear and quadratic responses of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
as

.

In this section, we describe the linear response with respect to the rotation speed vector

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
we write it as
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}

2.5.1.
The case of
\varepsilon=0
.

For
\varepsilon=0
, linear response with respect to the band vectors holds when
S=N
(all circular fibres rotate with distinct speeds) or

, and fails when
S\neq N
and

.

Proposition 2.12
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. For
\varepsilon>0
let
\Pi_{\varepsilon,\alpha,k}^{(\ell)}
denote the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}\{F_{k,\varepsilon,\alpha}\}
, where
F_{k,\varepsilon,\alpha}
is defined in (
17
). Whenever the limit exists, set
\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Pi_{\varepsilon,\alpha,k}^{(\ell)}.
Then:

(i)

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
N
-banded (all circular fibres rotate at distinct speeds), the map
\alpha\mapsto\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}
is locally constant in a neighbourhood of
\alpha
. In particular,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}=0

at every
N
-banded vector
\alpha
. The same conclusion holds when
k=0
and
\alpha
is
S
-banded for any
1\leq S\leq N
.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
S
-banded for some
1\leq S<N
and
k\neq 0
, then the map
\upsilon\mapsto\Pi_{k,\upsilon}^{(\ell)}
is discontinuous in a neighbourhood of
\alpha
. Consequently, linear response fails at
\alpha
.

Proof.

(i)

Assume that
\alpha
is
N
-banded. By Theorem
2.8
(2),
\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}
exists and is the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}\{F_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}\}
, where
F_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}(j,x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\ell\}}(j)e^{2\pi ikx}.
In particular, for
N
-banded parameters the range of
\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}
is generated by a function that does not depend on
\alpha
. Hence
\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}
itself is constant on the set of
N
-banded vectors. Since the set of
N
-banded vectors is open in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the map
\alpha\mapsto\Pi_{k,\alpha}^{(\ell)}
is locally constant at
\alpha
, and therefore its derivative with respect to
\alpha
vanishes there. The case
k=0
is analogous.

(ii)

Let
k\neq 0
and let
\alpha
be
S
-banded with
1\leq S<N
. Since the set of
N
-banded vectors is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, there exists a sequence
(\upsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
N
-banded vectors such that
