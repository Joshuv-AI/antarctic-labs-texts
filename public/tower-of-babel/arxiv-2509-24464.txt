Sinc basis set for molecular orbitals calculation of cavity in 1D
Xueyuan Yan

Abstract
. We develop a numerical approach based on the sinc basis set for first-principles electronic structure calculations in one-dimensional systems. The method exploits the inherent accuracy and non-local character of the sinc functions to handle long-range couplings effectively. As a proof of principle, we provide a computational study of a molecule in an optical cavity, with our code directly calculating the emergence of the energy level splitting in the spectrum.



1.
Introduction

The interaction between electrons and photons in an optical cavity is commonly described by the Pauli–Fierz Hamiltonian
[
3
,
7
]
. When molecules are placed inside such a cavity, their electronic structure is modified by the quantized electromagnetic field, giving rise to phenomena such as polaritonic states and modified chemical reactivity. However, standard quantum chemistry methods—developed for isolated molecules in free space—are not directly applicable in this setting, and dedicated computational tools remain limited.

A key challenge lies in the electron–photon coupling term, which is non-local and typically contributes only weakly to the total energy. Nevertheless, its long-range character demands a basis set capable of accurately representing both smooth and rapidly varying features of the wavefunction, while maintaining high numerical precision. The sinc basis set
[
4
]
offers a promising solution: it is orthonormal, systematically improvable, and converges to the complete basis set limit as the grid spacing is refined. Molecular orbitals can be expressed as linear combinations of sinc functions defined on a uniform real-space grid.

Despite these advantages, a direct implementation using the sinc basis incurs a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
for the two-electron integrals, where
N
is the number of grid points. Fortunately, the off-diagonal elements of the associated integral tensors decay rapidly with decreasing grid spacing, enabling the use of sparse or low-rank approximations to mitigate this scaling. Moreover, the sinc function arises naturally as the Fourier transform of the momentum-space eigenfunctions of a particle confined in a finite box
[-L/2,L/2]
, and in coordinate space it represents a band-limited function—properties that further support its suitability for problems involving extended or delocalized interactions.

In cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED), the photon field of a single-mode optical cavity is modeled as a quantum harmonic oscillator with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{p}^{2}+\omega^{2}\hat{q}^{2}\right)=\omega\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

where
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
are the canonical coordinate and momentum operators of the photon mode,
\omega
is the cavity frequency, and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
,
\hat{a}
are the corresponding creation and annihilation operators. Conventionally, the photon wavefunction is expanded in the eigenbasis of the harmonic oscillator (i.e., Hermite–Gaussian functions)
[
1
]
. However, in the strong-coupling or non-perturbative regime—where the interaction takes the form
\hat{q}\cdot\hat{\mu}
with
\hat{\mu}
the dipole operator—the photon wavefunction in
q
-space can develop non-Gaussian, multi-peaked, or spatially extended structures. Under such conditions, the Hermite basis converges slowly
[
2
]
, motivating the exploration of alternative representations.

In this work, we develop a real-space approach to cavity quantum chemistry based on the sinc basis. We begin by reviewing the Hartree–Fock framework in Section 2 and the Roothaan equations in Section 3. In Section 4, we introduce the sinc discretization and compare its performance against conventional basis sets. Section 5 discusses existing strategies for incorporating cavity effects into electronic structure theory. Finally, in Section 6, we analyze the electron–photon coupling and its manifestation as energy splittings in excited states. Technical details of the implementation, including efficient algorithms for tensor handling, are provided in the Appendix.

2.
Hartree-Fock Method

According to
[
8
]
, consider the Hamiltonian on
\bigotimes^{N_{e}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}h_{i}+\frac{1}{2}j_{0}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{e}}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{nu}}\frac{j_{0}}{\left|r_{i}-R_{k}\right|}\right)+\frac{1}{2}j_{0}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{e}}\frac{1}{\left|r_{i}-r_{j}\right|}

where
h_{i}
is the single-electron operator consisting of the kinetic energy and the interaction between electrons and nuclei, and
j_{0}
is the Coulomb constant. This many-body Hamiltonian discribe the interaction between
N_{e}
electrons with positions
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{e}}
and
N_{nu}
nuclei with positions
\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{nu}}
.

Let spatial orbitals denote
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{nu}}
that are the orthonormal basis in Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and for each spatial orbital
\varphi_{i}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then the Hartree-Fock(HF) ground state eigenfunction by Slater determinant is

\Psi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N_{e}})=\left(\frac{1}{N_{e}!}\right)^{1/2}\operatorname{det}\left|\varphi_{1}(x_{1})\varphi_{2}(x_{2})\ldots\varphi_{N_{SO}}(x_{N_{e}})\right|

where we denote the
n
th electron occupying
i
th orbital by
\varphi_{i}(x_{n})
and assume that the number of electrons equates to number of spinorbitals, i.e.
N_{e}=N_{SO}
. Corresponding HF energy is

E_{HF}=\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle

By Condon-Slater rules, the expectation of one-body operator is

\langle\Psi,\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}h_{i}\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}\langle\varphi_{i}(x_{1}),h_{i}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\rangle

and the expectation of two-body operator is

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi,\frac{1}{2}j_{0}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{e}}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}\Psi\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}j_{0}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{e}}\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})\varphi_{j}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\varphi_{i}(x_{2})-\varphi_{j}(x_{1})\varphi_{i}(x_{2})\right\}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}

We use the notation for short

(2.1)

\left(\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}\mid\varphi_{c}\varphi_{d}\right)=j_{0}\int\varphi_{a}^{*}(x_{1})\varphi_{b}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\varphi_{c}(x_{1})\varphi_{d}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}

Thus, the HF energy can be written as

E_{HF}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left\langle\varphi_{i},h_{1}\varphi_{i}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{e}}\left\{\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\right)-\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{j}\varphi_{i}\right)\right\}

By the variational principle, for some constants
\varepsilon_{i,j}
that are Lagrange multiplier, we have

f_{1}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{e}}\varepsilon_{i,j}\varphi_{j}(x_{1})

where
f_{1}
is the Fock operator

f_{1}=h_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{e}}\left\{J_{j}(x_{2})-K_{j}(x_{2})\right\}

with
J_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})
denotes the Coulomb operator,

J_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})=j_{0}\int\varphi_{j}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\varphi_{j}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})

and
K_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})
denotes the exchange operator,

K_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})=j_{0}\int\varphi_{j}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\varphi_{i}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\varphi_{j}(x_{1})

However, the Hartree-Fock equation for the even number of electrons can be described as closed-shell, where the number of electrons is twice the number of spinorbitals, i.e.
N_{e}=2N_{SO}
. The ground state eigenfunction by Slater determinant is

\displaystyle\Psi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{N_{e}})=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{N_{e}!}\right)^{1/2}\operatorname{det}\left|\varphi_{1}(x_{1})\varphi_{1}(x_{2})\ldots\varphi_{N_{SO}}(x_{N_{e}-1})\varphi_{N_{SO}}(x_{N_{e}})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{N_{e}!}\right)^{1/2}\operatorname{det}\left|\varphi_{1}\overline{\varphi_{1}}\varphi_{2}\overline{\varphi_{2}}\ldots\varphi_{N_{SO}}\overline{\varphi_{N_{SO}}}\right|

Then, the two-electron terms in the expression of the energy is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{e}}\left\{\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\right)-\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{j}\varphi_{i}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{SO}}\left\{\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\right)-\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{j}\varphi_{i}\right)\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{SO}}\left\{\left(\overline{\varphi_{i}}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mid\overline{\varphi_{i}}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\right)-\left(\overline{\varphi_{i}}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mid\overline{\varphi_{j}}\overline{\varphi_{i}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{SO}}\left\{\left(\varphi_{i}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mid\varphi_{i}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\right)-\left(\varphi_{i}\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mid\overline{\varphi_{j}}\varphi_{i}\right)\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{SO}}\left\{\left(\overline{\varphi_{i}}\varphi_{j}\mid\overline{\varphi_{i}}\varphi_{j}\right)-\left(\overline{\varphi_{i}}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{j}\overline{\varphi_{i}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{N_{SO}}\left\{2\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\right)-\left(\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mid\varphi_{j}\varphi_{i}\right)\right\}

Thus, the Hartree-Fock equation for a spinorbital
\varphi_{i}
occupied by electron 1 in a closed-shell atom is

\left\{h_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{SO}}\left(2J_{j}(x_{2})-K_{j}(x_{2})\right)\right\}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})=\varepsilon_{i}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})

where the constant
\varepsilon_{i}
is the one-electron orbital energy. It can be calculated by

\varepsilon_{i}=\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})h_{1}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{SO}}(2J_{ij}-K_{ij})

where

J_{ij}=\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})J_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}=j_{0}\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})\left(\int\varphi_{j}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\varphi_{j}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\right)\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

and

K_{ij}=\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})K_{j}(x_{2})\varphi_{i}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}=j_{0}\int\varphi_{i}^{*}(x_{1})\left(\int\varphi_{j}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\varphi_{i}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\right)\varphi_{j}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

We summarize that the orbital energies are eigenvalues of the Fock operator,

f_{1}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})=\varepsilon_{i}\varphi_{i}(x_{1})

where

\varepsilon_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left\langle\varphi_{i},h_{1}\varphi_{i}\right\rangle+\sum_{i,j}^{N_{e}}\left(J_{ij}-K_{ij}\right)

Note that the Hartree-Fock energy is not equal to the sum of occupied orbital energies, i.e.

E_{HF}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}\left\langle\varphi_{i},h_{1}\varphi_{i}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{N_{e}}\left(J_{ij}-K_{ij}\right)

However, Hartree-Fock method can not consider the correlation between electrons, so the total energy calculated by Hartree-Fock method is usually higher than the real ground state energy. When we consider the electron-photon coupling, the correlation energy is contributed to the total energy, which we will discuss it later.

3.
Roothaan equations

Let
\{\psi_{m}\}
be the set of orbital eigenfunctions, for the closed-shell system with doubly occupied orbitalsz, the Hartree-Fock equation is

f_{1}\psi_{m}(x_{1})=\varepsilon_{m}\psi_{m}(x_{1})

where the Fock operator

f_{1}=h_{1}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}\left(2J_{m^{\prime}}(x_{2})-K_{m^{\prime}}(x_{2})\right)

and the Coulomb operator
J_{m^{\prime}}
and exchange operator
K_{m^{\prime}}
are defined as before. As we have already discussed in the cases of hydrogen molecule-ino and hydrogen molecule, the orbital
\psi_{m}
can be considered as the linear combination of some basis set, like LCAO. Here we assume the basis set
\{\chi_{o}\}_{o=1}^{N_{o}}
, where
N_{o}
is the number of basis, such that

(3.1)

\psi_{m}(x_{1})=\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}c_{om}\chi_{o}(x_{1})

for some constant
c_{om}
. Then, the Hartree-Fock equation can be written as

f_{1}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}c_{om}\chi_{o}(x_{1})=\varepsilon_{m}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}c_{om}\chi_{o}(x_{1})

Take the distribution in senece of
\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}
,

\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}c_{om}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}(x_{1})^{*}f_{1}\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}=\varepsilon_{m}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}c_{om}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

Using the notation of matrix
F
and
S
, the above equation can be written as

\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}F_{o^{\prime}o}c_{om}=\varepsilon_{m}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{o}}S_{o^{\prime}o}c_{om}

where
F_{o^{\prime},o}
and
S_{o^{\prime},o}
are the matrix elements of the
F
and
S
, respectively. We define
F
denotes the Fock matrix with respect to Coulomb integral and resonance integral, also
S
denotes the overlap matrix with respect to overlap integral. If the number of orbitals with index
m
is equal to the number of basis set
N_{o}
, then we can write the above equation as matrix form,

Fc=\varepsilon Sc

where
\varepsilon
is the diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{m}
on the diagonal. This equation is called the Roothaan equation. The solution of the Roothaan equation is the set of coefficients
c_{om}
, which can be used to construct the orbital
\psi_{m}
.

If
c
is not trivial, then

det(F-\varepsilon S)=0

Consider the component of
F
,

\displaystyle F_{o^{\prime}o}=

\displaystyle\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})f_{1}\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\left(h_{1}+\sum_{m}\left(2J_{m}(x_{2})-K_{m}(x_{2})\right)\right)\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})h_{1}\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

\displaystyle+2j_{0}\sum_{m}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\left(\int\psi_{m}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\psi_{m}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\right)\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

\displaystyle-j_{0}\sum_{m}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\left(\int\psi_{m}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\chi_{o}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\right)\psi_{m}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

Here, we use the linear combination of basis, i.e.
\psi_{m}^{*}=\sum_{q=1}^{N_{o}}c_{qm}^{*}\chi_{q}^{*}
and
\psi_{m}=\sum_{r=1}^{N_{o}}c_{rm}\chi_{r}
, then

\displaystyle F_{o^{\prime}o}=

\displaystyle\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})h_{1}\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}

\displaystyle+2j_{0}\sum_{m,q,r}c_{qm}^{*}c_{rm}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\chi_{q}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\chi_{o}(x_{1})\chi_{r}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}

\displaystyle-j_{0}\sum_{m,q,r}c_{qm}^{*}c_{rm}\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})\chi_{q}^{*}(x_{2})\left(\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right)\chi_{r}(x_{1})\chi_{o}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{o^{\prime}o}+\sum_{m,q,r}c_{qm}^{*}c_{rm}\{2\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{o}\chi_{r}\right]-\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{r}\chi_{o}\right]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{o^{\prime}o}+\sum_{q,r}P_{qr}\{2\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{o}\chi_{r}\right]-\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{o}\chi_{r}\right]\}

with the notation defined in equation (
2.1
) and

h_{o^{\prime}o}=\int\chi_{o^{\prime}}^{*}(x_{1})h_{1}\chi_{o}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}\tau_{1},\qquad P_{qr}=\sum_{m}c_{qm}^{*}c_{rm}

We call the matrix
P
as the density matrix consisting of the elements
P_{qr}
. When
q=r
, the element
P_{qq}
is the electron density on atom
q
. When
q\neq r
, the element
P_{qr}
is the bond order between atom
q
and atom
r
. Sum of
m
is depends on the orbitals occupied by electron. Recall the liniear combination of basis in equation (
3.1
), the density operator can be written as

\left[\rho_{m}\right]_{o^{\prime}o}=\left|\psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|_{o^{\prime}o}=c_{o^{\prime}m}^{*}c_{om}\left|\chi_{o^{\prime}}\right\rangle\left\langle\chi_{o}\right|.

So, the sum of orbital energies can be calculated by

\sum_{m}\varepsilon_{m}=\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[F\rho_{m}]=\sum_{m}\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}c_{o^{\prime}m}^{*}c_{om}F_{o^{\prime}o}=\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}F_{o^{\prime}o}.

Similarly, for HF energy of closed-shell, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle E_{HF}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}h_{o^{\prime}o}+\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}\sum_{q,r}P_{qr}\{2\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{o}\chi_{r}\right]-\left[\chi_{o^{\prime}}\chi_{q}\mid\chi_{o}\chi_{r}\right]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}h_{o^{\prime}o}+\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}\left(F_{o^{\prime}o}-h_{o^{\prime}o}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{o^{\prime},o}^{N_{o}}P_{o^{\prime}o}\left(h_{o^{\prime}o}+F_{o^{\prime}o}\right)

Once we have the density matrix
P
, we can get the HF energy by equation (
3.2
) and the density of electrons. We will discuss in next section with exact basis set.

4.
Lagrange-sinc functions

The sinc function on a grid point
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

L_{i}^{sinc}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\frac{\sin\left[\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h\right]}{\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h}

with scaling factor
h
. The roots of a sinc function form a set of grid points
\{x_{j}\}
with uniforms pacing
h
. We have the relations as

L_{i}^{sinc}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}\qquad\int L_{i}^{sinc}(x)L_{j}^{sinc}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{ij}

Let us consider the one-dimensional case. First, denote the sinc function as

f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\frac{\sin\left[\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h\right]}{\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h}

Figure 1.
three sinc functions:
f_{-5}(x)
,
f_{0}(x)
,
f_{2.5}(x)
with spacing
h=0.1

It is easy to show
\{f_{i}(x)\}
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is an orthonormal basis, because

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(x)}{x}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}(x)}{x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\pi

In our inplementation, we use the one dimensional sinc function as a basis function to represent the spinorbitals
\varphi_{i}(x)
,

\varphi_{i}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{ki}f_{k}(x)

where
c_{ki}
is the coefficient and
N
is the number of grid points. We can calculate the overlap integral by the property of sinc function as

S_{ij}=\int f_{i}(x)f_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x=f_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}

where
\lambda_{k}
is the weight of the grid point
x_{k}
. To obtain the kinetic energy, we use the finite-difference method

\nabla^{2}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{{\mathrm{d}x}^{2}}\left(\psi(x+{\mathrm{d}x})-2\psi(x)+\psi(x-{\mathrm{d}x})\right)

for any function
\psi(x)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and in our case,
\mathrm{d}x
is the difference between two sinc functions on the grid points. To calculate the expectation of the kinetic energy, we first consider the Fourier transform of the sinc function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[f_{i}(x)](k)=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i}(x)e^{-ikx}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\left[\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h\right]}{\pi\left(x-x_{i}\right)/h}e^{-ikx}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-ix_{i}k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}e^{-i\frac{h}{\pi}kx}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-ix_{i}k}\mathcal{F}\left[\frac{\sin(x)}{x}\right]\left(\frac{kh}{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-ix_{i}k}\pi\cdot\text{rect}\left(\frac{kh}{2\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\cdot e^{-ikx_{i}}\cdot\text{rect}\left(\frac{kh}{2\pi}\right)

where
\text{rect}(x)
is the rectangular function defined as

rect(x)=\frac{1}{2}(sgn(1-x)+sgn(1+x))=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{2\sin(x)}{x}\right)

By Parseval’s theorem, the expectation of the kinetic energy on diagonal is

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{i}\mid-\nabla^{2}f_{i}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i}^{*}(x)\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}[f_{i}^{*}](k)\mathcal{F}[-\nabla^{2}f_{i}](k)\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|rect\left(\frac{kh}{2\pi}\right)\right|^{2}k^{2}\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{h}}^{\frac{\pi}{h}}k^{2}\mathrm{d}k=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3h^{2}}

Thus,

T_{ii}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\cdot\left\langle f_{i}\mid-\nabla^{2}f_{i}\right\rangle=\frac{\hbar^{2}\pi^{2}}{6m_{e}h^{2}}

For non-diagonal elements, by the finite-difference method as above, we have the approximation

T_{ij}\approx\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\cdot(-1)^{j-i}\cdot\frac{2}{(x_{j}-x_{i})^{2}}

where
m_{e}
is the mass of electron. The Hamiltonian matrix refer to one-electron operator is defined as

H_{ij}=T_{ij}+V_{ij}

Corresponding to

\sum_{i}^{N_{e}}\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\nabla_{i}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{nu}}\frac{j_{0}}{\left|r_{i}-R_{k}\right|}\right)

where
V_{ij}
is the interaction between electrons and nuclei. It is consisted of attraction and repulsion terms. And the attraction between the nuclei with position
R
and electron with position
r_{i}
is

v_{i}=-\frac{j_{0}}{\sqrt{(r_{i}-R)^{2}}+\epsilon^{2}}

where
\epsilon
is respected to soft-core Coulomb potential to avoid infinity. We use the fact that the sinc function is only estimated around the grid points. So, we can approximate the expectation as

V_{ii}=\left\langle f_{i},v_{i}f_{i}\right\rangle=v_{i}

and for the non-diagonal elements, we take the average of the attraction terms,

V_{ij}=\left\langle f_{i},\frac{1}{2}(v_{i}+v_{j})f_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(v_{i}+v_{j})S_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(v_{i}+v_{j})\delta_{ij}

where
S_{ij}
is the overlap integral which is identity matrix in our case. The results can be represented in the following figures

Figure 2.
f_{2}(x)\frac{1}{x}f_{2}(x)
,
f_{3}(x)\frac{1}{x}f_{3}(x)
,
f_{4}(x)\frac{1}{x}f_{4}(x)
with spacing
h=0.01

It shows that by decreasing the spacing
h
, the potential becomes more and more concentrated around the grid points.

Before building the Fock matrix, we first need to calculate the double electrons integral. First let us assume the positions of two electrons are
r_{1}
and

6.
Energy level splitting

As we discussed last section, if the electron-photon coupling term is not contributed to energy, then we only get the energy shift by the polarization term. However, when there is electron-photon coupling, the energy levels will split. Here we use the configuration interaction method to estimate the energy levels. Recall HF ground state,

\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{e}!}}\left|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{N_{e}}\right\rangle.

We define the single excited state as

\left|\Psi_{a}^{r}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{e}!}}\left|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{a-1}\varphi_{r}\varphi_{a+1}\cdots\varphi_{N_{e}}\right\rangle

and the double excited state as

\left|\Psi_{ab}^{rs}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{e}!}}\left|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\cdots\varphi_{a-1}\varphi_{r}\varphi_{a+1}\cdots\varphi_{b-1}\varphi_{s}\varphi_{b+1}\cdots\varphi_{N_{e}}\right\rangle

Then the configuration interaction singles and doubles (CISD) wave function is defined as

\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle=C_{0}\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle+\sum_{ar}C_{a}^{r}\left|\Psi_{a}^{r}\right\rangle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{a<b}\\
{r<s}\end{subarray}}C_{ab}^{rs}\left|\Psi_{ab}^{rs}\right\rangle

We define the exact energy as

E_{exact}=\min_{\Phi_{0}\in\bigotimes^{N_{e}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\langle\Phi_{0}\left|\mathcal{H}\right|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

Note that the exact energy is different from the HF energy, we use CI to approximate the electron correlation energy.

Now, let us consider the configuration interaction singles(CIS). First, we define the single excited CI wave function as

is the number of occupied orbitals and
m
is the number of unoccupied orbitals. By Brillouin’s theorem, we have

\left\langle\Psi_{0}\left|\mathcal{H}\right|\Psi_{a}^{r}\right\rangle=0

and

Appendix A
implementation of sinc basis function

Our implementation of the cavity Hartree–Fock method relies on a real-space sinc basis and exploits the sparsity of electron–electron interactions to achieve feasible computational scaling. The key numerical components—evaluation of two-electron integrals and construction of the Fock matrix—are described below.

A.1.
Sparse Evaluation of Two-Electron Integrals

The two-electron repulsion integrals
(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)
in the sinc basis are approximated using an effective point-charge model. For basis functions centered at grid points
\{x_{i}\}
, the electron pair associated with
(\mu,\nu)
is represented by its midpoint
r_{\mu\nu}=(x_{\mu}+x_{\nu})/2
, and similarly for
(\lambda,\sigma)
. The integral is then approximated as

(A.1)

(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)\approx\frac{j_{0}}{\sqrt{(r_{\mu\nu}-r_{\lambda\sigma})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\cdot S_{\mu\nu}S_{\lambda\sigma},

where
S_{\mu\nu}
is the overlap matrix element,
j_{0}
is the effective Coulomb coupling strength, and
\varepsilon=0.07
is a softening parameter that regularizes the singularity at zero separation. A numerically stable special case is used when
\mu=\nu
and
\lambda=\sigma
.

To control computational cost, a Schwarz inequality-based screening criterion is applied:

(A.2)

\sqrt{(\mu\mu|\nu\nu)(\lambda\lambda|\sigma\sigma)}\geq\tau,

with threshold
\tau=10^{-10}
. Integrals failing this test are discarded. The remaining non-zero elements are computed in chunks over the
\mu
index (e.g., chunk size
C=10
) to limit memory usage, and stored in COO (coordinate) sparse format as a four-rank tensor of size
N^{4}
, where
N
is the number of basis functions and also the grid points.

A.2.
Fock Matrix Construction via Sparse Tensor Contraction

The Fock matrix incorporates the one-electron Hamiltonian, mean-field electron–electron interactions, and cavity-induced corrections (including dipole self-energy terms). It is given by

(A.3)

F_{\mu\nu}=H_{\mu\nu}+H_{c,\mu\nu}+\sum_{\lambda\sigma}\left[2(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)-(\mu\lambda|\nu\sigma)\right]P_{\lambda\sigma}+J^{\mathrm{DSE}}_{\mu\nu}-K^{\mathrm{DSE}}_{\mu\nu},

where
H
is the molecular core Hamiltonian,
H_{c}
accounts for direct electron–photon coupling,
P
is the density matrix, and
J^{\mathrm{DSE}}
,
K^{\mathrm{DSE}}
are the Coulomb- and exchange-like contributions from the dipole self-energy.

The two-electron contribution is evaluated without materializing the full
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
tensor. Instead, the antisymmetrized interaction tensor

V_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}=(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mu\lambda|\nu\sigma)

is handled implicitly by transposing the precomputed sparse integral tensor. The contraction

G_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{\lambda\sigma}V_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}P_{\lambda\sigma}

is performed by iterating over the non-zero entries of
V
in COO format: for each
(\mu,\nu,\lambda,\sigma)
with value
v
, the product
v\cdot P_{\lambda\sigma}
is accumulated into
F_{\mu\nu}
via a scatter-add operation.

Finally, the Fock matrix is optionally accelerated using the Direct Inversion in the Iterative Subspace (DIIS) method
[
5
]
to enhance SCF convergence. This entire pipeline ensures that both memory consumption and computational effort scale with the number of significant integrals—rather than
N^{4}
—making the approach tractable for moderate-sized systems in the sinc basis.
