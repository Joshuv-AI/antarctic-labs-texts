Dysregulated oligodendrocyte and myelin dynamics as an early pathological feature of neuropil degeneration in Alzheimer’s disease: an ultrastructural study
Authors: Alev Erisir, Erin E Maher, Zoe Anderson, Sahil Chawla, Lauren Hanley, Annie Zhao, Kerem Birisik, Ezgi Sila Toklucu, Elif Nedret Keskinoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder traditionally defined by the accumulation of amyloid‑β plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. Increasing evidence suggests that white‑matter degeneration and myelin disruption occur early in disease progression and may contribute to neuropathological vulnerability. Here, we performed ultrastructural analyses in the 3xTg and 5xFAD mouse models of AD across developmental stages (3–12 months of age), including ages preceding overt amyloid plaque formation or neuronal loss. We identify a spectrum of oligodendrocyte‑ and myelin‑associated abnormalities, including single‑membrane herniations, myelin outfolds, and ectopic myelination of neuronal processes, which are evident as early as 3 months of age and are frequently associated with altered neuropil architecture and incipient dystrophic neurite morphology. These malformations were confirmed to be oligodendrocyte‑derived through O4 immunolabeling. Collectively, our findings reveal early, widespread myelin‑associated ultrastructural alterations that form a consistent structural component of neuritic pathology in AD models. We propose that dysregulated oligodendrocyte membrane remodeling represents an early pathological feature of AD, providing a framework for future studies examining how glial pathology intersects with neuronal degeneration and plaque‑associated neuritic remodeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02320-z.Keywords: EM, Alzheimer’s models, Ultrastructural pathology, Myelin, Oligodendrocyte

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative condition traditionally defined by two hallmark lesions observed at late stages: extracellular neuritic plaques, clusters of amyloid-β (Aβ) surrounded by damaged neuritic processes, and neurofibrillary tangles, intracellular aggregates of insoluble cytoskeletal elements, including phosphorylated tau protein [1–5]. While most research has focused on protracted amyloid accumulation and tau pathologies as the primary drivers of neural deficits in clinical populations and transgenic animal models, recent studies highlight additional contributors, including DNA damage, cell cycle dysregulation, neuroinflammation, lysosomal and mitochondrial dysfunction and myelin regression [6–11]. Notably, myelin appears to play a prominent role in AD pathology [12–15], supported by evidence that AD patients display white matter degeneration that often precedes the development of neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaques [16, 17]. Human imaging and transgenic mouse studies consistently demonstrate microstructural and macrostructural myelin disruptions [18–22], implicating oligodendrocytes (OD) as potential early players in AD pathology. Early white matter alterations could account for cognitive deficits that emerge prior to widespread neurodegeneration, as disruptions in myelin and axonal integrity can impair action potential conduction and also promote downstream neuropathological changes characteristic of Alzheimer’s disease [23–27]. In this context, dysfunction of oligodendrocyte lineage cells, including oligodendrocyte precursor cells and mature oligodendrocytes, may play a critical role, as these cells are essential for maintaining myelin integrity, axonal metabolic support, and white matter homeostasis [24, 28].

Despite well documented caveats [29, 30], transgenic mouse models that mimic the genetic conditions of early-onset AD provide valuable opportunities to assess AD-associated pathogenesis. Although timeline for neurobiological alterations varies across models, an important feature of human AD is recapitulated in most models: behaviorally evident cognitive changes precede progressive cellular damage, and thus precedes the clinical diagnosis [19]. For example, in the 3xTg model, hippocampus-dependent impairments are evident around postnatal month (PM) 4–5, approximately 8–10 months before amyloid plaques and cellular degeneration are detected [31]. This window provides an opportunity to investigate pathological changes that correlate more closely with cognitive decline. To survey the structural alterations that emerge before ages when hallmark signs of AD can be detected, we used electron microscopy, which allows direct visualization of affected cellular compartments. Using 3xTg and 5xFAD mouse brains at ages 3–12 months, we aimed to identify the emergence of structural alterations involving amyloid plaques, vascular and intracellular amyloid deposits, intracellular fibril bundles, multivesicular bodies, myelin and degeneration in dendrites, axons, mitochondria, and other organelles. Our findings highlight pronounced alterations involving myelin and oligodendrocyte membranes at pre-plaque ages, implicating oligodendrocytes as primary contributors to AD pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Animals

Twenty-four 3xTG (3 animals each at PM3, PM5 and PM8, and one animal at PM12), harboring the human amyloid precursor protein Swedish mutant transgene, presenilin knock-in mutation, and tau P301L mutant transgene (#034830-JAX; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) was used. In addition, aged-matched wild-type C57BL/6 mice (3 animals each at PM3, PM5, PM8, and one animal at PM40) were used to document baseline ultrastructural features of healthy adult brain tissue and to establish reference morphology, rather than as strain‑matched controls for genotype‑dependent comparisons. Homozygous transgenic animals (15–21 days old) and nursing dams were purchased and maintained under protocols approved by the University of Virginia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The tissue samples used in 5xFAD analysis were produced for an independent study by Dr. Kesinoz in Acibadem University, Turkey, and included three PM6 and three PM9 transgenic mice. The 5xFAD breeder mice that contained Swedish, Florida and London mutant transgenes of amyloid precursor protein, and two presenilin1 mutations of Familial Alzheimer’s Disease (#034840-JAX) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and bred in colonies at Acibadem Mehmet Ali Aydinlar University, under protocols approved by Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (ACU-HADYEK 2019/46).

Tissue preparation

Mice were anesthetized with an overdose of euthasole, and transcardially perfused with a peristaltic pump maintaining a steady stream of return from the right atrium. The perfusates were 100 ml of Tyrodes buffer (137 mM NaCl, 5.5 mM Dextrose/Glucose, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM KCl, 0.4 mM NaH2PO4, 0.9 mM CaCl2, 11.9 mM NaHCO3, in 1L dH20; warmed close to mammalian body temperature), delivered in 2–3 min, followed by 300mls of 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1M PB (pH 7.4) at room temperature. Brains were postfixed overnight, sectioned at 60 μm using a vibratome (Leica) and collected in 0.1 phosphate buffer (PB). The sections were treated with 1% sodium borohydride to terminate cross-linking action of aldehydes, facilitating subsequent immunostaining experiments. Tissue then was transferred in 0.01 M PBS buffer containing 0.05% sodium azide for long term storage.

Immunohistochemistry

For visualization of oligodendrocyte related markers, the sections were pre-incubated for 30 min in 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton-X, 1% BSA in 0.01 M PB, and then transferred to monoclonal antibody anti-O4 (Cat#: MAB345, Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA) at 1:50 dilution in 0.1 M PBS containing 1% BSA, 0.05% sodium azide and 0.3% TritonX (for light microscopy) for 48 h at room temperature on a shaker. For immunoelectron microscopy, Triton-X omitted from the antibody incubation buffer. After multiple rinses in PBS, sections were treated with an anti-mouse secondary antibody conjugated to biotin (1:50 in PBS; Vector Labs., Newark, CA, USA) for 2 h, followed by treatment with avidin–biotin-complex (ABC; Vector) solution for 2 h. The sections were then rinsed with 0.01 M PBS and incubated in 0.02% hydrogen peroxide and 0.05% diaminobenzidine for 2–5 min. The sections prepared for light microscopy were mounted on subbed slides and coverslipped with DPX mounting media; other sections were embedded for electron microscopy.

Electron microscopy tissue preparation

Immunolabeled or unlabeled sections were fixed for 1 h in OsO4 before they are treated in 1% UA in 70% ethanol solution overnight in the refrigerator. The sections were then dehydrated in 70%, 90% and 100% alcohol series, treated in acetone twice for 10 min, followed by 1:1 mixture of acetone and EPON (Electron Microscopy Sciences; Hatfield, PA, USA) and pure EPON overnight each. Resin unfused sections were flat-embedded between two acetate sheets (Aclar; EMS; Hatfield, PA, USA) and allowed to polymerize at 30C for two days. Flat-embedded sections were examined on the light microscope, regions of interest excised and placed on the cap of BEEM capsules, which were subsequently filled with more resin and polymerized in oven for 2 more days. Blocks containing hippocampus CA1 and CA3, corpus collosum, fimbria, entorhinal cortex, basal forebrain were cut using and ultramicrotome (Leica UTC7) at 60–80 nm thickness.

Imaging and preparation of figures

Ultrathin sections were examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL 1010) equipped with a 16‑MP camera (SIA) and imaged at magnifications of 5000–15,000 × . At these magnifications, the effective image resolution ranged from 2.0 to 0.8 nm per pixel. For high resolution visualization of large areas, the field was imaged in minimally overlapping tiles at high magnification. The archive of digital electron micrographs examined in the current study comprises over 5000 images; representative original images will be made available at LabArchives upon publication. Manuscript figures were assembled using Photoshop (Adobe Creative Cloud). Tiled images were overlaid manually for composite scene visualization, and the gray levels in each tile were adjusted to match the transition regions. No other image adjustments, including counterstain artifact removal or AI-assisted edits, were performed. Prism (GraphPad) was used for composing graphs and for statistical analyses.

Classification criteria for normal and pathological morphology

For identification ultrastructural features of brain morphology and terminology, we referred to The Fine Structure of the Nervous System [32], The Atlas of Ultrastructural Neurocytology at SynapseWeb (https://synapseweb.clm.utexas.edu/atlas), and Fine Structure of the Aging Brain (https://www.bu.edu/agingbrain/) [33]. Typical myelinated axon morphology displays compact myelin surrounding an axon cross-section that does not contain any degeneration products (eg., dark granules), herniations or outfolds. In most cases leading edge of the myelinating oligodendrocyte is also discernable at the innermost layer. Myelin is multiple layers of lipid membrane, leading to a uniform thickness, which can vary based on the axon diameter, neuron type and the white matter tract.

Several features of myelin and myelination patterns that are common in young brains were also observed in 3xTg transgenic brains: 1) When a set of compact myelin lamellae seem to wrap around an already myelinated axon, they are classified as double or triple myelin, or in general, as myelin whorls. Such structures, which have been reported in aging brains as well as in various neurodegenerative conditions [34–36], were characterized by an extracellular space of non-uniform thickness between rounds of thick myelin sheaths. This morphology is consistent with the secondary wrapping of an already myelinated axon. To note, milder forms of such structures are also classified as uncompacting myelin or double-sheathed axon, or split myelin in other studies [34, 36]. 2) An outfold was identified when a stack of myelin formed a folded extension projecting away from the central axon. To be classified as an outfold, the structure was required to contain at least two distinct myelin stacks, with the innermost myelin sheath of each stack oriented toward the other. The oufold extended from the myelin sheath surrounding the axon, by a distance greater than at least the thickness of the original myelin, such that the folded configuration was readily apparent. Myelin outfolds are commonly observed in developing brains as well as in neurodegenerative states [18, 37, 38]. Similar structures encountered in aging brains are also described as redundant myelinbrain [35, 39]. 3) A myelin herniation was identified when one or several myelin membranes protruded either into the central axon or outward into the surrounding extracellular space or neuropil [39]. In cases where the point of origin of such aberrant membranes would not be clearly determined, the structure was classified as a membranous whirl based on the appearance of loosely packed membranes arranged in a spiral configuration. 4) A myelin bulb was identified as relatively large, rounded structure enclosed by myelin and containing heterogeneous intracellular material. These inclusions included degenerative elements such as electron-dense or dysmorphic mitochondria, membrane-bound degeneration products, membranous whirls and other unidentifiable cellular components, as well as morphologically intact organelles.

Results

Morphological changes in cell bodies and dendrites

Cellular degeneration

To establish a timeline for cellular and neuropil degeneration due to overexpression of amyloid and tau in the 3xTg model, we examined brains from 3-, 5-, 8-, and 12- month old (PM3, PM5, P8, and PM12) 3xTg mice. Aged-matched C57BL/6 mice at PM3, PM5, PM8, and PM40 were used to define normal brain ultrastructure. Brain regions studied included hippocampus CA1, C3 and dentate gyrus, entorhinal cortex, basal forebrain, and sensory cortices. For each condition, at least one ultrathin section (approximately 1 × 2mm) containing each brain region was examined from a minimum of two animals, and cellular morphology was assessed via systematic field sweeps.

Across all regions and ages examined in both the transgenic and WT brains, overtly degenerated cell bodies were encountered only rarely. A notable exception was observed in the basal forebrain of 3xTg PM8 mice, where neurons exhibited multiple ultrastructural features consistent with oxidative degeneration, including disruption of nuclear membrane, cytoplasm darkening, poorly differentiated mitochondria, vacuolization, dystrophic elements and dilated endoplasmic reticulum (Supp. Figure 1–2). While no cytoplasmic darkening and other signs of degeneration were observed in other areas, ultrastructural signs of impaired cellular health, such as vacuoles in somatic cytoplasm and nuclear karyoplasm, were evident in 3xTg PM8 and PM12 brains (Fig. 1C). Cells with double nucleoli were also frequently encountered in these brains (Fig. 1C-D).

Lipofuscin accumulation

More subtle aging-related features, such as cytoplasmic lysosomes and lipofuscin granules, were commonly observed both in 3xTg and wild-type brains. These structures appeared as large lipid droplets and irregularly shaped, electron-dense components embedded within a proteinaceous matrix composed of elements with varying electron density (Fig. 1A-C). To assess whether amyloid overexpression accelerates lipofuscin accumulation in somatic cytoplasm of 3xTg neurons, we imaged 20–25 CA1 somata from PM3, PM5, PM8, and PM12 3xTg brains and from PM3, PM8, and PM40 WT brains, quantifying the cytoplasmic area occupied by lipofuscin in each cell (Fig. 1D–E). Lipofuscin content increased progressively in both WT (PM3: 0.79 ± 0.25; PM5: 1.39 ± 0.26; and PM40: 4.15 ± 0.68) and 3xTg neurons (PM3: 0.59 ± 0.16; PM5: 0.83 ± 0.24; PM8: 0.94 ± 0.26; PM12: 3.05 ± 0.75; Fig. 1E). However, the rate of accumulation in 3xTg cells did not differ significantly from that of WT controls at two matched ages, PM3 or PM8 (Mann–Whitney tests; p = 0.65 and 0.09). These findings suggest that cytoplasmic lipofuscin accumulation reflects normal aging rather than being driven by overexpression of AD-related mutations in 3xTg mice.

In addition to neuronal localization described above, lipid droplets of varying electron density were observed in non‑neuronal compartments of 3xTg brains at all ages, specifically within glial and endothelial cells associated with blood vessels (Fig. 1G). Ependymal cells that line ventricles displayed pronounced accumulation of lipid droplets (Fig. 1 F, Suppl Fig. 3) at all ages, in agreement with studies showing oleic acid accumulation in the 3xTg model [40, 41].

Cytoskeletal changes

Because ultrastructural alterations in dendritic morphology can reflect declining neuronal health and functional impairments, we examined proximal segments of CA1 apical dendrites from PM3-PM12 3xTg brains to assess potential changes in cytoskeletal organization and organelle integrity. Consistent with previous reports showing that phosphorylated tau appears in hippocampal somata around PM6 and progressively fills pyramidal dendrites by PM12 [42], dendritic ultrastructure in PM3 and PM5 3xTg brains was qualitatively indistinguishable from typical dendrite structure encountered in wild-type brains (Fig. 2A, D). In WT CA1 pyramidal cell dendrites, the microtubules within apical dendrites were regularly spaced and aligned parallel to the longitudinal axis of the dendrite (Fig. 2A, C-D). This organization was preserved in PM3 and PM5 3xTg hippocampus, indicating that the learning impairments observed in 3xTg mice by PM5 are unlikely to be attributable to overt ultrastructural abnormalities in dendritic architecture. In contrast, pyramidal cell dendrites in PM8 and PM12 3xTg brains, frequently displayed a tortuous appearance (Fig. 2B, E–F). In these dendrites, the microtubules were more tightly bundled and displaced toward one side of the dendritic shaft, creating cytoplasm pockets largely devoid of microtubules. Mitochondria appearing trapped within these microtubule bundles were often fusiform or electron-dense, while others displayed disorganized and darker inner cristae. Although mitochondria in some dendrites could appear darker at all ages of 3xTg brains, these abnormalities were more abundant in PM8-PM12 brains. Evidence for mitochondrial fission and the presence teardrop mitochondria was also observed (Fig. 2B-F), similar to mitochondrial morphologies reported in other amyloidogenic models [43]. Together, the dendritic ultrastructural abnormalities emerging around PM8 are consistent with a late-onset, tau-mediated degenerative process that may contribute to neuronal dysfunction and eventual cell death in this model [42, 44].

Morphological changes in myelinated axon tracts

While the neuronal alterations observed in PM8 and older 3xTg animals do not temporally align with mechanisms that could explain the cognitive impairments evident by PM5, earlier reports implicate early pathological changes within myelinated tracts [19]. To characterize ultrastructural alterations that involve myelinated fiber pathways, we examined ultrathin sections containing ventral or dorsal external capsule, ventral or dorsal alveus, and fimbria. In these regions, myelinated axons in 3xTg white matter appeared disorganized (Fig. 3A), with frequent formation of vacuoles within the myelin lamellae of individual axons (Fig. 3A). Closer examination of both white matter and adjacent gray matter, revealed multiple forms of myelin disruptions present at all sites as early as PM3, including double or triple myelin, myelin outfolds, ectopic myelination, myelin herniations, and myelin bulbs.

Double or triple myelination consisted of thick myelin sheaths wrapping around already-myelinated axons (Fig. 3B). These profiles were distinct from simply thick myelination, as they displayed cytoplasmic or vacuous spaces between rounds of myelin sheath stacks, indicating a secondary or supernumerary wrapping of a previously myelinated axon. While the axon at the core of abnormal myelination sometimes appeared normal, it more commonly contained degenerative features, including vacuoles, disrupted neurofibrils or electron-dense degeneration products. In some instances, dark neuropil was trapped between the additional the myelin sheaths (Fig. 3B, green arrow).

Myelin outfolds, also commonly referred to as redundant myelin [33, 39, 45] are double folds of already formed thick myelin sheaths extending from their axons into the surrounding extracellular space (Fig. 3C). Although myelin outfolds have been described during early development as indicators of oligodendrocyte motility required for myelination [37], they were rarely observed in the wild-type mouse brain samples even as young as PM3. In contrast, myelin outfolds were encountered frequently in 3xTg brains, and these could extend long distances away from their axon of origin. For example, the myelin outfold emerging from axon 1 in Fig. 3C is approximately 10 μm long. In addition to extending in the extracellular space alongside neighboring axons, outfolds could curve and form a loop around multiple axons or even small glial cells, resulting in ectopic myelination morphology (Fig. 3D). More commonly, myelin outfolds loop around single myelinated axons, leading to double or triple myelination (i.e., axon whorls; Fig. 3B).

We also encountered myelin herniations frequently in 3xTg brains as young as PM3. Thin stacks or infolds of myelin sheath were observed herniating into the axonal structure and forming irregularly shaped knobs within (Fig. 4B-D). Similar myelin protrusions, or herniations, could also be seen emerging from the outside layers of the myelin sheath, herniating into the surrounding neuropil such as dendrites and unmyelined axons (Fig. 4A, Suppl Fig. 2).

Spatiotempotal progression of myelin malformations

One of the defining features of AD pathology is its prion-like spread of cellular degeneration and plaque formation, progressing from basal structures of the brain (e.g., basal forebrain, entorhinal cortex) to more dorsal and cortical areas [46, 47]. In parallel, changes that disrupt hippocampal circuitry are frequently implicated in the emergence of memory deficits [48, 49], suggesting that myelinated axon malformations may arise secondary to somatic degeneration within select brain nuclei. In addition, ependymal alterations have been proposed to contribute to malformations in myelinated tracts lining the ventricular system [41]. To determine whether the myelin abnormalities we observe in 3xTg brains follow these patterns, we quantified the progression of myelin tract defects across three major white matter pathways (Fig. 4E). The external capsule extends along the entire lateral aspect of the cortex and carries axons from subcortical structures, including basal forebrain and striatum, to cortical targets. The alveus lines the dorsal and lateral hippocampus, carrying hippocampal efferents that ultimately travel within fimbria and fornix. Both the alveus and external capsule run adjacent to the lateral ventricle, with ependymal cells forming the interface between cortex and subcortical structures. The fimbria, located along the dorsal, lateral aspect of the hippocampus beside the lateral ventricle, carries hippocampal output to subcortical and septal regions. Importantly, both the alveus and the external capsule contain dorsal and ventral segments, enabling comparisons between tracts situated near the base of the brain versus more dorsal regions.

To test whether myelin malformations are widespread or instead selective for hippocampal, cortical, or basal forebrain circuits, and whether their severity correlates with proximity to the subventricular zone, we imaged myelinated axons in five regions: dorsal alveus, ventral alveus, dorsal external capsule, ventral external capsule, and fimbria in PM3, PM5, PM8, and PM12 3xTg mice. For each tract, we analyzed 8–15 electron micrographs, yielding approximately 250–1000 myelinated axons per region (Table 1). Each myelinated axon cross‑section was classified as either typical (normal) or abnormal. Abnormal profiles were classified as, 1) full outfolds protruding from the axon (Fig. 3C–D), 2) herniations, which included membrane herniations extending into the axoplasm (i.e., infolds, Fig. 4B) and partial outfolds penetrating the neuropil (Fig. 4A–C), and 3) axonal whorls that have multiple myelin stacks (Fig. 3B). Across all white‑matter tracts examined, the proportion of abnormal myelin increased between PM3 and PM12, though with differing magnitudes and time courses (Table 1; Fig. 4E–F). The dorsal and ventral external capsule and the ventral alveus exhibited the highest levels of pathology, reaching 30–40% abnormal axons after PM5. These tracts showed the most pronounced increase in pathology between PM3 and PM5. In contrast, the dorsal alveus and the fimbria, both carrying hippocampal efferents, displayed comparatively milder abnormalities (10–15%). Within the abnormal myelin profiles, herniations, myelin membranes protruding into the resident axons or neighboring neuropil, leading fractionation of the axolemma, were the most common occurrence (Fig. 4F), followed by outfolds and whorls. Overall, both the pattern and progression of myelin pathology are consistent with a basal forebrain–to–frontal spread model of AD pathogenesis: the most severely affected tracts were those originating in, or located ventrally near, basal forebrain regions. Because all tracts displaying pathology lie adjacent to the ventricular system, we cannot exclude the possibility that subventricular zone alterations in transgenic animals contribute to aberrant myelination.

Observations on myelin bulbs

Here, we introduce the term “myelin bulb” not to describe a previously unrecognized pathological structure, but to distinguish a subset of myelin‑ensheathed degenerative profiles that differ ultrastructurally and temporally from classical dystrophic neurites. Classical dystrophic neurites, as defined by ultrastructural criteria including accumulations of vesicular and multilamellar membranes, dense organellar debris, and swollen neuritic processes lacking myelin ensheathment, have been well characterized in AD mouse models and human disease [1, 3, 50, 51]. In contrast to dystrophic neurites, myelin bulbs in the present study were defined by: 1) persistent, often multilayered myelin ensheathment; 2) absence of a clear axonal lead or nodal organization; 3) frequent compartmentalization by additional myelin or intact membranes; and 4) preferential appearance at early, pre‑plaque stages.

Ultrastructural features of myelin bulbs

When we extended survey into the neuropil adjacent to the white matter tracks, we encountered a striking neuropathological structure consistent with this definition, which we refer to as the myelin bulb. These were detected at low magnifications as large, myelinated structures, often positioned within or at the borders of myelinated axon tracts, and extending into neuropil-dense gray matter. At high magnification, myelin bulbs were characterized by thick stacks of myelin encasing membrane‑bound cytoplasm containing axonal, dendritic, or somatic organelles that exhibited varying degrees of cellular distress and degeneration (Fig. 5). In instances where cellular distress appeared relatively, mitochondria appeared darker and thinner than in the surrounding neuropil, and small vacuoles and multilamellar membrane formations were evident (Fig. 5B). More advanced cytoplasmic alterations included multi-vesicular bodies, electron-dense granules of various sizes, dark and degenerated mitochondria, membrane whirls interspersed among microtubule bundles and other cytoplasmic organelles. Some myelin bulbs were completely filled with multilamellar whorls, occasionally continuous with the surrounding myelin sheath, and electron-dense granules. While such profiles share ultrastructural features with dystrophic neurites according to established criteria [33, 50, 52, 53], they differed in that persistent myelin ensheathment was retained, a feature rarely observed in dystrophic neurites at more advanced stages of disease in 3xTg mice, other AD mouse models, or human AD tissue [33, 50, 51, 53, 54]. Accordingly, these structures were not classified as dystrophic neurites in the present study. Moreover, typical dystrophic neurite structure was not evident in 3xTg brains at the ages when myelin bulbs were encountered. The presence of myelin‑ensheathed structures containing partially degenerated cellular components therefore suggests that myelin bulbs may represent a transitional pathological stage that precedes the emergence of fully developed dystrophic neurites.

Comparison with previously described myelin‑associated structures

Structures resembling myelin bulbs, variously categorized as myelin debris, myelin ball or myelin ovoid, have been described in other Alzheimer mice models, oligodendrocyte defective mice as well as in multiple sclerosis [55–57], where they are generally interpreted as manifestations of axonal or somatic degeneration. However, several of ultrastructural features observed here argue that myelin bulbs are not simply enlarged axons resulting from axolemmal displacement of degenerated soma contents. In large cross-sections of myelin bulbs, the leading edge of organization characteristic of myelinated axons, was not readily apparent (Fig. 5A-B). In these instances, the surrounding myelin more closely resembled an outfold rather than de novo axonal myelination. Also consistent with this interpretation, some bulbs displayed multiple compartments separated by additional myelin layers (Fig. 5A) or by intact cellular membranes. Individual compartments often contained distinct neural elements, including large dendrites identified by regularly organized microtubules (Fig. 5C), and exhibited varying degrees of degeneration. These observations support the conclusion that myelin bulbs represent a form of ectopic myelination, potentially involving collections of gray matter neuropil rather than individual axons.

In opportune planes of cross-sections, paranodal-like myelin spirals were occasionally observed encircling and constricting around cell body fragments that retained intact cellular membranes (Fig. 5C). In these profiles, planar myelin sheaths surrounded non‑axonal elements in a configuration resembling the paranodal architecture of myelinated axons, suggesting a mechanism by which portions of neuronal somata or nearby neuropil become progressively enclosed by myelin.

Proposed origin and pathological significance of myelin bulbs

Taken together, the ultrastructural characteristics of large myelin bulbs support the interpretation that neuronal degeneration within these structures may occur secondary to ectopic myelination. In this framework, myelin outfolds or stacks surround neuropil elements, potentially restricting metabolic exchange while simultaneously imposing mechanical constraint. The resulting physical and metabolic stress may contribute to degenerative changes that appear to progress from the periphery toward the center of the enclosed profiles.

For classification purposes, we defined a pathological structure as a myelin bulb only when the size of the structure qualitatively exceeded that of axon cross-sections within the same regions. For example, the diameter of the myelin bulbs in Fig. 5A and B are approximately 10 and 25 mm, respectively, whereas the neighboring axons in the same images range from ~ 1–5 mm in diameter.

Progression of myelin bulb pathology

The earliest myelin bulbs were detected as early as PM5 in 3xTg brains, predominantly in the white matter tracks and the basal forebrain. By PM8, large myelin bulbs could be encountered within the hippocampal gray matter, particularly close to the alveus and external capsule. Together, their large size, abnormal ultrastructural organization, and region‑ and age‑dependent appearance distinguish myelin bulbs from degenerating axonal profiles and point to a progressive pathological process that expands from white matter into adjacent gray‑matter regions over time.

Evidence for ectopic myelination and outfold invasion in gray matter

While myelin outfolds, axon whorls, and aberrant or ectopic myelination were frequently observed in white matter, neuropil‑dense gray‑matter regions of 3xTg brains at all ages also exhibited ultrastructural features consistent with myelin‑associated pathology (Fig. 6–7). In particular, myelin outfolds surrounding and sequestering neurotransmitter vesicle-filled terminals and dendrites were commonly observed in hippocampus and entorhinal cortex (Fig. 6A-C). In opportune cross-sections, myelin outfolds could be observed to surround and partially constrict neighboring processes (Fig. 6D). In these cases, the enclosed neuropil frequently contained degenerative features, suggesting that physical sequestration by myelin outfolds may contribute to local neuropil injury. Notably, such pathological formations were often embedded within otherwise intact-appearing neuropil and synaptic terminals, suggesting that ectopic myelination enclosures can occur prior to widespread neuropil degeneration and functional defects. Similarly, dendrites with degeneration products comparable to those within neuropil surrounded by ectopic myelin often retained morphologically intact synapses. This observation suggests that synaptic loss occurs subsequent to dendritic degeneration, consistent with a pathological sequence in which ectopic myelination and myelin outfold invasion precede and potentially contribute to degenerative changes in gray‑matter neuronal processes.

More subtle ultrastructural features consistent with aberrant oligodendrocyte membrane behavior were observed in hippocampus neuropil in the form of loose membranous whirls, comprised of single membrane folds spiraling into themselves (Fig. 4D, 6C). These membranous whirls most frequently detected within large boutons of mossy terminals in the CA3 region of hippocampus (Fig. 6C, 7A), where they sequestered neurotransmitter vesicles as early as PM3. Similar to other instances of ectopic myelination, mossy terminals containing the membranous whirl retained morphologically identifiable synapses, particularly in PM3—5 brains. Although membrane-invaded mossy terminals displayed intact synapses and abundant neurotransmitter vesicles, the mitochondria appeared dark, shrunken, with compressed cristae, and were often clustered at one side of the bouton (Fig. 7A). In contrast, mossy terminals in the CA3 region of age-matched wild-type mouse rarely displayed membranous whirls (Fig. 7B-C). Quantitatively, mossy terminals containing membranous whirls were observed at a frequency more than three times higher in 3xTg mice than in age‑matched wild‑type controls across all ages examined (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the number or the structural complexity of membranous whirls increased in PM5-PM12 brains.

The most severe examples of early neuropil damage associated with invasion of ectopic membranes were observed in the basal forebrain regions as early as PM3 (Fig. 7D-E). In basal forebrain areas, including the diagonal bed nucleus and substantia innominata bed nuclei of stria terminalis, ventrobasal nucleus, and regions ventral to the anterior commissure, loose membrane stacks densely occupied the neuropil, intercalated among neuronal processes that otherwise lacked overt degenerative features. In these regions, invasive membranes were also observed within smaller synaptic boutons. Mitochondria within affected neuropil appeared dark and shrunken, with markedly condensed cristae. In some areas, the extent of membrane accumulation was so pronounced that the neuropil was largely composed of stacked membranes rather than recognizable cross‑sections of dendrites or synaptic terminals (Fig. 7D).

Invasive membranes originate from oligodendrocytes

To determine whether the membranes invading neuropil in 3xTg brains originated from oligodendrocytes, we examined ultrathin sections immunostained for O4, a surface antigen expressed by oligodendrocyte lineage cells during the transition from progenitor to differentiated oligodendrocytes. O4 was selected as it labels oligodendrocyte‑lineage membranes, allowing ultrastructural visualization of oligodendrocyte‑derived membrane folds and processes within neuropil [58, 59]. O4-stained cells were rarely encountered in wildtype brains, whereas numerous O4 + cells were observed throughout the 3xTg brains (Fig. 8A-B). The ultrastructural examination of O4 labeling using electron microscopy revealed dense chromogen accumulation within oligodendrocyte cytoplasm (Fig. 8C), and within associated membrane processes. Notably, O4-positive membrane folds were observed extending into hippocampus and basal forebrain neuropil, including penetrations into dendritic shafts and synaptic terminals (Fig. 8D-E). These observations indicate that membranes invading neuronal and synaptic profiles in 3xTg brains are derived from oligodendrocytes.

Oligodendrocyte pathology is common in another APP overexpression model

To determine whether the invasion of oligodendrocyte-derived membranes into neuropil observed in 3xTg brains is model-specific or associated more broadly with amyloid overexpression, we examined brains from the 5xFAD model, in which myelinated axon abnormalities have previously been reported [60]. We focused on oleic acid droplets in ependymal cells and on myelin-related morphopathologies, including myelin outfolds and myelin bulbs.

Oleic acid droplets were observed in 5xFAD brains at PM3 and PM9, indicating that lipid accumulation within the subventricular zone is also present in this amyloid overexpression model. Ultrastructural examination of the white matter at PM3 and PM9 revealed neuropathological structures resembling those observed in 3xTg brains. At PM3, myelin herniations, infolds and short outfolds and disrupted myelin stacking were evident, consistent with prior observations in this model [52, 60]. Collections of dystrophic neurites, myelin bulbs, and membranous whirls invading large neuropil elements such as CA3 mossy fiber terminals, were also present at PM3, and became qualitatively more prevalent at PM9 (Fig. 9A-C).

The glial cells closely associated with amyloid deposits exhibited ultrastructural features consistent with oligodendrocytes, including a round, heterochromatic nucleus and relatively electron‑dense cytoplasm containing characteristic organelles [52]. Discussion

Our ultrastructural analysis of the 3xTg Alzheimer’s disease (AD) model revealed three key findings:

Early myelin abnormalities precede overt cellular degeneration, appearing in both white and gray matter by 3 months, well before detectable neuronal deteriorations at 8 months.

Myelin malformations in pre-plaque stages

Growing evidence highlights oligodendrocytes and myelin disruption as common pathological features in both human Alzheimer’s disease and transgenic AD models; white matter deterioration has been recognized as one of the earliest brain alterations in AD [12, 13, 62]. Human neuroimaging studies frequently reveal both microstructural and macrostructural abnormalities indicative of myelin disruption in white matter [20, 21]. Oligodendrocyte-related abnormalities are also evident in familial AD and other conditions involving amyloid-β or presenilin-1 mutations in humans [63–66]. Similar to human AD, various AD mice models exhibit demyelination and white matter irregularities [18, 19, 22, 60, 67–69]. In agreement with these studies, our ultrastructural analyses revealed myelin irregularities and aberrant formations, and notably, novel observations of neuropil damage caused by single fold oligodendrocyte membranes, emerging well before measurable cognitive deficits and months prior to the appearance of electron-dense amyloid deposits in transgenic mice. These observations provide a structural basis for the white matter thinning and myelin loss observed in light microscopy, as well as the microstructural abnormalities detected in imaging studies.

Many of the myelin-related pathologies we documented, such as outfolds and redundant myelin, have been described for decades in electron microscopy studies of aging or neurologically compromised brains. Alan Peters’s extensive work on the aging primate brain [34, 70, 71], and studies in human and transgenic AD models [60, 72–74] have reported similar structures. Dystrophic neurites encased in myelin have also been observed in aging monkey and human brains, as well as in various transgenic AD mouse models. These are often classified as dystrophic axons [17, 75, 76], likely due to their location within axon-rich regions and the presence of surrounding myelin. However, in our study, we were able to identify membrane-encased dystrophic neurites as non-axonal based on the presence of intact organelles or cytoskeletal features characteristic of dendrites or synaptic boutons. This suggests that some dystrophic neurites may arise secondarily from ectopic myelination by oligodendrocyte outfolds. Furthermore, recent advances in serial electron microscopy have provided unambiguous evidence of myelinated dendrites, somata, and neuropil bundles in the human brain [77] (see Gallery at https://h01-release.storage.googleapis.com/gallery.html for ‘Myelinated Debris’, ‘Myelinated Dendrite’ and ‘Dual-Myelinated Structures). Taken together, our findings support a model in which progressive neuronal damage may be promoted, in part, by dysregulated oligodendrocyte membrane extensions into neuronal and synaptic compartments.

Progression of hypermotile oligodendrocyte membranes and myelin-led malformations

Our ultrastructural analyses across multiple brain regions in 3xTg and 5xFAD mice at 3, 5, 8, and 12 months revealed a progressive pattern of oligodendrocyte-related malformations that increasingly compromised neuropil integrity (Fig. 11). To summarize, neuropil damage, in all instances, appeared to arise directly or imminently from aberrant extensions of oligodendrocyte membranes, either as isolated membrane folds or as protrusions from pre-existing myelin. Each type of oligodendrocyte malformation, whether involving single-membrane folds or myelin-derived structures, followed a distinct pathological trajectory. The earliest and simplest form, typically observed as early as PM3, involved single membrane folds herniating into the extracellular space or neuropil, affecting thin dendrites, axons, and terminal boutons (Fig. 11a–c). These protrusions originated from either the innermost or outermost layers of axonal myelin and extended into adjacent structures, forming ‘rogue’ whorls or knobs within the cytoplasm (Fig. 11a–b, Fig. 4). Such herniations breached cellular membranes, potentially disrupting intracellular transport and initiating degenerative processes. They were frequently accompanied by cytoplasmic abnormalities, including displaced mitochondria and electron-dense inclusions. Notably, these single-membrane herniations also occurred in gray matter regions, where myelinated axons are sparse (Fig. 11b). In CA3 mossy fiber terminals, for example, oligodendrocyte-derived whirls induced vesicle sequestration and mitochondrial displacement (Fig. 6, 7). A more severe manifestation was observed in the basal forebrain of PM5–8 3xTg mice, where oligodendrocyte membrane folds formed irregular stacks that invaded large extracellular spaces, severely disrupting the surrounding neuropil (Figs. 7, 11c). While simple membrane herniations involving the axons were primarily confined to white matter, gray matter involvement was common in regions adjacent to major white matter tracts such as the alveus, fimbria, corpus callosum, and anterior commissure.

A second class of malformations originated from myelinated axons and involved myelin outfolds. The outfold structure emerged from axons in the white matter, extended over long distances (Figs. 3, 11d), and wrapped around neighboring myelinated axons to form double or triple sheaths or coiled into compact myelin whorls (Figs. 4, 11e). Outfolds also extended into gray matter, where they indiscriminately encase neuropil bundles (Fig. 6). The sequestered neuropil often exhibited signs of degeneration, including mitochondrial damage, vacuolization, and multilamellar electron-dense structures characteristic of dystrophic neurites.

The most severe form of outfold pathology involved large-scale myelin wrapping of entire neuronal somata or proximal dendrites (Figs. 5, 11f), forming large myelin bulbs. These were most frequently observed in gray matter regions adjacent to white matter tracts. Neurons encased in outfold wraps often transitioned into dystrophic neurites, with degeneration progressing from the outer membrane inward. This pattern suggests that the surrounding myelin may induce degeneration, potentially through mechanical compression or toxic signaling, contributing to formation of dystrophic neurites. Notably, myelin-wrapped dystrophic neurites were frequently observed within amyloid plaques. Because amyloid plaques are themselves well known to recruit dystrophic neurites and glial processes that encircle the amyloid core [78, 79] myelin involvement in these structures is likely to reflect a contributory role in neuropil damage. In this framework, oligodendrocyte-derived membrane abnormalities may facilitate or exacerbate dystrophic neurite formation, which subsequently becomes concentrated within plaque‑associated pathology through established plaque–neurite interactions.

Role of oligodendrocytes in myelin-related malformations

The observations described above, specifically the extension of single or stacked oligodendrocyte‑derived membranes that disrupt the normal morphology of myelinated axons and surrounding neuropil, are consistent with dysregulated oligodendrocyte membrane remodeling in 3xTg brains. During developmental myelination, oligodendrocyte membranes undergo extensive remodeling to explore and ensheath axonal substrates. Myelin outfolds, in particular, represent structures associated with active membrane remodeling and are commonly observed during these developmental stages. The high prevalence of myelin outfolds in adult 3xTg brains therefore raises the possibility that oligodendrocytes in this model adopt membrane behaviors reminiscent of an immature or developmentally plastic state. Such dysregulated membrane remodeling could underlie the formation of multiple pathological myelin‑associated structures observed here, including myelin herniations, outfolds, axonal whorls, and myelin bulbs.

While the ultrastructural examinations that visualize fully formed myelin structure provided unambiguous evidence that neuropathological formations observed in the white and gray matter were oligodendrocytes in origin, the source for more subtle neuropil damage caused by single folds of membrane could not be determined through morphology alone. To address this, we employed immunocytochemistry using oligodendrocyte-specific markers. In particular, we used O4, a marker expressed in both non-myelinating and myelinating oligodendrocytes, to confirm that the membranous whorls and herniations invading the neuropil were indeed oligodendrocytic in origin. Because O4 immunolabeling in this study was optimized for ultrastructural localization rather than unbiased stereological sampling, we did not perform numerical comparisons of O4‑positive cell density or expression levels across genotypes, and therefore interpret O4 labeling strictly in qualitative terms.

Our immunolabeling experiments also revealed that the 3xTg mice exhibit notably elevated O4-positive cell labeling compared to relative baseline staining observed in WT mice, suggesting an upregulation of both myelinating and premyelinating oligodendrocyte populations. However, markers specific to mature oligodendrocytes, such as myelin basic protein, show a reduction in expression in 3xTg brains [17, 75, 76]. This discrepancy supports the interpretation that the increase in O4 immunoreactivity reflects a selective expansion of premyelinating oligodendrocyte lineage cells—typically encountered during active myelination stages of development.

This interpretation aligns with the widespread presence of outfold morphologies in 3xTg mice, which resemble the dynamic, unstructured membrane sheaths characteristic of premyelinating oligodendrocytes rather than the compact, multilamellar sheaths of mature cells [37]. Such a developmental-like profile suggests a pathological reversion of mature oligodendrocytes to a more unstable, immature state. These retrogressive changes may stem from upstream dysfunction in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells. For instance, aberrant lipid accumulation in the neurogenic niche of 3xTg-AD mice has been shown to impair neural stem cell proliferation and contribute to widespread neurogenic deficits [40, 41]. Similarly, early myelin loss and oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC) atrophy, without a reduction in OPC numbers, have been reported by 6 months of age in 3xTg mice, indicating progenitor dysfunction [62]. Accelerated OPC loss and dystrophy have also been observed in the APP/PS1 model of AD [80], supporting the interpretation that early myelin outfolds reflect a pathological reactivation of developmental membrane dynamics in oligodendrocytes, likely driven by neural progenitor and OPC pathology in prodromal Alzheimer’s disease. This model is also consistent with observations of similar myelin abnormalities in other neurodegenerative conditions, traumatic brain injury, and normal aging, where reduced neurogenic potential and chronic neuroinflammation may contribute to impaired glial progenitor cell function [81].

Alternatively, dysregulated extension and reorganization of myelin and oligodendrocyte membranes may point to a cytoskeletal deficit in OPCs induced by Aβ overexpression. Notably, oligodendrocyte-selective deletion of N-Wasp, a key regulator of actin filament assembly, leads to aberrant outfold elongation and disorganized myelination [72, 82]. Similarly, the direct involvement of WASP-family proteins in Aβ phagocytosis by microglia [83, 84] may lead to their overutilization in these cells during early stages of effective fibrillar Aβ clearance, potentially impairing WASP function in oligodendrocytes. This imbalance could contribute to the emergence of outfold morphologies and dystrophic neurite formations via oligodendrocyte dysfunction, while also promoting subsequent amyloid plaque development through neuroinflammatory changes in microglia function [15, 85, 86].

Conclusions

Together, our findings reveal that oligodendrocyte dysfunction and myelin malformations are not merely secondary consequences of neurodegeneration but may represent early, initiating events in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. The presence of aberrant myelin structures, including single-membrane herniations and large-scale outfolds, prior to plaque formation and cognitive decline underscores a critical, underappreciated role for oligodendrocytes in the prodromal stages of AD. Our data suggest that these abnormalities arise from a pathological reactivation of developmental membrane dynamics, likely driven by progenitor cell dysfunction and cytoskeletal instability, potentially exacerbated by Aβ-induced stress and neuroinflammation. This model not only aligns with observations across multiple AD models and human studies but also offers a unifying framework for understanding myelin pathology in aging and other neurodegenerative conditions. Targeting oligodendrocyte lineage cells and their regulatory pathways may therefore represent a promising avenue for early intervention in AD and related disorders.

While surrounding neuropil appears structurally normal, oligodendrocyte-related pathologies are present, including myelin whorls (mye-w), inward herniations (mye-inf), myelin outfolds (mye-outf), and vacuolations (v). B. Pronounced ER lumen widening (black arrows) in a basal forebrain cell, suggesting ER stress. Abbrev.: m: mitochondria; r: free ribosomes; rER: rough endoplasmic reticulum. C. Myelin membrane herniations (black arrows) emerging from a myelinated axon, which also shows structural disruptions. Herniated membranes extend into the extracellular matrix (arrowheads). Abbrev.: m: mitochondria; ax: axon.

Formation of the lateral ventricle between ependymal cell linings of the cortical white matter and alveus dorsal to CA1 in PM3 mice.
