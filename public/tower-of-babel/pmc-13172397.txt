Carbon enriched mixed metal oxides as novel nanocomposites for efficient brilliant green decontamination
Authors: Ehab A Abdelrahman, Sawsan Alashqar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrilliant green dye is a cationic pollutant that poses serious ecological and public-health implications due to its mutagenic as well as cytotoxic effects and its resistance to biodegradation, leading to contamination of aquatic ecosystems and potential organ toxicity in humans. In this study, novel SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C nanocomposites, designated as MSP600 and MSP800, were successfully synthesized via a facile Pechini sol–gel route at 600 and 800 °C, respectively, for the efficient removal of brilliant green dye from aqueous media. XRD revealed that the average crystallite sizes of MSP600 and MSP800 nanocomposites is 65.29 nm and 76.94 nm, respectively. EDX confirmed the presence of O, C, Sr, Pb, and Mg, with atomic percentages consistent with temperature-induced phase transformation. FE-SEM images showed spherical, homogeneously distributed nanoparticles in MSP600 and irregular, rod-like aggregates in MSP800. HR-TEM analysis confirmed these morphologies, revealing average particle diameters of 23.48 nm for MSP600 and 59.09 nm for MSP800. The maximum uptake capacities were 375.94 mg/g for MSP600 and 304.88 mg/g for MSP800, demonstrating superior performance compared with some adsorbents described previously. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the uptake method was physical, spontaneous, and exothermic. Kinetic and isotherm studies confirmed that adsorption obeyed the pseudo-first-order model as well as the Langmuir isotherm. Both nanocomposites exhibited excellent regeneration efficiency, maintaining high removal performance over five adsorption–desorption cycles, establishing them as promising, reusable adsorbents for dye-contaminated wastewater treatment.Keywords: Brilliant green dye, Pechini sol–gel method, Carbon/Metal oxide nanocomposites, Adsorption kinetics and isotherms, Wastewater treatmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Contamination of water resources by organic dyes has emerged as a pressing environmental challenge due to the exponential growth of the textile, printing, plastic, leather, and paper industries. These sectors release substantial amounts of dye-laden discharges directly into aquatic systems without sufficient treatment1–3. Organic dyes are designed to be chemically and photolytically stable, enabling them to resist degradation by light, temperature, and microbial activity. Such stability, while beneficial for industrial applications, poses a severe obstacle to natural self-purification processes in the environment. The persistent discharge of these compounds alters the physicochemical properties of water, including pH, dissolved oxygen, and transparency, and results in long-term ecological damage4–6. Moreover, many dyes contain aromatic rings, amine groups, and heteroatoms that contribute to their toxicity and persistence, making them difficult to eliminate through conventional wastewater treatments. The environmental and human health implications of organic dye pollution are extensive. Upon entry into aquatic environments, dyes decrease sunlight diffusion and disrupt photosynthetic activity in aquatic plants, leading to oxygen depletion and the death of aquatic organisms7,8. Several dyes in addition to their degradation products display carcinogenic, mutagenic, as well as teratogenic properties. Long-term exposure throughout polluted water or food chains can cause serious health problems, involving liver malfunction, skin irritation, neurological disorders, and respiratory distress. The accumulation of dyes in sediments also poses chronic ecological risks, creating reservoirs of toxic materials that can be reintroduced into the water system during natural disturbances9,10. Therefore, the control and removal of organic dyes from industrial wastewater are essential for maintaining ecosystem balance and protecting human health. Among the numerous dyes of environmental concern, brilliant green dye has attracted significant attention because of its prevalent industrial use as well as potential toxicity. Brilliant green, a cationic triarylmethane dye, is extensively used in textile dyeing, paper printing, and biological staining. Its complex aromatic structure makes it highly soluble in water but also resistant to biodegradation. When released into the environment, it can cause harmful effects on aquatic life by inhibiting enzymatic activity and disrupting cellular metabolism. For humans, exposure to Brilliant Green through contaminated water or dermal contact may result in skin irritation, eye damage, and potential mutagenic effects11,12. Prolonged ingestion can also lead to systemic toxicity affecting the liver and kidneys. The dye’s vivid color and strong affinity for organic matter further exacerbate its environmental persistence, making its removal a high priority in modern wastewater management research. Several procedures have been established to eliminate organic dyes from polluted water, each with its own advantages and limitations. Conventional methods include coagulation–flocculation, membrane filtration, electrodialysis, photocatalytic degradation, and bioremediation. Coagulation–flocculation employs chemical coagulants to aggregate dye molecules into larger particles for sedimentation, but it often generates secondary sludge requiring further disposal13–15. Membrane filtration offers precise separation but suffers from membrane fouling as well as high operational costs16. Electrodialysis relies on ion migration through selective membranes but is limited by energy consumption and scale-up challenges17. Photocatalytic degradation utilizes semiconductor catalysts under light irradiation to oxidize dye molecules into harmless products, though the process is restricted by recombination of charge carriers and low efficiency under visible light18–20. Bioremediation provides an eco-friendly alternative using microorganisms or enzymes to degrade dyes; however, the method is typically slow and highly sensitive to temperature, pH, and dye structure21–23. Among these approaches, adsorption stands out as the most efficient, economical, and versatile method for dye removal from aqueous systems. The adsorption process is simple, does not require complex instrumentation, and can be employed to a diverse set of pollutants. It effectively removes dyes even at low concentrations and allows possible regeneration and reuse of the adsorbent24,25. Unlike photocatalysis or biological methods, adsorption is not significantly affected by light, temperature, or microbial activity. Moreover, the process avoids the formation of harmful by-products and is easily scalable for industrial applications. The selection of a suitable adsorbent with high surface area, chemical stability, and abundant active sites is critical to achieving high removal efficiency. Therefore, considerable research has been directed toward developing advanced adsorbent materials with enhanced surface chemistry and adsorption capacity. Metal oxide nanoparticles have emerged as favorably promising materials for dye adsorption because of their unique physicochemical properties. Their high specific surface area, variable oxidation states, and surface reactivity allow strong association with organic dye molecules via electrostatic attraction, ion exchange, as well as complexation26,27. Composites that combine metal oxides with carbon or other metal oxides can further enhance adsorption efficiency through synergistic effects28–33. Carbon materials improve dispersion, conductivity, and adsorption affinity, while the inclusion of multiple metal oxides creates heterogeneous active sites and structural stability. Such hybrid materials have shown superior performance compared to single-component adsorbents, offering enhanced dye removal rates, selectivity, and recyclability. The development of mixed metal oxide–carbon composites thus represent a strategic approach for addressing dye contamination effectively and sustainably. The synthesis method plays a vital responsibility in governing the structural, morphological, as well as surface characteristics of nanomaterials. The Pechini sol–gel process has acquired substantial attention over other synthetic routes due to its ability to produce homogeneously mixed multicomponent oxides at relatively low temperatures34–36. In this process, metal ions are chelated by an organic acid such as tartaric acid and subsequently polymerized with a polyalcohol such as polyethylene glycol to form a polymeric gel37–42. Upon calcination, this gel decomposes uniformly, yielding finely dispersed metal oxide nanoparticles with controlled stoichiometry and crystallinity. Compared to coprecipitation, hydrothermal, or combustion methods, the Pechini route offers superior control over particle size, morphology, and phase distribution43–48. It also allows precise incorporation of multiple metal species into a single network, which is particularly advantageous for designing complex nanocomposites with synergistic functionalities. Numerous adsorbents have been reported in the literature for the removal of brilliant green dye from aqueous media. Examples include Schiff base–chitosan–vanillin/hydroxyapatite composite with an adsorption capacity of 295.74 mg/g49, chitosan grafted with itaconic acid with 135.00 mg/g50, activated carbon with 243.90 mg/g51, and ferroferric oxide/polypyrrole magnetic composite with 263.85 mg/g52. Although these materials exhibit good performance, most of them are affected by limitations, for example, poor structural stability, restricted recyclability, or complex synthesis procedures. Biopolymer-based composites like chitosan derivatives are prone to swelling and dissolution in acidic media, while magnetic composites may suffer from aggregation and reduced surface activity upon repeated use. Consequently, there remains an ongoing need to develop new, stable, and cost-effective adsorbents with higher efficiency and improved reusability for the sustainable removal of brilliant green dye. In the present work, novel SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C nanocomposites were synthesized employing the Pechini sol–gel approach at 600 as well as 800 °C, respectively, for the efficient adsorption of brilliant green dye from aqueous environments. The innovative aspect of this research lies in the incorporation of multiple metal oxides and carbon within a single nanostructured framework to create a synergistic effect that enhances adsorption capacity. The coexistence of several crystalline phases increases the density of active sites, facilitates dye–surface interaction, and improves charge distribution across the material. These features collectively contribute to superior adsorption efficiency, making the synthesized products hopeful candidates for the treatment of dye-polluted wastewater.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals used in this work were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. Tartaric acid (C4H6O6), magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2.6H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), polyethylene glycol 400 (H(OCH2CH2)nOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), strontium nitrate (Sr(NO3)2), potassium chloride (KCl), lead nitrate (Pb(NO3)2), and brilliant green dye (C27H34N2O4S) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Distilled water was used throughout the experimental procedures to ensure the preparation of pure and uncontaminated solutions.

Synthesis of SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C nanocomposites

The SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C products were synthesized by the Pechini polymeric-precursor method, as shown in Fig. 1. Primarily, 21 g of tartaric acid was solubilized in about 75 mL of distilled water to form a clear chelating solution. Separately, 12 g of Mg(NO3)2.6H2O, 12 g of Sr(NO3)2, and 12 g of Pb(NO3)2 were dissolved in 125 mL of distilled water to obtain a homogeneous metal nitrate solution. The tartaric acid solution was subsequently incorporated gradually into the metal nitrate solution maintained under stirring for 30 min to ensure complete complexation of metal ions with the organic ligand. Subsequently, 10 mL of polyethylene glycol 400 was introduced as a polymerizing agent, and the mixture was agitated continuously at 200 °C until the solvent had fully evaporated, resulting in the formation of a dry, resin-like precursor. The obtained powder was then calcined at 600 and 800 °C for 3 h to produce SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C nanocomposites, respectively, which were designated as MSP600 and MSP800.

Schematic representation of the synthesis procedure for SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C products.

Equipment

The structural identification of all constructed products was carried out employing an X-ray diffraction diffractometer (X’Pert PRO, PANalytical, Netherlands) to determine their crystallographic phases and crystal systems. The surface morphology and elemental composition of the nanocomposites were examined employing a field emission scanning electron microscope outfitted with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FE-SEM/EDX, JSM-IT800 Schottky, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The detailed microstructural and morphological characteristics were further investigated using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM JEM-2100Plus, JEOL Ltd., Japan). Raman spectrum of MSP600 sample was recorded using a LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Scientific, Japan). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using a Nicolet IS10 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The specific surface area and pore characteristics of the samples were determined by N2 adsorption–desorption analysis using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method on a surface area analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2020, USA). The carbon content of the synthesized samples was determined using a CHN elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany).

Besides, the concentration of brilliant green dye in aqueous solution during adsorption studies was obtained employing a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (V-670, Jasco, Japan).

Uptake of brilliant green dye from aqueous media

The adsorption performance of the synthesized nanocomposites toward brilliant green dye was evaluated under various experimental conditions, as shown in Table 1. All adsorption experiments were performed in triplicate, and the reported values represent the mean. In addition, error bars representing the standard deviation have been added to all adsorption-related plots in the manuscript. In the pH study, 150 mL of a 250 mg/L brilliant green dye solution was prepared, and 0.1 g of the nanocomposite was added at 298 K. The pH of the solution was tuned within the range of 2–10, and the mixture was agitated on a magnetic stirrer for 300 min to investigate the optimal pH for dye remediation. For the contact time study, 150 mL of a 250 mg/L brilliant green dye solution was mixed with 0.1 g of the nanocomposite at 298 K and pH 10. The solution was stirred magnetically across a time window of 10–100 min to investigate the influence of contact time on adsorption efficiency. In the temperature study, 150 mL of a 250 mg/L brilliant green dye solution containing 0.1 g of the nanocomposite at pH 10 was agitated magnetically at different temperatures over the range 298–328 K for 60 min in the case of MSP600 and 80 min in the case of MSP800 to evaluate the thermal effect on adsorption. To examine the influence of the initial dye concentration, 150 mL of brilliant green dye solutions across the concentration range 100–350 mg/L were prepared at pH 10 as well as 298 K. Each solution contained 0.1 g of the nanocomposite and was stirred magnetically for 60 min for MSP600 and 80 min for MSP800 to determine the adsorption behavior under different concentrations.

Evaluation of the pHPZC of the synthesized adsorbent materials

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of MSP600 and MSP800 was obtained by a batch adsorption approach using 0.1 M KCl as the supporting electrolyte. Besides, a series of 50 mL aliquots of the KCl solution was prepared, and the initial pH values (pHI) were adjusted from 2 to 12 with dilute HCl or NaOH. A mass of 0.05 g of the product was incorporated into each aliquot, and the suspensions were magnetically agitated at room temperature for 24 h to reach equilibrium. Also, the final recorded pH values (pHF) were recorded, and the pH change ΔpH was calculated using Eq. 458–60.

A plot of ΔpH versus pHI was constructed, and the pH value at which ΔpH is zero was taken as the pHPZC for each nanocomposite.

Results and discussion

Characterization results

XRD

Referring to Fig. 2A, the XRD pattern of the MSP600 nanocomposite reveals that it is composed of orthorhombic SrPbO3 (JCPDS-01-085-1961), cubic MgO (JCPDS-01-074-4911), orthorhombic SrCO3 (JCPDS-01-078-4340), and orthorhombic PbO (JCPDS-00-005-0570)61,62. The diffraction peaks corresponding to SrPbO3 appear at 2θ = 21.28, 30.34, 47.67, 48.90, 49.82, 53.98, 62.98, 71.59, 75.10, and 75.84, which are indexed to the (002), (112), (213), (114), (131), (204), (224), (116), (242), and (206) planes. The cubic MgO phase exhibits reflections at 2θ = 37.03, 42.90, 62.31, and 78.88, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (222) planes. The orthorhombic SrCO3 phase is observed at 2θ = 20.38, 25.22, 25.80, 31.38, 34.43, 35.25, 36.58, 41.34, 43.35, 44.17, 51.61, 55.02, 57.26, 58.96, 61.19, 65.28, 67.14, and 77.70, indexed to the (110), (111), (021), (121), (102), (031), (130), (220), (040), (221), (222), (310), (311), (241), (004), (242), (322), and (260) planes. The PbO phase displays its characteristic reflections at 2θ = 15.02, 29.08, 32.64, 36.07, 37.78, 39.55, 45.13, 46.48, 50.79, 56.06, 60.30, 68.26, 68.86, 72.79, and 73.53, corresponding to the (001), (111), (200), (201), (210), (112), (202), (003), (220), (311), (222), (400), (114), (204), and (313) planes. Referring to Fig. 2B, the diffraction pattern of the MSP800 nanocomposite indicates the presence of orthorhombic SrPbO3 (JCPDS-01-085-1961), cubic MgO (JCPDS-01-074-4911), and orthorhombic Sr2PbO4 (JCPDS-00-022-1434)61,62. The Sr2PbO4 phase is identified by its peaks at 2θ = 16.81, 17.56, 22.76, 28.92, 30.63, 31.09, 34.06, 35.61, 38.59, 39.93, 44.24, 44.99, 46.55, 52.19, 54.58, 57.84, 58.88, 60.74, 61.49, 64.68, 66.76, 69.08, 70.72, 72.56, 73.38, 76.81, 77.39, and 78.07, which are assigned to the (110), (020), (120), (200), (111), (021), (220), (040), (140), (211), (041), (310), (240), (330), (151), (340), (331), (202), (132), (042), (142), (421), (351), (171), (322), (332), (441), and (510) planes. The average crystallite size of MSP600 is 65.29 nm, while that of MSP800 is 76.94 nm. The disappearance of characteristic reflections of SrCO3 and PbO in MSP800, together with the appearance of new peaks corresponding to Sr2PbO4, indicates that a temperature-induced solid-state reaction occurred during calcination at 800 °C. Such transformations are commonly observed during high-temperature treatment of mixed metal precursors, where carbonate decomposition and oxide interaction promote the formation of more thermodynamically stable mixed oxide phases. Besides, semi-quantitative phase analysis of the XRD patterns revealed that MSP600 is composed of 35.7% SrPbO3, 12. % MgO, 28.8%SrCO3, and 3.5% PbO. After alcination at 800 °C, a clear phase transformation occurred, leading to the formation of Sr2PbO4 as the dominant phase in MSP800 with a phase composition of 13.2% SrPbO3, 29. % MgO, and 576% Sr2PbO4. Th increase in crystallite size for MSP800 is accredited to the elevated calcination temperature, which enhances atomic diffusion as well as grain growth. Additionally, the transformation of SrCO3 and PbO phases into Sr2PbO4 during heating reduces structural defects and promotes the coalescence of crystalline domains, resulting in larger crystallites.

EDS

Referring to Fig. 3; Table 2, the EDS spectra verify the occurrence of O, C, Mg, Sr, as well as Pb in both MSP600 (Fig. 3A) and MSP800 (Fig. 3B) nanocomposites. The detectable C originates from the Pechini sol–gel chemistry conducted with tartaric acid in addition to polyethylene glycol 400, which forms a polymeric metal–organic resin. After calcination, residual carbon can remain and appear as a C signal in EDS. The lower C in MSP800 arises from more resin combustion and decarbonation at the higher calcination temperature, which removes carbon-containing species as CO₂ and yields a cleaner oxide surface. The decrease in O is consistent with CO2 release, desorption of moisture, and a lower fraction of surface hydroxyls after grain growth. The modest increases in Mg and Sr reflect improved cation homogeneity and densification promoted by the Pechini network, where tartaric acid chelates the cations and polyethylene glycol 400 drives polyesterification, thereby enhancing uniform distribution before crystallization. The marked increase in Pb indicates temperature-driven phase evolution toward Pb-rich oxide domains, such as Sr2PbO4 observed for MSP800, along with reduced coverage by volatile or weakly bound oxygenated species; both effects increase the relative Pb contribution within the EDS interaction volume. Consequently, the differences in atomic percentages between MSP600 and MSP800 arise from the combined effects of resin burnout, decarbonation, phase transformation, and microstructural coarsening during high-temperature treatment.

It should be noted that the carbon component generated during the synthesis is expected to exhibit a broad diffraction feature around 2θ ≈ 24–26o; however, due to its low crystallinity and partially amorphous nature, this peak is weak and may overlap with nearby reflections, making it difficult to clearly distinguish in the XRD patterns. Importantly, the presence of carbon in the prepared materials was independently confirmed by CHN elemental analysis. The CHN analyzer results showed carbon contents of 7.9 wt% for MSP600 and 6.8 wt% for MSP800, confirming that carbon is indeed present even if it does not appear as a well-defined crystalline peak in XRD.

Raman spectroscopy was performed on MSP600 (Fig. 4) as an illustrative example to provide additional insight into the lattice vibrational features of the synthesized system. Raman spectroscopy of MSP600 exhibits three distinct low-wavenumber bands at ~ 84.37, 141.82, and 286.17 cm⁻¹. The band at ~ 84.37 cm⁻¹ is attributed to ultra-low-frequency lattice vibrations (external modes), which are typically enhanced in Pb-containing oxide frameworks due to the large mass of Pb²⁺ and the resulting low-energy phonons, indicating the formation of a Pb-O-based crystalline lattice. The feature at ~ 141.82 cm⁻¹ corresponds to external lattice modes involving translational motions of Sr/Pb cations relative to the oxygen sublattice and/or Pb–O lattice vibrations, consistent with lead-containing oxide/perovskite domains. The pronounced band at ~ 286.17 cm⁻¹ is assigned to higher-energy lattice vibrations associated with M–O (M = Sr/Pb) bending/stretching within the oxide network, further supporting the development of stable inorganic oxide/perovskite structures. The absence of clear D (~ 1350 cm⁻¹) and G (~ 1580 cm⁻¹) bands suggests that the carbon component is either present in a low amount and/or predominantly amorphous, while the lack of pronounced MgO Raman bands is expected due to its rock-salt symmetry (first-order Raman inactivity). Overall, the Raman features corroborate the XRD-identified Pb-containing oxide/perovskite phases in MSP600.

FE-SEM

Referring to Fig. 5A–B, the FE-SEM micrographs show the surface morphology of the MSP600 (Fig. 5A) and MSP800 (Fig. 5B) nanocomposites synthesized by the Pechini sol–gel method. In Fig. 5A, the MSP600 sample exhibits nearly spherical and uniformly distributed nanoparticles that are closely packed, indicating a homogeneous nucleation process controlled by the polymeric network formed by tartaric acid and polyethylene glycol 400. The particles appear fine and agglomerated due to incomplete crystallite growth at the lower calcination temperature, which limits grain coalescence and preserves a nanostructured texture. In contrast, Fig. 5B, which corresponds to the MSP800 sample, reveals non-homogeneous shapes with a combination of spherical as well as rod-like morphologies, as well as larger aggregates. The higher calcination temperature leads to enhanced atomic diffusion, particle fusion, and recrystallization, resulting in grain growth and the creation of denser and more heterogeneous assemblies. The morphological evolution from fine spherical nanoparticles in MSP600 to coarser irregular particles in MSP800 is therefore attributed to the temperature-induced coarsening effect and partial phase transformation during high-temperature treatment, which increase crystallinity and reduce surface energy through particle consolidation.

FE-SEM images of (A) MSP600 and (B) MSP800 samples.

HR-TEM

Referring to Figs. 6 and 7, the HR-TEM micrographs provide detailed insight into the morphology as well as particle size dispersal of the MSP600 and MSP800 products synthesized by the Pechini sol–gel method. In Fig. 6A, the MSP600 sample displays uniformly distributed nanoparticles with nearly spherical and polygonal shapes, forming loose agglomerates of fine grains. The particles are small and well-dispersed, indicating that the polymeric matrix generated by tartaric acid and polyethylene glycol 400 effectively restricted particle growth and promoted homogeneous nucleation during the gelation and calcination processes. In contrast, Fig. 6B, corresponding to MSP800, shows the formation of larger crystallites with irregular, spherical, and faceted morphologies, resulting from the boosted atomic diffusion in addition to grain coalescence at the elevated calcination temperature. The increase in crystallite size and the emergence of well-defined boundaries confirm the higher degree of crystallinity achieved at 800 °C. Figure 6 presents the particle size dispersal histograms derived from HR-TEM images. For MSP600 (Fig. 7A), the histogram exhibits a narrow Gaussian distribution centered around an average particle size of 23.48 nm, reflecting the uniform and fine nature of the nanocomposite. In contrast, the MSP800 histogram (Fig. 7B) shows a broader and shifted distribution with a mean particle size of 59.09 nm, indicating pronounced particle growth as well as size heterogeneity. The broadening of the size distribution at higher temperatures suggests that Ostwald ripening and sintering processes occurred during calcination, allowing smaller particles to merge into larger ones. The difference in average particle size between MSP600 and MSP800 arises mainly from the influence of temperature on crystallization kinetics. At 600 °C, the thermal energy is sufficient to induce crystallization but not enough to promote extensive diffusion, leading to smaller, uniformly sized nanoparticles. At 800 °C, however, the higher energy input enhances cation mobility and accelerates grain boundary migration, causing particle coalescence and growth. Consequently, MSP800 exhibits larger crystallites with more pronounced crystallinity, while MSP600 retains a finer and more nanostructured morphology.

Optimization

Role of pH in governing adsorption behavior and adsorption mechanism

Referring to Fig. 8, the relationship between the initial pH and ΔpH for the MSP600 and MSP800 nanocomposites reveals that the point of zero charge (pHPZC) occurs at 8.59 and 9.18, respectively. When pH < pHPZC, both materials exhibit positively charged surfaces due to protonation, as illustrated in Fig. 9, which leads to electrostatic repulsion with the cationic brilliant green dye63. This behavior explains the low adsorption efficiency observed at a solution pH of 2, where the elimination percentages were 2.22% fr MSP600 and 1.76% fr MSP800, as shown in Fig. 10. Under these acidic conditions, excess H⁺ ions compete with dye cations for the available adsorption sites, resulting in poor dye uptake. In contrast, when pH > pHPZC, the surface of the nanocomposite becomes negatively charged due to deprotonation, enhancing electrostatic attraction between the adsorbent and the cationic dye, as shown in Fig. 9. As shown in Fig. 10, this effect leads to a remarkable rise in elimination efficiency at pH 10, reaching 98.60% regarding MSP600 in addition to 77.48% regarding MSP80063. The higher adsorption capacity of MSP600 compared with MSP800 at the same pH can be accredited to its reduced crystallite size as well as larger specific surface area, which support more accessible active sites for interaction with the dye molecules. Therefore, the adsorption process is strongly pH-dependent, with maximum brilliant green dye removal achieved under basic conditions where electrostatic attraction dominates the dye–adsorbent interaction.

Referring to Fig. 11A-B, the FTIR spectra of the SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C nanocomposite (MSP600), as an illustrative example, before (Fig. 11A) and after (Fig. 11B) adsorption of brilliant green dye provide clear evidence for the interaction mechanism between the dye molecules and the adsorbent surface. The bands observed at 405, 543, 702, 861, 1068, 1440, 1772, 2013, and 2102 cm⁻¹ appear in both spectra and are therefore attributed to the pristine MSP600 structure before adsorption. The absorptions at 405 and 543 cm⁻¹ are assigned to metal–oxygen vibrations (Sr–O, Pb–O, and Mg–O), confirming the presence of mixed metal oxide phases. The bands at 702 and 861 cm⁻¹ correspond to carbonate-related bending modes (CO₃²⁻), while the band at 1068 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C–O stretching vibrations. The peak at 1440 cm⁻¹ is assigned to asymmetric stretching of carbonate groups, and the bands at 1772, 2013, and 2102 cm⁻¹ are related to surface carbonyl and lattice-related vibrational modes within the carbon-enriched framework. After adsorption, slight shifts are observed in certain bands, particularly from 1647 to 1668 cm⁻¹ and from 3744 to 3711 cm⁻¹ as well as from 3841 to 3780 cm⁻¹, indicating the involvement of C = O and O–H groups in the adsorption process. The shift of the 1647 cm⁻¹ band to 1668 cm⁻¹ confirms the participation of surface carbonyl groups in complexation with the dye molecules. Moreover, the decrease and shift in the hydroxyl stretching bands demonstrate hydrogen bonding between the surface –OH groups and the nitrogen atoms of brilliant green. Importantly, several new bands appear only after adsorption at 950, 1150, 1253, 1343, 1592, 2923, and 3048 cm⁻¹. These peaks are characteristic of brilliant green functional groups: the bands at 1150–1253 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–N stretching of aromatic amine groups; 1343 and 1592 cm⁻¹ are assigned to aromatic C = C stretching vibrations; and 2923 and 3048 cm⁻¹ are related to aliphatic and aromatic C–H stretching vibrations of the triarylmethane structure61,64,65. The appearance of these new bands confirms successful adsorption of the dye onto MSP600. Collectively, the band shifts and the emergence of dye-specific vibrations verify that the adsorption mechanism involves electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged surface (at pH > pHPZC) and the cationic dye molecules, hydrogen bonding through surface hydroxyl groups, complexation between metal centers and dye nitrogen atoms, and π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of brilliant green and the carbonaceous domains within the nanocomposite as shown schematically in Fig. 9.

Influence of time variation on adsorption performance

Referring to Fig. 12, the adsorption of brilliant green dye on the MSP600 and MSP800 nanocomposites exhibits a typical time-dependent behavior characterized by a quick rise in dye elimination efficiency during the early stages, with a progressive move toward equilibrium. For the MSP800 sample, the removal percentage increased significantly from 31.70% at 10 min to 77.30% at 80 min, after which no noticeable change occurred, indicating that equilibrium was reached. Similarly, for the MSP600 sample, the percentage of dye removal increased sharply from 47.84% at 10 min to 98.58% at 60 min, after which it remained constant, signifying that the adsorption sites were saturated. The higher and faster removal rate observed for MSP600 compared with MSP800 can be accredited to its reduced crystallite size as well as greater surface area, which support more active positions for dye molecules to attach. Once equilibrium was attained, no further improvement in removal was observed for either sample because the available adsorption sites were completely occupied by brilliant green dye molecules, confirming that both nanocomposites achieved saturation at their respective equilibrium times.

Influence of temperature variation on adsorption performance

Referring to Fig. 16, the elimination efficiency of brilliant green dye on MSP600 and MSP800 nanocomposites decreases upon raising the temperature, indicating that the elimination is exothermic in nature. At 298 K, the removal efficiencies are 98.58% for MSP600 and 77.30% for MSP800, demonstrating that both materials operate efficiently at lower temperatures, with MSP600 showing superior adsorption capacity. When the temperature rises to 328 K, the elimination efficiencies reduce to 94.29% regarding MSP600 in addition to 67.87% regarding MSP800, confirming that elevated thermal energy weakens the electrostatic interactions between the dye molecules as well as the adsorbent surfaces. The stronger adsorption exhibited by MSP600 compared with MSP800 at both temperatures can be accredited to its reduced crystallite size as well as higher surface area, which support more active positions for dye uptake. Overall, the decrease in elimination efficacy with rising temperature indicates that physical adsorption dominates the process, and elevated temperatures promote desorption of dye molecules from the surface of adsorbent.

The developed MSP600 nanocomposite exhibited a maximum adsorption capacity of 375.94 mg/g toward brilliant green dye, which is considerably higher than many biomass-derived activated carbon adsorbents reported in the literature, such as Spathodea campanulata derived activated carbon (165.28 mg/g for Rhodamine B) and superparamagnetic activated carbon (108.06 mg/g for malachite green)87,88. Unlike the previously reported systems, which are primarily single-phase carbon-based adsorbents (activated carbon or magnetically modified activated carbon), the present work introduces a carbon-enriched mixed metal oxide nanocomposite consisting of multiple crystalline oxide phases integrated with carbon (e.g., SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C). This multiphase oxide–carbon architecture provides heterogeneous active sites and enables synergistic dye–surface interactions beyond those typically available on conventional activated carbon surfaces, thereby offering a distinct materials-level novelty.

The BJH pore distribution shown in Fig. 21 also indicates that MSP600 contains a greater population of smaller mesopores, whereas MSP800 is dominated by larger pores formed as a consequence of particle coarsening at the higher calcination temperature. The presence of smaller and more abundant mesopores in MSP600 enhances the available surface-active sites and improves the diffusion and interaction of brilliant green dye molecules with the adsorbent surface. In contrast, the larger pore size and reduced surface area of MSP800 decrease the density of accessible adsorption sites and limit dye–surface interactions. Consequently, these textural differences explain the superior adsorption capacity of MSP600 compared with MSP800.

Regeneration/reuse studies

Referring to Fig. 23, the desorption efficacy of brilliant green dye from MSP600 and MSP800 products rises steadily with rising the concentration of HCl, confirming that acidic conditions strongly enhance the dye release process. At 1 M HCl, the desorption percentages are 88.12% regarding MSP600 in addition to 84.06% regarding MSP800, demonstrating that a large portion of the adsorbed dye can already be removed under mildly acidic conditions. When the concentration increases to 1.5 M, the desorption efficiencies rise to 94.04% for MSP600 and 93.12% for MSP800, showing that stronger acidity further facilitates the breaking of electrostatic interactions between the positively charged dye molecules and the negatively charged adsorbent surface. At 2 M HCl, nearly complete desorption is achieved, reaching 99.74% for MSP600 and 99.51% for MSP800, which confirms the excellent regeneration capability of both materials. The superior performance of HCl as a desorbing agent is accredited to its high proton concentration, which promotes the displacement of cationic dye molecules through ion exchange and electrostatic repulsion. This behavior demonstrates that acid treatment is an efficient strategy for regenerating the adsorbents and reusing them in subsequent adsorption cycles.

Influence of HCl concentration on the desorption efficacy of brilliant green from MSP600 and MSP800 products.

Referring to Fig. 24, the reusability accomplishment of MSP600 in addition to MSP800 products demonstrates that both substances maintain high adsorption efficiency for brilliant green dye over multiple regeneration cycles, with only a gradual decline in removal percentage. For MSP600, the dye removal decreases slightly from 98.58% before regeneration to 93.07% after the fifth cycle, while for MSP800 it decreases from 77.30% to 69.90% over the same number of cycles. Despite this slight decline, both nanocomposites retain substantial adsorption capacity, confirming their chemical stability and regeneration capability.

Reusability of MSP600 and MSP800 over five adsorption–desorption cycles.

To confirm the chemical stability of the synthesized nanocomposites during the regeneration process, MSP600 was selected as a representative sample for leaching evaluation. Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) analysis was conducted on the filtrate collected after regeneration, and the results confirmed that no detectable Pb, Mg, or Sr ions were found in the regenerated filtrate. This clearly indicates the excellent stability of the adsorbent and confirms that no measurable dissolution or loss of its constituent elements occurred during the regeneration step. In addition, the XRD pattern of MSP600 after the fifth regeneration cycle (Fig. 25) was compared with the XRD pattern of the fresh MSP600 sample (Fig. 2A). The comparison demonstrates that no new diffraction peaks related to newly formed substances appeared, and the peak positions remained unchanged, indicating preservation of the original crystalline phases. Only a slight variation in peak intensities was observed, which can be attributed to minor changes in crystallinity and/or surface rearrangement after repeated adsorption/desorption cycles, without altering the material’s phase composition or structural framework.

Conclusions

Novel SrPbO3/MgO/SrCO3/PbO/C and SrPbO3/MgO/Sr2PbO4/C nanocomposites were successfully constructed through a facile Pechini sol–gel approach at 600 as well as 800 °C, respectively, for the efficient elimination of brilliant green dye from aqueous media. Structural identification confirmed the construction of well-crystallized multiphase nanocomposites with mean crystal sizes of 65.29 nm regarding MSP600 in addition to 76.94 nm regarding MSP800. Elemental analysis demonstrated the presence of C, O, Mg, Sr, and Pb with atomic ratios that varied with calcination temperature due to phase evolution and carbon burnout. Morphological studies revealed that MSP600 exhibited uniformly distributed spherical nanoparticles, whereas MSP800 showed irregular, rod-like structures, consistent with temperature-induced grain growth. HR-TEM analysis confirmed these findings and indicated average particle diameters of 23.48 nm for MSP600 and 59.09 nm for MSP800. The adsorption studies revealed that MSP600 achieved a maximum uptake capacity of 375.94 mg/g, while MSP800 reached 304.88 mg/g, both surpassing many reported adsorbents in the literature. The elimination process was spontaneous, exothermic, as well as physical in nature, obeying the pseudo-first-order kinetic equation in addition to the Langmuir equation. The superior performance of the nanocomposites arises from the synergistic effect among multiple oxide and carbon phases that create abundant active sites for dye adsorption. Both materials exhibited excellent regeneration capability, preserving high efficacy over five adsorption–desorption cycles. These findings highlight the great potential of the synthesized nanocomposites as cost-effective, stable, and reusable adsorbents for the treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater.
