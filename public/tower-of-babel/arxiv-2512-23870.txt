Max-Entropy Reinforcement Learning with Flow Matching and A Case Study on LQR
Yuyang Zhang, Yang Hu, Bo Dai, Na Li

Abstract
Soft actor-critic (SAC) is a popular algorithm for max-entropy reinforcement learning. In practice, the energy-based policies in SAC are often approximated using simple policy classes for efficiency, sacrificing the expressiveness and robustness. In this paper, we propose a variant of the SAC algorithm that parameterizes the policy with flow-based models, leveraging their rich expressiveness. In the algorithm, we evaluate the flow-based policy utilizing the instantaneous change-of-variable technique and update the policy with an online variant of flow matching developed in this paper. This online variant, termed importance sampling flow matching (ISFM), enables policy update with only samples from a user-specified sampling distribution rather than the unknown target distribution. We develop a theoretical analysis of ISFM, characterizing how different choices of sampling distributions affect the learning efficiency. Finally, we conduct a case study of our algorithm on the max-entropy linear quadratic regulator problems, demonstrating that the proposed algorithm learns the optimal action distribution.



I
Introduction

Maximum entropy reinforcement learning (max-entropy RL) has emerged as a popular reinforcement learning (RL) framework for a wide spectrum of tasks
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Based on the max entropy principle, it optimizes a return function augmented by entropy regularization of the current policy. Empirical studies have demonstrated that max-entropy RL learns policies that not only maximize rewards, but are also robust to the environmental disturbances
[
4
]
.

To solve max-entropy RL problems, one of the most prominent algorithms is Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)
[
2
]
. It maintains an energy-based model as its policy, and alternates between policy improvement and policy evaluation to improve the entropy-regularized return. Theoretical analysis has shown that SAC indeed converges to the optimal solution of max-entropy RL given that the policy evaluation and improvement steps are performed accurately
[
2
,
5
]
.
However, the improvement and evaluation of energy-based policies remain notoriously challenging. Early MCMC-based algorithms improve the energy-based policy by learning the gradient of an energy function
[
6
]
. Their policy improvement has limited accuracy in RL, because the required gradient information is difficult to approximate. Recent work uses diffusion models to parameterize the energy-based policies
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Their evaluation is intractable due to the lack of efficient methods to approximate policy entropy.
In practice, many existing algorithms restrict the policy class to simple families, such as Gaussian policies
[
2
,
10
]
. This restriction significantly limits the expressiveness and robustness of the learned policies.

Fortunately, flow-based models have emerged as powerful universal distribution approximators
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. To train flow-based models, a popular and efficient algorithm is flow matching, which matches the velocity field of the flow using samples from the target distribution.
Unlike traditional likelihood-based training methods
[
12
,
14
]
, flow matching neither involves Jacobian gradient calculation, nor constrains the neural network structure to be invertible, which makes it more computationally efficient and flexible
[
15
]
.
These make flow-based models a promising choice to parameterize energy-based policies in max-entropy RL.

In this paper, we propose to parameterize policies in the SAC algorithm with flow-based models trained via flow matching.

For policy improvement
, we propose an online variant of flow matching, termed importance sampling flow matching (ISFM). In contrast to standard flow matching that requires samples from the unknown target distribution, ISFM requires only samples from a user-specified distribution, making it appropriate for online RL settings. We develop a theoretical analysis of ISFM, characterizing how different choices of sampling distributions affect the learning accuracy. We then use the result to guide the choice of sampling distributions.
For policy evaluation,
we leverage the instantaneous change-of-variable formula
[
16
]
, a well-established result in flow-based model literature, to evaluate the entropy-regularized return of the flow-based policy.

Finally, as a case study for the proposed algorithm,
we investigate the max-entropy Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problems
[
17
]
. We show that the SAC algorithm, integrated with flow-based policies, learns the optimal solution.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In
Section

II
, we introduce necessary background on max-entropy RL and flow matching. In
Section

III
, we propose the adapted SAC algorithm framework, providing details on policy evaluation and improvement for flow-based policies. Finally, in
Section

IV

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Max-Entropy Reinforcement Learning

An MDP can be defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\rho^{0},P,r,\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the state space,
\mathcal{U}
is the action space,
\rho^{0}
is the initial state distribution,
P:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{U})
is the transition probability,
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\gamma
is the discount factor.

Consider any policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{U})
that maps a state to an action distribution. We define the corresponding entropy-regularized return function:

\begin{split}J^{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left[r(x^{t},u^{t})+\alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|x^{t}))\right]\big|x^{0}=x\right],\end{split}

where superscript
t
denotes the time step in MDP.
In max-entropy RL, our objective is to find the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
that maximizes
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho^{0}}J^{\pi}(x)
.

To facilitate later discussions, we also define the soft Q function of policy
\pi
:

\begin{split}{}&Q^{\pi}(x,u)\coloneqq r(x,u)+\\
{}&\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left[r(x^{t},u^{t})+\alpha\mathcal{H}\left(\pi(\cdot|x^{t})\right)\right]\Big|(x^{0},u^{0})=(x,u)\right].\end{split}

It is the only fixed point of the following soft Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}

[
2
]
:

\begin{split}{}&\mathcal{T}^{\pi}Q(x,u)\coloneqq r(x,u)\\
+{}&\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(x,u),u^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|x^{\prime})}\left[Q(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})+\alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|x^{\prime}))\right].\end{split}

(1)

II-B

Flow Matching

Flow-based models are expressive models for learning distributions. They define a time-dependent vector field
v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
that governs the evolution of random variable
u_{\tau}
through the oridinary differential equation (ODE)
\text{d}u_{\tau}/\text{d}\tau=v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})
. Here subscript
\tau\in[0,1]
denotes the time in the ODE. Under the ODE, samples
u_{0}\sim p_{0}
are transformed to samples
u_{\tau}\sim p_{\tau},\forall\tau\in[0,1]
. We say that
p_{\tau}
is generated by vector field
v_{\tau}
.

The vector field transforming
p_{0}
to
p_{1}
is generally not unique. To specify a vector field that facilitates learning, we first choose a conditional probability path
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
such that it is easy to derive a conditional vector field
v_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
generating
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
. Moreover,
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
should be consistent with
p_{0}(u_{0})
and
p_{1}(u_{1})
, in the sense that
\int p_{0}(u|u_{1})p_{1}(u_{1})\text{d}u_{1}=p_{0}(u)
and
\int p_{1}(u|u_{1})p_{1}(u_{1})\text{d}u_{1}=p_{1}(u)
.
The corresponding marginal probability path is then given by
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau})=\int p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})p_{1}(u_{1})\text{d}u_{1}
. As shown in previous work (
[
15
]
), the marginal vector field generating
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau})
is given by the following equation

\begin{split}v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})=\int v_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})\frac{p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})p_{1}(u_{1})}{p_{\tau}(u_{\tau})}\text{d}u_{1}.\end{split}

(2)

In practice, we choose
p_{0}
that is easy to sample from, e.g., Gaussian distributions, parameterize the vector field as
v^{\theta}_{\tau}
, and learn the aforementioned vector field
v_{\tau}
by minimizing loss:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{marginal}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\text{Unif}[0,1],u_{\tau}\sim p_{\tau}}\left\|v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})-v^{\theta}_{\tau}(u_{\tau})\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

With
Equation

2
, loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{marginal}}(\theta)
simplifies to the following flow-matching loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
(
[
18
]
):

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{marginal}}(\theta)={}&\mathbb{E}_{u_{1}\sim p_{1},\tau\sim\text{Unif}[0,1],u_{\tau}\sim p_{\tau}(\cdot|u_{1})}\\
{}&\hskip 0.0pt\left\|v_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})-v^{\theta}_{\tau}(u_{\tau})\right\|^{2}+C\\
\eqqcolon{}&\mathcal{L}(\theta)+C\end{split}

where
C
is a constant.
This formulation translates the loss on unknown marginal vector field
v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})
to conditional vector field
v_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
, which can be easily derived given
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})
, thereby making the training objective tractable.

A popular choice of the conditional distribution is
p_{\tau}(\cdot|u_{1})=\mathcal{N}(\tau u_{1},(1-\tau)^{2}I)
, often referred to as the conditional optimal transport (CondOT) probability path.
It can be generated by conditional vector field
v_{\tau}(u_{\tau}|u_{1})=(u_{1}-u_{\tau})/(1-\tau)
.
With this conditional distribution, the above flow matching loss simplifies to

\begin{split}{}&\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{u_{1}\sim p_{1},\tau\sim\text{Unif}[0,1],\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\\
{}&\hskip 40.00006pt\left\|(u_{1}-\epsilon)-v^{\theta}_{\tau}(\tau u_{1}+(1-\tau)\epsilon)\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

III-A

SPI with Flow-based Policies

Starting from any policy
\pi
, SPI iteratively performs soft policy evaluation and soft policy improvement. In soft policy evaluation, we estimate the soft
Q^{\pi}
function for current policy
\pi
by repeatedly applying the soft Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}
until convergence (line 5).
In soft policy improvement, we update
\pi

\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)\propto\exp\left(Q^{\pi}(x,\cdot)/\alpha\right)
for every
x
(line 6).
If every step is performed perfectly without error, the above procedure is guaranteed to learn a
\pi^{\star}
such that
Q^{\pi^{\star}}(x,u)\geq Q^{\pi}(x,u)
for any policy
\pi
and
(x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
.

To apply such flow-based policies to the SPI algorithm, the following challenges need to be resolved.

•

Challenge 1. How to calculate policy entropy?
The first challenge lies in policy evaluation
Equation

3
, where we need to estimate the policy entropy
H(\pi(\cdot|x))=\mathbb{E}_{u}(\log(\pi(u|x)))
. The estimation of policy entropy requires action probability
\pi(u|x)
, which is non-trivial to calculate for a flow-based policy. To tackle this issue, we will revisit the well-established technique of instantaneous change-of-variables
[
16
]
(
Section

III-B

•

Challenge 2. How to perform policy improvement?
The second challenge lies in policy improvement
Equation

4
. To update the flow-based policy from
\pi(\cdot|x)
to
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)\propto\exp\left(Q^{\pi}(x,\cdot)/\alpha\right)
using standard flow matching, we update its velocity field
v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(u_{x,\tau})
by minimizing the following loss:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{x}(\theta)={}&\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\pi^{+}(\cdot|x),\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 0.0pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

(5)

This would require samples
u
from the target distribution
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)
, which are unavailable before policy improvement. To tackle this issue, we draw ideas from importance sampling
[
19
,
7
]
and propose an online variant of flow matching, importance sampling flow matching (ISFM), which requires only samples from a sampling distribution with a support larger than the target distribution and is theoretically guaranteed to output a close estimation to the target distribution with enough samples (
Section

III-C
).

III-B

Calculating Action Probability in Policy Evaluation

To evaluate the policy entropy in soft
Q
function
Equation

3
for any given policy
\pi(\cdot|x)
, we need to calculate action probability
\pi(u|x)
. We revisit the instantaneous change-of-variables technique for this calculation.

Theorem 1

(Theorem 4.7 in
[
16
]
)
.

Consider vector field
v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})
and the generated probability
p_{\tau}(u_{\tau})
. Suppose both of them are continuously differentiable in
(\tau,x)
. The following holds for all
\tau\in[0,1]
:

\begin{split}\frac{\text{d}\log p_{\tau}(u_{\tau})}{\text{d}\tau}=-\text{tr}\left(\frac{\partial v_{\tau}(u_{\tau})}{\partial u_{\tau}}\right).\qed\end{split}

Now consider any state
x
and flow-based policy
\pi(\cdot|x)
with velocity field
v_{x,\tau}^{\theta}(u_{x,\tau})
and generated probability
p_{x,\tau}^{\theta}(u_{x,\tau})
. By the above theorem, we have,

\begin{split}\log\pi(u_{x,1}|x)=&\log p_{x,1}^{\theta}(u_{x,1})\\
=&\log p_{x,0}^{\theta}(u_{x,0})+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\text{d}\log p_{x,\tau}^{\theta}(u_{x,\tau})}{\text{d}\tau}\text{d}\tau\\
=&\log p_{x,0}^{\theta}(u_{x,0})-\int_{0}^{1}\text{tr}\left(\frac{\partial v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(u_{x,\tau})}{\partial u_{x,\tau}}\right)\text{d}\tau.\end{split}

(6)

Thus, to calculate action probability
\log\pi(u_{x,1}|x))
for action
u_{x,1}=u_{x,0}+\int_{0}^{1}v_{x,\tau}^{\theta}(u_{x,\tau})\text{d}\tau
, we only need to calculate Jacobian of the vector field
\partial v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}/\partial u_{\tau}
and integrate the trace of the Jacobian along the path.
An empirical mean of this probability then gives an estimation of the desired entropy term
\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|x))
in the soft policy evaluation
Equation

3
.

III-C

Importance Sampling Flow Matching in Policy Improvement

To perform soft policy improvement
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)\propto\exp\left(Q^{\pi}(x,\cdot)/\alpha\right)
for state
x
, standard flow matching requires samples from the target distribution
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)
to compute and minimize the flow matching loss
Equation

5
. However, as discussed in the previous sections, such samples are generally unavailable.

To address this issue, we adopt the idea of importance sampling by drawing samples from a easy-to-sample distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
, which will be specified later.
With
\{u^{(i)}\sim\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we compute the following importance sampling loss:

\begin{split}{}&\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\pi^{+}(u^{(i)}|x)}{\tilde{p}(u^{(i)}|x)}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u^{(i)}+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u^{(i)}-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

(7)

In expectation,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}
is equivalent to the original loss
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\theta)
because

\begin{split}{}&\mathcal{L}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{u^{(i)}\sim\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)\right)\\
={}&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int\tilde{p}(u^{(i)}|x)\frac{\pi^{+}(u^{(i)}|x)}{\tilde{p}(u^{(i)}|x)}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u^{(i)}+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u^{(i)}-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}\text{d}u^{(i)}\\
={}&\int\pi^{+}(u|x)\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}\text{d}u\\
={}&\mathcal{L}_{x}(\theta).\end{split}

One can then minimize the importance sampling loss within a parameter set
\Theta
, obtain minimizer

\begin{split}\widehat{\theta}\coloneqq\mathop{\arg\max}_{\theta\in\Theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)\in\Theta,\end{split}

and output policy
\widehat{\pi}^{+}(\cdot|x)=p_{x,1}^{\widehat{\theta}}(\cdot)
.

To analyze the error of
p_{x,1}^{\widehat{\theta}}(\cdot)
and quantify how the sampling distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
influence this error, we present the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Theta
be a finite parameter space. Suppose the target distribution
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)
satisfies
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)=p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot)
for some
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
. Suppose
v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(u)
is both continuously differentiable in
(\tau,u)
and Lipschitz in
u
with constant
L
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

Let
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
be any sampling distribution whose support is a super set of the support of
p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot)
. Suppose

\begin{split}{}&\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\tilde{p}(\cdot|x),\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u-\epsilon)\right\|^{8}\leq M^{2}.\end{split}

(8)

for constant
M
. Then the output distribution
p_{x,1}^{\widehat{\theta}}
satisfies

\begin{split}{}&\mathbb{E}\left[W_{2}(p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}},p_{x,1}^{\widehat{\theta}})^{2}\right]\leq 4\sqrt{2}\exp\left(1+2L\right)\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt\cdot\sqrt{M\log(2|\Theta|)}\frac{\sqrt[4]{\exp\left(3D_{4}(p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}\|\tilde{p}(\cdot|x))\right)}}{\sqrt{N}}.\end{split}

Here
W_{2}(p,q)
is the 2-Wasserstein distance and
D_{4}(p\|q)=1/3\ln\mathbb{E}_{u\sim q}\left[(p(u)/q(u))^{4}\right]
is the fourth-order Renyi divergence
[
20
]
.
∎

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

A
.
As shown in
Theorem

2
, when we sample from
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
and optimize the empirical loss
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)
,
the error in the output distribution
p^{\widehat{\theta}}_{x,1}
is inversely proportional to the square root of the sample size
N
and proportional to
D_{4}(p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}\|\tilde{p}(\cdot|x))
, i.e., the fourth-order Renyi divergence between sampling distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
and target distribution
p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}
. Therefore, the closer
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
is to
p_{x,1}^{\theta^{\star}}
, the less samples we need for policy improvement.

Based on this intuition, we propose to use the current policy as sampling distribution, i.e.,
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)=\pi(\cdot|x)
. As indicated by previous works
[
2
,
5
,
17
]
, the sequence of policies
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
from the SAC algorithm converges to an optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
, indicating an decreasing upper bound on the distance between current policy
\pi(\cdot|x)
and next policy
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)
. Consequently, by using
\pi(\cdot|x)
as the sampling distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)
, we can expect increasingly accurate policy improvement as the algorithm proceeds.

Remark 1
.

We leave for future work to provide rigorous upper bounds on the distance, especially the fourth-order Renyi-divergence
D_{4}(\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)\|\pi^{k}(\cdot|x))
, between
\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)
and
\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)
. Given this upper bound, one can carefully design an efficient sampling schedule that dynamically determines how many samples are needed in each episode to perform policy improvement.∎

Finally, with sampling distribution
\tilde{p}(\cdot|x)=\pi(\cdot|x)
, we obtain the following importance sampling loss

\begin{split}{}&\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{IS},x}(\theta)\\
={}&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\pi^{+}(u^{(i)}|x)}{\pi(u^{(i)}|x)}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 0.0pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u^{(i)}+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u^{(i)}-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}\\
\propto{}&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\exp\left(Q^{\pi}(x,u^{(i)})/\alpha\right)}{\pi(u^{(i)}|x)}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\tau\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]}\\
{}&\hskip 0.0pt\left\|v^{\theta}_{x,\tau}(\tau u^{(i)}+(1-\tau)\epsilon)-(u^{(i)}-\epsilon)\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

(9)

Here in the second line we have used definition
\pi^{+}(\cdot|x)\propto\exp\left(Q^{\pi}(x,\cdot)/\alpha\right)
. Finally, to calculate

SAC with Flow-based Policies

IV
A Case Study — Max-Entropy LQR

In this section, as a case study, we introduce the max-entropy LQR problem, a class of max-entropy RL problems with linear state transition and quadratic reward function.
In max-entropy LQR problems, the SPI algorithm generates a policy with a closed-form solution in every episode. By analyzing the closed-loop solutions, we can theoretically confirm that the final output policy is optimal. We then perform simulations with SAC-ISFM (

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose variants of the SPI and SAC algorithms with flow-based policies. In policy evaluation, we employ the instantaneous change-of-variables to estimate entropy of the flow-based policy. In policy improvement, we propose importance sampling flow matching, a provably efficient algorithm that updates the flow-based policy using samples from a user-specified sampling distribution rather than the target distribution as required by standard flow matching.
Finally, we conduct a case study of the proposed algorithms on max-entropy LQR problems. We confirm that SPI and SAC with flow-based policies learn the optimal solution accurately.
