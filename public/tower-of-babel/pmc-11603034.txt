Spatial variation, 20-year trends, and determinants of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under-five children in Ethiopia: a geo-spatial and multivariate decomposition analysis (2000–2019)
Authors: Tegene Atamenta kitaw, Biruk Beletew Abate, Befkad Derese Tilahun, Molla Azmeraw, Mulat Ayele, Eyob Shitie Lake, Alemu Birara Zemariam, Gizachew Yilak, Mulat Awoke Kassa, Ribka Nigatu Haile
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite extensive efforts, malnutrition persists as a critical public health concern, particularly in developing nations like Ethiopia. Concurrent occurrence of wasting and stunting poses severe consequences. Analyzing trends and factors contributing to this double burden is vital for evaluating and enhancing existing nutritional programs. Additionally, understanding the spatial distribution of malnutrition is crucial for targeted resource allocation, expediting progress towards optimal child growth. A 20-year trend and spatial analysis was conducted among 27,138 weighted samples of under-five children using EDHS (2000–2019). The data extraction was conducted between October 1 and November 1, 2023. STATA 18, ArcGIS Pro, and Sat Scan 9.6 were used for analysis. A logit-based multivariate decomposition analysis was used to identify contributing factors for the change in the double burden of wasting and stunting in the past 20 years. The statistical significance was declared at a P-value < 0.05. The double burden of wasting and stunting had shown a decline from 8.42% in 2000 to 3.31% in 2019. There is a clustered distribution of wasting and stunting (Moran’s I = 0.0712, p-value = 0.0290). Significant clustering is detected in the Somali, Afar, and Tigray regions. Among the changes in the effect of the characteristics (coefficient), maternal education level [β = −0.0012, 95% CI −0.0021, −0.0006], wealth level [β = −0.0049, 95% CI −0.0223, −0.0014], family size [β = −0.0014, 95% CI −0.0132, −0.0009] and media access [β = −0.0027, 95% CI −0.0039, −0.0014] were found to be significant contributors to the reduction of the double burden of wasting and stunting over the past 20 years. Despite the significant progress observed in the past 20 years, the problem still requires urgent attention. Thus, improving maternal education and household wealth levels is crucial. Furthermore, enhancing access to and utilization of family planning services and media coverage will help alleviate the burden and end malnutrition. Additionally, deploying additional resources in hotspot areas is also recommended.Keywords: Spatial variation, Trends, Determinants, Double burden of wasting and stuntingSubject terms: Paediatrics, Paediatric research

Introduction

Combating childhood undernutrition continue to be one of a significant global health challenge. Globally, reducing the burden of wasting and stunting among under five children is a top priority. In 2022, stunting affected 148.1 million children worldwide, representing 22.3% of those under the age of 5. Additionally, around 45 million children under five experienced wasting. The majority of children affected by wasting and stunting were in Asia and Africa1. The burden of wasting and stunting is profound in low- and middle-income countries2. Urgent measures are required to achieve Sustainable Development Goal 2, which aims to eradicate all types of malnutrition by 20303.

Wasting signifies a recent and substantial decline in weight caused by insufficient food intake or the presence of infectious illnesses like diarrhea in individuals. Stunting arises from long-term or recurrent malnutrition, often associated with impoverished socioeconomic conditions, inadequate maternal health and nutrition, frequent bouts of illness, and/or insufficient early-life feeding and care for children4. Wasting and stunting, among the most prevalent types of undernutrition, are closely interconnected and frequently coexist in the same children5. The double burden of wasting and stunting is referring to children under 5 years experiencing both acute malnutrition (wasting) and chronic malnutrition (stunting) simultaneously6,7. When both wasting and stunting occur concurrently, the risk of mortality is substantially increased compared to when they occur separately8,9. While wasting represents a short-term health concern, repeated occurrences of it could result in stunting10. This scenario is likely common in numerous resource-constrained environments where dietary intake remains consistently insufficient and infectious diseases are widespread. In such contexts, the potential for catch-up growth might be limited, consequently leading to a persistently high proportion of children experiencing stunting11.

The simultaneous occurrence of wasting and stunting is clinically significant due to the significant high risk of death among younger children, estimated to be 11.1 times higher than that of children experiencing either wasting or stunting alone7. Additionally, the combined impact is estimated to explain approximately 51% of total mortality12. Children facing both wasting and stunting are particularly vulnerable to infections due to weakened immune systems, exacerbating their already precarious health status13. Moreover, malnutrition during critical growth periods can impair cognitive development, potentially leading to long-term deficits in learning and intellectual abilities14. The coexistence of wasting and stunting further delays physical growth, resulting in shorter stature and diminished overall body size15. Perhaps most alarmingly, children affected by both conditions face a significantly higher risk of mortality9, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions to address these intersecting forms of undernutrition and safeguard the health and future prospects of vulnerable children worldwide.

Recent estimates from 84 countries and India, show that approximately 3.0% and 8.7% of children under five suffer from the coexistence of these two forms of undernutrition, respectively5,16. In Africa, country-level studies have also revealed different prevalence rates, such as 12% in Niger17, 3.5% in Mozambique12, 1.4% in Ghana18, 6.2% in Senegal18, 5.0% in Uganda19, and 5.8% in Ethiopia20.

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aim to reduce newborn and under-five mortality by 2030, targeting rates as low as 12 deaths per 1000 live births for newborns and 25 deaths per 1000 live births for children under the age of five21. However, malnutrition is one of the major causes of death in children under five, especially in Ethiopia. To achieve the SDG goal and accelerate progress towards ending all forms of malnutrition, recent data are crucial. Despite the prevalence of concurrent wasting and stunting investigated in many regions, the trend for changes in the extent of the problem and factors contributing to the double burden of wasting and stunting are not adequately studied. However, understanding these trends and their contributing factors is crucial for assessing progress and developing tailored interventions aimed at ending malnutrition. Furthermore, the current spatial distribution of the problem, which involves mapping to show its extent with respect to geographical location, is not well understood. Mapping the problem is critical for developing geographically targeted interventions and deploying resources effectively to areas where they are needed most.

Methods

Study setting, study period and data source

According to forecasts from trading economics and data from recent census figures, the total population of Ethiopia was 115.0 million by 202022. The Ethiopian Demographic and Health Surveys (EDHS) final report includes inclusive data at the country level from the nine regional states and two municipal administrations. The administrative levels were divided into zones, woreda, and so forth. A spatial and decomposition analysis was conducted among under five children. The EDHS collects pertinent information mainly regarding maternity health care utilization, marriage and sexual behaviors, child feeding practice, children and women’s dietary condition, and children’s mortality23.

Data extraction, population

This study includes data from EDHS surveys conducted between 2000 and 2019. First, the project proposal was sent to the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program. After a detailed review process, the DHS program accepted the proposal and granted access with an approval letter to use the survey datasets. Data extraction was done to select children aged under 5 years. The data extraction for this study was done between October 1 and November 1, 2023. All children aged under five were the source population, whereas all children aged under five in the selected enumeration area were the study population.

Sampling methods

The EDHS sample was stratified and selected in two levels. sampling strata’s were produced after stratifying each region into urban and rural areas. Using probability proportion, the enumeration areas (from urban and from rural) were selected in the first stage. Newly formed household listing was used in the second stage to choose a set number of households per cluster with an equal probability of systematic selection. Sample allocation was done to verify that survey precision was equivalent across regions. Proportional number enumeration areas were selected from the largest the smallest regions (including two city administrations). The complete sampling procedure is available in the EDHS final report24. In the current study, a total of 27,138 weighted children aged under five were participated.

Definitions

Double burden of wasting and stunting referred to if the child had both stunting and wasting at a time. Stunting: height for age < − 2 SD of the WHO Child Growth Standards median. Wasting: weight for height < − 2 SD of the WHO Child Growth Standards median25.

Data processing and analysis

Data were extracted from the five EDHS datasets (2000–2019) using STATA version 17. This statistical software was employed for performing quantitative analyses and developing models to interpret the data. Sorting and listing were used to find any missing values. Descriptive statistics were calculated using frequency and percentage. Data were weighted, cleaned, edited, and recoded. STATA version 17 was used for non-spatial analysis. ArcGIS Pro and SaTScan version 9.6 were used to map the double burden of wasting and stunting at regional and district levels. ArcGIS Pro was utilized for mapping and spatial analysis, helping to visualize geographic patterns and spatial relationships within the data. SaTScan was applied to detect spatial clusters and analyze spatial patterns, facilitating the identification of areas with significant spatial associations. The weighting factor built in the dataset (v005/100000) and the “svy command” were applied to deal with over and under sampling biases and to gauge for the complex survey design and generalizability of the findings respectively.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation with Global Moran’s I was computed to determine whether double burden of wasting and stunting among children is randomly distributed, clustered, or dispersed. Unlike traditional analyses that assume observations are independent of each other, spatial autocorrelation considers the geographic relationship between data points. This is crucial for identifying regional clusters or hotspots of issues like malnutrition, which standard analyses might overlook. Global Moran’s I was specifically used to assess the overall spatial distribution pattern of the study variables. This measure evaluates whether similar values, whether high or low, are clustered together or dispersed across the study area. Positive Moran’s I values indicate spatial clustering, where high values are surrounded by other high values, and low values are surrounded by other low values, suggesting that similar values are more likely to be found in close proximity. Conversely, negative Moran’s I values reflect spatial dispersion, meaning that high values are surrounded by low values and vice versa, indicating a tendency for dissimilar values to be adjacent. Values close to zero suggest a random distribution of values with no significant spatial autocorrelation, meaning that the location of high or low values is not systematically related to their neighbors.

Hot spot and spatial interpolation analysis

Hot spot analysis was done using Getis-Ord Gi* spatial statistics to identify statistically significant spatial clusters of cold spots(low) and hot spots (high) of double burden of wasting and stunting. Clusters with high Gi* values are statistically significant hot spots, while clusters with low Gi* values are statistically significant cold spots. Ordinary kriging interpolation was computed to estimate the value of unsampled locations from sampled data points.

Trend and decomposition analysis

The trend and multivariate decomposition analyses were performed using STATA version 18. Multivariate decomposition analysis is a statistical method used to break down differences in outcomes, such as malnutrition rates, between two groups. The change in the double burden of wasting and stunting is attributed to differences in survey composition (variations in characteristics or endowments) and/or variations in the effects of explanatory variables across surveys (such as differences in coefficients). Unlike conventional regression analysis, which typically examines relationships within a single group, multivariate decomposition offers a deeper understanding of disparities. It clarifies how much of the gap is due to tangible differences and how much is due to less obvious factors, guiding more targeted interventions. For example, in studying regional differences in the double burden of wasting and stunting, this method can reveal whether the gap is primarily due to socioeconomic factors or other significant influences. This approach provides valuable insights for addressing the root causes of health and social inequalities. For the decomposition analysis, we appended the extracted data from 2000, 2005, 2011, 2016, and 2019 using the Stata command “append using” after ensuring the consistency of variables across the surveys. The multivariate decomposition analysis technique was used to analyze the difference in the double burden of wasting and stunting between two points in time (2000 and 2019). Decomposition analysis is a frequently employed method in public health research, aiming to identify components of a change over time and identify factors affecting the change26–28. A logit based multivariate decomposition analysis (using mvdcmp STATA command) was used to identify factors that contribute for the change in the double burden of wasting and stunting for the past 20 years. Statistical significance was determined at p-values of ≤ 0.05.

Trends of the double burden of wasting and stunting

The double burden of wasting and stunting had shown decrement from 8.42% in 2000 to 3.31% in 2019. (Fig. 1).

Spatial and incremental autocorrelation

The spatial distribution of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under five children in Ethiopia is clustered with Global Moran’s I value of 0.0712, p-value = 0.0290, and Z-score of 2.183. Thus, the double burden of wasting and stunting has a spatial dependency. In addition, the likelihood of this clustered pattern because of random chance is less than 5%. (Fig. 2). The line graph of incremental autocorrelation shows the minimum and the maximum distance band. The minimum distance at the beginning was 155193.00 m (Z-score = 2.06, P-value < 0.001), whereas the first maximum peak was 179260.57 m (Z-score = 2.24, P-value < 0.001). (Fig. 3).

Hotspot and cold spot analysis

Hotspot and cold spot analysis was computed to identify areas with a high and low prevalence of double burden of wasting and stunting. Thus, significant clustering of the double burden of wasting and stunting is detected in Somali, Afar and Tigray regions. (Fig. 4).

Hotspot and cold spot analysis of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under five children in Ethiopia.

Spatial interpolation

Ordinary kriging interpolation was computed to predict the double burden of wasting and stunting distribution among under five children in Ethiopia. Thus, the highest predictive prevalence of the double burden of wasting and stunting is found in the Eastern, North Eastern and Northern Ethiopia. Meanwhile, low prediction was found mainly in central parts of Ethiopia. (Fig. 5).

Ordinary kriging interpolation to predict the prevalence of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under five children in Ethiopia.

Satscan analysis

Purely spatial analysis using the Bernoulli model was done to identify clusters with high or low rates of double burden of wasting and stunting. The primary significant sat scan was identified in Eastern Ethiopia (Somali) at 6.639662 N, 44.465853 E / geographic location (radius of 381.83 km km) with a relative risk of 3.53 (P-value ≤ 0.001) and Log likelihood ratio (LLR) of 13.65. Children live in this area were more than three and half times likely to suffer from the double burden wasting and stunting. The prevalence of double burden wasting and stunting was higher with the circle hole than the outside. (Fig. 6).

Spatial clustering of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under five children in Ethiopia.

Multivariate decomposition analysis

The multivariable decomposition analysis revealed that 88.41% of decline in the double burden of wasting and stunting from 2000 (8.42%) to 2016 (3.31%) was attributed to change in the effect of coefficient. Only 11.59% of change was because of difference in characteristics (population dynamics). (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the trend, determinant factors for the changes, and spatial distribution of the double burden of wasting and stunting in Ethiopia over the past 20 years. Thus, the double burden of wasting and stunting had shown decrement from 8.42% in 2000 to 3.31% in 2019. The spatial distribution of the double burden of wasting and stunting among under five children in Ethiopia is clustered. Significant clustering of the double burden of wasting and stunting is detected in Somali, Afar and Tigray regions. Maternal education level, wealth level, family size and media access were found to be a significant contributor for the change in the double burden of wasting and stunting from 2000 to 2019.

Significant clustering of the double burden of wasting and stunting is detected in Somali, Afar and Tigray regions. Resources such as food aid, healthcare services, and nutritional programs need to be directed specifically to these areas, with interventions tailored to the unique cultural and environmental contexts of each region. This includes adapting nutritional programs to local dietary practices and improving healthcare access, especially in remote communities. Robust monitoring and evaluation systems are essential to track the effectiveness of these efforts and allow for adjustments as needed. By focusing on these high-burden regions, interventions can more effectively reduce undernutrition and improve the health and development of children in these vulnerable populations.

This study indicates a decline in the double burden of wasting and stunting was observed, plummeting from 8.42% in 2000 to a mere 3.31% by 2019. A similar pattern of reduction was evident in a study conducted in India, with figures decreasing from 8.7% in 2005–06 to 5.2% in 2019–202016. This study found that 3.31% of children experienced the double burden of wasting and stunting by 2019. This finding differs from a recent study conducted in the Kersa district, where 5.8% of children were affected by both wasting and stunting20. Similarly, studies in Senegal and Uganda revealed concomitant wasting and stunting rates of 6.2%30 and 5.0%31, respectively. However, lower prevalence rates were reported in studies conducted in Ghana (1.4%)18 and Indonesia (2.4%)32. A meta-analysis involving 84 countries also found a prevalence of 3.0%5. The possible discrepancy in the findings could stem from various factors such as differences in sample size, demographics, geographical locations, methodologies employed in data collection, and timing of the studies. Additionally, variations in healthcare infrastructure, access to resources, socio-economic conditions, cultural practices, and dietary habits among populations studied may contribute to the differences observed in the prevalence rates of wasting and stunting. Furthermore, the variation in prevalence could be due to differences in food insecurity, agro-pastoral practices, ethnicity and social stability.

Over the past two decades, there has been a remarkable 1.49% decrease in wasting and stunting, attributed to the effect of family size. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Iran, which showed that inequality between surveys in under-five child malnutrition was influenced by the coefficient of family size33. Over the past two decades, the observed decrease in wasting and stunting, particularly linked to family size, can be attributed to several factors. Improvements in socioeconomic conditions, education—especially among women—health interventions, and access to healthcare have likely contributed to better nutrition and child health outcomes. Additionally, cultural shifts toward smaller family sizes can enhance resource allocation per child, while advancements in agricultural practices and environmental conditions, along with supportive policies, have further promoted food security and health. These interrelated changes impact the coefficients associated with wasting and stunting, highlighting the complex dynamics influencing child nutrition over time.

This underscores the critical role of family planning in promoting child health and well-being on a global scale. This statistic highlights the immense impact that family planning can have on improving child health and well-being. Policymakers can leverage these insights to prioritize and invest in comprehensive family planning programs aimed at empowering individuals with access to contraceptive methods and reproductive healthcare services. By integrating family planning initiatives into broader public health agendas, policymakers can address not only immediate health concerns but also long-term challenges such as malnutrition and stunted growth. Moreover, recognizing the transformative potential of smaller family sizes can inform the design of targeted interventions and policies that prioritize maternal and child health outcomes, thereby contributing to the achievement of sustainable development goals and ensuring a healthier future for generations to come.

Improved maternal educational level is a significant contributing factor to the decrement of the double burden of wasting and stunting from 2000 to 2019. Several factors could be responsible for the changing coefficients over time in this study. For instance, the impact of education on health outcomes may vary due to shifts in societal norms, access to resources, or changes in policy and programs aimed at improving maternal and child health. Additionally, as maternal education levels improve, mothers may become more aware of nutritional practices, healthcare access, and child-rearing strategies, leading to better health outcomes for their children. Similarly, in other studies conducted in A study conducted in Burkina Faso found that mothers’ educational levels made the most significant contribution to the decline in malnutrition rates between 2003 and 201034. Similarly, a study from India found that the effect of maternal education levels accounted for 8% of the change in stunting and 16% of the change in wasting35.

The household wealth level emerges as a significant contributor to changes in the concurrent levels of wasting and stunting in Ethiopia. The changing coefficients over time in this study, particularly regarding the influence of household wealth on wasting and stunting, can be attributed to several factors. Economic fluctuations, such as periods of growth or recession, may impact household income and access to resources, directly affecting nutritional outcomes. Policy interventions, such as government-led health and nutrition programs, can also shift the role wealth plays in mitigating malnutrition, especially if targeted at low-income groups. Access to healthcare and improvements in maternal and child health services may further reduce the effects of poverty on child malnutrition. Consequently, middle and high-income households influence a decrease in the double burden of wasting and stunting. This is also evident in a study conducted in Pakistan, which found that wealth status is a significant contributor to the observed changes in malnutrition36. This suggests that socioeconomic factors significantly influence nutritional outcomes among children, with impoverished households facing a heightened risk of experiencing both forms of malnutrition concurrently. Consequently, there is a clear imperative for policymakers and stakeholders to prioritize poverty reduction strategies and implement comprehensive interventions that improve access to nutrition, healthcare, and other essential resources for vulnerable households, thereby mitigating the double burden of malnutrition and promoting better health outcomes for children worldwide.

Furthermore, Media access significantly influenced the reduction in the co-occurrence of wasting and stunting. The changing coefficients over time in this study could be attributed to various factors. One key reason might be the evolving content and messaging provided through media channels. As public health campaigns, nutritional education, and health awareness programs become more prevalent in the media, their influence on household behavior, particularly regarding child nutrition, may increase. Improved media access could lead to better awareness of proper feeding practices, hygiene, and healthcare services, which in turn could contribute to the reduction of malnutrition, including the co-occurrence of wasting and stunting. Similarly, previous studies conducted in India found that media exposure during the inter-survey period had the most significant influence on changes in malnutrition levels37.

Exposure to mass media can significantly impact efforts to address wasting and stunting, two critical forms of malnutrition affecting children in various parts of the world. Mass media serves as a powerful tool for disseminating essential information about proper nutrition, breastfeeding practices, timely introduction of complementary foods, and recognizing signs of malnutrition38. By reaching a broad audience, including mothers, caregivers, and communities, mass media campaigns can raise awareness about the severity of wasting and the importance of seeking timely treatment. Similarly, mass media can educate the public about the long-term consequences of stunting and promote behaviors that support optimal growth and development39, such as proper maternal nutrition, exclusive breastfeeding, and access to clean water and sanitation. By leveraging mass media channels, stakeholders can mobilize support, influence behavior change, and advocate for policies and interventions that address the root causes of wasting and stunting, ultimately improving the nutritional status and well-being of children worldwide.

The finding that differences in the distribution of observable characteristics or endowments were not statistically significant for the 20-year trends in the double burden of wasting and stunting implies that changes in factors such as income, education, or other demographic characteristics have not significantly influenced these trends over time. This stability suggests that the underlying risk factors associated with wasting and stunting have remained relatively constant, indicating that interventions aimed at altering these characteristics might not have been sufficiently impactful or that the characteristics themselves have not changed significantly in the population. As a result, the focus of efforts might need to shift from merely addressing these observable characteristics to exploring differences in coefficients or other factors that might be driving the trends.

This study utilizes 20 years of nationally representative data, enhancing its overall applicability and statistical robustness, and making its findings relevant on a national level. However, reliance on secondary data limits the exploration of underlying causes behind this phenomenon. The data may not capture all factors influencing the shift in the double burden of wasting and stunting over the past two decades. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of the data hinders the establishment of causal relationships between explanatory and dependent variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand causality and the impact of temporal factors.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight both progress and ongoing challenges in addressing the double burden of wasting and stunting. While the reduction in prevalence from 8.42% in 2000 to 3.31% in 2019 is promising, significant clustering in the Somali, Afar, and Tigray regions points to persistent regional disparities that require targeted intervention. Key factors influencing the burden include maternal education level, wealth status, family size, and media access. Therefore, multifaceted interventions are necessary. Investing in education, especially for women, is crucial for further reducing the burden, as it empowers individuals to make informed decisions about nutrition and healthcare. Additionally, prioritizing poverty alleviation through microfinance programs and job training can support low-income households. Enhancing access to family planning and reproductive health services can also help mitigate the risk of malnutrition. Finally, leveraging media for nutrition education campaigns can promote healthy habits and foster optimal child health and growth. Addressing these areas with focused interventions will be essential in continuing to combat the double burden of wasting and stunting effectively.
