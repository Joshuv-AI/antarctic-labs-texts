Center of double extension regular algebras of type (14641)
Andrés Rubiano

Abstract
. In this paper we compute the center and, in several cases, central subalgebras of double Ore extensions of type ( 14641 14641 ) under suitable restrictions on the defining parameters. Part of the analysis is supported by computations in SageMath . As an application, we provide new examples related to the Zariski cancellation problem.

Introduction

Ore
[
29
,
30
]
introduced a fundamental class of noncommutative polynomial rings that now permeates ring theory and noncommutative algebra. Given an associative unital ring
R
, an endomorphism
\sigma
of
R
, and a
\sigma
-derivation
\delta
, the Ore extension (skew polynomial ring)
R[x;\sigma,\delta]
is generated by
R
and one element
x
subject to
xr=\sigma(r)x+\delta(r)
for all
r\in R
. A wide body of work studies their ring-theoretic, homological, and geometric features and applications (see, for instance,
[
6
,
7
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
28
,
22
,
33
,
35
]
and references therein).

Artin-Schelter regular algebras, introduced in
[
1
]
, are widely regarded as noncommutative analogues of commutative polynomial algebras and constitute a cornerstone of noncommutative projective geometry (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
32
]
). In particular, the classification of quantum
\mathbb{P}^{3}
’s is intimately tied to the classification of Artin-Schelter regular algebras of global dimension four. Motivated by the search for new regular algebras generated in degree one (compare
[
32
, Examples 1.8 and 1.9]
and the dimension-three classifications
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, cf.
[
37
]
), Zhang and Zhang
[
41
,
42
]
introduced
double Ore extensions
as a natural two-generator generalization of Ore extensions. Although double extensions share some formal similarities with iterated Ore extensions, neither class contains the other in general
[
41
, Example 4.2 and Proposition 0.5(c)]
, and necessary and sufficient conditions for being an iterated Ore extension were given by Carvalho et al.
[
8
, Theorems 2.2 and 2.4]
. Moreover, as emphasized in
[
41
]
, standard techniques for Ore extensions often fail in the double-extension setting, and structural properties are substantially harder to control; see also, e.g.,
[
15
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
27
,
31
,
38
,
43
]
.

In
[
42
]
, Zhang and Zhang studied Artin–Schelter regular algebras
B
of global dimension four generated in degree one. By
[
25
]
, such an algebra is generated by
2
,
3
, or
4
elements, and when
B
has four generators the minimal projective resolution of the trivial module
\Bbbk_{B}
has the form

(1.1)

0\to B(-4)\to B(-3)^{\oplus 4}\to B(-2)^{\oplus 6}\to B(-1)^{\oplus 4}\to B\to\Bbbk_{B}\to 0.

Accordingly, these algebras are said to be
of type

(14641)
. Their classification in
[
42
]
yields
26
families of double extensions, labeled
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\dots,\mathbb{Z}
; we denote by
\mathcal{LIST}
the class of all algebras in these families. While many algebras in
\mathcal{LIST}
are still Ore extensions, there may exist nonzero
\delta
and
\tau
such that the same underlying data
(P,\sigma)
produces a double extension
R_{P}[y_{1},y_{2};\sigma,\delta,\tau]
that is not an Ore extension
[
42
, p. 374]
.

We mention that differential-geometric aspects of these double Ore extensions have also been investigated recently. In particular, the author and Reyes studied the differential smoothness problem for double extension regular algebras of type
(14641)
and proved that these algebras are not differentially smooth in the sense of noncommutative differential geometry; see
[
34
]
for details.

From a computational perspective, Gröbner-Shirshov techniques have also proved effective for these families. Indeed, Herrera, Higuera, and the author computed finite Gröbner-Shirshov bases for several regular double extension algebras of type
(14641)
via an algorithmic approach, and showed that these families admit PBW bases; see
[
20
]
.

The center
Z(A)
is a primary commutative invariant of a noncommutative algebra
A
and often serves as the bridge between noncommutative structure and commutative geometry. It governs, for instance, the behavior of representations and central characters, controls finiteness properties (e.g., module-finiteness over the center in many PI situations), and provides access to geometric stratifications of spectra via central localizations. In quantum algebra this philosophy is particularly vivid: large centers at roots of unity underpin deep links between noncommutative algebras and Poisson geometry, as illustrated by the “quantum coadjoint action” framework of De Concini–Kac–Procesi
[
12
]
.

From a computational and structural viewpoint, explicit knowledge of
Z(A)
(or useful central subalgebras) can unlock rigid invariants that constrain automorphisms and isomorphisms. A prominent example is the discriminant method, which uses central data to control automorphism groups and related rigidity properties for broad families of noncommutative algebras
[
9
,
10
]
. In the realm of skew PBW extensions, concrete descriptions of centers and centralizers have been developed and applied to structural questions
[
21
,
39
]
. These perspectives motivate the explicit center computations carried out in this work for double Ore extensions of type (
14641
).

The (commutative) Zariski cancellation problem asks whether an isomorphism
X\times\mathbb{A}^{1}\cong Y\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
forces
X\cong Y
, a question intertwined with the rigidity and recognition of affine spaces and with invariants coming from derivations and automorphism groups. Classical results settle cancellation for large classes of affine surfaces (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
), while striking counterexamples exist in positive characteristic for affine spaces of dimension
\geq 3

[
18
,
19
]
. In the noncommutative setting, Bell and Zhang formulated and developed cancellation analogues and established substantial positive results for several families, highlighting the role of central invariants
[
4
]
. Consequently, new explicit classes with well-controlled centers provide valuable testbeds and additional evidence in this active area.

The goal of this paper is to compute the centers (and certain central subalgebras) for selected members of
\mathcal{LIST}
under explicit conditions on the parameters; several computations were carried out with the aid of
SageMath
. As an application, we obtain new examples that contribute to current work on cancellation phenomena in the sense of the Zariski cancellation problem.

The article is organized as follows. Section
2
collects the necessary preliminaries on double Ore extensions and Artin-Schelter regular algebras, fixing notation and making the paper self-contained. In Section
3
we establish and exploit commutation formulas for powers of the generators in the double Ore setting, and we use these relations to compute the centers (and, in several cases, explicit central subalgebras) for selected double Ore extensions of type
(14641)
; this section also includes a brief illustrative computation in
SageMath
. Section
4
is devoted to the cancellation application: we identify those algebras in our families that are (universally/strongly) cancellative in the sense of the Zariski cancellation problem. Finally, the Appendix contains tables recording the defining relations for the
26
families in the classification, for convenient reference.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers including zero. The word
ring
means an associative ring with identity, not necessarily commutative. All vector spaces and algebras (always associative and unital) are over a fixed field
\Bbbk

Double extension regular algebras of type (14641)

We briefly review the notion of double extensions introduced by Zhang and Zhang
[
41
]
. Since their papers contain some misprints in the compatibility conditions imposed on the DE-data (see
[
41
, p. 2674]
and
[
42
, p. 379]

[

]

Definition 2.1

(
[
41
, Definition 1.3]
;
[
8
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

Let
R
be a subalgebra of a
\Bbbk
-algebra
B
.

(a)

We say that
B
is a
right double extension
of
R
if:

(i)

B
is generated by
R
together with two new indeterminates

;

(ii)

the generators
y_{1},y_{2}
satisfy

(2.1)

y_{2}y_{1}=p_{12}y_{1}y_{2}+p_{11}y_{1}^{2}+\tau_{1}y_{1}+\tau_{2}y_{2}+\tau_{0},

for some
p_{12},p_{11}\in\Bbbk
and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{0}\in R
;

(iii)

B
is a free left

-module with basis
\{y_{1}^{i}y_{2}^{j}\mid i,j\geq 0\}
;

(iv)

one has
y_{1}R+y_{2}R+R\subseteq Ry_{1}+Ry_{2}+R
.

(b)

A right double extension
B
of
R
is called a
double extension
of
R
provided:

(i)

p_{12}\neq 0
;

(ii)

B
is a free right
R
-module with basis
\{y_{2}^{i}y_{1}^{j}\mid i,j\geq 0\}
;

(iii)

y_{1}R+y_{2}R+R=Ry_{1}+Ry_{2}+R
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is classical that a two-step iterated Ore extension of the form

R[y_{1};\sigma_{1},\delta_{1}][y_{2};\sigma_{2},\delta_{2}]

is a free left
R
-module with basis
\{y_{1}^{n_{1}}y_{2}^{n_{2}}\}_{n_{1},n_{2}\geq 0}
; see
[
41
, Lemma 1.5]
.
In general, such an iterated Ore extension need not be a (right) double extension in the sense of Definition
2.1
(a), since it may fail to admit a quadratic relation of the form (
2.1
).
However, when
\sigma_{2}
is chosen so that (
2.1
) does hold, one indeed obtains a (right) double extension
[
41
, p. 2671]
.
This explains why many double extensions fall inside the iterated Ore framework, and why the freeness requirement in Definition
2.1
(a)(iii) is natural.
As Zhang and Zhang put it
[
41
, p. 2671]
, “Our definition of a double extension is neither most general nor ideal, but it works very well in
[
42
]
”.

Condition
2.1
(a)(iv) is equivalent to the existence of maps

\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}:R\to M_{2\times 2}(R),\qquad\delta=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\delta_{2}\end{bmatrix}:R\to M_{2\times 1}(R),

such that

(2.2)

\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}r=\sigma(r)\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\delta(r)\quad\text{for all }r\in R.

Whenever
B
is a right double extension of
R
, we write

B=R_{P}[y_{1},y_{2};\sigma,\delta,\tau]
,
where
P=(p_{12},p_{11})\in\Bbbk^{2}
,
\tau=\{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\}\subseteq R
, and
\sigma,\delta
are as above.
Following standard terminology,

is the

and
\tau
is the

; the collection

is referred to as the

.
A particularly important subclass is that of

[
41
, Convention 1.6(c)]
, for which
\delta=0
and
\tau=\{0,0,0\}
.
In this case we use the shorthand notation

.

For a right double extension
R_{P}[y_{1},y_{2};\sigma,\delta,\tau]
, each

and
\delta_{i}
is an endomorphism of the
\Bbbk
-vector space

.
Moreover, by

\sigma
is an algebra homomorphism and
\delta
is a
\sigma
-derivation, i.e.

is
\Bbbk

\delta(rr^{\prime})=\sigma(r)\delta(r^{\prime})+\delta(r)r^{\prime}
for all

In particular, if the matrix
\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is triangular, then
\sigma_{11}
and
\sigma_{22}
are algebra homomorphisms.

If

, then the subalgebra of

generated by

is a double extension

\Bbbk_{P}[y_{1},y_{2};\sigma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}]

\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma|_{\Bbbk}
is the canonical embedding of

into

and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta|_{\Bbbk}=0

Carvalho et al.
[
8
, Proposition 1.2]
show that this latter algebra is always an iterated Ore extension.

Remark 2.4

(i)

3.
Center and central subalgebras of double extensions

In this section we compute, in the general setting of double extensions, the commutation relations among the generating variables, with the purpose of extracting structural information about the center. Nevertheless, even under a PBW-type presentation, the amount of symbolic manipulation required in the “universal” parameter-dependent case quickly becomes prohibitive: the mixed relations involve many coefficients and produce a combinatorial explosion of overlaps and reductions. For this reason, the center (or central subalgebras) must be determined separately for each of the double extensions under consideration, that is, on a case-by-case basis. Our computational strategy is to fix a rewriting system that pushes the “bad pairs” into an ordered normal form and then to compute normal forms and commutators inside a free algebra over a suitable fraction field of parameters. This procedure is implemented in
SageMath
through the following code, which automates the reduction to normal form and the computation of commutators:

from sage.all import *

R = QQ[
’q12’,’q11’,’p12’,’p11’,
# a_{1111} ... a_{2222}
’a1111’,’a1112’,’a1211’,’a1212’,
’a1121’,’a1122’,’a1221’,’a1222’,
’a2111’,’a2112’,’a2211’,’a2212’,
’a2121’,’a2122’,’a2221’,’a2222’
]
K = FractionField(R)
(q12,q11,p12,p11,
a1111,a1112,a1211,a1212,
a1121,a1122,a1221,a1222,
a2111,a2112,a2211,a2212,
a2121,a2122,a2221,a2222) = K.gens()

A.<x1,x2,y1,y2> = FreeAlgebra(K, 4)

rules = {
# intern in x’s y y’s
(’x2’,’x1’): q12*x1*x2 + q11*x1^2,
(’y2’,’y1’): p12*y1*y2 + p11*y1^2,

# mix
(’y1’,’x1’): a1111*x1*y1 + a1112*x2*y1 + a1211*x1*y2 + a1212*x2*y2,
(’y1’,’x2’): a1121*x1*y1 + a1122*x2*y1 + a1221*x1*y2 + a1222*x2*y2,
(’y2’,’x1’): a2111*x1*y1 + a2112*x2*y1 + a2211*x1*y2 + a2212*x2*y2,
(’y2’,’x2’): a2121*x1*y1 + a2122*x2*y1 + a2221*x1*y2 + a2222*x2*y2,
}

GEN = {’x1’: x1, ’x2’: x2, ’y1’: y1, ’y2’: y2}

def _term_dict(f):
try:
return f.monomial_coefficients()
except AttributeError:
return f.dict()

def _word_to_elem(word_letters):
e = A.one()
for s in word_letters:
e *= GEN[s]
return e

def reduce_once(f):
"""Apply ONE rewrite to each monomial if possible."""
out = A.zero()
changed = False
for mon, coeff in _term_dict(A(f)).items():
letters = [str(a) for a in mon.to_word()]
done = False
for i in range(len(letters)-1):
pair = (letters[i], letters[i+1])
if pair in rules:
prefix = _word_to_elem(letters[:i])
suffix = _word_to_elem(letters[i+2:])
out += coeff * (prefix * rules[pair] * suffix)
changed = True
done = True
break
if not done:
out += coeff * _word_to_elem(letters)
return out, changed

def NF(f, max_steps=20000):
"""Normal form by iterated rewriting."""
g = A(f)
for _ in range(max_steps):
g2, ch = reduce_once(g)
if not ch:
return g
g = g2
raise RuntimeError("It didn’t finish: increase max_steps
or check if the rules produce loops.")

def comm(u,v):
return NF(u*v - v*u)

print("NF(x2*x1) =", NF(x2*x1))
print("NF(y2*y1) =", NF(y2*y1))
print("NF(y1*x2) =", NF(y1*x2))
print("[y1,x2] =", comm(y1,x2))
print("[x2,x1] =", comm(x2,x1))

Because of the size of the computations involved, we will only present two explicit examples below: one corresponding to an algebra whose center can be completely described, and another in which the same approach allows us to exhibit only a nontrivial central subalgebra.

Definition 3.1
.

For
m\geq 1
define the
q
-integer and
q
-factorial by

[m]_{q}:=1+q+\cdots+q^{m-1},\qquad[m]_{q}!:=\prod_{j=1}^{m}[j]_{q},\qquad[0]_{q}!:=1.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
,

x_{2}x_{1}^{\,n}=q_{11}[n]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+1}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n}\,x_{1}^{\,n}x_{2}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. For
n=1
, the defining relation yields

x_{2}x_{1}=q_{11}x_{1}^{2}+q_{12}x_{1}x_{2},

which is exactly the claimed formula since
\sum_{k=0}^{0}q_{12}^{k}=1
.

Assume the identity holds for some
n\geq 1
, i.e.,

x_{2}x_{1}^{\,n}=q_{11}[n]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+1}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n}\,x_{1}^{\,n}x_{2}.

Multiply on the right by
x_{1}
:

x_{2}x_{1}^{\,n+1}=q_{11}[n]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+2}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n}\,x_{1}^{\,n}(x_{2}x_{1}).

Using
x_{2}x_{1}=q_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{1}^{\,n}x_{1}=x_{1}^{\,n+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle x_{2}x_{1}^{\,n+1}

\displaystyle=q_{11}[n]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+2}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n}\,x_{1}^{\,n}\bigl(q_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}x_{1}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=q_{11}[n]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+2}\;+\;q_{11}q_{12}^{\,n}\,x_{1}^{\,n+2}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n+1}\,x_{1}^{\,n+1}x_{2}

\displaystyle=q_{11}[n+1]_{q_{12}}\,x_{1}^{\,n+2}\;+\;q_{12}^{\,n+1}\,x_{1}^{\,n+1}x_{2},

which is precisely the desired formula for
n+1
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

x_{2}^{\,n}x_{1}\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{\,k}\,q^{\,n-k}\,\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[n-k]_{q}!}\;x_{1}^{\,k+1}x_{2}^{\,n-k}.

Proof.

Set
c_{n,k}:=q_{11}^{\,k}q_{12}^{\,n-k}\dfrac{[n]_{q_{12}}!}{[n-k]_{q_{12}}!}
for
0\leq k\leq n
.
We prove by induction on
n
that

x_{2}^{\,n}x_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}\,x_{1}^{\,k+1}x_{2}^{\,n-k}.

For
n=1
, the defining relation gives

x_{2}x_{1}=q_{12}\,x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}\,x_{1}^{2}=c_{1,0}\,x_{1}x_{2}+c_{1,1}\,x_{1}^{2},

since
c_{1,0}=q_{12}
and
c_{1,1}=q_{11}
.

Assume the statement holds for some
n\geq 1
. Multiply the normal form on the left by
x_{2}
:

x_{2}^{\,n+1}x_{1}=x_{2}(x_{2}^{\,n}x_{1})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}\,x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k+1}x_{2}^{\,n-k}.

Using
x_{2}x_{1}=q_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}x_{1}^{2}
and the fact that
x_{1}
commutes with itself, we obtain

x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k+1}=(x_{2}x_{1})x_{1}^{\,k}=(q_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}x_{1}^{2})x_{1}^{\,k}=q_{12}\,x_{1}(x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k})+q_{11}\,x_{1}^{\,k+2}.

Now apply the inductive hypothesis to the factor
x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k}
:

x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}q_{11}^{\,j}q_{12}^{\,k-j}\frac{[k]_{q_{12}}!}{[k-j]_{q_{12}}!}\,x_{1}^{\,j+1}x_{2}^{\,k-j}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k+1}

\displaystyle=q_{12}\,x_{1}\,x_{2}x_{1}^{\,k}+q_{11}\,x_{1}^{\,k+2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}q_{11}^{\,j}q_{12}^{\,k+1-j}\frac{[k]_{q_{12}}!}{[k-j]_{q_{12}}!}\,x_{1}^{\,j+2}x_{2}^{\,k-j}\;+\;q_{11}\,x_{1}^{\,k+2}.

Reindexing with
j^{\prime}=j+1
and observing that
[k+1]_{q_{12}}=[k]_{q_{12}}+q_{12}^{k}
yields the standard
q_{12}
-binomial-type recursion

c_{n+1,k}=q_{12}\,c_{n,k}+q_{11}\,[n]_{q_{12}}\,c_{n,k-1},\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

together with
c_{n+1,0}=q_{12}^{n+1}
and
c_{n+1,n+1}=q_{11}^{n+1}[n+1]_{q_{12}}!
.
A direct check from the definition
c_{n,k}=q_{11}^{k}q_{12}^{n-k}\dfrac{[n]_{q_{12}}!}{[n-k]_{q_{12}}!}

shows that these recursions hold identically (using
[n+1]_{q_{12}}!=[n+1]_{q_{12}}[n]_{q_{12}}!
),
hence the coefficients produced by normalizing
x_{2}^{n+1}x_{1}
are exactly
c_{n+1,k}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The commutation formulas obtained above for the pair

admit a completely analogous counterpart for the pair

carries over verbatim. Concretely, the normal forms of the words

are obtained from Propositions

by replacing the parameters

with

–integers and

–factorials, i.e.

Proposition 3.5
.

Define, for integers
k\geq i\geq 0

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}^{n}c(k,i)\,\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{s1t1}+\beta_{n,k}a_{s1t1}\bigr)\;+\;\mathbf{1}_{i\geq 1}\,\bigl(\alpha_{n,i-1}a_{s1t2}+\beta_{n,i-1}a_{s1t2}\bigr),

\displaystyle\beta_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}^{n}c(k,i)\,\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{s2t1}+\beta_{n,k}a_{s2t1}\bigr)\;+\;\mathbf{1}_{i\geq 1}\,\bigl(\alpha_{n,i-1}a_{s2t2}+\beta_{n,i-1}a_{s2t2}\bigr),

where

denotes the indicator (equal to

Proof.

We argue by induction on

n=1

Assume that for some
n\geq 1

Multiplying on the right by
x_{t}
gives

y_{s}x_{t}^{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x_{1}^{\,n-k}x_{2}^{\,k}\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}y_{1}+\beta_{n,k}y_{2}\bigr)x_{t}.

Using the relations for
y_{s}x_{t}
we expand

\displaystyle(\alpha_{n,k}y_{1}+\beta_{n,k}y_{2})x_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{11t1}+\beta_{n,k}a_{21t1}\bigr)\,x_{1}y_{1}+\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{11t2}+\beta_{n,k}a_{21t2}\bigr)\,x_{2}y_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{12t1}+\beta_{n,k}a_{22t1}\bigr)\,x_{1}y_{2}+\bigl(\alpha_{n,k}a_{12t2}+\beta_{n,k}a_{22t2}\bigr)\,x_{2}y_{2}.

Substituting and regrouping, the terms involving

x_{2}^{\,k}x_{1}

x_{2}x_{1}=q_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+q_{11}x_{1}^{2}

The computations needed to determine the center of a double Ore extension of the type considered in this work
are typically quite lengthy. Indeed, imposing the centrality conditions

[s,x_{1}]=[s,x_{2}]=[s,y_{1}]=[s,y_{2}]=0

for a generic PBW element
s
leads to a rapidly growing collection of rewriting steps and coefficient constraints.
For this reason, besides hand computations in low degrees, we implemented in
SageMath
a PBW normal form
algorithm based on iterated rewriting rules compatible with the fixed monomial order

x_{1}<x_{2}<y_{1}<y_{2}.

Below we present two explicit examples illustrating the method.

Algebra
\mathbb{D}
:
Throughout this example we assume
p\in\{-1,1\}

p^{2}=1

Let
\mathbb{D}

\Bbbk
-algebra generated by
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}

(3.1)

\displaystyle x_{2}x_{1}

\displaystyle=-x_{1}x_{2},

(3.2)

\displaystyle y_{2}y_{1}

\displaystyle=p\,y_{1}y_{2},

and the mixed relations

(3.3)

\displaystyle y_{1}x_{1}

\displaystyle=-p\,x_{1}y_{1},

(3.4)

\displaystyle y_{1}x_{2}

\displaystyle=-x_{2}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{2},

(3.5)

\displaystyle y_{2}x_{1}

\displaystyle=p\,x_{1}y_{2},

(3.6)

\displaystyle y_{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}.

Claim:

Z(\mathbb{D})=\Bbbk\big[x_{1}^{2},\;y_{2}^{2}+p\,y_{1}^{2}\big].

Proof.

\Bbbk[x_{1}^{2},y_{2}^{2}+p\,y_{1}^{2}]\subseteq Z(\mathbb{D})
.
First we show that
x_{1}^{2}
is central. Clearly
[x_{1}^{2},x_{1}]=0

Using (
3.1

x_{2}x_{1}^{2}=(x_{2}x_{1})x_{1}=(-x_{1}x_{2})x_{1}=-x_{1}(x_{2}x_{1})=-x_{1}(-x_{1}x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}x_{2},

so
[x_{1}^{2},x_{2}]=0

Using (
3.3

y_{1}x_{1}^{2}=(y_{1}x_{1})x_{1}=(-p\,x_{1}y_{1})x_{1}=-p\,x_{1}(y_{1}x_{1})=-p\,x_{1}(-p\,x_{1}y_{1})=p^{2}x_{1}^{2}y_{1}=x_{1}^{2}y_{1},

[x_{1}^{2},y_{1}]=0

Similarly, from (
3.5

y_{2}x_{1}^{2}=(y_{2}x_{1})x_{1}=(p\,x_{1}y_{2})x_{1}=p\,x_{1}(y_{2}x_{1})=p\,x_{1}(p\,x_{1}y_{2})=p^{2}x_{1}^{2}y_{2}=x_{1}^{2}y_{2},

[x_{1}^{2},y_{2}]=0

x_{1}^{2}\in Z(\mathbb{D})

Next we show that

z

y_{1}
and
y_{2}

since (
3.2

y_{1}y_{2}=p\,y_{2}y_{1}
and hence

y_{1}y_{2}^{2}=y_{2}^{2}y_{1}
and
y_{2}y_{1}^{2}=y_{1}^{2}y_{2}

Also, by the previous computation,
y_{1}^{2}
and
y_{2}^{2}
commute with
x_{1}

[z,x_{1}]=0
.

It remains to check
[z,x_{2}]=0

3.4

3.6

First, from (
3.4

3.3

\displaystyle y_{1}^{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=y_{1}(y_{1}x_{2})=y_{1}(-x_{2}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{2})

\displaystyle=-(y_{1}x_{2})y_{1}+(y_{1}x_{1})y_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{2}y_{1}^{2}-x_{1}y_{2}y_{1}-p\,x_{1}y_{1}y_{2}.

3.2

-x_{1}y_{2}y_{1}=-p\,x_{1}y_{1}y_{2}

(3.7)

\displaystyle y_{2}^{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle(y_{2}^{2}+p\,y_{1}^{2})x_{2}

The following
SageMath

from sage.all import *

def build_D(pval):
K = QQ
A.<x1,x2,y1,y2> = FreeAlgebra(K,4)
p = K(pval)

rules = {
(’x2’,’x1’): -x1*x2,
(’y2’,’y1’): p*y1*y2,

(’y1’,’x1’): -p*x1*y1,
(’y1’,’x2’): -x2*y1 + x1*y2,
(’y2’,’x1’): p*x1*y2,
(’y2’,’x2’): x1*y1 + x2*y2,
}
GEN = {’x1’: x1, ’x2’: x2, ’y1’: y1, ’y2’: y2}

def _term_dict(f):
try:
return f.monomial_coefficients()
except AttributeError:
return f.dict()

def _word_to_elem(word_letters):
e = A.one()
for s in word_letters:
e *= GEN[s]
return e

def reduce_once(f):
out = A.zero()
changed = False
for mon, coeff in _term_dict(A(f)).items():
letters = [str(a) for a in mon.to_word()]
done = False
for i in range(len(letters)-1):
pair = (letters[i], letters[i+1])
if pair in rules:
prefix = _word_to_elem(letters[:i])
suffix = _word_to_elem(letters[i+2:])
out += coeff * (prefix * rules[pair] * suffix)
changed = True
done = True
break
if not done:
out += coeff * _word_to_elem(letters)
return out, changed

def NF(f, max_steps=20000):
g = A(f)
for _ in range(max_steps):
g2, ch = reduce_once(g)
if not ch:
return g
g = g2
raise RuntimeError("It’s not finished: increase max_steps
or check loops.")

def comm(u,v):
return NF(u*v - v*u)

return A, x1,x2,y1,y2, p, NF, comm

for pval in [1,-1]:
A, x1,x2,y1,y2, p, NF, comm = build_D(pval)
z = y2^2 + p*y1^2

print("\n=== Case p =", pval, "===\n")
for g,name in [(x1,"x1"),(x2,"x2"),(y1,"y1"),(y2,"y2")]:
print("[x1^2,",name,"] =", comm(x1^2,g))
for g,name in [(x1,"x1"),(x2,"x2"),(y1,"y1"),(y2,"y2")]:
print("[z,",name,"] =", comm(z,g))

Next we compute
y_{1}w
in PBW normal form. Using (
3.10
) and (
3.12
),

\displaystyle y_{1}x_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=(y_{1}x_{1})x_{1}=(x_{1}y_{1}+fx_{2}y_{2})x_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}(y_{1}x_{1})+fx_{2}(y_{2}x_{1})

\displaystyle=x_{1}(x_{1}y_{1}+fx_{2}y_{2})+fx_{2}(fx_{2}y_{1}-x_{1}y_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+fx_{1}x_{2}y_{2}+f^{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{1}-fx_{2}x_{1}y_{2}.

Since
x_{2}x_{1}=-x_{1}x_{2}
, we have
-fx_{2}x_{1}y_{2}=fx_{1}x_{2}y_{2}
, thus

(3.14)

y_{1}x_{1}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+f^{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{1}+2fx_{1}x_{2}y_{2}.

Similarly, using (
3.11
) and (
3.13
),

\displaystyle y_{1}x_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(y_{1}x_{2})x_{2}=(-x_{2}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{2})x_{2}

\displaystyle=-x_{2}(y_{1}x_{2})+x_{1}(y_{2}x_{2})

\displaystyle=-x_{2}(-x_{2}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{2})+x_{1}(x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{2}^{2}y_{1}-x_{2}x_{1}y_{2}+x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}y_{2}.

Again
x_{2}x_{1}=-x_{1}x_{2}
, hence
-x_{2}x_{1}y_{2}=x_{1}x_{2}y_{2}
, and we obtain

(3.15)

y_{1}x_{2}^{2}=x_{2}^{2}y_{1}+x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+2x_{1}x_{2}y_{2}.

Subtracting
f\eqref{eq:O_y1_x2sq}
from (
3.14
) yields

\displaystyle y_{1}w

\displaystyle=y_{1}x_{1}^{2}-f\,y_{1}x_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+f^{2}x_{2}^{2}y_{1}+2fx_{1}x_{2}y_{2})-f(x_{2}^{2}y_{1}+x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+2x_{1}x_{2}y_{2})

\displaystyle=(1-f)x_{1}^{2}y_{1}+(f^{2}-f)x_{2}^{2}y_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-f)(x_{1}^{2}y_{1}-fx_{2}^{2}y_{1})

\displaystyle=(1-f)\,wy_{1}.

The identity
y_{2}w=(1-f)\,wy_{2}
is proved analogously from (
3.12
)–(
3.13
).
Finally, by induction one has
y_{i}w^{n}=(1-f)^{n}w^{n}y_{i}
for
i=1,2
. If
(1-f)^{n}=1
,
then
y_{i}w^{n}=w^{n}y_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Since
w^{n}
also commutes with
x_{1},x_{2}
, we conclude
w^{n}\in Z(\mathbb{O})
,
and hence
\Bbbk[w^{n}]\subseteq Z(\mathbb{O})
.

The following code checks the normality identities and the vanishing of commutators with
x_{1},x_{2}
via PBW normal forms.

from sage.all import *

# Parameter field: f
R = QQ[’f’]
K = FractionField(R)
f = K.gen()

A.<x1,x2,y1,y2> = FreeAlgebra(K,4)

rules = {
(’x2’,’x1’): -x1*x2,
(’y2’,’y1’): -y1*y2,

(’y1’,’x1’): x1*y1 + f*x2*y2,
(’y1’,’x2’): -x2*y1 + x1*y2,
(’y2’,’x1’): f*x2*y1 - x1*y2,
(’y2’,’x2’): x1*y1 + x2*y2,
}

GEN = {’x1’: x1, ’x2’: x2, ’y1’: y1, ’y2’: y2}

def _term_dict(F):
try:
return F.monomial_coefficients()
except AttributeError:
return F.dict()

def _word_to_elem(word_letters):
e = A.one()
for s in word_letters:
e *= GEN[s]
return e

def reduce_once(F):
out = A.zero()
changed = False
for mon, coeff in _term_dict(A(F)).items():
letters = [str(a) for a in mon.to_word()]
done = False
for i in range(len(letters)-1):
pair = (letters[i], letters[i+1])
if pair in rules:
prefix = _word_to_elem(letters[:i])
suffix = _word_to_elem(letters[i+2:])
out += coeff * (prefix * rules[pair] * suffix)
changed = True
done = True
break
if not done:
out += coeff * _word_to_elem(letters)
return out, changed

def NF(F, max_steps=20000):
G = A(F)
for _ in range(max_steps):
G2, ch = reduce_once(G)
if not ch:
return G
G = G2
raise RuntimeError("It’s not finished: increase max_steps
or check loops")

def comm(U,V):
return NF(U*V - V*U)

w = x1^2 - f*x2^2

print("[w,x1] =", comm(w,x1))
print("[w,x2] =", comm(w,x2))

print("NF(y1*w - (1-f)*w*y1) =", NF(y1*w - (1-f)*w*y1))
print("NF(y2*w - (1-f)*w*y2) =", NF(y2*w - (1-f)*w*y2))

∎

All remaining cases in our classification are handled in the same spirit.

Namely, one fixes the PBW order

, writes the defining relations as rewriting rules, and computes PBW normal forms to evaluate the commutators

,
[s,x_{2}]
,
[s,y_{1}]
, and
[s,y_{2}]
for suitable candidate central elements

While the resulting expansions are often too long to be displayed in full detail, the procedure is completely explicit and relies only on the relations established in Propositions

,
3.3
,
3.5
and
3.6

Definition 4.1

(
[
21
, Definition 4.1]
)
.

Let
A
be a
\Bbbk
-algebra.

(i)

A
is
cancellative
if
A[t]\cong B[t]
for some
\Bbbk
-algebra
B
implies that
A\cong B
.

(ii)

A is
strongly cancellative
if, for any
d\geq 1
, the isomorphism
A[t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}]\cong B[t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}]
for some
\Bbbk
-algebra
B
implies that
A\cong B
.

(iii)

A
is

if, for any finitely generated coomutative
\Bbbk
-algebra and a domain
R
such that
R/I=\Bbbk
for some ideal
I\subset R
and any
\Bbbk
-algebra
B
,
A\otimes R\cong B\otimes R
implies that
A\cong B
.

It is clear that universally cancellative implies strongly cancellative, and in turn, strongly cancellative implies cancellative.

)

.
