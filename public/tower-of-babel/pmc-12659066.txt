Global, regional and national burden of asthma attributable to metabolic diseases from 1990 to 2021 and projected trends to 2040
Authors: Zhi-qiang Zhang, Jing-yang Li, Chi-yi Fu, Ya-Lun Li, Qian Guo, You-wei Bao, Jun Wu, Jun-chao Liao, Yu-Qi Song, Dong-xu Li, Xin-hua Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma is a prevalent inflammatory condition of the lower respiratory tract, affecting over 272 million people worldwide. Although prevalence and mortality rates have declined, asthma continues to burden healthcare systems, particularly in low-income regions. Metabolic diseases significantly influence the development and progression of asthma. This study aimed to evaluate global, regional, national, and temporal trends in asthma burden attributable to metabolic diseases from 1990 to 2021 and to project changes through 2040. The GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2021 dataset was used to analyze key epidemiological variables, focusing on disability survival, years of life lost, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Descriptive analyses and trend evaluations illustrated the burden of asthma due to metabolic diseases (MAS) over the last 30 years, while decomposition analyses quantified the impacts of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes. In 2021, deaths totaled 62,363, with DALYs reaching over 3.28 million. High socio-demographic index (SDI) quintiles reported the highest age-standardized rates of DALYs and years of disability (YLDs), while low-middle SDI quintiles had the highest death rates. South Asia experienced the highest number of MAS-related deaths, years of life lost (YLLs), and DALYs. From 1990 to 2020, rates of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs declined, with the most significant decrease occurring from 1993 to 2000. The analysis indicated that females bore a greater burden than males, with changes in DALYs more influenced by population growth. Inequalities associated with SDI decreased significantly, with a notable reduction in the health concentration index. Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent inflammatory disease of the lower respiratory tract, characterized by symptoms such as dyspnea, coughing, wheezing, and chest tightness. It affects people of all ages, with 43.12 million new cases and 272.68 million existing cases reported in 2017, along with 0.49 million deaths, exerting significant pressure on healthcare systems [1–3]. Although standardized prevalence and mortality rates have declined in recent decades, the overall asthma burden remains high due to population growth [4, 5]. The disease's impact differs by income, with wealthier areas seeing higher incidence rates and poorer regions facing more deaths [4]. Some countries in East and Central Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East experienced rising death rates from 2010 to 2019 [6].

Metabolic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases, involve abnormalities in metabolic processes affecting carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins [7]. These conditions often co-occur with asthma, suggesting potential interrelations [8]. There are bidirectional links between asthma and metabolic disorders [9, 10]. Individuals with obesity [11], metabolic syndrome [12], or diabetes [9, 13] are at higher risk for asthma, while those with asthma are more likely to develop diabetes and metabolic syndrome [14]. Metabolic diseases can also influence asthma's onset, progression, and management [15]. For instance, obesity can worsen asthma by increasing lung resistance, causing gastroesophageal reflux, and producing pro-inflammatory mediators, which additionally contribute to insulin resistance and vitamin D deficiency [15]. Shared pathogenic mechanisms complicate the management of both conditions [8, 9]. Inflammation can negatively impact both the respiratory system and metabolic processes, potentially creating a harmful cycle for respiratory and metabolic health [9, 10]. This interaction helps explain the correlation between metabolic diseases and asthma.

While relationships between metabolic diseases and asthma have been explored, much research relies on outdated data [11, 12] or is limited to specific regions [12, 16]. Therefore, it's essential to systematically compare the burdens of asthma related to metabolic diseases (MAS) across various countries using robust methodologies.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study is a major public health initiative assessing the burden of diseases and injuries globally. The GBD 2021 study, which served as the data source for this analysis, provides a comprehensive assessment of the epidemiological burden for 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories [17, 18].

This study intends to analyze GBD 2021 data to examine global, regional, and national asthma burdens linked to metabolic diseases, track trends over time, and forecast future changes, considering demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental factors.

Method

Data sources and diagnostic criteria

GBD 2021 provides a systematic assessment of 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries using the latest epidemiological data. Data on MAS was sourced from GBD 2021 (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021/sources) and is freely available upon registration. The GBD 2021 study employed a rigorous and standardized data processing and modeling approach. This approach encompassed comprehensive data extraction and correction, utilized advanced techniques such as spatio-temporal Gaussian process regression to address missing data, and applied the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) and DisMod-MR 2.1 to ensure data consistency and fill gaps. All data sources underwent quality assessment and were assigned quality grades. Detailed descriptions of these processes, including a complete list of data sources and their quality ratings, are available through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) and the GBD 2021 core publications [17, 18]. As this study represents a secondary analysis of publicly available, finalized estimates, we relied on the robust internal validation and data imputation processes inherent within the GBD framework. Detailed information on methods and statistical models from previous studies can be found in [18], which also supports an online tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) for analyzing results. GBD 2021 integrates various data sources, each with a unique identifier in the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx), and follows the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Reporting of Health Estimates (GATHER) [19]. We applied the GBD 2021 framework to conclude that metabolic disease is an attributable risk factor for asthma [20].

In the GBD 2021 database, we selected "metabolic risk" as a risk factor and "asthma" (ICD-10 codes J45, J46; ICD-9 code 493) as a cause.

Risk factor quantification follows the GBD Risk Factor Collaborators' framework [21].

However, it is important to recognize that the GBD 2021 study itself already includes a rigorous data processing and model validation procedure, which ensures the robustness of its published estimates through internal sensitivity analyses [17, 18]. This study is a secondary analysis of these validated final estimates, so our results are based on the robust methodology of the GBD.

Disability survival, YLLs, DALYs, and sociodemographic index

We extracted estimates for occurrences, deaths, YLDs, YLLs, and DALYs for MAS from GBD 2021, including 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). YLLs are calculated by multiplying deaths by remaining life expectancy. Combined with YLDs, these metrics assess disease burden. DALYs, indicating one year of healthy life lost, are calculated by summing YLDs and YLLs per 100,000 population to identify main causes of disease burden.

The sociodemographic index (SDI) [22] summarizes per-capita income, average education for those 15 and older, and fertility rates for females under 25. It quantifies development across five levels (low to high) on a scale from 0 to 100 [23].

We analyzed the global, regional, and national burden of MAS through descriptive analyses. Trends in cases, crude death rates, age-standardized death rates (ASR), DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs from 1990 to 2021 were displayed using world and age-sex-stratified maps.

Overall trend analysis

We evaluated global trends in MAS deaths, DALYs, and annual deaths from 1990 to 2021, examining disease burden at various levels. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) indicates burden trends over time. Analyzing ASR trends via EAPC reliably monitors disease burden changes using a linear regression model [24]. The linear regression model is expressed as y = α + βx, where y = ln(ASR) and x = the calendar year. The EAPC is calculated as (exp(β) −1) *100%, and its95% confidence interval (CI) is also derived from the model [25]. A regression model's natural logarithm aligns observations to a straight line, with the slope estimating the annual rate of change. A positive or negative EAPC and its 95% CI indicate upward or downward trends in disease burden.

Localized trend analysis (Joinpoint Regression Analysis)

Localized trends in MAS burden were analyzed using Joinpoint Regression Software 4.9.1 from the National Cancer Institute. Detailed methods are available on the software's website and in Li et al. [26]. The burden trend from 1990 to 2021 was segmented using Joinpoint analysis, which provided annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [27]. Positive or negative APC and AAPC values (P < 0.05) indicate increasing or decreasing trends, while values near zero suggest stabilization.

Decomposition analysis

We used decomposition analysis from Das Gupta et al. [28] and Cheng et al. [29] to assess the impact of population growth, aging, and epidemiologic changes on DALYs for MAS over the last three decades. The formula for calculating the number of DALYs in each region is DALYsay, py, ey = (ai,y * py * ei,y), which references the method proposed by Xie and his colleagues in 2018 [30]. Each variable's contribution to DALY changes for MAS was quantified by separating standardized effects. Decomposition analysis across gender and SDI groups revealed drivers of DALY changes in MAS from 1990 to 2021.

Health inequality

Two standardized measures assessed global and regional disparities in MAS: the Inequality Slope Index (absolute measure) and the Concentration Index (relative measure). DALY rates were regressed against an SDI-based table to calculate the Inequality Slope Index [31, 32]. The Lorenz curve illustrated the relationship between cumulative DALY proportions and population distribution by SDI level. The Concentration Index, indicating relative inequality, is derived from the area under the Lorenz curve using cumulative DALY scores and SDI-ranked population distribution [33].

Predictive analysis

We used Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) analysis with Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) to predict MAS burden trends through 2040. This approach combines past data and probability distributions, addressing issues in traditional Markov chain Monte Carlo methods and enhancing model efficacy. It allows for a more precise assessment of future epidemiologic trends in MAS by considering age, period, and cohort effects [34].

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.1), except for direct online analyses. P-values and 95% confidence intervals were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

The specific parameter settings of the Bayesian age-period-cohort model (including the choice of prior distributions) are detailed in Supplementary File 1.

Results

Descriptive analysis of MAS burden at global, regional, and national levels

Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 show case numbers, ASR, and EAPC for MAS deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs for 1990 and 2021. Globally, ASR for these metrics slightly increased from 1990 to 2021, with distinct rises in case numbers. In 2021, the high SDI quintile had the highest ASR for DALYs and YLDs, while the low SDI quintile had the highest YLLs, and the low-middle quintile had the highest deaths. Oceania reported the highest ASR for MAS deaths (6.10, 95% CI: 2.65–10.88), DALYs (162.79, 95% CI: 73.99–285.55), and YLLs (143.84, 95% CI: 64.40–256.07), and high-income North America led in YLDs (89.48, 95% CI: 43.02–140.08). EAPC trends varied by SDI quintile, with decreases in deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, while over half of YLDs showed upward trends. The low-middle SDI quintile had the most significant ASR increase, and the EAPC for deaths and DALYs in the low SDI quintile was stable. The highest counts for deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs were in the low-middle, low-middle, high, and low-middle SDI regions, respectively, with high-income North America having the highest YLDs (368,531.05, 95% CI: 173,032.38–582,749.78), and South Asia reporting the highest MAS deaths (25,159.69, 95% CI: 10,125.98–45,192.89), DALYs (776,006.01, 95% CI: 327,108.28–1,289,399.31), and YLLs (642,086.63, 95% CI: 258,357.02–1,101,485.27). Overall, decreases in EAPC were more common in regions with middle or higher SDI quintiles. Overall trends in MAS burden using broad estimation method

Figure 2 shows that global numbers, crude rates, and age-standardized rates (ASRs) of deaths, YLDs, YLLs, and DALYs for MAS varied. Most cases had rising trends, but crude YLD rates and ASRs for deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs declined from 1990 to 2020. From 1990 to 1998, females had a lower ASR for deaths than males but higher rates in other categories. ASR curves converged significantly in YLLs by 1990 and deaths by 1998, with the least pronounced convergence in cases between females and both genders combined. Convergence for deaths and YLLs was greater in 1990 than in 2020, with the combined curve showing the highest case numbers but falling between male and female curves in ASR and crude rates (Fig. 2). From 1990 to 2021, global trends in disease burden varied significantly. Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) for MAS were: deaths at −1.13 (95% CI: −1.23 to −1.04), DALYs at −0.85 (95% CI: −0.96 to −0.73), YLDs at −0.66 (95% CI: −0.83 to −0.48), and YLLs at −1.03 (95% CI: −1.12 to −0.94) per year on average (Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

The joinpoint regression analyses of the MAS burden from 1990 to 2020 (Fig. 4) reveal a sharp increase in DALYs (AAPC = −0.79), YLDs (AAPC = −0.74), and YLLs (AAPC = −0.84), with a modest decrease in death rates (AAPC = −0.97). From 1990 to 2021, the low-middle SDI quintile had the highest increase in DALYs. Aging, population growth, and changes in epidemiology contributed 60.43%, 106.07%, and −66.5% to the global DALY increase, respectively, with population growth being the largest factor. Significant effects of aging (634.94%) and population growth (954.96%) were seen in the high SDI quintile, especially among females in the high-middle SDI quintile (576.27%). Epidemiological changes had the largest effect on DALYs, followed by aging, with population dynamics exerting the least effect. Females generally bore a higher burden than males. The influence of population growth and epidemiological changes varied by SDI quintiles, affecting females more in high and low SDI quintiles. Aging and population growth impacted females more, while males were more affected by epidemiological changes. Discussion

Our results show a global increase in deaths, DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs from MAS between 1990 and 2021, likely linked to significant lifestyle and dietary changes. Public health policies should target regions with the highest burdens and recent increases. In 2021, high SDI areas had the highest ASRs for DALYs and YLDs, while low SDI areas had the highest YLLs, and low to medium SDI regions exhibited the highest mortality rates. Oceania had the highest ASRs for DALYs and YLLs from MAS, and high-income North America saw the highest YLDs, likely due to local dietary habits and vitamin D deficiencies [35, 36]. A GBD 2019 study noted that metabolic diseases and obesity contribute to rising mortality trends [37]. Thus, public health policies should prioritize obesity and metabolic disorders. Some regions saw decreases in EAPC of deaths, DALYs, and YLLs, possibly due to healthcare improvements. Advances in clinical diagnostics and treatment have enhanced MAS management in some areas. However, public health strategies targeting metabolic diseases in high-burden regions are essential to reduce asthma-related impacts.

Just over half of the world’s YLDs have shown a high growth trend, likely due to poor lifestyle choices (e.g., smoking, overeating) and air pollution, increasing the metabolic disease burden linked to MAS [38–43]. Effective public health policies and health promotion support are essential, especially in regions with significant YLD increases.

At the national level, India and the United States have the highest burdens of MAS, highlighting the need for tailored health policies. In India, addressing environmental pollution is a priority, while the U.S. has seen a gradual increase in obesity, contributing to the MAS burden [44]. The NHLBI should focus on adjusting the dietary structure to benefit the U.S. and provide insights for other countries facing similar obesity-related challenges. Although lifestyle and dietary factors are key drivers, the regional disparities in MAS burden revealed by our analysis must also be interpreted through broader lenses such as healthcare accessibility and environmental determinants. Areas with lower SDI often face systemic barriers beyond nutrition, including limited access to quality healthcare and affordable medications for diagnosing and treating asthma and metabolic diseases [45]. This can lead to delayed diagnosis, poor management, and consequently severe outcomes and increased mortality, reflected in the elevated YLLs observed in low-SDI regions. Furthermore, our findings on SDI-related inequalities underscore the role of fundamental socioeconomic determinants—such as education, income, and infrastructure—that influence everything from health literacy to living conditions. Finally, environmental exposures unrelated to diet—such as household and environmental air pollution [41] and occupational allergens, prevalent in many developing regions—are established risk factors for asthma onset and exacerbation. Thus, the high asthma burden in regions like South Asia and Oceania likely stems from a convergence of dietary shifts, limited healthcare capacity, socioeconomic challenges, and adverse environmental conditions.

While Monaco and Tokelau have the lowest MAS burdens, the condition still requires priority attention to maintain or reduce this burden. Age-standardized data shows Fiji and the UAE have the highest burdens, with Monaco retaining the lowest. The high burden in Fiji may be linked to migration, as studies indicate migrant children face altered asthma risks in new environments [46]. MAS burden trends show significant decreases in EAPC for Belarus, Turkmenistan, and Singapore, while Zimbabwe and Macao experienced increases, possibly influenced by epigenetic differences among racial groups [47]. Therefore, regional differences, age structures, racial backgrounds, and genetic factors must be considered when formulating public health policies to enhance disease control efforts.

In assessing the MAS burden trend, we observed a localized decrease amidst a general increase, likely due to effective public health education and policies from 1990 to 2021 [45, 48]. Notably, females had lower age-standardized death rates than males from 1990 to 1998, but higher rates in subsequent years, possibly due to an older age distribution among females. Thus, public health policies should be more age-specific to address the unique circumstances of different age groups.

The overall MAS burden is significantly lower than expected across different SDI regions. With global economic growth, access to healthcare has increased. DALYs are similar in high and low SDI regions, indicating that all can reduce the MAS burden through effective policies [49]. However, economically disadvantaged areas face high medical costs, limiting access. Governments should develop strategies to protect low-income patients [50], and international aid can help reduce the global disease burden.

From 1990 to 2020, we observed significant increases in DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs for MAS, despite declines in mortality rates. This may be due to advances in medical technology improving disease detection, resulting in a higher burden overall. Effective prevention, management, and treatment programs for MAS are essential. Notably, YLDs and DALYs rose the most between 2015 and 2021, especially during 1993–2000, which saw rapid changes in MAS indicators. A cohort study from 2014 to 2016 indicated that asthma imposes a significant economic burden due to high management costs and loss of young workers [51]. The relationship between disease control and economic status in MAS creates a vicious cycle [52, 53]. Thus, targeted measures should focus on young people, along with adequate financial support.

Aging, demographic, and epidemiologic changes significantly influence the MAS burden. From 1990 to 2021, these factors increased DALYs for MAS, with epidemiologic changes as the largest contributor, according to a GBD 2019 study on respiratory diseases [54]. Local healthcare organizations must monitor these changes. Females bear a higher MAS burden than males, likely due to dietary differences, as studies suggest higher fat intake and eating disorders among females [55, 56]. These factors can increase metabolic diseases, emphasizing the need for gender-specific dietary habits. With an increasing elderly population [57], the incidence of metabolic diseases rises significantly with age [58], and elderly patients face more severe airway obstruction [59], worsening MAS-related DALYs. The WHO’s first world report on aging and health provides a public health framework [57]. Countries should use this framework to develop flexible policies to reduce MAS DALYs among the elderly.

Cross-country inequality analysis shows significant disparities in disease burden across SDI regions over the past three decades, with low SDI countries disproportionately affected by higher MAS burdens due to economic challenges and unaffordable healthcare. Although this inequality has decreased, likely due to rapid economic development [60, 61], global MAS inequality persists. We recommend tailoring preventive policies to each region's economic status and creating targeted programs based on regional conditions.

Projections through 2040 show a steady rise in global deaths and DALYs, with annual increases in DALYs. However, the key lies in interpreting these projected increases amid model uncertainty. Our Bayesian model provides a best estimate based on past trends, but the actual future burden will depend on the complex interplay of various factors not fully captured by the model—including the effectiveness of global health policies, economic shifts, and technological advances in managing metabolic diseases. Our findings underscore this inherent uncertainty. Regarding the possible outcomes, countries should strengthen healthcare systems, develop adaptable public health policies, and focus on aging populations, dietary patterns, and healthcare resource allocation. Stratifying MAS cases by underlying causes is vital for targeted prevention efforts. Enhanced international communication is crucial for developing strategies to address changing trends in the MAS burden and reduce its impact.

Our study has several notable advantages, distinguishing it from previous research on the asthma burden caused by metabolic risks. Firstly, to our knowledge, this is the most recent and comprehensive assessment using the GBD 2021 dataset, providing a detailed panorama from 1990 to 2021. While earlier studies have explored various aspects of this relationship [11, 12, 16], they often relied on older data or were limited to specific countries or regions. Secondly, our analysis goes beyond descriptive epidemiology, employing multiple methods such as decomposition analysis (quantifying the contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes) and health inequality analysis (rigorously examining socioeconomic differences). In addition, we used a Bayesian age-period-cohort model to predict future disease burden trends up to 2040, providing valuable forward-looking insights for public health planning. The combination of temporal breadth, geographic granularity, methodological depth, and predictive insight offers a unique global perspective on the burden of metabolic asthma.

However, our study has several limitations. First, low medical care levels and potential misdiagnosis in underdeveloped countries may lead to an underestimation of MAS cases. Second, variable data quality from GBD raw data can cause inconsistencies in MAS measurements and complicate population comparisons. Third, while aiming for comprehensive analyses, data accuracy may vary by region, introducing potential bias. Additionally, it is worth noting that, when interpreting our findings, the inherent limitations of GBD data should be considered. The accuracy of our estimates depends on the quality and coverage of underlying source data, which vary considerably across countries. In many low-resource settings, challenges such as underreporting, misdiagnosis, and incomplete vital registration systems likely lead to underestimation of the true burden of MAS. Furthermore, diagnostic criteria for asthma and metabolic diseases have evolved over the 30-year study period, and variations in how these criteria are applied across healthcare systems may affect the temporal and cross-regional consistency and comparability of our estimates. While the GBD study employs sophisticated models to mitigate these issues, residual biases warrant caution when interpreting absolute burden values, particularly in direct cross-country comparisons. Furthermore, although this study conducted a detailed analysis of the global asthma burden attributable to metabolic risks from 1990 to 2021 based on the GBD 2021 dataset, it should be noted that the GBD 2023 data have now been released. Therefore, our study results represent the most comprehensive assessment available at the time of study initiation and analysis. The GBD 2023 data provide updated estimates, which may offer new insights and have the potential to refine our projected trends through 2040. Future research incorporating these latest data will help validate and extend our observations. Lastly, our 2040 projections depend on assumptions influenced by external factors, and historical data may reflect evolving diagnostic standards and medical technologies. Due to data limitations, we could not segment metabolic diseases to predict future trends, but we hope future studies will explore this in greater depth. Our findings offer valuable insights into global MAS trends, but should be interpreted cautiously due to complexities and biases. Improved collaboration, standardized diagnostic criteria for high-prevalence diseases, and better national health data collection could enhance disease burden estimates and related study accuracy.

Conclusion

In summary, the MAS burden has increased over the past 30 years, despite a decline in age-standardized prevalence rates. ASR of DALYs and YLDs is rising in high SDI quintiles, while deaths are increasing in low and middle SDI quintiles. Decomposition analyses show a higher burden for females, influenced by population growth. Predictive analyses indicate a continued rise in MAS incidence, underscoring the need for effective public health strategies and ongoing research.
