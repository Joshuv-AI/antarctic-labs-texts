Cell wall-forming chitin synthases in a chytrid fungus
Authors: Trupti Gaikwad, Michael Cunliffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chitin is a critical structural component of fungal cell walls, yet our understanding of its synthesis across the kingdom Fungi remains limited. Here, we investigate chitin synthase diversity, transcription and localisation in the aquatic saprotrophic chytrid Rhizoclosmatium globosum, expanding insights into fungal cell wall biology beyond Dikaryan models. We identified 20 chitin synthase genes in the R. globosum genome, mostly canonical Division I and II types with conserved functional motifs. Transcriptomic analysis through zoospore, germling and immature thallus developmental stages revealed stage-specific expression patterns, with active gene diversity correlating with increasing morphological complexity. Using electroporation-based transformation and fluorescent fusion constructs, we indicate expression and localisation of two chitin synthases during cell development. Localisation patterns suggest dynamic redistribution from cytoplasmic dispersion in early encysted cells to concentrated signals at the sporangium wall. Expression in and around the apophysis indicates the importance of these structures in cell maintenance. Our findings highlight functional specialisation among chitin synthases and underscore the importance of cell wall integrity in chytrid development. This work establishes R. globosum as a genetically tractable model for studying chytrid cell biology and contributes to a broader understanding of fungal evolution and cell wall dynamics.

Introduction

Fungi are one of the most globally prevalent and impactful group of organisms1. Key to fungal success is a dynamic cell wall that controls viability, morphology and functionality, through a range of biophysiochemical properties including strength and elasticity2. Chitin, a β-1,4-linked homopolymer of N-acetylglucosamine, is a major strengthening structural component of most fungal cell walls and chitin synthases are key in fungal cell wall formation, adding N-acetylglucosamine monomers to the growing polymer at the plasma membrane3.

Fungal chitin synthase genes fit broadly into three divisions based on domain architecture and phylogenetic analysis4. Division I chitin synthases include both type 1 (Pfam01644, CS1) and type 2 (Pfam03142, CS2) chitin synthase domains, along with a chitin synthase N-terminal domain (Pfam08407, CS1N). Division II chitin synthases have CS2 plus additional domains, and Division III chitin synthases have only CS2 domains5. Across yeast and hyphal fungi, chitin synthases retain a conserved region (CON1), which has the conserved motifs QXXEY, EDRXL and QXRRW as the functional core of the Pfam03142 CS2 domain6–8.

Current understanding of the biology of the fungal cell wall is primarily made from studies of Dikaryan fungi (Ascomycota and Basidiomycota), and of which, most are focused on human- and agricultural plant-associated models. The Chytridiomycota (chytrids) are a major group of fungi that are evolutionarily and biologically distinct from Dikaryan fungi. Found throughout terrestrial, marine, freshwater and artificial habitats, chytrids are important saprotrophs and parasites of other organisms including algae and amphibians9–11. Despite their prevalence and importance, there remains a limited understanding of chytrid cell biology, including their cell walls12.

Chytrids are commonly monocentric, developing from a wall-less zoospore to a single-celled thallus made up of a spherical sporangium for reproduction connected to tubular rhizoids for substrate/host attachment and feeding (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Video 1). Within mature sporangia (zoosporangia), uniflagellate zoospores are produced that are usually motile when released (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). After swimming, and in some species, crawling, to find a suitable substrate or host, chytrid zoospores retract the flagellum and encyst by initiating cell wall formation, developing into a walled thallus with an emerging germ tube from which the rhizoid grows (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Video 1).Fig. 1Major features of the Rhizoclosmatium globosum JEL800 cell and zoospore release.The chytrid in brightfield (BF) and confocal microscopy with cell wall chitin of the sporangium, apophysis and rhizoid labelled with calcofluor-white (CFW) (A). There are limited established model chytrids with available molecular and cell biology tools, including genetic transformation12. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation has been developed for Spizellomyces punctatus13, a terrestrial chytrid found in soil habitats that functions as a saprotroph decomposing organic matter13,14. More recently, electroporation-based transformation has been established for Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis15,16. Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis infects the skin of amphibians and is the primary cause of the disease Chytridiomycosis17. Since identification in the late 1990s17, B. dendrobatidis has been implicated in population collapses and even extinctions of amphibian species across multiple continents18,19.

Here, our approach is to develop the aquatic saprotrophic chytrid Rhizoclosmatium globosum (Fig. 1) to explore chitin synthase genes and domain architecture with transcription through the dynamic chytrid cell cycle. Rhizoclosmatium globosum is found on chitin-rich particulate substrates (such as insect exuviae) in freshwater habitats20. The saprotroph exhibits adaptive branching rhizoids for chitin substrate attachment21, and zoospores that are chemotactically attracted to the chitin monomer N-acetylglucosamine, enabling efficient colonisation of chitinous material22. Genomic analyses revealed that R. globosum has an expanded array of glycoside hydrolase family GH18 chitinases and bacteria-like chitin-binding modules, traits likely adaptive for its saprotrophic niche of degrading chitin in aquatic environments22. Our step change is the development and application of chitin synthase fluorescent fusion constructs successfully transformed into R. globosum to indicate the expression and localisation of selected chitin synthases during the chytrid cell cycle.

Results

Chitin synthase genes and domains

We identified and characterised the diversity, domain architecture and conserved motifs of putative chitin synthase genes in R. globosum to infer their functional and evolutionary relationships. Twenty putative chitin synthases were predicted in the R. globosum genome based on the presence of the conserved CS1 and CS2 domains in the genes. Of which, 11 are likely members of Division I because of the characteristic CS1N, CS1 and CS2 domain structure and no other associated domains (Fig. 3A). CS1 domains were not detected in the other genes which formed three groups (Fig. 3B). One group made up of 4 genes, contained CS2 domains and additional domains with 3 including N-terminal myosin motor domains (Pf00063, MMD) and DEK C-terminal domains (Pf08766, DEKC), and 1 also having a cytochrome b5-like domain (Pf00173, Cyt-b5). One of the 4 genes (ORY46363) clustered in the group had no additional domains (i.e. CS2 only). A second group, made up of 4 genes, had no additional domains other than CS2. ORY39038 is distinctive compared to the other R. globosum chitin synthases, containing both a predicted CS2 domain that branched separately from the other CS2 domains and a glycoside hydrolase 16 domain (Pf00722, GH16). All three canonical fungal chitin synthase CON1 motifs (i.e. QXXEY, EDRXL and QXRRW) were present in most of the R. globosum CS2 domains (Fig. 3Aii and Bii, Supplementary Table 4). Transcription of chitin synthases through cell development

Using RNA-Seq data from a previous study24, we examined how transcription of the putative chitin synthase genes (Fig. 3A, B) varied across the zoospore, germling and immature thallus developmental stages of R. globosum to link gene expression patterns with changes in cell morphology. The abundance (FPKM) of chitin synthase gene mRNA transcripts varied between the R. globosum cell stages, with increasing diversity of transcribed genes matching growing cell morphological complexity (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 3). In zoospores, Division I ORY52914 (CS1N, CS1 and CS2) dominated the mRNA transcripts compared to the other stages (ANOVA Tukey HSD p ≤ 0.001), then became less abundant in the other cell stages. The change from the wall-less zoospores to walled germlings correlated with an increase in the range of major chitin synthase mRNA transcripts from other Division I and Division II genes, with ORY44681 (CS2), ORY53285 (MMD, CS2 and DEKC) and ORY48847 (CS1N, CS1 and CS2) increased (ANOVA Tukey HSD p ≤ 0.003). Immature thalli (i.e. developed rhizoid with no obvious zoosporegenesis) had the greatest range of chitin synthase gene mRNA transcripts compared to the less morphologically complex germlings and zoospores. The Division I ORY48850, ORY48848, ORY48890, ORY46998 (all CS1N, CS1 and CS2) and Division II ORY36855 (MMD, Cyt-b5, CS2 and DEKC), ORY38111 (CS2) and ORY51535 (CS2) were increased in the immature thalli compared to the earlier germling and zoospore cell stages (ANOVA Tukey HSD p ≤ 0.02).

Expression and localisation of chitin synthases

To explore the expression and localisation of the Division I ORY52914 (CS1N, CS1 and CS2) and Division II ORY53285 (MMD, CS2 and DEKC), fusion constructs under each specific promoter control were generated (ORY52914-mRuby3 and ORY53285-mRuby3) and used to transform R. globosum. In both transformants, the mRuby3 signal was distributed throughout the cytoplasm of the early encysted cell (Fig. 4Ai, Bi). As the cells developed to the germling stage (i.e. smaller sporangium with initial rhizoid development), both the ORY52914-mRuby3 and ORY53285-mRuby3 mRuby3 signals were concentrated along the outer edge of the sporangium wall and to some extent the rhizoid with ORY53285-mRuby3, corresponding with the CFW signal (Fig. 4Aii, Bii). During the germling stage, increased ORY52914-mRuby3 mRuby3 signal was also associated with the chitin-rich area at the sporangium-germtube/rhizoid junction (Fig. 4Aii). In the immature thallus (i.e. sporangium expanding in size but with no obvious zoospores), the mRuby3 signals in both ORY52914-mRuby3 and ORY53285-mRuby3 remained along the sporangium edge, matching the CFW-defined cell wall (Fig. 4Aiii, Biii). At the immature thallus stage, the apophysis linking the sporangium with the rhizoids becomes apparent. Discussion

Perhaps the major finding from our study is the indication of the expression and localisation of chitin synthases in a chytrid cell, with distribution changing through stages of the walled cell development cycle. For both chitin synthase fusion constructs, the mRuby3 signal was dissipated in the cytoplasm of the early stages of the chitin-containing walled encysted cell. In the following germling and subsequent stages, the chitin synthase mRuby3 signals became evenly distributed at the outer wall edge, in accordance with the archetypical plasma membrane association of fungal chitin synthases and correlating with the observed isotropic growth of the expanding sporangium part of the R. globosum cell (Supplementary Video 1)24. Confirmation experiments with immunoblot analysis could validate the expression we suggest here, alongside testing the impact of cell cycle inhibitors and colocalisation of actin with chitin synthases.

As well as indicating chitin synthase at the sporangium wall, we also suggest the possibility that the R. globosum apophysis has chitin synthase present, implying a role of the apophysis in wall maintenance of the chytrid cell. Despite being common across the Chytridiomycota, the apophysis has not been well studied, and there is a limited understanding of the biological role of the structure in the chytrid cell12,24. Through a combination of 3D model reconstruction with serial block-face scanning electron microscopy and live-cell imaging of FM1–43 endomembrane, we have previously shown that the apophysis is enriched with endomembrane and vacuoles and is an active site for intracellular trafficking between the rhizoids and the sporangium via an annular pore. As part of the intracellular trafficking function, the apophysis could have a role in mediating transport for chitin synthases for cell wall formation that warrants focused study. Future work could consider the use of higher-resolution microscopy (e.g. high-resolution ultrastructure expansion microscopy U-ExM25) to increase the spatial detail of the apophysis.

Chitin synthase gene copy number is variable across the fungal Kingdom5,26,27, and the biological reasoning for the variation is not yet fully understood28. For example, the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe (Ascomycota) has only one chitin synthase gene27 whilst the rice blast Magnaporthe oryzae (Ascomycota) has seven chitin synthase genes29. Surveys of chitin synthase genes across the fungal kingdom have shown that non-Dikaryan fungi, including chytrids, have higher numbers of chitin synthase genes compared to Dikaryan fungi5,26,27, as we show here with R. globosum.

Most of the R. globosum chitin synthase genes fit within the canonical view of fungal chitin synthase domain structures, such as the likely Division I made up of the conserved CS1N, CS1 and CS2 domains found widely across the kingdom26. Also, most R. globosum CS2 domains have all three CON1 motifs found across yeast and filamentous fungi chitin synthases6–8, suggesting that they are functional chitin synthases. The additional domains accompanying some of the CS2 domains have also been found in representatives across the kingdom5. Three of the CS2 domain containing chitin synthase genes had N-terminal myosin motor domains (MMD), which in Dikaryan fungi have been linked to chitin synthase interaction with cytoskeleton actin and tethering the enzyme to specific locations in the cell30–32. Though commonly found with Division II chitin synthases, the specific roles in chitin-related wall formation of the DEK C-terminal domains (DEKC) and cytochrome b5-like domain (Cyt-b5) in fungi are currently unknown5.

Contrasting with the largely canonical chitin synthase genes, one of the R. globosum chitin synthase genes (ORY39038) with an associated GH16 domain was made up of a distinctive putative CS2 domain that lacked any of the CON1 motifs and is therefore likely nonfunctional as a chitin synthase. We have previously suggested that this GH16 could be involved with R. globosum cell wall β-glucan production, an observation supported by measurements of β-glucan in R. globosum biomass and the fungal β (1,3) glucan synthesis inhibitor caspofungin, causing deformed rhizoid growth21. In Dikaryan fungi, β (1,3) glucan is the other major cell wall polysaccharide and, alongside the strength from chitin, controls the elasticity of the cell wall matrix28. Along with further work on chitin in chytrid cell walls, other core structural polysaccharides (e.g. β-glucans) and potential wall ‘decorating’ polysaccharides need attention.

Transcription of chitin synthase genes changed through R. globosum cell development, with a positive relationship between the diversity of transcribed chitin synthase genes and increases in cell morphological complexity (i.e. zoospore > germling > immature thallus). In the multicellular M. oryzae, transcription of the seven chitin synthases varies between different major cell types (e.g. vegetative hyphae and appressoria) and when infecting rice leaves, indicating that specific chitin synthases have different cellular roles29. For example, specific chitin synthases have been linked to roles in the M. oryzae rice infection process, such as the CHS7 gene function in appressorium formation29.

Half of the chitin synthase genes in the R. globosum genome were not detected or minimally detected (≤5 FPKM) in the transcriptomes of any of the three stages. It is possible that some of these genes are active during the mature zoosporangium stage when zoosporegenesis is taking place, including having a role in remodelling the cell wall of the zoosporangium to accommodate the discharge plug for zoospore release (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Video 2). Chitin synthase gene duplication, including paralogs, is particularly common in non-Dikaryan fungi, including chytrids5, and it is therefore likely that some degree of functional redundancy is taking place as we indicate here.

This study establishes R. globosum as a genetically tractable chytrid model, expanding the scope of fungal cell wall research beyond Dikaryan systems. We identified multiple chitin synthases, most with predicted canonical domain architectures and conserved functional motifs. Transcriptomic and protein localisation analyses suggest dynamic, cell development stage-specific expression, indicating enzymes concentrated at key chitin-containing structural regions such as the expanding sporangium wall and apophysis. Our findings highlight the role of chitin synthases in chytrid cell morphogenesis and open avenues for future studies of chytrid cell biology, including wall biosynthesis.

Methods

Strain and culture conditions

We used the R. globosum strain JEL800, a model saprotrophic chytrid21,22,24. For all experiments, R. globosum was maintained at 22 °C in the dark on peptidised milk, tryptone and glucose (PmTG) medium33 either as liquid or on agar plates (15 g l−1).

Construction of plasmids

The plasmids pEK36, pEK39 and tdTomato-Lifeact-7 used previously with B. dendrobatidis were obtained from the Addgene plasmid repository. pEK36 and pEK39 were a gift from Lillian Fritz-Laylin (Addgene plasmids 210598 and 210599)15 and tdTomato-Lifeact-7 was a gift from Michael Davidson (Addgene plasmid 54528)34 (Supplementary Table 1). We also generated five new plasmids in this study by isolating genomic DNA from R. globosum with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen), PCR amplifying with Phusion high-fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs) and seamless cloning (NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly, New England Biolabs, Cat. No. E5520S). To follow the Kalinka et al (2024) B. dendrobatidis experiments to validate R. globosum electrotransformation, the R. globosum ORY39038 (putative chitin synthase) promoter was randomly selected and used to produce ORY39038 promoter mClover, mRuby3 and tdTomato expression constructs with digested pEK36, pEK39 and tdTomato-Lifeact-7 (Supplementary Table 2). Following confirmation of successful R. globosum electrotransformation, to determine expression and localisation of the chitin synthases ORY52914 and ORY53285, we also made two plasmids for fluorescent fusion proteins using each ORY52914 and ORY53285 promoter (Supplementary Table 2). All plasmid sequences were verified with PCR amplification using Phusion high-fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs) and whole plasmid sequencing (Source Bioscience).

Generation of expression strains via electrotransformation

For the transformation experiments, Escherichia coli TOP10 in 10 mL Luria–Bertani medium containing each plasmid was grown overnight, followed by plasmid preparations with a miniprep kit (QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Transformation of R. globosum followed the protocol developed for B. dendrobatidis15,16 with minor modifications as follows. Plasmids were diluted in 15 µl RNA-free water and mixed with 25 µl electroporation buffer AS27 (25 mM D-Mannitol, 0.7 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM KCl). Additional AS27 buffer, 1% tryptone and 1 mm electroporation cuvettes (Geneflow Electroporation Cuvettes, E6-0050) were held on ice. Zoospores were harvested from 24 h old lawns on agar plates and washed three times in 10 ml chilled AS27 buffer with centrifugation for 5 min at 2500g and 4 °C. The washed zoospore pellet was resuspended in 100 µl AS27 buffer, abundance determined with a Luna Cell Counter (Luna Automated Fluorescence Cell Counter, Luna-FL (L20001)) and made to a working abundance of 3 × 107 zoospores ml−1. To electroporate, 40 µl of zoospores and 30 µg plasmid in 40 µl working solution were added to a pre-chilled cuvette, before the cuvette was held on ice for a further 10 min. The cuvette contents were mixed by pipetting 3–4 times, and the cuvette was placed in the electroporator (Bio-RAD-Gene Pulser XCell). Impedance was measured, followed by two positive polarity 350 V/1 ms poring pulses at 100 ms intervals, with an immediately following 20 V/50 ms polarity-exchanged transfer pulse. The cuvette was placed on ice for 10 min and then 200 µl chilled 1% tryptone was added. Following a further 10 min on ice, 400 µl chilled 1% tryptone was added, mixed by pipetting and the contents of the cuvette transferred to a 5 ml tube (Falcon) containing 3.9 ml chilled 1% tryptone before being separated into 600 µl aliquots in 24-well tissue culture plates for recovery. After recovery for 18 h, dependent on the plasmid transformed (Supplementary Table 2), either hygromycin B (Gibco) or neomycin (Gibco) dissolved in 1% tryptone was added to a final concentration of 20 µg ml−1 and 3 mg ml−1 respectively and incubated for a further 78 h.

Cell fixation, staining and microscopy

Chitin was labelled with calcofluor white (CFW) staining21. For actin staining with Alexa fluor 488 phalloidin, 200 µl PmTG grown cells were added to poly-L-lysin coated glass-bottom dishes for 15 min before the medium was removed and the attached cells were washed three times with 500 µl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 1 hr. The fixed cells were again washed three times with PBS and then once with 100 mM PEM buffer (100 mM PIPES (piperazine-N,N′-bis(2- ethanesulfonic acid)) buffer at pH 6.9, 1 mM EGTA (ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid), and 0.1 mM MgSO4. Cells were then stained with 150 nM Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (Thermo Fisher) in PEM buffer for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by washing three times with PEM buffer.

Cells were imaged with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica) with acquisition settings as follows: CFW excitation/emission 405/426-470 nm (intensity 0.098 %, gain 10), mRuby and tdTomato 561/570–620 nm (intensity 2.7 %, gain 651), mClover3 488/500–650 nm (intensity 1%, gain 113) and Alexa fluor 488 phalloidin 488/450–570 nm (intensity 1.1%, gain 77). All comparisons between cell stages were made under identical acquisition settings. Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin and tdTomatolifeact7 images are maximum intensity projections at 0.52 µm z-intervals. All imaging was at room temperature. Images were processed using LasX software (Leica).

Identification and analysis of chitin synthases

To identify putative chitin synthase genes, the R. globosum genome35 was initially screened for glycosyl transferase family 2 genes through the MycoCosm database36 targeting the conserved type 1 (Pfam01644, CS1) and type 2 (Pfam03142, CS2) chitin synthase domains. All resulting amino acid sequences from reciprocal open reading frames were further assessed for associated Pfam matches via the Conserved Domain Database37. Sequence alignments and phylogenetic analysis followed5, using a combination of MUSCLE and the Maximum likelihood method, with phylogeny tested with 100 bootstrap replications in MEGA38. Division I chitin synthases were aligned based on the combined CS1N, CS1 and CS2 domains. Division II was aligned based only on the CS2 domains. The aligned Pfam03142 CS2 domains were screened for the presence of the Conserved Region 1 (CON1) motifs (QXXEY, EDRXL and QXRRW)6,7.

Transcription of chitin synthases

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was used to determine the presence and quantity of chitin synthase gene mRNA at zoospore, germling and immature thallus stages of the R. globosum cell cycle. The RNA-Seq data used are from a previous study characterising the general R. globosum cell cycle24. In summary, synchronised R. globosum cultures (n = 3) were grown with PmTG medium and harvested at 0 h (zoospore), 1.5 h (germling) and 10 h (immature thalli). Cell stages were confirmed with microscopy. Differences in transcription level (Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped fragments, FPKM) between the three cell stages (i.e. zoospore, germling and immature thallus) were determined by initial normality testing (Shapiro-Wilk) followed by Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test using SigmaPlot (SigmaPlot v15, Grafiti LLC).
