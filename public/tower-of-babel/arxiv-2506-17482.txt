On the excitability of two-level atoms by spectrally encoded single-photon wave packets
Hamid Reza Naeij

Abstract
Single-photon wave packets are utilized as quantum information carriers within quantum information science and quantum communication applications. Here, we analyze the time-dependent interaction of a two-level atom with a spectrally encoded single-photon wave packet and obtain the excitation probability of the atom based on the Heisenberg-Langevin equations. Our results show that the spectral encoding process causes the single-photon wave packet to spread in the time domain, and the intensity of the wave packet decreases. Therefore, an encoded single-photon wave packet excites a two-level atom much weaker than an uncoded one. These results are crucial for understanding how encoded quantum light interacts with quantum nodes in realistic quantum networks. In particular, they reveal limitations and trade-offs in using spectrally encoded photons for secure, multiplexed quantum communication. The findings offer insights into optimizing encoding strategies for efficient atom-photon interactions and contribute to the development of scalable and secure quantum networking protocols.

Encoded single-photon wave packets

In this section, we study the interaction between a two-level atom and an encoded single-photon wave packet. First, we review the spectral phase encoding approach.

III.1
Spectral encoding approach

The spectral phase encoding approach to encode the spectrum of a photon wave packet is used in CDMA communication systems. See

A spectral phase-shifting operator

U=e^{-i\sum_{\omega}\theta(\omega)a^{\dagger}(\omega)a(\omega)}=\prod_{\omega}U(\omega)

where

\omega

\xi(\omega)

U(\omega)=e^{-i\theta(\omega)a^{\dagger}(\omega)a(\omega)}

Ua^{\dagger}(\omega)U^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}(\omega)e^{-i\theta(\omega)}

(12)

Applying Eq. (12) to the photon wave packet creation operator in Eq. (8), we have
Ua^{\dagger}_{\xi}U^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}_{\xi^{e}}
where

\xi^{e}(\omega)=\xi(\omega)e^{-i\theta(\omega)}

(13)

in which superscript

denotes the encoded photon wave packet. Moreover, the encoded wave packet in the temporal domain can be obtained by the Fourier transform as

\xi^{e}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\operatorname{d}\omega\xi(\omega)e^{-i\big(\omega t+\theta(\omega)\big)}

(14)

Therefore, we conclude that the spectral encoding process just affects the spectral amplitude
\xi(\omega)
of the quantum wave packet

IV
Conclusion

We studied the time-dependent interaction of a two-level atom with a spectrally encoded single-photon wave packet based on the Heisenberg-Langevin equations. We used a spectral phase encoding method inspired by classical CDMA systems to encode quantum information in the frequency domain of the photon. Considering this formalism, we showed that for an encoded wave packet, the excitation probability of the atom is much lower than an unencoded one. On the other hand, the encoding process causes the wave packet to spread in the time domain and the ability to excite a two-level atom decreases. Moreover, we evaluated how encoding parameters—such as code length and phase randomness—affect atomic excitation probabilities.

These findings are particularly relevant for quantum networks, where atoms serve as quantum nodes and qubits are transmitted over long distances via single-photon wave packets. In such systems, an encoded single-photon wave packet carries quantum information to the receiver. If the receiver applies a decoding mask that is the conjugate of the original encoding mask, the photon’s spectral profile is correctly reconstructed, allowing it to interact effectively with the atomic node. However, if the decoding mask does not match the encoding mask, the wave packet fails to recover its original shape and remains a temporally spread, low-intensity pulse. As a result, it has minimal impact on the atomic excitation. This behavior provides a built-in filtering mechanism: atoms can act as selective photodetectors, responding only to correctly encoded photons while rejecting mismatched or unauthorized signals. Such properties are valuable for enhancing the security and robustness of quantum communication protocols.

Moreover, this research provides a fundamental insight into how spectral encoding, commonly used in CDMA systems, affects the quantum mechanical behavior of light-matter interaction. Understanding this effect is essential for developing robust quantum communication protocols that rely on encoding information in the frequency domain. Our findings indicate that while spectral encoding enhances information security and multiplexing capabilities, it also introduces trade-offs in terms of interaction efficiency with quantum nodes, which must be addressed in the system design. This study also opens the door to future research in optimizing spectral encoding schemes that balance encoding complexity and atom-photon interaction strength. In practical implementations of quantum repeaters or quantum routers, such trade-offs must be well-understood to ensure high fidelity and reliability of quantum information transfer. Furthermore, the methodology presented here can be extended to investigate other encoding formats, multi-level atoms, and more complex photonic structures, including entangled wave packets or time-bin encodings.

Ultimately, our work contributes to the theoretical foundation necessary for advancing quantum network architectures and can inform experimental setups aiming to test photon-mediated interactions at the quantum level with encoded information. The ability to predict and control atomic excitation probabilities in the presence of encoded quantum signals is a step toward realizing scalable and secure quantum communication infrastructures.
