Merging of Kolmogorov-Arnold networks trained on disjoint datasets
Andrew Polar, Michael Poluektov

Abstract
Training on disjoint datasets can serve two primary goals: accelerating data processing and enabling federated learning. It has already been established that Kolmogorov-Arnold networks (KANs) are particularly well suited for federated learning and can be merged through simple parameter averaging. While the federated learning literature has mostly focused on achieving training convergence across distributed nodes, the present paper specifically targets acceleration of the training, which depends critically on the choice of an optimisation method and the type of the basis functions. To the best knowledge of the authors, the fastest currently-available combination is the Newton-Kaczmarz method and the piecewise-linear basis functions. Here, it is shown that training on disjoint datasets (or disjoint subsets of the training dataset) can further improve the performance. Experimental comparisons are provided, and all corresponding codes are publicly available. Keywords: Newton-Kaczmarz method, piecewise-linear functions, training on disjoint datasets.

Since the publication of Liu et al.

Several works have proposed alternative basis functions, aimed at reducing the computational overhead relative to neural networks, such as radial or alternative polynomial bases. In the present paper, it is shown that such difference is not intrinsic to the KAN model class; rather, it arises from particular choices of basis functions and optimisation algorithms. For the piecewise-linear basis functions and the Newton-Kaczmarz optimisation scheme, this disparity is reversed, and highly-efficient KANs’ training is achieved while maintaining comparable accuracy. Moreover, in the present paper, for the first time, it is shown that the application of the federated-learning idea, i.e. training on disjoint subsets of a training dataset and merging the models, can significantly accelerate the training and represents a form of parallelisation of the training.

Although basis functions with continuous derivatives are essential for some applications, such as physics-informed modelling, they are not universally required. Moreover, the basis used during the training does not need to coincide with the basis used in the final model. In particular, a model may be trained efficiently using a piecewise-linear representation and subsequently upgraded to splines or other smooth bases with only minor post-training, thereby preserving accuracy while substantially reducing the training time. Thus, selecting piecewise-linear functions does not represent a move towards a more primitive model; instead, it enables faster optimisation while retaining full descriptive capabilities. The latter is further elaborated in

2  Approach

A Kaczmarz-based training method for KANs with piecewise-linear basis functions was introduced by the authors in 2020 in a preprint later published as
[
3
]
; the approach was generalised to the Newton-Kaczmarz training method for KANs with arbitrary basis functions in 2023 in a preprint later published as
[
5
]
. The reader is referred to the above publications for details; however, for completeness of the paper, a brief summary is provided below, assuming that the reader is familiar with KANs.

The basic building block of KAN is an operator that converts
n
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{y}
into scalar
\hat{z}
. It can be called the discrete Urysohn operator or a generalised additive model:

\hat{z}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}\left(y_{j}\right),

(1)

where
g_{j}
are some functions. A layer of KAN consists of a set of such blocks, forming
m
-dimensional vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
, such that

\hat{z}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{i,j}\left(y_{j}\right),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,m.

(2)

Thus, a layer converts vector
\boldsymbol{y}
into vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
. KAN is a chain of such layers, where the outputs of the previous layer are the inputs for the next layer.

Here, functions
g_{i,j}
are taken to be piecewise-linear. Each argument
y_{j}
lies within a single linear segment. For uniformly-spaced nodes with inter-nodal distance
\Delta y
, the interpolation is trivially computed via

\displaystyle s=1+\left(y_{j}-y_{\mathrm{min}}\right)/\Delta y,\qquad k=\lfloor s\rfloor,\qquad f=s-k,

(3)

\displaystyle g_{i,j}\left(y_{j}\right)=\left(1-f\right)G_{i,j,k}+fG_{i,j,k+1},

(4)

where
k
is the index of the left node,
f
is the relative offset from that node, and
G_{i,j,k}
are the model’s parameters (values of
g_{i,j}
at the nodes).

Training of KAN consists in updating parameters layer-by-layer for each record of the training dataset. To update one layer, a ‘desired’ (i.e. ‘target’) vector
\boldsymbol{z}
is required. One can execute such update by performing one ‘damped’ step of a gradient descend, applied to layer loss function
L=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}\right\rVert^{2}
, resulting in

\displaystyle G_{i,j,k}^{\mathrm{new}}=G_{i,j,k}^{\mathrm{old}}+\mu\left(z_{i}-\hat{z}_{i}\right)\left(1-f\right),

(5)

\displaystyle G_{i,j,k+1}^{\mathrm{new}}=G_{i,j,k+1}^{\mathrm{old}}+\mu\left(z_{i}-\hat{z}_{i}\right)f,

(6)

where
k
and
f
depend on index
j
according to equation (
3
), multiplier
\mu
is the scaling factor, which depends on all layers in the original form of the method.

For the outer layer of KAN, the target vector is given in the training dataset. For some given layer, if target vector
\boldsymbol{z}
is known, then one can calculate target vector
\boldsymbol{y}
for the preceding layer simply by the chain rule for the derivatives:

\Delta\boldsymbol{y}=J^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\boldsymbol{z},

(7)

where
\Delta\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}
are the residuals; matrix
J
consists of partial derivatives, such that
J_{i,j}=\partial g_{i,j}/\partial y_{j}
, which are trivial to compute for piecewise-linear functions.

3  Merging of the models

The described approach iterates record-by-record through the training dataset. The hypothesis of the present paper is that such training can be parallelised using the algorithm as follows.

A number of equal-size subsets (batches) are formed using the records from the training dataset. Each batch should be sufficiently representative of the modelled system, i.e. it is implied that the algorithm will be used for large data. Hence, one should choose the number of batches and the batch size.

For each batch, an exact copy of the model (KAN) is created and trained on the corresponding batch (one pass through the data). The models are trained
in parallel
. After the training is finished, the models are merged into a single model simply by computing the mean value of every parameter.

The above steps (forming batches, creating copies of the model, training copies, merging copies) are iteratively repeated until the selected convergence criteria is reached. The performance of this approach should of course depend on the number of batches and on the batch size. In contrast to federated learning, these parameters may be chosen arbitrarily.

Using this approach, processing of the records from the training dataset should be parallelisable almost ideally, with minor losses for model coping/merging. However, there will be some loss of accuracy due to the merging, and one may expect increasing error with the number of batches. Therefore, there should be an optimal number of batches. Thus, the computational experiments serve two
completely separate
purposes: the first is to establish the scaling of the implementation of the algorithm (i.e. how much time is lost for coping/merging), the second is to find how the accuracy drops with the number of batches.

The loss of accuracy due to the merging is related to the parallel models all being equal in the beginning of the training on the batches. However, in the training algorithm, the model’s update depends on the current model’s parameters. Thus, in the sequential case, each update is performed for the up-to-date model, while in the parallel case, threads starting from the second work with the ‘old’ model.

6  Concurrent-serial alternations of Newton-Kaczmarz

The conceptual description of the Newton-Kaczmarz algorithm provided in section
2
highlights its alternating concurrent-sequential structure. Each layer maps an input vector to an output vector through multiple independent operations that may be executed concurrently. However, the outputs of all concurrent computations must be synchronised before proceeding to the next layer. The backward updates follow the same pattern — a chain of potentially concurrent steps separated by unavoidable sequential dependencies.

These serial steps between independent computations represent a significant obstacle for concurrent training. Neither CPU nor GPU kernels are well suited for such patterns. This limitation can be illustrated using the classical vector-input-scalar-output two-layer KAN:

\hat{z}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j},\qquad A_{j}=g_{j}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{p}f_{j,l}\left(x_{l}\right)\right),

(8)

where
A_{j}
stands for one addend, and each addend is itself a small tree mapping inputs
x_{l}
to a scalar. All
n
addends must be computed in order to obtain the residual
\Delta z
, and these computations can be executed concurrently. This residual, divided evenly among the addends and scaled by the learning rate, is the only quantity required for updating all functions
g_{j}
and
f_{j,l}
. In the piecewise linear model, evaluating a function requires only two operations (one division and one multiplication), which is far too little computation for concurrency on CPUs or GPUs to provide any advantage. The synchronisation required after all
n
addends are computed often takes longer than the time saved through parallelism.

However, this pattern is well suited for customised hardware, such as field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) or application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs). A CPU-based simulation demonstrating a custom synchronisation barrier designed by the authors is available online
2
2

2

https://github.com/andrewpolar/detfast
, but it serves only as a conceptual demonstration; synchronising at each training record introduces substantial overhead. The example implemented in the code is training a model for determinates of
5\times 5
matrices. Training MLPs for the latter requires several hours
[
5
]
, whereas the CPU-based KAN code completes it in a few minutes. An FPGA or ASIC implementation could reduce this further to seconds, because each addend can be mapped to a dedicated hardware processing block. In such customised hardware, concurrency is not emulated through threads or kernels but is realised through physical parallelism, where synchronization and data flow are governed directly by wiring rather than by software-controlled barriers.

6.1  Pretraining

Structure (
8
) motivates a pretraining strategy, in which groups of addends are trained concurrently over the entire dataset to predict an equal share of target
z
. For the example of determinants of
5\times 5
matrices and a dataset of
10\mathrm{M}
records, such pretraining produces a model with the Pearson correlation near
70\%
, providing a strong initialisation for the main training phase. When pretraining is combined with the concurrent-serial alternations, the full training process for
5\times 5
determinants completes in just over
2
minutes on the CPU used for the baseline examples.

7  Conclusions

The results demonstrate that KANs’ training can indeed be accelerated by iteratively training copies of the model on disjoint datasets (batches) and merging them. The speedup ratios between sequential and concurrent KAN training typically fall in the range of
3
–
4
, meaning that acceleration is possible, but there are some limits.

At the same time, even the sequential KAN implementation by the authors consistently outperforms MLPs on CPUs and, remarkably, also on a GPU. While direct CPU-GPU comparisons are formally inappropriate due to differences in hardware, these results are included for reference, taking into account that the GPU used for the benchmarking can be considered to be a typical laptop GPU (it has been noticed by the authors that some researchers have unrealistic expectations from GPU implementations).

Since methodology adopted in the authors’ code is FPGA-friendly, the authors believe that this direction is a perspective research capable to reduce hours into seconds of processing time for very large models and datasets.
