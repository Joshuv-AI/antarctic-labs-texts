Evaluation of the serum levels of endothelin-1 and lipid profile in chronic periodontitis patients, before and after periodontal therapy
Authors: Vanathy Desingu, Saranyan Ravi, Manovijay Balagangadharan, Sayeeganesh Nagarajan, Priya Kesavan, Kurinchichelvan Ramalingam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the serum level of endothelin-1 (ET-1) and lipid profile in chronic periodontitis patients before and after periodontal therapy.Materials and Methods:Sixty systemically healthy patients were divided into two groups, each with 30 patients. Group A with 30 healthy individuals and Group B with 30 chronic periodontitis patients. Group B was further divided into Group B1 and Group B2. Group B1 consisted of 15 patients with clinical attachment loss (CAL) of 1–2 mm treated by nonsurgical periodontal therapy. Group B2 of 15 patients with CAL of 3–4 mm, treated by periodontal flap surgery. Blood samples were collected before and 3 months following periodontal treatment to determine the serum level of ET-1 and lipid profile.Results:There was a statistically significant difference in ET-1 and lipid profile levels before and after periodontal treatment in both Groups B1and B2. However, the difference was more significant in Group B2 than Group B1 (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Serum ET-1 and lipid profile could be a potential biomarker for periodontal and cardiovascular diseases. Periodontal therapy could reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease by reducing ET-1 and lipid profile postoperatively.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is one of the common oral diseases that affects people globally. It is a multifactorial disease that affects the periodontium and surrounding structures of the tooth. It is enhanced by periopathogenic bacterium, ultimately leading to tooth loss and affecting overall systemic health.

Periodontitis is “an inflammatory disease of the supporting tissues of the teeth caused by specific microorganisms or groups of specific microorganisms, resulting in progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone with increased probing depth formation, recession, or both.”[1]

Chronic periodontitis is a slowly progressing disease but also occasionally has a period of more rapid destruction.[2] The host-bacterial interaction may be influenced by local, systemic, or environmental differences, which could explain the elevated levels of inflammatory mediators and cytokines.

The advantages of these noninvasive traditional tools include ease of use and cost-effectiveness. Conventional diagnostic techniques have inherent limitations and only give insight into the current and past periodontal disease and future disease progression. Therefore, research is moving toward advancements where biomarkers and other objective measurements can be used to evaluate and quantify periodontal risk.[3]

A biomarker is an objective, quantifiable measurement or indicator of normal, pathologic, or pharmacological response to any stimuli or therapeutic intervention.[4]

Biomarker-based disease diagnostics are useful in periodontal disease as they are simple, effective, and noninvasive.

Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is a potential biomarker in diagnosing periodontal disease. ET-1 is a potent vasoconstrictor comprises of 21 amino acid peptides secreted by fibroblasts, epithelial, and smooth muscle cells. It has been linked to the etiology of several systemic disorders as well as periodontal conditions, such as drug-induced gingival overgrowth and chronic periodontitis.

Lipid contains water-insoluble substances such as triglycerides (TGs) and cholesterol; therefore, it is transported through a water-soluble fatty acid called lipoprotein. It acts on inflammatory conditions by modifying the cell membrane of macrophages, interfering with proinflammatory cytokines and enzymes as an immune response and are also associated with periodontal disease. Based on their density, these are classified into low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and very LDL (VLDL).

ET-1 and lipids are observed in periodontal disease and also in systemic disease. Thus, the current study focuses on periodontitis and its correlation between ET-1 and lipid profile, which helps in the early diagnosis of periodontitis and systemic disease.

Therefore, in light of the above facts, this study was performed to assess and evaluate ET-1 and lipid profile serum levels in chronic periodontitis patients, both before and after periodontal treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sixty participants participated in this study, with thirty healthy subjects in the control group and thirty subjects in the test group (diagnosed with chronic periodontitis). The study’s purpose and data confidentiality were discussed with the participants. Written and signed consent was collected from all the patients before the procedure. The Institutional Ethical Committee approved the study protocol and followed the Helsinki Declaration 1975, as revised in 2000.

Chronic periodontitis patients (mild and moderate), both male and female, between 30 and 50 years, were included in this study. Patients with any systemic disorders affecting periodontal health who’d used antibiotics in the past 6 months and with <20 natural teeth were excluded. Pregnant and lactating mothers, smokers, alcoholics, betel nut chewers, and patients with severe periodontitis and vertical bone loss were excluded.

A total of 60 subjects were divided into two groups: Group A (Control group): 30 individuals with clinically healthy periodontium and no clinical attachment loss (CAL), and Group B (Test group): 30 individuals with CAL of ≥4 mm at least in 10 sites. The patients were treated either nonsurgically or by sextant periodontal flap surgical periodontal procedures according to CAL.

Based on the treatment plan, the test group (Group B) is further divided into:

Group B1: Patients with CAL of 1-2 mm were treated with nonsurgical periodontal treatment and Group B2: Patients with CAL of 3-4 mm were treated by sextant periodontal flap surgical procedure.

Case history and oral examination were recorded using a mouth mirror, explorer, and William’s probe. Oral hygiene index simplified, gingival index, and clinical attachment level (CAL) were recorded at the baseline and a month later.

Blood samples (3 ml) were collected from the antecubital vein, before and 3 months postoperatively in both Groups A and B patients under aseptic conditions for analyzing ET-1 and lipid profile [Figure 1].

3 ml of blood sample collected from antecubital vein under aspetic condition

No antibiotics or any other medications were prescribed after periodontal therapy, as these factors could influence the expression of ET-1 and lipid profile in the serum.

Following a 30-min clotting time at the room temperature, the collected blood was then centrifuged for 15 min at 1000 rpm [Figure 2]. Serum was isolated and stored at 2°C–8°C and transported to the laboratory for assessing the level of serum ET-1 and the lipid profile using ELISA kit and colorimetric analysis [Figures 3 and 4].

DISCUSSION

Periodontitis is a multifactorial disease and destruction of the supporting tissue of the teeth, marked by elevated levels of inflammatory cytokine and recurrent bacteraemia that often leads to local and systemic inflammatory responses. Periodontitis is considered a risk for various systemic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, peripheral artery disease, diabetes mellitus, respiratory diseases, cerebrovascular disorder, and hyperlipidaemia. Bacteremia caused by chronic infections increases the susceptibility to cardiovascular disease by damaging the vascular endothelium.[5]

ET-1 is a potent vasoconstrictor comprising 21 amino acid peptides secreted by vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and smooth muscle cells.[6] It is the most common endothelin within the human tissues during inflammatory conditions which causes vasodilation by producing nitrous oxide (N2O).[78] It is considered one of the potent cardiovascular biomarkers due to its endothelial dysfunction. This ET-1 is also encountered in periodontal diseases such as chronic periodontitis and drug-induced gingival enlargement.

ET-1 binds to the endothelin A receptor and increases in intracellular levels of calcium. This results in vasoconstriction and smooth muscle contraction.[9] Reactive oxygen species like nitrous oxide (N2O) interact with the endothelin B receptor on endothelial cells, resulting in vasodilation and reduction in nitric oxide (N2O) is a marker of endothelial dysfunction, which is observed in altered endothelium-dependent dilation.[10] This not only identifies but also helps predict future cardiovascular disease.

Porphyromonas gingivalis, a major etiologic factor of chronic periodontitis, produces endopeptidase PgPepO. PgPepO has a striking structural similarity with endothelin-converting enzyme-1, associated with endothelial dysfunction.[11]

Hyperlipidemia is a condition in which any or all lipids/lipoproteins (total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, VLDL, and TG) are elevated in the blood. The factors such as genetics, dietary habits, lifestyle management and other conditions such as diabetes, obesity, and liver disorders also lead to lipid abnormalities.

Lipids are fatty acids that interact directly with the macrophage cell membrane, interfering with membrane receptors and enzyme systems. Porphyromonas gingivalis is composed of lipopolysaccharides in its cell wall that promote the recruitment of defence cells, especially macrophages that secrete tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-1 beta. This increases lipogenesis and lipolysis that leads to hyperlipidemia and promotes phagocytosis and chemotaxis alterations of the defence cells.[12]

This traps excess cholesterol from the periphery and transport to liver, resulting in liver disorders. An elevated level of LDL redistributes lipids from center to peripheral indicates that lipids being accumulated in the unwanted regions such as coronary blood vessels. This correlates with an increased incidence of coronary heart disease and considered as one of the etiologic factors for cardiovascular disorder which is also associated with chronic periodontitis.[12]

Endothelial dysfunction and lipid disorders have established similar findings, such as increased inflammatory mediators, release of reactive oxygen species, disruption in biosynthesis, and their correlation with other systemic disorders. However, there is little to no systemic evidence regarding the correlation between ET-1 and lipid profile.

Therefore, an attempt was made in this study to focus on periodontitis and its correlation between ET-1 and lipid profile, which helps in the early diagnosis of periodontitis and systemic disease.

Although salivary biomarker is considered the “gold standard” for periodontal disease, serum is a stable biomarker correlating with systemic disease.

ET-1 and lipid are observed in periodontal disease and also in systemic disease. Thus, the current study focuses on periodontitis and its correlation between ET-1 and lipid profile, which helps diagnose periodontitis early and systemic disease.

Thus, the current study evaluated and substantiated ET-1 and lipid profile as a potent biomarker for both periodontal and systemic disease.

Khalid et al. assessed to determine ET-1 serum levels in healthy individuals and chronic periodontitis without any systemic disease and attempted to correlate those levels with clinical parameters.[13] ET-1 levels were noticeably greater in chronic periodontitis patients and they showed a reduction that is statistically significant in ET-1 level after the treatment, but still, the reduction in ET-1 levels did not have any significant correlation with clinical criteria.

The current study’s findings differed from those mentioned above [Table 3 and Graph 1], suggesting a statistically significant reduction in serum ET-1 level in Group B1 (non-surgical periodontal therapy). The difference may be attributed to treatment planning and modalities variations, as the current study included surgical periodontal therapy. In contrast, the previously mentioned study focused solely on nonsurgical periodontal therapy. Variation in treatment depends on the extent of inflammation and defect, thereby influencing tissue healing and the outcome.

Blum et al. conducted a study among 22 patients diagnosed with severe periodontitis that was treated conservatively and monitored for 3 months in the absence of systemic problems.[14]

Endothelin and periodontal status were reviewed before and 3 months following treatment. According to their findings, patients with periodontitis were more likely to experience dysfunction than those without it.

Kumar et al. assessed the serum lipid profile between chronic periodontitis patients and healthy individuals before and following nonsurgical periodontal treatment.[15] Fifteen healthy and 15 chronic periodontitis patients (CAL ≥ 3 mm) without systemic disease were included.

All clinical parameters and lipid profiles were noted before and 1 month after nonsurgical periodontal therapy, and there was a significant decrease in total cholesterol, LDL, and significantly more HDL postoperatively. However, no significant change in TG levels was reported before or after nonsurgical periodontal treatment.

The current study’s results also follow the study cited above [Tables 5 and 6], showing a statistically significant decrease in lipid profile levels (TC, HDL, and LDL) in Group B1. However, a significant decrease of TGs in Group B1 (nonsurgical periodontal therapy) postoperatively negatively correlated with the study mentioned above.

There is a lack of literature review regarding the correlation between ET-1 level and lipid profile in chronic periodontitis patients. There is also a lack of interventional studies that evaluate ET-1 and lipid profile levels before and after nonsurgical and surgical periodontal therapy.

Pathak et al. researched to find the correlation between the atherosclerosis risk associated with chronic periodontitis in 45 patients.[16] They were divided into 30 chronic periodontitis patients in the case group and 15 healthy subjects in the control group. Lipid profiles such as total cholesterol levels, LDL, HDL, and TG were recorded. They concluded that TG and LDL were significantly more in the case group.

Raheem et al. assessed and compared the serum lipid levels between healthy and chronic periodontitis patients.[17] The mean values were greater in chronic periodontitis patients than in healthy patients and concluded that chronic periodontitis patients are at greater risk for atherosclerosis.

Sanikop et al. included 100 subjects and suggested there might be a correlation between chronic periodontitis and coronary artery disease, but the explanation for the periodontitis cardiovascular link was unclear.[18]

Therefore, an attempt was made to assess and correlate ET-1 and lipid profile serum levels in chronic periodontitis patients before and following nonsurgical and surgical periodontal therapy in this study. There was a significant decrease in ET-1 and lipid profile in Group B. Although Group B1 (non-surgical) and Group B2 (surgical) significantly reduced ET-1 and lipid profile levels, the significance was more appreciated in Group B2 patients.

This study also compared the difference in ET-1 and lipid profile levels between nonsurgical and surgical periodontal therapy. The result obtained from this study shows that both ET-1, as depicted in Tables 3, 4 and Graph 1 (Group B2-100.31) and lipid profile (LDL, HDL, TG, and TC) were elevated in chronic periodontitis patients [Tables 5 and 6] compared to healthy individuals [Table 2] before surgical periodontal therapy. A significant decrease in both ET-1 (Group B2-68.42) and lipid profile (LDL, HDL, TG, and TC) after surgical periodontal therapy [Tables 3–6] was established.

According to the literature review, ET-1 and lipid profile were also increased in systemic disorders such as cardiovascular disease and reduced after periodontal therapy.

Limitations

The primary drawback is the small sample size. Further epidemiological and interventional studies with large sample sizes can help delineate the exact mechanism of these biomarkers during the various stages of systemic and periodontal disease.

CONCLUSION

Hence, it can be concluded that ET-1 and lipid profile could be considered a prospective biomarker for both periodontal and systemic diseases.
