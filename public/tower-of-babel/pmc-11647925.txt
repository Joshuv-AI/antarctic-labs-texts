The summary of risk response on radioactive substances contained in food items in the aftermath of the Fukushima nuclear accident
Authors: Naomi Hayashida, Itsuko Horiguchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant accident occurred after the Great East Japan Earthquake on 11 March 2011. The regulations for food items contaminated with radioiodine or radioactive cesium were introduced immediately after the accident by establishing a tentative limit for the contamination level. These regulations excluded or minimized the excessive internal radiation exposure in Japan. Subsequently, the Food Safety Commission of the Japanese Cabinet Office established based on the Food Safety Basic Act evaluated the influence of food items on human health, and the information was reviewed to establish finalized reference values according to the Food Sanitation Act. This study aimed to compile a summary from published sources to examine the risk analysis conducted by the Japanese government over 5 years since the disaster occurred, which was carried out to prevent internal exposure to radioactive substances in food. Findings revealed that items exceeding the reference value were mostly found in the item group not under cultivation/feeding management. In addition, the risk management measures to prevent internal exposure to radioactive substances in food have proven to be effective.

INTRODUCTION

The aftermath of the Great East Japan Earthquake and tsunami that occurred on 11 March 2011 caused an accident at the Tokyo Electric Power Company’s Fukushima nuclear power plant, which resulted in radioactive substances, including radioactive cesium, being scattered over a wide area. This study aimed to compile a summary from published sources [1–9] to assess the risk analysis conducted by the Japanese government over 5 years since the disaster occurred (Table 1), which was carried out to prevent internal exposure from radioactive substances in food items.

RISK ANALYSIS

Risk assessment

Establishing a reference value

The Japanese government established provisional regulation values for radioactive substances contained in food items within 1 week of the occurrence of the disaster based on the index values presented by the Nuclear Safety Commission [1]. After the introduction of this measure, the influence of food on human health was evaluated by the Food Safety Commission of the Japanese Cabinet Office, which was established based on the Food Safety Basic Act [2]. It was reviewed by the Pharmaceutical Affairs and Food Sanitation Council of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare to reestablish the finalized reference values according to the Food Sanitation Act, which came into effect on 1 April 2012.

According to this review, reference values were established for all age groups, including children, for radioactive cesium and other substances, based on the standards established to limit the annual radiation dosage from radioactive substances contained in foods to ≤1 mSv. The assumed values of foods containing radioactive substances were set high (at 50%), assuming that all domestically produced items contained radioactive substances, while food intake according to age and sex as well as the influence of nuclides other than radioactive cesium were also taken into consideration. This percentage was set higher than other regulation such as the USA (at 30%), EU (at 10%) or Codex Alimentarius Commission for the standardization of food (at 10%). The values were as follows: 10 Bq/kg for drinking water, 50 Bq/kg for milk, 50 Bq/kg for baby food and 100 Bq/kg for general food items. For comparison, the Codex standard value was set at 1000 Bq/kg. This food health impact assessment adheres to the fundamental principle that it is an evaluation of the effects on human health associated with food intake, and its nature does not change based on whether it is an emergency or non-emergency situation, considering the criteria for scientific evaluation. Importantly, it is not specifically limited to the residents of Fukushima Prefecture, reflecting a broader scope of assessment.

Risk management

Point of production

Various measures were introduced to reduce the number of radioactive substances transmitted to farm produce, including treating the soil with potassium to prevent rice from absorbing radioactive substances; reducing the adhesion of radioactive substances on tree trunks by cleansing the bark of trees using high-pressure water or shaving the coarse outer layer of the bark; reducing the transmission of radioactive substances into tea leaves by pruning the leaves, plant and branches; and avoiding the use of raw timber for mushroom cultivation to ensure the usage of safe production materials [3].

Guidelines

To prevent the occurrence of health hazards related to food consumption and protect public health, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare established provisional standards on 17 March 2011. The purpose was to ensure that foods exceeding the regulatory limits were not distributed for consumption. Notifications were issued to local authorities to enforce the standards. However, as the standards were set urgently without undergoing a comprehensive food health impact assessment, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare made an evaluation request to the Food Safety Commission on 20 March 2011 based on the Food Safety Basic Law. Considering the urgent societal situation, the Food Safety Commission prioritized and conducted an intensive review as part of emergency response measures, notifying the Minister of Health, Labour and Welfare of the ‘Emergency Report on Radioactive Substances’ on 29 March 2011 [2]. Subsequent evaluations continued, and the final reports were compiled on 27 October 2011.

To ensure food safety in Japan, a risk analysis defined by the Codex Alimentarius Commission has been implemented. Typically, upon request from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the Food Safety Commission conducts risk assessments, notifies the results and establishes standards through deliberation in the ministry’s council. However, due to the emergency nature of this situation, the process deviated, and the Nuclear Safety Commission initially provided reference values, which was followed by a subsequent risk assessment. This deviation from the usual flow illustrates that the risk analysis conducted in this instance was oriented toward ‘crisis response’, differing from the conventional risk analysis for food safety.

Based on this emergency report, guidelines for the inspection of food containing radioactive substances were formulated by the Nuclear Emergency Response Headquarters on 4 April 2011 within 1 month of the incident. By 2016, the guidelines had been revised seven times. In the guidelines, the essential items of the inspection plan were defined for local municipalities, including municipalities subject to the guidelines, items subject to the guidelines, frequency of inspections and responses based on the inspection results. In addition, these guidelines detailed restrictions on food distribution as a measure by the Japanese government in cases where the content value exceeded the reference value, and the extent of contamination was widespread. The guidelines also placed restrictions on food intake in cases where extremely high concentrations of radioactive substances were found in the food.

Initially, the following 11 municipalities were subject to the guidelines: Fukushima Prefecture, Ibaraki Prefecture, Tochigi Prefecture, Gunma Prefecture and the neighboring municipalities of Miyagi Prefecture, Yamagata Prefecture, Niigata Prefecture, Nagano Prefecture, Saitama Prefecture, Chiba Prefecture and the Tokyo metropolitan area. Kanagawa Prefecture and its neighboring municipalities (Yamanashi and Shizuoka Prefectures) were added to the list in June 2011 as the level of radioactive substances in tea products exceeded the provisional regulation value. Iwate Prefecture, Akita Prefecture and the neighboring municipality of Aomori Prefecture were added to the list in August of the same year because the level of radioactive substances in beef products exceeded the provisional regulation value. As of August 2011, the list comprised 17 prefectures, but since then, no revisions have been made [4].

Items subject to inspection

Items subject to inspection were categorized into the following four groups: items in which radioactive cesium was detected in excess of the reference value, items in which radioactive cesium was detected in an amount >50% of the reference value, items that required constant monitoring and inspection because they were strongly affected by feeding management and aquatic products. These items were individually applied to the 17 municipalities. Items requiring planned inspection by each municipality included higher-rank items for intake, major produce, items eliminated from the list of restricted items for distribution, items circulating in markets, processed food items distributed after the drying process, items exceeding 50% of the reference value owing to a failure in production control and items shipped for the first time following the nuclear incident, with no previous inspection results within the region.

Inspection equipment and cost

The inspection equipment used for the screening tests was the thallium drifted sodium iodide (NaI (Tl)) scintillation detector, with the detection limit of ≤25 Bq/kg, which was 25% of the reference value, and the measurements for finalizing the inspection results were performed using a germanium (Ge) semiconductor detector (Fig. 1). The detection limit of 25 Bq/kg is defined by the screening method for radioactive cesium in food products established by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare [5]. It represents the performance requirements mandated for the analytical instruments. Although the inspection equipment was provided at the institute of public health and agricultural experimental stations in each prefecture, there were cases where the inspections were commissioned to private analysis laboratories for a fee ranging from a few thousand yen to several tens of thousands of yen for each inspection sample. In addition, there are non-profit monitoring stations operated by citizens, and some of them were funded by certain consumer cooperatives. However, details about standardization of equipment are unclear.

The number of inspection devices used for the inspection program in 2016, based on the inspection guidelines, was 137 NaI (Tl) scintillation spectrometers and 63 Ge semiconductor detectors. According to a survey of the 17 prefectures, the cost of inspections from 2011 to 2015 was ~4 billion yen, excluding labor costs [3]. In light of the risk communication, four obligations [6] have been imposed, including the institutional obligation that states, ‘People expect the government to regulate risks effectively (risk reduction) and efficiently (cost-effectiveness), and information conveying that this responsibility is appropriately fulfilled by the government’. However, discussions on cost-effectiveness in risk communication have been notably absent.

Notably, in Japan, despite the original target of 17 prefectures, 45 of 47 prefectures conducted measurements due to government subsidies. Incurring costs both in terms of personnel and finances, the final expenses surpassed the purchase and maintenance costs of the equipment. Subsidies being discontinued led to a reduction in the number of items tested or the cessation of measurements.

Inspection results

The total number of items inspected before shipment in all affected prefectures from 2011 to 2015 is shown in Fig. 2. Numbers are presented by category, including items under cultivation/feeding management, items not under cultivation/feeding management and other items, such as processed foods. The items under cultivation/feeding management included vegetables/tubers and roots, fruits/seeds, rice, wheat/barley, beans/cereals, meat, eggs, fresh milk, tea under drinkable conditions, cultivated mushrooms and cultivated edible mountain plants. Items not under cultivation/feeding management included wild mushrooms, edible mountain plants, meat of wild birds and animals, aquatic produce and honey. Total number of inspections in 2011 included the number of items inspected in March 2011. The total number of items inspected in 2011 was 93 121; however, this number dramatically increased to 212 927 in 2012. Since then, the number has increased slightly. Analysis of each item group revealed that the number dramatically increased in 2012, but since then, it has remained flat or has exhibited only a minor increase.

The number of food items detected as containing >100 Bq/kg of radioactive cesium, which is the Japanese reference value established as the limit, is shown in Fig. 4. The overall number of detected items has been showing an annual decline. Only five items (0.002%) were detected in the item group under cultivation/feeding management after 2014. Meanwhile, the number of items exceeding the reference value for radioactive cesium in the item group labeled not under cultivation/feeding management was 259 (1.2%) in 2015, although the number and ratio of detected numbers to the number of inspected items have annually decreased. Table 2 shows the transition in the number of items detected as containing >100 Bq/kg of radioactive cesium. Only a few items were detected among beans/cereals in the item group under cultivation/feeding management. Two rice items were detected in 2015; however, these products were produced in 2014. Inspection results published after this date indicated no instances of exceeding the standard limit for radioactive cesium in rice produced after 2015. In September 2023, radioactive cesium exceeding the stipulated limit of 100 Bq/kg was detected in buckwheat from Fukushima Prefecture. Subsequent reexamination revealed that the contamination resulted from cross-contamination caused by dust adhering to the equipment used. This contamination occurred because some of the equipment involved in harvesting and processing was used for the first time since the disaster without proper cleaning, allowing dust and soil particles to adhere. It was confirmed that the level of contamination from radioactive substances on this equipment was higher than the surrounding environment. In the item group not under cultivation/feeding management, there have been no instances of exceeding the standard limit for radioactive cesium in aquatic produce (marine). However, >100 Bq/kg of radioactive cesium was detected in a few items, such as mushrooms, honey and mountain plants grown in the wild and aquatic produce (freshwater), although the ratio of detection was small. In addition, 20% of the meat from wild birds and animals exceeded the reference value [3, 7].

Confirmatory inspection for distributed products

In addition to inspections conducted at the production stages under the guidelines, a market basket survey was conducted at the food distribution stage [1]. This research was conducted by purchasing food products at 15 locations nationwide, including 3 locations in Fukushima Prefecture, to precisely determine the content of radioactive cesium. The method of measuring radioactive cesium content was to purchase food products according to the local average food intake based on a National Health and Nutrition Survey and then combine the foods prior to the measurement of radiation levels. Food items were subjected to assessments after being cooked in a simple manner, whereas fresh food was purchased from local areas to the extent possible. The results of the research conducted in February and March 2015 showed an annual radiation dosage of 0.0006–0.0011 mSv in food products throughout markets nationwide, including the Fukushima Prefecture.

Risk communication

Surveys concerning inspection guidelines have been performed since June 2016, mainly conducted by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, with 71 groups of organizations, including consumer groups, food-related business operators, producers’ groups, export promoters, mass media and people with relevant knowledge and experience. The free opinion sections of the surveys revealed that ~70% of the participants agreed with the draft revision of the guidelines [8]. Meetings with municipalities from the 17 prefectures conducting inspections were held four times between June and December 2016. In August and September 2016, public meetings were held in Fukushima and Tokyo for stakeholders hosted by food safety authorities, including the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries; Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare; Consumer Affairs Agency and Food Safety Commission [9, 10].

During these meetings, inspection results for radioactive substances contained in food items from 2011 to 2015 were presented. Furthermore, participants completed surveys during these meetings [11, 12]. The questionnaire was developed by the four government agencies that organized risk communication and by the author who facilitated it. According to these surveys, no differences were found in the age and sex distribution of participants between Fukushima and Tokyo (Fig. 5a and b). However, distinct patterns emerged regarding participants’ occupations (Fig. 5c). In Fukushima, more than half of the participants were administrative officials, whereas in Tokyo, there was a higher representation of food processing and distribution industry professionals. Regarding questions about newly acquired information and enhanced understanding, participants in Tokyo significantly indicated a higher awareness that ‘the concentration of radioactive cesium in food has been on a decreasing trend, leading to limited exceedance of regulatory limits’. Although statistical significance was not observed, a higher proportion of participants in Tokyo also acknowledged that ‘various precautionary measures are being implemented at production sites to prevent the exceedance of regulatory limits’ when compared to those in Fukushima.

The difference in the occupations of the participants in this meeting highlights varying needs in different regions affected by the nuclear accident. In Fukushima Prefecture, directly impacted by the accident, there may be an ongoing demand for responses and explanations to residents, addressing their concerns over an extended period after the incident. On the other hand, in the Kanto region, a key hub in the food distribution network, there is a necessity to address concerns related to the impact on food distribution due to reputational damage caused by unfounded fears. From this, it seems crucial that, even more than 5 years after the accident, acquiring accurate knowledge among those involved in the domestic food distribution system and implementing comprehensive risk communication to address diverse stakeholders as a measure against reputational damage are essential.

From January 2017, public meetings were held in Fukushima, Tokyo and Osaka to clarify the circumstances and inspection results along with a draft revision of the guidelines [13]. These meetings were organized by the relevant ministries and agencies to explore the streamlining and efficiency enhancement of inspections since 2017. Opinions on the rationalization and efficiency improvement of inspections were solicited from stakeholders such as consumer groups and food-related businesses. A survey of participants indicated that ~80% of the participants had no opinion, while those who either agreed or disagreed with the draft revision were ~10% each. Additionally, concerning inspections since 2017, ~70% of the surveyed organizations expressed support or leaned toward support for the inspection proposals. Favorable comments such as ‘the inspection proposals are reasonable’, ‘reduction of inspections can be achieved through the guidelines’ and ‘it instills confidence’ were prevalent [14]. This suggests that stakeholders generally find the revision of guidelines to be reasonable.

SUBSEQUENT ACTIONS

The inspection results for the first 5 years indicate that ample farm produce continued to be inspected in 2015 (Fig. 2), whereas items exceeding the reference value were mostly found in the item group not under cultivation/feeding management, and items exceeding the reference value were restricted (Fig. 4 and Table 2). As a result of a comprehensive evaluation that considered the results of related persons and organizations, opinions and survey results from stakeholders who participated in public meetings, new guidelines were announced on 24 March 2017 [15]. This revision focused on the inspection areas and target crops, aiming to streamline the inspection process. These guidelines noted that the item group not under cultivation/feeding management and mushrooms cultivated with raw timber should be categorized into a new group to streamline and optimize inspections. The subjected municipalities and items for inspections were also revised. In addition, for the item group under cultivation/feeding management, a criterion requiring constant inspection was established irrespective of whether radioactive cesium was detected in amounts greater than 50% of the reference value in the inspection results for the preceding 3 years. With this revision, the municipalities subjected to inspection of the item group under cultivation/feeding management (excluding raw timber-cultivated mushrooms) amounted to four prefectures. Subsequently, a policy of reduced inspections was announced, with the media reporting a reduction from whole-bag inspections to spot checks for rice in 2018 and from whole-cow inspections to spot checks for beef in 2019 [16, 17].

Furthermore, these guidelines have undergone annual revisions, considering the accumulation of inspection results and reassessing the items and municipalities subject to inspection. The latest revision occurred on 30 March 2023, during the Reiwa era’s fifth year. This ongoing process reflects the commitment to ensure the effectiveness and adaptability of the guidelines in response to evolving circumstances and scientific understanding. Within this latest guideline, considering the significant variation in the detection status of radioactive substances between groups of items with difficult cultivation/feeding management and those with feasible management, inspection target municipalities are specified for each inspected item [18]. For instance, for item groups with challenging cultivation/feeding management, such as considering the difficulty of management, and for log-grown mushrooms, considering radioactive substances’ impact on production materials, inspection target municipalities are determined. For item groups with feasible cultivation/feeding management, inspection target municipalities are specified based on the detection of radioactive cesium exceeding half of the standard value in the last 3 years for each item.

Additionally, considering the detection status of radioactive substances, separate instructions are provided to designate inspection target municipalities. Moreover, some municipalities conduct inspections, as needed, for other items not designated as inspection targets. Currently, the inspection target municipalities, according to these guidelines, are 17 prefectures. Note that the currently inspected items include those with radioactive cesium detected exceeding the standard value or half of the standard value. This applies to wild mushrooms, mountain vegetables and wild bird and animal meats. For item groups with feasible cultivation/feeding management, the inspected items include vegetables, including cultivated mountain vegetables and log-grown mushrooms. Milk and beef, due to the significant impact of feeding management, are considered items requiring continuous monitoring inspections.

CONCLUSION

Risk communication regarding radioactive substances in food is primarily coordinated by the Consumer Affairs Agency, and it is conducted through information dissemination and panel discussions. As mentioned in this paper, the inspection guidelines have undergone multiple revisions. However, the process has not been made public and the opinions of stakeholders have not been consolidated. The only instance where broad stakeholder input was sought, and a revision was carried out, took place in 2017, making it a special case.

In responding to the food safety concerns following the Fukushima nuclear disaster, this ‘crisis response’ differed from the conventional risk analysis for food safety. However, it is believed that the prompt and irregular response played a crucial role in minimizing food contamination to the lowest possible levels unlike the situation in Chernobyl. The early establishment of a provisional regulation value, formulation of guidelines and measures taken at production points to reduce the number of radioactive substances transmitted to foods and the implementation of inspections and their results indicate that the risk management of food containing radioactive substances to prevent internal exposure has proven to be effective.
