Early prediction of in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with heart failure using an interpretable machine learning model: a retrospective study based on MIMIC-IV
Authors: Meiqi Sun, Ping Lu, Jing Cheng, Linzhi Zhao, Junyang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and acute decompensation frequently necessitates intensive care. Early identification of high-risk patients is essential, yet traditional HF risk scores were developed largely in chronic or ward-based cohorts and often fail to capture early physiologic deterioration in the ICU. Explainable machine-learning (ML) models may improve early risk stratification.MethodsUsing the MIMIC-IV v3.1 database, we conducted a retrospective study of adult ICU patients with heart failure identified by ICD-10 codes (I50.x) between October 1, 2015 and 2022. Predictors included demographics, comorbidities, pre-ICU cardiovascular medications, and vital signs and laboratory tests obtained within 0–6 h of ICU admission. Implausible values were removed; missing data were imputed using medians or modes. An XGBoost classifier was trained with an 80/20 stratified split and class weighting. Performance in the test cohort was assessed using AUC, average precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and Brier score. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used for global and feature-level interpretability.ResultsA total of 12,110 ICU patients with HF were included, of whom 2,041 (16.9%) died in hospital. In the test cohort (n = 2,422), the model achieved an AUC of 0.797 (95% CI, 0.772–0.822) and an average precision of 0.491. Sensitivity and specificity were 0.632 and 0.793 at the default threshold; using the Youden-optimal threshold increased sensitivity to 0.708. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant separation of survival curves across predicted risk quartiles (log-rank P < 0.001), supporting the survival stratification of early risk predictions. SHAP analysis identified FiO₂, age, lactate, Charlson comorbidity index, BUN, and systolic blood pressure as the strongest predictors, revealing clinically coherent non-linear relationships and interactions.ConclusionThese findings suggest that an interpretable model based on routinely available early ICU data may facilitate structured and quantitative risk stratification within the first hours of admission, thereby assisting clinicians in identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from closer monitoring and timely escalation of supportive care, rather than replacing clinical judgment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05943-1.

Background

Heart failure (HF) represents the final common pathway of many cardiovascular diseases and is a major cause of mortality, morbidity, and health-care expenditure worldwide. Recent epidemiological data indicate that approximately 64 million people are living with HF globally, and its prevalence is expected to rise further with population aging, while prognosis remains poor [1]. Acute heart failure or acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) is one of the leading causes of hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) admissions. Large registries such as ADHERE and OPTIMIZE-HF have reported in-hospital mortality rates of about 4–7% overall, and more than 20% among patients with hypotension, renal dysfunction, or cardiogenic shock [2, 3]. Early identification of high-risk patients, particularly within the first few hours after ICU admission, is therefore crucial for guiding treatment intensity and allocating critical-care resources.

Several conventional risk scores for HF prognosis—such as the Seattle Heart Failure Model and the MAGGIC score—have been developed mainly in chronic HF or general ward cohorts. These tools rely largely on baseline clinical characteristics, comorbidities, and laboratory measurements, and pay limited attention to the dynamic vital signs and laboratory results obtained during the early phase of ICU stay [4, 5]. Moreover, traditional regression-based models usually assume linear or proportional-hazards relationships and may fail to capture the complex non-linear effects and high-order interactions that are common in critically ill patients. With the increasing availability of high-dimensional electronic health record data, such limitations may compromise the predictive performance and clinical utility of conventional scores in ICU populations.

Machine-learning (ML) methods have recently shown superior discrimination over traditional models in predicting adverse outcomes among patients with HF by exploiting large numbers of clinical features and modeling complex non-linear relationships. Gradient boosting machines, random forests, and neural networks have been used to predict mortality, rehospitalization, and composite endpoints in various HF cohorts [6]. At the same time, explainable ML techniques, in particular SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), can quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to the model output at both global and individual levels, thereby improving clinicians’ trust in “black-box” models and helping reveal potential mechanisms or modifiable risk factors [7, 8]. However, most existing studies have focused on outpatients or general-ward HF populations and on medium- to long-term outcomes; explainable ML models that use early ICU data to predict in-hospital all-cause mortality among critically ill HF patients remain scarce, and their generalizability and bedside applicability have not been fully established.

Against this background, we sought to develop an interpretable and clinically grounded model for ultra–early risk stratification in critically ill patients with heart failure using routinely available data within the first 0–6 h of ICU admission. Given that substantial prognostic heterogeneity exists even at ICU admission, and that conventional risk scores often rely on longer observation windows or non-ICU populations, an early and transparent prediction framework may be clinically valuable. Therefore, we developed and internally validated an interpretable XGBoost model integrating early vital signs, laboratory results, comorbidities, and pre-ICU cardiovascular medications, with SHAP used to enhance transparency. The model was designed to provide quantitative early risk stratification that complements—rather than replaces—clinical judgment in time-sensitive critical care settings.

Methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective cohort study used the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) database, which contains de-identified clinical data for patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. For the present analysis, we included data from October 1, 2015 to 2022, corresponding to the period during which ICD-10 diagnostic coding was in use [9]. After completing the required training and data use agreement, full access to the database was granted. Because all data were fully de-identified, institutional review board approval was not required.

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a primary or secondary diagnosis of heart failure identified using ICD-10 codes (I50.x) were eligible. To ensure diagnostic consistency and avoid potential misclassification associated with ICD-9 to ICD-10 code mapping, the study cohort was restricted to admissions occurring on or after October 1, 2015. All ICU admissions corresponding to the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training or the test cohort to prevent information leakage, and no admission-level random split was performed. For patients with multiple ICU stays (either within the same hospitalization or across different hospital admissions), only the first (index) ICU stay per patient was included in the analysis to ensure independence of observations. All dataset splitting procedures were completed prior to model training. Patients were excluded if they lacked valid ICU timestamps or had no vital signs or laboratory measurements within the first 6 h after ICU admission.

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality, identified from hospital discharge records and patient-level death information.

Outcome and predictor variables

Predictor variables included demographic information (age, sex), comorbidities derived from ICD codes (diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, COPD, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, obesity, peripheral vascular disease, liver disease, prior stroke, malignancy, and dementia), the Charlson comorbidity index, and pre-ICU cardiovascular medications (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, β-blockers, diuretics, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and digitalis derivatives).

Early physiological and laboratory data were extracted from the first 0–6 h after ICU admission, including vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature, SpO₂, FiO₂) and laboratory tests (lactate, electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, hemoglobin, platelets, WBC, CRP, magnesium, PaO₂, BNP/NT-proBNP, troponin I/T, CK-MB). When multiple measurements were available within this time window, the first (earliest) recorded value was retained for analysis. Anthropometric measures (weight, height, BMI) were obtained from the derived module. All predictors were selected before model development and did not include any future information such as SOFA, SAPS II, or ICU length of stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). The dataset was split into training (80%) and test (20%) cohorts at the patient level (subject-id) using stratified sampling based on the outcome. All admissions from the same patient were assigned to a single cohort to avoid information leakage. Implausible physiologic values were set to missing. Continuous variables with < 40% missingness were retained and imputed using the median of the training set, and categorical variables were imputed using the mode. Variables with ≥ 40% missingness were excluded from model development. Continuous predictors were standardized using z-scores derived from the training set. BNP and NT-proBNP were extracted but excluded from model development due to high missingness.

An XGBoost classifier was used to predict in-hospital mortality. Hyperparameters were selected based on prior literature and further refined through iterative tuning within the training cohort. Model configurations were compared using five-fold stratified cross-validation, with mean cross-validated AUC as the primary selection criterion. To reduce overfitting, model complexity was constrained through subsampling and regularization parameters. Class imbalance was addressed by weighting positive cases (death) fivefold relative to survivors. Final model performance was evaluated on an independent held-out test cohort. Further details of the hyperparameter search strategy are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S2.

Model performance was assessed in the test cohort using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), area under the precision–recall curve (average precision), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, F1-score, and Brier score. The optimal probability threshold was determined by Youden’s index. AUC confidence intervals were estimated by 1000-bootstrap resampling. Calibration was assessed in the independent test cohort using calibration curves, the Brier score, and calibration intercept and slope. The calibration intercept and slope were estimated by fitting a logistic regression model of the observed outcome on the logit-transformed predicted probabilities (i.e., logit(Y) = α + β × logit(p)). To further explore potential miscalibration, a logistic recalibration model was fitted in the training cohort and subsequently applied to the test cohort (train-fit, test-apply approach). Recalibrated probabilities were used for visualization purposes only and were not used to retrain or modify the original model.

To evaluate the survival stratification of early predictions, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed in the independent test cohort. Patients were stratified into quartiles based on model-predicted in-hospital mortality risk, with risk cutoffs derived strictly within the test cohort. Time zero was defined as ICU admission. Time-to-event was calculated as the duration (in days) from ICU admission to in-hospital death. Patients discharged alive were treated as censored observations at the time of discharge. Follow-up was administratively censored at 60 days.

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), including global feature importance (mean SHAP values), SHAP beeswarm plots, dependence plots for the top predictive features. All analyses were performed in Python 3.10.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 12,110 adult patients with heart failure were included in this study, of whom 2,041 (16.9%) died during hospitalization (Table 1). The median age of the cohort was 70 years, and 56.5% were male. Comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, COPD, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation were common, and the median BMI fell within the overweight range. Vital signs and laboratory measurements obtained within the first 0–6 h after ICU admission showed characteristic abnormalities of critically ill patients with heart failure, including elevated heart and respiratory rates, impaired renal function, electrolyte disturbances, and abnormal inflammatory or cardiac injury markers. Overall, the cohort exhibited a substantial burden of chronic illness and multisystem abnormalities, representing a typical high-risk ICU population with heart failure.

Model performance

Among 2,422 patients in the testing cohort, the model demonstrated solid discrimination for predicting in-hospital mortality (Table 2). The ROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.797 (95% CI 0.772–0.822) (Fig. 1A). Precision–recall analysis showed an average precision (AP) of 0.491, notably above the baseline prevalence of 0.17 (Fig. 1B). At the default probability threshold of 0.50, the sensitivity was 0.632, specificity 0.793, and F1-score 0.477, with corresponding positive and negative predictive values of 0.383 and 0.914, respectively.

This threshold was selected as a data-driven operating point to balance sensitivity and specificity, while performance at the default probability threshold of 0.50 was also reported to facilitate clinical interpretability. However, since this threshold was selected on the test set, it should be considered exploratory and not as a fully independent or definitive clinical threshold. Calibration assessment demonstrated that the model shows good discrimination, but the predicted probabilities may require recalibration before direct clinical use. The calibration curve (Fig. 1C) compares the predicted and observed in-hospital mortality probabilities across bins of predicted risk. The curve includes three lines: the original model (blue solid line), the recalibrated model (train-fit, orange dashed line), and the perfect calibration line (black dashed line). The calibration curve indicated that the model tended to overestimate in-hospital mortality risk at higher predicted probabilities, while calibration was more accurate in the low-to-moderate risk range. Logistic calibration analysis yielded a calibration intercept of − 1.34 and a slope of 0.90, estimated by regressing the observed outcome on the logit-transformed predicted probabilities. These values suggest mild miscalibration with a tendency toward risk overestimation and slight overfitting across the prediction range. The recalibrated curve is provided for visualization purposes only and does not modify the final deployed model.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the model achieves strong discrimination, but the predicted probabilities may require recalibration before direct clinical use, particularly at higher predicted probability ranges, in predicting in-hospital mortality among patients with acute decompensated heart failure.

Temporal validation (Kaplan–Meier analysis)

To further evaluate the survival stratification of the early (0–6 h) prediction model, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed in the test cohort stratified by quartiles of predicted mortality risk (Fig. 2). Survival curves demonstrated clear and progressive separation across risk groups, with the high-risk quartile showing the steepest decline in survival probability and the shortest time-to-death. In contrast, patients in the low-risk quartile exhibited substantially higher survival probabilities throughout the hospitalization period. The differences between risk groups were statistically significant (log-rank P < 0.001), indicating that higher predicted risk based on early ICU data was associated with earlier and more frequent mortality events.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally validated an interpretable XGBoost model for predicting in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with heart failure using routinely available data from the first 0–6 h after ICU admission. Rather than aiming to replace clinical judgment, the primary objective of this model was to provide structured and quantitative early risk stratification in a population with substantial prognostic heterogeneity at ICU admission. Although patients admitted to the ICU are already critically ill, their short-term risk trajectories can vary considerably within the first hours of care, and early identification of higher-risk individuals may help prioritize monitoring intensity and supportive management.

Compared with traditional ICU risk scores, our XGBoost model incorporated a broader range of clinical variables and focused on early (0–6 h) ICU data for risk stratification. While direct comparisons with established risk scores were not performed in this study, prior work has suggested that machine learning–based approaches may offer useful complementary information [8, 10]. Accordingly, such models may serve as adjunctive tools to existing clinical risk scores in critically ill heart failure patients.

SHAP analysis highlighted several key physiological markers associated with mortality risk. Indicators of impaired oxygenation (e.g., higher FiO₂ requirements or lower PaO₂), signs of circulatory failure (lower systolic blood pressure, elevated lactate), and markers of renal dysfunction (elevated BUN and creatinine) were all strongly linked to increased mortality. These findings are consistent with established clinical pathophysiology, which enhances the face validity of the model and supports its interpretability in a real-world critical care context [11]. In addition, higher Charlson comorbidity index scores and advanced age emerged as major risk contributors, reflecting the impact of multimorbidity and frailty on prognosis [12–15]. Importantly, SHAP revealed non-linear effects and complex interactions—such as the pronounced risk escalation when markedly elevated lactate co-occurred with severe hypoxemia—relationships that traditional linear models often fail to capture.

A notable strength of this study is that the model relies solely on clinical data obtained within the first 0–6 h after ICU admission, enabling ultra–early risk prediction. This is clinically valuable because traditional prognostic tools (e.g., APACHE II, SOFA) typically require 24 h or longer to collect adequate information [16, 17]. The early prediction window allows the model to identify high-risk patients before substantial clinical deterioration occurs, offering clinicians additional time to initiate interventions such as hemodynamic optimization, oxygenation adjustment, or escalation of life support. Previous studies have emphasized that early physiological derangements—particularly hypoxemia, elevated lactate, and circulatory instability—carry significant prognostic meaning during the initial hours of acute heart failure or critical illness, and our findings corroborate this evidence [18, 19]. Thus, the model’s 6-hour predictive capability enhances clinical usability and strengthens its potential role as an early warning tool.

From a clinical perspective, the potential utility of the model lies in supporting early situational awareness rather than making autonomous decisions. For example, a high predicted risk within the first 6 h may prompt closer hemodynamic monitoring, more frequent reassessment, or earlier multidisciplinary attention, whereas a lower predicted risk may help contextualize prognosis alongside clinical evaluation. In this way, the model is intended as a complementary decision-support tool embedded within existing clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, the model was developed and internally validated using a single-center public critical care database (MIMIC-IV), and external validation in independent cohorts is needed to confirm generalizability. Although regularization, patient-level data splitting, and a held-out test set were applied to reduce overfitting, the absence of external validation may still limit applicability across different clinical settings. Second, formal subgroup and fairness analyses (e.g., by race/ethnicity or sex) were not performed. Model performance and calibration may differ across demographic subgroups, and future studies should assess potential bias and equity in diverse populations. Third, several clinically relevant variables were unavailable or incompletely recorded in MIMIC-IV. Functional classifications such as NYHA class and echocardiographic parameters (e.g., LVEF) were not included, as they are inconsistently documented and often unavailable within the early ICU time window targeted in this study. BNP and NT-proBNP also exhibited substantial missingness and were therefore excluded to avoid bias. Finally, the model showed a tendency to overestimate risk at higher predicted probability ranges, which may be related to class imbalance and sparse observations in extreme risk strata. Calibration curves and the Brier score were therefore used to provide a more clinically interpretable assessment of calibration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed an interpretable XGBoost model based on routinely available clinical data within the first 0–6 h of ICU admission to predict in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with heart failure. The model achieved good discrimination and provided clinically interpretable insights through SHAP, identifying oxygenation status, hemodynamic instability, renal dysfunction, and comorbidity burden as key contributors to risk. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, this early risk stratification approach may serve as a complementary tool to support timely identification of high-risk patients during the initial phase of ICU care, when risk heterogeneity remains substantial. Given that the model was internally validated using a single-center database, external validation and prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm its generalizability, calibration, and real-world clinical utility.
