The lasting influence of parenting intervention on child behavior: findings from a randomized clinical trial using care for child development guidelines
Authors: Kasra Talebi Anaraki, Nastaran Ghanbarian Alavijeh, Maryam Bemanalizadeh, Omid Yaghini, Negin Badihian, Mehri Khoshhali, Shervin Badihian, Neda Hosseini, Marziye Purpirali, Mansoore Abadian, Seyede Shahrbanoo Daniali, Roya Kelishadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have investigated the role of different parenting intervention programs in the early development of children. Here we aimed to determine the long-term efficacy of Care for Child Development (CCD) guideline interventions on behavioral dimensions of children’s development.MethodsThis randomized clinical trial took place at an outpatient public Pediatrics clinic in Isfahan, Iran from February 2020 to May 2024. Pregnant women between the ages of 18 and 45 in their third trimester were included in the study, and their children were followed for 48 months. The intervention group participated in 5 educational group sessions, each session lasting approximately 45 min. The control group underwent routine education on childcare, as suggested by WHO. The main outcome was the children’s socio-emotional behavior problems using the Children Behavior Checklist (CBCL) at 18, 36, and 48 months. The comparisons between groups were done using independent two-sample t-tests, or the Mann-Whitney test and the Chi-square test. Statistical software SPSS 22 was utilized for data analysis.ResultsFinally, the data of 166 participants (70 in the intervention group and 96 in the control group) were included in the current study. The adjusted mean differences between the intervention and control groups significantly differed in some scales after follow-up. After 36 months of follow-up, the scores in the following aspects were significantly lower in the intervention group: emotional problems, anxiety, seclusion problems, sleep disorders, aggressive behavior, and the total problem. After 48 months, only sleep problems were significantly lower in the intervention group.ConclusionAccording to the results of this intervention, delivering parenting interventions through group sessions according to the CCD recommendation indicated weak efficacy during long-term follow-ups. Further trials are needed to assess the effects of these programs on parents.Trial RegistrationIRCT20190128042533N2, Date of Registration: 16 January 2020, www.irct.ir.Keywords: Child development, Child behavior, Parenting education

Introduction

The earliest years of life (especially the first 5 years) are recognized as an imperative time that establishes the foundations of different aspects of health. Any type of disability occurring in this period could lead to lifelong complications for the individuals and those around them. As a result, this period of life has always been a matter of focus for researchers and healthcare providers [1–3]. Development of children, especially neurodevelopment is highly affected by both genetic and environmental factors [4]. In low- and middle-income countries, nearly 40% of children under the age of 5 years are at risk of not achieving their optimal developmental potential [5]. In our country, Iran, studies have reported different statistics on behavioral problems. A survey among elementary schoolchildren in Mashhad city indicated a prevalence of 36% for behavioral problems [6]. Another study among different provinces in Iran showed a prevalence of 1.42% [7]. Another cross-sectional investigation in Rafsanjan city demonstrated a prevalence of 22% among primary schoolchildren [8]. Therefore, early childhood interventions in this field possess a pivotal role in promoting child development [9]. These interventions are especially effective in the earliest years of childhood compared to the later years of life [10].

Parents as the forefront caregivers of the child could have a considerable effect on the various aspects of children’s development. Sensitive and responsive behavior of the caregiver with the child can result in better development in behavioral, cognitive, socio-emotional, and neuropsychological aspects [11, 12]. As a result, parents need to be supported by educational health programs to gain the necessary skills and capabilities for promoting early child development (ECD). WHO has developed a set of interventions named “Care for Child Development” (CCD) to promote ECD. This package consists of a participant manual, a clinical practical guide, facilitator points, and a monitoring and evaluation framework. These interventions are designed to assist parents or caregivers in communicative activities that enhance different aspects of children’s development. Additionally, they aim to improve responsive skills by coaching parents or caregivers on how to observe, interpret, and effectively respond to the child’s signals. These recommendations would vary as the child grows up; they are adjusted for different age groups from birth on [13].

There are conflicting results surrounding the effectiveness of parenting interventions in different domains of children’s development. Some investigations support their efficacy in different aspects such as language, behavior, cognition, and socio-emotional abilities [14–17]. However, some others did not find beneficial effects for these interventions in children who are not suffering from any developmental complications [18, 19]. Most of the studies did not suggest a specified method for implementing CCD guidelines or have utilized some programs in combination with nutritional intervention, or only investigated the effect of limited training, but not the whole guideline materials, on the development of children. Moreover, there are few investigations on behavioral and socio-emotional aspects in low- and middle-income countries [20]. In our previous investigation [17], we assessed the efficacy of CCD guideline interventions on socio-emotional and behavioral dimensions of children’s development using the Children Behavior Checklist (CBCL) at 18 months and Bayley scales at 12 months, respectively. Here we aimed to determine the long-term (up to 48 months) efficacy of CCD guideline interventions on behavioral dimensions of children’s development.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and protocol registration

This single-blind randomized clinical trial has been approved by the ethical committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1397.092). This investigation was performed based on the declaration of Helsinki principles. The study protocol and its aims were explained to the participants, and written informed consent was obtained from them.

Study participants

This trial was performed from February 2020 to May 2024 at an outpatient public pediatrics clinic in Isfahan, Iran. Included cases were healthy pregnant women (in the third trimester of pregnancy) aged 18 to 45, who had a healthy fetus based on the clinical evaluations. Then, their children were followed for 48 months. We excluded children with congenital abnormalities, metabolic or neurological disorders, seizures, or complications of labor. Individuals who were absent for more than 2 interventional sessions, or refused to continue the investigation, were also excluded. To determine the sample size, we employed the formula for comparing two means, utilizing a type I error rate of 1.96 and a statistical power of 0.90. The assumptions for the two groups, including an effect size of 0.40, were established based on previously published studies [21, 22], with the anticipated effect being considered clinically significant. The calculated sample size indicated a requirement of at least 133 participants per group. Nevertheless, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic and associated social restrictions, we were unable to recruit more than 105 participants in each group. The participants were initially selected based on the convenience sampling method.

Study design and intervention

A total of 210 pregnant women were eligible for inclusion in the investigation. Random numbers were generated by random allocation software in binary blocks, and the pediatric neurologist assigned participants into intervention or control groups of equal numbers. All included participants in the study were invited to participate in a session, as the starting session of the study. In this session, general concepts were discussed including the nutritional needs of children and their developmental status. Then, the intervention group was invited to attend 5 monthly educational sessions, every session lasted 45 min and the control group only attended general sessions for mothers. The sessions were started during the third trimester of pregnancy (the first session), and then 2–6 weeks, 2–6 months, 6–9 months, and 9–12 months after delivery. The educational content of each session was adopted from the Persian version of the CCD-2012 manual. The content of each session was described completely, previously [17]. Mothers in the intervention group received instruction on how to interact and communicate with their children according to their age. Different materials including educational slides, videos, counseling cards, and toys were utilized. Parents participated in play and communication activities to promote their children’s learning, as suggested by CCD guidelines. They also acquired the skills to be attuned to their children’s needs and effectively respond to them through play and communication. The training sessions were held by a pediatric neurologist. The control group received standard child care education recommended by WHO, commonly practiced in primary health care centers nationwide through pamphlets and general skills training, including breastfeeding support sessions. In the end, 166 participants have completed the investigation after 48 months of follow-up.

Measurements and data collection

Demographic data was collected from mothers at the beginning of the study. We asked for the number of children, the educational level of parents, the parent’s age, their income, history of addiction in parents, gestational age, and delivery type. We assessed the socio-emotional and behavioral aspects of children using the Children Behavior Checklist (CBCL) at 18, 36 and 48 months. CBCL was developed by Achenbach [23] and consists of 99 questions that are scored based on the Likert scale as absent (0 points), sometimes occurs (1 point), occurs often (2 points), and one open-ended question. The Persian version of the questionnaire, which was approved by Mohammad-Esmail [24], was completed by the mothers. The CBCL includes not only a comprehensive problem score but also 2 main subgroup scales (externalizing problems and internalizing problems) and 8 specific syndrome scales. The 8 subsections of problems consist of emotional, physical, sleep, anxiety, seclusion, attention, aggression, and other problems such as jealousy, nail-biting, nightmares, overeating, disobedience, etc. The CBCL has been approved and used in different studies among Iranian individuals [25, 26]. An acceptable test-retest reliability was observed with correlation coefficients varying between 0.37 and 0.91 for each item. The average test-retest reliability coefficients for the overall competence score and the total problems score were 0.80 and 0.79, respectively. In the test re-test analyses performed by Achenbach et al. with a two-week interval, a test-retest coefficient of 0.87 was noted [24, 27].

To prevent potential contact and data contamination between the intervention and control groups, sessions were held at separate locations on different days, ensuring that participants from each group did not interact. Independent facilitators conducted the sessions, and outcome assessments were performed by a blinded examiner, minimizing any risk of cross-group influence and maintaining the integrity of the data collected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were summarized as the mean (SD) for symmetry-distributed data or the median (inter-quartile range (IQR)) for non-symmetry distributed data. Categorical data were described by frequency (percent). Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in combination with box plots was utilized for assessing the normality of the data. The comparisons between groups were done using independent two-sample t-tests, or Mann-Whitney test for Continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Considering that our dependent variable in this research is binary, we have used logistic regression to fit the model. All analyses followed per-protocol principles. Data analyses were performed using the statistical software SPSS 22. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From 210 pregnant women included in the study, 105 women were allocated to the control group, and 105 to the intervention group. The data from 166 children were completed after 48 months of follow-up (70 children in the intervention group and 96 in the control group). We lost 23 participants in the intervention group at the beginning of the study, due to the COVID-19 outbreak and social distance. Discussion

This study aims to determine the long-term (up to 48 months) efficacy of CCD guideline interventions on children’s behavioral development. Our results suggest that educating mothers according to the CCD program could lead to better behavioral status in their children after 36 months, especially in subscales including emotional problems, anxiety, sleep disorders, seclusion, and aggression. However, after 48 months of follow-up, just sleep disorders were found to be significantly lower among the intervention group. The results of our previous investigation demonstrated no beneficial effect of the mentioned intervention on the behavioral status of children after 18 months [17]. These controversies might suggest an ambiguous efficacy for educational interventions. Going along the results of a systematic review indicated that in most cases the effectiveness of early parenting interventions on behavioral development was null over time [28].

To the best of our knowledge, this investigation and our previous trial were the first assessments in developing countries in a group-based setting, according to the CCD recommendations. While parenting programs in developing countries have undergone thorough evaluation, there has been less emphasis on addressing socio-emotional behavioral issues and other developmental areas, especially within group settings that may offer greater feasibility and accessibility within the healthcare system. A Cochrane review of 24 clinical trials found beneficial effects of group-based parenting programs in improving socio-emotional behavioral issues in children with a maximum age of 3 years [29]. Like our investigation, we only observed the beneficial efficacy of these interventions after 36 months. This might be due to other possible environmental factors affecting children’s childhood behavioral issues [30].

One of the frequent parenting programs that has been reviewed in our country is the positive parenting program (also known as triple-p). Studies have demonstrated its efficacy in improving behavioral problems in children [31, 32], and the interaction between parents and children [33].

The efficacy of parenting programs on children’s development was found to be inconsistent in different interventions with different follow-up durations [20, 34]. Results of a randomized controlled trial demonstrated that digital behavioral parent training programs may not be effective enough in modulating the behaviors of children and parents [35]. These controversies might arise from different training methods, different baseline characteristics of included children and their parents, and different sample sizes. Recognizing effective and feasible preventive interventions is beneficial for healthcare providers. This approach could illuminate strategies to alleviate the impact of neurodevelopmental disorders and behavioral issues by indicating the optimal allocation of resources especially in low- and middle-income countries.

Strengths and limitations

This study contains several strengths. This is the first interventional study in developing countries assessing the efficacy of parenting CCD recommendations in Iran. Second, we utilized a valid and reliable instrument to evaluate the child’s socio-emotional behavior. In contrast, numerous prior studies failed to provide information on the reliability or validity of the tool used to measure behavioral issues [20]. Third, we followed the included participants in order to determine the long-term effects of the mentioned intervention.

Despite these strengths, this research has some limitations. First, our sample sizes might not represent the whole population well. Given an example, most of the included mothers have university degrees. Thus, further intervention for families with different socioeconomic statuses is needed. Second, due to the COVID-19 restrictions too little time spent in meetings and unfortunately due to social distancing rules, a considerable number of the mothers in the intervention group were unwilling to participate in the meetings. Third, we lacked the resources to evaluate the caregivers’ understanding both before and after the intervention, as well as their compliance with CCD guidelines. Fourth, in this research, we exclusively engaged mothers in group sessions. We did not participate fathers in our parenting program because of their work schedules and possible limited adherence to the study. Moreover, a key limitation of this study is the lack of direct assessment of parent characteristics, such as parental mental health, parenting stress, or parenting practices, which may influence child behavioral outcomes. While the primary aim was to evaluate the impact of the intervention on child behavior, assessing parent variables could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which interventions impact child development. Future studies should consider including validated measures of parental factors alongside child behavior assessments to capture the bidirectional influences within the parent-child dynamic and evaluate any mediating or moderating effects of parent characteristics on child behavioral outcomes. Additionally, measuring parental outcomes would enable future research to assess whether changes in child behavior are accompanied by shifts in parental well-being or parenting approaches, potentially amplifying the long-term effects of interventions. Including these dimensions could enhance the interpretability of results, identify additional intervention targets, and ultimately provide a more holistic view of intervention efficacy. Furthermore, an important limitation of this study is the lack of control over participants’ exposure to information or resources outside of the intervention, which may have influenced parenting practices or child behaviors. Future studies should consider tracking participants’ access to additional information and resources, as this may provide a clearer understanding of the intervention’s unique effects.

Conclusion

According to the results of this intervention, delivering parenting interventions through group sessions according to the CCD recommendation, could lead to better behavioral status in their children after 36 months, especially in subscales including emotional problems, anxiety, sleep disorders, seclusion, and aggression. However, longer follow-ups (48 months) demonstrated only significant effect on sleep disorders. Further interventional studies, especially after the COVID-19 era, are needed in this regard, because of low participation rate of pregnant women in the.
