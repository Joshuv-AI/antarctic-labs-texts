Combining CMB datasets with consistent foreground modelling
M. Tristram, M. Douspis, A. Gorce, S. Henrot-Versillé, L. T. Hergt, S. Ilic, L. McBride, M. Muñoz-Echeverría, E. Pointecouteau, L. Salvati

Abstract
We present a joint cosmological analysis combining data from the Planck satellite, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope, and the South Pole Telescope, constructing a unified likelihood that reproduces the measured temperature and polarisation power spectra by jointly modelling the cosmic microwave background (CMB) signal, galactic and extragalactic foregrounds, and instrumental systematics across all datasets. This approach reduces reliance on external priors and improves the robustness of parameter estimation. Within this joint analysis, Λ \Lambda CDM parameters exhibit remarkable stability with respect to variations in foreground modelling. Extended cosmological parameters are more sensitive to these assumptions, with uncertainties increasing by up to 35%. Despite this, the combined constraints show no significant deviation from Λ \Lambda CDM expectations, and several previously reported tensions – such as the preference for non-zero curvature or the excess of lensing amplitude A L A_{\rm L} – are significantly reduced or resolved. In contrast, the determination of foreground parameters more severely depends on the assumptions made about the underlying models. Overall, this work demonstrates the feasibility and reliability of a fully joint analysis of current CMB experiments, and emphasizes the importance of consistent and accurate foreground modelling for the scientific goals of next-generation, high-sensitivity CMB surveys.



1
Introduction

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) remains one of the most powerful probes of the early Universe, providing precise measurements of the fundamental parameters that govern cosmology. Over the past decade, multiple ground-based and space-borne experiments – most notably the
Planck
satellite, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT), and the South Pole Telescope (SPT) – have independently mapped the CMB sky with increasing sensitivity, resolution, and frequency coverage.

Each of these experiments contributes complementary strengths:
Planck
offers full-sky coverage and excellent sensitivity at large angular scales; ACT and SPT provide high-resolution measurements on smaller scales with deeper observations over limited regions of the sky.
To illustrate the relative sensitivity of each experiment across angular scales, we show in Fig.

Planck

However, these differences in sky coverage, angular resolution, and frequency channels introduce critical challenges to a consistent joint analysis.
Historically, residual foregrounds – including Galactic dust, thermal and kinetic Sunyaev–Zel’dovich effects, the cosmic infrared background, and extragalactic sources – have been modelled independently by each collaboration, limiting the consistency of the combined analysis and the reliability of the derived constraints. Recently,
7
explored the combination of these three experiments using the ACT foreground model applied to
Planck
and SPT.

In this work, we present a coherent combination of the
Planck
, ACT, and SPT datasets, jointly analysing their temperature and polarisation power spectra within a common model for both cosmological and foreground contributions.
This approach leverages the full constraining power of each experiment accounting for differences in frequency coverage and resolution while maintaining inter-dataset consistency.
By fitting a unified model to all three datasets simultaneously, we are able to minimise reliance on external priors, tighten constraints on the standard
\Lambda
CDM parameters and gain new insights into the robustness of foreground treatments across experiments.

2
Likelihood

Information at large angular scales (
\ell<30
) is derived from CMB maps obtained after component separation of the
Planck
data and is treated separately from the smaller scales.

At high multipoles (
\ell\gtrsim 30
), current CMB likelihoods are based on frequency cross-spectra and we adopt a Gaussian approximation, as in the original implementations of the
Planck
, SPT, and ACT likelihoods.
Although the exact distribution of auto- and cross-spectra is non-Gaussian
(
27
, scaled-
\chi^{2}
and more complex forms; see)
, the large number of modes at high-
\ell
makes the Gaussian approximation sufficiently accurate
(
11
)
.
However, cutting the sky to reduce galactic contamination induces correlations between the
C_{\ell}
values, necessitating the estimation of a full covariance matrix. The cross-spectra from the frequency maps
\mathbf{C}_{\ell}
are then compared with the data model
\mathbf{\hat{C}}_{\ell}
, in the likelihood:

-2\ln\mathcal{L}=(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{\hat{C}})^{T}\,\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\,(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{\hat{C}})

(1)

The covariance matrix
\Sigma
includes both instrumental and cosmic variance and is, in principle, model-dependent.
Correlations between multipoles and cross-frequency spectra are included in the likelihood. However, in this work, we do not consider any correlation between the datasets.
As a consequence, to avoid overlap in multipoles, we restrict the multipole range of the ACT spectra when combined with
Planck
.
Specifically, we use
Planck
data for multipoles from
\ell=2
up to 2000 for
TT
, 1500 for
TE
, and 1000 for
EE
, while ACT data are used for multipoles above these thresholds.
The correlations between
Planck
and SPT are neglected, as the two experiments overlap over less than 5% of the sky.

We analyse each dataset (described in Sect.
3
) both independently and in combination.
The model includes the CMB signal, together with residuals of diffuse and unresolved foreground emissions in power spectra (Sect.
5
) and instrumental systematic effects (Sect.
4
).

4
Instrumental effects

In the combined
Planck
, SPT, and ACT likelihood, we implement the same systematic corrections as those used in the individual likelihoods. These corrections account for a variety of well-understood instrumental and astrophysical effects:

Calibration:

For each dataset, we sample map inter-calibration coefficients relative to a chosen reference. For
Planck
, the reference is the first detset map at 143 GHz and we sample coefficients for the five remaining detset maps. In the SPT likelihood, the 150 GHz channel is taken as the reference, and we vary the coefficients for the 90 and 220 GHz channel maps. For the ACT likelihood, we sample five inter-calibration parameters to rescale the wafer maps relative to
pa5
at 150 GHz. Gaussian priors from the original analyses are applied in each case (see Table
3
).

In addition, we include absolute calibration parameters for each dataset, using
Planck
as the reference. Its calibration,
A_{\textit{Planck}}
, is constrained with a strong prior (
A_{\textit{Planck}}=1.00\pm 0.0025
) based on the orbital dipole uncertainty, while the ACT and SPT calibrations are determined relative to it.

Polar efficiency:

The polarisation efficiencies
\rho
are sampled independently for each map with flat priors.

Super-sample lensing amplitude:

The super-sample lensing amplitude in SPT is parameterised by an additional parameter
\kappa
constrained by a Gaussian prior (
\kappa=0.00000\pm 0.00045
).
For ACT and
Planck
, the bias on the angular power spectra is negligible. However, ACT included a second-order correction in the
C_{\ell}
covariance matrix to account for the lensing super-sample covariance arising from fluctuations in the mean convergence across the observed area.

Aberration:

For SPT, the aberration is corrected in the likelihood with a fixed parameter, while for ACT the power spectra are already corrected using simulations. In the case of
Planck
, the aberration effect is negligible.

Bandpass integration:

To compute the foreground amplitudes for a given spectral energy density (SED), it is necessary to integrate over the bandpass of each channel. This is commonly approximated by assigning an effective frequency to each component and channel, as done in the SPT and
Planck
likelihoods. However,
25
showed that bandpass uncertainties in ACT-DR6 contribute significantly to the overall error budget in the recovery of foreground parameters. To account for this, we sample six additional bandpass shift parameters,
\Delta_{\rm bp}
, constrained by strong priors derived from instrumental calibration. In addition, the ACT team incorporates chromatic beam window functions to model the frequency dependence of the beam, an approach we adopt here following
15
.

Beam uncertainties:

Beam uncertainties are negligible for the
Planck
dataset
(
40
, see)
. For ACT, they are included as an additional contribution to the covariance matrix
(
25
)
. In the case of SPT, beam uncertainties are propagated by fitting the amplitude
\beta_{i}
of the first nine eigenmodes of the beam covariance matrix. Furthermore, the SPT likelihood includes additional freedom to rescale the polarisation amplitude of the sidelobes at each frequency
(
10
)
.

Temperature-to-Polarisation Leakage:

For
Planck
, the spectra are already corrected and the associated uncertainty is negligible.
In the case of ACT, it is included as an additional term in the covariance matrix, while the SPT likelihood propagates the uncertainty by fitting a contribution from quadrupolar beam leakage, modelled analytically for each frequency
(
10
)
. Associated Gaussian priors are given in Table
3
.

The nuisance parameters and their associated priors are listed in Table
3
. When combining all datasets, a total of 48 nuisance parameters are sampled. Although the large number of parameters can pose challenges for MCMC sampling – particularly due to degeneracies and long correlation lengths – they are essential for accurately propagating instrumental uncertainties that affect the angular power spectra. Including these parameters ensures that the cosmological constraints properly account for residual systematics and modelling uncertainties across all experiments.

5
Sky model

We now present the sky model (
\mathbf{\hat{C}}_{\ell}
) used in the CMB likelihood (Eq.
1
).
Foreground emissions are first mitigated by masking high-signal regions and selecting an appropriate multipole range. Residual diffuse and unresolved components are then modelled directly in the likelihood, with contributions in temperature (
T
) and polarisation (
P
) treated as follows:

•

galactic emissions (
T
,
P
),

•

the cosmic infrared background (
T
),

•

the Sunyaev-Zel’dovich effect: thermal, kinetic, and correlation between tSZ and infrared galaxies (
T
),

•

radio sources (
T
,
P
).

The cosmic infrared background (CIB) and the Sunyaev–Zel’dovich effects (tSZ, kSZ) as well as their correlation (tSZ
\times
CIB) are assumed to be unpolarised.
Emission from extragalactic carbon monoxide
(
29
;
19
)
is not included in our foreground model, as it remains difficult to model reliably.

In the original implementations of the CMB likelihoods, extragalactic foregrounds are typically modelled using fixed angular power spectrum templates, which amplitudes are fitted simultaneously with the CMB signal. These templates are often derived from external observations (e.g., SZ or CIB measurements), computed from simulations, or based on simplified assumptions (
power-law
).

In this study, we ensure that foregrounds are modelled consistently for all datasets. Foreground templates are shared by all CMB likelihoods and extra-galactic emissions (this includes the CIB, tSZ, kSZ and tSZxCIB) are fitted consistently using the same parameters across the datasets. The summary of all parameters related to the foreground modelling is given in Table
2
.

Crucially, combining multiple experiments with complementary frequency coverage, angular resolution, and noise properties significantly enhances our ability to disentangle foreground contributions.
Planck
is particularly effective for galactic components and the CIB, while ACT and SPT  with higher resolution and small-scale sensitivity, better constrain tSZ, kSZ, and point-source signals. This complementarity breaks degeneracies that persist in single-dataset analyses, allowing many of the informative priors usually imposed on foreground parameters to be relaxed or removed, resulting in a more robust, data-driven sky model (see Table
2
).

5.1
Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

The CMB signal,
\hat{C}^{\rm CMB}_{\ell}
, is computed by numerically solving the background and perturbation equations for a given cosmological model. In this paper, we consider a
\Lambda
CDM model with six free parameters: the present-day baryon density (
\Omega_{b}h^{2}
), cold dark matter density (
\Omega_{c}h^{2}
), the angular size of the sound horizon at recombination (
\theta_{\rm s}
), the reionisation optical depth (
\tau
), and the amplitude (
A_{\rm s}
) and the spectral index (
n_{\rm s}
) of the primordial scalar power spectrum. We open the model to some single-parameter extensions in Sect.
8
.

To compute the CMB power spectra, we rely on the Boltzmann solver
CAMB

(
23
)
, as well as the
CosmoPower
emulator
(
48
)
. The emulator is primarily used to accelerate the initial MCMC explorations, while final results are validated with the full Boltzmann codes to ensure accuracy.
To meet the precision requirements of high-resolution surveys, we adopt the accuracy settings of the ACT-DR6 analysis
(
25
)
.

Unlike the original analyses, we do not impose any prior on the reionisation optical depth
\tau
even when datasets covering only high multipoles (ACT or SPT) are considered individually.

5.2
Galactic emissions

For galactic emissions, CMB likelihoods consider only Galactic dust, neglecting synchrotron or free-free emissions in the frequency range of our datasets
(
35
)
.

At first order, the frequency dependence of Galactic dust grains can be modelled as a greybody
a_{\nu}^{\rm dust}=\nu^{\beta_{\rm d}}B_{\nu}(T_{\rm d})
.
Throughout the frequency range of our datasets, the Rayleigh–Jeans approximation applies, leading to a strong degeneracy between
\beta_{\rm d}
and
T_{\rm d}

T_{\rm d}=19.6
K.
The spectral indices can be fixed or marginalised over with Gaussian priors.

The power spectrum of the Galactic dust emission has been measured from
Planck

(
34
;
38

The dust spectrum is well fitted with a red index power law

In the ACT likelihood
(
25

\alpha_{\rm d}^{TT}=-2.6

\alpha_{\rm d}^{TE/EE}=\mathcal{N}(-2.42,0.04)

For the
Planck

54

-2.65

-2.55

-2.45

C_{\ell}\propto(1+h\ell^{k}\exp{-\ell/t})\times(\ell/\ell_{p})^{\alpha_{\rm d}}

Although the index can vary slightly across the sky, dust emissions are well described by a global

The amplitude

5.4
Thermal Sunyaev–Zeldovich (tSZ)

The tSZ effect is a spectral distortion of the CMB caused by inverse Compton scattering of photons off the hot gas of massive halos, groups and clusters of galaxies
(
50

Neglecting the relativistic correction, the frequency dependence of the tSZ effect is given by
a_{\nu}^{\rm tSZ}=x(e^{x}+1)/(e^{x}-1)-4

The

In addition,

5.5
Kinetic Sunyaev–Zeldovich (kSZ)

Due to the motion of galaxy clusters with respect to the CMB rest frame, an additional spectral distortion arises from the Doppler effect of their bulk velocity on the scattered CMB photons. This distortion is known as the kinetic Sunyaev–Zeldovich (kSZ) effect
(
61
;
49
)
, with amplitude proportional to the line-of-sight velocity
v_{c}
,
\Delta T_{\rm kSZ}/T_{\rm CMB}=-\tau_{e}v_{c}/c
, where
\tau_{e}
is the optical depth of the cluster.
The kSZ signal shares the same blackbody spectrum as the primary CMB, making it indistinguishable in frequency space and thus requiring template-based modelling.

The kSZ power spectrum is typically derived from simulations or halo models and is often decomposed into (i) a late-time, homogeneous component from scattering by free electrons in the ionised IGM after reionisation, and (ii) a patchy component from scattering off ionised bubbles during reionisation.

The ACT likelihood uses a model based on hydrodynamic simulations with AGN feedback
(
4
)
, while SPT adopts an Agora-based template
(
31
)
, both including only the late-time signal.
Planck
PR3 similarly used a late-time model
(
53
)
, whereas HiLLiPoP PR4 combined late-time
(
47
)
and patchy
(
6
)
spectra. More recently, models describing the power spectrum of the free-electron density contrast in terms of the history and morphology of reionisation have been proposed in
17
, enabling a natural combination of both contributions.

6.3
Impact of foregrounds models

Figure
8
illustrates how the posterior distributions of the
\Lambda

We run several chains corresponding to different combinations of foreground spectra in order to assess how parameter estimates vary with the details of the foreground modelling. In practice, we vary only one foreground model at a time and then concatenate the resulting MCMC chains into a pseudo-chain from which we draw the marginal posteriors.
This procedure approximates Bayesian model averaging under the assumption that the models considered are equally probable and yield nearly identical Bayesian evidence (which we checked to be the case). In this regime, the pseudo-chain posteriors provide a good proxy for full marginalisation over foreground models.

Figure 8:

The mean value of

We explored different assumptions for modelling the dust residual component. First, we remove the prior on the dust SED index

8
Extensions to
\mathsf{\Lambda}
CDM

We explore several extensions to the baseline
\Lambda
CDM model – namely the phenomenological lensing amplitude
A_{\mathrm{L}}
, the effective number of relativistic species
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the total neutrino mass
\textstyle\sum{m_{\nu}}
, and the spatial curvature
\Omega_{K}
– which have been at the center of key CMB tensions in recent years. Testing these parameters within our joint framework is particularly informative, as combining
Planck
with high-resolution, small-scale measurements from ACT and SPT helps break degeneracies (see Annex
D
) and assess whether these anomalies persist when the datasets are modelled consistently.

8.1
Amplitude of the gravitational lensing,
A_{\mathrm{L}}

The phenomenological parameter
A_{\mathrm{L}}
rescales the amplitude of the gravitational lensing potential in the CMB power spectra, effectively modulating the amount of peak smoothing induced by lensing. Previous analyses of
Planck
data have reported values of
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}>1
, suggesting an apparent excess of lensing compared to
\Lambda
CDM predictions and motivating further investigation with independent datasets.

Combining the three CMB datasets and averaging over the foreground models, we obtain

{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}=1.063\pm 0.033\,\text{(stat)}\pm 0.003\,\text{(fg)}\quad\text{({Planck}+ACT+SPT)}.

This result is slightly lower and more precise than the values reported in
10
for the same dataset combination (
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}=1.083\pm 0.037
) or in the P-ACT combination of
25
(
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}=1.081\pm 0.043
).

Overall we show a limited impact of the foreground modelling on the estimation of
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}
, and no evidence for anomalous lensing relative to
\Lambda
CDM expectations.

8.2
Sum of the neutrino masses,
\textstyle\sum{m_{\nu}}

Figure
16
shows the posterior distributions for the sum of the neutrino masses,
{{\textstyle\sum{m_{\nu}}}}
.
Averaging over the different foreground models, we obtain

{{\textstyle\sum{m_{\nu}}}}<0.181\,\text{eV}\quad\text{(95\% CL, {Planck}+{ACT}+{SPT})},

with a standard deviation of
0.019\,\text{eV}
across foreground models. This dispersion represents 35% of the 1-
\sigma
statistical sensitivity (
0.055\,\text{eV}
), indicating that the combined constraint is sensitive to the details of the foreground modelling.

Compared to the
Planck
-only limit (see Annexe
D
), the combined analysis improves the upper bound by more than a factor of two. The improvement is primarily driven by the small-scale information provided by ACT and SPT, which are sensitive to the modification of the smoothing due to gravitational lensing in the
TT
and
EE
power spectra.

8.3
Effective number of relativistic species,
N_{\mathrm{eff}}

The effective number of relativistic species,
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
, quantifies the total radiation energy density in the early Universe and is sensitive to any additional light relics beyond the three active neutrino species predicted by the Standard Model.

Figure
16
shows the posterior distributions for the combination of
Planck
, ACT, SPT for the various foreground templates considered in this paper. Averaging over foreground models, we obtain

{{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=3.031\pm 0.130\,\text{(stat)}\pm 0.045\,\text{(fg)}\quad\text{({Planck}+{ACT}+{SPT})}.

This result is in excellent agreement with the Standard Model expectation of
{{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=3.044
for three neutrino species undergoing non-instantaneous decoupling from the primordial plasma
(
14
;
3
)

This combined result is more precise than any individual dataset and highlights the importance of both the improved
Planck
PR4 data release and the use of a coherent joint likelihood. In contrast, earlier analyses based on PR3 data and obtained by only multiplying likelihoods reported lower values, such as
{{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=2.81\pm 0.12
in
10
and
{{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}}=2.73\pm 0.14
in
9
. With PR4 and the present methodology, these apparent downward shifts are no longer observed: our result is fully consistent with the standard model expectation and robust against the foreground modelling.

\Omega_{K}

The curvature parameter,
\Omega_{K}
, measures the deviation of the Universe from spatial flatness. Analyses based on
Planck
PR3 data have shown a mild preference for a closed Universe (
{{\Omega_{K}}}<0
), hinting at a small but notable tension with the flat
\Lambda
CDM prediction.

Figure

This combined result reduces the preference for a closed Universe that appeared in the

9
Conclusion

In this work, we have constructed a CMB likelihood that combines the latest data from
Planck
, ACT, and SPT within a single, coherent framework. Particular care was taken to implement a consistent treatment of both foreground modelling and instrumental systematics, ensuring that the different datasets can be robustly analysed together.
Compared to a simple multiplication of the individual likelihoods, our approach explicitly enforces consistency in the modelling assumptions and parameterisations across experiments. This is essential for disentangling true cosmological signals from experiment-specific systematics or foreground treatments, and it allows us to quantify uncertainties in a uniform way. Crucially, we can also then marginalise over the choice of foreground templates, ensuring that the resulting cosmological constraints reflect not only statistical precision but also the systematic uncertainty associated with different modelling assumptions.

We first examined the impact of this joint likelihood on the
\Lambda
CDM parameters.
The combined constraints are consistent with those obtained from the each experiments, and the parameter means show negligible dependence on the details of the foreground modelling. Marginalising over the foreground templates increases the uncertainties by less than 5%, except for
n_{\rm s}
(9%).
Relative to
Planck
alone, the joint analysis improves parameter precision by up to
20\%
, demonstrating the complementarity of the datasets and the benefit of a coherent and combined analysis.

Turning to foreground parameters constraints, we observe a stronger dependence on modelling choices. In particular, the reconstruction of the CIB amplitude, the kSZ signal and the tSZ
\times
CIB correlation show significant sensitivity to the adopted templates, while the tSZ is relatively more stable.
The uncertainties on
A_{\rm CIB}
(and, to a lesser extent, on
A_{\rm kSZ}
and
\xi_{\rm tSZ\times CIB}
) are dominated by variations in the foreground modelling rather than by statistical noise.
The treatment of point-source and dust residuals emerge as the most important factors influencing the inferred amplitudes of extragalactic foregrounds. This underlines the necessity of a unified multi-dataset framework to achieve reliable astrophysical constraints.

Finally, we investigate the impact of the joint likelihood on several simple extensions to
\Lambda
CDM. For the lensing amplitude
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}
, the sum of neutrino masses
{{\textstyle\sum{m_{\nu}}}}
, the curvature parameter
\Omega_{k}
, and the effective number of relativistic species
{{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}}
, the combination of datasets provides substantial gains in precision, with no evidence for departures from
\Lambda
CDM expectations.
Several previously reported tensions – such as hints of non-zero curvature, or values of
{{A_{\mathrm{L}}}}>1
– are significantly reduced or even resolved when using PR4 data and the combined likelihood. We further find that uncertainties in foreground modelling have a non-negligible effect, increasing the error bars by up to 35%.

Overall, this work demonstrates that a coherent combination of
Planck
, ACT, and SPT data is feasible and robust. While cosmological constraints remain stable against foreground modelling, the treatment of foregrounds emerges as an important source of uncertainty for
\Lambda
CDM extensions. The framework developed here provides a solid basis for future analyses, particularly those aiming to incorporate more sophisticated physical models, such as halo-based descriptions of extragalactic components
(
12
;
18
;
8
, as done for instance in)
, that can propagate astrophysical uncertainties when fitting CMB data in a controlled manner. Such developments will be crucial for the next generation of high-sensitivity surveys – including the Simons Observatory and the South Pole Observatory – where the precision of cosmological inference will increasingly depend on the accuracy of foreground modelling. In this context, the combination with
Planck
will continue to play a central role, both as a calibration anchor and as a unique source of large-scale information.
