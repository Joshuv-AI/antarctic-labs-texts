One year follow up of phase 1/2 randomized, placebo controlled trial of NDV-HXP-S vaccine in Thailand
Authors: Weerapong Phumratanaprapin, Viravarn Luvira, Saranath Lawpoolsri, Juthaporn Cowan, Sant Muangnoicharoen, Supitcha Kamolratanakul, Yupa Sabmee, Piengthong Narakorn, Somchaiya Surichan, Laina D Mercer, Rama Raghunandan, Christina S Polyak, Ponthip Wirachwong, Jorge Flores, Bruce L Innis, Punnee Pitisuttithum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo promote self-reliance in vaccine production, a chimeric Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV) based vaccine (NDV-HXP-S) was developed in Bangkok, Thailand, with the support of PATH. The interim results of phase 1 on initial safety and immune responses of five formulations: 1 µg, 3 µg, 10 µg, 1 µg+ CpG, and 3 µg+CpG adjuvant were reported. The 3 µg and 3 µg+CpG formulations were selected to advance into phase 2. The full one-year follow-up of safety and immune responses of the NDV-HXP-S vaccine from a pooled phase 1/2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in vaccine naïve individuals with no prior SARS-CoV-2 infection was reported here. The two-dose NDV-HPX-S primary series was safe and elicited a strong immune response over one year, especially in the 3 µg+CpG and 10 µg groups. The 3 µg+CpG group had a significantly higher geometric mean fold rise (GMFR) than the 3 µg group (57.1 vs 40.0, p = 0.028) 14 days after the second dose.Subject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Immunology, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

The emergence of new variants of concern of SARS-CoV-2 continues to challenge both public health authorities and vaccine developers1,2. In addition to the scientific and logistical difficulties of developing new variant-based vaccines, low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) face the challenge of not having sufficient surveillance data to determine when, if at all, the currently used vaccines need to be replaced by a variant-based vaccine3. An additional hindrance for wide use is the cost of the imported vaccines, especially variant-based products, despite the successful efforts of the COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX) initiative from the World Health Organization (WHO) that and /or procurement and equitable distribution of COVID-19 vaccines during the pandemic4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective vaccines against new variants of COVID-19 that can be manufactured at a sufficient scale and remain affordable for Thailand and other low- and middle-income countries.

Leveraging the existing infrastructure of egg-based inactivated influenza vaccine manufacturing facilities in Thailand, we developed an egg-based COVID-19 vaccine using a chimeric Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV) expressing the ectodomain of a novel membrane-anchored, six-proline pre-fusion-stabilized SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-variant Spike (S) protein (HexaPro). The purified and inactivated construct using beta-propionolactone is called NDV-HXP-S5. The use of an exogenous adjuvant may further increase the functional immune response to the vaccine, which may be important to ensure adequate protection in older adults, allowing for dose-sparing and/or increased magnitude and longevity of the immune response. For instance, CpG-adjuvanted HEPLISAV-B® vaccine (Dynavax Technologies Corporation, Emeryville, CA, USA) elicits increased antibody concentrations to co-administered antigen and prolonged duration of the response6. Additionally, CpG strongly favors the development of the Th1 subset of helper T cells and production of IFN-γ7, which is important in anti-viral immunity. Various dose levels of the NDV-HXP-S vaccine with and without CpG have been tested in clinical trials in Brazil8, Mexico9, Vietnam, and Thailand (NCT04764422). The interim analyses of the phase 1/2 in Thailand and Vietnam10 were published10,11. Unfortunately, phase 2/3, both in Brazil and Mexico, was cancelled due to changes in pandemic circumstances.

In this report, we present data from the phase 1 study from the time of the interim analysis through its completion, as well as the entire data from the phase 2 study in which we expanded the sample size of the 3-µg and 3-µg+ CpG groups to ascertain the potential beneficial effect of the adjuvant with more precision over one year.

Results

Study participants

In phase 1210 participants were randomized to each of the six study arms as previously described (35 per arm)11. Almost all participants (201, 95.7%) completed the Day 365 study visit (Fig. 1a). In phase 2, 245 participants were enrolled and randomized; 99 received 3-µg NDV-HXP-S, 98 received 3-µg NDV-HXP-S + CpG, and 48 received a placebo. Of 245, 218 (89%) completed the Day 365 study visit (Fig. 1b). Soon after the start of this study, enrolment in March 2021, the ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine became available for older adults and high-risk groups. The amended protocol with the unblinding process was revised. Immediately after ethical review approval, thirteen elderly participants randomized to the placebo group were discontinued from the study to receive the ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine after the first study visit.

Vaccine safety

Vaccine reactogenicity observed from the time of first vaccination through 14 days post-dose 2 amongst phase 1 participants was published8. The study vaccine was well-tolerated at all doses tested, including those adjuvanted with CpG. In phase 2, the most frequent local solicited reactions during the first week after the first dose were pain (24.2% unadjuvanted; 36.7% adjuvanted) and injection-site tenderness (9.1% unadjuvanted; 17.3% adjuvanted) (Fig. 2). No grade 3 (severe) or higher cases of pain were observed. Similar rates were observed after the second dose.

Solicited adverse events (phase II data only) reported among those groups who had received 3-μg NDV-HXP-S, 3-μg NDVHXP-S + CpG and a placebo.

The vaccine as safe. There were no deaths nor life threatening events reported from all the groups during one year follow up period. Most of the reported solicited adverse events were mild.

The most common systemic solicited reactions were headache (17.2% and 26.5% in the unadjuvanted and adjuvanted group, respectively), fatigue (25.3% and 24.5% in the unadjuvanted and adjuvanted group, respectively), and myalgia (14.1% and 22.4% in the unadjuvanted and adjuvanted group, respectively), in the first week after the first dose. Most of these symptoms were short-lived and mild to moderate in nature. They were similar solicited reactions reported in the placebo group as compared to the unadjuvant group. A few cases of fever (1.0–2.1%) were observed in the vaccine groups and none in the placebo group.

Serious adverse events (SAEs) over the entire study period were reported in 25 (14.3%) participants in phase 1 and 24 (12.2%) participants in phase 2 who received the study vaccines. Among placebo recipients, SAEs occurred in three participants (8.6%) in phase 1 and five (10.4%) participants in phase 2. None of the SAEs were related to the study vaccines (Table 2). One case of AE leading to death was reported in the 3-μg + CpG group. The subject was diagnosed with cholangiocarcinoma three days after the first vaccination and died approximately four months thereafter. This event was not related to the vaccine.

Neutralizing antibody response

The primary immunogenicity endpoint, neutralizing antibody response (50% neutralization or NT50), was measured by a SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus neutralization assay (PNA) based on the Wuhan hu-1 strain at the validated central laboratory (NEXELIS, Canada) at baseline (just before the first vaccination), 28 days post-dose one, 14 days, 6 months, and 12 months post-dose two. Geometric mean titer (GMT) of 50% neutralization (NT50) increased in all vaccine groups at Day 43 compared to baseline (Fig. 3a). A Mild increase was seen in the placebo group. The Geometric mean fold rise (GMFR) with 95% confidence interval of NT50 from the pure effects of study vaccines peaked at Day 43 and appeared to be highest in the 10 μg NDV-HXP-S vaccine group (172.5 [118.52–251.07]). The 3 µg+CpG group had higher GMFR than the 3 µg group (57.1 VS 40.0, p = 0.028) at Day 43, while other vaccine groups were comparable and increased by approximately 40–57 folds (Fig. 3b). At Day 197- or six months post-dose two, NT50 increased in all groups including the placebo group, except the 10 μg vaccine group (Fig. 3a, b). At Day 365, NT50 continued to rise in all groups. The highest GMT was observed in the 10 μg NDV-HXP-S vaccine group (2391.7 [1477.1–3872.9]) followed by 3 µg + CpG (1967.0 [1486.1–2603.4]), 1 μg + CpG (1835.4 [1112.6–3027.7]), 1 µg (1110 [495.4–2486.8]), 3 μg (864.1 [578.4–1290.9]), and placebo (351.7 [217.6–568.6]), respectively. Notably, GMT and GMFR of NT50 of the 3 µg + CpG were statistically higher than the 3-µg group at Day 365, p = 0.001 and <0.001, respectively (Fig. 3a, b).

Anti-S IgG

The secondary endpoint, S-specific binding immunoglobulin G (IgG antibodies to the full-length pre-fusion spike protein of SARS-CoV-2), was measured by validated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), at baseline, 28 days after the first vaccination, and 14 days, 6 months, and 12 months after the second vaccination. Anti-S IgG responses paralleled the neutralization results. The IgG geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) rose continuously from negligible titers at baseline to 889.2 (95% CI: 372.2–2124.4), 680.3 (454.0–1019.3), 1666.5 (1000.6–2755.6), 1334.8 (746.9–2385.7), and 1565.8 (1172.8–2090.6) BAU/mL among recipients of 1-μg, 3-μg, 10-μg, 1-µg + CpG, and 3 μg + CpG dose groups, respectively by Day 365 (Fig. 4a). Anti-S IgG GMC was negligible at baseline and at Day 43 (2 weeks post-dose 2) in the placebo group but increased afterwards to 531.9 (328.5–861.2) BAU/mL at Day 365 corresponding to 151.9 (95.3–242.1) folds rise (Fig. 4b). GMFRs at Day 365 ranged from 300.1 (in the 1-µg group) to 528.9 (in the 10-µg group).

Effect of hybrid immunity and CpG adjuvant

At Day 197, the GMT of NT50 among participants without hybrid immunity was 99.4 (49.8–198.1) for the 3 µg and 125.2 (59.5–263.4) for the 3 µg + CpG group, compared to 3637.3 (1976.8–6692.8) and 5024.0 (2812.1–8975.6), respectively, among participants with hybrid immunity (Table 3). A similar trend was observed at Day 365, with GMTs of 89.3 (29.9–267.2) and 1104.5 (355.7–3429.8) among participants without hybrid immunity, versus 2725.6 (1720.1–4318.8) and 4063.0 (2897.6–5697.0) in those with hybrid immunity. Notably, GMT of NT50 appeared higher in the CpG-adjuvanted vaccine group than the non-adjuvanted group at both time points (Table 3).

Discussion

Given the urgency of developing a local COVID-19 vaccine, we adopted an expedited approach to swiftly test the NDV-HXP-S vaccine, in which dose safety, escalation, and selection studies were conducted in a single phase 1/2 protocol. Following an interim analysis after the dose escalation phase, which confirmed safety and induction of functional neutralizing antibody across all tested doses, we extended the study to evaluate whether the addition of an adjuvant could enhance the magnitude or durability of the immune response, especially in light of antibody waning observed with other COVID-19 vaccines. The combined phase 1/2 data demonstrated relatively higher NT50 and IgG responses in the adjuvanted 3μg group at both 6 and 12 months compared to the unadjuvanted 3 μg group. The non-adjuvanted 10 µg group, which had shown the strongest response at two weeks post-dose two in the interim analysis, and remained high throughout the one-year follow-up. The observed reduction in the GMT and GMFR of NT50 titers in the 10 µg group on Day 197 at Day 197 (Fig. 3a, b) might be attributed to the lower number of participants reporting COVID-19 infection than those reported in the 3 µg and the 3 µg + CpG groups. In addition, all vaccine groups elicited higher immune responses than the placebo group, despite the confounding effects of hybrid immunity and additional vaccine doses received outside the study protocol across all groups, indicating the superior priming effect of the NDV-HXP-S vaccine.

While many studies have demonstrated that the immune responses wane over time following other COVID-19 vaccines12,13, the responses to the two doses of NDV-HXP-S were higher after 6 and 12 months than early after vaccination (14 days after the second dose). This was likely due to the boosting effect from additional vaccine doses outside the study and to natural infection in a third of participants. Most study participants had received four doses of COVID-19 vaccines (two from the study and two from outside the study) by Day 365. Comparing the immune response observed in our study versus other studies reporting immune response post-dose 4 based on mRNA prime-boost strategy, GMT NT50 of the Wuhan strain were similar14,15 even though the assays used were different. These results indicate that NDV-HXP-S is an effective vaccine for priming and facilitates the boosting effects of other vaccines and/or natural infection.

The COVID-19 variants that likely caused breakthrough infections among study participants were the Delta variant prior to Day 197 and the Omicron variant prior to Day 365, based on the enrollment period (March–August 2021). We observed that GMTs of NT50 to the Wuhan strain and GMCs of anti-S IgG were enhanced by infection, consistent with the concept of hybrid immunity. However, the hybrid immune response in the placebo group remained at least three times lower than that of the study vaccine groups, despite 38.6% and 22.9% of placebo participants receiving two or three doses of other licensed vaccines during the study (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). This suggests that NDV-HXP-S provides a strong priming effect. It is also important to note that CpG-adjuvanted NDV-HXP-S induced a stronger response even in those with hybrid immunity.

It is interesting to note that the NT50 slightly waned at Day 197 in the non-adjuvanted 10 µg group, while NT50 increased in all other groups (Fig. 3). This was unlikely to be due to differences in the number of vaccine doses since about 80% of participants in each group equally had other non-study COVID-19 vaccines. It was also unlikely to be due to hybrid immunity, given the limited number of participants with symptomatic infection between Day 43 and Day 197 (<10%). It may be possible that there could be a blunting of the immune response from repeated exposure to the same antigen by other vaccines in a short period16.

NT50 of the participants in the adjuvanted vaccines and the 10ug group were significantly higher than the placebo group at Month 12. Adjuvanted vaccines can spare vaccine doses, an effect that might be helpful in the pandemic situation where billions of vaccine doses are needed. In addition, the study data demonstrated an acceptable reactogenicity and safety profile of the CpG-adjuvanted formulations. This study illustrates the feasibility of LMICs to produce and test a novel vaccine using existing egg-based influenza vaccine manufacturing facilities.

The study has a number of limitations. Importantly, the continuous exposure of the study participants to COVID-19 infections and receipt of additional vaccination over the one-year follow-up confounded our analysis of the immune response over time to the initial 2-dose vaccination. Another major limitation is the fact that the NDV-HXP-S vaccine tested is based on the ancestral Wuhan strain, which is no longer circulating; however, the data obtained can be easily applied to similar NDV constructs based on current strains. NDV-HXP-S constructs are available for several SARS-CoV-2 variants17, and the manufacturing process is easily adaptable to rapidly produce variant-based vaccines.

The study had several strengths as well. It proved the value of a novel platform amenable to be economically produced in facilities that manufacture egg-based vaccines. The NDV-based vaccine platform is highly compatible with existing infrastructure and technology established for egg-based vaccine production18. Given that the SARS-CoV-2 virus does not replicate efficiently in embryonated chicken eggs19, the use of recombinant Newcastle disease virus (NDV) expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, enabled through genetic engineering, provided a viable alternative. This approach allowed for the rapid adaptation of current manufacturing facilities to produce the new vaccine without the need for substantial additional investment20.

The anti-S ELISA and PNA used to assess vaccine-homologous NT50 potency were validated, and results are expressed in international units that allow for comparisons with other vaccines and with future studies. The study demonstrated the boosting ability of the NDV-HXP-S-based immune response by other vaccines and/or by natural disease, as well as the persistence of vaccine-induced antibodies. The induction of anti-S binding and neutralizing antibodies is greater than that observed after natural infection and may last longer. Finally, an additional benefit of the study was the generation of acceptable safety and strong immunogenicity data for the NDV-HXP-S vaccine.

However, the 10 µg dose was chosen to advance further due to the initial higher immune responses and limited availability of the adjuvant. The 10 µg dose was not selected initially, as the inclusion of the adjuvant CpG1018 is anticipated to enhance the antigen-sparing capacity of the formulation. This approach allows for the use of a lower antigen dose, thereby increasing vaccine yield while still eliciting an immune response sufficient to mitigate disease severity, rather than aiming for the highest possible immunogenicity. Furthermore, to clearly assess the effect of the adjuvant, the 3 μg dose was chosen for comparison.

In conclusion, the two-dose NDV-HXP-S primary series is safe and elicits stronger immune responses than placebo throughout one year of follow-up. The CpG adjuvant appeared to induce a moderately higher immune response relative to the unadjuvanted vaccine of the same antigen dosage.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (ClinicalTrial.gov NCT04764422). The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University. All participants had signed consents.

This phase 1/2 single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial was conducted to evaluate the immunogenicity and safety of a two-dose primary series of the NDV-HXP-S vaccine in vaccine naïve participants not previously infected with SARS-CoV-2. The study was conducted at the Vaccine Trial Centre, Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University in Metropolitan Bangkok from 20 March 2021 to 12 September 2022. In phase 1, participants were randomly assigned to six arms: NDV-HXP-S vaccine at three dose levels (1-µg, 3-µg, and 10-µg), CpG-adjuvanted NDV-HXP-S at two dose levels (1-µg and 3-µg), and placebo. Each participant received two intramuscular doses of vaccine or placebo (n = 35 per arm) 28 days apart. Interim safety and immunogenicity results through Day 56 were previously published11. The 10-µg dose demonstrated superior immunogenicity, while the 3-µg CPG-adjuvanted regimen also elicited a strong immune response that may offer potential dose-sparing benefits. The advancement criteria to phase 2 included the safety results of phase I and the immune responses generated. A threshold of the immune response at 14 days after the second dose showed sero-response rate (defined as the percentage of subjects in a vaccine group with at least a 4-fold rise from baseline in NT80 titers) of at least 57% at the lower limit (LL) of the 95% confidence interval (CI). Phase 2 focused on the 3-µg dose with or without CPG adjuvant. The phase 1 participants continued to be followed for one year post-dose 2 for extended safety and immunogenicity assessments. The eligibility criteria of study participants were previously published11. Phase 1 included participants aged 18–59 years. In phase 2, participants aged 60–75 years and individuals at risk for severe COVID-19, including individuals with co-morbidities in stable condition over the last three months, were also eligible. Each participant was permitted to receive other available COVID-19 vaccines as of Day 43 or two weeks after dose 2.

The targeted sample size of phase 2 was 90 participants for each of the two study vaccine groups and 50 for the placebo group. Combined with at least 31 participants from phase 1, it was expected that there would be 121 evaluable participants, which would allow at least 86% power to detect a minimum 1.4-fold difference between the highest geometric mean titer of 50% neutralization of one of the study vaccine groups versus others.

Vaccine and vaccination schedule

NDV-HXP-S vaccine is an inactivated NDV chimera expressing a trimeric, pre-fusion form of the S protein from the Wuhan hu-1 SARS-CoV-2 that contains six stabilizing proline mutations (HexaPro, HXP). The vaccine expresses the ectodomain of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein on the virus surface21. The vaccine manufacturing methods were previously described5. The adjuvant used in this study was 1.5 mg CpG10187. CpG1018 is the adjuvant in the US-licensed HEPLISAV-B® vaccine (Dynavax Technologies Corporation, Emeryville, CA, USA), a synthetic 22-mer oligodeoxynucleotide that exerts its action by targeting toll-like receptor 9 (TLR-9) expressed on a few key immune-cell types. When used as a vaccine adjuvant, CpG1018 increases antibody concentrations to co-administered antigen22.

The placebo was a commercially available 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection (normal saline, NS). NDV-HXP-S vaccine, CpG adjuvant, and NS were stored at 2–8 °C. Two 0.5 ml intramuscular injections were administered 28 days apart in both trial phases.

Randomization

The randomization plan and corresponding lists were generated by an independent statistician at the Center of Excellence for Biomedical and Public Health Informatics (BIOPHICS) at the Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University, as previously described11. For phase 2, randomization was carried out at a 2:2:1 ratio (3-µg /3-µg+CpG/placebo) with age-stratification using a computer-generated randomization sequence prepared by an unblinded statistician. All participants and study personnel other than the unmasked pharmacy team members and vaccinators were masked to treatment.

The scheduled unblinding procedure was pre-specified in the protocol to allow participants aged 60–75 years who received a placebo to discontinue the study when the authorized COVID-19 vaccine became publicly available. When unblinding occurred before completion of enrollment of elderly participants, the randomization allocation to the placebo arm was skipped.

Vaccine safety

Safety monitoring was conducted by site investigators and reviewed periodically by an internal Safety Review Team (SRT). In addition, an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) reviewed blinded and unblinded cumulative safety data through Day 56 of both phases and was available for review of any specific safety signals identified by the SRT.

Solicited local and systemic AEs via self-record diary card, and unsolicited AEs within 28 days of each vaccination were assessed for vaccine-relatedness. SAEs, medically attended adverse events (MAAEs–unsolicited AEs for which the participants received medical attention), and AESIs were recorded. AESIs include PIMMC and AEs associated with COVID-19 during the entire study period. AE was graded following US Food and Drug Administration guidance23.

Vaccine immunogenicity

The primary immunogenicity endpoint, neutralizing antibody response (50% neutralization or NT50), was measured by a SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus neutralization assay (PNA) based on the Wuhan hu-1 strain at a central laboratory (NEXELIS, Canada), which has been validated24. Serum samples were obtained at baseline (just before the first vaccination), 28 days post-dose one, 14 days (day 43), 6 months (day 197), and 12 months (day 365) post-dose two. Additional details on the PNA have been published elsewhere11.

The secondary endpoint, S-specific binding immunoglobulin G (IgG), was measured by validated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) at NEXILIS, Canada, at baseline, 28 days after the first vaccination, and 14 days, 6 months, and 12 months after the second vaccination.

Vaccine responses among participants who had symptomatic COVID-19 infection during the course of the study (termed “hybrid immunity”) were also analyzed.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Safety outcomes were evaluated in the intention-to-treat population. The number and proportion of participants with adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) were summarized, with two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. Immunogenicity analyses were conducted in the per-protocol population. Neutralizing antibody and anti-S IgG antibody titers were log-transformed prior to analysis. Results were summarized as geometric means with 95% CIs, and geometric mean fold rises (GMFRs) from baseline with 95% CIs at each study visit for each vaccine arm. Although the study was not primarily designed to compare immune responses between vaccine arms, exploratory analyses were conducted, and p-values were reported for statistically significant findings (p < 0.05). In addition, hybrid immunity was assessed among participants who experienced symptomatic COVID-19 infection during the study period.
