Meropenem stress drives lipid remodeling and resistance gene dissemination via outer membrane vesicles in carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Fangfang Fan, Qiuyuan Shi, Guangzhang Chen, Huahan Zhan, Siqian Deng, Yibing Peng, Li Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CRKP-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) serve as multifunctional vectors for antibiotic resistance, encapsulating a diverse array of lipids and harboring the carbapenemase gene blaKPC-2.•Meropenem stress triggers a marked upregulation of membrane lipids in CRKP-OMVs, particularly sphingolipids (SP), glycerolipids (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), and fatty acids (FA), which likely fortify bacterial antibiotic resistance through enhanced membrane stability and efflux efficiency.•CRKP-OMVs mediate horizontal gene transfer of blaKPC-2 to both intraspecies and interspecies susceptible bacteria, with meropenem-induced lipid remodeling significantly boosting transformation efficiency—a potential accelerator for environmental resistance spread.

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), recognized by the WHO as a criticalpriority pathogen, poses a significant clinical challenge due to limited therapeutic options and high mortality rates in invasive infections (Wyres and Holt, 2022; De Oliveira et al., 2020; Tzouvelekis et al., 2012). Carbapenems, such as meropenem, remain the first-line therapy for severe infections caused by extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae (Nordmann et al., 2009). However, the dissemination of carbapenemase genes, such as blaKPC, has increasingly challenged the clinical efficacy of these last-resort antibiotics (Zielonka et al., 2017). Specifically, blaKPC-2 is frequently associated with mobile genetic elements, which facilitate its rapid spread across bacterial populations through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) (Yang et al., 2021).

The persistence of CRKP in clinical settings is largely attributed to its ability to form biofilms, which protects microbial communities within an extracellular matrix, rendering these sessile cells significantly less susceptible to antimicrobial agents than their planktonic counterparts (Hall and Mah, 2017). This protective environment also facilitates HGT, with bacterial plasmid conjugation serving as a primary mechanism for the rapid emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) (Ott and Mellata, 2022). In addition to these classical pathways, Gram-negative bacteria actively secrete outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) to transport DNA, proteins, and signaling molecules (Berleman and Auer, 2013). These nanoscale, lipid-bilayered structures act as critical vehicles for intercellular material exchange and the delivery of virulence factors, further contributing to the complexity of pathogenesis and the dissemination of resistance across bacterial populations (Grenier and Mayrand, 1987; Dorward and Garon, 1990; Dorward et al., 1989; Kahn et al., 1983; Kahn et al., 1982; Kolling and Matthews, 1999; Renelli et al., 2004; Rumbo et al., 2011; Tashiro et al., 2012; Yaron et al., 2000).

Recent studies have demonstrated that OMVs derived from Enterobacterales and Acinetobacter spp effectively mediate the horizontal transfer of blaKPC-2 to recipient cells (Shen et al., 2024; Chatterjee et al., 2017). Meropenem, a last-line carbapenem antibiotic, disrupts cell wall synthesis and induces envelope stress—a known trigger for OMV biogenesis (Nordmann et al., 2009; McBroom and Kuehn, 2007). While antibiotic stress is known to dynamically reshape the molecular composition of OMVs (Li et al., 2025), the specific impact of meropenem-induced lipidome remodeling on the efficiency of blaKPC-2 gene transfer remains unexplored. Elucidating this mechanism is essential for understanding the evolution of resistance in clinical settings.

In this study, we hypothesized that exposure to sub-MIC concentrations of meropenem reorganizes the proteolipid composition of OMVs, thereby facilitating the transfer of antibiotic resistance genes. We demonstrate that meropenem stress triggers substantial lipidomic reprogramming in CRKP-derived OMVs, characterized by significant upregulation of glycerophospholipids, glycerolipids, fatty acids, and sphingolipids. Given that these lipid classes are fundamental to maintaining outer membrane integrity, bacterial survival, and pathogenesis (Sohlenkamp and Geiger, 2016), this remodeling likely alters the structural and functional properties of meropenem-induced OMVs (M-OMVs). Crucially, M-OMVs exhibit significantly higher efficiency in transferring blaKPC-2 to both intraspecific and interspecific recipients compared to OMVs from untreated controls. By integrating quantitative proteomics, lipidomics, and functional HGT assays, our findings reveal that meropenem pressure enhances the ability of OMVs to act as vehicles for resistance gene dissemination. This study provides evidence that antibiotic treatment may inadvertently accelerate OMV-mediated resistance dissemination, highlighting the need for a reassessment of therapeutic strategies to mitigate such ecological consequences.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain characterization and antimicrobial susceptibility profiling

A clinical CRKP isolate harboring an IncFII-type plasmid encoding the blaKPC-2 carbapenemase gene was obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University. This strain demonstrated phenotypic resistance to carbapenems (imipenem minimum inhibitory concentrations MIC ≥16 μg mL⁻¹) and was genomically characterized using the high-fidelity PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing platform (Pacific Biosciences), confirming its classification as sequence type 11 (ST11)—a globally disseminated high-risk CRKP clone associated with nosocomial outbreaks (Fan et al., 2024). The whole-genome sequence of the clinical K. pneumoniae isolate was submitted to the GenBank database, with the accession number JBWXGY010000000.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines using broth microdilution and MIC Strips (bioMérieux) (Humphries et al., 2021). Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 (carbapenem-susceptible, MIC ≤0.25 μg mL⁻¹) and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 (pan-susceptible except for intrinsic ampicillin resistance [MIC ≥32 μg mL⁻¹]) served as recipient strains for transformation assays. Strain BAA-1706 was selected for its lack of endogenous β-lactamase activity, ensuring unambiguous detection of acquired blaKPC-2 resistance.

Isolation and purification of OMVs

Bacterial cultures were aerobically incubated in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (Solarbio, China) supplemented with or without 4 μg mL⁻¹ meropenem (sub-inhibitory concentration), maintained at 37 °C under orbital agitation (180 rpm) until reaching exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.8). OMVs were isolated and characterized in accordance with the MISEV 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al., 2024). OMVs were isolated from exponential-phase cultures of a clinically isolated CRKP strain using a modified protocol as previously described with optimizations (De Lise et al., 2019). The extraction and purification procedures were performed as described in our prior study (Fan et al., 2024), which incorporates sequential filtration (0.22 μm) ultracentrifugation (150,000 × g, 2 h, 4 °C), and density gradient ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 18 h, 4 °C)to minimize contamination from bacterial debris or extracellular components. The OMV layer was collected, resuspend in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and centrifuge again (100,000 × g, 2.5 h, 4 °C). The final OMVs pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

OMVs characterization and quantification

OMVs were characterized by morphological, biophysical, and biochemical analyses as described in our prior research (Fan et al., 2024). Briefly, vesicle integrity and size homogeneity were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, Japan) after negative staining with 2% uranyl acetate, while particle size distribution and concentration were determined via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; Particle Metrix, PMX-120, Germany). Total protein content was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Biosharp, China) with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard; all samples were analyzed in triplicate, with inter-assay variability < 5%. To profile intravesicular DNA, external (free) DNA was removed from OMV suspensions via sequential treatment with DNase I (2 U/mL, 37 °C, 1 h) and proteinase K (50 μg mL⁻¹, 56 °C, 30 min), followed by heat inactivation (75 °C, 10 min). Subsequently, OMVs were lysed with 1% Triton X-100, and intravesicular DNA was extracted using a trace genomic DNA kit (Transgen, China). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop ONE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with acceptance criteria of A260/280 = 1.8–2.0 and A260/230 > 2.0. Purified DNA was subjected to PCR amplification using genus-specific primers, with nuclease-free water serving as a negative control (Fan et al., 2024).

Proteomics profiling of OMVs

Proteomics analysis of OMVs was performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics) coupled online to a TimsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Peptides were initially trapped on a C18 precolumn (Acclaim PepMap100, 100 μm × 2 cm, 5 μm particles; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently separated on an analytical column (PepMap RSLC C18, 75 μm × 10 cm, 3 μm particles; Thermo Fisher Scientific) maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phases consisted of (A) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (84% aqueous acetonitrile), with a flow rate of 300 nL/min achieved using IntelliFlow gradient optimization. Total gradient durations were set to 60/120/240 min, depending on the specific experimental run.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in positive ionization mode using parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF). Full-scan MS1 spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–1700, with an ion mobility range (1/K0) of 0.6–1.6 v·s/cm-². Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) employed a TopN strategy (N = 10) with the following parameters: PASEF cycle time of 1.25 s, precursor target intensity of 1500, and an intensity threshold of 2500 counts. Dynamic exclusion was implemented with a 0.4-min release window to minimize redundant fragmentation.

Lipidomics profiling of OMVs

Lipidomic profiling of OMVs was performed using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) coupled with a QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer (Sciex, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Thermo Accucore C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm i.d.) maintained at 45 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 60:40 (v/v) acetonitrile/water, and mobile phase B consisted of 10:90 (v/v) acetonitrile/isopropanol; both phases were supplemented with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. The injection volume was 2 μL, and the flow rate was set to 0.35 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 20% B (0 min), 30% B (2 min), 60% B (4 min), 85% B (9 min), 90% B (14 min), 95% B (15.5–17.3 min), followed by reequilibration at 20% B from 17.5 to 20 min.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in both positive and negative ionization modes, with the electrospray ionization (ESI) source temperature set to 500 °C. Ion spray voltages were 5500 V and −4500 V for positive and negative modes, respectively. Each ion pair was scanned and detected based on optimized declustering potentials and collision energies.

OMV-Mediated HGT assay

The OMV-mediated transformation assay was performed following the protocol of Yaron et al. (Hua et al., 2022) with modifications. E. coli ATCC 25,922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 cultures in the logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.4 ± 0.05) were harvested by centrifugation (5000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in cold SOC medium to a density of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. Aliquots (60 μL) of bacterial suspension were coincubated with purified OMVs at varying protein concentrations (2, 5, 10, 20, and 50 μg of total protein, quantified by BCA assay), as described in prior research (Chatterjee et al., 2017). The mixture was incubated statically at 37 °C for 4 h to facilitate OMV adhesion, followed by orbital shaking at 180 rpm for another 4 h to enhance internalization. The transformed cultures were then diluted in 10 mL of fresh LB broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C (180 rpm, 16 ± 1 h). Subsequently, cells were harvested by centrifugation (10, 000 × g, 10 min), resuspended in 1 mL of LB broth, and serially diluted. The dilutions were plated on LB agar supplemented with 4 μg mL⁻¹ meropenem (Macklin, China), and transformant colonies were enumerated after 18 h of incubation at 37 °C.

To verify that genetic transfer was mediated specifically by intact OMVs, control experiments were performed in parallel. First, OMVs (20 μg) were treated with 1% Triton X-100 (37 °C, 30 min) to disrupt vesicular structures. Second, OMVs were subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion with Proteinase K (50 μg mL⁻¹, 37 °C, 30 min) and DNase I (2 U, 37 °C, 15 min) to eliminate surface proteins and external DNA, respectively. These treated OMVs were used in the transformation assay under identical conditions. Transformation frequency was calculated as the number of transformant colonies (CFU) per μg of total OMV protein.

Biofilm quantification and structural characterization

Biofilm biomass was quantified using a crystal violet (CV) staining assay (Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, bacterial cultures were grown in 96-well polystyrene plates under static conditions at 37 °C for 48 h. After removing planktonic cells, the adherent biofilms were stained with crystal violet solution for 30 min. The plates were washed three times with saline to remove excess dye. The bound crystal violet was solubilized in ethanol (15 min) and quantified spectrophotometrically at 590 nm using a microplate reader. Sterile medium blanks were used for background subtraction.

For structural characterization, biofilms were cultured in glass-bottomed dishes (NEST, 801,001). After rinsing with PBS to remove planktonic bacteria, biofilms were stained sequentially with 1 μM SYTO9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min and 1 μM propidium iodide (PI) (Elabscience, China) in the dark for 15 min. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was performed using a Nikon A1 laser scanning confocal microscope. Images were captured and processed to visualize the biofilm architecture.

Comparative genomic analysis of transformants

Total genomic DNA was extracted from transformants using a DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity and integrity were verified via spectrophotometry (NanoDrop ONE; Thermo Scientific, USA) and 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, confirming A260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 and A260/230 > 2.0. Genomic DNA was subsequently fragmented to approximately 500 bp, followed by endrepair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end mode). Following quality filtering of raw data, high-quality reads were assembled and evaluated. To characterize the transformants, the Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was calculated using FastANI v1.33 (fragment length = 1000 bp, identity threshold = 95%) against the parental strains (E. coli ATCC 25922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706). Finally, genomic features, including coding genes, noncoding RNAs, and repetitive sequences, were annotated and analyzed to provide a comprehensive genomic profile.

PCR-Based screening of transformants for blaKPC-2

Conventional PCR was used to screen and confirm the integration of the blaKPC-2 gene in E. coli ATCC 25,922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 transformants. The primer pairs targeting the conserved region of blaKPC-2 were designed via NCBI Primer-BLAST, with the forward sequence 5′-ATGTCACTGTATCGCCGTCTA-3′ and reverse sequence 5′-TTACTGCCCGTTGACGCCCAA-3′, which generated an expected 882 bp amplicon. All primers were synthesized and HAP-purified by Sangon Biotech. PCR amplicons were separated on 2% (w/v) agarose gels (Biowest, Spain), stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen, USA), and visualized using a blue-light transilluminator (Bio-Rad, USA). Subsequently, the target PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing for further molecular verification.

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiling of transformants strains

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted according to the 2021 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (Humphries et al., 2021). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of carbapenems (meropenem and imipenem) and extended-spectrum β-lactam antibiotics (ceftazidime and cefepime) were determined using the VITEK® 2 automated system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) with AST-GN83 cards. Bacterial inocula were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard in 0.45 % sterile saline prior to testing. Meanwhile, the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion assay on Mueller–Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) was performed for all CRKP isolates. The applied antibiotic disks included meropenem (10 μg), imipenem (10 μg), and ceftazidime/avibactam (30/20 μg). E. coli ATCC 25922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 were used as quality control reference strains throughout the experiments.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analyses for proteomic and lipidomic data, including principal component analysis, hierarchical clustering analysis, differential molecule screening, and metabolite set enrichment analysis, were performed using R software. PCA was implemented via the built-in prcomp function, and cluster analysis was conducted using the pheatmap package. Differential metabolites and proteins between groups were screened based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and P < 0.05 using Student’s t-test. All identified molecules were annotated against the KEGG Compound and KEGG Pathway databases, and enrichment analysis was performed with the hypergeometric test to evaluate pathway significance. Statistical analyses for bacterial colony transformation data were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA), and unpaired Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. All quantitative data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was defined as: ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, and ns (not significant) for P > 0.05.

Quantitative analysis revealed a 28.4% increase in total lipid content in M-OMVs compared to native OMVs (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1C). Proteomic profiling of antibiotic-induced membrane adaptation

Meropenem exposure induced upregulation of efflux machinery components and lipid transport systems, as evidenced by quantitative proteomics. Notably, efflux pump subunits (YccS: 1.73-fold; MdtQ: 1.41-fold; LptB: 1.4-fold) demonstrated upregulation, with the LptAB lipopolysaccharide export complex showing significantly upregulated (LptB: 1.4-fold, P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Concomitant membrane restructuring was evidenced by elevated expression of lipid-translocating enzymes, most prominently the outer membrane phospholipase Outs (1.76-fold, P < 0.01; Fig. 3B). Notably, critical biofilm formation mediators including the two-component system regulator RcsD, phospholipid trafficking protein MlaC, and OmpA family structural proteins exhibited pronounced upregulation (Fig. 3D). Topology-weighted network analysis further revealed crosstalk between sphingolipid and nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 5C). Notably, sphingomyelin SM(d18:2/20:1) showed a robust positive correlation (r = 0.88) with glutamine synthetase (GLUL, UniProt P15104). This interaction potentially facilitates nitrogen flux through sphingolipid-glutamine shuttling, thereby supporting amino acid homeostasis in OMV-producing cells.

Centrality analysis identified 1-acyl-sn-glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (AGPAT3, EC 2.3.1.51) as a key hub node (Betweenness Centrality = 0.92). AGPAT3 expression was positively associated with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) species [e.g., PE (16:1_16:0), r = 0.83; PE (16:0_14:0), r = 0.86] and negatively correlated with diacylglycerols (DG [14:0_16:0],) and lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC [15:0_10:0]). These findings suggest that AGPAT3 participates in the feedback regulation of phospholipid metabolism and mediates substrate-specific catalytic adaptation under antibiotic stress (Fig. 5D).

OMV-Mediated carbapenem resistance and phenotypic stability

Following incubation with CRKP-derived OMVs (37 °C, 24 h), K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 exhibited a 4-fold increase in imipenem MIC (from 2 to 8 μg mL⁻¹), shifting from an intermediate (I) to a resistant (R) phenotype. Similarly, E. coli ATCC 25922 showed a 16-fold MIC elevation (0.25 to 4 μg mL⁻¹), crossing the susceptibility breakpoint (S→R) (Table 1). Notably, M-OMVs induced resistance levels comparable to those of native OMVs, suggesting that the resistance transfer occurs independently of the initial antibiotic stress during OMVs formation. Serial passage experiments (14 generations in antibiotic-free LB medium) further demonstrated the phenotypic stability of the acquired resistance. Carbapenem stress enhances OMV-Mediated HGT efficiency

We further characterized the efficiency of OMV-mediated plasmid delivery, which exhibited marked dose- and species-specific patterns. Intraspecies transfer within K. pneumoniae (to ATCC BAA-1706) achieved a significant efficiency at 20 μg OMVs protein. Interspecies transfer to E. coli ATCC 25,922 required a 2.5-fold higher OMVs dose (50 μg) and yielded lower efficiency (Tables S1–2). Crucially, carbapenem stress significantly potentiated HGT. M-OMVs increased intraspecies and interspecies transfer efficiencies by 3.52-fold and 12.08-fold, respectively, compared to native OMVs (Tables S3–4). This enhancement correlated with the increased DNA cargo observed in M-OMVs under meropenem pressure (Fig. 6B). Control experiments demonstrated that vesicle structural integrity is essential for this process, as membrane disruption completely abolished colony formation. Functional assays confirmed that M-OMVs, potentially due to higher DNA loading and optimized transport efficiency, serve as potent vehicles for the dissemination of blaKPC-2 (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

The global emergence of CRKP poses a critical threat to public health, with mortality rates exceeding 40% in bloodstream infections and among immunocompromised populations (Chen and Kreiswirth, 2018; Munoz-Price et al., 2013). Understanding the mechanisms governing the HGT of resistance determinants is critical for developing containment strategies. Our study demonstrates that meropenem, while a cornerstone of anti-Gram-negative therapy, may paradoxically facilitate the spread of blaKPC-2 by stimulating the production of OMVs. This process, characterized by altered proteolipidic profiles and enhanced DNA packaging, provides a potent vehicle for both intra- and inter-species resistance transmission. Consistent with previous evidence, carbapenem exposure induces SOS response-mediated vesiculation through peptidoglycan hydrolase activation (Ye et al., 2021), collectively suggesting that carbapenems may inadvertently accelerate resistance spread via extracellular vesicle trafficking.

Proteolipid profiling further revealed envelope stress-associated OMVs biogenesis under meropenem exposure. The upregulation of outer membrane assembly proteins, such as BamB and LptD, alongside an enrichment of phospholipids (e.g., glycerophospholipids), indicates that meropenem disrupts bacterial membrane integrity, which in turn induces compensatory OMVs production via the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM) and LPS transport pathways (Gu et al., 2019; White et al., 2021). Lipidomic results showed marked alterations in OMVs lipid composition, with increased levels of sphingolipids, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids and fatty acids. Previous studies have reported similar upregulation of glycerophospholipids, fatty acids, lysolipids and sphingolipids in OMVs from polymyxin B-resistant K. pneumoniae, whereas these lipid species were significantly reduced in polymyxin B-susceptible strains upon drug exposure (Jasim et al., 2018). Such lipid remodeling may represent a conserved adaptive strategy for maintaining membrane stability under antibiotic stress. Proteomic analysis also identified elevated expression of efflux pumps and biofilm-related lipoproteins, which may cooperate with membrane remodeling to reinforce bacterial antibiotic resistance. Enrichment of phospholipids may further stabilize membrane structure against antibiotic penetration while preserving selective permeability, revealing a dual adaptive mechanism supporting bacterial survival under antibiotic pressure (Sohlenkamp and Geiger, 2016; van Dalen and de Kruijff, 2004).

We observed distinct species-specific preferences in OMV-mediated HGT. The intra-species transfer efficiency from ST11 K. pneumoniae to recipient K. pneumoniae was 4.2-fold higher than the inter-species transfer to E. coli. Such preferential compatibility between OMVs and recipient cells facilitates the dissemination of blaKPC-2-harboring plasmids, and notably elevated the imipenem MIC of recipient strains from susceptible (2 μg mL⁻¹) to resistant levels (8–16 μg mL⁻¹). In addition, M-OMVs increased HGT efficiency in E. coli by 12.08-fold. This phenotype may be attributed to SOS response-induced vesiculation and enhanced plasmid packaging within OMVs. Compared with earlier reports focusing mainly on blaNDM-1 transmission (Zhao et al., 2023), our data suggest that OMV-facilitated HGT is a shared mechanism for Ambler class A (KPC) and class B (NDM, VIM) carbapenemase genes. HGT is well recognized as a primary driver of genetic exchange among microorganisms (Bedhomme et al., 2019), with transformation, transduction and conjugation traditionally regarded as the three major routes contributing to bacterial genetic diversity (Redondo-Salvo et al., 2020; Lerminiaux and Cameron, 2019). While previous studies have established that β-lactam resistance genes can be disseminated via K. pneumoniae-derived OMVs (Dell'Annunziata et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022), our findings provide a detailed characterization of blaKPC-2 horizontal transfer under meropenem stress, revealing a significantly higher efficiency in intra-species transmission compared to inter-species transfer. Of clinical relevance, sub-lethal carbapenem concentrations were found to further facilitate this dissemination process. This implies that clinical antibiotic treatment, particularly under suboptimal dosing regimens or in the context of deep-seated infections, may unintentionally expand the reservoir of resistant strains within the host microbiota.

These results highlight an underappreciated risk that conventional antibiotic therapy may inadvertently accelerate the dissemination of resistance genes. Multi-omics profiling further identified meropenem-altered proteins and lipids that may participate in OMVs DNA packaging and HGT efficiency. Changes in specific outer membrane proteins and lipid components may improve OMVs stability or fusion capacity with recipient bacteria, thereby promoting gene transfer.

Despite these insights from multi-omics analyses, several limitations remain to be addressed. Whether this pro-HGT effect is carbapenem-specific or represents a general class effect of β-lactams requires further verification using other agents such as ceftazidime/avibactam. In addition, the pivotal regulatory proteins and lipid molecules screened by multi-omics integration warrant further experimental validation in subsequent work.

In conclusion, this study establishes that meropenem stress reshapes the proteomic and lipidomic profiles of K. pneumoniae-derived OMVs, enhances bacterial biofilm formation and accelerates the horizontal spread of blaKPC-2 via OMVs. This work underscores the necessity of incorporating resistance ecological risks into rational antibiotic administration and stewardship strategies.
