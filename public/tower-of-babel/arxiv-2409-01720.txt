On ergodic property of the solution to a Lévy-driven SDE
Victoria Knopova, Yana Mokanu

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate ergodicity in total variation of the process X t X_{t} , related to a Lévy-driven stochastic differential equation with unbounded coefficients, and describe the speed of convergence to the respective invariant measure. Some examples are provided.

1.
Problem Statement

Let
Z
be a Lévy process on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
. Denote by
\mathcal{N}(du,ds)
its Poisson random measure, by
\nu(du)ds
its compensator, and consider a Lévy-driven SDE (in the integral form):

(1)

\begin{split}X(t)&=X(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\ell(X(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{u\neq 0}k(X(s-),u)\mathds{1}_{|k(X(s-),u)|>1}\mathcal{N}(du,ds)\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{u\neq 0}k(X(s-),u)\mathds{1}_{|k(X(s-),u)|\leq 1}\left(\mathcal{N}(du,ds)-\nu(du)ds)\right),\end{split}

where
\ell(\cdot):{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to{\mathds{R}^{d}}
,
k(\cdot,\cdot):{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to{\mathds{R}^{d}}
are measurable functions.

We assume that there exists (weak or strong) solution to (
1
), which is a strong Markov process; the respective conditions could be found in
[
1
]
or
[
2
]
.

It can be shown that for any function from the space
C^{2}_{0}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
of twice continuously differentiable functions with compact support, the process

(2)

M_{t}:=f(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}Lf(X_{s})ds,

where

(3)

\begin{split}Lf(x)&=\ell(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)\\
&\quad+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{0\}}\left(f(x+k(x,u))-f(x)-\nabla f(x)\cdot k(x,u)\mathds{1}_{|k(x,u)|\leq 1}\right)\nu(du)\end{split}

is a martingale (cf.
[
1
]
). The domain of
L
can be extended (see e.g. the proof of the Ito formula (cf.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
) to the space
C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
.
We call
(\mathcal{L},C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}}))
the extended generator of the semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
P_{t}f(x)=\mathds{E}^{x}f(X_{t})
.

In this paper we investigate ergodicity in total variation of the process
X_{t}
related to (
3
) and describe the speed of convergence to the invariant measure. The usual path for such investigation is the following:
if the transition probability kernel satisfies certain irreducibility assumption (e.g. the local Dobrushin condition) and if the extended generator
(\mathcal{L},C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}}))
satisfies the Lyapunov-type condition with some Lyapunov function, then one can reduce the continuous time setting to the discrete one (cf., e.g.,
[
4
]
) and apply ergodic theorems to the skeleton chain
X^{h}:=\{X_{nh},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
. Under irreducibility assumption, ergodicity of the skeleton chain implies the ergodicity of the initial Markov process with the same convergence rates.

The existence of an invariant measure and the speed of convergence to it are important, particularly in simulating stochastic processes. A prime example is their role in investigating the existence of a stationary state in a stochastic system and in simulating functionals related to the stationary distribution. Information about the speed of convergence to the stationary distribution allows us to evaluate how accurately the stationary solution is approximated by simulating the original stochastic differential equation.

This paper is in a sense a continuation of the investigation started in
[
5
]
, where the one-dimensional case was studied in the context of Lévy-type processes and the Lévy-type kernel was assumed to be bounded in
x
. In the multi-dimensional case the situation is much more complicated due to the geometry of the support of the Lévy measure
\nu(\cdot)
.

Loosely speaking, ergodicity of the solution to (
1
) can be induced by the drift as well as by the noise.
It is a kind of a “general knowledge” that in a pure diffusion setting one can get the Lyapunov condition for
L
due to the fast growth of the off-diagonal coefficients; in this case we have the noise-induced ergodicity. Such examples can be deduced from
[
6
]
, which is a classical reference on ergodic Markov processes. Motivated by such examples, we try to answer the question, which assumptions on the support of the Lévy-type kernel provide the Lyapunov-type condition for the Lévy-type operators given by (
3
). We are interested in the “noise-induced” ergodicity as well as in its interplay with the “drift-induced” one.
With some efforts, this result can be reformulated for the situation, when we have the varying intensity of the process rather than varying jump size.

Let us give a brief overview of the existing results. While for diffusion processes the literature is rather rich, for jump-diffusion and general Lévy-type processes the literature is not so extensive. The pioneering works devoted to the topic belong to Skorokhod; in
[
7
]
a long-time behaviour of general Markov processes and solutions to SDEs was discussed, as well as stabilization properties of solutions to systems of SDEs.
Ergodicity of a solution to the jump-diffusion SDE was studied in Wee
[
8
]
, where the conditions are given in the integral form. Masuda in
[
9
]
investigated a Lévy-driven Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, in particular, the existence of a smooth density, ergodicity and convergence rates; later in
[
10
]
a general case of jump-diffusion with compound Poisson jump measure was investigated. Exponential ergodicity of a solution to a Lévy-driven SDE was investigated in Kulik
[
11
]
, as well as the assumptions, which imply the Doeblin condition. See also Wang
[
12
]
for conditions for a 1-dimensional Lévy-driven SDE. In Fort, Roberts
[
13
]
and Douc, Fort, Guillin
[
14
]
sufficient conditions for ergodicity are given in the form of so-called
(f,r)
-modulated moments.
For a general Lévy-type process conditions ensuring ergodicity were studied by Sandrić in the series of papers
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
, in particular, in
[
17
]
, where the conditions are provided in a (very general) integral form.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

3

4

2.
Preliminaries

Recall that a Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
with a transition probability kernel
P_{t}(x,dy)
is called (strongly)

ergodic
if there exists an invariant probability measure
\pi(\cdot)
such that

(4)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{TV}=0

for any
x\in\mathds{R}^{d}
.
Here
\|\cdot\|_{TV}
is the total variation norm. Under additional assumptions one can find the speed of convergence to the invariant measure. We say, that the process is (non-uniformly)
\psi
-ergodic, if there exists a function
\psi\geq 0
,
\psi(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, such that

(5)

\|P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{TV}\leq C(x)\psi(t),\quad t\geq 1,

with some
C\colon{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to[0,\infty)
.

As we already mentioned in Section
1
, one of the possible ways to show the ergodic property relies on the following milestones: reduction of the initial Markov process to the skeleton chain, (local) Dobrushin condition and the Lyapunov-type condition for this chain, and finally the “return route” to the initial process.
Let us recall these conditions.

We say that a Markov chain
X
with a transition kernel
\mathds{P}(x,dy)
satisfies the local Dobrushin condition (cf.
[
4
]
) on a measurable set
B\subset{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{R}^{d}}
if

(6)

\sup_{x,y\in B}\|\mathds{P}(x,\cdot)-\mathds{P}(y,\cdot)\|_{TV}<2.

The sufficient condition for (
6
) is provided by the existence of an absolutely continuous component of the transition kernel
\mathds{P}(x,dy)
of the chain, such that
x\mapsto\mathds{P}(x,\cdot)
is continuous in
L_{1}
, see
[
4
]
. This condition is clearly satisfied if the initial Markov process has a strictly positive transition probability density
p_{t}(x,\cdot)
, such that
x\mapsto p_{t}(x,\cdot)
is continuous in
x
in
L_{1}
.
On the language of semigroups this condition provides that the respective semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
P_{t}f(x):=\mathds{E}^{x}f(X_{t})
, possesses the
C_{b}
-Feller property. Dobrushin condition ensures, that the processes with different starting points are attracted at infinity by the same measure; in other words, this condition ensures that two processes can be coupled at infinity.

The second condition we need is the
Lyapunov-type
inequality. This inequality allows to establish moment estimates, responsible for (non-uniform) ergodic rates of convergence. Let us recall this condition for the skeleton chain.

Suppose that there exist

•

a norm-like function
V:\,{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to[1,\infty)
(i.e.
V(x)\to\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
), bounded on a compact
K
;

•

a function
f:\,[1,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, that admits a non-negative increasing and concave extension to
[0,\infty)
;

•

a constant
C>0
,

such that the following relations hold:

(7)

\mathds{E}_{x}V(X_{1})-V(x)\leq-f(V(x))+C,\quad x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}},

(8)

f\left(1+\inf_{x\notin K}V(x)\right)>2C.

Inequality (
7
) is called the
Lyapunov-type inequality
, and the respective function
V
is called the
Lyapunov function
.

Since we start not with a Markov chain but with a Markov process, it is more convenient to check a condition, which ensures that the respective skeleton process possesses the Lyapunov-type condition. The required condition is formulated in terms of the extended generator
\mathcal{L}
(because Lyapunov functions are usually unbounded, we need to extend the domain of
L
to a bigger space):

(9)

\mathcal{L}V(x)\leq-f(V(x))+C,

where
V
and
f
have the same meaning as above.

To summarize, if we manage to prove (
9
), then by
[
4
]
inequality (
7
) holds true for the skeleton chain. Lyapunov and Dobrushin conditions allow to prove (cf.
[
4
]
) that the chain
X
is ergodic and to find the (non-uniform) ergodic rate. This theorem together with the Dobrushin condition ensures the ergodicity of the chain and yields the rate of convergence to the ergodic ditribution. Moreover, the same convergence rate will still hold for the initial Markov process, see the discussion in
[
4
]
, also
[
17
]
. The speed of convergence to
\pi
in (
5
) as
t\to\infty
is (cf.
[
4
]
)

(10)

\psi(t):=\left(\frac{1}{f(F^{-1}(\gamma t))}\right)^{\delta},\qquad F(t):=\int\limits_{1}^{t}\frac{dw}{f(w)},\quad t\in(1,\infty),

where
\gamma,\delta\in(0,1)
. Moreover,

\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f(V(x))\pi(dx)<\infty.

The approach described above is not the only possible one; for the discussion of other possible ways of showing ergodicity we refer to
[
4
]
.

3.
Settings and results

Our standing assumptions in this paper would be

(
A1)

There exists (weak or strong) a solution
\{X_{t},\,t>0\}
to (
1
)

(
A2)

The skeleton chain
X^{h}:=\{X_{nh},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
satisfies the Dobrushin condition.

Condition (
A2
) provides the required irreducibility of the skeleton process.

As usual, we denote by
|u|
the norm of a vector
u\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
.
For
\phi:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
,
\phi\in C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
, such that
\phi(x)=|x|
for
|x|>1
and
\phi(x)\leq|x|
for
|x|\leq 1
, define

(11)

V(x)=1+\phi^{p}(x),\quad p\in(0,1),\quad x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}.

This function
V(x)
is the Lyapunov function which we will use in order to show (
9
).

For
x\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
we denote by
e_{x}:=\frac{x}{\|x\|}
its unit vector, and by
\gamma_{x,u}
the cosine of the angle between the two unit vectors:

(12)

\gamma_{x,u}:=\cos\angle(e_{x},e_{u}).

Let

(13)

\mathcal{B}(r):=\{u:\,|k(x,u)|\leq r\},\quad\mathcal{A}(r,R):=\{u:\,r<|k(x,u)\leq R\}.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
. For
V(x)
as above split
\mathcal{L}V
into the “drift” and “kernel” components:

(14)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}V(x)&=\nabla V(x)\cdot\Big(\ell(x)+\int_{\mathcal{A}(1,|x|)}k(x,u)\nu(du)\Big)\\
&\quad+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left(V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)-\nabla V(x)\cdot k(x,u)\mathds{1}_{|k(x,u)|\leq|x|}\right)\nu(du)\\
&=:\mathcal{L}^{drift}V(x)+\mathcal{L}^{ker}V(x).\end{split}

It is easy to see that for
|x|
big enough we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{drift}V(x)&=p|x|^{p-1}\Big(|\ell(x)|\gamma_{x,\ell(x)}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(1,|x|)}|k(x,u)|\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}\,\nu(du)\Big).\end{split}

Denote

\phi^{drift}(x):=|x|^{p-1}\max{\Big(|\ell(x)|,\int_{\mathcal{A}(1,|x|)}|k(x,u)\,\nu(du)\Big)}.

Clearly, there exists
\zeta\in[-2,2]
, such that

(15)

\mathcal{L}^{drift}V(x)\leq-p\zeta\phi^{drift}(x).

Next, split the “kernel” part
\mathcal{L}^{ker}
into the “ball” and “tail” components:

(16)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{ker}V(x)&=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}\left(V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)-\nabla V(x)k(x,u)\right)\nu(du)\\
&\qquad+\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}^{c}(|x|^{\lambda})}\left(V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)-\nabla V(x)k(x,u)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}\right)\nu(du)\\
&=:\mathcal{L}^{ball}V(x)+\mathcal{L}^{tail}V(x).\end{split}

Finally, split the “tail” part

(17)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{tail}V(x)&=\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}\left(V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)-\nabla V(x)\cdot k(x,u)\right)\nu(du)\\
&\qquad+\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}^{c}(|x|)}\left(V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)\right)\nu(du)\\
&=:\mathcal{L}^{big}V(x)+\mathcal{L}^{large}V(x).\end{split}

To compare the impact of each component on
\mathcal{L}
, consider the respective “growth” functions:

(18)

\phi^{ball}_{+}(x):=|x|^{p-2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}|k(x,u)|^{2}\,\nu(du)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{ball}_{-}(x):=|x|^{p-2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}|k(x,u)|^{2}\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}^{2}\,\nu(du),

(19)

\phi^{big}(x):=\frac{p}{2}|x|^{p-1}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}|k(x,u)|\,\nu(du),

(20)

\phi^{large}(x):=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}^{c}(|x|)}|k(x,u)|^{p}\,\nu(du).

Lemma 1
.

We have

(21)

\mathcal{L}V(x)\leq-p\zeta\phi^{drift}(x)+\frac{p}{4}(1+o(1))(\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)+(p-2)\phi^{ball}_{-}(x))+\phi^{big}(x)+\phi^{large}(x),

where
|x|\to\infty,\,\zeta\in[-2,2]
.

To illustrate relations between different components we define several constants. Let

(22)

C^{ball}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)}{\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)}

If
C^{ball}<\infty
, then the measure
\nu(\cdot)
is “thick” in the direction of
k(x,u)
or in the opposite one, meaning that
\phi^{ball}_{-}
is faster than
\phi^{ball}_{+}
, which is crucial for “ball”-induced ergodicity. Note that by definition
C^{ball}\geq 1
.

Next,

C^{big}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{big}(x)}{\phi^{drift}(x)},\quad C^{large}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{large}(x)}{\phi^{drift}(x)}.

Finite
C^{big}\vee C^{large}
assures that the “drift” is faster, than the “tail”. Depending on the relation between “drift” and “ball” parts, we can expect here either “drift”– or “ball”–induced ergodicity.

Similarly, we define

\tilde{C}^{drift}_{+}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)}{\phi^{drift}(x)},\quad\tilde{C}^{drift}_{-}:=\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)}{\phi^{drift}(x)}.

Finally, depending whether
\zeta
is positive or negative we define respectively

\tilde{C}^{ball}_{+}:=\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{drift}(x)}{\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)},\quad\tilde{C}^{ball}_{-}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{drift}(x)}{\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)}.

In order to make our results easily applicable, we introduce some assumptions on lower bounds of
\phi^{drift}
and
\phi^{ball}
. Namely, suppose that there exist positive constants
K^{drift},K^{ball}
and
\gamma^{drift},\gamma^{ball}
such that for
|x|\gg 1

(23)

\phi^{drift}(x)\geq K^{drift}|x|^{\gamma^{drift}},

(24)

\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)\geq K^{ball}|x|^{\gamma^{ball}}.

We introduce the conditions in such a way that one can distinguish between the “drift-induced” and “noise-induced” ergodicity, as well as determine the part of the kernel, responsible for it.

Theorem 1
.

Case 1. (“Drift-induced” ergodicity). Suppose that (
15
) holds with
\zeta\in(0,2]
, and
C^{big}\vee C^{large}
,
C^{drift}_{+}\vee C^{drift}_{-}
are finite. Then (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Ku^{\gamma^{drift}/p},K>0
, if

(25)

-p\zeta+\frac{p}{4}\left(\tilde{C}^{drift}_{+}+(p-2)\tilde{C}^{drift}_{-}\right)+C^{big}+C^{large}<0.

Case 2. (“Ball-induced” ergodicity). Let either
C^{big}\vee C^{large}=\infty
or
C^{big}\vee C^{large}<\infty
, but

p<C^{big}+C^{large}
. Denote

\tilde{C}^{ball}:=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\max{(\phi^{big}(x),\,\phi^{large}(x))}}{\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)}.

Then (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Ku^{\gamma^{ball}/p},K\!>\!0
, if
C^{ball},\tilde{C}^{ball}<\infty
, and one of the following inequalities is satisfied:

a)

(26)

-p\zeta\tilde{C}^{ball}_{+}+\frac{p}{4}(C^{ball}+p-2)+2\tilde{C}^{ball}<0,\qquad\zeta>0;

b)

(27)

-p\zeta\tilde{C}^{ball}_{-}+\frac{p}{4}(C^{ball}+p-2)+2\tilde{C}^{ball}<0,\qquad\zeta\leq 0.

4.
Proofs

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We begin the proof with the behaviour of the “ball” part.
Note that for
\theta\in[0,1]
and
|k(x,u)|\leq|x|^{\lambda}
we get by the triangle inequality

(28)

|x|\cdot|1-|x|^{\lambda-1}|\leq|x+\theta k(x,u)|\leq|x|(1+|x|^{\lambda-1}).

Then, since in
\mathcal{L}^{ball}
we have
|k(x,u)|\leq|x|^{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, for
|x|
big enough we have

V(x+k(x,u))=1+|x+k(x,u)|^{p}.

Therefore, by Taylor’s formula,

V(x+k(x,u))-V(x)-\nabla V(x)\cdot k(x,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)k^{\prime}(x,u)\cdot D^{2}V(x+\theta k(x,u))\cdot k(x,u)\,d\theta,

where

D^{2}V(x)=\left\{\partial_{ij}^{2}V(x)\right\}_{i,j=1}^{d}=p|x|^{p-2}\begin{pmatrix}1+(p-2)\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{|x|^{2}}&(p-2)\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{|x|^{2}}&\cdots&(p-2)\frac{x_{1}x_{d}}{|x|^{2}}\\
(p-2)\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{|x|^{2}}&1+(p-2)\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{|x|^{2}}&\cdots&(p-2)\frac{x_{2}x_{d}}{|x|^{2}}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\ddots&\cdots\\
(p-2)\frac{x_{1}x_{d}}{|x|^{2}}&(p-2)\frac{x_{2}x_{d}}{|x|^{2}}&\cdots&1+(p-2)\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{ball}V(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}\left[\int\limits_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)k^{\prime}(x,u)\cdot D^{2}V\left(x+\theta k(x,u)\right)\cdot k(x,u)\,d\theta\right]\,\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}\int\limits_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{p-2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\left(1+(p-2)\frac{(x_{i}+\theta k_{i}(x,u))^{2}}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{2}}\right)k_{i}^{2}(x,u)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+(p-2)\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{d}{k_{i}(x,u)k_{j}(x,u)\frac{\left(x_{i}+\theta k_{i}(x,u)\right)\left(x_{j}+\theta k_{j}(x,u)\right)}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{2}}}\right)d\theta\nu(du).

Now using the sum of squares formula for
k_{i}(x,u)(x_{i}+\theta k_{i}(x,u))
, we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{ball}V(\!x\!)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\!\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}\!\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{1-\theta}{|x+\theta k(x,\!u)|^{2-p}}{\left(|k(x,u)|^{2}+(p-2)\!\left(\!k(x,u)\frac{x+\theta k(x,u)}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|}\!\right)^{2}\right)}\!d\theta\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}\left[\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-\theta)|k(x,u)|^{2}}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{2-p}}\left(1+(p-2)\left(e_{k(x,u)}\cdot e_{x+\theta k(x,u)}\right)^{2}\right)d\theta\right]\nu(du).

Using (
28
) we get

(29)

\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{1-\theta}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{2-p}}\,d\theta\leq\frac{|x|^{p-2}}{2\left(1-|x|^{\lambda-1}\right)^{2-p}}

and

(30)

(p-2)\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{1-\theta}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|^{2-p}}\,d\theta\leq\frac{p-2}{2}\frac{|x|^{p-2}}{\left(1+|x|^{\lambda-1}\right)^{2-p}}.

Finally, transforming the scalar product of the vectors
e_{k(x,u)}
,
e_{x+\theta k(x,u)}
and applying the estimate (
28
), we get

(31)

\begin{split}|e_{k(x,u)}\cdot e_{x+\theta k(x,u)}|^{2}&=\left|e_{k(x,u)}\cdot e_{x}\frac{|x|}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|}+\frac{\theta|k(x,u)|}{|x+\theta k(x,u)|}\right|^{2}\\
&\geq\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}^{2}\left(\frac{|x|}{|x|+|k(x,u)|}\right)^{2}-\frac{2\theta|x|\cdot|k(x,u)|}{(|x|-|k(x,u)|)^{2}}\\
&\geq\frac{\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}^{2}}{(1+|x|^{\lambda-1})^{2}}-\frac{2|x|^{\lambda}}{|x|(1-|x|^{\lambda-1})^{2}}.\end{split}

Combining (
29
), (
30
) and (
31
), we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{ball}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{4}|x|^{p-2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}(|x|^{\lambda})}|k(x,u)|^{2}\left(C_{1}(x)+(p-2)C_{2}(x)\gamma^{2}_{x,k(x,u)}\right)\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{4}C_{1}(x)\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)+\frac{p(p-2)}{4}C_{2}(x)\phi^{ball}_{-}(x),

where

(32)

C_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\left(1-|x|^{\lambda-1}\right)^{2-p}}+\frac{2(2-p)|x|^{\lambda-1}}{(1+|x|^{\lambda-1})^{2-p}(1-|x|^{\lambda-1})^{2}},\quad C_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{\left(1+|x|^{\lambda-1}\right)^{4-p}}.

Note that both
C_{1}(x)
and
C_{2}(x)
tend to 1 as
|x|\to\infty
, hence

(33)

\mathcal{L}^{ball}V(x)\leq\frac{p}{4}(1+o(1))(\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)+(p-2)\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)).

Consider now the “tail”. For the big jumps

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{big}V(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}(|x+k(x,u)|^{p}-|x|^{p}-p|x|^{p-1}\cdot|k(x,u)|\gamma_{x,k(x,u)})\,\nu(du)

\displaystyle=|x|^{p}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}\Big(\Big(1+2\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}\frac{|k(x,u)|}{|x|}+\Big(\frac{|k(x,u)|}{|x|}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}}-1\Big)\,\nu(du)

\displaystyle-p|x|^{p-1}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}|k(x,u)|\gamma_{x,k(x,u)}\,\nu(du)=:J_{1}(x)+J_{2}(x).

Since on
\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)
we have
|k(x,u)|\leq|x|
, then

\begin{split}J_{1}(x)&\leq|x|^{p}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}\Big(\Big(1+(1+2\gamma_{x,k(x,u)})\frac{|k(x,u)|}{|x|}\Big)^{\frac{p}{2}}-1\Big)\nu(du)\\
&\leq\frac{p}{2}|x|^{p-1}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}(1+2\gamma_{x,k(x,u)})|k(x,u)|\,\nu(du).\end{split}

Combined with
J_{2}
, the above estimate yields

(34)

\mathcal{L}^{big}V(x)\leq\frac{p}{2}|x|^{p-1}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\lambda},|x|)}|k(x,u)|\nu(du)=\phi^{big}(x).

For the large jumps we simply have

(35)

\mathcal{L}^{large}V(x)\leq\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}^{c}(|x|)}|k(x,u)|^{p}\nu(du)=\phi^{large}(x).

Combining (
15
), (
33
), (
34
) and (
35
), we derive the statement of the lemma:

\mathcal{L}V(x)\leq-p\zeta\phi^{drift}(x)+\frac{p}{4}(1+o(1))(\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)+(p-2)\phi^{ball}_{-}(x))+\phi^{big}(x)+\phi^{large}(x),\,|x|\to\infty.\qed

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Case 1
. Consider first the “drift-induced” ergodicity. If
\zeta\in(0,2]
, the drift is negative. Divide both sides of (
21
) by
\phi^{drift}(x)
and take
\limsup
as
|x|\to\infty
:

(36)

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}V(x)}{\phi^{drift}(x)}\leq-p\zeta+\frac{p}{4}\left(\tilde{C}^{drift}_{+}+(p-2)\tilde{C}^{drift}_{-}\right)+C^{big}+C^{large}.

Since both
C^{big}
and
C^{large}
are positive, we need them to be finite to ensure that the drift is faster than the tail. Then the Lyapunov-type condition (
9
) is satisfied, if the right-hand side of (
36
) is negative.

Case 2
. The “ball induced” ergodicity may appear, if either the tail is faster than the drift (
C^{big}\vee C^{large}=\infty
) or the drift (if negative) is fast enough, but still it cannot compensate the positivity of the tail, i.e.
C^{big}\vee C^{large}<\infty
, but
p<C^{big}+C^{large}
. In this scenario in order to have (
9
), the ball part has to be negative and fast enough, i.e
C^{ball}<2-p
and
\tilde{C}^{ball}<\infty
. Dividing both sides of (
21
) by
\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)
, taking
\limsup
as
|x|\to\infty
, we see that the sufficient condition for (
9
) is either (
26
), if
\zeta>0
, or (
27
), if
\zeta<0
.

In both cases the choice of
f
in (
9
) follows from (
23
) and (
24
). ∎

5.
Examples

Example 1
.

d=1
. Consider a one-sided
\alpha
-stable Lévy measure
\nu(du)=u^{-1-\alpha}\mathds{1}_{u>0}du
(we assume for simplicity that the normalizing constant equals 1). Take
k(x,u)=ub(x)
, where
b(\cdot)
is Lipschitz and
b(x)=|x|^{\beta}\operatorname{sign}(-x)(1+o(1))
as
x\to\pm\infty
with
\beta\in(0,1)
;
\ell(x)=|x|^{\eta}
,
\eta\in(0,\alpha\beta)
. Condition (
A1
) is clearly satisfied. In order to see that (
A2
) holds true as well, one can use the fact that the process
X
possesses a strictly positive transition probability density, see, for example,
[
18
]
.

To make calculations a bit more transparent let us introduce an auxiliary function

(37)

\phi^{ann}_{\tau}(x):=|x|^{p-1}\int\limits_{\mathcal{A}(|x|^{\tau},|x|)}|k(x,u)|\nu(du),\quad\tau\in\{0,\lambda\}.

Depending on the value of
\alpha
we have

\phi^{ann}_{\tau}(x)\asymp\begin{cases}(\alpha-1)^{-1}|x|^{p-1+\alpha\beta+\tau(1-\alpha)},&\alpha>1,\\
(1-\alpha)^{-1}|x|^{p-\alpha+\alpha\beta},&\alpha<1,\\
(1-\tau)|x|^{p-1+\beta}\ln|x|,&\alpha=1.\end{cases}

Then

\phi^{drift}(x)=\phi^{ann}_{0}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{big}(x)=\frac{p}{2}\phi^{ann}_{\lambda}(x).

Next

\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)=\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)=\frac{1}{2-\alpha}|x|^{p-2+\alpha\beta+\lambda(2-\alpha)}(1+o(1)),

and

\phi^{large}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha-p}|x|^{p-\alpha+\alpha\beta}(1+o(1)).

Note that
\tilde{C}^{drift}_{\pm}=0
for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
, which means that the “ball” is slower than the “drift”, and that we are in the settings of the case of the “drift-induced” ergodicity, but the “kernel part” of the drift dominates due to the assumption
\eta<\alpha\beta
. Moreover
\zeta=1
for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
.

If
\alpha>1
,
C^{big}=C^{large}=0
. Then (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-1+\alpha\beta}{p}}
,
C\leq p
.

If
\alpha<1
,
C^{big}=\frac{p}{2}
,
C^{large}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha-p}
. Then (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-\alpha+\alpha\beta}{p}}
,
C\leq\frac{p}{2}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha-p}
, provided
p^{2}-p\alpha+2(1-\alpha)<0
.

If
\alpha=1
,
C^{big}=\frac{p}{2}(1-\lambda)
,
C^{large}=0
. Then (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-1+\beta}{p}}\ln{u}
,
C\leq\frac{p}{2}(1+\lambda)
.

It is a known result (see, for example,
[
19
]
), that in this case the limit probability measure is trivial.

Example 2
.

Let
d=1
. Consider a kernel, such that
\nu\{\pm K\}\!=\!1
for some
K>0
. Take again
k(x,u)=|x|^{\beta}u
,
\beta\in(0,\lambda)
, and
\ell(x)
such that
|\ell(x)|=|x|^{\eta},\eta>1-p
and
x\ell(x)<0
. In this case,

\phi^{ball}_{+}(x)=\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)=2K^{2}|x|^{2\beta}|x|^{p-2}

and

\phi^{drift}(x)=-|x|^{p-2}|x\ell(x)|=-|x|^{p-1+\eta}.

Moreover,
C^{ball}=1
, and for
|x|
big enough
\phi^{big}(x)
,
\phi^{large}(x)
tend to zero. Therefore,

(38)

\mathcal{L}V(x)\leq-p|x|^{p-1}\left(|x|^{\eta}+\frac{(1-p)K^{2}}{2}|x|^{2\beta-1}\right).

Then for
\eta\leq 2\beta-1
the Lyapunov inequality (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-2+2\beta}{p}}
,
C\leq\frac{K^{2}}{2}(1-p)
. Otherwise (
9
) holds with
f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-1+\eta}{p}}
,
C\leq p
.

The limit probability measure is again trivial.

Example 3
.

Consider now a multidimensional case, but with zero drift part
\ell
. Take a multiplicative
k(x,u)=\Phi(x)u
, where
\Phi\colon{\mathds{R}^{d}}\mapsto{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{R}^{d}}
is a matrix, whose elements satisfy the Lipschitz and linear growth conditions from
[
1
]
. We assume that
\Phi(x)=|x|^{\beta}U(x)
, where
U(x)
is a unitary matrix and
\beta>1-p/2
. Assume also that
\nu(\cdot)
is symmetric and concentrated entirely on the sphere in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
centered at
0
and of radius
R
. Then the tail part
\mathcal{L}^{tail}
is simply absent, and the annulus part turns into zero due to the symmetry of
\nu(\cdot)
.

Since matrix
U
is unitary, we have
|\Phi(x)u|=|x|^{\beta}|u|
, and thus

\displaystyle\phi^{ball}_{-}(x)

\displaystyle=|x|^{p-2}|x|^{2\beta}\int\limits_{|u|=R}|u|^{2}\gamma_{\Phi(x)u,x}^{2}\,\nu(du)

\displaystyle=|x|^{p-2}|x|^{2\beta}R^{2}\int\limits_{|u|=R}\gamma_{\Phi(x)u,x}^{2}\,\nu(du).

Therefore,

C^{ball}=\frac{\nu(u:|u|=R)}{\int\limits_{|u|=R}\gamma_{\Phi(x)u,x}^{2}\,\nu(du)}.

If we chose
p<\min(2-C^{ball},1)
, we can achieve that (
9
) holds true with

f(u)=Cu^{\frac{p-2+2\beta}{p}},\quad\text{where}\quad C\leq\frac{p}{4}R^{2}(2-p-C^{ball}).
