Missing-data–aware machine learning prediction of in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events after primary percutaneous coronary intervention for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Seyedeh-Tarlan Mirzohreh, Neda Roshanravan, Tahir Suleymanov, Dmitriy M Makarov, Arian Zargarzadeh, Elnaz Javanshir, Ahmad Separham, Alireza Ghaffari, Parsa Mostaghimi Motlagh, Samad Ghaffari, Abolghasem Jouyban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning (ML) offers opportunities to improve prognostication after ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), but real-world registries frequently contain incomplete data, and inappropriate handling of missingness may degrade performance. We retrospectively evaluated 659 consecutive STEMI patients undergoing PCI during the index admission. The primary outcome was in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Eighty clinical, electrocardiographic, laboratory, echocardiographic, and angiographic variables were analyzed, with up to 40% missingness in some predictors. A hybrid feature-selection approach incorporating CatBoost feature importance, SHAP values, variance filtering, and correlation screening identified the most informative predictors. Models were trained using stratified 5-fold cross-validation, comparing native missing-value handling in CatBoost with transformer-based imputation (TabImpute) and a pretrained tabular model (TabPFN). MACE occurred in 282 patients (42.8%). CatBoost trained directly on incompletely observed data achieved the best performance using approximately 8–10 predictors (AUC 0.73), with balanced accuracy 0.69, precision 0.68, and recall 0.53. Transformer-based imputation did not improve discrimination. SHAP analysis indicated that impaired pre-PCI TIMI flow, reduced LVEF, elevated troponin, greater ST-segment deviation, inflammation, and renal dysfunction were the strongest contributors. A class-weighted CatBoost model for in-hospital mortality achieved excellent accuracy (AUC = 0.93). Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is one of the most common and life-threatening cardiovascular diseases, characterized by rapid progression and high mortality1. It is usually caused by atherosclerotic plaque rupture with subsequent platelet aggregation and thrombosis, leading to prolonged myocardial ischemia and necrosis. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the standard treatment for AMI because it rapidly restores coronary perfusion and relieves ischemic symptoms1. However, empirical studies indicate that, notwithstanding thesignificant benefits conferred by PCI, patients remain susceptible to the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which encompass recurrent AMI and heart failure2.

The incidence of MACE not only diminishes the quality of life for patients but also exacerbates their economic and social burdens, while significantly affecting their long-term prognosis and overall well-being3. Despite advancements in the assessment of MACE risk among patients with AMI, existing methodological constraints hinder a thorough comprehension of the intricate mechanisms and the robustness of associations between clinical data, thereby restricting the clinical applicability of these findings4.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as an important tool in the analysis of clinical data, attracting growing interest from clinicians and researchers because of its ability to model complex relationships and support clinical decision-making. By integrating large volumes of clinical, laboratory, and imaging information, machine learning techniques may uncover patterns and risk factors that are less apparent using traditional statistical approaches, thereby contributing to more precise risk stratification and individualized patient management5. Prior studies suggest that these methods are particularly well suited to cardiovascular medicine, where data are often high-dimensional, and outcomes are influenced by multiple interacting factors6. When appropriately developed and validated, ML–based models may help clinicians estimate complication risk more accurately and tailor treatment strategies to patient-specific profiles, with the potential to improve both short- and long-term outcomes7,8.

Despite the promise of ML for improving risk prediction in cardiovascular disease, its practical application is challenged by the pervasive presence of missing data in real-world clinical datasets9. Incompleteness can arise from variations in clinical practice, inconsistent documentation, or patients presenting with unstable conditions, and the conventional practice of excluding incomplete cases can reduce sample size and introduce bias. Evidence from large imaging-based registries shows that missing data consistently reduce the discriminative performance of ML models compared with fully observed datasets, and different handling strategies vary substantially in their impact on model accuracy10. Moreover, prediction model studies often fail to report or appropriately address missing data, highlighting a methodological gap and a potential source of bias in clinical ML research11. These issues underscore the need for deliberate, transparent missing-data strategies when developing ML–based prediction models for real-world healthcare data.

Building on these limitations in prior work, the present study aimed to develop and evaluate a missing-data–aware ML framework for predicting in-hospital MACE in patients with STEMI undergoing PCI during the index admission, using routinely collected real-world registry data. The main contributions of this study were the retention of partially observed but clinically relevant variables, comparison of native missing-value handling with transformer-based imputation and a pretrained tabular model, application of a hybrid feature-selection strategy, and use of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to support clinical interpretability.

Materials and methods

Data source and participants

We retrospectively screened 897 consecutive patients with STEMI admitted to Shahid Madani Heart Center, Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, between April 10, 2019 and September 25, 2021. STEMI was defined according to contemporary international guidelines12 as persistent ST-segment elevation ≥ 1 mm in at least two contiguous limb leads or ≥ 2 mm in two contiguous precordial leads, or new left bundle branch block, in the presence of ischemic symptoms and elevated cardiac troponin-I. Clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic, and angiographic data were prospectively entered into the institutional STEMI registry, including demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, admission ECG findings, biomarker levels, lipid indices, procedural characteristics, and in-hospital outcomes, including MACE (Table 1).

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were ≥ 18 years of age, had STEMI, and underwent PCI during the index admission as part of the reperfusion strategy. The PCI cohort included patients treated with primary PCI as well as patients who received thrombolytic therapy before subsequent PCI, including rescue or pharmaco-invasive PCI when clinically indicated. Patients treated with thrombolysis alone without subsequent PCI, those not undergoing coronary angiography, patients younger than 18 years, and cases with missing essential eligibility or outcome-defining data were excluded. Essential data were defined as age, confirmed STEMI diagnosis, PCI status, in-hospital MACE status, and vital status at discharge. Patients were not excluded solely because of missing values in candidate predictor variables; these missing values were retained and handled within the missing-data–aware modeling framework. The final cohort consisted of 659 adult STEMI patients who underwent PCI during the study period. (Fig. 1)

Definition of in-hospital MACE

Given the hemodynamic instability frequently observed in acute STEMI, this study focused on in-hospital MACE, particularly those occurring within the first 7 days after PCI. Patients were monitored continuously throughout hospitalization, with special attention to the early post-procedural period. In-hospital MACE was defined as the occurrence of any of the following complications during the index admission: all-cause mortality, new or recurrent myocardial infarction after PCI, urgent repeat coronary revascularization including PCI or CABG (excluding planned elective procedures), stroke, cardiogenic shock, severe heart failure requiring escalation of therapy, malignant ventricular arrhythmias such as sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, cardiac arrest, or clinically significant hemorrhage. The presence of any single component event was considered a MACE outcome.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis for baseline information

Continuous variable information was described using mean ± standard deviation (± SD), and categorical information was expressed as frequency and percentage n (%). For continuous variable information that conforms to normal distribution, the independent samples t-test method was used to test the difference; those that did not conform to normal distribution were statistically analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and discrete categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test to compare the difference. Because multiple variables were compared, p-values were interpreted as unadjusted nominal values for exploratory purposes only and were not used for confirmatory inference, feature selection, or claims of independent association. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered nominally significant.

Machine learning algorithms

Among the evaluated algorithms, including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost, the CatBoost model demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy (see the S1 Document in the Supplementary Information). The dataset was divided into training and validation subsets in a 7:3 ratio. All models were trained on the training subset, and their performance was evaluated on the validation subset. Consequently, this study concentrates on two main ML approaches: gradient boosting machine (GBM) and neural network–based techniques, with particular attention to CatBoost13. This model was selected because of its strong performance and its inherent capacity to manage missing data without the need for explicit imputation, which helps reduce the potential for bias or alteration of the underlying data structure.

In addition, a modern neural network-based method, TabPFN (Tabular Prior-Data Fitted Networks)14, was used to address the classification task. TabPFN is a pretrained transformer-based model that approximates a Bayesian predictor for tabular data and achieves high predictive accuracy without requiring extensive task-specific training. The model was applied in a small-data regime, producing final predictions via an ensemble of seven independent prediction heads, thereby improving robustness and uncertainty handling.

Model evaluation and validation procedure

To assess the generalization performance of the classification models, stratified k-fold cross-validation was employed with k = 5. Stratification ensured that the original class distribution was preserved in each fold, which is particularly important in the presence of class imbalance. At each cross-validation iteration, the model was trained on four folds and evaluated on the remaining fold. Final performance metrics were computed as the mean values across all five folds.

Classification performance was evaluated using a set of complementary metrics derived from the confusion matrix, including the number of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) predictions.

A dedicated CatBoost classification model was developed to predict all-cause mortality. Mortality prediction represents a highly imbalanced classification problem, as deaths are far fewer than survival events. To mitigate bias toward the majority class, class weighting was applied during model training. Model performance was assessed using stratified 5-fold cross-validation to maintain the outcome class distribution within each fold.

For each fold, class weights were computed only from the training subset to prevent information leakage. A balanced weighting scheme was used, assigning weights inversely proportional to class frequency. This increased the penalty for misclassifying minority-class cases while maintaining numerical stability. The weights were implemented in CatBoost via the class-weights parameter, modifying the loss function so that mortality misclassifications contributed more strongly to the total error and improving sensitivity to rare but clinically important outcomes.

No resampling methods (over-sampling or under-sampling) were applied in order to preserve the original data distribution and avoid biases from synthetic data generation or sample removal. Instead, class-weighted loss optimization allowed full-dataset training while explicitly addressing outcome imbalance.

Feature selection methodology

The dataset consisted of 80 variables, including demographic characteristics, clinical history, laboratory parameters, and procedural data. Missing values were present across multiple features.

No explicit imputation was applied for models capable of handling missing values natively. For models requiring complete input data, missing values were imputed using a transformer-based tabular imputation approach15, which leverages contextual relationships between variables to estimate missing entries. The main advantage of TabImpute is its zero-shot imputation capability, allowing applications to new datasets without additional training, hyperparameter tuning, or iterative fitting, while preserving complex feature dependencies learned during pretraining.

To identify the most informative predictors while maintaining robustness to missing data and nonlinear feature–outcome relationships, we applied a hybrid feature selection strategy that integrates model-based, explanation-based, and statistical criteria. This approach combines CatBoost feature importance, SHAP values, variance filtering, and correlation analysis into a single aggregated feature ranking score.

CatBoost feature importance served as the primary model-based criterion. The model was trained within each fold of the cross-validation procedure using default hyperparameters, and feature importance scores were computed separately for each training fold using CatBoost’s internal metric. This metric reflects each feature’s contribution to predictive performance and is defined as the mean reduction in the loss function when that feature is used for splitting across all trees in the ensemble. To complement the model-based importance, an explanation-based criterion was obtained using SHAP values16, computed via CatBoost’s built-in SHAP implementation. SHAP values quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to individual predictions based on principles from cooperative game theory. Feature importance was defined as the mean absolute SHAP value across all samples, thereby capturing both the magnitude and consistency of each feature’s influence on the model output. Bias terms were excluded, and for classification tasks, SHAP values corresponding to the positive class were used when applicable.

In addition to model-derived measures, two statistical criteria were used to provide complementary, model-independent assessments of feature relevance. Higher-variance features were prioritized, assuming low-variance variables offer limited discriminatory power. Variance was computed after temporary median imputation to address missing data. The second criterion was correlation with the target variable, quantified as the absolute Pearson correlation coefficient to capture linear associations as a complementary signal alongside nonlinear model-based criteria, rather than as a primary selection mechanism.

To ensure comparability across the four heterogeneous importance measures (CatBoost importance, SHAP importance, variance, and correlation), each metric was normalized to the range [0,1] using min–max scaling.

Features were ranked in descending order according to the composite importance score, and subsets of the top-ranked predictors were evaluated across a range of sizes. The optimal number of features was determined based on cross-validated model performance, with the best results observed for subsets containing approximately 8–10 predictors for the MACE model and 40 predictors for the Mortality prediction model. These selected features were subsequently used as input for downstream modeling, including CatBoost and TabPFN classifiers.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 659 patients with STEMI undergoing PCI during the index admission were included in the analysis, comprising 377 patients without in-hospital MACE and 282 patients (42.8%) who developed at least one in-hospital MACE. All-cause in-hospital mortality occurred in 30 patients (4.6% of the total cohort), indicating that mortality accounted for only a minority of the composite MACE endpoint. The remaining MACE burden was therefore attributable to nonfatal in-hospital complications included in the composite endpoint, such as recurrent myocardial infarction, urgent repeat revascularization, stroke, cardiogenic shock, severe heart failure requiring escalation of therapy, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, or clinically significant hemorrhage. However, component-level frequencies for these nonfatal events were not available for separate analysis; therefore, the composite MACE outcome was analyzed as a single endpoint. The mean age of the cohort was 62 ± 15 years, and 19.7% were female, with no significant difference in sex distribution between groups. Patients who experienced MACE were older than those without MACE (64 ± 15 vs. 61 ± 16 years; p = 0.005), whereas BMI was similar between groups. Cardiovascular comorbidities, including smoking history, hypertension, prior MI, and prior CABG, were more frequent among patients with MACE.

At presentation, cardiac arrest (6.4% vs. 1.3%; p < 0.001) and cardiogenic shock were more common in the MACE group. The magnitude of ST-segment elevation was also higher in patients with MACE (11.5 ± 10.0 vs. 9.4 ± 7.3 mV; p = 0.010). Culprit LAD lesions and impaired pre-PCI TIMI flow occurred more frequently in the MACE group, although final TIMI 3 remained high in both groups following PCI.

Echocardiographic parameters differed between groups, with lower LVEF in patients who developed MACE (35 ± 8 vs. 40 ± 12%; p < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of significant mitral regurgitation (p = 0.010). Given that severe heart failure requiring escalation of therapy was included in the composite MACE endpoint, the LVEF difference should be interpreted in light of this partial clinical overlap.

Several admission laboratory markers also differed significantly. Compared with the non-MACE group, patients with MACE had higher cardiac troponin-I (cTnI), creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), blood glucose (BS), white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune inflammation index (SII), along with lower hemoglobin and lymphocyte counts (all p < 0.05). Lipid indices were generally comparable between groups.

Overall, patients who developed MACE tended to present with greater ischemic burden, reduced left-ventricular function, heightened systemic inflammation, and renal dysfunction at baseline.

Machine learning workflow for MACE risk prediction. This figure summarizes the workflow used to develop machine learning models for predicting in-hospital MACE in STEMI patients. Registry data containing 80 clinical, ECG, laboratory, echocardiographic, and angiographic variables were used as input, with in-hospital MACE as the primary outcome. The dataset included substantial missingness and multicollinearity. After preprocessing, a missing-data–aware modeling strategy was applied, comparing CatBoost with native missing-value handling, CatBoost with TabImpute transformer-based imputation, and the TabPFN pretrained classifier. A hybrid feature selection process combined model-based and statistical criteria to rank variables and select the top 8–10 predictors. Models were trained with stratified 5-fold cross-validation and evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. CatBoost without prior imputation showed the best overall performance (AUC = 0.73). Model explainability was achieved using SHAP-based feature importance.

Of the 80 analyzed features, 31 (38.8%) contained missing values, with the maximum proportion of missing data reaching 39.6% for the parameter Sigma STE at 90 min. Variables with the highest levels of missingness were predominantly angiographic in nature, including culprit lesion (37.8%), TIMI flow before PCI (37.8%), TIMI flow after PCI (37.8%), and vessel disease category (single vs. multivessel) (37.2%).

Correlation analysis between individual parameters and the MACE endpoint identified a subset of 14 features exhibiting statistically significant but moderate associations (0.1≤∣r∣<0.3, p < 0.05). No variable reached the threshold for a strong correlation (∣r∣≥0.3), indicating a multifactorial etiology of MACE, where individual predictors contribute incrementally to the overall risk. The strongest inverse correlations with MACE were observed for the angiographic parameter TIMI flow before PCI (r = −0.22, p < 0.001) and LVEF (r = −0.21, p < 0.001). Among laboratory variables, the most pronounced positive correlations with MACE were observed for markers related to systemic inflammation and renal function, including BUN (r = 0.18, p < 0.001), NLR (r = 0.16, p < 0.001), SII (r = 0.16, p = 0.0003), and WBC count (r = 0.13, p = 0.0008).Particular attention was paid to features that demonstrated both statistically significant correlations with MACE and a high proportion of missing values. Despite missingness rates of 37.8%, the angiographic parameters culprit lesion (r = −0.14, p = 0.004) and TIMI flow after PCI (r = −0.14, p = 0.005) retained significant associations with the outcome. Similarly, Sigma STE at baseline (r = 0.14, p = 0.004), reflecting the cumulative ST-segment deviation on the baseline electrocardiogram, showed a moderate positive correlation with MACE despite 30.8% missing data. This parameter may serve as a quantitative marker of ischemic myocardial injury and warrants further investigation in a prognostic modeling context.

Substantial heterogeneity in data availability was observed across different categories of clinical variables. Core demographic and laboratory parameters, such as age, sex, and standard biochemical measurements, were nearly complete, whereas specialized angiographic and selected instrumental variables were available only for a limited subset of patients.

Multicollinearity analysis revealed an apparent paradox within the feature set. Although no individual pair of features exhibited extremely high pairwise correlation (no correlation coefficient exceeding 0.9), the condition number of the feature matrix reached a critically high value of 1.7 × 1026. This phenomenon is likely attributable to the cumulative effect of multiple moderate correlations, particularly among electrocardiographic parameters, where the maximum pairwise correlation reached 0.62. While not indicative of strong linear dependence in isolation, these interrelationships collectively contributed to the near-singularity of the feature matrix.

Notably, the strongest inter-feature correlations were observed within the ECG-derived variables, whereas laboratory parameters showed relatively weak interdependence (maximum correlation of 0.33), and echocardiographic and angiographic features demonstrated moderate correlations (up to 0.22 and 0.50, respectively).

Feature selection

Models with varying numbers of input features were constructed to evaluate predictive performance under cross-validation and to determine the optimal feature subset size. Models including between 5 and 15 features were evaluated with a step size of one, followed by additional models with larger subsets evaluated using a step size of five features.

The results of the hybrid feature selection procedure, summarized in Fig. 3, indicate that CatBoost achieves optimal performance when using approximately 8–10 predictors. The maximum ROC-AUC reached 0.73, indicating good discriminative ability of the model. A consistent pattern was observed across performance metrics. Specificity remained high and stable, exceeding 0.78 across most configurations and peaking at 0.82 when nine features were included. In contrast, sensitivity (Recall) exhibited greater variability, reaching a maximum value of 0.55.

Effect of feature number on CatBoost model performance for MACE prediction. Accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and ROC-AUC were evaluated across models trained with increasing numbers of features. Performance stabilized after inclusion of approximately 8–10 predictors, beyond which additional features provided minimal improvement.

Precision and overall accuracy followed similar trends, increasing with the number of features and reaching a plateau in the range of 8–10 predictors, with values of approximately 0.69 and 0.70, respectively. Importantly, further increasing the number of features beyond ten did not result in statistically meaningful improvements in any performance metric. Moreover, sensitivity declined with larger feature sets, suggesting the of redundant or weakly informative variables.

This approach achieves a favorable balance between model discrimination, robustness, and clinical interpretability.

The key finding is that CatBoost trained directly on data containing missing values achieved the best or jointly best performance across most evaluation metrics. In particular, this model attained the highest values for Accuracy (0.69), Recall (0.53), and ROC-AUC (0.73), as well as the maximum F1-score (0.60) and G-mean (0.66), indicating the most balanced classification performance.

Comparison of imputation strategies showed that pre-imputation with the advanced TabImpute method did not produce statistically meaningful improvement over CatBoost’s native handling of missing values. The largest source of error was the misclassification of true MACE cases as non-events, consistent with the model’s lower sensitivity. Interpretation of MACE model

To enhance interpretability and identify the most influential predictors of MACE risk, SHAP analysis was applied to the final CatBoost model. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 5) illustrates both the relative importance of each feature and the direction of its contribution to model output.

SHAP analysis of key predictors in the MACE prediction model. This figure presents the SHAP summary plot for the CatBoost model, illustrating the relative importance and directional effect of the ten most influential predictors of in-hospital MACE. Each point represents an individual patient, with color indicating the magnitude of the corresponding feature value. Positive SHAP values indicate increased predicted risk, whereas negative values indicate decreased risk. TIMI flow before PCI, LVEF, cTnI, baseline cumulative ST-segment deviation, WBC count, culprit lesion characteristics, BUN, creatinine, random blood sugar, and serum potassium were among the strongest contributors to model output.

TIMI flow before PCI was the most influential predictor. Lower pre-procedural TIMI grades were associated with positive SHAP values and higher predicted MACE risk, whereas higher TIMI flow showed a protective effect, consistent with evidence linking impaired coronary perfusion to adverse outcomes. LVEF was the second most important feature. Reduced LVEF contributed positively to SHAP values, indicating elevated risk, while preserved systolic function was protective, reflecting the prognostic role of ventricular dysfunction in acute coronary syndromes (ACS).

Among laboratory markers, elevated cTnI and greater baseline cumulative ST-segment deviation (Sigma STE Base) were strongly associated with increased risk, reflecting myocardial injury and ischemic burden. Higher WBC and BUN values were also linked to positive SHAP contributions, underscoring the roles of inflammation and renal dysfunction.

Angiographic characterization of the culprit lesion contributed meaningfully as well, despite missing observations, highlighting CatBoost’s ability to use partially observed angiographic data. Renal biomarkers (Cr) and metabolic or electrolyte parameters (random BS and serum potassium) showed moderate but consistent positive effects on predicted risk when abnormal.

Overall, SHAP analysis shows that the CatBoost model relies on clinically plausible predictors spanning angiographic, functional, electrocardiographic, laboratory, and metabolic domains, with directions of effect consistent with established mechanisms of MACE development.

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of patients with STEMI undergoing PCI during the index admission, we developed ML-based models to predict in-hospital MACE and all-cause mortality using routinely collected clinical, electrocardiographic, laboratory, and angiographic features. Using a hybrid feature-selection strategy and CatBoost, a gradient boosting algorithm with native support for missing values, we identified a compact set of approximately ten predictors that achieved acceptable discrimination for MACE (AUC = 0.73). A separate mortality model showed excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.93). These findings suggest that routinely available clinical data, when analyzed using missing-data–aware ML methods, may support early risk stratification in real-world STEMI populations.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the occurrence of MACE after PCI is associated with extended hospital stays, higher medical costs, and increased mortality rates in patients with STEMI, with potential long-term effects on prognosis and quality of life17. Accordingly, early identification of patients at increased risk of MACE is clinically important for guiding monitoring intensity and inpatient management.

Several recent studies have applied ML methods to predict adverse outcomes after PCI, with generally promising but heterogeneous results. In short-term STEMI cohorts, Chen et al. reported that logistic regression achieved AUCs of 0.814 and 0.776 in training and validation sets, respectively, after Boruta-based selection of seven predictors from 40 candidate variables18. In ACS patients undergoing PCI, Choi et al. used a time-to-event ML framework and reported C-indices of 0.743 at 30 days and 0.616 at one year, highlighting that predictor importance may vary over time19.

Larger AMI or PCI cohorts have also shown strong discrimination using ensemble-based approaches, including GBM, RF, and Super Learner models, with reported AUCs ranging from approximately 0.81 to 0.98 across different outcomes and follow-up periods. Tofighi et al., for example, reported AUCs of 0.92 and 0.91 for Distributed RF and GBM models, respectively, for 1-year MACE prediction in STEMI patients undergoing PCI20. However, these studies differed substantially in sample size, outcome definition, prediction horizon, selected predictors, and missing-data management. Missing values were commonly handled using single imputation, KNN imputation, multivariate imputation, or deletion of variables with substantial missingness, and the extent of missingness was not consistently reported. Therefore, although prior studies support the potential value of ML for post-PCI risk prediction, their applicability to real-world STEMI registries with substantial incomplete data remains limited.

Recent meta-analyses also support the potential value of ML for post-PCI risk prediction, while emphasizing important methodological limitations. Nayebirad et al. reported that ML models achieved a pooled c-statistic of 0.85 for MACE prediction compared with 0.75 for LR, although the difference was not statistically significant. Across post-PCI outcomes, tree-based ensemble models, including GBM, RF, and CatBoost, were frequently among the best-performing approaches7. Similarly, a second meta-analysis found generally good to excellent discrimination for ML-based cardiovascular outcome prediction after PCI, but concluded that the incremental advantage over conventional regression remained inconsistent and depended on data quality, outcome definition, and model development methods8. Importantly, both reviews highlighted recurring limitations, including high risk of bias, incomplete reporting of missing-data handling, and limited external validation. These findings support the use of ML for post-PCI risk prediction but also reinforce the need for transparent missing-data handling and clinically interpretable modeling in real-world registries.

In this study, SHAP analysis showed that the final model relied on clinically plausible predictors across angiographic, functional, electrocardiographic, laboratory, renal, and metabolic domains. Lower TIMI flow before PCI, reduced LVEF, higher cTnI, and greater baseline ST-segment elevation contributed to higher predicted MACE risk, reflecting impaired coronary perfusion, ventricular dysfunction, myocardial injury, and ischemic burden21–24. Other influential predictors, including WBC count, admission glucose, BUN, creatinine, and potassium, suggest that systemic inflammation, metabolic stress, renal dysfunction, and electrolyte imbalance also contributed to short-term risk stratification25–27. These findings support the clinical face validity of the model. However, the association between lower LVEF and MACE should be interpreted in light of the endpoint definition, as severe heart failure requiring escalation of therapy was included as a component of the composite outcome.

A major methodological finding of this study was that retaining partially observed but clinically relevant variables was feasible and informative. More than one-third of predictors were incomplete, with some angiographic and ECG variables having up to 40% missing data. Instead of excluding these variables or restricting the analysis to complete cases, we compared different missing-data strategies within the same registry. CatBoost trained directly on the incomplete dataset achieved the best overall balance of accuracy, recall, F1-score, and AUC, whereas transformer-based imputation and TabPFN did not improve performance. This finding was further supported by imbalance-aware metrics, as CatBoost with native missing-value handling achieved the highest F2-score, balanced accuracy, and G-mean among the evaluated models. These results indicate relatively balanced performance across outcome classes, although the moderate recall shows that missed MACE events remain an important limitation. This suggests that native missing-value handling may be a practical strategy for real-world STEMI registry data, particularly when missingness affects clinically meaningful variables.

The relatively high in-hospital MACE rate observed in this cohort should be interpreted in the context of the broad composite endpoint and the clinical profile of the study population. MACE included both fatal and nonfatal early complications during the index hospitalization. All-cause in-hospital mortality occurred in 30 patients, indicating that mortality accounted for only a minority of the composite endpoint, suggesting that much of the remaining MACE burden reflected nonfatal complications included in the composite definition. The cohort also appeared to include many high-risk STEMI presentations, with frequent anterior ST-segment elevation and LAD involvement, suggesting substantial ischemic burden and ventricular dysfunction. However, component-level frequencies for nonfatal MACE events were not available for separate analysis.

From a clinical perspective, the model is intended to support early risk stratification after PCI rather than replace clinical judgment. Patients identified as being at higher risk for in-hospital MACE may benefit from closer rhythm and hemodynamic monitoring, longer observation in a coronary care setting, early reassessment of ventricular function, and careful optimization of medical therapy. A high-risk prediction may also alert clinicians to the need for increased surveillance for recurrent ischemia, arrhythmias, shock, bleeding, or worsening heart failure. Therefore, the model may help guide the intensity of inpatient monitoring and support more efficient allocation of clinical resources during the early post-PCI period. Prospective validation, however, is required before routine clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In this single-center STEMI cohort treated with primary PCI, we developed ML models using routinely collected clinical data to predict in-hospital MACE and mortality with acceptable to excellent discrimination. A central aspect of this work was the explicit management of substantial missing data through algorithms and imputation strategies optimized for real-world registries, enabling inclusion of clinically important but partially observed variables. These results demonstrate the feasibility of ML-based short-term risk prediction in STEMI, while underscoring the need for rigorous missing-data handling and validation in larger, independent populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was conducted within a single registry, and no external validation was performed. Therefore, the possibility of model overfitting and overly optimistic performance estimates cannot be excluded. Second, the prevalence of MACE in our cohort was relatively high (approximately 40%), which may limit the generalizability of the model to populations with lower event rates. Finally, the outcome assessment was restricted to in-hospital events, meaning that our findings complement rather than replace studies examining longer-term prognosis. In addition, component-level frequencies of the composite MACE endpoint were not available for separate analysis, limiting interpretation of the relative contribution and clinical severity of individual MACE components.
