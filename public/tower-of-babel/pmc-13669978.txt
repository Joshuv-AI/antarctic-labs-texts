Australian healthcare professionals' perspectives and experiences on screening and monitoring islet autoimmunity: A qualitative exploration
Authors: Mia Majstorovic, Kelly J. McGorm, John M. Wentworth, Alyssa Sawyer, Melissa Oxlad, Jennifer J. Couper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIslet autoantibodies characterise a period of immune‐mediated destruction preceding clinical (symptomatic) type 1 diabetes. Screening and monitoring programmes for islet autoimmunity are being planned and implemented globally. The perspectives and experiences of healthcare professionals working in paediatric screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity and clinical type 1 diabetes can inform these programmes.MethodsWe interviewed 32 healthcare professionals (81% women) caring for children with islet autoimmunity and/or clinical type 1 diabetes in Australia (median of 13 years of type 1 diabetes clinical experience). Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsWe developed seven themes: 1. implementing population screening and monitoring is contested, 2. considerations for the structure of screening and monitoring, 3. prioritising equitable access and convenience, 4. focus on managing children's testing experiences, 5. potential psychological impacts of islet autoimmunity on children, 6. caregivers' perceived coping ability and associated responses to screening and monitoring and 7. importance of relationships with and support from healthcare professionals. Participants expressed benefits, concerns and considerations necessary for the well‐organised, equitable and acceptable implementation of screening and monitoring. They described varying effects of testing and islet autoimmunity on families, emphasising the importance of trusted relationships between families and healthcare professionals.ConclusionsHealthcare professionals' insights can guide how screening and monitoring programmes are designed and implemented, particularly in justifying their introduction, ensuring equitable care and reducing adverse impacts on families. Further research will focus on the experiences of children and caregivers to integrate wider input into the design of these programmes.

INTRODUCTION

Islet autoimmunity, characterised by the presence of islet autoantibodies, is a pre‐symptomatic period of variable length that indicates underlying immune‐mediated pancreatic β‐cell destruction. 1 , 2 Islet autoimmunity heralds clinical type 1 diabetes (T1D), when symptoms appear, and insulin therapy becomes necessary. 1 , 2 Different islet autoantibody profiles confer varying levels of risk for disease progression. The presence of multiple islet autoantibodies, also known as early‐stage T1D, signifies an almost certain lifetime risk of developing clinical T1D. 3

Current screening for T1D involves blood testing for genetic risk and islet autoantibodies. Subsequent monitoring of children with detectable islet autoantibodies is required to achieve the objectives of screening. 1 , 2 Namely, (1) to prevent life‐threatening diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), (2) to enable gradual family education and the provision of psychosocial support and (3) to facilitate access to disease‐modifying treatment in trials or routine care. 1 , 2 , 4 The first disease‐modifying therapy has been approved for use in the United States, Europe and more recently, in May of 2026, in Australia. 5 , 6 , 7 Shown to delay the onset of clinical T1D, teplizumab has provided further impetus to international screening and monitoring efforts. 8 Italy also legislated universal screening for islet autoimmunity in 2023. 9 Similar programmes are established or being modelled in Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, Israel and across Europe. 10 , 11 In Australia, children at risk of T1D have been identified through research programmes: the National Screening Pilot (ACTRN12622000381785), 10 Type1Screen (ACTRN12620000510943) 12 and ENDIA (Environmental Determinants of Islet Autoimmunity; ANZCTRN12613000794707). 13 The establishment of islet autoimmunity monitoring clinics as part of the Australian Diabetes Antibody Program (AusDAP) has driven interest in how families' and healthcare professionals' (HCPs') needs and preferences can inform the development of national screening and monitoring programmes.

While part of a broader project on children's, caregivers' and HCPs' perspectives and experiences, the present study exclusively includes data from HCPs. Their views are critical given the direct role they hold in delivering care to children identified to be at risk or in the early stages of T1D and their families. 1 Regarding prior international research, a survey found that paediatricians in the United States prioritised DKA risk reduction, access to insulin‐delaying treatment and removing screening fees for consumers. 14 More than 80% of Italian paediatricians surveyed (N = 62) wanted to engage in the country's newly legislated T1D screening program, but raised concerns about unpredictable disease progression, children's interpretations of testing, family anxiety and overprotective behaviours, follow‐up pathways and increased HCP workloads. 15 Qualitative and mixed method studies with HCPs from the United Kingdom and United States revealed similar findings, alongside an emphasis on accessible testing and psychological counselling being available for families. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Few studies have gathered Australian HCPs' views. HCPs working in T1D co‐designed an early‐stage T1D clinical pathway; however, this work employed group‐based data collection and did not gather data outside of one state. 20 We also presented the first data from HCPs on their perspectives and experiences of knowledge regarding screening and monitoring islet autoimmunity. 21 Our findings revealed that HCPs gained knowledge through self‐directed experiential and peer learning. However, they expressed knowledge gaps in monitoring practices, clinical diabetes risk prediction, insulin initiation, communication with families, and supporting families emotionally. 21 The current study is the first national (interview‐based) exploration of HCPs' opinions and suggestions for islet autoimmunity screening and monitoring with the aim of guiding programme design and delivery.

The research question was: What are the perspectives and experiences of HCPs involved in islet autoimmunity and/or clinical T1D on screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity in Australia?

METHODS

Study design and participants

Study methodology has been outlined previously (see Majstorovic et al. 21 for a full description), in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research. 22 Briefly, we undertook a descriptive qualitative interview study with HCPs involved in screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity via ENDIA, Type1Screen, the National Screening Pilot and/or specialist monitoring clinics; and we additionally recruited HCPs who delivered clinical T1D care. 23 Participants were interviewed between February and March 2025.

Ethical considerations

The Women's and Children's Hospital ethics committee, based in Adelaide, South Australia, approved the research (2024/HRE00146). The Declaration of Helsinki was followed. Participants provided voluntary informed consent.

Recruitment

We employed purposive sampling, selecting diversity in years of clinical experience, occupations and states/territories of residence. After each interview, MM used a snowball sampling approach by requesting that participants share study details with their colleagues. We applied information power to decide sample size as data collection progressed, rather than determining it a priori. 24 Specifically, we were guided by the relatively targeted study aim(s), a fairly specific sample, a rich data corpus, exploratory data analysis and limited past research. 24

Data collection and analysis

MM interviewed 32 HCPs (24 on Zoom and eight in person) based on a semi‐structured interview guide that KJM, MM, MO and JJC formulated (refer to File S1). The guide was informed by existing literature and input from seven ENDIA caregivers with lived experience of T1D. An ENDIA research nurse piloted the guide with KJM and MM, with no changes recommended. The audio‐recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and de‐identified.

We employed reflexive thematic analysis. 25 As we refrained from preconceived ideas of participants' perspectives, our analytic approach was inductive. MM generated codes in NVivo15 and produced themes. MO oversaw this process, and KJM and JJC provided input. We sent participants the themes; one responded, seeking clarification of an analytic point. All authors agreed upon the final themes.

Reflexivity considerations

MM maintained an audit trail to record research developments and personal reflections. MM is a female researcher with a doctorate in health psychology and qualitative research training. She is not an HCP, meaning she interpreted the data from an outsider's lens. KJM, JMW, JJC, AS, and MO worked as a research nurse, an endocrinologist, a paediatric endocrinologist, a clinical psychologist, and a health and clinical psychologist, respectively. We had no personal lived experience of clinical T1D, nor of screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity; hence, we approached the research less emotively.

Thematic summary

Seven themes represented HCPs' perspectives and experiences of screening and monitoring islet autoimmunity (see Figure 1).

Thematic diagram.

Theme 1: Implementing population screening and monitoring is contested

HCPs debated the pros and cons of screening and monitoring with no consensus across participants. Participants, most of whom worked in islet autoimmunity, discussed the benefits of screening and monitoring: increasing families' awareness of clinical signs and symptoms, preventing DKA, facilitating optimal clinical management, and enabling families to prepare for and emotionally adjust to clinical T1D. They perceived monitoring being connected to trials and/or treatments as important and offering families hope. …the immune therapy work does offer as much optimism as there's ever been, really. It's just a trial right now, but there will be… [a licensed treatment]… (P1, research nurse coordinator)

…the immune therapy work does offer as much optimism as there's ever been, really. It's just a trial right now, but there will be… [a licensed treatment]… (P1, research nurse coordinator)

However, participants claimed that treatments and trials were lacking in Australia, with some consequently expressing resistance to screening and monitoring being expanded. They questioned whether funding screening and monitoring was justified, and cited other social and health inequities that could be targeted. Participants mentioned already limited public healthcare resources, equipment provision, and workforce capacity for clinically recognised T1D. …bang for buck, we could change people's health outcomes by focusing on different things rather than type one screening right at this moment… I just feel that again, we are widening the gap in health outcomes… (P30, paediatric endocrinologist).

…bang for buck, we could change people's health outcomes by focusing on different things rather than type one screening right at this moment… I just feel that again, we are widening the gap in health outcomes… (P30, paediatric endocrinologist).

Additionally, participants involved in islet autoimmunity care raised concerns about whether detectable autoantibody results might affect individuals' health insurance access and the implications for screening.

Theme 2: Considerations for the structure of screening and monitoring

HCPs expressed a need for nationally coordinated, well‐organised and efficient service delivery. Participants questioned the optimal population screening model, particularly regarding the location of screening and the clinicians who should be involved. Participants, most of whom worked in islet autoimmunity, recommended coordination between screening teams, local/primary HCPs, and clinical monitoring services, with co‐location of screening and clinical care.…If clinic [staff] are concerned, they just call up [the research screening facility], and they will say, “I've got this kid here, what's going on, or mum and dad are interested in screening, what do I offer them?” Easy. We can run down and chat to the families… (P9, research coordinator).

…If clinic [staff] are concerned, they just call up [the research screening facility], and they will say, “I've got this kid here, what's going on, or mum and dad are interested in screening, what do I offer them?” Easy. We can run down and chat to the families… (P9, research coordinator).

Participants favourably discussed a nationally coordinated monitoring programme, seeking a clear, standardised pathway for families following autoantibody detection. Those working in islet autoimmunity also emphasised having a centralised and nationally consistent data management approach. Participants (mostly doctors and research coordinators involved in islet autoimmunity care) proposed the ideal clinicians in monitoring clinics, suggesting paediatric endocrinologists, diabetes nurse educators, mental healthcare professionals and (possibly) dietitians. One participant questioned whether a complete multidisciplinary team was financially defensible. Other participants not involved in islet autoimmunity care said a multidisciplinary team was needed to justify monitoring clinics separate from other diabetes clinics.…If you can run it as a multi‐d clinic with a specific focus on whatever their needs are, then as a separate clinic, I think that would be worthwhile… (P12, paediatric endocrinologist).

…If you can run it as a multi‐d clinic with a specific focus on whatever their needs are, then as a separate clinic, I think that would be worthwhile… (P12, paediatric endocrinologist).

Additionally, research coordinators emphasised the importance of timely autoantibody results for appointment scheduling and minimising family stress.

Theme 3: Prioritising equitable access and convenience

HCPs discussed barriers for certain people in accessing screening and monitoring and offered solutions for how these could be overcome. Particularly, they discussed challenges for rural/regional families in accessing both monitoring and trials. They noted limited access to pathology services and medical specialists as well as an absence of trial sites and research capacity outside of Australian cities. Paediatric endocrinologists proposed healthcare models that empower local services, supported by tertiary centres, to care for rural/regional families, conflicting with other suggestions to centralise monitoring in city centres.…So, they've got a multidisciplinary team in that [rural/regional] site, and they get support from a tertiary centre to either help them with the running of the clinic, do the case conferencing after the clinic, or actually see the really difficult patients… (P31, paediatric endocrinologist).

…So, they've got a multidisciplinary team in that [rural/regional] site, and they get support from a tertiary centre to either help them with the running of the clinic, do the case conferencing after the clinic, or actually see the really difficult patients… (P31, paediatric endocrinologist).

Participants viewed telehealth as convenient and enabling access to monitoring for non‐metropolitan and rurally based families. Some participants recommended a hybrid telehealth and in‐person approach, expressing a need for in‐person contact for physical assessments and rapport‐building.…We saw a lot of telehealth obviously during COVID and particularly in paediatrics… the families are very open with this, it's hard to engage kids via telehealth… (P20, diabetes nurse educator).

…We saw a lot of telehealth obviously during COVID and particularly in paediatrics… the families are very open with this, it's hard to engage kids via telehealth… (P20, diabetes nurse educator).

Research coordinators and a diabetes nurse educator also claimed that personal circumstances, caring responsibilities, work, travel to the city and parking were barriers to screening and monitoring participation for families. They proposed all appointments being justified, flexible appointment times, home testing and blood spot cards (for screening).…if they're physically having to come to the hospital for a purpose, then we should see them then instead of asking them to come for extra clinic appointments, and we can be remotely monitoring them… (P13, diabetes nurse educator).

…if they're physically having to come to the hospital for a purpose, then we should see them then instead of asking them to come for extra clinic appointments, and we can be remotely monitoring them… (P13, diabetes nurse educator).

Participants additionally raised concerns about access to continuous glucose monitors (CGM), as they are not subsidised for individuals with islet autoimmunity, and when funded by hospitals, families must attend in person.

Theme 4: Focus on managing children's testing experiences

HCPs discussed the importance of prioritising children's comfort during tests, explaining that some children displayed distress and that caregivers influenced testing experiences. Data were largely derived from research coordinators and diabetes nurse educators; most worked in islet autoimmunity. Participants considered that children may not recognise the rationale or benefits of monitoring and/or trials. Blood tests (intravenous and finger‐prick), oral glucose and mixed‐meal tolerance tests, and CGM were generally said to distress children. However, participants perceived that some children coped well with tests, even enjoying the novelty of equipment like CGM. …some kids like to be different… some kids have seen sensors like CGMs on TikTok and stuff, and they think it's cool they get to be part of this club… (P26, diabetes nurse educator).

…some kids like to be different… some kids have seen sensors like CGMs on TikTok and stuff, and they think it's cool they get to be part of this club… (P26, diabetes nurse educator).

Participants perceived factors influencing children's reactions to tests, including school experiences with CGM wear, age and observing family members using testing equipment. Participants further discussed how caregivers shaped children's testing experiences, often negatively when they overtly worried about children's pain and distress. On some occasions, caregivers' anticipation of children's distress reportedly conflicted with children's reactions to tests. …We put the EMLA [cream] on with the VR [virtual reality] goggles on these two boys, and they had no f****** idea they were having bloods taken. The needle was in, the blood was done, and the mum was like, “Oh, that wasn't anywhere near as bad as I thought it would be”… (P4, research nurse coordinator).

…We put the EMLA [cream] on with the VR [virtual reality] goggles on these two boys, and they had no f****** idea they were having bloods taken. The needle was in, the blood was done, and the mum was like, “Oh, that wasn't anywhere near as bad as I thought it would be”… (P4, research nurse coordinator).

Some participants questioned invasive testing for monitoring as it offered no immediate benefits and was not a clinical emergency. Children's assent was also considered important. Participants viewed it as challenging when caregivers forced blood tests (such as when children were at higher autoantibody risk) or conversely, placed complete decision‐making responsibility on their children. …[caregivers] will say, “Oh, we don't want you to do anything that you don't want to do”… It's really hard because they're leaving it to us [HCPs] to encourage the children to participate… (P5, research coordinator).

…[caregivers] will say, “Oh, we don't want you to do anything that you don't want to do”… It's really hard because they're leaving it to us [HCPs] to encourage the children to participate… (P5, research coordinator).

Positive testing experiences for children, to improve comfort and future procedural experiences, were emphasised. To this end, participants considered routine, trusted relationships, tailored approaches for children's physical or psychological needs, granting children supported autonomy, offering reassurance and managing expectations of pain as critical.

Theme 5: Potential psychological impacts of islet autoimmunity on children

While some participants considered that islet autoimmunity did not adversely affect children, others voiced concerns about emotional impacts. Paediatric endocrinologists and research coordinators, most of whom worked in islet autoimmunity, perceived that children were either unconcerned about detectable autoantibody results or accepted them. A diabetes nurse educator and a research coordinator expressed that some children wanted to use equipment or appeared unconcerned about developing clinical T1D like their family member(s).…she'll [girl being monitored] sit there and be like, “Oh, I'm going to maybe get type one like daddy. I'm going to be like daddy. I'm more like daddy than mum”… I feel like [she's] a little bit excited in a way… (P11, research coordinator).

…she'll [girl being monitored] sit there and be like, “Oh, I'm going to maybe get type one like daddy. I'm going to be like daddy. I'm more like daddy than mum”… I feel like [she's] a little bit excited in a way… (P11, research coordinator).

However, participants questioned the emotional impacts on children of a sudden clinical T1D diagnosis versus early risk awareness. They expressed concerns about altering children's childhoods and quality of life.…you don't want that 30 per cent [who do not develop clinical T1D] to turn around and say, “Well, I was involved with this, and I felt my whole childhood was changed because everyone treated me differently”… (P10, diabetes nurse educator).

…you don't want that 30 per cent [who do not develop clinical T1D] to turn around and say, “Well, I was involved with this, and I felt my whole childhood was changed because everyone treated me differently”… (P10, diabetes nurse educator).

Research coordinators and diabetes nurse educators, most with clinical experience in islet autoimmunity, considered that children's age, the nature of their healthcare interactions and family attitudes towards clinical T1D influenced children's psychological responses to islet autoimmunity and clinical T1D. They suggested that having a sibling with clinical T1D heightened children's worry about developing the condition.…if it's a sibling that they've also had to kind of be part of the family journey when they've been diagnosed, and that's really stressful… So, I can imagine that they're affected… (P7, research nurse coordinator).

…if it's a sibling that they've also had to kind of be part of the family journey when they've been diagnosed, and that's really stressful… So, I can imagine that they're affected… (P7, research nurse coordinator).

Further, participants considered engagement in (long‐term) monitoring, transitioning on and off insulin during regimen adjustments in some cases of clinical onset and moving from paediatric to adult care, to be difficult for children and young people.

Theme 6: Caregivers' perceived coping ability and associated responses to screening and monitoring

HCPs considered how caregivers' diverse appraisals of their capacity to cope with children's clinical risk and potential clinical T1D, often shaped by personal characteristics or experiences, influenced their varying adaptive and non‐adaptive responses. HCPs (mostly research coordinators) explained that some caregivers declined autoantibody results due to concerns about their ability to cope and avoidance of anxiety regarding children's progression to clinical T1D. Participants further thought caregivers experienced anxiety when expecting or awaiting results. Other caregivers were interpreted as seeking results for control and predictability.…some people say they prefer not to know [the results], and then it's just like a ticking time bomb that they can't prevent, and what's the point? It just brings anxiety. And then other families said, “It makes me feel in control for at least 12 months”… (P32, counsellor).

…some people say they prefer not to know [the results], and then it's just like a ticking time bomb that they can't prevent, and what's the point? It just brings anxiety. And then other families said, “It makes me feel in control for at least 12 months”… (P32, counsellor).

In response to detectable autoantibody results, participants perceived caregivers as experiencing diverse emotions, such as anxiety, grief, guilt, denial and shock. They viewed that caregivers struggled with uncertain disease progression, becoming hypervigilant for signs/symptoms of clinical onset. However, participants voiced that some caregivers accepted the results and displayed resilience.…they're quite practical about it. “Yes, this has happened, we need to deal with it, this is how we're going to do it”… (P8, paediatric endocrinologist).

…they're quite practical about it. “Yes, this has happened, we need to deal with it, this is how we're going to do it”… (P8, paediatric endocrinologist).

Participants, most of whom worked in islet autoimmunity, proposed that personal stressors (not associated with monitoring), greater educational attainment (linked to future‐focused thinking) and higher autoantibody risk profiles adversely affected caregivers' coping. Clinical T1D management technology reportedly reassured caregivers, though.…new families seem to have adapted to their diagnosis better in a way because the pump has made life easier with diabetes, and so, they feel like, “OK, we can do this. We could do it for another child if that happens”… (P30, paediatric endocrinologist).

…new families seem to have adapted to their diagnosis better in a way because the pump has made life easier with diabetes, and so, they feel like, “OK, we can do this. We could do it for another child if that happens”… (P30, paediatric endocrinologist).

Finally, participants perceived that having a family history of clinical T1D influenced coping; some families responded pragmatically to detectable autoantibodies due to confidence in managing clinical T1D, while others displayed anxiety from being able to picture their child's future with the condition.

Theme 7: Importance of relationships with and support from healthcare professionals

HCPs described how they displayed care and nurtured relationships with families as a means of supporting them through the emotional challenges of screening and monitoring. Research coordinators, in particular, described developing relationships with families over time.…if you do have to ring them with bad news [detectable autoantibody results], it's almost, we are not friends, but it's sort of almost like a friend giving the news… (P6, research coordinator).

…if you do have to ring them with bad news [detectable autoantibody results], it's almost, we are not friends, but it's sort of almost like a friend giving the news… (P6, research coordinator).

A paediatric endocrinologist also described that they valued forming relationships, but lacked time. Participants discussed being attentive to families' broader circumstances and emotions, listening to and counselling them, and following up on their concerns about clinical T1D. They discussed tailoring monitoring to families' desired levels of intervention and support, based on their personal circumstances and preferred HCP involvement.…we just need to be helping you [families] along that process and empowering them to get through life in general with the knowledge that we're here to support them if and when it's needed… (P25, advanced endocrinology trainee).

…we just need to be helping you [families] along that process and empowering them to get through life in general with the knowledge that we're here to support them if and when it's needed… (P25, advanced endocrinology trainee).

Nonetheless, participants recommended access to specialised psychological services for families experiencing ongoing distress. They discussed availability and funding issues, though, referencing an existing lack of psychological services for clinical T1D. Therefore, while participants' perspectives on implementing islet autoimmunity screening and monitoring varied, they agreed on the need to embed support for families who experience distress due to screening and monitoring outcomes.

DISCUSSION

We present the first Australian interview‐based study on HCPs' perspectives and experiences of paediatric screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity. Our findings reveal debate among HCPs regarding the introduction of a population screening and monitoring programme; the need for nationally coordinated, well‐organised multidisciplinary care; and concerns about equitable access to monitoring, particularly for rurally/regionally residing families. Prioritising children's comfort during testing and considering the psychological impacts of islet autoimmunity were other key insights. Last, the critical role of HCPs in supporting families through screening and monitoring was highlighted. Below, we elaborate on our findings to inform practice in the context of AusDAP, the national islet autoimmunity monitoring programme, and other international initiatives.

First, HCPs expressed conflicting perspectives regarding whether the introduction of an Australian screening and monitoring program was justified. In line with international consensus guidance, 1 , 2 participants displayed a comprehensive understanding of the intended benefits of monitoring. HCPs raised concerns about a lack of trials and treatments to prevent or delay clinical T1D, though. Since then, the disease‐modifying treatment landscape has changed in Australia with the approval of teplizumab. 7 However, teplizumab is not subsidised under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, and research on Australian families' willingness to pursue, and experiences of, this immunotherapy is needed, noting preliminary work in the United States. 26 HCPs' uncertainty about whether screening and monitoring are defensible is clearly important to re‐investigate in light of the recent approval of teplizumab.

Further, participants raised concerns about the cost of screening and monitoring islet autoimmunity, thereby demonstrating an awareness of broader health‐economics considerations. Economic modelling in Australia is being undertaken in relation to offsetting the cost of DKA. 27 , 28 Another financial consideration mentioned by HCPs was whether detectable islet autoantibodies would impact individuals' health insurance access. Under the Australian Genetic Testing Protections in Life Insurance and Other Measures Bill 2025, insurers cannot discriminate based on genetic test results, but these protections do not apply once a diagnosis has been made. 29 With impetus to reclassify the diagnosis of T1D as beginning at earlier, pre‐symptomatic stages, the insurance implications for individuals with islet autoimmunity remain unclear. 1 , 30

Second, participants agreed that monitoring pathways should be coordinated, consistently delivered and designed to promote equitable access for rural/regional families. This emphasis on organised pathways reflects international research with HCPs, 15 , 16 and our earlier findings, where HCPs sought knowledge on screening and monitoring procedures to inform families. 21 Our findings further highlight concerns about access to monitoring and trials for rural/regional families. Children living in rural/regional areas and from lower socio‐economic backgrounds present with increased rates of DKA, suggesting they may particularly benefit from early detection and monitoring. 28 , 31 To support access for these families, participants endorsed telehealth use, further recommending a hybrid approach that incorporates in‐person care. While telehealth has been successfully employed in clinical T1D, its optimal hybrid implementation warrants investigation in islet autoimmunity care. 32

Third, the potential for adverse impacts of screening and monitoring on children and caregivers was another focus of the results. One Australian study showed that islet autoimmunity in young children followed in an at‐risk cohort did not significantly increase their mothers' perceived stress after 1–2 years. 33 However, our HCPs perceived factors as undermining caregivers' coping, namely: personal stressors, greater educational attainment and higher autoantibody risk in children. International research substantiates the impact of personal stressors, with mothers of children genetically at risk of T1D who had recently experienced negative life events reporting increased anxiety. 34 More current work is needed, though. 34 Higher islet autoantibody risk in children, particularly parents' perceptions that their children are at elevated risk, has likewise been linked to heightened parental anxiety in international studies. 35 , 36 However, contrary to our participants' views, one study found that parents of children with islet autoantibodies who had lower educational attainment experienced greater anxiety. 35 These findings indicate that caregivers facing additional stressful life events and caring for children at higher clinical risk require more intensive psychosocial support during monitoring. It is encouraging that Australian HCPs recognised these needs and expressed that they already offered emotional support to families. Studies should clarify how educational attainment influences caregivers' coping to allow better identification of those likely to need additional support.

Moreover, participants demonstrated care and concern for children by (accurately) discussing how little is known about how islet autoimmunity affects them. 37 Although, consistent with other findings, 18 , 38 HCPs emphasised that tests are central to children's monitoring experiences, highlighting the need to make testing as tolerable as possible for children to minimise families' distress and sustain their monitoring participation. Our ongoing project will report children's and caregivers' perspectives on islet autoantibody screening and monitoring. A complex understanding of the breadth of impacts on families will support the development of tailored resources and interventions to improve their screening and monitoring experiences. 1 , 2 , 37

Limitations and strengths

No general practitioners participated, despite attempts to recruit them through a peak organisation. Research should seek their perspectives, as they will play a role in universal screening programmes. 1 Additional insights from HCPs in rural/regional settings would better inform how to deliver care for rural/regional families. Nonetheless, our sample comprised a diversity of experienced HCPs from across all Australian states (except for one), including metropolitan and regional locations.

CONCLUSION

Our study contributes to international discourse on HCPs' perspectives on how paediatric screening and monitoring of islet autoimmunity can be optimally designed and delivered. Findings highlight differences among HCPs in how justified they thought screening and monitoring were in the current context, alongside an emphasis on delivering care equitably, minimising children's testing‐associated distress and gaining a deeper understanding of the psychological impacts on families. These insights offer reassurance regarding the level of consideration Australian HCPs have in providing accessible, well‐organised and psychologically informed islet autoimmunity care.
