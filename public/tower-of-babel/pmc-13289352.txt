Maternal B-vitamin status modifies the risk of congenital heart disease in offspring associated with prenatal persistent organic pollutants exposure
Authors: Sainan Li, Hai Lin, Chengrong Wang, Chen Yang, Qianhui Cheng, Jufen Liu, Yali Zhang, Lei Jin, Zhiwen Li, Aiguo Ren, Linlin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNo epidemiological studies have systematically evaluated the associations between prenatal exposure to organophosphate esters and polychlorinated biphenyls and the risk of congenital heart disease (CHD) in offspring. Moreover, the potential modifying role of maternal B-vitamin status in persistent organic pollutants (POPs)-CHD associations has not been examined. We therefore investigated the cardiotoxic effects of prenatal POPs exposure and evaluated effect modification by maternal B-vitamin levels.MethodsA multicenter case-control study was conducted in China from 2016 to 2021, including 425 participants. Thirty POPs and seven plasma B vitamins were quantified using high-resolution mass spectrometry. Single-exposure associations were examined by logistic regression, while multipollutant effects were assessed through Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) and Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) models. Potential effect modification by B vitamins was systematically evaluated.ResultsPrenatal exposure to p-cresyl diphenyl phosphate was associated with an increased risk of CHD (odds ratio [OR] = 1.36, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08, 1.71). Mixture analyses consistently showed an increasing trend in CHD risk with higher exposure to the POPs mixture, with the WQS model yielding a statistically significant association (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.40). Higher concentrations of pyridoxamine, pyridoxal, and vitamin B12 were inversely associated with CHD risk, and mixture analyses using both BKMR and WQS regression further demonstrated a significant negative association between the overall B-vitamin mixture and CHD risk. In the interaction analysis, B vitamins significantly modified the association between prenatal POPs exposure and CHD risk (OR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.52).ConclusionsThis study provides the first population evidence that prenatal POPs exposure is associated with increased CHD risk and adequate maternal B-vitamin levels may attenuate the developmental cardiotoxicity of emerging flame retardants, highlighting the importance of maternal nutritional status in modifying environmental risk factors for CHD.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04900-1.

Background

Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) represent a class of environmentally persistent chemicals that bioaccumulate in living organisms [1]. Among them, organophosphate esters (OPEs) and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) have emerged as two predominant subclasses. Following the phase-out of several brominated flame retardants, OPEs have been increasingly adopted as alternative flame retardants and plasticizers, leading to a rapid rise in their global production and widespread use across industrial and consumer products [2, 3]. PCBs, previously utilized in various industrial applications including electrical equipment and plastic manufacturing, continue to persist in the environment despite global restrictions [4]. They remain ubiquitous in the environment due to their chemical stability and continued release from legacy materials and aging infrastructure [5]. Human exposure to OPEs and PCBs occurs through multiple pathways, including inhalation, dermal contact, and ingestion, with dietary intake considered a dominant source for the general population [6, 7]. Of particular concern is accumulating evidence that both OPEs and PCBs can traverse the placental barrier, as demonstrated by their detection in maternal blood, placental tissue, and fetal compartments [8, 9]. Such transplacental transfer is especially alarming given the heightened vulnerability of the developing fetus to chemical perturbations during critical periods of organogenesis.

Congenital heart disease (CHD), defined as structural or functional abnormalities of the heart present at birth, represents the most common class of congenital malformations, affecting approximately 1.8% of live births worldwide [10–12]. Although genetic factors contribute to CHD etiology, they account for only about 30% of cases [13]. Increasing evidence suggests that environmental exposures (nutritional deficiencies, teratogens) and gene-environment interactions play a substantial and potentially modifiable role in CHD risk [14]. Experimental studies have provided biological plausibility for such associations that early-life exposure to specific OPEs—including triphenyl phosphate and mono-isopropylated triaryl phosphate—induces severe cardiac malformations in vertebrate models [15]. Similarly, PCBs have shown to disrupt cardiac morphogenesis in both zebrafish and avian systems [16, 17]. To date, no population-based study has systematically evaluated the associations between these POPs and the risk of CHD.

Growing epidemiological and experimental evidence suggests that nutritional interventions may mitigate the adverse health effects of environmental pollutant exposure, representing a promising target for public health prevention strategies [18–20]. Maternal nutritional status critically influences both pregnancy outcomes and long-term health trajectories of offspring. Among essential micronutrients, B vitamins—a group of water-soluble compounds obtained exclusively through dietary sources—play pivotal roles in fundamental physiological processes. Specifically, folic acid (FA) and vitamin B12 (VB12) serve as critical cofactors in nucleotide synthesis, genomic maintenance, and methylation reactions [21–23], while vitamin B6 (VB6) participates in amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter production, and hemoglobin formation [24, 25]. These nutrients collectively regulate homocysteine metabolism, with deficiencies leading to hyperhomocysteinemia—an established risk factor for CHD [26]. Emerging evidence suggests a U-shaped association between FA status and CHD risk: both deficient and elevated maternal serum folate levels during early-to-mid pregnancy correlate with increased CHD incidence [27]. Notably, this risk profile appears exacerbated in cases of concomitant VB12 deficiency or elevated homocysteine levels [27]. Maternal FA + VB6 + VB12 containing supplement use during pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of CHD [28]. Nevertheless, the potential modulating role of B-vitamin status on POPs-CHD associations remains completely uninvestigated. This knowledge gap is critical because the key mechanisms implicated in POPs toxicity, including the induction of oxidative stress and disruption of epigenetic regulation, are pathways in which B vitamins are intricately involved.

Therefore, this study examines the associations between developmental exposure to POPs and the risk of CHD, while further assessing whether maternal B-vitamin status modifies these relationships. By integrating chemical exposure and nutritional factors, our findings aim to advance understanding of underlying biological pathways and identify potential opportunities for nutritional risk mitigation.

Methods

Study population

This investigation utilized data from a multicenter case-control study conducted across China. Participants were recruited from six major cities (Beijing, Baotou, Jining, Zhengzhou, Changzhou, Changsha) and multiple towns within Shanxi Province. The geographic distribution of participants is presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. The original multicenter study aims to identify environmental determinants for selected major structural birth defects, including CHD. The detailed methodology has been previously published [29]. The present analysis focused specifically on the CHD case-control subset. Cases were defined as pregnancies with fetal CHD, diagnosed prenatally by ultrasound or postnatally by clinical examination, with all assessments performed by certified physicians following a unified protocol according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, tenth revision (ICD-10) criteria. Eligible case subjects met the following inclusion criteria: (1) maternal residence in the study region throughout pregnancy, and (2) planned completion of prenatal care, delivery, or pregnancy termination at a participating hospital. Exclusion criteria for cases included: (1) coexistence of other major congenital anomalies, (2) presence of severe pregnancy complications (e.g., gestational hypertension, preeclampsia), or (3) maternal use of teratogenic medications within one month prior to enrollment. For each case, we selected one control from the same institution consisting of pregnancies delivering structurally normal neonates. Controls were matched to cases by approximate last menstrual period date (± 4 weeks) following case identification. The same exclusion criteria applied to both groups.

After applying these selection criteria and excluding subjects with incomplete questionnaire data or insufficient biological samples, the original case-control matching was disrupted. Consequently, the present analysis represents an unmatched multicenter case-control study design. Our final analytical cohort comprised 183 CHD cases and 242 controls recruited between 2016 and 2021 across participating centers. The spectrum of CHD phenotypes in our cohort included ventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, and other major subtypes, with complete classification details provided in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Questionnaire and sample collection

Maternal exposure data were collected using standardized structured questionnaires, encompassing demographic characteristics, dietary habits, lifestyle factors, and other relevant covariates during the periconceptional period. Following questionnaire completion and informed consent, maternal venous blood samples were drawn into EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes. Blood tubes were gently inverted for mixing and processed within two hours of collection. Centrifugation was performed at 4 °C and 2500 × g for 10 min to separate the plasma. The plasma layer was subsequently aliquoted into pre-labeled cryovials to minimize freeze-thaw cycles. These aliquots were then transported on dry ice to the central laboratory, where they were stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis.

POPs assessment

Target POPs were quantified using the following deuterated and 13 C-labeled internal standards (ISs): triethyl-D15-phosphate (TEP-D15), triphenyl phosphate-D15 (TPHP-D15), tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate-D12 (TCEP-D12), 3,4,4’,5-Tetrachlorobiphenyl-13C12 (PCB81-13C12), and 2,3,3’,4,4’,5,5’-Heptachlorobiphenyl-13C12 (PCB189-13C12). The complete panel of 30 target POPs are detailed in Additional file 1: Table S3. The sample preparation protocol was adapted and optimized from established methods [30]. Briefly, 400 µL plasma was aliquoted into a 15 mL polypropylene tube, spiked with ISs, and vortex-mixed. Liquid-liquid extraction was performed using 4 mL ethyl acetate under vigorous vortexing and orbital shaking. After centrifugation, the organic phase was transferred to a clean tube. The extraction was repeated with an additional 3 mL ethyl acetate. The combined organic phases were then concentrated under nitrogen, reconstituted in ethyl acetate, and transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Following high-speed centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for analysis. Target analytes were separated and quantified using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS; Agilent 8890-7000D, USA) equipped with a HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm). The injection volume was 1 µL. Optimal multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions and limits of detection (LODs) are detailed in Additional file 1: Table S3.

B vitamin assessment

We measured seven B vitamins: three VB6 congeners [pyridoxamine (PM), pyridoxine (PN), pyridoxal (PL)], FA, 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF), 5-formyltetrahydrofolate (5-FTHF), and VB12, using methotrexate as the IS. Total folate (TFA) in plasma was measured as the sum of FA, 5-MTHF, and 5-FTHF. Plasma samples (50 µL) were mixed with IS and dithiothreitol, followed by protein precipitation using a methanol-acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) solution containing ascorbic and citric acids. After vortexing and centrifugation, the supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen, reconstituted, and analyzed via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS; Agilent 6495, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a ZORBAX RRHD StableBond Aq column (3.0 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The injection volume was 10 µL. Instrument parameters and LODs are provided in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Quality control and assurance

To ensure data reliability, stringent quality control measures were implemented across all analytical batches. For both POPs and B vitamins, calibration curves prepared in surrogate matrices demonstrated excellent linearity (R² > 0.998). Instrument stability was monitored by interspersing quality control samples at regular intervals. Analytical reproducibility was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) derived from a two-way random-effects model (consistency type, average unit), with ICC values > 0.8 indicating high repeatability. Compounds with detection rates < 70% were excluded, and non-detected values were imputed as LOD/√2 to minimize bias.

Covariates

The covariates included in the final adjustment were selected via a systematic process that integrated prior epidemiological literature 29 with directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). These covariates encompassed maternal age, gestational weeks at sample collection, sample collection district, maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index, parity, fetal sex, maternal educational level, history of gestational diabetes mellitus, alcohol consumption, and B-vitamin status.

Statistical analysis

Given the non-normal distribution of all continuous variables, as confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk tests, baseline characteristics were categorized and presented as frequencies with percentages. Exposure concentrations of POPs and B vitamins were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges, with between-group differences assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Additional analyses included Spearman correlation to assess: (1) interrelationships among different POPs compounds, (2) associations between POPs concentrations and gestational weeks at sample collection, and (3) interrelationships among different B vitamins.

For single-exposure analyses, unconditional logistic regression was employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of individual POP and B vitamin with CHD risk. Statistical analyses were conducted using three progressively adjusted models to evaluate POPs-CHD associations. Model 1 examined unadjusted associations, while model 2 incorporated adjustments for demographic and lifestyle factors. Model 3 extended these adjustments by additionally including B-vitamin levels that differed between groups. Meanwhile, model 1 and model 2 were applied to assess the associations between B vitamins and CHD risk. To explore the shape of the dose-response relationships, we assessed potential nonlinear associations between B vitamins and CHD risk using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression.

To address the complexities of mixed exposures, we utilized two complementary approaches: Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) and Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) [31, 32]. The BKMR analysis incorporated a hierarchical structure that grouped OPEs and PCBs as distinct clusters, with posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) used to identify the most influential mixture components. This analysis was run for 20,000 iterations to ensure model convergence. The WQS model constructs a weighted index to evaluate the combined impact of exposure variables on outcomes, quantifying each variable’s contribution to the overall effect. The WQS regression analysis employed bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) for uncertainty estimation.

Interaction analysis and stratified analysis

To assess both single and mixed interaction effects between POPs and B-vitamin levels on CHD risk, we employed a two-stage analytical approach. First, we evaluated multiplicative interactions by including cross-product terms (POP × B-vitamin) in logistic regression models. Second, for the evaluation of mixture interaction effects, we derived a weighted nutrient score (NS) based on the weights obtained from the WQS regression model that quantified the association between B-vitamin levels and CHD. This score, representing overall nutrient status, was then incorporated into a subsequent WQS regression model to assess its interaction with the POPs mixture. Effect estimates were expressed as ORs with 95% CIs. After identifying significant interaction effects, we conducted stratified analyses by median B-vitamin levels to further examine their modifying effects on the associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation) and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic profiles

The baseline characteristics of the included participants are presented in Additional file 1: Table S4. The median maternal age was 29.04 years, and 69.65% of the participants were from northern China. Most participants (93.88%) self-identified as Han Chinese, and plasma samples were collected at an average of 25.14 weeks of gestation. Compared to controls, cases exhibited a higher prevalence of alcohol consumption during pregnancy (54.10% vs. 47.52%). Approximately 80% of the women were multiparous, and male fetuses were more common than female fetuses (P < 0.0001). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in maternal occupation, education, prior pregnancy with a birth defect-affected fetus, pre-pregnancy body mass index, gestational diabetes mellitus, or periconceptional smoking between the groups.

The associations between plasma POPs and CHD risk

Among the 30 targeted POPs, 14 compounds were detected in over 70% of the samples (Additional file 1: Table S5). Four exhibited significant correlations with sample collection timing (Additional file 1: Table S6) and were excluded to mitigate temporal confounding. The remaining 10 POPs were retained for association analyses with the outcome. Measurements for POPs exhibited excellent reproducibility, as indicated by ICCs of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.95, 0.99). As shown in Fig. 1A, cases exhibited significantly higher levels of p-cresyl diphenyl phosphate (pCDPP) (median: 0.0141 vs. 0.0111 ng/mL) and 2,2’,3,4,4’,5’-Hexachlorobiphenyl (PCB138) (0.0107 vs. 0.0078 ng/mL) compared to controls. In contrast, controls had elevated concentrations of TPHP (medians: 0.44 ng/mL) relative to cases (0.30 ng/mL). No significant differences were observed in the median concentrations of the remaining POPs between the two groups. Spearman correlation analyses revealed significant intra-class associations among POPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

After adjusting for potential confounders, prenatal exposure to pCDPP was associated with an increased risk of CHD (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.61; Fig. 2A). This association remained significant after further adjustment for B-vitamin levels (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.08, 1.71; Fig. 2A). No other POPs showed statistically significant associations with CHD in the adjusted models.

The associations of POPs exposure with CHD risk

In the BKMR analysis, the exposure-response curve for the mixture exhibited a modest upward trend between the 25th and 50th percentiles before plateauing, though the overall association was non-significant (Fig. 2B). Complete PIPs are provided in Additional file 1: Table S7. In the WQS analysis, the POPs-derived index exhibited a positive association with CHD risk in the positive direction, with an OR of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.40) (Fig. 2C). Specifically, pCDPP dominated the positive component (weight = 0.53).

The associations between plasma B vitamins and CHD risk

The detection rates for all B vitamins were above 80% (Additional file 1: Table S5). As shown in Fig. 1B, controls had elevated concentrations of PN, PL, and VB12 (medians: 0.71, 4.56, and 0.29 ng/mL, respectively) relative to cases (0.61, 3.78, and 0.21 ng/mL). Plasma TFA concentrations were significantly elevated in cases compared to controls. No significant differences were observed in the median concentrations of the PM between the two groups. Spearman correlation analyses revealed significant intra-class associations within B vitamins (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

In the analysis examining the association between plasma B-vitamin levels and CHD risk, higher concentrations of PM (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.79), PL (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.90), and VB12 (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31, 0.70) demonstrated negative associations with CHD risk after adjusting for potential confounders (Fig. 3A). Dose-response analyses using the RCS model indicated a nonlinear relationship for VB12, while the associations for PM and PL were consistent with a linear model (P for nonlinearity > 0.05) (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

The associations of B-vitamin exposure with CHD risk

Mixture exposure analyses using BKMR showed a consistent decreasing exposure-response relationship between the 25th and 75th percentiles, with an OR of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.75, 0.98) when comparing the 75th percentile to the 50th percentile (Fig. 3B). VB12 exhibited the highest PIP (PIP = 0.999) in this analysis, indicating its predominant role in the observed protective association (Additional file 1: Table S8). These findings were further supported by WQS analysis, which revealed a significant negative association between the overall B-vitamin mixture and CHD (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.69, 0.87). Notably, VB12 again demonstrated the highest weight (weight = 0.58) in the negative effect direction (Fig. 3C).

Interaction and stratified analysis

In pairwise multiplicative interaction analyses, significant effect modification was observed between pCDPP and VB12 (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.35, 0.75). Similarly, a significant interaction was identified between 2,4’-Dichlorobiphenyl (PCB8) and VB12 (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.29, 0.91). In addition, 2,2’,4,5,5’-Pentachlorobiphenyl (PCB101) exhibited a significant interaction with PM, yielding an OR of 0.65 (95% CI: 0.45, 0.94). No significant interaction of PN, PL, and TFA was observed in the POPs-CHD association. The detail information is shown in Additional file 1: Table S9 and Figs. S5-6. In the mixture interaction analysis, we observed a significant negative interaction between the WQS index and NS (OR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.52).

In low PM-level group, both pCDPP (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.12, 2.68) and PCB101 (OR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.49, 4.25) exhibited significant associations with CHD risk. Mixture analyses revealed a consistent risk-enhancing trend (WQS: OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.35, 2.75), predominantly driven by pCDPP, though BKMR trends did not reach significance. In high PM-level group, the BKMR model displayed a non-significant decreasing trend, and WQS analysis showed a negative association (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.57, 0.94) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Among VB12 deficient individuals, pCDPP (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.25, 2.60) and PCB138 (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.07, 2.33) showed significant positive associations with CHD risk. The BKMR model indicated a monotonic exposure-response relationship with significant effects at lower exposure levels (25th percentiles), while WQS regression estimated an overall positive mixture effect (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.19, 2.00). Conversely, in VB12 sufficient participants, mixture analyses demonstrated inverse associations (WQS: OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.52, 0.91) (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

In participants with low B-vitamin levels (Fig. 4), logistic regression demonstrated significant positive associations of pCDPP (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.10, 2.23) with CHD risk. The BKMR model revealed a non-significant increasing trend between the 25th and 75th percentiles, while WQS regression yielded an OR of 1.50 (95% CI: 1.19, 1.89). pCDPP contributed most substantially to these mixture effects (PIP = 0.987, weight = 0.66). In contrast, among participants with high B-vitamin levels (Fig. 4), pCDPP showed no significant association. The BKMR-derived exposure-response curve displayed a non-significant decreasing trend, and WQS analysis showed a negative association (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.53, 0.86). Complete PIPs are provided in Additional file 1: Table S7.

Discussion

This multicenter case-control study demonstrates that prenatal exposure to certain POPs, particularly pCDPP, is associated with an increased risk of CHD in offspring. Importantly, our findings indicate that maternal B-vitamin status may modify these associations, with higher B-vitamin levels attenuating the cardiotoxic effects observed for the POPs mixture.

The environmental concentrations of POPs measured in our study were comparable to those reported in previous epidemiological investigations. Specifically, pCDPP levels in our cohort (75th percentile: 0.024 ng/mL) were comparable to levels reported in studies of gestational diabetes (0.02 ng/mL) [33], while median TNBP concentrations (0.24 ng/mL) were similar to those observed in Chinese populations assessing tri-OPE exposure (median: 0.17 ng/mL) [34]. Likewise, PCB138 concentrations (median: 8 pg/mL) aligned closely with values reported in studies of prenatal PCB exposure [35]. The consistency of these measurements across populations and study designs enhances confidence in our exposure data and supports the broader relevance of our findings.

We further observed that the association between prenatal pCDPP exposure and CHD was evident only among mothers with lower B-vitamin levels, whereas no significant association was detected in the higher B-vitamin group. This pattern suggests that adequate maternal B-vitamin status may mitigate pCDPP-related developmental cardiotoxicity. Meanwhile, joint exposure to POPs was associated with increased CHD risk specifically in the low B-vitamin levels group. These results provide epidemiological evidence that maternal nutritional status may play an important role in shaping susceptibility to chemical mixture exposures during fetal development. The observed association is biologically plausible in light of existing experimental evidence. As an emerging organophosphate ester flame retardant, pCDPP has been shown to disrupt cardiac development in zebrafish by altering the expression of key transcription factors, including BMP4, nkx2.5, tbx5, ultimately impairing heart tube formation [36]. Mammalian studies further show that related OPEs induce mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes, thereby activating apoptotic pathways and promoting cardiac fibrosis [37]. Similarly, other POPs like PCBs interfere with calcium homeostasis and cardiac morphogenetic pathways [38]. Experimental studies in zebrafish further demonstrate that PCB exposure can dysregulate multiple developmental pathways, including aryl hydrocarbon receptor, retinoic acid and Wnt signaling cascades [39]. Notably, POPs may alter DNA methylation patterns, leading to aberrant gene expression and increased risk of congenital anomalies [40]. This study underscores the imperative for targeted interventions to mitigate prenatal exposure to POPs, with a particular focus on the emerging OPEs characterized in the present work. Core strategies encompass the enhancement of biomonitoring for these specific contaminants in pregnant populations, the implementation of stringent regulatory frameworks governing their application in consumer products associated with indoor exposure, and the development of evidence-based, clear guidance to minimize exposure via dietary intake and dust ingestion pathways.

Although numerous epidemiological studies have evaluated the role of B vitamins in the etiology of CHD, their potential interaction with environmental toxicants has received little attention. Emerging evidence suggests that B vitamins may mitigate the cardiotoxic effects of POPs through multiple synergistic biological pathways. B vitamins (particularly vitamin B2, VB6, vitamin B9, and VB12) serve as critical cofactors in antioxidant defense systems and are involved in maintain redox homeostasis. By supporting the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase, adequate B-vitamin status may reduce POPs-induced reactive oxygen species production and limit oxidative damage to developing cardiomyocytes [41]. In addition, B vitamins play a central role in one-carbon metabolism, where they contribute to the regulation of DNA methylation processes that are critical for preserving appropriate gene expression during cardiac development [42, 43]. Disruption of these epigenetic mechanisms has been implicated in the pathogenesis of congenital anomalies. B vitamins also function as key cofactors in mitochondrial energy metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis. For example, VB6 is required for dopamine biosynthesis, and deficiencies in these pathways may exacerbate POPs-induced disturbances in cellular metabolism, cardiac electrophysiology, and contractile function [44]. Therefore, these mechanisms suggest that sufficient B-vitamin availability may enhance cellular resilience to POPs exposure by supporting antioxidant capacity, maintaining epigenetic stability, and preserving metabolic homeostasis during critical windows of cardiac development [43, 45]. The observed protective association of elevated maternal B-vitamin status highlights the importance of ensuring adequate B-vitamin intake during pregnancy, through diet or targeted supplementation, as a modifiable strategy to enhance fetal resilience against such disruptions. Further research is needed to establish causality and determine optimal intervention thresholds, particularly for women with heightened environmental exposure risks.

This study provides several important advances in understanding environmental influences on CHD. As the first study to simultaneously examine OPEs and PCBs in relation to CHD risk while assessing the modifying effects of B vitamins, this study provides novel insights through comprehensive single- and mixture-exposure analyses. Methodologically, our study represents a significant improvement over prior work by simultaneously quantifying thirty POPs, exceeding the scope of previous detection methods. Furthermore, we measured plasma B-vitamin concentrations rather than relying on the information of supplement use or dietary estimates, providing more precise nutritional status assessment. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample collection time did not precisely align with cardiac teratogenic susceptibility windows, despite our rigorous analysis excluding compounds showing temporal concentration variations. For persistent pollutants such as PCBs, which have long elimination half-lives (ranging from several years to decades), their concentrations in late pregnancy are likely representative of stable internal exposure throughout gestation [46]. Regarding OPEs, which have relatively shorter half-lives, their continuous environmental emission renders them “pseudo-persistent” pollutants [47, 48]. Furthermore, the study population resided in a relatively stable living environment, and dietary habits—a major exposure pathway for OPEs—tend to remain consistent across pregnancy [49]. Therefore, we speculate that PCB and OPE levels measured in late pregnancy may, to some extent, reflect sustained exposure during early pregnancy. Second, the exposure assessment in this study was limited in scope, with POPs comprised of OPEs and PCBs, and B vitamins restricted to TFA, VB6, and VB12. Future studies should expand the spectrum to include a wider array of contaminants and vitamins to assess the generalizability of the observed interactions. Third, plasma TFA concentrations were significantly higher in the case group than in the control group in this study. This difference may partially reflect changes in maternal supplement use following fetal diagnosis, which could have influenced circulating TFA levels [28]. Fourth, although statistical power was sufficient for primary analyses, it remained constrained for secondary examinations. Consequently, the stratified estimates should be regarded as preliminary and require prospective verification in studies with larger sample sizes. Last, while our mechanistic interpretations are grounded in established literature, they would benefit from direct experimental validation through animal models or cardiac organoid systems in subsequent research. These findings lay important groundwork for future prospective cohort studies designed to better capture critical exposure windows and expanded environmental contaminant profiles.

Conclusions

This study provides the first human evidence that prenatal exposure to POPs is associated with increased CHD risk in offspring, with maternal B-vitamin status appearing to modify this relationship. These findings underscore the public health relevance of monitoring prenatal exposure to emerging contaminants such as pCDPP and highlight the potential importance of maternal nutritional status in modifying environmental risk factors for CHD.
