Understanding eating disorders in French undergraduate students: gender gaps, lifestyle factors, and barriers to care
Authors: Elisa De Taevernier, Rodeline Telfils, Noémie Leveque-Morlais, Jeanne Pfister, Annabelle Yon, Manon Couvreur, Marie-Pierre Tavolacci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUniversity students are at increased risk of eating disorders (EDs) due to academic pressure, financial strain, and social stressors.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to estimate ED prevalence among undergraduate students in France and identify associated factors, including gender identity, socioeconomic hardship, field of study, lifestyle behaviors, psychiatric comorbidities, and healthcare avoidance.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among undergraduates students. ED diagnosis was based on self-report. Multivariable logistic regression models assessed associations between ED and sociodemographic, health-related, and behavioral factors, adjusting for age, sex, and field of study.ResultsAmong 2,678 respondents (75.2% women; mean age 20.2 ± 1.6 years), 6.7% (95% CI 5.7–7.6) reported ED: 7.7% of women, 2.8% of men, and 35.2% of non-binary students (p < 0.01). ED was strongly associated with non-binary identity, obesity, negative body image, financial difficulties, poor self-rated health, psychiatric disorders, and recent suicide attempts. Behavioral correlates included daily lunch skipping, poor sleep quality, and frequent substance use. Students with ED more often avoided healthcare, with 40.5% avoiding general practitioners and 51.7% psychiatrists versus 15.2% and 15.7% among peers without ED (p < 0.001). Main reasons included financial barriers, fear of seeking care, and absence of a regular physician.ConclusionsEDs affect a substantial proportion of undergraduate students, with marked vulnerability among non-binary individuals. The findings highlight interactions between socioeconomic stress, body image, behavioral factors, and psychiatric comorbidity. Frequent healthcare avoidance underscores the need for accessible, inclusive, and stigma-free prevention and care strategies within universities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40519-026-01871-3.Keywords: Eating disorders, University student, Body image, Mental health, Socioeconomic factors

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are severe psychiatric conditions characterized by persistent disturbances in eating behaviors, body image distortions, and excessive preoccupation with weight and shape [1]. They include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge-eating disorder, each defined by distinct diagnostic criteria but sharing significant adverse consequences for physical and mental health [2]. Beyond profound psychological distress, EDs are associated with a wide range of medical complications, such as cardiovascular dysfunction, electrolyte imbalances, and gastrointestinal disorders, and represent one of the psychiatric conditions with the highest mortality rates [3, 4]. EDs most often emerge during adolescence and young adulthood, stages of life marked by critical developmental, psychological, and social transitions [5–7]. University students are therefore a population at particular risk. International studies indicate that 10–50% of students display disordered eating behaviors or symptoms suggestive of an ED [8, 9]. Multiple factors make university students particularly vulnerable to ED, including academic pressure, financial insecurity, social and emotional transitions, and disruptions to daily routines [10–12]. In addition, social media and peer influences often promote unrealistic body ideals and dieting behaviors, exacerbating body dissatisfaction and increasing the risk of disordered eating [13, 14].

More recent studies, however, point to a broader and more complex array of determinants. Gender minority status has emerged as a significant yet understudied risk factor, reflecting the impact of minority stress and stigma [15, 16]. Socioeconomic disadvantage also contributes to EDs by increasing food insecurity, chronic stress, and limited access to healthcare [17–19]. In addition, barriers to healthcare and tendencies toward care avoidance, despite high health needs, may delay diagnosis and treatment, further exacerbating risk [20].

Lifestyle factors, including substance use, poor sleep, meal skipping, and restrictive dieting, are increasingly recognized as associated with disordered eating, yet remain insufficiently explored in student populations [21–23]. The COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted students’ vulnerability by disrupting daily routines through lockdowns, social restrictions, and remote learning, while simultaneously limiting access to support networks and increasing financial and psychosocial stressors with increase in both the incidence and severity of EDs during the pandemic [9, 24]. These conditions were linked to higher levels of anxiety, depressive symptoms, food insecurity, and maladaptive coping strategies, all of which are associated with disordered eating [25, 26].

In France, most research on EDs in higher education has focused on healthcare or medical students, who are considered at higher risk due to academic pressures and exposure to health-related knowledge [27]. Evidence on the broader undergraduate population remains limited, and little is known about how sociodemographic characteristics, health perceptions, lifestyle behaviors, psychiatric comorbidities, and patterns of healthcare use interact to shape vulnerability to ED. The first 3 years of university represent a critical period marked by multiple transitions, uncertainties, and adjustments, rendering students particularly vulnerable to ED. Changes in environment, increased autonomy in meal management, irregular daily routines, and academic pressures can all challenge psychological well-being and health behaviors [28, 29]. Focusing on this period allows for the identification of emerging risk trajectories and the development of targeted prevention strategies at a time when interventions are likely to be most effective. Body image concerns, unhealthy eating patterns, and restrictive dieting may place specific subgroups at heightened risk. By examining these intersecting factors within a diverse student population, this study seeks to fill a critical knowledge gap, enhance early identification of at-risk individuals, and inform the development of targeted prevention and support strategies—making it a timely and essential contribution to understanding and addressing ED in higher education.

The aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of eating disorders among undergraduate students in France and to identify associated risk factors, with a particular focus on gender identity, field of study, socioeconomic conditions, health perceptions, lifestyle habits, comorbidities, and healthcare avoidance.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study using an online questionnaire (see Supplementary Material 1) was conducted in 2022 at the University of Normandy, France. Specifically, eligible undergraduate students received email invitations sent directly by the universities to their institutional email addresses. Up to five reminder emails were subsequently sent to maximize participation. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential; completion took approximately 15–30 min. The study population included volunteer undergraduate students enrolled in their first, second, or third year of study. Students in professional programs or advanced degree programs were excluded to ensure a focus on those in the early stages of their academic careers. Fields of study were grouped into five categories: Healthcare, Sciences and techniques, Humanities and Social Sciences, Law and economics, and multiple study programs.

Measures

Sociodemographic factors

The questionnaire collected detailed information on sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, nationality, level and field of study, job status, and financial difficulties.

Weight-related data

Health-related variables included self-reported height and weight, which were used to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI) according to the standard formula (weight [kg]/height [m2]) and classified following WHO guidelines. Body image perception was assessed using a single item in which participants rated their perception of their own body size (too thin, a little too thin, normal, a little too fat, too fat).

Lifestyle behaviors

Lifestyle and behavioral variables included dietary habits, assessed through: quality of diet (very healthy, healthy enough, not healthy enough, not healthy at all); frequency of skipping lunch (never/rarely, sometimes [1–3 times per week], almost every day [4–6 times per week], every day); and adherence to specific diets (vegetarian, vegan, pescetarian). Sleep quality was evaluated using a single self-reported item with five response categories: very good, good, fairly good, poor, and very poor. Substance use was also assessed, including tobacco and cannabis (never, occasional or frequent (once a week or more) and alcohol consumption explored through binge drinking episodes in the past month (none, 1–2 episodes, 3 or more episodes), with binge drinking defined as the consumption of five or more alcoholic drinks in about 2 h [30].

Health status

Eating disorders were identified using the question: “Do you have an illness or health problem that recurs or persists over time?” with one of the response options specifically referring to eating disorders (anorexia, bulimia, and binge-eating disorder). Students also self-reported their perceived health status (very good, good, fairly good, poor, very poor) and any chronic digestive diseases. Psychiatric variables included self-reported diagnosed psychiatric conditions (such as depression or anxiety) and suicide attempts within the previous 12 months.

Forgoing healthcare

Students were asked whether they had forgone seeing a general practitioner (GP) or a psychiatrist in the past 12 months, as well as the reasons for forgoing care from a GP: lack of time, non-urgent GP visit, financial difficulties, appointment’s delay, self-medication, GP too far from home, fear of seeking medical care, and lack of regular GP. The perceived impact of forgoing healthcare on mental and physical health, academic performance, and social life was also collected.

Statistical analysis

Given that the sample was not fully representative of the overall student population, all variables were weighted to adjust for potential biases related to sex, age, level of study, and university. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages with 95% confidence intervals, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. To examine factors associated with eating disorders, multivariable logistic regression models were performed, adjusting for age, gender, and field of study. Associations are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the robustness of the observed associations, a sensitivity analysis was performed using an extended multivariable logistic regression model. This model simultaneously included financial difficulties alongside lunch skipping frequency, diet quality, and healthcare avoidance (forgone GP and psychiatric consultations), with adjustment for gender, age, and field of study.

Ethics

Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Completion and submission of the online questionnaire were considered implied informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the French Ethics Committee for the Protection of Persons (Comité de Protection des Personnes, Île-de-France) (reference: 21.04361.000045-22002), in accordance with the French Jardé Law (Category 3) governing research involving human participants. University agreements were also obtained. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sociodemographic factors

In this study, 6.7% (95% CI [5.7–7.6]) of university students reported an ED. The prevalence was 7.7% (95% CI [6.4–9.0]) among women, 2.8% (95% CI [1.8–3.8]) among men, and 35.2% (95% CI [24.1–46.3]) among non-binary students (p < 0.01) (Table 1). Multivariable analysis showed that eating disorders were significantly associated with gender. Female students had an elevated risk, and the association was particularly strong among non-binary students (aOR = 18.8; 95% CI [9.5–33.6]). Older age, studying outside healthcare fields, and financial hardship were also linked to higher ED risk (Table 2).

Weight-related factors

Obesity and negative body image were both significantly associated with ED, with perceiving oneself as “too fat” showing the strongest association (aOR = 13.6; 95% CI [8.0–33.2]) (Table 2).

Lifestyle behaviors

Several lifestyle behaviors were independently associated with increased ED risk: frequent tobacco and cannabis use, binge drinking (three or more episodes per month), poor sleep quality, and skipping lunch on a daily basis (Table 2).

Health status

Poor self-rated health, psychiatric disorders, and chronic digestive diseases were all independently linked to higher ED risk. Students reporting a suicide attempt in the previous 12 months had a markedly increased risk (aOR = 5.8; 95% CI 3.3–10.3).

Forgoing healthcare

Regarding healthcare forgoing, 40.5% of students with ED reported not consulting a GP and 51.7% reported not consulting a psychiatrist, compared with 15.2% and 15.7% among students without ED, respectively (both p < 0.001) (Table 1). The reasons for forgoing GP visits that differed significantly between students with and without ED were financial difficulties, fear of seeking medical care, and lack of regular GP (Fig. 2). The consequences of forgoing healthcare were most frequently reported on mental health and were significantly greater among students with ED than among those without, regardless of the type of consequence (Fig. 3).

Patterns of healthcare avoidance were also significant. Students who had forgone consultations with a general practitioner had higher odds of ED (aOR = 3.4; 95% CI 2.3–4.9). Furthermore, both forgoing GP and psychiatric consultations were significantly associated with ED (Table 2).

In the additional multivariable model that included financial difficulties (Supplementary Material 3), skipping lunch remained strongly associated with eating disorders in a dose–response manner (aOR = 1.81–5.24; all p < 0.05). Forgoing psychiatric care (aOR = 3.22; 95% CI 2.12–4.90; p < 0.001) and GP consultations (aOR = 2.12; 95% CI 1.36–3.32; p < 0.001) also remained independently associated. Diet quality and financial difficulties were no longer significant after mutual adjustment (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the prevalence, associated factors, and healthcare behaviors related to ED among French university students. The overall prevalence of ED was 6.7%, with marked differences by gender: 7.7% among women, 2.8% among men, and 35.2% among non-binary students. These estimates are based on self-reported ED diagnoses which may explain their lower values compared to studies using standardized screening tools such as SCOFF. We used self-reported diagnosed eating disorders rather than a screening tool such as the SCOFF to focus on recognized cases and better study healthcare use and care pathways; however, this approach may underestimate prevalence, as self-reporting requires prior recognition or diagnosis, whereas screening tools also capture undiagnosed individuals with suggestive symptoms [31].

While previous research has consistently reported higher ED prevalence in women, this study highlights the particularly high vulnerability of non-binary students, a population often underrepresented in epidemiological studies. Prior studies have reported similar patterns, highlighting the disproportionate burden of ED among gender minority groups, likely explained by minority stress, stigma, and body image [32]. By quantifying this risk, our findings underscore the urgent need for targeted support and inclusive interventions for gender-diverse students, adding empirical weight to calls from recent literature.

Regarding the field of study, students outside of healthcare disciplines were more likely to report ED. Previous research has often focused on healthcare students, especially medical students, due to their known vulnerability. The higher risk observed among non-health students may reflect lower health literacy, reduced access to supportive resources, differing social pressures regarding body image, and exposure to distinct psychosocial stressors [33, 34]. However, this apparent difference could also be explained by underreporting by health students, due to denial, fear of stigma, or lack of recognition of symptoms [35].

The strong association with financial difficulties underscores the role of socioeconomic stress in ED risk. Economic hardship has been consistently linked to poorer mental health outcomes, including disordered eating behaviors, likely through increased stress, food insecurity, and reduced access to care [19, 36]. In the supplementary multivariate model, financial difficulties were no longer independently associated with eating disorders after adjusting for skipping lunch and avoiding healthcare, suggesting that these behavioral and structural factors may play a partial mediating role in the relationship between socioeconomic difficulties and eating disorders; however, formal mediation analyses would be necessary to confirm this hypothesis.

Our findings highlight substantial body image misperception, with a large proportion of normal-weight students perceiving themselves as overweight and nearly half of underweight students misclassifying their weight status, while body image perception was markedly associated with higher risk among students perceiving themselves as “too fat” or “too thin.” Recent studies have consistently shown that body image perception is strongly associated with eating disorder symptoms and may represent a key determinant beyond objective weight status [37, 38]. These findings emphasize that interventions should focus not only on students with overweight or obesity but also on those whose self-perception deviates from reality [39, 40].

Our findings also highlight lifestyle factors with substantial impact. Daily lunch skipping emerged as one of the strongest correlates, pointing to a simple and observable marker of vulnerability. Meal skipping has been linked to weight gain and obesity in prior studies among university students [41], but its role in ED remains poorly documented. Behavioral factors also played a significant role. Substance use—including tobacco, alcohol, and cannabis—was associated with ED, echoing previous studies that have emphasized the co-occurrence of disordered eating and substance use, possibly reflecting shared vulnerability factors such as impulsivity or emotion regulation difficulties [12, 42, 43].

The presence of psychiatric or digestive comorbidities was strongly associated, in line with the well-established bidirectional links between ED and both physical and mental health conditions [44, 45]. Emotional problems, suicidality, and depressive symptoms were identified as the most important factors associated with general help-seeking [46].

Finally, indicators of psychological vulnerability such as suicide attempts were strongly associated with ED. These findings reinforce the high psychiatric comorbidity and elevated suicide risk among individuals with ED [47]. Moreover, the association with healthcare avoidance highlights a paradox: despite their elevated health risks, students with disordered eating may be less likely to seek professional help, reflecting stigma, limited awareness, or perceived barriers to care [46, 48]. These findings suggest that students with ED face compounded barriers to healthcare access, which may exacerbate both physical and mental health outcomes. Fear of stigmatization or judgment, financial difficulties, and absence of a primary care contact may prevent early detection and treatment of ED, potentially contributing to worsening symptoms and academic impairment. While previous work has described barriers to healthcare access in university populations, our results quantify the magnitude of avoidance specifically in students with ED and identify associated reasons—financial constraints, fear of seeking care, and lack of a regular GP. This paradox, whereby students with heightened health needs are simultaneously less likely to seek care, has received little attention in the literature and underscores structural barriers that may delay diagnosis and treatment [49].

Our results are consistent with previous literature highlighting that ED patients often underutilize healthcare services despite experiencing more severe consequences, and that financial and structural barriers, as well as psychological factors, play a key role in delayed or avoided care. Interventions targeting these barriers—such as providing accessible, low-cost, and non-stigmatizing care, along with education about the importance of regular medical follow-up—may reduce disease progression and improve outcomes for students with ED.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides one of the few large-scale assessments of ED among undergraduate students in France, addressing a major gap in national data. The inclusion of a wide range of study fields allowed comparison across disciplines, extending research traditionally limited to healthcare students. The large sample size (over 2,600 participants) and weighting procedure to account for sex, age, level of study, and university composition enhanced representativeness and reduced selection bias. The questionnaire covered diverse domains, sociodemographic, health-related, behavioral, and psychosocial, allowing a multidimensional analysis of risk factors. The simultaneous examination of healthcare avoidance added an important and underexplored perspective, linking mental health needs and access to care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, limiting conclusions about the direction of associations between risk factors and ED. The use of self-reported data may introduce reporting biases, especially diagnosis of ED. Because ED was identified through self-declared diagnoses rather than validated screening tools, the prevalence may be underestimated. In addition, the sample was drawn from a single university consortium and included undergraduate students only, excluding those enrolled in advanced degree programs. Findings should therefore be interpreted as generalizable to undergraduate students rather than to the entire French student population.

Conclusion

Eating disorders affect a substantial proportion of French university students, with heightened vulnerability among non-binary students and those outside health-related fields, shaped by socioeconomic stress, body image misperception, behavioral factors, and psychiatric comorbidities. Despite elevated health needs, affected students often avoid care due to stigma, financial constraints, or lack of a regular GP, highlighting the urgent need for accessible, inclusive, and low-stigma interventions. Future research should examine longitudinal trajectories and mechanisms linking psychosocial, behavioral, and economic factors to ED. Interventions should address structural and psychological barriers, enhance health literacy, and provide accessible, non-stigmatizing care, particularly for gender-diverse and non-health-related student populations, to reduce the burden of ED and improve overall well-being.
