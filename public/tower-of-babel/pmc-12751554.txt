Glucotypes in patients with type 2 diabetes and either diabetic kidney disease or end stage kidney disease under different anti-diabetic treatments
Authors: Yu-Yun Chou, Feng-Hsuan Liu, Chia-Hung Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe profile of glycemic changes in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) remains unclear. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate glucotypes in patients with type 2 diabetes and end stage kidney disease (ESKD) and those with DKD through continuous glucose monitoring (CGM).MethodsWe analyzed ambulatory glucose profiles. The primary outcome was time in range (TIR) and the secondary outcomes included hypoglycemia and glucose variability.ResultsTwenty-one patients with ESKD and 42 patients with DKD were enrolled after matching for age and gender. There was no significant difference in TIR between the ESKD and DKD groups. However, patients with ESKD exhibited higher standard deviation (SD), and nocturnal glucose levels compared to those with DKD. For different dialysis treatments, patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) had higher continuous overlapping net glycemic action (CONGA), J-Index, high blood glucose index (HBGI), glycemic risk assessment in diabetes equation (GRADE), and M-value than those undergoing HD. Additionally, higher all day glycemic values were observed in the PD group. Patients with ESKD on premixed insulin therapy had higher standard deviation of sensor glucose across 24 h and lower nocturnal glucose levels compared with those on Multiple daily injection (MDI).ConclusionsPatients with ESKD exhibited higher glucose variability and nocturnal glucose levels compared with those with DKD. Patients receiving PD showed higher glycemic variability and glucose levels compared with those undergoing HD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02108-7.

Background

The global rise in the number of individuals with type 2 diabetes has been dramatic. In 2022, an estimated 828 million adults had diabetes, marking a rise of 630 million since 1990, with 85–95% of these cases being type 2 diabetes [1]. Diabetes is the primary cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), accounting for up to 89.7% of dialysis cases. It leads to diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and progression to ESKD, necessitating dialysis or kidney transplantation, and is a leading cause of ESKD in the United States [2]. The coexistence of type 2 diabetes and DKD necessitates meticulous glucose management to prevent further complications. As DKD progresses to ESKD, patients often require dialysis, which further complicates glucose control.

Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels are commonly used to assess long-term glycemic control, reflecting average glucose levels over the past 2–3 months. However, the accuracy of HbA1c levels is limited in patients with DKD or ESKD due to factors such as altered red blood cell turnover and anemia [3]. Patients undergoing dialysis have erythrocytes with a reduced life span; hence, they often use erythropoiesis stimulating agents to treat nephrogenic anemia. These agents, while increasing erythropoiesis, increase the proportion of young non-glycated erythrocytes, resulting in an underestimation of the mean glycemia over the previous few months [4]. Consequently, HbA1c readings may be inaccurate, making them less reliable for managing diabetes in this population. Therefore, the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines highlight the inaccuracy of HbA1c levels and suggest the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) devices as an alternative [5].

Patients with ESKD have a high risk of hypoglycemia due to reduced renal gluconeogenesis, frequent use of insulin, and dysregulation of counterregulatory hormones [6, 7]. Moreover, dialysis significantly impacts glucose control. Peritoneal dialysis (PD), involving continuous treatment, also influences glucose levels due to the glucose containing dialysate [7].

Since the 1990s, landmark studies like the UK Prospective Diabetes Study [8] (UKPDS) and Diabetes Control and Complications Trial [9] (DCCT) have demonstrated that maintaining adequate glycemic control significantly reduces the risk of cardiovascular complications in patients with diabetes. Additionally, high glucose variability has been linked to increased risks of cardiovascular events, hypoglycemia, and mortality. The Action in Diabetes and Vascular Disease: Preterax and Diamicron MR Controlled Evaluation trial highlighted the association between glucose variability and both macrovascular and microvascular events, as well as increased mortality risk [10]. Similarly, the Veterans Affairs Diabetes Trial showed that variability in fasting glucose contributes to cardiovascular disease complications beyond standard fasting glucose levels [11]. Moreover, long-term changes and variability in HbA1c levels are independently associated with heart failure risk in patients with type 2 diabetes [12].

CGM provides continuous data on glucose levels, offering insights into daily fluctuations and trends. Unlike HbA1c measurements, CGM captures real-time glucose variability, crucial for managing diabetes in patients with DKD or ESKD. In adults with poorly-controlled type 2 diabetes, CGM leads to significantly lower HbA1c levels and improves glucose control compared with regular blood glucose monitoring (BGM) [13–15]. The broader adoption of CGM in individuals with type 1 diabetes is expected to decrease diabetes-related complications and mortality while maintaining an acceptable cost-effectiveness ratio [16]. As a transformative tool in diabetes management, CGM offers a cost-effective approach that can significantly improve glycemic outcomes and enhance the quality of life for people with diabetes [17, 18]. Recent studies have also applied CGM in patients with advanced kidney disease, demonstrating its accuracy in hemodialysis populations and distinct glycemic profiles in peritoneal dialysis patients [19, 20]. In patients with DKD or ESKD, glucose fluctuations and risk of hypoglycemia are deeply concerning. Detailed glucose monitoring is especially crucial for those taking insulin. Hence, identifying specific glucotypes through CGM may enable more targeted interventions, thereby reducing the risk of complications related to glucose variability. Few studies have investigated the profile of glycemic changes in diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Therefore, we aimed to evaluate glucotypes in patients with type 2 diabetes and ESKD undergoing dialysis and those with DKD not undergoing dialysis through CGM.

Methods

Participants

Study participants were eligible for inclusion if they were over 18 years of age, clinically diagnosed with type 2 diabetes for more than 1 year, and undergoing treatment with either oral anti-diabetes drugs or insulin injections. Patients were categorized into two groups according to renal function. Those with DKD were defined as type 2 diabetes with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of at least 15 ml/min/1.73 m² and not undergoing dialysis. In contrast, patients with ESKD were defined as type 2 diabetes requiring maintenance dialysis therapy, including either hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis. Among 204 enrolled patients, 42 with DKD and 21 with ESKD were selected through age- and gender-matching to ensure comparability between groups.

The exclusion criteria included: known allergy to sensor adhesive, extensive skin lesions or scars that hindered sensor adhesion, infection or edema at the sensor application sites, and type 1 diabetes. All the participants had attended structured diabetes education and treatment programs. Prior to enrollment, participants were informed about the study both verbally and in writing, and provided written informed consent. Individuals who used CGM but did not provide data were excluded from the analysis. The recruitment period spanned from October 10, 2018, to November 15, 2024.

Clinical data on body mass index (BMI), HbA1c levels, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and standardized CGM metrics were collected from all participants.

Study design

This case-control study was conducted at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Linkou, Taiwan. We used the Medtronic CGM system to continuously measure glucose concentrations every 5 min from interstitial glucose levels, with a detection range of 40–400 mg/dL. The monitoring period lasted up 7 days, and calibration was performed at least every 12 h using capillary blood glucose samples. Patients received instructions on how to use the device, including calibration and management of sensor alarms. Blood samples were collected at the end of each CGM period.

From the CGM profiles, we calculated various metrics, including: mean sensor glucose values; glucose management indicator (GMI); time in range (TIR, 70–180 mg/dL); time above range (TAR, > 180 mg/dL); time below range (TBR, < 70 mg/dL); and glycemic variability, measured as the standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV) and mean amplitude of glycemic excursion (MAGE).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was TIR, defined as the percentage of time that glucose levels were within the target range of 70–180 mg/dL. Secondary outcomes included changes in nocturnal hypoglycemic events (0000 h to 0600 h), TBR, and the low blood glucose index (LBGI) [21] as a risk indicator for severe hypoglycemia. Other inter-group differences analyzed included HbA1c levels, mean sensor glucose levels, TAR, and various measures of glycemic variability accessed by SD, CV, M-value [22], MAGE [23], average daily risk ratio (ADRR) [24], lability index (LI) [25], J-Index [26], high blood glucose index (HBGI) [21], continuous overlapping net glycemic action (CONGA) [27], glycemic risk assessment in diabetes equation (GRADE) [28], and mean absolute glucose (MAG) [29]. The index formulas are reported in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and CGM parameters are summarized using descriptive statistics. Results are presented as medians with 25th percentile, 75th percentile (Q1, Q3), depending on the distribution of the data. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, and the median difference with 95% confidence interval was estimated using the Hodges–Lehmann estimator. Additionally, separate analyses were performed to compare CGM data between patients with ESKD undergoing PD and hemodialysis (HD). Furthermore, for patients undergoing HD, we compared CGM data between dialysis days and non dialysis days, ensuring that the number of days compared was the same for both conditions. CGM data across different medication regimens within patient groups were also compared, including multiple daily injection (MDI) insulin therapy versus premixed insulin therapy.

To visualize the CGM data, we created glucose charts for each category: DKD vs. ESKD, PD vs. HD, HD day vs. non HD day, and different medication regimens. These charts provided a graphical representation of the glucose trends and variability among the different groups. For the statistical analysis of these longitudinal and repeated measures data, we used Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE). The GEE approach was employed to account for the correlation between repeated measurements within the same subject, providing robust standard errors. This method was appropriate for our study as it accommodated the non-normal distribution of CGM data and allowed for the analysis of time dependent covariates. The GEE model was also used to analyze the impact of different medication regimens on glycemic control within the patient groups.

Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for MAC version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Participant characteristics and baseline data

Among total 63 participants, 21 had ESKD and were on dialysis, while 42 with DKD not undergoing dialysis were matched by age and gender. All 63 participants had their mean sensor glucose values, GMI, TIR (70–180 mg/dL), TAR (> 180 mg/dL), TBR (< 70 mg/dL), SD, and CV analyzed. However, two patients on dialysis were excluded from the analysis of glycemic variability and glucose charts due to incomplete 24-h CGM data.

Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The 63 participants had a median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) age of 65.8 (58.6, 69.1) years, a median BMI of 26.0 (24.0, 30.2) kg/m², and a median HbA1c level of 11.7 (10.2, 14.0)%. 34 (54.0%) of the participants were women. The glucose-lowering treatments primarily included metformin (36.5%) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (25.4%). Among those under insulin therapy, 34.9% were on MDI and 27.0% were on premixed insulin. Compared to patients with DKD, those with ESKD had a longer duration of diabetes (P = 0.002), lower hemoglobin levels (P < 0.001), and lower HbA1c levels (P = 0.023).

Regarding secondary glycemic outcomes, the risk indicator for severe hypoglycemia, TBR, showed no difference between participants in the DKD group, with a TBR of 0.0 (0.0, 3.0)%, and the ESKD group, with a TBR of 1.0 (0.0, 2.0)% (P = 0.730). However, ESKD groups had higher glucose variability, measured by the SD (56.0 [43.0, 66.0] vs. 75.0 [61.0, 81.0], respectively, P = 0.008) (Table 2). Other glycemic variability indices did not differ significantly between groups (Additional file 1: Table S2). Moreover, time-based glucose profile analysis revealed that the dialysis group had significantly higher nocturnal glucose levels (P < 0.001) and greater standard deviation of sensor glucose across 24 h (P = 0.042), as shown in Fig. 1A and B.

Comparison of glycemic metrics between PD and HD subgroups

In the dialysis group, the TIR was similar between patients undergoing HD (52.0 [22.5, 63.0]%) and PD (28.0 [24.0, 33.5]%) for among those with ESKD (P = 0.303). However, patients undergoing PD had a significantly higher CONGA (15.0 [13.5, 15.2] vs. 9.5 [7.7, 11.9], respectively, P = 0.023), J-Index (114.2 [107.1, 119.9] vs. 65.9 [44.2, 90.0], respectively, P = 0.008), HBGI (30.0 [26.3, 30.7] vs. 15.6 [9.7, 20.1], respectively, P = 0.014), GRADE (22.6 [19.2, 22.7] vs. 8.3 [5.8, 15.1], respectively, P = 0.033) and M-value (55.9 [46.5, 58.8] vs. 21.0 [10.9, 33.1], respectively, P = 0.014) than those undergoing HD (Additional file 1: Table S3). Additionally, the PD group exhibited higher all day glycemic levels (P < 0.001) and nocturnal glucose levels (P < 0.001) compared with the HD group, according to glucose chart (Fig. 1C and D). Both indicated that patients undergoing PD had a significantly higher risk of hyperglycemia and glycemic variability compared with those undergoing HD.

Glycemic changes between HD and Non HD days

In the HD group, TIR was similar between HD and non HD days, with TIR values of 51.0 (29.5, 71.0)% for HD days and 35.0 (15.0, 54.0)% for non HD days (P = 0.289). Furthermore, glucose variability was comparable between HD and non HD days, measured by the CV (28.9 [21.6, 42.9]% for HD days vs. 25.4 [19.1, 41.1]% for non HD days, P = 0.581) and MAGE (7.6 [5.9, 8.8] for HD days vs. 7.6 [4.9, 9.3] for non-HD days, P = 0.927) (Additional file 1: Table S4). Hence, no significant difference was found in TIR and glycemic variability between HD and non HD days among patients undergoing HD. Additionally, TBR (0.0 [0.0, 0.0]% for HD days vs. 0.0 [0.0, 0.0]% for non HD days, P = 0.968) and the LBGI (0.6 [0.0, 1.7] for HD days vs. 0.5 [0.0, 1.4] for non HD days, P = 0.947) were also similar. However, nocturnal glucose levels were significantly lower on HD days compared with non HD days (P < 0.001) as indicated by the glucose charts (Fig. 1F). Moreover, patients undergoing HD exhibited higher all day glucose levels on HD days compared to non-HD days (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E).

Impact of insulin regimens on glycemic control

Analysis of medication effects showed no difference in TIR between MDI insulin therapy and premixed insulin therapy (54.0 [24.0, 66.0] vs. 56.0 [32.0, 69.5], respectively; P = 0.705) in the DKD group. Glucose variability, as measured by the CV, was similar between MDI insulin therapy and premixed insulin therapy (31.5 [25.5, 37.5] vs. 28.6 [26.0, 40.2], respectively; P = 0.705) in the DKD group (Additional file 1: Table S5).

Additionally, no difference in TIR was observed between MDI insulin therapy and premixed insulin therapy (39.0 [23.3, 57.0] vs. 54.0 [42.5, 71.8], respectively; P = 0.181) in the ESKD group. Glucose variability, as measured by the CV, was also similar between MDI insulin therapy and premixed insulin therapy (38.9 [30.2, 46.7] vs. 37.0 [28.5, 44.6], respectively; P = 0.713) in the ESKD group (Additional file 1: Table S6).

According to the glucose charts, patients with DKD on premixed insulin therapy showed no difference in standard deviation of sensor glucose across 24 h (P = 0.689) but had significantly lower nocturnal glucose levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A and B) compared with those on MDI insulin therapy. Meanwhile, patients with ESKD on premixed insulin therapy had higher standard deviation of sensor glucose across 24 h (P < 0.001) and lower nocturnal glucose levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C and D) than those on MDI insulin therapy.

Discussion

Limitations of HbA1c levels in patients on Dialysis

This study compared CGM metrics between patients with ESKD undergoing dialysis and those with DKD not receiving dialysis. No significant differences were observed in primary glycemic outcomes, including TIR and mean sensor glucose levels, between the two groups. However, glucose variability, as indicated by SD and time-based glucose profile analysis, was greater in ESKD patients than in those with DKD. Notably, baseline HbA1c levels were significantly lower in the ESKD group. Our CGM data quantitatively confirm that HbA1c underestimates actual glycemic exposure in dialysis patients. Despite lower HbA1c levels, patients with ESKD exhibited comparable mean glucose but markedly greater glucose variability compared with those with DKD. This discordance between HbA1c and CGM-derived metrics underscores the limited reliability of HbA1c for assessing glycemic control in ESKD. This phenomenon is likely related to altered erythrocyte turnover and the effects of erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy in dialysis patients, which shorten erythrocyte lifespan and reduce glycation time. Together, our results provide quantitative evidence supporting this mechanism and strengthen the rationale for using CGM as a complementary assessment tool in this population. Our results support the KDIGO guidelines, which recommend using alternative glucose monitoring methods like CGM in patients with ESKD [30, 31].

Glucose variability and nocturnal hypoglycemia

One notable finding of this study is the lower nocturnal glucose levels and higher all day glucose levels observed on HD days compared to non HD days. This variability may stem from several factors inherent to dialysis. The intermittent nature of HD can lead to significant fluctuations in glucose levels, potentially resulting in post dialysis hypoglycemia [32]. This highlights the challenges of maintaining stable glucose control in this patient population.

Additionally, the differences in glucose variability and hyperglycemia risk between HD and PD patients are significant. Our findings revealed that patients on PD experienced a higher risk of hyperglycemia and greater glucose variability compared to those on HD. This may be attributed to the use of glucose containing dialysates in PD, where the continuous infusion of glucose can lead to sustained hyperglycemia. In contrast, the intermittent nature of HD likely explains the relatively lower glucose levels observed in HD patients. Although these modality-specific glycemic patterns have been documented previously, our study provides updated, quantitative confirmation using multiple CGM-derived indices. This multidimensional CGM analysis strengthens the existing literature and underscores the clinical importance of individualized glucose management strategies tailored to dialysis modality.

Glycemic outcomes according to medication regimens

The study also examined the impact of different medication regimens on glycemic outcomes, particularly comparing MDI insulin therapy with premixed insulin therapy. No significant differences in TIR were observed between these regimens within the DKD or ESKD groups. However, glucose charts with time parameter analysis revealed that patients with ESKD on premixed insulin therapy exhibited greater standard deviation of sensor glucose across 24 h and lower nocturnal glucose levels compared to those on MDI insulin therapy. These observations, while consistent with prior reports, provide practical insights into glucose management in ESKD patients, indicating that more intensive regimens like MDI may better manage glucose fluctuations and reduce the risk of nocturnal hypoglycemia, whereas premixed regimens may be less optimal in the context of dialysis-related variability. Given the high risk of glucose fluctuations in patients on dialysis, particularly during nocturnal hours, these findings highlight the importance of tailoring insulin therapy to dialysis modality, timing, and individual glucose variability profiles.

These results are consistent with previous studies, such as the UKPDS [8] and DCCT [9], which highlight the importance of tight glycemic control and the risks associated with glucose variability. High glucose variability has been linked to increased risks of cardiovascular events, hypoglycemia, and mortality, particularly in vulnerable populations like those with DKD and ESKD [33]. Our study demonstrates the value of CGM for monitoring glucose variability and guiding individualized insulin therapy in patients with ESKD, reinforcing its clinical utility. Implications for clinical practice

Our findings have several important implications for clinical practice. First, CGM should be considered a valuable tool in managing diabetes in patients with ESKD as it offers monitoring of glucose levels and variability, which is crucial in a population prone to both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia. Moreover, HbA1c levels alone are insufficient for assessing glycemic control in these patients, and reliance on this measure may underestimate the true extent of glycemic dysregulation.

Second, this study highlights the need for individualized treatment strategies based on the type of dialysis and medication regimen. For example, patients undergoing PD may require more aggressive management of hyperglycemia due to the continuous glucose infusion from dialysates, whereas patients undergoing HD may benefit from strategies aimed at minimizing post dialysis hypoglycemia. Additionally, the choice of insulin regimen should be tailored to the patient’s dialysis schedule and glucose variability profile, with MDI regimens potentially offering better glycemic control than simpler regimens like premixed insulin.

Finally, our findings suggest that nocturnal glucose monitoring should be a key focus in patients with ESKD, particularly on HD days, as this population is at high risk for nocturnal hypoglycemia.

Limitations and future research

This study has a few limitations. First, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly within the ESKD subgroup, which may reduce statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, the retrospective design of our study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between dialysis type, medication regimens, and glycemic outcomes. In addition, potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors—such as comorbidities, dialysis duration, residual renal function, and concurrent medications—may have influenced the observed associations. Future research should focus on larger, prospective studies to confirm these findings and explore the long term impact of CGM guided therapy on clinical outcomes such as cardiovascular events, kidney function decline, and mortality in patients with ESKD.

This study highlights the distinct glycemic challenges faced by patients with DKD and ESKD. While TIR and mean glucose levels were similar between the groups, patients with ESKD—particularly on HD days—exhibited greater glucose variability, underscoring the limitations of HbA1c in this population. Dialysis modality significantly influenced glycemic patterns, with PD associated with higher glucose levels and variability, likely due to the glucose content of the dialysate. Additionally, the use of premixed insulin was linked to increased glucose fluctuations, indicating a higher risk of hypoglycemia. These findings support the need for individualized glucose management strategies tailored to dialysis modality, insulin regimen, and daily glycemic patterns.

Conclusions

In summary, patients with ESKD exhibited greater glycemic variability and higher nocturnal glucose levels compared to those with DKD, especially among those receiving peritoneal dialysis or premixed insulin therapy. These distinct glycemic patterns highlight the limitations of HbA1c and emphasize the clinical value of CGM in optimizing individualized glucose management for patients with advanced diabetic kidney disease.
