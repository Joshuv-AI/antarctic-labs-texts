Use of emergency medical services by adults with developmental disabilities receiving integrated or usual care
Authors: Daniel Gilmore, Emily Johnson, Lauren Wang, Corey Keeton, Aurora Rivendale, Susan M. Havercamp, Lindy L. Weaver, Ingrid Adams, Brittany N. Hand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Adults with developmental disabilities (DD) frequently utilize the emergency department (ED) and are hospitalized for psychiatric emergencies. Community healthcare models like integrated care where primary care and mental health services are co-located may better meet the needs of adults with DD and reduce use of emergency services.Objective:To determine odds of all-cause, psychiatric, and non-psychiatric ED use and hospitalization among adults with DD receiving integrated care relative to usual care.Methods:We analyzed inpatient and outpatient encounters in the electronic health record from 2022 to 2023 from an integrated and usual care clinic. Adults with DD were identified using ICD-10 codes, and HCUP Clinical Classification Software was used to identify psychiatric hospitalizations and psychiatric ED visits. Logistic regression was used to determine odds of study outcomes among patients receiving integrated versus usual care, controlling for demographic characteristics.Results:N = 6706 adults with DD were included. Relative to usual care, individuals receiving integrated care had 50% lower odds of all-cause ED use (OR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.44 – 0.57), and 38% lower odds of all-cause hospitalization (OR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.52 – 0.74). Odds of psychiatric ED use and hospitalization were 72% (OR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.17 – 0.44) and 65% (OR = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.19 – 0.65) lower for adults with DD receiving integrated care.Conclusions:Integrated care was associated with lower odds of ED use and hospitalization for adults with DD and may be a promising model of service delivery. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the effect of integrated care on use of emergency services for this population.

Introduction

Adults with developmental disabilities (DD) have complex physical and mental health status, and like other populations, sometimes experience physical and mental health emergencies that require emergency department (ED) services or hospitalization. Evidence indicates, however, that adults with DD utilize these services more frequently than non-disabled adults. For example, adults with DD use the ED at approximately twice the rate of adults without DD,1,2 and are approximately 3-11 times or more likely to be hospitalized for psychiatric reasons than adults without DD.3,4 More complex health status relative to adults without DD5,6 may, in part, explain this pattern as more complex health status may reasonably result in higher emergency service utilization. However, the growing body of literature on healthcare access and healthcare experiences for adults with DD suggests that high use of ED and hospital services is likely a modifiable consequence of inaccessible or ineffective community healthcare and social determinants of health for adults with DD,7-10 rather than an unmodifiable higher need for these services imposed by complex health status, intractable health conditions, or particular susceptibility to health emergencies.

Effective treatment of acute and chronic health concerns via community healthcare services is suggestive of healthcare quality.11,12 However, utilization of emergency services for non-emergent health concerns or for decompensated health concerns ineffectively managed through community healthcare services is common.13 One plausible way to promote effective treatment of health concerns in the community, particularly for adults with DD, is adoption of community healthcare delivery models that promote access to primary care and mental health services.14 Integrated care, where primary care and mental health services are co-located in one care setting, is one such model that adults with DD have recently championed as a top priority for increasing their access to physical and mental health services.15 Integrated care has been associated with positive outcomes (e.g., symptom reduction) among various patient populations with complex health status, including children with DD.16-18 However, how receipt of integrated care is associated with utilization of the ED and hospitalizations has not been examined for adults with DD.

In this study, we determined associations between receipt of integrated care and utilization of ED services and hospitalizations among adults with DD using electronic health record (EHR) data. Understanding how receipt of integrated care is associated with utilization of ED services and hospitalizations for adults with DD is important to gauge effectiveness of primary care and mental health services delivered via this model, and the potential for integrated care to promote receipt of care in care settings appropriate to needs for adults with DD.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study to compare utilization of the ED and hospitalizations for any reason and for psychiatric reasons among adults with DD at an integrated care clinic called the Freeman Center (FC) relative to those at a usual care clinic called the Mood Disorder Clinic (MDC). Both clinics specialize in care for adults with DD and are affiliated with the same university-based hospital system. The FC offers integrated care, where primary care and mental health providers (behavioral health and psychiatry) are co-located in the clinic and collaborate to manage patients’ physical and mental health. A more detailed description of the FC is provided in Smith et al.19 At the MDC, patients receive psychiatric care only one day per week, and may receive primary care services elsewhere in the community.

Data source

We obtained data for this study from the Center for Health Informatics at the University of Cincinnati Medical Center. Data were de-identified, individual-level encounters and included inpatient and outpatient encounters from any location or provider within the healthcare system from January 1, 2022-December 31, 2023.

Sample identification

We included all patients with DD at the FC who were 18-85 years old in the analysis, including patients receiving care coordination services only. Because some DD diagnoses qualify a patient for the MDC but not FC (e.g., attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder), we only included MDC patients (18+) years old with DD diagnoses that would qualify them for the FC. To identify qualifying diagnoses for MDC patients, we used International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes and are included in Table 1.

Variable definitions

We extracted demographic information for included individuals from the date of their first observed encounter. The primary independent variable was status as a FC or MDC patient. The dependent variables were the presence of at least one all-cause ED visit, all-cause hospitalization, psychiatric-specific ED visit, psychiatric hospitalization, non-psychiatric ED visit, and non-psychiatric hospitalization. Psychiatric ED visits and hospitalizations were identified with the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) Clinical Classification Software (CCS)20 and were defined as encounters where the primary diagnose(s) associated with the encounter were for a mental health condition or neurodevelopmental disorder as specified by the CCS.

Statistical analysis

To summarize demographic variables we used descriptive statistics, with categorical variables presented with frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables presented with means and standard deviations. Logistic regression was used to compare groups on the odds of all-cause ED use, all-cause hospitalization, and psychiatric and non-psychiatric ED use and hospitalization. Covariates included age, sex, intellectual disability, insurance type, and residence in Hamilton County, where the FC and MDC are located. To examine how ED and hospitalization outcomes differed by autism status and age, we conducted sensitivity analyses among autistic and non-autistic subgroups and when including age categories as a covariate. To understand the effects of race/ethnicity on study outcomes, we also ran the regression models including these demographic variables.

Ethical approval

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at our institution reviewed this study and determined it to be IRB-exempt (Protocol Number: 2023B0326).

Results

A total of 6706 adults with DD were included in the analysis. N = 5311 (79.2%) adults with DD were MDC patients, and N = 1395 (20.8%) were FC patients. Demographic characteristics of included individuals are provided in Table 2. Most beneficiaries were male, white, and the average age of the sample was 37.5 years old. Autism (34.3%) and Other unspecified ID (38%) were the most common DD diagnoses, and approximately 48% of adults with DD had an ID. Approximately 53% of adults with DD receiving usual care, and 70% of those receiving integrated care, lived in Hamilton County.

Relative to MDC patients, FC patients had significantly lower odds of all-cause and psychiatric and non-psychiatric ED visits and hospitalizations (Table 3). Odds ratios are presented in a forest plot in Fig. 1. Appendix 1 includes a regression table with model fit statistics.

Sensitivity analysis

With the exception of psychiatric ED and psychiatric hospitalization outcomes, with increasing age category there was a nearly monotonic increase in odds of study outcomes. Odds of ED and hospitalization outcomes were significantly lower in subgroups of autistic and non-autistic adults receiving integrated care relative to usual care. ED and hospitalization outcomes remained lower for adults with DD receiving integrated care when controlling for race and ethnicity (Appendix 2).

Discussion

Understanding utilization of emergency medical services like the ED and hospitalization is important to evaluating the effectiveness of services delivered via community healthcare services. In conventional (usual) care delivery models where primary care and community mental health services are disaggregated, adults with DD have typically been found to be hospitalized and use the ED as much or more often than non-disabled adults,2,3 suggesting that this care model may not be optimal to meet their physical and mental health needs. An integrated care model where primary care and mental health services are co-located is an alternative to usual care that is recommended by self-advocates in the DD community to promote their physical and mental health.15 In the present study, we measured utilization of the ED and hospitalization among adults with DD under integrated care relative to utilization of these services among adults with DD receiving usual care.

Our findings indicated that adults with DD receiving integrated care had significantly lower odds of all-cause and non-psychiatric ED use and hospitalization than adults with DD receiving usual care. Lower odds of these outcomes at the integrated care clinic which is smaller than the usual care clinic aligns with some evidence of associations of clinic size with service utilization outcomes.21 Regarding all-cause ED use, the integrated care model is not specifically designed for the purpose of reducing ED visits. However, features of its specific implementation at the FC parallel some characteristics of programs that reduce ED use among patient populations with high physical and mental health needs. Thus, reductions in all-cause ED use, as opposed to solely psychiatric-ED use, are plausible despite the emphasis of integrated care on access to mental health services.22 Case management and care coordination services, for example, have been shown to be more effective in ED visit reduction programs relative to other features.23-25 Case managers and care coordinators support patients in various aspects of healthcare navigation and accessing needed care to avoid care gaps and decompensated health conditions. Similar roles are fulfilled by the integrated care manager and clinical nurse coordinator at the FC, which may help explain our findings. It is also possible that lower odds of all-cause ED use may be related to fewer emergencies like physical injury among FC patients. While many injuries (e.g., accidents) occur regardless of healthcare services received or health status, injury for adults with DD may in some cases be self-injury and related to physical or mental health,26 and thus amenable to access to co-located primary care and mental health services under integrated care.

Additionally, less ED use among FC patients may be related to access to a primary care provider (PCP). Access to a PCP has been thought to be important to achieving reductions in ED visits across the health system for decades. Availability of primary care is not certainly protective against ED use,27-32 but it is vital to community health, and has been shown to be more strongly associated with reductions in ED use among adults with DD than among non-disabled adults.33 Further, adults with DD have high rates of health conditions sensitive to ambulatory care,34 suggesting that ED use for this patient population may be particularly responsive to PCP access, as such conditions are ideally managed in these settings. All FC patients in this study were established patients with a FC primary care provider (PCP) for at least 1 year, and while MDC clinic patients were also established for at least 1 year (psychiatry only), some may not have a PCP. We did not collect data on duration of care among MDC patients with a PCP, but the potential impact of differences in PCP access and utilization between groups is important to consider.

Lower odds of all-cause hospitalization among patients receiving integrated care is likely an artifact of these patients having fewer ED visits and fewer psychiatric hospitalizations. Proportions of hospital admissions for adults with DD for psychiatric reasons are relatively uncertain, estimated between 3% and 36% based on chart review.35,36 In our study, the proportion of admissions for psychiatric reasons for both groups was lower than these figures. However, the proportion of psychiatric admissions for usual care patients (2.1%) was more than double that of patients receiving integrated care (0.9%), which suggests that lower odds of all-cause hospitalization was, in part, driven by fewer psychiatric admissions among patients receiving integrated care. Thus, integrated care may have potential to facilitate effective community mental healthcare for adults with DD.

Relative to odds of all-cause ED visits and hospitalizations, lower odds of psychiatric ED use and psychiatric hospitalization for patients receiving integrated care were more pronounced. Similar to all-cause outcomes, we cannot determine explanatory factors for this finding. However, these findings do suggest that integrated care may be particularly effective at managing this population's mental health conditions, aligning with evidence supporting the collaborative care model for other populations on which the integrated care model at the FC is based.37,38 Acute psychiatric issues requiring ED visits or hospitalization are often related to adverse medication events39 (e.g., sedation, delirium), which may be less likely in the setting of provider-to-provider consultation and collaboration under integrated care. Psychiatric emergencies related to suicidality and suicidal ideation are also common for adults with DD, which may be mitigated with regular psychiatric care and behavioral health services.40,41

Per sensitivity analyses, increasing age was consistently associated with increasing odds of study outcomes, but this pattern was less clear for psychiatric outcomes which warrants future study. Subgroup analyses were in alignment with the primary analysis, as adults with DD receiving integrated care had significantly lower odds of ED use and hospitalization regardless of autism status, or when controlling for race and ethnicity. Low event rates particularly for psychiatric ED visits and hospitalizations limit the interpretability of these findings, and analyses of healthcare service utilization among DD subgroups should be studied further.

Limitations

We would like to acknowledge several limitations to this study. Unmeasured confounding variables make determination of causal factors for observed differences in healthcare service use exceedingly difficult to identify even with intricate study designs. Though we did control for some possible confounding variables in our cross-sectional analysis such as ID,42 we cannot comment on integrated care as a causal factor for our findings. Adults with DD may self-select into different care types, so those who receive integrated care may be different from those who do not. Understanding these differences via qualitative methods and considering them in experimental study designs where possible may be important in future work. Additionally, only two clinics were included in this study, which precluded our ability to adjust for intra-group correlations. We were unable to match clinic patients on mental health factors (e.g., symptom severity), demographic characteristics, or legal guardianship. It is possible that observed differences in emergency services were due, in part, to differences in mental health needs between FC and MDC patients. However, degree of mental health needs does not exclude patients from either clinic, which increases our confidence that observed differences in service use were largely related to receipt of integrated care. The possibility of coding errors is an issue inherent to EHR data, and psychiatric ED visits and hospitalizations could have been classified via different methods besides the categorization scheme employed by the HCUP CCS.

Conclusion

Community healthcare delivery models designed to meet the physical and mental health needs of adults with DD are important to identify and evaluate to promote this population's health and wellbeing. Integrated care was associated with lower odds of ED use and hospitalization for adults with DD and therefore may be a promising model of service delivery for this population. Future studies are needed to more directly measure the effect of integrated care on emergency service use for adults with DD.
