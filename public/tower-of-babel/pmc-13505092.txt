Sensory processing profiles and their association with tic severity in children with tic disorders: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yajun Tang, Duan Lin, Yanting Lu, Jiqiang Xie, Qinyu Li, Xiumei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSensory symptoms have been increasingly recognized as core characteristics of tic disorders (TD). This study aimed to systematically investigate the core features of sensory processing abnormalities in Chinese children with tic disorders, analyze their association with tic severity, and explore potential subtypes based on sensory profiles.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 151 children diagnosed with TD (encompassing Tourette syndrome, chronic motor tic disorders, and provisional tic disorders) along with 100 age-matched healthy controls (HC). Sensory processing characteristics were assessed using the Short Sensory Profile, and tic severity was evaluated with the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale. Principal component analysis (PCA) and latent profile analysis (LPA) were employed to explore sensory subtypes. Correlation analyses and regression models were used to examine sensory-symptom relationships.ResultsChildren with TD showed significantly lower total SSP scores than HCs (P < 0.001), with deficits primarily in “under-responsive/sensation seeking”, “auditory filtering” and “low energy/weakness”. PCA extracted two core dimensions: “sensory dysfunction” and “sensory hypersensitivity”. A significant positive graded relationship was found between the degree of sensory abnormality and tic severity (P for trend = 0.025). The “under-responsive/ sensation seeking” dimension was an independent predictor of tic severity (β = -0.164, P = 0.044). LPA identified two subtypes: a “relatively typical sensory processing” subtype (88.7%) and a “sensory over-responsivity” subtype (11.3%). However, no significant difference in tic severity was found between these subtypes.ConclusionsSensory processing abnormalities, particularly generalized sensory modulation dysfunction, are prevalent across TD subtypes and are closely associated with tic severity. Sensory under-responsivity represents a core risk dimension, while the unique “sensory over-responsivity” subtype may be relatively independent of tic severity. This study provides important evidence for sensory-based subtyping of TD and for developing individualized interventions targeting sensory symptoms.Keywords: Tic disorders, Sensory under-responsive, Sensory dysregulation, Sensory hypersensitivity, Latent profile analysis, Child

Introduction

Tic Disorders (TD) represents a neurodevelopmental condition predominantly emerging during childhood and adolescence, characterized by the presence of sudden, rapid, repetitive, and non-rhythmic motor and/or vocal tics. Clinically, TD is categorized into three distinct subtypes based on symptom characteristics and duration: Provisional Tic Disorders (PTD), Chronic Motor or Vocal Tic Disorders (CTD), and Tourette Syndrome (TS) [1]. Epidemiological data from international studies estimate the global prevalence of TD at approximately 2.5%, with TS specifically affecting about 1% of children and adolescents [2]. A 2022 Chinese epidemiological investigation corroborated these findings, reporting an overall TD prevalence of 2.5% in pediatric populations, with specific prevalence rates of 1.2% for PTD, 0.9% for CTD, and 0.4% for TS [3]. Contemporary research has established that TD exhibits phenotypic complexity extending beyond motor and vocal manifestations, encompassing significant non-motor components. Notably, approximately 80% of TD patients report abnormal sensory experiences across multiple modalities, including heightened sensitivity to specific sensory stimuli (e.g., tactile sensations from clothing tags, light, and specific sounds) [4], as well as the presence of premonitory urges [5]. These sensory abnormalities not only contribute to heightened anxiety and irritability, leading to emotional dysregulation in affected individuals, but also substantially impair their cognitive functioning, social interactions, and overall quality of life [6–8]. Recent studies have demonstrated that interoceptive processing, particularly the perception of heartbeat, is associated with premonitory urge intensity, suggesting that sensory phenomena may be intrinsically linked to specific physiological processes underlying tic disorders [9].

Current research delineates the sensory phenomena in TD patients into two primary categories: premonitory urges and multimodal sensory hypersensitivity. Premonitory urges are defined as the progressive accumulation of “internal tension” preceding motor or vocal tics, often manifested as localized bodily sensations such as tightness, itchiness, or pressure, or as psychological discomfort characterized by a sense of incompleteness or feelings that something is “not just right”, which is transiently alleviated by the execution of tics [9]. This phenomenon is intrinsically linked to sensory regulation dysfunction [10]. Premonitory urges occur transiently in conjunction with tics, whereas sensory hypersensitivity represents a persistent, amplified perception of both internal and external stimuli. TD patients demonstrate significantly elevated sensitivity across tactile, visual, and auditory modalities, with frequent self-reports of heightened sensitivity to stimuli such as clothing tags, collars, sunlight, and television screen brightness. Empirical studies utilizing standardized questionnaires further indicate increased sensitivity in gustatory and olfactory domains among TD patients [4, 11, 12]. Additionally, some patients exhibit sensory cravings (e.g., for deep pressure stimuli such as hugging or massage) or engage in behaviors aimed at mitigating discomfort through intense tactile stimulation (e.g., skin scratching). The broader concept of sensory modulation disorder (SMD) or sensory dysregulation encompasses these sensory phenomena, including but not limited to sensory hypersensitivity [8, 13]. This framework indicates that sensory problems in TD represent a complex, multidimensional spectrum.

While studies conducted in Chinese cohorts have predominantly examined the clinical manifestations of premonitory urges and their correlations with tic characteristics and comorbid conditions, fundamental aspects of sensory processing features and their clinical implications remain underexplored [14]. Considering the potential influence of cultural context, educational approaches, and sociocultural environment on individual sensory expression and behavioral patterns, region-specific data from China are essential for developing targeted assessment protocols and intervention strategies [15]. Furthermore, current research exhibits several notable limitations. Primarily, investigations have predominantly focused on TS populations, with insufficient attention to the sensory profiles of other TD subtypes, thereby impeding a comprehensive understanding of sensory abnormality distribution patterns across the TD spectrum. Additionally, the quantitative relationship between distinct dimensions of sensory abnormalities and tic severity remains inadequately characterized. Although preliminary studies have identified correlations between these variables [16], robust evidence supporting a graded relationship is lacking, and multivariate analyses have yet to identify the core sensory dimensions that most effectively predict tic symptom severity.

Addressing the identified research gaps, this study employs a cross-sectional design to systematically investigate sensory processing abnormalities in Chinese pediatric populations with TD and their quantitative associations with tic severity. Utilizing the Short Sensory Profile (SSP) as a standardized assessment tool, the research will quantitatively evaluate multidimensional sensory characteristics across different TD subtypes, and to delineate subtype-specific sensory abnormality patterns. The study will implement quantitative association analyses to examine the graded relationship between sensory sensitivity indices and Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) scores. Furthermore, multiple regression modeling will be conducted to identify independent sensory predictors of tic severity. The anticipated outcomes will provide comprehensive clinical data regarding sensory comorbidities in Chinese children with TD, while elucidating the quantitative relationships between sensory processing dysfunction and tic symptomatology. These findings are expected to contribute substantially to the understanding of TD pathophysiology and inform the development of evidence-based assessment protocols and targeted sensory-focused intervention strategies.

Participants and methods

Participants

A total of 151 children with TD were recruited from the Developmental Behavioral Pediatrics Clinic at Fujian Children’s Hospital between January 2025 and June 2025. Inclusion criteria: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for TD as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5); (2) absence of regular use of medications specifically indicated for tic management (such as aripiprazole, tiapride, clonidine transdermal patches, etc.). Exclusion criteria: (1) a history or current diagnosis of neurological disorders (such as intracranial infections, epilepsy, etc.); (2) other conditions potentially associated with neurological metabolic abnormalities (such as hepatolenticular degeneration, etc.); (3) significant organic lesions in the head or body; (4) participation in other clinical drug trials; (5) accompaniment by individuals other than the primary caregiver during the visit; (6) primary caregivers who demonstrated difficulty understanding the majority of the scale items. A total of 100 age- and gender-matched children who underwent routine health check-ups at the same clinic during the corresponding period were selected as the healthy control group (HC).

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Children’s Hospital (Ethics number: 2022ETKLR10036). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants prior to their inclusion in the research. All clinical interview procedures were conducted by qualified physicians with specialized clinical expertise in TD.

Methods

Participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, or their legal guardians, completed the baseline data collection. Children diagnosed with TD underwent assessment using the YGTSS, while guardians of TD children completed the evaluation using the SSP. Similarly, guardians of children in the healthy control group also completed the SSP assessment to ensure comparative analysis.

Instruments

Basic information collection

Data Collection Methodology: A structured, investigator-developed questionnaire was employed to systematically gather demographic and clinical information pertaining to TD. The instrument encompassed two primary domains: (1) pediatric patient characteristics, including identification data (name, gender), chronological age, age at symptom onset, perinatal history, allergic predisposition, and comorbid conditions; (2) familial background, incorporating household composition, parental educational attainment, family history of TD, and neuropsychiatric disorders among first-degree relatives.

Sensory assessment

The SSP is a standardized instrument designed for sensory processing assessment. This caregiver-reported questionnaire consists of 38 items categorized into seven distinct domains: tactile sensitivity, taste/smell sensitivity, vestibular sensitivity, under-responsive/sensation seeking, auditory filtering, low energy/weakness, and visual/auditory sensitivity [17, 18]. The composite sensory score is derived from the summation of all subscale scores. Employing a 5-point Likert scale, the total score ranges from 38 (representing maximal sensory dysfunction) to 190 (indicating typical sensory processing), with lower scores corresponding to greater sensory impairment.

The “under-responsive/sensation seeking” domain reflects a pattern grounded in Dunn’s theoretical framework of sensory processing, which posits that individuals’ responses to sensory stimuli are determined by the interaction between neurological thresholds and self-regulation strategies [19]. Children with high neurological thresholds require more intense stimuli to register sensory input; this may manifest as passive under-responsivity (e.g., appearing disengaged or unaware of sensory input) while simultaneously seeking strong sensory experiences as a compensatory mechanism to achieve optimal arousal [20]. The “low energy/weakness” domain, as originally defined in the SSP development literature, captures a distinct pattern of “difficulty sustaining energy or activity level; appears fatigued, lethargic, or lacks endurance for daily tasks”. Children with elevated scores in this domain often exhibit low arousal, passivity, and difficulty maintaining engagement in daily activities, which may reflect underlying differences in central nervous system arousal [20].

The SSP captures broad sensory processing patterns, including under-responsivity, sensory seeking, and sensory sensitivity, and has been widely validated across neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), with established associations with daily functioning and behavioral outcomes [21, 22]. The Chinese version of the full-length Sensory Profile has demonstrated favorable psychometric properties, including robust internal consistency and reliability, within Chinese populations [23]. As the Short Sensory Profile (SSP) used in the present study is derived from this full-length version, its application in Chinese pediatric samples is empirically supported.

Assessment of tic severity

The YGTSS was employed to evaluate the severity of tics. The YGTSS is a semi-structured interview scale, currently the most widely used clinical assessment tool for TD symptom severity and the gold standard for evaluating tic disorders, demonstrating good reliability and validity [24]. It consists of three parts: a symptom checklist, a tic severity rating, and a single-item rating of overall tic-related impairment. The severity of tics is assessed based on five dimensions: frequency, intensity, number of symptoms, complexity, and impact on daily life. The corresponding five item scores are summed to obtain the motor tic score and the vocal tic severity score, each ranging from 0 to 25. The total tic severity score is the sum of the motor and vocal tic scores, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses of demographic and clinical characteristics in children with TD and healthy controls were conducted using SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)] were reported, with group differences evaluated through the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage [n (%)] and analyzed using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Ordinal data were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, while the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise comparisons, was applied for multiple-group analyses. Trend associations in ordinal categorical data were examined using the Cochran-Armitage trend test. Bivariate correlations were analyzed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify predictive factors, adjusting for potential confounders, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes were modeled using forward stepwise multiple linear regression. Key analytical procedures included: Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the seven subscales of the SSP to reduce dimensionality, followed by latent profile analysis (LPA) using the extracted component scores to determine the optimal latent class solution for identifying sensory processing subtypes. All tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

All participants underwent comprehensive evaluation using the YGTSS and completed the SSP assessment. A control group of 100 healthy individuals was included, all of whom completed the SSP assessment. Demographic analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between the patient and control groups in terms of gender distribution, age, or primary caregivers’ educational attainment (P > 0.05), as detailed in Table 1.

Abnormal characteristics of sensory processing in children with TD

To examine potential sensory processing abnormalities in children with TD, we conducted a comparative analysis using the SSP between the TD cohort (n = 151) and the HC group (n = 100). The distribution of total SSP scores and domain-specific scores was systematically evaluated for both groups. In the TD group, statistical analysis revealed that the total sensory sensitivity score and the “auditory filtering” domain scores followed a normal distribution (P > 0.05), whereas all other domain scores demonstrated non-normal distributions (P < 0.05). Conversely, in the control group, both the total SSP score, and all domain-specific scores exhibited non-normal distributions (P < 0.05).

Intergroup analysis revealed significant differences in sensory processing patterns between TD children and HC group. The Mann-Whitney U test demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the total SSP score among the TD group compared to controls (Z = -7.602, P < 0.001). In accordance with the SSP scoring system, wherein lower scores correspond to greater sensory processing abnormalities, these findings indicate that children with TD generally have more severe sensory processing dysfunction.

Children with TD demonstrate distinct sensory processing impairments across particular domains. To elucidate the distinct patterns of sensory processing abnormalities, we performed a comprehensive analysis of the sub-dimensions within the SSP. As detailed in Table 2, statistical analysis demonstrated that children in the TD cohort exhibited significant impairments across multiple core sensory domains: underresponsive/sensation seeking (Z = -8.285, P < 0.001), auditory filtering (Z = -10.426, P < 0.001), and low energy/weakness (Z = -3.398, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that, compared to neurotypical controls, children with TD manifest pronounced deficits characterized by delayed responses to sensory stimuli, heightened sensory-seeking behaviors, impaired ability to filter extraneous auditory information, and increased susceptibility to fatigue and physical weakness.

The correlation between atypical sensory processing profiles and the clinical severity of tic in children with TD

Trend correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between the severity of sensory abnormalities and tic manifestation in TD patients. Sensory abnormalities, as measured by the total SSP score, were stratified into three distinct categories: “normal” (n = 65, 43.0%), “suspicious” (n = 45, 29.8%), and “abnormal” (n = 41, 27.2%). Concurrently, tic severity, assessed using the YGTSS, was classified into “mild” and “moderate to severe” categories. The Cochran-Armitage trend test for linear association revealed a statistically significant graded relationship (χ²=5.057, P = 0.025), indicating that progressive deterioration in sensory sensitivity from “normal” to “suspicious” to “abnormal” was associated with an increasing proportion of patients exhibiting moderate to severe tics. Binary logistic regression analysis substantiated these findings, demonstrating that patients with “abnormal” sensory sensitivity had a 2.44-fold increased risk of developing moderate to severe tics compared to those with “normal” sensory sensitivity (OR = 2.44, 95% CI: 1.091–5.454, P = 0.03). Notably, no significant risk difference was observed between the “suspicious” and “normal” sensory sensitivity groups.

The multivariate linear regression model suggests that under-responsive/sensation seeking is an independent predictor of tic severity. To exclude confounding factors and identify core predictive variables, we conducted a multivariate linear regression analysis with the YGTSS total score as the dependent variable. After controlling for factors such as gender, age, and tic type, variables were screened using the forward method. Ultimately, only the “under-responsive/sensation seeking” dimension was included in the regression model, which was significant (β = -0.164, F = 4.140, P = 0.044). This indicates that the “under-responsive/sensation seeking” score can independently explain 2.1% of the variance in the YGTSS total score (adjusted R² = 0.021).

Identification of distinct subtypes in children with TD based on sensory processing profiling

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was implemented to identify potential subtypes characterized by distinct sensory processing profiles among TD children. Given the high intercorrelation among the seven dimensions of the SSP, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to reduce dimensionality, thereby mitigating model complexity and optimizing statistical power. The data demonstrated suitability for factor analysis, as evidenced by the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.736) and the significant result of Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.001). Two principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser criterion) were extracted, which collectively accounted for 58.618% of the total variance explained. This cumulative variance contribution rate falls within the acceptable range for behavioral sciences and effectively captures the underlying structure of the data.

Factor analysis (Table 4) identified two clinically significant dimensions of sensory processing. The first component, sensory dysregulation, demonstrated substantial factor loadings for under-responsive/sensation seeking (0.788), auditory filtering (0.777), visual/auditory sensitivity (0.774), and low energy/weakness (0.708). This component reflects a comprehensive pattern of dysregulation in arousal modulation and sensory filtering, characterized by elevated sensory thresholds (under-responsiveness) and associated energy deficits. The second component, sensory hypersensitivity, exhibited strong loadings specifically for tactile sensitivity (0.805) and taste/smell sensitivity (0.772), with minimal cross-loadings on other dimensions, indicating its distinct nature as an independent trait characterized by heightened sensitivity to tactile and chemosensory stimuli.

Identification and characterization of potential subtypes. Standardized scores for each participant on the two principal components were derived through regression analysis and subsequently employed as manifest variables in LPA to identify potential subtypes based on core sensory processing characteristics. The LPA results revealed two distinct latent categories (Fig. 2). Subtype 1, designated as “sensory over-responsivity” (n = 17, 11.3%), exhibited scores approximating the mean on the “sensory dysregulation” dimension (M = 0.038) but demonstrated significantly lower scores on the “sensory hypersensitivity” dimension (M = -2.131). This profile suggests that the primary deficit in this subgroup is characterized by heightened sensitivity to tactile, gustatory, and olfactory stimuli. Subtype 2, identified as “relatively typical sensory processing” (n = 134, 88.7%), displayed scores near the overall sample mean on both sensory processing dimensions (sensory dysregulation: M= -0.300; sensory hypersensitivity: M = 0.038), indicating that their sensory processing characteristics were comparable to the average levels observed in the TD children group within this study.

Comparison of tic severity across sensory processing subtypes in children with TD

To examine the potential association between tic severity and latent subtypes derived from sensory processing profiles, we conducted comparative analyses of YGTSS scores between the “sensory hypersensitivity” subtype and the “relatively typical sensory processing” subtype. As detailed in Table 5, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two sensory processing subtypes across YGTSS total scores, motor tic scores, phonic tic scores, or impairment scores (all P > 0.05), and all observed effect sizes were minimal (all |δ| < 0.15). Through latent profile analysis, this study identified a distinct TD subtype characterized by prominent tactile and taste/smell hypersensitivity features. However, our findings demonstrate that this specific “sensory hypersensitivity” phenotype does not exhibit a significant association with tic severity within our cohort. These results suggest that classification within the sensory hypersensitivity subtype does not serve as a predictive indicator of tic severity, implying that sensory processing patterns and tic severity may represent independent clinical dimensions in TD.

Discussion

This investigation conducted a systematic assessment of sensory processing characteristics in children with TD, elucidating the intricate patterns of sensory abnormalities and their correlations with tic symptomatology. The principal findings can be delineated into three key observations: Firstly, sensory processing abnormalities were highly prevalent among the pediatric TD cohort, predominantly manifesting in two core dimensions: generalized sensory dysregulation and specific tactile/gustatory/olfactory hypersensitivity. Secondly, significant heterogeneity was observed in sensory processing profiles among children with TD, enabling classification into distinct subtypes: a severe sensory hypersensitivity subtype (11.3%) and a relatively typical sensory processing subtype (88.7%). Thirdly, a quantifiable graded relationship was identified between the magnitude of sensory abnormalities and tic severity, with the score of “under-responsive/sensation seeking” dimension demonstrating an independent negative predictive value for overall tic severity, while the sensory subtype classification maintained relative independence from tic severity measures.

Core structure and potential mechanisms of sensory processing abnormalities in children with TD

This investigation systematically characterized sensory processing abnormalities in children with TD through comparative analysis of SSP scores between TD and HC cohorts. The TD group exhibited significantly lower total SSP scores compared to HC (P < 0.05), substantiating the prevalence of sensory processing anomalies in this population. Multidimensional analysis revealed non-uniform distribution of sensory abnormalities, with significant impairments specifically observed in the core domains of “under-responsive/sensation seeking"、 “auditory filtering,” and “low energy/weakness” (all P < 0.05). These findings align with previous neurophysiological investigations by Belluscio et al., which documented enhanced auditory perception for distant or faint sounds and preference for intense tactile stimulation in TS patients [25]. While olfactory, tactile, and auditory sensory thresholds in TD patients were observed to fall within normal ranges [25, 26], neurophysiological studies employing prepulse inhibition (PPI) of the startle reflex, a well-established measure of sensorimotor gating, have consistently demonstrated impaired gating in TD patients [27, 28].PPI reduction is thought to reflect dysfunction within cortico striato thalamo cortical (CSTC) circuits, which are critical for filtering sensory information and preventing overflow of irrelevant stimuli [29]. Together, these observations suggest that the sensory processing abnormalities observed in children with TD are likely attributable to central nervous system dysregulation, particularly involving impaired sensory gating mechanisms, rather than to peripheral sensory detection deficits.

However, these dimensions exhibit significant interrelationships, making it difficult to depict their intrinsic connections through a single dimension. To address this limitation, we implemented PCA to investigate the latent structure underlying these anomalous dimensions. The analysis effectively consolidated dimensions demonstrating significant intergroup disparities—specifically “under-responsive/sensation seeking”, “auditory filtering”, " low energy/weakness” and the associated “visual/auditory sensitivity”—into a unified principal component (component 1: Sensory Dysregulation). This statistical integration substantiates established clinical observations, indicating that these ostensibly distinct phenomena originate from a more fundamental, pervasive sensory regulation dysfunction. Concurrently, “tactile sensitivity” and “taste/olfactory sensitivity,” which did not exhibit significant intergroup differences, were collectively loaded onto an independent component (component 2: Sensory Hypersensitivity) in the PCA. This finding suggests that while these sensory modalities do not manifest as prominent features in the overall mean of the TD population, they represent a distinct dimension of sensory variation within this population, independent of sensory regulation disorder. This structural configuration demonstrates strong concordance with recent advancements in TD neurophysiological model research. The “sensorimotor integration dysfunction” hypothesis proposes that tics may result from impaired filtering and regulation of sensory signals within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit, thereby facilitating the involuntary expression of premonitory urges through tic movements [10]. Additionally, evidence indicates that the limbic components of the CSTC circuit, along with dopaminergic and GABAergic receptor systems in the striatum, are critically involved in the modulation of pre-pulse inhibition (PPI) [29].

The consistent observations across clinical subtypes (TS, CMTD and PTD) further reinforce the above view. This investigation demonstrates that distinct TD subtypes manifest comparable impairment profiles in sensory modulation dysfunction, exhibiting significant deviations from healthy controls while showing no inter-subtype variations. This compelling evidence suggests that sensory modulation dysfunction may represent a transdiagnostic core endophenotype within the TD spectrum, rather than being exclusive to TS. Consequently, this finding repositions sensory abnormalities from a “secondary phenomenon” associated with specific subtypes to a central component in elucidating the overarching pathophysiology of TD.

Consistent with previous findings by Smith et al. [12], our study demonstrated significant alterations in taste/smell sensitivity specifically in children with TS, whereas no such abnormalities were observed in other tic disorders subtypes (PTD or CMTD). These findings suggest that sensory processing abnormalities are not uniformly distributed across all TD subtypes, and that taste/smell sensitivity abnormalities may be a feature associated with more severe and complex tic phenotypes. In PTD or CMTD, the “abnormal load” on the nervous system may not initially reach a threshold sufficient to induce overt dysfunction in gustatory-olfactory pathways. Such sensory abnormalities typically become clinically detectable only upon disease progression or the emergence of more complex phenotypic manifestations. From a neurobiological standpoint, taste/smell processing is anatomically and functionally interconnected with limbic structures (e.g., the amygdala and insula), which constitute key neural substrates implicated in premonitory urges and emotional dysregulation within the pathophysiology of TS [10, 30]. Consequently, gustatory/olfactory hypersensitivity observed in pediatric TS patients may arise from atypical sensory modulation that affects the affective processing of sensory input, resulting in aberrant amplification of emotional salience attributed to specific taste and olfactory stimuli. Future prospective investigations should evaluate whether gustatory/olfactory hypersensitivity could serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the progression of PTD toward more chronic and phenotypically complex forms of TS.

The complex association between sensory symptom and tic severity

This study provides novel insights into the intricate association between sensory abnormalities and tic severity. Our analysis revealed a significant graded relationship, demonstrating that increased severity of sensory processing dysfunction correlates with elevated risk of moderate-to-severe tic manifestation (OR = 2.44). Similarly, Hendry et al. [16], who employed the Dunn Sensory Quadrant Model to analyze data from the SSP-2 and Adolescent/Adult Sensory Profile scales, establishing that heightened sensory dysregulation in children with CTD is predictive of more severe tic symptomatology. Notably, a substantial proportion (80%) of their sample presented with at least one co-occurring psychiatric condition, and further analysis revealed that increased severity of these comorbidities was significantly associated with dysregulated sensory sensitivity and avoidance profiles. These collective results emphasize the critical need for integrating comprehensive sensory assessments into standard clinical evaluations to facilitate accurate risk stratification and prognosis. Future studies should investigate the potential moderating role of psychiatric comorbidity in the relationship between sensory processing and tic severity. In a small-sample exploratory investigation conducted by Price et al. (n = 7), the implementation of the sensorimotor based “Alert Program”, designed to enhance sensory self-regulation in children with TS, demonstrated a potential reduction in tic symptoms, with 71% of participants (n = 5) showing improvement [31]. These preliminary findings offer initial empirical evidence supporting the clinical relevance of this intervention, as documented in the current study. This provides preliminary interventional empirical support for the clinical implications of our findings.

Another significant finding of this study is the independent negative predictive effect of the “under-responsive/sensation seeking” dimension on tic severity, as measured by the YGTSS total score. This observation can be effectively interpreted through the framework of computational psychiatry, particularly the Bayesian active inference model proposed by Rae et al. [32]. Within this theoretical paradigm, tics may represent a maladaptive self-regulatory mechanism employed by the brain to mitigate internal sensory uncertainty. The observed " under-responsive " phenotype in children may reflect a state of “insufficient sensory registration” or “ambiguous internal sensory signals,” corresponding to elevated sensory uncertainty in the Bayesian model. To compensate for this aberrant state and achieve robust sensory feedback that reduces internal prediction errors, these individuals may exhibit more pronounced and frequent tic movements. From this perspective, tics can be conceptualized as a manifestation of “active inference” behavior, wherein motor outputs generate predictable proprioceptive inputs, thereby transiently alleviating the discomfort associated with sensory uncertainty. This observation provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed correlation between heightened sensation-seeking behavior (indicative of under-responsive) and increased tic severity. Our findings provide quantitative clinical evidence from a Chinese population that supports this theoretical framework, while expanding the functional interpretation of tics beyond the traditional “impulse release” model to incorporate the novel dimension of “active sensation seeking for internal state stabilization.”

Discovery of sensory processing subtypes and their clinical implications: from population-level averages to individual heterogeneity

A key finding of this study is the identification of a distinct “sensory hypersensitivity” subtype through LPA. This discovery holds substantial clinical importance as it uncovers severe individual distress that conventional group-level analyses failed to detect. While no significant differences in tactile/taste-olfactory sensitivity were observed between the TD and HC group in aggregate comparisons, this finding likely reflects a masking effect, wherein the predominant “relatively typical sensory processing” profile obscured the extreme responses of the minority “sensory over-responsivity” subgroup. The LPA approach successfully isolated children exhibiting marked distress in tactile and taste-olfactory domains, who may demonstrate pronounced avoidance behaviors triggered by clothing tags, food textures, or environmental odors—clinically significant challenges that are frequently overlooked in standard assessments [4, 33].

Notably, the observed dissociation between the “sensory hypersensitivity” subtype and tic severity provides valuable clinical insights. This finding suggests that qualitative aspects of sensory abnormalities and tic severity may constitute distinct clinical dimensions with independent pathophysiological mechanisms. Current evidence indicates that tic severity appears to be more directly influenced by the “sensory dysregulation” dimension, which may be underpinned by sensorimotor gating deficits [29]. In contrast, heightened sensory sensitivity likely represents a parallel phenotype mediated by different neural pathways, as supported by a comprehensive narrative review demonstrating that sensory hypersensitivity does not strongly correlate with tic severity and is therefore a dissociable phenomenon [4]; case observations also illustrate this dissociation by showing differential responses of tics and sensory phenomena to intervention [34]. Importantly, all these studies involved populations with high rates of comorbid psychiatric conditions, most commonly obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) and ADHD, which affect approximately 60% of individuals with Tourette syndrome. The presence of such comorbidities may independently shape sensory profiles, underscoring the necessity of comprehensive comorbidity assessment in future research to disentangle the complex interplay between sensory processing, tic severity, and psychiatric dimensions.

Sensory processing abnormalities also exert a direct and independent impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Cavanna and colleagues demonstrated that the severity of sensory phenomena in patients with TS was significantly associated with reduced HRQoL, even after controlling for tic severity and obsessive-compulsive symptoms [35]. A narrative review further highlighted that sensory hypersensitivity in TD is associated with increased anxiety and functional impairment [4]. Given our findings, the “sensory hypersensitivity” subtype (11.3% of the TD cohort) may be particularly vulnerable to such adverse outcomes. These observations underscore the clinical importance of differentiated therapeutic approaches, wherein interventions targeting sensory abnormalities should utilize strategies distinct from those addressing core tic symptoms (e.g., behavioral interventions or pharmacotherapy) [36]. For patients exhibiting the “sensory hypersensitivity” phenotype, therapeutic efforts should focus on attenuating hypersensitivity in affected sensory modalities to improve daily functioning, an outcome that may significantly enhance quality of life and emotional regulation independent of tic severity modulation [6, 7].

Limitations

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, a cross-sectional design was employed in this study, which precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between sensory processing abnormalities and tic symptoms, or whether they share more fundamental neuropathophysiological underpinnings. Second, while LPA identified a distinct “sensory hypersensitivity” subtype, the limited sample size (n = 17) within this subgroup may compromise statistical power for comparative analyses with tic severity measures. The reliability and external validity of this phenotypic classification require replication in larger, independent cohorts. Third, given that sensory phenomena are currently considered transdiagnostic features of neuropsychiatric disorders [37], this study did not systematically assess or control for common comorbidities such as ADHD, OCD, anxiety disorders, and ASD. These conditions are themselves closely related to sensory processing patterns and may constitute potential confounders for the current results. Finally, all data in this study were derived from parent-reported questionnaires. Future research integrating children’s subjective experiences, clinical observations, and objective neurophysiological indicators (such as sensory gating P50 and PPI) would enable the construction of more comprehensive and robust conclusions.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This study systematically delineated the characteristic profile of sensory processing abnormalities in children with TD, established quantitative associations between the severity of sensory abnormalities and tic severity, and innovatively identified a potential subtype characterized by “sensory hypersensitivity” through a data-driven approach. Based on the findings and limitations of this study, future research directions may focus on the following aspects: First, systematically controlling for potential confounders, particularly psychiatric comorbidities, medication status, age, and gender, to enhance the generalizability of findings. The high prevalence of co-occurring conditions in TD populations necessitates rigorous confounding control to disentangle the independent contributions of sensory processing abnormalities to clinical outcomes. Second, conducting prospective longitudinal studies to elucidate the evolution of sensory abnormalities in the course of tic disorders and their causal relationship with symptom fluctuations. Third, validating the proposed sensory subtypes in larger samples and further exploring their underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Fourth, and most importantly in terms of clinical translation, investigating the differential response patterns of various sensory processing subtypes to behavioral interventions (e.g., comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics) or sensory integration therapy, thereby providing critical empirical evidence for ultimately achieving personalized and precise interventions for tic disorders.
