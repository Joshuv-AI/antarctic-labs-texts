Stationary flows for viscous heat-conductive fluid in a perturbed half-space
Mingjie Li, Masahiro Suzuki and Katherine Zhiyuan Zhang

Abstract
We consider the non-isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation in a perturbed half-space with an outflow boundary condition as well as the supersonic condition. This equation models a compressible viscous, heat-conductive, and Newtonian polytropic fluid. We show the unique existence of stationary solutions for the perturbed half-space. The stationary solution constructed in this paper depends on all directions and has multidirectional flow. We also prove the asymptotic stability of this stationary solution.



Keywords:

compressible Navier–Stokes equation, stationary solution, unique existence, asymptotic stability, multidirectional flow

Introduction

The motion of a compressible viscous, heat-conductive, and Newtonian polytropic fluid is governed by the following non-isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\rho_{t}+\textrm{div}(\rho{u})=0,\\
\vskip 8.53581pt\displaystyle(\rho{u})_{t}+\textrm{div}(\rho{u}\otimes{u})+\nabla P=\mu\Delta u+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla(\textrm{div}{u}),\\
\vskip 8.53581pt\displaystyle(\rho E)_{t}+\textrm{div}\big({u}(\rho{E}+P)\big)\displaystyle=\Delta(\kappa\theta+\frac{1}{2}\mu|u|^{2})+\textrm{div}\big(\mu u\cdot\nabla{u}+\lambda u\textrm{div}u\big).\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

Here
t\geq 0
is time,
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the spatial coordinate, and the unknowns
\rho,{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})^{tr}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\theta
denote the fluid density, velocity, and absolute temperature, respectively.
The pressure
P=P(\rho,\theta)
is a function of
\rho
and
\theta
, and the specific total energy
{E}
is given by

{E}:=(e+|{u}|^{2}/2)

with
e
being the internal energy and
|{u}|^{2}/2
the kinetic energy. The constant viscosity coefficients of the flow
\mu
and
\lambda
satisfy the physical restrictions

\mu>0,\quad 2\mu+3\lambda\geq 0.

The parameter
\kappa>0
is the ratio of the heat conductivity coefficient over the heat capacity.
The first equation is the conservation of mass, the second one
is the equation of momentum in which the pressure gradient
and viscosity as well as the convection effect are taken into account,
and the third equation is the balance of energy.
We study the ideal polytropic fluids
so that
P
and
e
are given by the state equations

P(\rho,\theta)=R\rho\theta,\quad e=c_{v}\theta,

where
R
is a positive constant and
c_{v}:=\frac{R}{\gamma-1}
is the specific heat at constant volume with
\gamma>1
being the adiabatic exponent. We study an initial–boundary value problem of (
1.1
)
in a perturbed half-space

\Omega:=\{x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,x_{1}>M(x^{\prime})\}\quad\text{for $M\in\cap_{k=1}^{\infty}H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Note that for classical solutions, the initial–boundary value problem of (
1.1
) can be rewritten
as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\rho_{t}+\textrm{div}(\rho{u})=0,\\
\vskip 8.53581pt\displaystyle\rho{u}_{t}+\rho{u}\cdot\nabla{u}+\nabla P=\mu\Delta u+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla(\textrm{div}{u}),\\
\vskip 8.53581pt\displaystyle c_{v}\rho\big(\theta_{t}+{u}\cdot\nabla\theta\big)+P\textrm{div}{u}\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta\theta+2\mu|\mathcal{D}(u)|^{2}+\lambda(\textrm{div}u)^{2},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle(\rho,{u},\theta)(0,x)=(\rho_{0},{u}_{0},\theta_{0})(x),

(1.2d)

\displaystyle\lim_{x_{1}\to\infty}(\rho,u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},\theta)(t,x_{1},x^{\prime})=(\rho_{+},u_{+},0,0,\theta_{+}),

(1.2e)

\displaystyle u(t,M(x^{\prime}),x^{\prime})={u}_{b}(x^{\prime})\quad\text{for}\ x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(1.2f)

\displaystyle\theta(t,M(x^{\prime}),x^{\prime})=\theta_{b}(x^{\prime})\quad\text{for}\ x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(1.2g)

where
\mathcal{D}(u)=(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{tr})/2
is the deformation tensor,
and
\rho_{+}>0
,
u_{+}<0
,
\theta_{+}>0
are constants.
It is assumed that

{u}_{b}\cdot n\geq c_{1}>0,\quad\theta_{b}\geq c_{2}>0,

(1.3)

with
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants.
The unit outer normal vector of the boundary

\partial\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,x_{1}=M(x^{\prime})\}
is represented by

{n}(x^{\prime})=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})(x^{\prime}):=\left(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla M|^{2}}},\frac{\partial_{x_{2}}M}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla M|^{2}}},\frac{\partial_{x_{3}}M}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla M|^{2}}}\right)(x^{\prime}).

(1.4)

We assume that the initial density and initial absolute temperature are uniformly positive:

\quad\inf_{x\in\Omega}\rho_{0}(x)>0,\quad\inf_{x\in\Omega}\theta_{0}(x)>0.\quad

We will construct solutions whose density is positive everywhere.
The outflow boundary condition
u_{b}\cdot n>0
guarantees that no boundary condition is suitable for the density
\rho
.
The compatibility conditions are also necessary for the initial data
u_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
.
We will state clearly the conditions in Section
2
.

Furthermore, we assume that the Mach number for the end states
satisfies the supersonic condition:

\frac{|u_{+}|}{\sqrt{\gamma R\theta_{+}}}>1.

(1.5)

The initial–boundary value problems of the compressible Navier–Stokes equation have been studied extensively. We are interested in the long-time behavior of the solutions. In this direction, Matsumura and Nishida
[
7
]
made a pioneering progress on the non-isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation (
1.1
) by considering the initial–boundary value problems over an exterior domain and a half-space. In
[
7
]
, the existence of the time-global solution is established, and the solution is proved to converge to the stationary solution as time goes to infinity, with the assumption that the initial perturbation from the stationary solution belongs to
H^{m}
and its
H^{m}
-norm is small enough. Many researchers followed
[
7
]
, but they focused mainly on the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation which does not include the equation of entropy, while a pressure term
P=K\rho^{\gamma}
with constants
K>0
and
\gamma\geq 1
is involved in the equation of motion to make up for the missing equation of entropy, since detailed studies on the non-isentropic equation were still hard in that time. Kagei and Kobayashi
[
3
]
further analyzed the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation over the half-space in the case that the stationary solution is a constant state, and obtained an accurate convergence rate of the time global solutions toward the constant state by assuming the initial perturbation belongs to
H^{m}\cap L^{1}
. Valli
[
11
]
considered the case when the equation of motion has an small external forcing term, and then proved the unique existence and stability of stationary solution over an bounded domain with the non-slip boundary condition. The velocity of the stationary solution in
[
11
]
is nonzero but small due to the perturbative nature of the argument and the smallness of the external forcing term. Matsumura
[
6
]
classified all possible time-asymptotic states for a one-dimensional half-space problem of the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation with no external force. It is also conjectured in
[
6
]
that one of time-asymptotic states for an
outflow problem
is a stationary solution of which end state satisfying the
supersonic condition
(
1.5
).

An outflow problem in our context is an initial–boundary value problem with an
outflow boundary condition
in (
1.3
). The asymptotic stability of the stationary solution (Matsumura’s conjecture) was proved in Kawashima, Nishibata and Zhu
[
5
]
. After that, Nakamura, Nishibata and Yuge
[
8
]
verified an exponential convergence rate toward the stationary solution by assuming that the initial perturbation belongs to some weighted Sobolev space. For a three-dimensional half-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
(that is, the case
M\equiv 0
), Kagei and Kawashima
[
2
]
showed that a
planar stationary solution
, that is, a special solution independent of tangential direction
x^{\prime}
, with its tangential velocities
(u_{2},u_{3})
being zero, is time asymptotically stable. It has been also known from
[
9
]
that the convergence rate is exponential if the initial perturbation decays exponentially fast at an infinite distance. Suzuki and Zhang
[
10
]
extended the results in
[
2
,
9
]
by considering the problem on a
perturbed half-space
with a curved boundary, and established the unique existence and asymptotic stability of stationary solution of the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation. Remarkably, the stationary solution constructed in
[
10
]
depends on all directions
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
and has multidirectional flow, in contrast to the planar stationary solution studied in
[
2
,
9
]
, which is independent of tangential
x^{\prime}
and has unidirectional flow along the
x_{1}
-axis.

In this paper, we investigate viscous heat-conductive fluid of which motion is governed by the non-isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation (
1.1
). As mentioned above, the outflow problem for the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equation has been well studied. It is of greater interest to study the outflow problem for the
nonisentropic
compressible Navier–Stokes equation.
Kawashima, Nakamura, Nishibata and Zhu
[
4
]
showed the unique existence and asymptotic stability of
the planer stationary solution
over a half line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
assuming the outflow condition in (
1.3
) and the supersonic condition (
1.5
).
They also obtained the convergence rate of the time-global solution towards the planer stationary solution.
Wang
[
12
]
studied the planer stationary solution over a three-dimensional half-space
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
, and then established the asymptotic stability as well as obtained the convergence rate.
It would be worth extending the results
[
4
,
12
]
to that over the perturbed half space
\Omega
. In this paper, we show the unique existence and asymptotic stability of the stationary solutions of (
1.1
) over the perturbed half space
\Omega
with a curved boundary.

Remark 1.6
.

It is worth noticing that in Theorems
1.2
–
1.4
, the existence and stability are established as long as the boundary of domain is given by a graph, so a domain boundary with a large curvature is allowed. The works
[
2
,
9
]
adopted the boundary condition as

for the half-space

It is also worth pointing out that Corollary

is the unit outer normal vector given in (

The stationary problem (

) reduces to an ODE (

). It is also different from the stationary incompressible Navier-Stokes equation, in which the system is elliptic. Our stationary equations are not categorized as elliptic equations. To overcome these difficulties and solve (

1.2
), and then construct a stationary solution making use of this time-global solution.

Let us further specify the idea to construct the time-global solution. We use a continuous argument combining time-local solvability and an a priori estimate. The key of the proof is the dissipative structure which makes solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equations decay exponentially fast as time tends to infinity. On the other hand, we expect from the stability theorem in
[
9
]
,
[
4
]
introduced above that the solution
(\rho,u,\theta)
to the problem (
1.2
) with
u_{b}=(u_{+},0,0)
will converge to the constant state
(\rho_{+},u_{+},\theta_{+})
at an exponential rate as time tends to infinity. When
u_{b}\neq(u_{+},0,0)
, after suitable reformulation, all effects coming from
u_{b}\neq(u_{+},0,0)
are represented by inhomogeneous terms in the equations. We define a perturbation

\Phi(t,x):=(\varphi,\psi,\zeta)(t,x):=(\rho,u,\theta)(t,x)-(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{u},\tilde{\theta})(\tilde{M}(x))-(0,U,\Theta)(x)

and reformulate (

) into a problem for
\Phi
. We are then able to derive a priori estimates of the solutions
\Phi
to the reformulated problem utilizing the dissipative structure. For the construction of stationary solutions, a similar approach as in
[

,
11
]
is taken. More precisely, we define the translated time-global solutions
\Phi^{k}(t,x):=\Phi(t+kT^{*},x)
for any

and
k=1,2,3,\ldots
. It can then be proved that the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}\}
converges to a certain time-periodic solution with a period
T^{*}
. After that, the uniqueness of time-periodic solution and the arbitrariness of
T^{*}
allow us to show that the time-periodic solution is actually time-independent, yielding a stationary solution to our problem.

This subsection is devoted to derive the

0<c\leq(\rho,\theta)(t,x)\leq C,\quad|u(t,x)|\leq C.

Lemma 5.2

Under the same conditions as in Proposition

Proof.

By a computation similar to the derivation of (
3.11

the energy form

(\rho\mathcal{E})_{t}-\mathop{\rm div}(G_{1}+B_{1})+\mu|\nabla\psi|^{2}+(\mu+\lambda)(\mathop{\rm div}\psi)^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{\theta}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}=R_{1},

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle:=-\rho u\mathcal{E}-R\theta^{s}\varphi\psi-R\rho\zeta\psi,

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mu\nabla\psi\cdot\psi+(\mu+\lambda)\psi\mathop{\rm div}\psi+\kappa\frac{\zeta}{\theta}\nabla\zeta,

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle:=\!\!\frac{R\theta^{s}\varphi}{\rho}f+\psi\cdot g+\frac{\zeta}{\theta}h+R\varphi\psi\cdot\nabla\theta^{s}+R\zeta\psi\cdot\nabla\rho^{s}

\displaystyle\quad+R\rho\eta\left(\frac{\rho^{s}}{\rho}\right)u\cdot\nabla\theta^{s}-R\theta^{s}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{\rho\rho^{s}}u\cdot\nabla\rho^{s}+c_{v}\rho\eta\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta^{s}}\right)u\cdot\nabla\theta^{s}

\Omega

\displaystyle=\frac{R\tilde{\theta}\varphi}{\rho}(\varphi_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\varphi)+R\rho\eta(\frac{\tilde{\rho}}{\rho})u\cdot\nabla\tilde{\theta}-\frac{R\tilde{\theta}\varphi^{2}}{\rho\tilde{\rho}}u\cdot\nabla\tilde{\rho},

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=c_{v}\rho\frac{\zeta}{\theta}(\zeta_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\zeta)+c_{v}\rho\eta(\frac{\theta}{\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})u\cdot\nabla({\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})+c_{v}\rho({\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})\frac{\zeta}{\theta}u\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{\tilde{\theta}+\Theta}.

Note that the first term on the right hand side is zero owing to
\eqref{ns1}_{1}
.
Let us rewrite terms
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
, and
I_{3}
as follows.
Use (
2.1b
) to obtain

\begin{split}I_{1}&=L\psi\cdot\psi-R\theta\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi-R\rho\psi\cdot\nabla\zeta+\psi\cdot(g+G).\\
\end{split}

Notice that

\displaystyle L\psi\cdot\psi=\mathop{\rm div}\left\{\mu\nabla\psi\cdot\psi+(\mu+\lambda)\psi\mathop{\rm div}\psi\right\}-\mu|\nabla\psi|^{2}-(\mu+\lambda)(\mathop{\rm div}\psi)^{2}.

Using (
2.1a
), we arrive at

\begin{split}I_{2}&=\frac{R\tilde{\theta}\varphi}{\rho}(-\rho\mathop{\rm div}\psi+f+F)+R\rho\eta(\frac{\tilde{\rho}}{\rho})u\cdot\nabla\tilde{\theta}-\frac{R\tilde{\theta}\varphi^{2}}{\rho\tilde{\rho}}u\cdot\nabla\tilde{\rho}.\\
\end{split}

Furthermore, by (
2.1c
), it is seen that

\begin{split}I_{3}&=\frac{\zeta}{\theta}\Big(-R\rho\theta\textrm{div}\psi+\kappa\Delta\zeta+2\mu|\mathcal{D}(\psi)|^{2}+\lambda(\textrm{div}\psi)^{2}+h+H\Big)\\
&\quad+c_{v}\rho\eta(\frac{\theta}{\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})u\cdot\nabla({\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})+c_{v}\rho({\tilde{\theta}+\Theta})\frac{\zeta}{\theta}u\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{\tilde{\theta}+\Theta}.\\
\end{split}

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{\theta}(\Delta\zeta)\zeta=\mathop{\rm div}\left(\kappa\frac{\zeta}{\theta}\nabla\zeta\right)-\frac{\kappa}{\theta}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+\kappa\frac{\zeta}{\theta^{2}}\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta.

We use the following terms from
I_{1},I_{2}
and
I_{3}
to get the cancellation

\displaystyle-R\theta\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi-R\rho\psi\cdot\nabla\zeta-R\tilde{\theta}\varphi\mathop{\rm div}\psi-R\rho\zeta\mathop{\rm div}\psi

\displaystyle=-R(\zeta+\Theta)\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi-R\tilde{\theta}\mathop{\rm div}(\psi\varphi)-R\rho\mathop{\rm div}(\psi\zeta)

\displaystyle=-R\Theta\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi-R\mathop{\rm div}(\tilde{\theta}\psi\varphi)+R\varphi\psi\cdot\nabla\tilde{\theta}-R\mathop{\rm div}(\rho\psi\zeta)+R\zeta\psi\cdot\nabla\tilde{\rho}.

Plugging the results above into (
A.1
), we conclude (
3.11
).
One can also have (
5.6
) just by
replacing
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{u},\tilde{\theta},U,\Theta,\nabla\tilde{M})
by
(\rho^{s},u^{s},\theta^{s},0,0,0)
in the computation above.

Appendix B
Basic inequalities and estimates

This section provides some basic inequalities and estimates that are frequently used throughout the paper.
The following lemmas cover the case
M\equiv 0
, that is,
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
.
For the proofs of Lemmas
B.1
–
B.6
, see
[
10
, Appendix A]
.

Lemma B.1
.

(Hardy’s Inequality)

Let
\alpha>0
. For
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, it holds that

\int_{\Omega}e^{-\alpha x_{1}}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx\lesssim\|\nabla f\|^{2}+\|{f(M(\cdot),\cdot)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}.

(B.1)

Lemma B.2
.

(Sobolev’s Inequalities)

For
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
g\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\quad 2\leq p<6,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(B.3)

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

(B.4)

Lemma B.3
.

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg Inequality)

Let
k=2,3,4\cdots
.
For
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
, there holds that

\|f\|_{H^{k-1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{1-1/k}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/k}.

(B.5)

Lemma B.4
.

(Commutator Estimate)

Let
k=0,1,2,\cdots
.
For
f,g,\nabla f\in H^{k}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

\|[\nabla^{k+1},f]g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

\|[\nabla^{k+1},\nabla M]g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}.

Lemma B.5
.

(Cattabriga Estimate)

Consider the following Stokes system

\bar{\rho}\mathop{\rm div}u=h,\ -\hat{\mu}\Delta u+\hat{p}\nabla p=g\ \text{on $\Omega$},\quad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\ \lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}u=0

with some constants
\bar{\rho}>0
,
\hat{\mu}>0
, and
\hat{p}>0
. For
k=0,1,\cdots,4
and

(h,g)\in H^{k+1}(\Omega)\times H^{k}(\Omega)
,
if
(u,p)\in H^{k+2}(\Omega)\times H^{k+1}(\Omega)
is a solution to the Stokes system,
then it holds that

\|\nabla^{k+2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla^{k+1}p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{0}(\|h\|^{2}_{H^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\|g\|^{2}_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}),

(B.6)

where
C_{0}=C_{0}(\Omega)
is a positive constant depending on
\Omega
.
Furthermore, there exists a positive constant
\eta_{0}
such that
if
\|M\|_{H^{9}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\eta_{0}
,
then (
B.6
) holds with
C_{0}
independent of
\Omega
.

Lemma B.6
.

(Elliptic Estimate)

Consider the following elliptic system

\begin{split}-\hat{\mu}\Delta u-(\hat{\mu}+\hat{\lambda})\nabla\mathop{\rm div}u=f\ \text{on $\Omega$},\quad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}u=0\end{split}

with some constants
\hat{\mu}>0
and
\hat{\mu}+\hat{\lambda}\geq 0
. For
k=0,1,2
and
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
,
if
u\in H^{k+2}(\Omega)
is a solution to the elliptic system, then it holds that

\|\nabla^{k+2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Lemma B.7
.

(Elliptic Estimate II)

Consider the following elliptic equation

\begin{split}-\hat{\kappa}\Delta u=f\ \text{on $\Omega$},\quad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}u=0\end{split}

with some constants
\hat{\kappa}>0
.
For
k=0,1,2
and
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
,
if
u\in H^{k+2}(\Omega)
is a solution to the elliptic equation, then it holds that

\|\nabla^{k+2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(B.7)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
\hat{\kappa}=1
.
We recall the change of valuables (
3.43
) and the notation (
3.46
)–(
3.50
).
The elliptic equation can be written in
y
-coordinate as

-\hat{\Delta}\hat{u}=\hat{f}\ \text{on $\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}$},\quad\hat{u}|_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}=0,\quad\lim_{|y|\rightarrow\infty}\hat{u}=0.

(B.8)

Let us consider only the case
k=0
, since the other cases
k=1,2
can be shown in a similar way with higher derivatives applied. Applying
\partial_{y_{l}}
for
l=2,3
to the first equation in (
B.8
), multiplying it by
\partial_{y_{l}}\hat{u}(y)
, and integrating the result over
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}\left|\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}A_{kj}\partial_{y_{j}}\right)\partial_{y_{l}}\hat{u}(y)\right|^{2}dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}\hat{f}(y)\partial_{y_{l}}^{2}\hat{u}(y)dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}([\hat{\Delta},\partial_{y_{l}}]\hat{u})\partial_{y_{l}}\hat{u}(y)dy

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}}\left\{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}A_{kj}\partial_{y_{j}}\right)\partial_{y_{l}}\hat{u}(y)\right\}\left\{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\partial_{y_{j}}A_{kj}\right)\partial_{y_{l}}\hat{u}(y)\right\}dy

\displaystyle\leq\nu\|\nabla^{2}_{y}\hat{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}+C\nu^{-1}\|(\hat{f},\nabla_{y}\hat{u})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})},

where we have used Schwarz’s inequality in deriving the last inequality, and
\nu
is a positive constant to be determined later.
Summing up the results for
l=1,2
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{|\bm{b}|=2,\ b_{1}\neq 2}\|\partial^{\bm{b}}_{y}\hat{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}\leq\nu\|\nabla^{2}_{y}\hat{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}+C\nu^{-1}\|(f,\nabla_{y}\hat{u})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}.

On the other hand, the normal derivative
\partial_{y_{1}y_{1}}\hat{u}
can be estimated by using the first equation in (
B.8
) as

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y_{1}y_{1}}\hat{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}\lesssim\sum_{|\bm{b}|=2,\ b_{1}\neq 2}\|\partial^{\bm{b}}_{y}\hat{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}+\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})}.

Using these, letting
\nu\ll 1
, and performing the change of variables
y\rightarrow x
, we conclude (
B.7
).
∎
