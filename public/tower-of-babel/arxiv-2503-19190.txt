Abstract
This paper addresses the task of learning convex regularizers to guide the reconstruction of images from limited data. By imposing that the reconstruction be amplitude-equivariant, we narrow down the class of admissible functionals to those that can be expressed as a power of a seminorm. We then show that such functionals can be approximated to arbitrary precision with the help of polyhedral norms. In particular, we identify two dual parameterizations of such systems: (i) a synthesis (or atomic) form with an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -penalty that involves some learnable dictionary; and (ii) an analysis form with an ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -penalty that involves a trainable regularization operator. After having provided geometric insights and proved that the two forms are universal, we propose an implementation that relies on a specific architecture (tight frame with a weighted ℓ 1 \ell_{1} penalty) that is easy to train. We illustrate its use for denoising and the reconstruction of biomedical images. We find that the proposed framework outperforms the sparsity-based methods of compressed sensing, while it offers essentially the same convergence and robustness guarantees.



keywords

convex optimization, regularization, learning, image reconstruction, sparsity, dictionaries

Introduction

The context of this work is computational imaging where the goal is to reconstruct an image
from indirect and possibly incomplete measurements. We focus on scenarios where the measurements are linear functionals
of the object under investigation, such as line integrals in computer tomography
[
62
]
or samples of its Fourier transform in magnetic resonance imaging
[
47
]
.
These quantities are corrupted by noise according to the relation
{\bf{y}}={\bf{H}}{\bf{s}}+{\bf{n}}_{\rm noise}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where

{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown signal to be recovered and
{\bf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
is the system matrix that results from the discretization of the physics. Given a candidate signal
\tilde{{\bf{s}}}
, the forward model is simulated as
{\bf{H}}\tilde{{\bf{s}}}
which, for consistency, ought to match
{\bf{y}}
within the noise level.

Computational imaging
is currently dominated by two paradigms
[
52
,
66
]
.
The first one is the variational approach, where the reconstruction task is formulated as a cost minimization problem, as in (
1
).
Variational techniques have driven the development of image reconstruction algorithms over the past three decades. They are supported by an extensive theory and benefit from the powerful computational tools of convex optimization
[
28
,
10
]
and compressed sensing
[
29
,
20
,
32
]
. Solutions are typically computed iteratively through the minimization of a cost, either by steepest descent or by proximal-gradient techniques
[
31
,
24
,
30
,
7
]
.
Under standard hypotheses (convexity and coercivity of the cost functional), this framework provides guarantees for convergence
[
28
]
, stability
[
26
]
, and signal recovery from limited measurements
[
27
,
17
]
.

The second paradigm, at the forefront of research, is the data-driven approach, often referred to as artificial intelligence (AI), where the traditional reconstruction pipeline is replaced or complemented by deep neural networks (DNN)

[
43
,
51
,
78
,
48
]
. Formally, the resulting reconstruction algorithm is a nonlinear map
{\bf{f}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(the weights of the neural net). These
are pre-trained for best performance in a regression task such that
{\bf{f}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf{y}}_{k})\approx{\bf{s}}_{k}
on a representative
set of data
({\bf{s}}_{k},{\bf{y}}_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
, where
K
(the number of training images) is assumed to be sufficiently large.
DNN-based methods generally achieve superior image reconstructions
[
79
]
but suffer from a lack of robustness
[
3
]
and theoretical understanding.
More importantly, they have been found to remove or hallucinate structures
[
61
,
57
]
, which is unacceptable in medical imaging.

Hybrid approaches aim to combine the strengths of both paradigms
[
59
]
and can be broadly
classified in two categories.

(i) Trainable regularizers
: The guiding principle here is to select a parametric regularization functional

{\bf{s}}\mapsto g_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf{s}})
and to then optimize its parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(training) for best performance within the variational setting
[
41
,
1
]
.
An early example is the field of expert (FoE) model
[
68
]
, where
g({\bf{s}})=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}({\bf{w}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{s}})=\langle{\bf{1}},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\bf{W}}{\bf{s}})\rangle
is a sum of ridges. These ridges result from the application of a convex function
\phi_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
(potential) to some filtered versions of the signal, with the filtering templates
{\bf{w}}_{k}
(collectively encoded in
{\bf{W}}
) being learned. Enhanced versions of this model allow for trainable potentials under convexity constraints
[
21
,
63
,
35
]
.
A notable alternative involves using an input-convex neural network
[
2
]
to model the regularizer, as suggested in
[
58
]
.

(ii) Deep unrolling
: The methods in this category typically originate from an existing variational reconstruction algorithm that is reconfigured it into a trainable architecture (unrolling)
[
56
]
.
This is exemplified in the plug-and-play (PnP) framework
[
77
,
44
]
, where deep-learning-based denoisers replace traditional proximal operators
[
19
,
70
,
74
]
. For these methods to remain variational, the learned denoiser must satisfy both firm non-expansiveness (for convergence
[
42
, Proposition 15]
) and monotonicity
[
6
,
36
]
, the latter being challenging to enforce.

This raises a fundamental question: To what extent can one improve variational methods by learning the regularizer, while maintaining their theoretical guarantees? While the FoE model substantially improves upon “handcrafted” regularizers such as total variation (TV)
[
34
]
, its expressiveness may still be insufficient to capture the full range of convex regularizations. We contend that further improvements are possible within the variational framework under adequate constraints to avoid hallucinations.

To address these issues, we introduce a general parametric framework for image reconstruction under the constraints of amplitude equivariance (AE) and convexity.
This parameterization involves polyhedral norms and results in a formulation that is reminiscent of compressed-sensing
techniques with a trainable dictionary
[
69
,
29
]
. Our present contribution is two fold:

1.

The identification of two dual forms of polyhedral regularization with proof of their universality, in the sense that they are able to encode any AE convex regularizer to an
\epsilon
-level of precision.

2.

The transcription of the theory into practical reconstruction architectures, including a constrained version that lends itself well to training.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formalize the variational reconstruction problem and invoke higher-level principles (amplitude-equivariance and coercivity) to narrow down the class of admissible regularization functionals to some power of a norm, as dictated by Theorem
3
. In Section
3
, we prove that we can
achieve universality with the help of polyhedral norms (Theorem
7
), the latter admitting two dual parameterizations in terms of some linear operator, as stated in Theorem
6
.
The theoretical foundation for our results is presented in Section
4
with a complete characterization of the geometry of polyhedral Banach spaces (Theorem

) in connection with atomic norms
[
20
]
. In Section
5
, we use our results to specify explicit computational architectures, including one that relies on weighted
\ell_{1}
-minimization and Parseval filterbanks, which is easy to train. We then demonstrate that this approach provides competitive results for denoising and image reconstruction.

2
Problem Formulation

2.1
Variational Setup for the Resolution of Inverse Problems

Given the data
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and a linear measurement model, the generic variational formulation of our signal-reconstruction problem is

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf y}-{\bf Hs}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\,g({\bf{s}})\right),

(1)

where
{\bf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
is a known matrix that models the physics of the acquisition, and where
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a regularization functional that promotes “regular” solutions. The relative strength of the regularization is modulated by the regularization factor
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, according to the standard practice in the field.
In this work, we aim at investigating schemes where
g=g_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is a learned regularizer
with trainable parameters collected in
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

Since our goal is to identify useful classes of regularizers, we can focus on the noise-free scenario (the limit case of (
1
) as
\lambda\to 0
), without loss of generality. The solution of the corresponding generalized interpolation problem reads

\displaystyle{\bf{f}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf{y}})=\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{g_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf{s}})\ \mbox{ s.t. }\ {\bf{y}}={\bf{H}}{\bf{s}}\right\},

(2)

where
{\bf{f}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the underlying (nonlinear) reconstruction operator that implements the minimization and depends on the hyperparameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the regularization functional
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The underlying assumption here is that (
2
) with
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
fixed is well-defined with a unique global minimum. This is the standard requirement for most works involving convex analysis, including compressed sensing
[
27
,
17
]
.

2.2
Equivariant Regularizers

Since the measurement operator in (
1
) is linear, a change of amplitude of the signal
{\bf{s}}
by the multiplicative factor
\alpha
results in a corresponding rescaling of the measurements, as in
\alpha{\bf{y}}={\bf{H}}(\alpha{\bf{s}})
. Accordingly, we shall insist that this property
also carry over to the reconstruction specified by (
2
) in the noise-free scenario and,
more generally, by (
1
) for
\lambda
sufficiently small, say, below some critical value
\lambda_{0}=\lambda({\bf{y}})
. Specifically, when we amplify the measurements and signal by the same factor
\alpha\neq 0
, we get that

\displaystyle\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\alpha{\bf y}-{\bf H\alpha s}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\,g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\alpha{\bf{s}})=\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf y}-{\bf H}{\bf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda_{0}}{\alpha^{2}}\,g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\alpha{\bf{s}}),

which, according to our requirement, needs to remain compatible with (
1
). The desired equivalence is achieved for

\lambda=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\alpha^{2}}\,\frac{g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\alpha{\bf{s}})}{g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\bf{s}})}
, but only under the condition
that the ratio does not depend upon
{\bf{s}}
.
This is to say that the effect of scaling needs to factor out of the regularization, which is a property that we shall refer to as
amplitude equivariance
.

Definition 1
.

A regularization functional
g:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
amplitude-equivariant if there exists a function
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

g(\alpha x)=a(\alpha)g(x)
for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
.

By adding conditions of convexity to ensure that the solution of (
1
) or that of (
2
) exist along with symmetry (which is standard in image processing), we now show that we can narrow down the options to
g({\bf{x}})=|p({\bf{x}})|^{\gamma}
, where
p
is a seminorm on
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\gamma\geq 1
.

To formulate this result rigorously, we start by isolating the structural properties of

g
that are essential for our analysis.

Definition 2
.

Let
g:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a (regularization) functional on a Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then,
g
can be endowed with the following properties.

1.

Convexity:
g\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big)\leq\lambda g(x)+(1-\lambda)g(y)
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

2.

Coercivity:
g(x)\to+\infty
as
\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\to+\infty
.

3.

Symmetry:
g(x)=g(-x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

4.

Unbiasedness:
g(x)\geq g(0)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

5.

Homogeneity of order
\gamma
:
g(\alpha x)=|\alpha|^{\gamma}g(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Convexity and coercivity are the classical assumptions in convex analysis
[
28
]
; together, they guarantee the existence of minimizers for the reconstruction problems (
1
) and (
2
).
The unbiasedness condition ensures that, in the absence of prior information, the preferred reconstruction is the trivial element

x=0
.
Finally, homogeneity is the key property that enforces the desired amplitude-equivariant behavior.

Theorem 3
.

Let
g:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex, symmetric (and continuous) functional on the Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

1.

The functional
g
is amplitude-equivariant.

2.

The functional
g
is
\gamma
-homogeneous for some
\gamma\geq 1
.

3.

g(x)=|p(x)|^{\gamma}
, where
p
is a seminorm
and
\gamma\geq 1
is the order of homogeneity of
g
.

The reason why “continuity” is parenthesized in the statement of Theorem
3
is that the hypothesis is superfluous in the finite-dimensional setting because: (i) all Banach norms, as well as the associated notions of continuity, are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
55
]
; and (ii) all convex functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are
continuous on their domain
[
28
,
10
]
. We also note that the covered scenarios all require that
g
be symmetric and unbiased with
g(0)=0
.

Proof.

First, we observe that
\gamma
th-order homogeneity (with
\alpha=-1
) implies symmetry.
The statements in the theorem summarize the following chain of implications, with the first three items covering the classic equivalence: convex
1
-homogeneous functional = seminorm (see Appendix A, Definition
12
).

1.

Convexity +
1
-homogeneity
\Rightarrow
subadditivity. It suffices to take the convexity inequality with
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

2.

Subaddivity +
1
-homogeneity
\Rightarrow
convexity:

g(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq g(\lambda x)+g\big((1-\lambda)y\big)=\lambda g(x)+(1-\lambda)g(y)
.

3.

Continuity and
\gamma
th-order homogeneity
\Rightarrow

g(0)=0
:
Given some arbitrary
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}\backslash\{0\}
, we construct the sequence
x_{n}=\tfrac{1}{n}x_{0}\neq 0
. It is converging to
0
in
{\mathcal{X}}
because
\|x_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\frac{1}{n}\|x_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By invoking the
\gamma
-homogeneity and continuity of
g
, we then get that

\lim_{n\to\infty}g(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)^{\gamma}g(x_{0})=0=g(\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n})=g(0).

4.

Convexity + symmetry
\Rightarrow
unbiasedness: Indeed,
g(0)=g\big(\tfrac{1}{2}x+\tfrac{1}{2}(-x)\big)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}g(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}g(-x)=g(x)
.

5.

Convexity and
\gamma
th-order homogeneity
\Rightarrow

\gamma\geq 1
: For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, we must have that

g(\alpha x+0)=(\alpha)^{\gamma}g(x)\leq\alpha g(x)
, which implies that
\gamma\geq 1
.

6.

Ampliture-equivariance
\Leftrightarrow

\gamma
th-order homogenity: Let
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}>0
.
Then,
g(\alpha x)=a(\alpha)g(x)=a(\alpha_{1})a(\alpha_{2})g(x)
, which implies that

a(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1})=a(\alpha_{1})a(\alpha_{2})\Leftrightarrow\log a(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})=\log a(\alpha_{1})+\log a(\alpha_{2}).

This means that the function

\log a:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear and hence of the form
\log a(\alpha)=C_{0}\alpha
, which is the desired result with
C_{0}=\log\gamma
. The reverse implication is obvious.

7.

Convexity and
\gamma
th-order homogeneity
\Leftrightarrow g(x)=|p(x)|^{\gamma}
, where
p
is
convex and
1
-homogeneous.

Indirect part: The function
g(x)=|p(x)|^{\gamma}
is convex because it is the composition of two convex functionals
p:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
|\cdot|^{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Moreover, it is such that

g(x)=|p(\alpha x)|^{\gamma}=|\alpha p(x)|^{\gamma}=|\alpha|^{\gamma}g(x)
.

Direct part: We now show that the convexity of
g(x)\geq 0
implies that of
p(x)=\big(g(x)\big)^{1/\gamma}
, which is 1-homogeneous.
For any
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
with
p(x),p(y)\neq 0
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
and by letting
P=\lambda p(x)+(1-\lambda)p(y)>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{g\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big)}{P^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=g\left(\frac{\lambda p(x)}{P}\frac{x}{p(x)}+\frac{(1-\lambda)p(y)}{P}\frac{y}{p(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda p(x)}{P}g\left(\frac{x}{p(x)}\right)+\frac{(1-\lambda)p(y)}{P}g\left(\frac{y}{p(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ =\frac{\lambda p(x)}{P}+\frac{(1-\lambda)p(y)}{P}=1,

(3)

where we also used that
g(x)=p(x)^{\gamma}
. We then take the
\gamma
th root of (
3
), which yields the desired convexity inequality
p\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big)\leq P
. (The latter obviously also holds for

p(x)=0
and/or
p(y)=0
because of the homogeneity of
p
.)

In the context of regularization, the amplitude-equivariance property is fundamental
for it ensures that the recovery procedure is covariant (through a proper adjustment of the regularization strength) to any global rescaling of the input data. In view of Theorem
3
, this reduces the options of acceptable regularizations to (semi)norms.
Since
|\cdot|^{\gamma}
is increasing convex, we also note that there is no loss of generality
if we replace
g_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
in (
2
) by
p=|g|^{1/\gamma}
. The same holds true
for (
1
) under a suitable adjustment of the regularization parameter
\lambda
.

If we also want to make sure that the solution of Problem (
1
)
exists, irrespective of the system matrix
{\bf{H}}
, we need
p
to be coercive,
which then limits the options to the case where
p
is a norm.

3
Universal Equivariant Convex Regularizers

So far, we have seen that a
coercive, equivariant convex regularizer
g
can always be replaced by an appropriate norm
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our next step will be to show that this can all be reformulated using convex sets. We then attain universality by approximating the underlying regularization balls as closely as desired with the help of (learned) polytopes. The crucial issue there is to retain the norm property, which is fundamental to our argumentation.

3.1
Properties of the Unit Regularization Ball

A basic result in functional analysis is that every seminorm
p
on a vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
has an equivalent geometric description in terms of the characteristic set:

B=B_{p}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:p(x)\leq 1\}
, which happens to be a disk.
The key to this equivalence is the possibility of recovering
p
from
B
with the help of the Minkowski functional, as stated in Theorem
5
.

Definition 4
.

A subset
B\subset{\mathcal{X}}
of a topological vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
is called a disk if it is convex and center-symmetric.
The Minkowski functional (or gauge) associated with such a disk
B
is

\displaystyle\mu_{B}(x)=\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:x\in\lambda B\},

(4)

with the convention that
\mu_{B}(x)=\infty
when the infimum in (
4
) does not exist.

A set is said to be absorbing if,
for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, there exists some
r>0
such that
x\in\lambda B
for all
|\lambda|>r
. In particular, if
B
is a disk, then it is absorbing if and only if it includes the origin as an interior point.

Theorem 5
(
[
60
, p. 120-121,115-154]
).

Let
B
be an absorbing disk in a topological vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
.

1.

The functional
\mu_{B}
specified by (
4
)
is a seminorm on
{\mathcal{X}}
.

2.

If
A=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\mu_{B}(x)<1\}
and
C=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\mu_{B}(x)\leq 1\}
, then

A\subseteq B\subseteq C
and
\mu_{A}=\mu_{B}=\mu_{C}
. In particular, if
B
is open (in the topology of
{\mathcal{X}}
), then

A=B
. Likewise, if
B
is closed, then
B=C
.

3.

The gauge
\mu_{B}
is continuous on
{\mathcal{X}}
if and only if
B
is a neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathcal{X}}
.

4.

The gauge
\mu_{B}
is a norm on
{\mathcal{X}}
if and only if
B
does not contain any linear subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}
, except
\{0\}
.

Conversely, let
p
be a (semi)norm on
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then,
B=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:p(x)\leq 1\}

is convex, balanced, and absorbing with
p=\mu_{B}
.

The powerful aspect of this result is that the Banach disk
B
does not even need to be closed (see Item 2).
We note that the fourth condition (induction of a norm) is automatically met if
the set
B
in Theorem
5

is bounded. In such a scenario,
B
can be assimilated to the unit ball of the norm
\|\cdot\|=\mu_{B}(\cdot)
, while the determination of the norm for a particular point
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
(see (
4
)) amounts to inflating

B
by
\lambda
(resp., deflating
B
if
\lambda<1
) up to the limit point where
x
enters
\lambda B
(resp,
x
leaves
\lambda B
).

{\bf{x}}\mapsto\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{1}}

B_{p_{1}}
.

To illustrate the concept, we show the contour-plot of the regularizer
p_{1}({\bf{x}})=\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{1}}
for
{\bf{L}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
in Figure
1
. We observe that
B_{p_{1}}
is symmetric, convex and bounded, where the latter reflects the property that the underlying regularization operator is injective. These properties are characteristic of a norm, in conformity with Item 4 in Theorem
5
. The unit ball of this particular norm has

N=6
extreme points/vertices
{\bf{g}}_{1},\dots,{\bf{g}}_{N}
(overlaid as light vectors), so that it can also be described as the
convex hull
of its vertices:
B_{p_{1}}={\rm Conv}\{{\bf{g}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. We shall see that
B_{p_{1}}
also admits a dual representation
as an intersection of half-spaces, whose normals (facet-vectors) are overlaid in blue.

3.2
Universal Parametric Polyhedral Regularizers

Theorem
3
implies that a convex equivariant regularization
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be imposed via the minimization of a corresponding (semi)norm
p=p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. For mathematical convenience, we shall now restrict our attention
to the Banach setting where
p
is a norm that is characterized by its unit ball
B_{p}
.
Since the latter is a convex, symmetric, and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it can itself be approximated arbitrary closely by a convex symmetric polytope that has sufficiently many vertices. This process is supported by Theorem
5
: to induce an equivalent (semi-)norm, it is sufficient to reproduce the unit ball of the regularizer.
By searching among all convex symmetric polytopes, we are actually spanning
the norms associated with the family of polyhedral Banach spaces (see Section
4.2
).
These spaces have some remarkable geometric properties, which are made explicit in Section
4
with the key results being collected in Theorem
10
.

We now present of a variant of Theorem
10
that offers a practical answer to our design problem: a constructive parameterization of
all polyhedral norms in terms of dictionaries or, alternatively, regularization operators.

Theorem 6
(Dual parameterization of all polyhedral norms).

Let us consider the two complementary functionals

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}\mapsto

\displaystyle\|{\bf{x}}\|_{1,{\bf{G}}}=\min_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{N}}}\{\|{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}:{\bf{x}}={\bf{G}}{\bf{z}}\}

(5)

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}\mapsto

\displaystyle\|{\bf{x}}\|_{{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}},\infty}=\|{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}

(6)

parameterized by some rectangular matrix
{\bf{G}}=[{\bf{g}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\bf{g}}_{\tilde{N}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\tilde{N}}
.
These are norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
{\rm rank}({\bf{G}})=d
, in which case
{\mathcal{X}}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{1,{\bf{G}}})
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}},\infty})

form a dual pair of polyhedral Banach spaces.

4.1

We start by recalling a few geometric and topological concepts.
Let

A

The fundamental theorem for polytopes states that that every bounded polyhedron is a convex polytope and vice versa

{\bf{B}}_{N}=[{\bf{b}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\bf{b}}_{2^{N}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2^{N}}
is the matrix representation of all “binary”
vectors
{\bf{b}}_{m}
in (
24
) with components in
\{-1,1\}
.
The theoretical relevance of (
25
) is the possibility of representing an
\ell_{1}
-norm by an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, while the reverse generally does not hold for
N>2
(see Appendix B).

The dual of
\ell_{1}(\mathbb{I})
is
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{I})=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{\infty}})

whose unit ball is the
N
-dimensional hypercubic
B_{\ell_{\infty}}
, which has
2^{N}
binary vertices
{\bf{b}}_{m}
and
2N
facets with normal vectors
\pm{\bf{e}}_{n}
. In this scenario, it is the S-form

\displaystyle\|{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}=\min_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{N}}}\{\|{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}:{\bf{x}}={\bf{B}}_{N}{\bf{z}}\},

(26)

that is non-standard,
while the A-form reverts to the classical formula.

5
Parametric Reconstruction Pipelines

Given the constraint of an equivariant regularizer
g
, we now use
Theorems
6
and
7
to specify some
polyhedral approximation(s) of the generic variational signal-reconstruction problem (
1
).
We shall consider three architectures, with the third being the one that presently lends itself the best to training.

5.1
Sparse Encoding in a Frame/Dictionary

The first form in Theorem
6
, together with the universality result (
9
), suggests the reformulation of our problem as

\displaystyle S_{\rm I}=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf{s}}\|_{{\rm S},{\bf{G}}}\}\right),

(27)

where the underling regularizer—the synthesis/atomic norm defined by (
5
)—is parameterized
by the matrix
{\bf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
. The literal form (
27
) involves two nested optimizations: an outer one to minimize the overall cost, and an inner one to evaluate
\|{\bf{s}}\|_{{\rm S},{\bf{G}}}
for every candidate
{\bf{s}}
, which, according to the definition, requires
the determination of an optimal codeword
in the dictionary
{\bf{G}}
. Fortunately, we can simplify the
task by setting
{\bf{s}}={\bf{G}}{\bf{z}}
and recasting (
27
) in terms of the auxiliary codeword variable
{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. After substitution and mutualization of the minimization, this results in

\displaystyle S_{\rm I}=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}={\bf{G}}\tilde{{\bf{z}}}:\ \tilde{{\bf{z}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{G}}{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}\},

(28)

which has a strong feel of “déja vu.”
Indeed, (
28
) is the standard “synthesis form” (a.k.a. sparse encoding) used in compressed sensing
[
29
]
, for which a multitude of efficient iterative solvers have been developed. The present contribution is the proof that (
28
) with a learnable dictionary
{\bf{G}}
, as advocated in
[
69
]
,
is universal for the class of equivariant convex regularizers.

We have already hinted to the property that the matrix
{\bf{G}}=[{\bf{g}}_{1}\cdots{\bf{g}}_{N}]
defines a dictionary with atoms
{\bf{g}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The admissibility condition
{\rm rank}({\bf{G}})=d
in Theorem
6

implies that the
{\bf{g}}_{n}
form a frame of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
23
]
. This means that
there exist two constants
A
and
B
such that

\displaystyle{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\quad A\|{\bf{s}}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\langle{\bf{s}},{\bf{g}}_{n}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|{\bf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(29)

The optimal frame bounds
A
and
B
are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of the frame operator
{\bf{s}}\mapsto\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bf{g}}_{n}\langle{\bf{s}},{\bf{g}}_{n}\rangle={\bf{A}}{\bf{s}}
, with
{\bf{A}}={\bf{G}}{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

[
23
]
.

To ensure that all the elements of the dictionary are extreme points (irreducible scenario), we propose to impose the normalization constraint
\|{\bf{g}}_{n}\|_{2}=1
.
Indeed, the strong convexity of
{\bf{z}}\mapsto\|{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}
implies that

\|\lambda{\bf{g}}_{n}+(1-\lambda){\bf{g}}_{n^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}<\lambda\|{\bf{g}}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+(1-\lambda)\|{\bf{g}}_{n^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{2}=1
,
for any
\lambda\in\,]0,1[
and
{\bf{g}}_{n}\neq{\bf{g}}_{n^{\prime}}
. This means that all non-extreme points
{\bf{g}}\in{\rm conv}\{{\bf{\pm}}{\bf{g}}_{1},\dots,\pm{\bf{g}}_{N}\}
that are in the symmetric convex hull of the
{\bf{g}}_{n}
have an
\ell_{2}
-norm that is smaller than
1
. We can then insert a new element
{\bf{g}}_{N+1}
with
\|{\bf{g}}_{N+1}\|_{2}=1
without spoiling the extreme-point properties of the others.

While (
28
) is very attractive for inference because of the availability of efficient resolution methods when the dictionary is known, it is much harder to optimize over
{\bf{G}}
in order to learn the “optimal” dictionary for a given class of signals, also in the simpler denoising scenario (
{\bf{H}}={\bf{I}}_{d}
) commonly used for training. We attribute this state of affairs to the fact that
{\bf{G}}
has a huge null space (of dimension
N-d
), which makes
the optimization difficult and costly. There is also the necessity to maintain the condition
{\rm rank}({\bf{G}})=d
to ensure that (
28
) with
{\bf{G}}
fixed is well-posed. This observation aligns with prior reports that convolutional sparse coding (CSC)
[
64
,
67
]
—the special case of (
28
) with
{\bf{=}}{\bf{I}}
and
{\bf{G}}
shift-invariant—is not among the most effective dictionary-based denoising techniques
[
22
,
18
,
46
]
.

5.2
Universal Weighted
\ell_{\infty}
-Regularization

The optimization problem associated with the second form in Theorem
6
is

\displaystyle S_{\rm II}=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf{F}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\}

(30)

where we are now using a distinct symbol for the regularization matrix
{\bf{F}}=[{\bf{f}}_{1}\cdots{\bf{f}}_{\widetilde{N}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\tilde{N}}
formed from the facet vectors
{\bf{f}}_{n}
of the unit ball
B_{{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm S}}
. Here too, (
30
) is reminiscent of the regularization methods used in compressed sensing, with the important difference that the regularizer now involves an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. Since the proximity operator
\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
(adaptive soft-clip) is (almost) as convenient as that of

\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{1}}
(soft-threshold), it is possible to adapt all the traditional algorithms (e.g., FISTA or ADMM) for the resolution of (
30
) with
{\bf{F}}^{\mathsf{T}}
fixed.

Observe that (
30
) is conceptually simpler and less constraining than (
28
) because there is no (higher-dimensional) auxiliary variable
{\bf{z}}
nor any issue with some hidden null space.
In fact, the scheme has the capacity to encode all seminorms if one drops the rank constraint on
{\bf{F}}
.

As for training, it can in principle be achieved based on a classic regression task where
{\bf{F}}^{\mathsf{T}}
is optimized for the minimum mean-square error denoising (
{\bf{H}}={\bf{I}}_{d}
)
of a representative set of images.
The caveat is that the back-propagation of the gradient (training error)
to the regularization weights will be very slow because of the underlying
\ell_{\infty}
-norm acting as a binary switch. Finding a way around this limitation is a topic that deserves further investigation because
(
30
) is our most expressive convex architecture.

5.3
Weighted
\ell_{1}
-Regularization Revisited

Due to the difficulties encountered with the training of the two aforementioned architectures, we decided to revisit the regularization setup more typical of compressed sensing
[
16
,
49
]
; namely,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{\rm I}=\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf y}-{\bf Hs}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{1}},

(31)

which involves an operator
{\bf{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
with
N\geq d
and a
\ell_{1}
-penalty instead of the less standard
\ell_{\infty}
-norm in (
30
).
We now show that (
31
) with trainable
{\bf{L}}
constitutes a valid alternative to (
27
) or (
30
) in the sense that it
also induces a polyhedral regularization. For completeness, we also identify the underlying dual Banach geometry, which is analogous to the first part of Theorem
6
, with the role of the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{\infty}
-norm being interchanged.

Theorem 11
.

Let us consider the two complementary functionals

\displaystyle{\bf{s}}\mapsto

\displaystyle\|{\bf{s}}\|_{{\bf{L}},1}=\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{1}}

(32)

\displaystyle{\bf{s}}\mapsto

\displaystyle\|{\bf{s}}\|_{\infty,{\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}}=\inf\bigl\{\|{\bf{t}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}:{\bf{s}}={\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{t}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bf{u}}_{n}t_{n}\bigr\}

(33)

parameterized by some rectangular matrix
{\bf{L}}=[{\bf{u}}_{1}\cdots{\bf{u}}_{N}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
.
These are norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
{\rm rank}({\bf{L}})=d
, in which case
{\mathcal{X}}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{{\bf{L}},1})
and
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},{\|\cdot\|_{\infty,{\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}}})

form a dual pair of polyhedral Banach spaces.

Proof.

First, we show that
\|{\bf{\cdot}}\|_{{\bf{L}},1}
is a polyhedral norm. Using (
25
), we express it as
\|{\bf{s}}\|_{{\bf{L}},1}=\|{\bf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}{\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}=\|{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}
with
{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\bf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}{\bf{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
, where

{\bf{B}}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
is the binary matrix whose column vectors are the
M=2^{N}
vertices of the
\ell_{\infty}
unit ball (hypercube) in
N
dimensions. The validity of (
25
) for all
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
also implies that

{\bf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}
has full row rank (i.e.,
{\rm rank}({\bf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N})=N
), as it would not yield a norm otherwise.
Since the left multiplication with such a matrix is rank-preserving
[
73
, p. 96]
, one has that
{\rm rank}({\bf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{N}{\bf{L}})={\rm rank}({\bf{L}})\leq d
.
We know from Theorem
6
that
{\bf{s}}\mapsto\|{\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}

induces a polyhedral norm if and only if
{\rm rank}({\bf{G}})=d
. Since
{\rm rank}({\bf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}})={\rm rank}({\bf{G}})
is equal to
{\rm rank}({\bf{L}})
in the present setting, this is the desired result.

The dual norm is necessarily polyhedral as well. To reveal it, we consider the
zonotope
induced by the row vectors of
{\bf{L}}
:

\displaystyle Z:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}[-\,{\bf{u}}_{n},{\bf{u}}_{n}]=\Bigl\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}{\bf{u}}_{n}:\|{\bf{t}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1\Bigr\}.

(34)

This is a convex, center-symmetric subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that includes the origin as an interior point (because the
{\bf{u}}_{n}
span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).
Since
Z
is also bounded, its gauge
\mu_{Z}
defines a valid norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(by Theorem
5
). Specifically, we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{Z}({\bf{y}})=\inf\{\lambda>0:{\bf{y}}\in\lambda Z\}=\inf\bigl\{\|{\bf{t}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}:{\bf{y}}=\textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}\,{\bf{u}}_{n}\bigr\},

(35)

with

Z
being the unit ball of
\mu_{Z}
by construction. The dual norm of
\mu_{Z}
is given by

\displaystyle\forall{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\quad\mu_{Z^{\circ}}({\bf{x}})=\sup_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mu_{Z}({\bf{z}})\leq 1}\langle{\bf{z}},{\bf{x}}\rangle=\sup_{{\bf{z}}\in Z}\langle{\bf{z}},{\bf{x}}\rangle

(36)

where
Z^{\circ}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the polar of
Z
(see Appendix A).
Now the key is that

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bf{z}}\in Z}\langle{\bf{z}},{\bf{x}}\rangle=\sup_{\|{\bf{t}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}\langle{\bf{u}}_{n},{\bf{x}}\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\langle{\bf{u}}_{n},{\bf{x}}\rangle|=\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{1}}

(37)

with the maximum being attained for
t_{n}={\rm sign}(\langle{\bf{u}}_{n},{\bf{x}}\rangle)
.
Consequently,
we can identify
\mu_{Z^{\circ}}=\|\cdot\|_{{\bf{L}},1}
, which, by duality, implies that

\mu_{Z}=\|\cdot\|_{\infty,{\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}}
. This then also yields
Z=B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
and
Z^{\circ}=B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

The matrix
{\bf{G}}
identified in the proof of Theorem
11
yields the explicit description of the underlying
unit ball, as formulated in the final part of Theorem
6
; that is, the canonical polyhedral form (
17
) with
{\bf{F}}={\rm Ext}({\bf{G}})=[{\bf{f}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\bf{f}}_{M}]
. This is illustrated in Figure
1
b with the facet vectors
{\bf{f}}_{m}
being overlaid.

The only limitation of (
31
) with trainable
{\bf{L}}
is that this parameterization is not universal (see Proposition
13
in Appendix B).
Yet, we can make an interesting connection with the synthesis formulation in
(
28
):
The rank condition in Theorem
11
guarantees the existence of a generalized inverse matrix
\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}=[\tilde{{\bf{g}}}_{1}\cdots\tilde{{\bf{g}}}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
(not necessarily unique) such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}{\bf{L}}={\bf{I}}_{d},

(38)

with the canonical solution being the pseudo-inverse

\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}={\bf{L}}^{\dagger}=({\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{L}})^{-1}{\bf{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
By making the change of variable
{\bf{z}}={\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}
in (
31
) and by observing that
\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}
is a valid (bilateral) inverse of
{\bf{L}}
as long as its domain is restricted to
{\rm Ran}({\bf{L}})=\{{\bf{z}}={\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}:{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, we get

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{\rm I}=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}=\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}\tilde{{\bf{z}}}:\ \tilde{{\bf{z}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{z}}\in{\rm Ran}({\bf{L}})}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}\},

(39)

which is almost the same as (
27
), except for the restriction on the search space for
{\bf{z}}
.
While the latter results in some loss of expressivity, it has the advantage of making the training of
\widetilde{{\bf{G}}}
much better posed by getting rid of the aforementioned “nontrivial null space” problem.

To demonstrate our point, we now concentrate on the scenario where the regularization operator can be factorized as
{\bf{L}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
{\bf{\Lambda}}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{N})
is a diagonal matrix of adjustable weights with
\lambda_{n}>0

and
{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
is a tight frame parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The defining property here is

\displaystyle{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\bf{I}}_{d},

(40)

which makes the inversion process straightforward. By using the same technique as in the derivation of
(
39
), we reformulate (
31
) as

\displaystyle S_{\rm III}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}={\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{{\bf{z}}}:\ \tilde{{\bf{z}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{z}}\in{\rm Ran}({\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}})}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}\}.

(41)

Next, we define the barrier functional

\displaystyle i_{{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\mathsf{T}}}({\bf{z}})=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{ if }{\bf{z}}\in{\rm Ran}({\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}})=\{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{z}}={\bf{z}}\}\\
+\infty,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

(42)

which is lower semicontinuous and convex.
With the help of the latter, we recast (
41
) as

\displaystyle S_{\rm III}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}={\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{{\bf{z}}}:\ \tilde{{\bf{z}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+i_{{\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}}({\bf{z}})+\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\bf{z}}\|_{\ell_{1}}\}

(43)

which is easier to minimize because the constraint on the search space has been removed.

5.4
Implementation of a Trainable Convex Regularizer

A standard requirement in computational imaging is that the regularizer be shift-invariant. In the context of (
31
), this is achieved by employing an
N_{\rm chan}
-channel filterbank as the operator
{\bf{L}}
. This takes
{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as input and produces
N_{\rm chan}
output channels, resulting in a feature vector
{\bf{z}}={\bf{L}}{\bf{s}}
of size
N=N_{\rm chan}\times d
. In our implementation, we use a Parseval filterbank

{\bf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(tight frame) parametrized by an orthogonal matrix of size
N_{\rm chan}\times N_{\rm chan}
, as described in
[
75
]
.
We also found it beneficial to force all the active regularization filters to have zero mean. This constraint is imposed within our orthogonal parametrization by setting the first convolution mask to be proportional to
{\bf{1}}
, effectively creating a moving average filter.

To learn the regularizer
{\bf{s}}\mapsto\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\bf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{s}}\|_{\ell_{1}}
that best represents a given class of signals/images, we follow the strategy of
[
34
]
and consider a basic denoising task with
{\bf{H}}={\bf{I}}
where the signal is corrupted by additive white Gaussian noise. To adjust the underlying model such as to achieve the best denoising on a representative set of images, we unroll the recurrent neural network in Algorithm 1 and/or use deep equilibrium
[
33
]
to learn the model parameters
({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

For the purpose of experimentation, we considered the more general family of optimization problems

\displaystyle S_{\rm IV}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{{\bf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{s}}\|_{2}^{2}+\langle{\bf{1}},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\bf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{s}})\rangle

(44)

\displaystyle=\big\{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}={\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\tilde{{\bf{z}}}:\ \tilde{{\bf{z}}}\in\arg\min_{{\bf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\bf{y}}-{\bf{H}}{\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\bf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+i_{{\bf{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}}({\bf{z}})+\langle{\bf{1}},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\bf{z}})\rangle\big\}

(45)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=(\phi_{n}):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a generic (pointwise) potential with convex component functions
\phi_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Our implementation is based on the equivalent analysis-by-synthesis formulation (
45
), which decouples the effect of the filters from the nonlinearities induced by
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
. The corresponding regularizer is

\displaystyle g({\bf{z}})=\langle{\bf{1}},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({\bf{z}})\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{n}(z_{n}),

(46)

which represents the most general separable form of potential.
Equation (
43
) is recovered by setting
\phi_{n}(z)=\lambda_{n}|z|
.
Here, we also consider the option of learning the
\phi_{n}
using the framework described in
[
76
]
,
which yields an architecture that offers greater expressivity than (
43
).

Since (
45
) is the sum of three functionals
whose gradient and/or individual proximal maps are easy to compute, it can be minimized
efficiently using proximal splitting methods
[
25
]
.
For our experiments, we adopted the Douglas-Ratchford splitting
[
11
,
65
]
, which resulted in the application-specific implementation
summarized in Algorithm 1.

Input:
The data vector
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the proximal operator of the potential
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, the tight frame
{\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, and the step size
\tau
.

{\bf{z}}^{(0)}={\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{H}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{y}}
(backprojection)

Main loop:

for

do

end

for

Output:

{\tilde{{\bf{s}}}}={\bf{T}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\bf{z}}^{(n)}
as solution of (
44
).

5.5.1
Training

The training was performed on a basic denoising task and is common to all scenarios, including MRI reconstruction.
The dataset consists of 238400 patches of size

Our convex denoisers come in three variants: The first, denoted by

5.6
Discussion

Our experiments demonstrate that our weighted-
\ell_{1}
scheme matches the performance of state-of-the-art ML frameworks for convex image reconstruction, while requiring significantly fewer parameters and reduced computational resources.

That said, there remains room for improvement, as the architecture used in our experiments only partially exploits the degrees of freedom offered by the polyhedral framework. This limitation arises because of: (i) our use of Parseval filterbanks, rather than general convolutional operators, to simplify training; and (ii) the adoption of an analysis-by-synthesis formulation, which serves only as a proxy for the universal dictionary-based architecture established by our theory.

Further research is needed to develop an architecture that is both machine-learning friendly and universal in the sense described in this work. Such an architecture would help delineate the ultimate capabilities of convex regularization.

Appendix A: (Semi)Norms and Banach Duality

Definition 12
(Norms and seminorms).

A seminorm
p
on a vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
is a real-valued functional
{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that, for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, satisfies the following properties:

•

Homogeneity:
|\alpha|\,p(x)=p(\alpha\,x)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{C}
).

•

Subadditivity (or triangle inequality):
p(x+y)\leq p(x)+p(y)
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

If, in addition,
p(x)=0\Leftrightarrow x=0
(positive-definiteness), then
p
is called norm and is
commonly denoted by
\|\cdot\|=p(\cdot)
.

A normed space is a vector space equipped with a norm—the generic form being
({\mathcal{X}},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
.
The latter is a Banach space whenever it is complete; that is, when every Cauchy sequence in
({\mathcal{X}},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})
has a limit in
{\mathcal{X}}
.

We denote the (closed) unit ball of a Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}=({\mathcal{X}},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})
by
B_{\mathcal{X}}
with

\displaystyle B_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1\}.

(48)

The continuous dual of
{\mathcal{X}}
is the vector space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
of continuous linear functionals on
{\mathcal{X}}
with generic element
f:x\mapsto\langle f,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. A fundamental property is that

{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}})

equipped with the dual norm

\displaystyle\forall f\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}:\quad\|f\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\sup_{x\in B_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\langle f,x\rangle.

(49)

is a Banach space as well
[
55
]
.

The unit ball
B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is a convex, center-symmetric subset
of
{\mathcal{X}}
(i.e., a disk) whose rescaled versions specify all Banach neighborhoods of the origin

0\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
Similar to (
49
) which makes an explicit connection between
B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
and the dual norm,
it possible to relate
B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
to the initial norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu_{B_{{\mathcal{X}}}}(\cdot)
with the help of the Minkowski functional (see Theorem
5
).

In finite dimensions, one defines the polar of any subset
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle B^{\circ}=\{{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle{\bf{y}},{\bf{x}}\rangle\leq 1,{\bf{x}}\in B\}=\{{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sup_{{\bf{x}}\in B}\langle{\bf{y}},{\bf{x}}\rangle\leq 1\},

(50)

with the latter necessarily being a closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that contains the origin (see
[
12
]
). If
B=B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is the unit ball of the Banach space

{\mathcal{X}}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})
, then its polar
B^{\circ}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
coincides with the unit ball
B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
of the dual space, in conformity with (
49
). Accordingly, we have
that
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\big(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu_{B^{\circ}_{{\mathcal{X}}}}(\cdot)\big)=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}})
, as well as
(B^{\circ}_{{\mathcal{X}}})^{\circ}=B_{{\mathcal{X}}}
because
{\mathcal{X}}
is reflexive.

Appendix B: Weighted
\ell_{1}
-norms are not universal

The following result implies
that the family of weighted
\ell_{1}
-norms is not universal in the sense of Theorem
7
.

Proposition 13
.

For
d>2
, there does not exist a rectangular matrix
{\bf{L}}
such that
\|{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}=\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{1}}
for all
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let us assume that there exists a matrix
{\bf{L}}=[{\bf{u}}_{1}\cdots{\bf{u}}_{N}]^{\mathsf{T}}
such that
\|{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{\infty}}=\|{\bf{L}}{\bf{x}}\|_{\ell_{1}}
. Then, by Theorem
11
, the
dual unit ball
B_{\ell_{1}}=B^{\circ}_{\ell_{\infty}}
must be a zonotope
Z
whose generic form is given by (
34
)
[
54
,
72
]
. On the one hand, we know that
B_{\ell_{1}}
is the
d
-dimensional cross-polytope, which is simplicial, with all its 2-faces being triangular. On the other hand, we can invoke
a classical result
[
71
, Thm. 3.5.2 p. 191]
, which states that a polytope is a zonotope if and only if all its 2-faces are centrally symmetric.
In fact, there is an even stronger result by McMullen stating that if the
k
-faces for
2\leq k\leq d-2
of a
d
-polytope
P
are centrally symmetric, then all the faces of
P
of every dimension are centrally symmetric
[
53
]
. Ultimately this means that a polytope is a zonotope if and only if all its faces are centrally symmetric.
This leads to a contradiction for
d\geq 3
since a non-degenerate triangle cannot be centrally symmetric.
