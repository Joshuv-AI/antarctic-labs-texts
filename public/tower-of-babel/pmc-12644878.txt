Improving retinal vessel assessment precision by integrating deep learning with interactive editing and graphical modeling
Authors: Sojung Go, Jaemin Chae, Uichan Kim, Jongsoo Lim, Jooyoung Kim, Stephen Hogg, Emanuele Trucco, Sang Jun Park, Soochahn Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present the SEoul Retinal Vessel Assessment Library (SERVAL), a novel software platform for precise quantitative measurement of vascular structures in fundus images. SERVAL integrates deep learning-based automatic artery and vein mask initialization, subpixel vessel centerline and boundary refinement, and interactive editing tools within a user-friendly graphical interface. From the refined artery and vein delineations, it enables accurate computation of a wide range of vessel assessment metrics, facilitating better characterization of complex vascular structures. We evaluate SERVAL through: (1) comparative analyses with existing platforms, highlighting its superior precision and structural detail; (2) longitudinal image studies demonstrating measurement consistency; and (3) a usability study confirming its clinical practicality. We expect SERVAL to serve as a valuable tool in clinical research, supporting the development of novel vascular biomarkers and diagnostic metrics for retinal and systemic diseases.

Introduction

Fundus cameras are widely available because they are noninvasive, inexpensive, and require neither radiation nor pharmaceuticals1. In addition to their accessibility, fundus imaging provides clear, high-resolution visualization of retinal tissues, including arteries, veins, the macula, and the optic disc, enabling direct observation of vascular structures. While they are primarily used for diagnosing retinal diseases including glaucoma, epiretinal membrane, and age-related macular degeneration, they can also be utilized to diagnose2 and assess3 chronic cardiovascular or neurovascular diseases such as diabetes and hypertension.

Many works have shown that effects of chronic vascular diseases may manifest as systemic alterations in the retinal vasculature4–8. For instance, chronic changes in blood pressure may cause changes in vessel tortuosity or caliber6, or a new vessel branch growth can be triggered from ischemia and hypoxia7. The tortuosity of retinal vessels may also change according to the progression of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP)9. To precisely monitor this change, the vessels need to be accurately segmented from the image, with delineation between arteries and veins. The calibers and structural characteristics of the vessels should then be precisely measured and assessed to enable rigorous quantitative comparisons.

To this end, many deep learning methodologies have made significant strides in advancing fully automatic vessel segmentation10–12 and artery/vein (A/V) classification11,13. Public datasets with expert annotations for vessel segmentation14–17 and A/V classification18,19 have been key to this progress. However, expert annotations remain constrained by limited availability, high cost, and potential errors, and no deep learning based method has yet achieved error-free performance13,20.

To achieve precise delineation of retinal arteries and veins, multiple processes must be integrated to correct errors and refine vessel masks. Consequently, a graphic user interface (GUI) provides a practical framework for combining these component processes. Software packages such as IVAN21, VAMPIRE22, SIVA23, and ALTAIR24 exemplify such systems, integrating diverse image processing or machine learning algorithms within interactive GUIs.

Despite substantial progress in deep learning based retinal vessel analysis, existing GUI packages have yet to incorporate recent methods for vessel segmentation25 and A/V classification26. Compared with the automatic vessel delineation algorithms embedded in current GUI packages, these methods offer significant improvements in both accuracy and structural detail. Accordingly, GUI design must evolve to accommodate the enhanced capabilities of deep learning based delineation, including the development of more effective tools for manual error correction and advanced parametric representations of complex vascular structures. These advancements warrant comprehensive analyses of user experience—such as the time required for manual interaction—and of resulting outcomes, including the characteristics of the generated vessel masks, to better understand the clinical implications of these emerging technologies.

In this paper, we present the SEoul Retinal Vessel Assessment Library (SERVAL), a unified framework that integrates methods addressing all of the aforementioned requirements. SERVAL combines modules for automatic vessel mask initialization, subpixel-level vessel centerline and boundary refinement with parametric structural modeling, interactive editing, and computation of diverse vessel metrics within a user-friendly GUI. Compared with existing packages, SERVAL offers a more comprehensive suite of functionalities, yielding vessel delineations that are both more accurate and richer in structural detail. We make SERVAL publicly available (https://github.com/snubhretina/SERVAL); the executable runs on Microsoft Windows and is optimized for systems with a modern CPU (e.g., Intel Xeon or i5), at least 16 GB of RAM, and a GPU with at least 4 GB of memory for efficient single-image processing.

The effectiveness of SERVAL is evaluated through comparative analyses with SIVA23, QUARTZ27, and VAMPIRE22, qualitative assessments on longitudinal sequences, and user studies with ten expert ophthalmologists. The results show clear improvements in accuracy, structural detail, and usability over existing tools. We expect SERVAL to facilitate the development of novel diagnostic metrics and to advance clinical research by overcoming current limitations in the assessment of fine vascular structures.

Related works

Multiple GUI systems have been developed for retinal vessel assessment. Unlike general-purpose medical image annotation tools such as MITK28 or ITK-Snap29, these systems provide functions specifically tailored for retinal fundus images. IVAN (integrative vessel analysis) was an early system requiring manual annotation of arteriolar and venule segments for automatic computation of vessel widths21. VAMPIRE (vessel assessment and measurement platform for images of the REtina)22, automated optic disc and vessel detection using ellipse fitting and supervised Bayesian classification based on wavelet feature responses30. SIVA (Singapore I vessel assessment)23 employed similar approaches, combining automated optic detection via ellipse fitting31 and vessel segmentation using a modified unsupervised trench detection algorithm32, while additionally incorporating A/V classification through supervised expectation maximization (EM).

ALTAIR (automatic image analyser to assess retinal vessel calibre)24 and QUARTZ (Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and size)27 also integrate multiple image processing and machine learning algorithms for optic disc detection, vessel segmentation, and A/V classification. Both SIVA and ALTAIR include interactive tools for editing, such as adding or removing vessels, connecting vessel segments, and toggling between vessel types. QUARTZ further enhances accuracy by parameterizing vessel centerlines with splines and estimating caliber via Gaussian fitting for subpixel precision. More comprehensive frameworks, such as ReVMS33 and RetinaCAD34, have also been proposed, combining automatic algorithms with interactive interfaces to support vessel topology and crossing point analyses.

Notably, none of these systems incorporate deep learning based methods for vessel segmentation or A/V classification.

Many of these GUI systems have been employed in various clinical studies21,35–37. Several comparative studies have evaluated the differences among IVAN, VAMPIRE, and SIVA in the retinal parameters they compute, particularly vessel caliber measurements38–41. These studies reported considerable discrepancies, leading to efforts toward standardization39,41.

With the advent of deep learning, much of the recent work has focused on automatic vessel segmentation42–49 and A/V classification50–53. While these methods have demonstrated steady performance improvements13,54, their accuracy remains limited by the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality annotated datasets for supervised learning. Unlike the massive datasets used to train large language models55,56, retinal vessel data are inherently difficult to collect and standardize, constraining the practical utility of fully automatic approaches.

To date, only a few studies have attempted to integrate deep learning within vessel assessment software. In SIVA-DLS (Deep Learning System)57 incorporates a CNN trained to directly estimate vessel calibers, while RMHAS (Retina-based Microvascular Health Assessment System)58 employs a CNN to sequentially predict vessel, A/V, and optic disc masks. Building upon these efforts, SERVAL integrates state-of-the-art deep learning models with post-processing and interactive editing tools within a unified framework.

Related works on vessel modeling and parameterization are also noteworthy. Various approaches have utilized filtering and differentiation59 or parametric modeling with polynomial60, Gaussian61, or Gaussian derived functions62 to analyze vessel intensity profiles for caliber estimation. Spline fitting has been applied to represent vessel centerlines59,62, enabling normal direction estimation, and corresponding intensity profiling. In SERVAL, the estimated vessel caliber is further used to refine the vessel centerline, leveraging the accuracy of deep learning-based segmentation results to enhance structural consistency.

Following the findings of Isensee et al.63, which showed that a properly configured U-Net64 can match or surpass more complex models, we adopt existing CNN-based architectures65,66 and prioritize the quality of training data over architectural modifications.

Training dataset construction

Ground truth (GT) vessel masks were generated using paired fundus and fluorescein angiography (FA) images, as vessels are more distinctly delineated in FA. We constructed the FIREFLY (Fundus Images REgistered with FLuorescein angiographY) dataset, following the procedures described in Noh et al.25,65,66, comprising 554 paired fundus FA images from 402 patients acquired with Canon CF60Uvi, Kowa VX-10, and Kowa VX-10a cameras. Each FA sequence contains up to 20 frames, and all images were resized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1536 \times 1024$$\end{document}. Data collected between 2003 2016 were anonymized to ensure confidentiality, and the dataset primarily includes healthy participants. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (no. B-1810-501-104; approval date: 12 Oct 2018). The study was carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital.

GT construction consisted of: (i) deep learning based vessel mask initialization65 followed by manual refinement, (ii) deep learning based A/V mask initialization66 followed by manual refinement, and (iii) an optional generative data augmentation step that synthesizes A/V masks and corresponding fundus images67. The overall process is illustrated in Fig. 2. Most of these steps were implemented using an early version of SERVAL with CNNs pretrained on the DRIVE dataset14,18.

For vessel mask initialization, vessel segmentation was applied to each FA frame using a CNN trained on inverted grayscale DRIVE14 images. The resulting FA vessel masks were aligned via two-step registration—SIFT-based keypoint matching68 followed by B-spline non-rigid deformation optimized with L-BFGS-B69—and combined using pixelwise max-pooling. The aggregated FA mask is then registered to the fundus image—using a similar two-step registration process—and refined manually in SERVAL by two experts (a board-certified ophthalmologist and a researcher).

A/V mask initialization employed the hybrid CNN GNN model of Noh et al.66, using the fundus image, FA sequence, and vessel mask as input. The model, initially trained on GT A/V masks of DRIVE18, produced preliminary A/V masks that were again manually refined. Following Go et al.26, arteries and veins were encoded as separate channels to enable distinct vessel tree construction.

To augment the dataset, synthetic fundus A/V mask pairs were generated using the method of Go et al.67. We employed a diffusion model (DDPM)70,71 to synthesize A/V masks, and a pix2pix GAN72 to produce corresponding fundus images. The resulting FIREFLY-Gen dataset is publicly available73, while the original FIREFLY dataset remains restricted due to Institutional Review Board (IRB) regulations.

Vessel segmentation and A/V classification models

The FIREFLY and FIREFLY-Gen datasets were used to train the scale-space approximated CNN (SSANet)74 for vessel segmentation. SSANet employs multi-scale feature representations via subsampling and upsampling, enabling accurate delineation of fine vessels that are more clearly visible in FIREFLY images due to the use of FA.

For A/V classification, a dual hierarchical vessel graph network (Dual HVGN)26—a hybrid CNN GNN architecture extending HVGN66—was trained using the same datasets. The model takes the fundus image and SSANet-derived vessel mask as input and outputs two-channel artery and vein masks.

Both models were trained using the Adam optimizer75 with a pixelwise binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss function.

Optic disc detection

We adopt the concept of concentric zones based on the optic disc diameter, as originally defined in the Beaver Dam Eye Study21, to standardize vessel measurement regions. To automate this process, we implement a deep learning based optic disc detection method using Faster R-CNN76, which offers an effective balance between computational efficiency and detection accuracy.

The model was trained on a dataset of 13,383 fundus images annotated by a board-certified ophthalmologist with disc center points and a fixed bounding box size. During inference, the disc center is extracted from the bounding box with the highest confidence score (threshold = 0.9).

Following detection, a heuristic ellipse fitting procedure is applied. A rough disc radius is estimated by identifying the bounding box that maximizes the color contrast between pixels inside and outside the box. Potential ellipse boundary points are then determined along eight equiangular lines radiating from the detected center, within a predefined search range around the estimated radius. Finally, the five ellipse parameters are analytically computed from these boundary points.

Interactive editing tools

In addition to a general brush tool for manual vessel correction, SERVAL provides three interactive semi-automatic editing tools designed to address common errors: (i) missing connections at vessel branching regions, (ii) mislabeling of artery or vein segments, and (iii) inaccurate ellipsoid fitting of the optic disc. Connecting disconnected vessels

Disconnections in vessel masks commonly occur in thin vessels with low contrast or at bifurcations and crossings where visual ambiguity causes confusion with background textures.

Overview of the vessel mask refinement and parameterization process. The vessel mask consists of two channels representing arteries and veins.

When such errors are identified, the user provides two anchor points on the vessel mask to indicate the disconnection. If these points do not align with the vessel centerline, they are adjusted to the nearest centerline locations. The geodesic curve connecting the anchors is then computed using the fast marching method77 on the logit map from the vessel segmentation CNN. This curve is added to the existing centerline mask. The initial radius of the new segment is set to the mean radius estimated through the refinement and spline fitting process and is subsequently updated through iterative refinement.

Toggling artery/vein labels

Artery/vein mislabeling often occurs in thin vessels adjacent to other branches of a different type. In toggle mode, the user simply clicks on the mislabeled region, after which region growing is performed to identify the connected component containing the clicked pixel. The labels of all pixels within this component are then switched between artery and vein.

Adjusting the optic disc

Automated optic disc detection may yield inaccurate sizes due to under- or overestimation of the disc boundary. To correct such errors, the user selects five or more points along the true optic disc margin. A linear least-squares fitting is then applied to determine an optimal ellipse, updating its five parameters: center coordinates, major and minor axis lengths, and rotation angle.

Branching point detection and branch partition

Skeletonization of the vessel masks is first performed using Lee’s algorithm79, which iteratively erodes the binary mask through shape-based lookup operations until convergence. This produces an initial vessel centerline mask with 8-neighbor connectivity.

Branching points are then identified using the hit-or-miss transform80 with predefined 3-by-3 pixel patterns corresponding to typical branching configurations. The detected branching points serve as seeds for partitioning the vessel mask into individual vessel branches.

To define branching regions, we examine pixel labels along concentric circles of varying radii centered at each branching point. A branching region is identified when the number of label transitions (among artery, vein, or background) along a circle exceeds five. The circle with the smallest such radius is used to delineate the boundary between the branching region and adjoining branches. Centerline pixels within this circle are defined as branching region pixels, while those outside are grouped into individual branches using connected component analysis. A representative example of this partition process is shown in Fig. 4c.

Finally, two separate tree objects—one for arteries and one for veins—are constructed to encode the complete vascular topology. As shown in Fig. 7, branching points and regions serve as vertices, and individual branches as edges. These tree structures provide a unified foundation for computing global quantitative metrics of the vascular network.

Vessel assessment metrics

With the vessels precisely delineated and parameterized, various quantitative metrics can be computed. These metrics are categorized into three groups: (i) attributes of individual branch segments, (ii) characteristics of branching points, and (iii) aggregate measurements describing the overall vascular structure. The following subsections summarize the key metrics implemented in SERVAL, many of which have also been introduced in prior systems such as VAMPIRE22, SIVA23, and QUARTZ27.

To ensure consistent regional measurements across subjects, SERVAL employs zone definitions based on the optic disc region, following established conventions22,23,27. Although the optic disc is represented as an ellipse in SERVAL, the closest fitting circle with radius r is used for zone-based analysis. Zone B is defined as the annular region between 2r and 3r, while Zone C covers the area between 2r and 5r.

Experiment

Settings

Experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with a 2.1 GHz Intel Xeon CPU and a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. The processing and annotation of a single image required 4282 MiB of GPU memory, with an inference time of less than 5 seconds at a resolution of 1024 pixels. The client application operated with a memory footprint of less than 90 MB.

Comparative evaluation

Datasets

Evaluation was performed on 75 images from the FIREFLY dataset, consisting of 25 optic disc-centered and 50 macular-centered images, each with a resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1024 \times 1536$$\end{document}. Additional experiments were conducted on publicly available datasets representing various disease conditions, including CHASE_DB116, HRF17, UoA-DR83, and the UK Biobank (UKBB)84 datasets.

The CHASE_DB1 dataset16 contains images from both eyes of 14 primary school children (8 White, 3 South Asian, and 3 of other ethnic origin; mean age 10 years) from North-East London, captured with a hand-held Nidek NM-200-D fundus camera. The HRF dataset17 comprises 45 images, including 15 from healthy patients, 15 from patients with diabetic retinopathy, and 15 from patients with glaucoma. The UoA-DR dataset83 contains 200 images collected in collaboration with three hospitals in India, acquired with a Zeiss VISUCAM 500 fundus camera. Subject demographic information is unavailable due to anonymization. The UK Biobank dataset84 consists of approximately 174,000 retinal fundus images from 500,000 UK participants, obtained using Topcon 3D OCT-1000 Mark II and Topcon TRC-NW6S cameras, covering both healthy individuals and patients with diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or glaucoma.

Comparison methods

We conducted comparative evaluations between SERVAL, SIVA23, QUARTZ27, and VAMPIRE22. For SIVA, we used a licensed version (v3.0); for QUARTZ, we employed the MATLAB implementation (v.R2014a) provided in the QUARTZ_git repository85. VAMPIRE evaluations were performed using the original implementation, operated directly by its developers as described in Mookiah et al.86.

For SERVAL, vessel analysis was performed through automatic initialization followed by manual expert editing, and finalized via refinement and parameterization. The same deep learning models trained on the FIREFLY dataset were used for vessel mask initialization across all datasets. With SIVA, experts manually annotated the centerlines of major arteries, from which vessel caliber and related metrics were automatically computed. For QUARTZ, only automatic vessel segmentation and analysis were performed, as no manual editing functionality is provided.

Manual editing for both SERVAL and SIVA was carried out by ten board-certified ophthalmologists. Since VAMPIRE s initialization model was trained on the HRF dataset, comparative results are presented only for the CHASE_DB1, UoA-DR, and UK Biobank datasets.

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative comparisons of the delineated vessel regions from the automatically obtained (initial) results of SIVA, QUARTZ, VAMPIRE, and SERVAL, as well as the post-editing results of SIVA, VAMPIRE, and SERVAL, are summarized in Table 1.

For centerline distances, the post-editing results of SERVAL show the highest consistency with the reference, which is expected since the reference annotations were produced using the same system. In addition, SERVAL consistently yielded a higher number of high-level branch segments across all datasets. Specifically, the number of Level 7 (L7) and Level 8 (L8) branches followed the order of SERVAL > VAMPIRE > SIVA > QUARTZ, reflecting the superior level of structural detail achievable with SERVAL.

Across datasets, a notable increase in centerline distances was observed for the initialization results of SERVAL— and, to a lesser extent, for QUARTZ and VAMPIRE—particularly in the UKBB and UoA-DR datasets. For SERVAL, these deviations were primarily attributed to segmentation errors caused by membrane-like textures. Although the corresponding editing time for SERVAL increased, the final post-editing results aligned closely with the reference, demonstrating the adequacy and usability of the GUI platform. Conversely, SIVA exhibited more consistent centerline distances across datasets, indicating greater robustness to domain variations. Overall, the results highlight a tradeoff between the level of structural detail—quantified by the number of branch segments per level—and the manual editing time required.

Furthermore, Table 2 presents a comparison of the average CRAE, CRVE, and AVR values obtained from SIVA, QUARTZ, and SERVAL. While SIVA computes these parameters directly in microns, the measurements for QUARTZ and SERVAL were calculated from pixelwise masks and converted to micron units following the protocol of Mautuit et al.41. Discrepancies among the methods were observed, primarily due to variations in vessel masks and pixel-to-micron conversion rates, suggesting the need for further examinations on measurement consistency in SERVAL.

In addition, four images were randomly sampled from each dataset—resulting in a total of 20 images—to evaluate performance on both original and half-resolution images. As summarized in Table 3, the number of branch segments per level decreased at most levels for the half-resolution images, indicating the loss of finer vessels due to reduced image detail and subsequent segmentation omissions. Nevertheless, thin vascular branches remained detectable up to approximately Level 7, suggesting that while low-resolution images allow analysis of major vascular structures, high-resolution images are essential for accurate and detailed assessment of the microvasculature.

The zoomed regions highlight detailed comparisons against the reference centerline annotations. A relatively greater number of venules and arterioles are clearly delineated in the results from SERVAL compared to the other methods. This improvement is attributed to the deep learning-based initialization employed by SERVAL, which enhances the accuracy of vessel delineation and enables computation of more detailed, fine-scale vascular metrics.

Furthermore, Fig. 9 shows an example from the CHASE_DB1 dataset where membrane-like textures caused numerous false artery detections. A marked reduction in these false positives is observed after fine-tuning the deep learning model on the dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 9c.

Usability study

To evaluate the usability and practical utility of the program, we conducted a user study involving ten ophthalmic experts. Each expert was assigned a distinct subset of fundus images drawn from the same cohort as the FIREFLY dataset, totaling 248 images. Example GT annotations from public datasets were provided as references, along with detailed instructions for editing the initial vessel segmentation and A/V classification results generated by the deep learning-based modules using the interactive editing tools in SERVAL.

For each expert, we recorded the total elapsed time required to edit each image. Since the editing time varied substantially across experts and image difficulty levels, we defined a normalized metric, editing time per error reduction (ET/ER), calculated as the total editing time divided by the reduction of error. Here, the error is defined as the mean centerline distance with the reference GT.

Notably, for several experts, the total editing time decreased considerably over the course of the study, and in the best case (Expert 8), editing was completed in only one-fifth of the initial time required.

Discussion

The proposed SERVAL system provides a comprehensive framework for retinal vessel assessment by integrating deep learning based vessel segmentation, artery/vein classification, and anatomical landmark detection with interactive refinement and parametric modeling tools. Unlike previous GUI-based systems such as SIVA, QUARTZ, and VAMPIRE, which rely on conventional machine learning algorithms and manual annotations, SERVAL incorporates CNN and GNN architectures trained on the FIREFLY dataset to enable automatic initialization with state-of-the-art structural accuracy. The integration of subpixel edge localization, spline-based vessel parameterization, and tree-structured topology modeling allows for fine-grained analysis of vascular geometry and improved consistency in quantitative measurements.

Furthermore, SERVAL introduces a user-centric interface that bridges automated segmentation with manual expert correction, providing both efficiency and precision in vessel delineation. The usability study demonstrated that experts could achieve subpixel accuracy within minutes, with editing times decreasing as familiarity with the system increased. Combined with robust performance across multiple public datasets and consistency in longitudinal analysis, these results highlight SERVAL as a practical and extensible platform for clinical and research applications in retinal vascular assessment.

In addition to our longitudinal evaluation presented in the Experimental Section, prior studies have also reported that vascular changes are more pronounced in penetrating and internal arterioles and venules compared to pial vessels in normal and diseased mice87. These findings further underscore the clinical potential of fine-scale vessel assessment. By enabling precise analysis of microvascular structures within fundus images, SERVAL offers a promising tool for advancing early diagnosis of systemic and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer s disease, where subtle vascular alterations may serve as early biomarkers.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. First, improving the robustness of deep learning based initialization across heterogeneous image domains is essential. Recent progress in domain generalization and domain adaptation88,89 provides potential directions to enhance the adaptability of the current framework. Second, reducing the degree of manual interaction remains an important goal. Incorporating recent methods in interactive segmentation90,91 within the GUI could further streamline the editing process. Future work will focus on integrating these developments to improve both the scalability and usability of SERVAL for broader clinical and research applications.
