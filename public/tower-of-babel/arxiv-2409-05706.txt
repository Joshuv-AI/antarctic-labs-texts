Quantitative approximation of stochastic kinetic equations: from discrete to continuum
Zimo Hao, Khoa Lê and Chengcheng Ling

Abstract
. We study the convergence of a generic tamed Euler-Maruyama (EM) scheme for the kinetic type stochastic differential equations (SDEs) (also known as second order SDEs) with singular coefficients in both weak and strong probabilistic senses. We show that when the drift exhibits a relatively low regularity compared to the state of the art, the singular system is well-defined both in the weak and strong probabilistic senses. Meanwhile, the corresponding tamed EM scheme is shown to converge at the rate of 1 / 2 1/2 in both the weak and the strong senses. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): Primary 60H35, 65C30, 60H10; Secondary 60H50, 60L90, 35K65, 35R05, 35B65. Keywords: Singular SDEs; degenerate noise; weak approximation; strong approximation; kinetic SDEs; Second order SDEs; (tamed-)Euler-Maruyama scheme; regularization by noise; stochastic sewing; Zvonkin’s transformation



1.
Introduction

We consider the following SDE:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=V_{t}dt\\
dV_{t}=b(t,Z_{t})dt+dW_{t},\quad Z_{0}=\zeta=(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\end{cases}

where
Z_{t}=(X_{t},V_{t})\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
,
t\geqslant 0
,
b:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is measurable, and
W
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},({\mathscr{F}}_{s})_{s\geqslant 0},{\mathbb{P}})
. (
1.1
) is one of the typical models that describes the Hamiltonian mechanics in the form of Langevin equation (
[
34
,
37
]
). In such context,
Z_{t}=(X_{t},V_{t})
usually denotes the position and velocity of a moving particle at a time
t
. In statistical mechanics, (
1.1
)
leads to the following diffusion (or ”Fokker–Planck”) equation which describes the evolution of the probability density
u(t,x,v)
of
Z_{t}
(
[
33
,
38
]
):

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{v}u+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u+b\cdot\nabla_{v}u.

In the literature, it has been rather well-understood that the
kinetic structure has good “propagation” properties from the
v
-component to the
x
-component (e.g.
[
1
,
6
,
21
]
). Moreover, notice that even though in (
1.1
) the noise is degenerate because it acts directly only on the momentum coordinates and
not positions, it can also be shortly wrote as for
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}

\displaystyle dZ_{t}=F(t,Z_{t})dt+\Sigma(t,Z_{t})dW_{t},F(t,z):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v\\
b(t,z)\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},\Sigma(t,z):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

which falls into the setting that known Hörmander condition (
[
18
]
) holds. In this spirit, the study on the well-posedness of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) with singular
b
in parallel gains quite development: on the regularity results for (
1.2
),
[
1
,
33
]
gain the optimal Sobolev regularity when
b\equiv 1
; later on for non-constant particularly singular
b
, various of regularity estimates also are shown within series of work
[
21
,
6
,
42
,
15
,
4
]
(also the references inside); meanwhile due to the strong connection between (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), the well-posedness for (
1.1
) is established for various types of singular
b
correspondingly within
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
10
,
36
,
13
]
. Along this process, we can see that the system (
1.1
) concerning its well-posedness is gradually understood well for singular
b
comparing with classical first order SDEs (
[
44
,
39
,
23
]
).

Due to the needs from simulation and application side, also from the observation that stochastic numerical analysis for the first order singular SDEs obtains delightful results (
[
2
,
7
,
28
,
30
,
22
,
20
,
17
]
), it is demanding to implement numerical methods to the second order singular SDEs as well, which however exist fewer. Let us first mention some of results on the one of simplest numerical approximation– the Euler-Maruyama scheme (EM scheme for short) for the singular first order SDEs: on the strong convergence rate (i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{n}\|
)
[
2
,
7
,
28
,
30
,
31
]
show the known
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
-optimal convergence rate, and
[
22
,
17
]
show the weak convergence rate (i.e.
|{\mathbb{E}}f(Z_{T})-{\mathbb{E}}f(Z_{T}^{n})|
for suitable test function
f
). According to our best knowledge, in the early stage
[
29
]
and
[
37
]
study the time-discrete approximations (for instance, explicit and implicit Euler-Maruyama scheme) for (
1.1
) under Lyapunov function type conditions which usually require certain regularity on
b
. Later
[
24
]
obtains the non asymptotic bounds for the Monte Carlo algorithm associated to the Euler discretization for bounded
b
. Recently
[
25
]
that seems to be the first one obtains the quantitative strong convergence bound on the EM scheme with order
n^{-\frac{1}{4}}
for
b
being piecewise Lipschitz function. Evidently, comparing with the study on EM scheme for singular first order SDEs, the quantitative analysis on singular second order SDEs is largely open.

In the current paper we are interested in giving the upper bound of the strong and weak EM scheme for (
1.1
) under somehow the minimal
assumptions (see
Assumption

2.6
and
Assumption

2.7
) that guarantee the SDE is correspondingly strongly and weakly well-posed.
Therefore we propose the following
tamed Euler scheme
to approximate (
1.1
).

Proposed numerical scheme

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
k_{n}(t):=\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n},t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Let
\{Z^{n}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}:=\{(X^{n}_{t},V^{n}_{t})\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be the solution to the following Euler scheme

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X^{n}_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}V^{n}_{s}ds,\\
V^{n}_{t}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{s-k_{n}(s)}b_{n}(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds+W_{t},\end{cases}

where for some parameter
\vartheta>0
and a probability density function
\phi
, let

(1.6)

\displaystyle b_{n}:=b*\phi_{n},\quad\phi_{n}(x,v):=n^{4d\vartheta}\phi(n^{3\vartheta}x,n^{\vartheta}v),

and for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}f(z):=f(\Gamma_{t}z):=f(x+tv,v),\quad z=(x,v).

As we may observe, comparing with standard Euler-Maruyama scheme for the first order SDEs, we replace the classical Euler scheme
\int^{t}_{0}b(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
with the term
\int^{t}_{0}\Gamma_{s-k_{n}(s)}b_{n}(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
. There are mainly the following reasons that
\int^{t}_{0}\Gamma_{s-k_{n}(s)}b_{n}(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
should be the considered one:

Firstly, we use tamed term
b_{n}
instead of
b
itself to guarantee that the solution sequence
Z_{t}^{n}
to the discretized equation (
1.5
) is well-defined, which is similar to the idea from
[
16
,
28
]
(known as “taming technique"). Moreover, the scheme with
\int^{t}_{0}\Gamma_{s-k_{n}(s)}b_{n}(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds
provides a faster convergence rate which could be better for applications. We can illustrate this point easily: for any smooth function
f=f(x,v)=f(x)
mapping
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
to
\mathbb{R}
, we analyze the following error: denote

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\Big|f\Big(\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,W_{t}\Big)-f\Big(\int_{0}^{k_{n}(t)}W_{s}ds,W_{k_{n}(s)}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big|f\Big(\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds\Big)-f\Big(\int_{0}^{k_{n}(t)}W_{s}ds\Big)\Big|,

clearly

\displaystyle I_{n}\lesssim\int_{k_{n}(t)}^{t}\mathbb{E}|W_{s}|ds\lesssim\int_{k_{n}(t)}^{t}s^{\frac{1}{2}}ds\lesssim n^{-1}.

In contrast, if we replace
I_{n}
with
J_{n}
defined as

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\Big|f\Big(\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,W_{t}\Big)-\Gamma_{t-k_{n}(t)}f\Big(\int_{0}^{k_{n}(t)}W_{s}ds,W_{k_{n}(s)}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big|f\Big(\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds\Big)-f\Big(\int_{0}^{k_{n}(t)}W_{s}ds+t-k_{n}(t)W_{k_{n}(s)}\Big)\Big|,

it yields

\displaystyle J_{n}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\Big|\int_{k_{n}(t)}^{t}(W_{s}-W_{k_{n}(s)})ds\Big|\lesssim\int_{k_{n}(t)}^{t}(s-k_{n}(s))^{\frac{1}{2}}ds\lesssim n^{-\frac{3}{2}},

which shows that the tamed term could provide a convergence rate superior to
n^{-1}
.

Secondly, from one technical view point,
given that in this paper we employ the semi-group to enhance the regularity of some singular functions like drift term
b
, which may not even be continuous, for the kinetic SDEs, in general we can not avoid to consider the semi-group with the following form (see also (
3.11
) and (
3.12
))

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x,v):={\mathbb{E}}f\left(x+tv+\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,v+W_{t}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\Big(f\big(G_{t}(\Gamma_{t}(x,v))\big)\Big),G_{t}:=(\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,W_{t}).

Comparing with the standard heat semi-group which relates to the heat equation, the above one is the “analog semi-group” corresponding to the kinetic equation (
1.2
) (
[
13
,
14
,
8
,
24
]
). It implies that we need to consider regularizing effect from
P_{t}
for the singular functional with the form

(1.7)

\displaystyle P_{t}f-\Gamma_{t-s}P_{s}f,\quad 0<s<t,

rather than the classical form
P_{t}f-P_{s}f
used for the singular first order SDEs (e.g.
[
2
,
7
,
28
]
). For further details, we refer to
[
43
,
13
]
. In this paper, we derive an estimate for

\displaystyle P_{t}f-P_{s}\Gamma_{t-s}f,

which exhibits the same level of regularity as (
1.7
) (see
Remark

3.11
below).

Obtained convergence rate

In the end, we are able to show both the weak and strong convergence of the EM scheme with order
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
(see details in
Theorem

2.8
) under
Assumption

2.6
and
Assumption

2.7
. It turns out to be the first new quantitative bounds on the EM scheme for singular kinetic SDEs; the rate also is faster than limited known results from
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
. We also want to mention the conjecture from
[
25
]
which says the rate
n^{-1/4}
obtained in
[
25
]
is not optimal and expects better convergence rate. Our result can also be evident for this point.
Even for the non-degenerate case, it should be noted that in
[
22
]
, the weak convergence rate for taming
L^{q}L^{p}
drift is
(1-\frac{d}{p}-\frac{2}{q})/2
, which is called the "gap to singularity". Our weak convergence rate
1/2
is independent of
p
and gives a strong evidence that this gap to singularity can be removed.
Meanwhile the assumptions are weaker than the known results for the weak well-posedness (
[
4
,
24
]
) and strong well-posedness (
[
3
,
40
]
).

Here we roughly comment on the key ideas inside the proof.

Idea of the proof

(1)

For quantitative weak error estimates, i.e. for the bounds of
|{\mathbb{E}}f(Z_{T})-{\mathbb{E}}f(Z_{T}^{n})|
, where
f
is just being bounded (again this is singular), by using Itô’s formula to
r\to P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})
and
P_{t-r}\varphi(Z^{n}_{r})
, it reads (see also details from (
6.8
))

\displaystyle|{\langle}\varphi,\rho_{t}-\rho^{n}_{t}{\rangle}|\leqslant

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}(b-b_{n})(r,Z_{r})\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})dr\Big|

\displaystyle+\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}b_{n}(r)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r})-b_{n}(r)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r}^{n})dr\Big|

\displaystyle+\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\big(b_{n}(r,Z^{n}_{r})-\Gamma_{r-k_{n}(r)}b_{n}(r,Z_{k_{n}(r)}^{n})\big)\cdot\nabla_{v}P_{t-r}\varphi(Z_{r}^{n})dr\Big|

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}^{n}(t)+I_{2}^{n}(t)+I_{3}^{n}(t).

Since in this case
b
and
\phi
both have low regularity, known estimates can not be applied. We need to fine estimate
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
separately. Inside the proof, we get the desired bound of
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
via applying the regularization properties of kinetic semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
collected in
Section

3
, estimates on
I_{1}
further heavily relies on the paraproduct analysis.

(2)

For the strong convergence rate, we first write

\displaystyle Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t}V_{s}-V_{s}^{n}ds\\
\int_{0}^{t}b(s,Z_{s})-\Gamma_{s-k_{n}(s)}b_{n}(s,Z^{n}_{k_{n}(s)})ds\end{pmatrix}=:\begin{pmatrix}I_{t}^{n}\\
S_{t}^{n}\end{pmatrix}.

For the bound of
{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{n}|^{m}
,
m\geqslant 1
, the main task is to estimate
{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|S_{t}^{n}|^{m}
since the linear
I_{t}^{n}
term is ready for the form of applying Grönwall’s inequality. Via John-Nirenberg inequality (
[
27
]
) together with Girsanov transformation and Zvonkin transformation, it is then reduced to the estimate on

\displaystyle\Big\|\sup_{t\in(0,1)}\big|\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}U(r,M_{r}(z)){\Big(}\Gamma_{r-k_{n}(r)}b(r,M_{k_{n}(r)}(z))

\displaystyle-b(r,M_{r}(z)){\Big)}dr\big|\Big\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}

where
M_{t}(z):=(x+tv+\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,v+W_{t})
,
\forall z=(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,
U
is the solution to (
1.2
) with extra forcing field
b^{n}
(so call
Zvonkin transformation
method). The bound
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is achieved by applying
stochastic sewing lemma
(
[
26
]
) together with regularity estimates involved with (
1.7
), which also yields the desired strong convergence rate
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
after applying the Grönwall’s inequality in the end.

2.1.
Notations

Here we collect all of the notations that we quite often use in the whole text. We start with introducing the anisotropic Besov spaces.

Anisotropic Besov spaces

For an
L^{1}
-integrable function
f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, let
\hat{f}
be the Fourier transform of
f
defined by

\hat{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}f(z)dz,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

and
\check{f}
the inverse Fourier transform of
f
defined by

\check{f}(z):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}f(\xi)d\xi,\quad z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

Let
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(3,1)
. For
z=(x,v)
,
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, we introduce the anisotropic distance

|z-z^{\prime}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}:=|x-x^{\prime}|^{1/3}+|v-v^{\prime}|.

Note that
z\mapsto|z|_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is not smooth.
For
r>0
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, we also introduce the ball centered at
z
and with radius
r
with respect to the above distance
as follows:

B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}(z):=\{z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}:|z^{\prime}-z|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant r\},\ \ B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}:=B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r}(0).

Let
\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
be a symmetric
C^{\infty}
-function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with

\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi)=1\ \mathrm{for}\ \xi\in B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}\ \mathrm{and}\ \chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi)=0\ \mathrm{for}\ \xi\notin B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}.

We define

\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}(\xi):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(2^{-j{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi)-\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(2^{-(j-1){\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi),\ \ &j\geqslant 1,\\
&\chi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}(\xi),\ \ &j=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
,

2^{s{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi=(2^{3s}\xi_{1},2^{s}\xi_{2}).

Note that

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\rm supp}(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j})\subset\big\{\xi:2^{j-1}\leqslant|\xi|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant 2^{j+1}\big\},\ j\geqslant 1,\ {\rm supp}(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0})\subset B^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2},

and

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}(\xi)=1,\ \ \forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the space of all Schwartz functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
the dual space of
{\mathcal{S}}
, called the tempered distribution space.
For given
j\geqslant 0
, the dyadic anisotropic block operator
\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}
is defined on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f(z):=(\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}\hat{f})\check{\ }(z)=\check{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}*f(z),

where the convolution is understood in the distributional sense and by scaling,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\check{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}(z)=2^{(j-1)4d}\check{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}(2^{(j-1){\boldsymbol{a}}}z),\ \ j\geqslant 1.

For
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, by definition it is easy to see that

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}={\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j},\ \mbox{ where }\ \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}:=\sum_{|i-j|\leqslant 2}{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},

where we used the convention that
{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}:=0
for
i<0
. Moreover,
by the symmetry of
\phi^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}
,

{\langle}{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f,g{\rangle}={\langle}f,{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}g{\rangle},\ \ f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime},\ g\in{\mathcal{S}}.

Similarly, we can define the isotropic block operator
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f=\check{\phi}_{j}*f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\rm supp}(\phi_{j})\subset\big\{\xi:2^{j-1}\leqslant|\xi|\leqslant 2^{j+1}\big\},\ j\geqslant 1,\ {\rm supp}(\phi_{0})\subset B_{2}.

Now we introduce the following anisotropic Besov spaces (cf.
[
35
, Chapter 5]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
q\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
. The anisotropic Besov space is defined by

\mathbf{B}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}:=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}:\|f\|_{\mathbf{B}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}:=\left(\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\big(2^{js}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\big)^{q}\right)^{1/q}<\infty\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{z}^{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}:=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|f(\cdot,v)\|_{p_{x}}^{p_{v}}dv\right)^{1/p_{v}}.

In the following we also use the notation
\|f\|_{L_{T}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}_{z}^{\boldsymbol{p}})}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\boldsymbol{p}}_{z}}^{q}dt\right)^{1/q}

Note that for any
f\in{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}
, by (
2.2
) we have

(2.8)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}f\ \mbox{ in ${\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}$.}

The mixed Besov space defined as follows
shall be used in the study of strong solutions of kinetic SDEs.

Definition 2.2
.

Let

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}:={\mathbb{B}}^{s,\infty}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}},\ \ {\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{p}:={\mathbb{B}}^{s,\infty}_{p}.

Definition 2.3
.

For a function

\delta^{(1)}_{h}f(z):=\delta_{h}f(z):=f(z+h)-f(z),\ \ z,h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

For

\delta^{(M+1)}_{h}f(z)=\delta_{h}\circ\delta^{(M)}_{h}f(z).

[

]

[

Proposition 2.4
.

For
s>0
,
q\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty]^{2}
, an equivalent norm of

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\asymp\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\left(\frac{\|\delta_{h}^{([s]+1)}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}{|h|^{s}_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)^{q}\frac{dh}{|h|^{4d}_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)^{1/q}+\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}=:[f]_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s,q}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}+\|f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}},

where

is the integer part of
s
. In particular,
{\mathbb{C}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}:=\mathbf{B}^{s,\infty}_{\infty;{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is the anisotropic

Some conventions

On finite dimensional vector spaces we always use the Euclidean norm.

Without confusion we sometimes also use

and

between vectors if their each component shares the same order, e.g. we say

for each

In proofs, the notation

depends only on the parameters claimed in the corresponding statement. If the constant depends on any further parameter

First, we define for

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda(t-s)}(t-s)^{-\frac{\theta}{2}}\Big(\|{b}_{s}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\|u_{s}\|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{1+\delta\vee 0}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}+\|f_{s}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\Big)ds.

\displaystyle\|{b}\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}({\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}})}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda(t-s)}(t-s)^{-\frac{1-\delta\wedge 0+{\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot d/{\boldsymbol{p}}}{2}}\|u_{s}\|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{1+\delta\vee 0}_{\boldsymbol{a}}}ds

\displaystyle+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}({\mathbb{B}}^{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}})}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\lambda s}s^{-\frac{1-\delta\wedge 0+{\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot d/{\boldsymbol{p}}}{2}}ds.

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}({\mathbb{B}}^{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}})}\lambda^{\frac{\theta-2}{2}}.

(ii) Noting that for any

The following is another auxiliary lemma that is also crucial for showing the weak uniqueness and pathwise uniqueness.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

and

Let

\displaystyle u(t,Z_{t})=u(0,Z_{0})+\int^{t}_{0}f(s,Z_{s})ds+\int^{t}_{0}\nabla_{v}u(s,Z_{s})dW_{s}.

Proof.

Firstly observe that (

\displaystyle[\lambda^{-\gamma}\wedge 1]\int_{0}^{1/2}s^{-\alpha}ds+\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-s)^{-\beta}\left([(\lambda(1-s))^{-\gamma}]\wedge 1\right)ds

\displaystyle[\lambda^{-\gamma}\wedge 1]+\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\beta}\left([(\lambda s)^{-\gamma}]\wedge 1\right)ds\lesssim[\lambda^{-\gamma}\wedge 1]+\lambda^{-1+\beta}\lesssim\lambda^{-1+\beta}

and complete the proof.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
f
be a measurable function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, define
f_{n}:=f\ast\varphi_{n}
where
\varphi_{n}(x,v):=n^{4d\vartheta}\varphi(n^{3\vartheta}x,n^{\vartheta}v),

\vartheta\geqslant 0
and
\varphi
is a probability density function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. Then for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\beta\in(0,1]
,
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in[1,\infty)^{2}
,
we have

(A.6)

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}\lesssim n^{-\vartheta\beta}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s+\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

Proof.

By
Definition

2.1
, it reads

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}=\sup_{j}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}.

Notice that, since
\varphi_{n}
is again a probability density function, for any
(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})(x,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi_{n}(y,w)\big({\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x,v)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(x-y,v-w)\big)dydw.

It implies

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{\boldsymbol{p}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|\varphi_{n}(y,w)|(|y|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|w|^{\beta})\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{}_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}}dydw

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}n^{-\vartheta\beta}=\sup_{k}2^{k\beta}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-\vartheta\beta}.

In then end we get

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{s}_{{\boldsymbol{p}};{\boldsymbol{a}}}}=\sup_{j}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(f-f_{n})\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\lesssim\sup_{j}2^{(s+\beta)j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}n^{-\vartheta\beta}.

∎
