4,6-α-Glucanotransferase: An enzymatic biocatalyst for the modification of starch and starch-food systems
Authors: Rana Roshanineshat, Salwa Karboune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increased demand for healthier, low-calorie foods and ingredients with improved physicochemical properties has increased interest in starch modification strategies aimed at enhancing nutritional and techno-functional properties. Among these strategies, the use of a starch-converting enzyme known as 4,6-α-glucanotransferase (4,6-α-GTase) has attracted considerable attention. 4,6-α-GTase, found in various bacterial sources, most significantly in lactic acid bacteria, converts starch into diverse α-glucans depending on the enzyme type, substrate characteristics and reaction conditions. The resulting products include linear α-(1,6)-linked glucose units, such as isomalto-maltopolysaccharides (IMMP) or a reuteran-like polymer with alternating α-(1,4) and α-(1,6) linkages. These structural modifications have been shown to increase the proportion of slowly digestible and resistant starch while also improving physicochemical and functional attributes in food. This review summarizes current knowledge on 4,6-α-GTase, its catalytic properties, and its potential applications in food systems, offering insights to support further research and development of enzyme modified starch ingredients.

Introduction

Carbohydrates are considered the primary source of food and energy for humans. Starch is the second most abundant carbohydrate in the world and is found in granular form in the chloroplasts of green leaves and the amyloplasts of seeds, pulses and tubers (Gourineni et al., 2017; Yashini et al., 2024). Upon consumption, starch digestion initiates in the mouth and continues throughout the intestine, where it is hydrolyzed into maltooligosaccharides through the action of salivary and pancreatic enzymes, specifically α-amylases. Ultimately, brush border enzymes, including α-glucosidases, digest maltooligosaccharides into glucose as the main source of energy for various body functions (Fig. 1) (Gourineni et al., 2017; Kong et al., 2022; C. Starch granules consist of amylose and amylopectin molecules. Amylose has linear α-(1,4) linked glucose units, whereas amylopectin is a branched polysaccharide with α-(1,4) linkages and α-(1,6) branching points every 20–25 glucose residues (Tetlow & Bertoft, 2020; Ye et al., 2021). Together, amylose and amylopectin contribute to the semicrystalline structure of the starch molecules alternating between crystalline and amorphous regions. The crystalline region is the tightly packed section of parallel amylopectins, whereas the amorphous region includes the less orderly amylose chains and amylopectin branches. The crystallinity, shape, size, and morphology of starch granules affect multiple techno-functional properties of the starch molecule, including its gelatinization, pasting profile, retrogradation properties and ultimately its digestibility (Dome et al., 2020; Malki et al., 2023; Miao & BeMiller, 2023). In terms of nutritional properties, starch is divided into rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS). This classification is based on the glucose release rate during its digestion and absorption in the body (Doan et al., 2025). RDS primarily consists of amorphous regions, which are easily digested and absorbed in the duodenum and small intestine. SDS contains inaccessible amorphous regions that undergo slower digestion and absorption throughout the intestine. RS is not easily broken down into glucose and reaches the colon, where it serves as carbon source for beneficial bacteria to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate (Doan et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2023; C. Li et al., 2023). Excessive and prolonged consumption of RDS is one of the factors contributing to various health concerns, such as obesity, type II diabetes, chronic heart diseases and dental cavities. Indeed, the rapid digestion and absorption of RDS leads to a sudden increase to blood glucose, exerting significant pressure on the factors and mechanisms responsible for regulating the glucose balance (Wang et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023). Two-thirds of patients with symptomatic chronic heart conditions have abnormal glucose homeostasis (Garrido-Romero et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2022). One of the most effective approaches for preventing chronic diseases is diet. In accordance, consumers are actively seeking health-conscious diet choices and health-promoting functional foods. This trend has drawn attention to slowly digestible and resistant carbohydrates, as they can be formulated into products with low glycemic index (GI) values and offer prebiotic benefits (Gourineni et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2022).

In terms of their techno-functional properties, some of characteristics of native starches make them undesirable for various food industry applications, such as encapsulants, thickeners, edible coatings and gelling agents. For instance, starches have high retrogradation, low shear, poor water dispersibility, and high viscosity after gelatinization. Therefore, to improve the nutritional and techno-functional properties of starch, attention should be given to the modification of its molecules, with a focus on alterations in the amylose and amylopectin contents, chain length, branching points, and formation of novel glycosidic linkages (X. Li et al., 2023; Miao & BeMiller, 2023; Punia Bangar et al., 2022). Various physical, chemical and enzymatic methods are used for the modification of starch to enhance its physicochemical, techno-functional and nutritional properties (RS and SDS contents) (Ye et al., 2021). Despite diverse applications and benefits of physical and chemical methods, several drawbacks hinder their efficient use in starch modification. Traditional physical methods have long reaction time, and novel physical methods are considered expensive and complex with limited consistency (Han et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022). Additionally, chemical modification techniques are time consuming, negatively perceived by consumers, and can be considered environmentally hazardous (Olawoye et al., 2025). Conversely, high selectivity and specificity of enzymes result in environmentally friendly modification processes that produce fewer unwanted compounds, yielding pure, high-quality, and natural products (S. Park & Kim, 2021; Punia Bangar et al., 2022). Considering the abundance of detailed reviews on physical and chemical methods for the modification of starch as well as many published reviews on various starch altering enzymes, this review focuses on the enzymatic modification of starch using a selected enzyme that has been discovered and explored in recent years, known as 4,6-α-glucanotransferase (4,6-α-GTase). The subsequent sections cover the mechanisms of reaction, catalytic properties, and end product profiles of 4,6-α-GTases, their applications in food systems and techno-functional properties of enzymatically modified starches.

4,6-α-glucanotransferase catalyzed starch modification

4,6-α-glucanotransferases (4,6-α-GTases, EC 2.4.1.-) are starch-active members of glycoside hydrolase family 70 (GH70) and a subfamily of glucansucrases. Glucansucrases use sucrose as substrate and produce a variety of α-glucans including dextran with mainly α-(1,6) linkages, mutan with α-(1,3) linkages, reuteran with α-(1,4) and α-(1,6) linkages and alternan with alternating α-(1,3)/α-(1,6) linkages. In contrast, 4,6-α-GTases act on starch and starch hydrolysates by cleaving α-(1,4) linkages and the transferring of a glucosyl units to acceptor molecules. This transglycosylation reaction introduces new α-(1,6) linkages, either within linear glucan segments or at branch points, and generates structurally diverse starch-derived end products (Fig. 2) (Gangoiti et al., 2018; W. Yang et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2022).Fig. 2Amylose and/or starch catalyzed by the 4,6-α-GTase can undergo hydrolysis, α-(1,4) transglycosylation, and α-(1,6) transglycosylation with the latter one being the main reaction. Few types of this enzyme can also catalyze the α-(1,3) transglycosylation reaction. These reactions lead to diverse end products including linear α-(1,6) linked glucan, known as isomalto-maltopolysaccharides, a reuteran-like glucan with alternating α-(1,6)/α-(1,4) linkages and a glucan with alternating α-(1,3)/α-(1,4) bonds.Fig. 2

The functional diversity of 4,6-α-GTases is mainly governed by differences in active site architecture, loop length, donor and acceptor subsite accessibility, and substrate binding. These structural features determine whether the enzyme favors α-(1,6) linkage formation, α-(1,4) transglycosylation, hydrolysis, processive chain elongation, or transfer of short maltooligosaccharide units. As such, the structure of the enzyme directly affects the linkage pattern, molecule size, branching distribution, and chain length of the modified carbohydrates. These characteristics could later affect functional properties of products such as digestibility, fermentability, retrogradation characteristics, or rheological properties.

The first identified 4,6-α-GTase subfamily, known as GtfB, was identified mainly in lactic acid bacteria (LAB). Thereafter, GtfC and GtfD subfamilies, which are predominantly found in Gram-positive (non-LAB) and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively, were discovered. These subfamilies differ in their structures and amino acid sequences which influence substrate specificity and end product profiles (Pijning & Dijkhuizen, 2024). Crystallographic and phylogenetic studies have revealed that 4,6-α-GTase starch-converting enzymes are the evolutionary intermediate of GH13 starch-acting amylases and GH70 glucansucrases. This is evident from the similarities in the structures and catalytic mechanisms of GH13, GH70 and GH77 which are members of the GH-H enzymatic clan. This evolutionary shift likely initiated with changes in the human diet, particularly when simple sugars such as sucrose started replacing more complex carbohydrates such as starch. Therefore, the evolution of 4,6-α-GTases began in response to the cooking and breakdown of starch in the human diet (Bai et al., 2017). Similar to glucansucrases and GH13 amylases, 4,6-α-GTases contain A/B/C core domains, with domain A having a (β/α)8 barrel-shaped topology. However, unlike GH13 enzymes, glucansucrases and 4,6-α-GTases have an additional domain IV. Glucansucrases and GtfB enzymes also have one extra domain known as V, whose function remains not fully understood (Bai et al., 2017). Furthermore, GtfC and GtfD, similar to GH13 and GH77 enzymes, have a nonpermuted domain organization, whereas GtfB and glucansucrases have undergone a circular permutation of core domains and have the same order of conserved sequence motifs in the form of U-shaped folds ordered as II, III, IV and I. The noncircular permutation of the core domains of GtfC, GtfD and GH13 has resulted in the sequence motif order of I, II, III and IV (Gangoiti, Lamothe, et al., 2017; Meng et al., 2016). This difference is important as it influences the three-dimensional arrangement of catalytic motifs around the active site and contributes to the differences in substrate orientation and product specificity.

One of the key structural determinants responsible for variations in the mechanisms of action and the end products is the presence of loop structures around the active sites. Crystallographic studies on N-terminally truncated Lactobacillus reuteri 121 4,6-α-GTases, known as Lr121 GtfB-ΔN, revealed that two loops, A1 and B, fold over the donor subsites of the binding groove and form a tunnel-like structure. These loops are longer in GtfB than in glucansucrases, whereas the A2 loop is shorter, creating a more accessible binding groove that can accommodate starch-derived substrates such as amylose. These loop structures are particularly important as they can retain the glucan chains near the catalytic site and support the processive elongation and favor the sequential formation of α-(1,6) linkages (Bai et al., 2017; Pijning et al., 2021).

In addition to loop structures, catalytic and substrate-binding residues strongly contribute to the differences in enzymatic specificity. The core catalytic triad residues consist of aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and aspartic acid which are conserved among 4,6-α-GTases and contribute to disproportion reactions of glycosidic linkages cleavage and formation. Additional conserved residues, including arginine, histidine, and aspartic acid, contribute to substrate binding, orientation and stabilization. In motif III, glucansucrases and GH13 enzymes possess a tryptophan residue involved in donor-sugar binding, whereas 4,6-α-GTases have a tyrosine instead at this position. This substitution alters the substrate specificity by reduced hydrogen binding at acceptor subsite and contributes to distinct substrate specificity of 4,6-α-GTases. The ability of 4,6-α-GTases to form α-(1,6) linkages is related to a lysine residue, which replaces the glutamine residue found in glucansucrases at the corresponding position. Additional residues such as leucine and valine in domain B contribute to a wider acceptor subsite +1, which allows the binding of longer or less-branched substrates (Fig. 3) (Bai et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2022). The GtfC subfamily shows differences in product formation properties. The first reported GtfC from Exiguobacterium sibiricum 255–15 (Es255–15 GtfC), a non-LAB bacterial source predominantly catalyzes the formation of isomalto-maltooligosaccharides (IMMOs), rather than high molecular weight IMMPs (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016). Some GtfC-like enzymes feature an additional C-terminus domain with a bacterial Ig2-like fold of unknown function and lack the N-terminus and domain V previously seen in GtfB enzymes (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016). Furthermore, the only available crystal structure of GtfC, from Geobacillus 12AMOR1 (Gb GtfC), revealed the presence of an auxiliary (noncore) domain IV and a tunnel-like structure over subsites −2 and − 3. Although this resembles the tunnel-like region of GtfB enzymes, the amino acid residue composition and subsite conformation differ, which may explain why some GtfC enzymes transfer maltosyl units instead and form shorter liner α-(1,4)/α-(1,6) linkages rather than long IMMPs (Pijning et al., 2022). GtfC enzymes share several conserved active site residues with GtfB enzymes, including glutamine, lysine, and asparagine residues involved in substrate binding and transglycosylation. However, GtfC contains a glutamine residue at a position where GtfB has arginine, which may contribute to differences in product specificity. Similar to GtfB, GtfC also contains tyrosine instead of the tryptophan found in glucansucrases near the acceptor subsites +1 and + 2, further supporting its starch-active specificity (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016; Meng et al., 2016).

GtfD is the most recent addition to the subfamilies of 4,6-α-GTase and was first characterized from non-LAB sources, including Gram-negative bacterium Azotobacter chroococcum NCIMB 8003 (AcNCIMB8003 GtfD) and the Gram-positive bacterium Paenibacillus beijingensis DSM 24997 (PbDSM24997 GtfD) (Gangoiti, Lamothe, et al., 2017; Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, et al., 2016). GtfD enzymes have an additional 50 amino acid residues with unknown function in the N-terminus region but lack the Ig2-like domain found in some GtfC enzymes. The lysine residue in GtfB is replaced by histidine in GtfD, which may contribute to reduced polymer formation. Furthermore, residues asparagine and aspartic acid in GtfD possibly block active subsites beyond −1, thereby limiting the accessibility of longer oligosaccharides as substrates; this restriction results in the transfer of one single glucose unit per reaction cycle, as a result, GtfD enzymes tend to produce more branched products (Gangoiti, Lamothe, et al., 2017; Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, et al., 2016). In general, GtfB enzymes prefer to generate IMMPs with processive α-(1,6) linkage formation while GtfC tends to produce shorter IMMO type of products and GtfD is associated with more branched type of end products.

Reaction mechanism of 4,6-α-glucanotransferase

The reaction mechanism of 4,6-α-GTase activity is not yet fully understood. Bai et al. (2017) proposed the following mechanism for the reaction of Lr121 GtfB-ΔN, involving several key steps. First, the donor substrate binds to the active cleavage site. The donor subsites have greater affinity than acceptor subsites, leading to their occupation. This may be related to the tunnel-like structure formed by A1 and B loops. The substrate is then cleaved through the action of the general acid/base residue, glutamic acid, at the cleavage site between subsites +1 and − 1. Subsequently, the catalytic nucleophile attacks carbon 1 (C1) of the glucose unit at subsite −1, forming a covalent glycosyl-enzyme intermediate. Two possible pathways may then occur: hydrolysis in the presence of a water molecule or transglycosylation in the presence of another suitable sugar molecule (Fig. 5).Fig. 5This schematic figure illustrates the proposed reaction mechanism of the 4,6-α-GTase for the production of IMMPs. After the binding of the substrate to the active subsites, the donor substrate is cleaved, resulting in the formation of covalent intermediate with the amino acid residue. In the next step, at the presence of water, hydrolysis would occur. Alternatively, if a sugar molecule is available, transglycosylation takes place instead and IMMP is formed.Fig. 5

This schematic figure illustrates the proposed reaction mechanism of the 4,6-α-GTase for the production of IMMPs. After the binding of the substrate to the active subsites, the donor substrate is cleaved, resulting in the formation of covalent intermediate with the amino acid residue. In the next step, at the presence of water, hydrolysis would occur. Alternatively, if a sugar molecule is available, transglycosylation takes place instead and IMMP is formed.

During the hydrolysis reaction, a water molecule attacks C1 of the covalent intermediate, leading to glucose release and during transglycosylation, a sugar acceptor attacks the intermediate, resulting in the formation of a new glycosidic linkage. The orientation of acceptor molecule is therefore a key determinant of whether α-(1,4), α-(1,6) or even α-(1,3) linkages are formed. Several residues, including those involved in acceptor positioning and stabilization, contribute in orienting the incoming sugar molecule and the formation of α-(1,6) linkages (Bai et al., 2017). However, the specificity of 4,6-α-GTase should not be interpreted simply as intrinsic preference for α-(1,6) linkage formation. Pijning et al. (2022) suggested that both α-(1,4) and α-(1,6) linkages can occur, however, the α-(1,4)-linked transglycosylation products can participate again as donor substrates because of the reversibility of α-(1,4) donor and acceptor reactions. In contrast, α-(1,6)-linked products cannot be used as donors in the same way. This progressively enriches the reaction products in α-(1,6) linkages (Pijning et al., 2022). This mechanistic understanding of the enzyme can help explain why different substrates and reaction conditions can lead to different α-(1,6) linkage content even when the same enzyme is used.

Upon the formation of α-(1,6) linkages, mixed IMMPs can either diffuse from the active sites or shift along toward the tunnel-like structure. The enzyme may exhibit more efficient transglycosylation after the initial products are formed, likely because multiple subsites are occupied following the shift of the substrate toward the donor subsites −1 to −6. In some cases, the newly formed transglycosylation product is not immediately released but remains bound in the tunnel-like region, allowing another round of transglycosylation without complete dissociation. This phenomenon, where the product of one catalytic reaction remains bound and becomes the substrate for the next reaction event, is referred to as processive catalysis (Bai et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2022). Processivity is functionally important because it supports the formation of longer α-(1,6) rich segments, which may increase resistance to digestive enzymes and alter molecular size distribution.

Substrate structure also strongly influences the reaction outcome. For example, Lr121 GtfB-ΔN shows greater catalytic capacity for α-(1,6) linkage formation when amylose served as the substrate reaching approximately 80% α-(1,6) linkages. In contrast, when amylopectin is used, the capacity to produce α-(1,6) linkages decreases to approximately 14% (Leemhuis et al., 2014; Pijning et al., 2021). This decrease can be explained by the restricted access of branched substrates to the tunnel-like active site. Long A1 and B structures may limit the access of branched α-(1,4) glucans, whereas linear or debranched substrates can more readily occupy the donor subsites and support processive α-(1,6) elongation, explaining the preference for amylose over amylopectin (Bai et al., 2017; W. Yang et al., 2021).

Crystallographic studies of LrNCC2613 GtfB-ΔN revealed that a more open arrangement of the A1 and B loops allows the binding of branched points at subsite +1 without strong steric hindrance (Pijning et al., 2021). This suggests that loop openness may broaden substrate specificity and allow more effective modification of branched substrates such as amylopectin. Klosterman et al. (2021) further showed that GtfB-ΔN cannot use substrates with DP lower than 6 as donor substrates, although these shorter substrates can act as acceptors. In contrast, α-(1,4) linked substrates with DP higher than 6 can serve as donor substrates, indicating that longer oligosaccharides are required to initiate efficient reaction cycles (Klostermann et al., 2021). Therefore, differences reported among studies may reflect not only enzyme specific behavior, but also differences in substrate preparation, debranching, gelatinization state, chain length distribution and reaction time.

GtfC and GtfD 4,6-α-GTases appear to follow different reaction tendencies. Various GtfC and GtfD enzymes do not preferentially transfer single glucosyl units sequentially through α-(1,6) linkages. Instead, they may transfer short maltooligosaccharides units, which results in the production of branched α-(1,6) polymers with alternating α-(1–4)/α-(1,6) linkages (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, et al., 2016). These mechanisms or reaction differences are a key factor in product functionality. Linear α-(1,6)-rich IMMPs may be more resistant to digestion, whereas shorter or branched α-(1,4)/α-(1,6) products may show different fermentation and rheological properties.

Reaction specificity of 4,6-α-glucanotransferases

4,6-α-GTases produce a diverse range of starch-derived carbohydrates, including IMMPs, IMMOs, reuteran-like α-glucan and in rare cases, α-(1,3) linkages in glucans (Fig. 2). This diversity reflects differences in enzyme subfamily, active site structure, substrate type, chain length and reaction conditions. Lr121 GtfB-ΔN uses amylose as a substrate and synthesizes linear IMMPs. Conversely, Es255–15 GtfC E. sibiricum 255–15 synthesizes IMMOs from amylose and is not active toward maltose or maltotriose (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016; Kralj et al., 2011). Gb GtfC shows another distinct reaction profile. When maltoheptaose (DP of 7) was used as the substrate, Gb GtfC produced maltose, whereas Lr121 GtfB-ΔN produced glucose with the same substrate. With amylose, Gb GtfC led to the synthesis of maltose and small linear carbohydrate with an approximate DP of 15, composed of maltose units connected by α-(1,6) linkages (Te Poele et al., 2021). These differences suggest that Gb GtfC transfer and reattach the maltosyl units. As a result, the final product contains alternating α-(1,4)/α-(1,6) structural features rather than long linear α-(1,6)-rich segments.

The AcNCIMB8003 GtfD differs from Lr121 GtfB-ΔN in both substrate use and product structure. Unlike GtfB, AcNCIMB8003 GtfD is not active on isomaltose or isomaltooligosaccharides but shows hydrolysis and transglycosylation activity on maltooligosaccharides with DP of 3–7. It can synthesize carbohydrate polymers from maltooligosaccharides with DP of 4 and higher, while oligosaccharides are also detected when amylose, potato starch and amylopectin are used as substrates. The end product polymer of AcNCIMB8003 GtfD is similar to reuteran from glucansucrases but contains more α-(1,6) branches and linear α-(1,4) linkages (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, et al., 2016). Similarly, PbDSM24997 GtfD is inactive on maltose, and produces high molecular weight (HMW, 27 MDa) and low molecular weight (LMW, 19 MDa) polymers from amylose, mainly containing linear α-(1,4) linkages (Gangoiti, Lamothe, et al., 2017). These findings indicate that GtfD enzymes may be less suited for producing highly linear α-(1,6)-rich IMMPs but may be useful for generating branched α-glucans with distinct molecular weight and rheological properties.

The unique GtfB-like 4,3-α-GTase from L. fermentum NCC 2970 (LfNCC2970 GtfB) exhibits hydrolysis and transglycosylation activity on maltooligosaccharide with DP of 6 and 7. The incubation of LfNCC2970 GtfB with amylose and starch produces oligosaccharides and glucans containing linear and branched α-(1,3) linkages (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, Gerwig, et al., 2017). This enzyme shows that relatively small changes in active site structure can shift linkage specificity from α-(1,6) toward α-(1,3), leading to products with potentially distinct digestibility and functional properties. LfNCC2613 GtfB also acts on maltooligosaccharides; however, unlike Lr121 GtfB, it does not accumulate glucose as the main hydrolysis or transglycosylation product. Instead, it tends to produce maltose, low-molecular-mass oligosaccharides, and reuteran-like polymers with approximately 21% α-(1,6) linkages (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, Meng, et al., 2017).

LrE81 GtfB from L. reuteri E81 is active on maltose and produces oligosaccharides. It is also active on amylose and starch, synthesizing polymers consisting of α-(1,6) and α-(1,4) linkages (İspirli, Yüzer, et al., 2019).

The GtfB-type 4,6-α-GTase from Streptococcus thermophilus (St GtfB) has been used to modify wheat starch. The modified starches showed reductions in amylose content and long amylopectin branch chains by approximately 5.73% and 10.76%, respectively. These changes indicate that the enzyme exhibits significant activity on shorter-branched amylopectin, potentially limiting starch retrogradation by decreasing the availability of long chains capable of reassociation and double-helix formation (X. Li, Fei, et al., 2018). This example provides a direct mechanistic link between reaction specificity and food functionality which will be discussed later in the following sections.

The 4,6-α-GTases from L. aviaries subsp. aviaries DSM 20665 include two types of GtfB-like enzymes, GtfX (LaDSM20665 GtfX) and GtfY (LaDSM 20,665 GtfY), whose conserved motifs differ slightly from those of other GtfB enzymes. For instance, in motif III, tyrosine is replaced by tryptophan. GtfX and GtfY are both inactive on maltose and maltotriose and produce polymers and oligomers from maltooligosaccharides with DP higher than 4. Both enzymes are active on amylose, amylopectin, and starches, although GtfY exhibits lower activity on amylopectin. The polymers produced by GtfY are quite similar to those made from Lr121 GtfB, whereas, GtfX produces highly branched reuteran-like polymers similar those of AcNCIMB 8003 GtfD, which consists of maltooligosaccharides of varying lengths connected at linear or branch points by α-(1,6) linkages (Meng et al., 2018). This comparison shows that conserved motif variation can shift the enzyme from IMMP-like product formation toward reuteran-like branching.

The thermostable GtfC-like 4,6-α-GTase from Bacillus coagulans DSM 1 (BcDSM1 GtfC) is active on maltotetraose and converts debranched starch into α-(1,6)-linked maltooligosaccharides (Xiang et al., 2021). Dong et al. (2024) identified a 4,6-α-GTase from Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis TMW 11304 (FsTMW11304 GtfB), which is believed to have shorter A1 and B loops and therefore a more open binding groove. This enzyme is active on amylose and branched α-(1,4) substrates such as amylopectin, resulting in the production of low- and high- molecular weight IMMPs, depending on the substrate (Dong, Bai, Chen, et al., 2024). Another GtfB-type 4,6-α-GTase from Weissella confusa MBF8–1 (Wc MBF8–1 GtfB) uses amylose to synthesizes low-molecular weight linear and branched α-(1,6) linked products and shows hydrolysis and transglycosylation activity toward maltooligosaccharides with DP of 3–7 (Dong, Bai, Fan, et al., 2024). The structural and reaction specific differences among 4,6-α-GTases described above help explain the variations in the catalytic properties of various types of enzymes.

Catalytic properties of 4,6-α-GTases

The catalytic properties of 4,6-α-GTases vary according to enzyme source, subfamily, structural features and reaction conditions. In general, LAB-derived 4,6-α-GTases demonstrate optimum pH activity under mildly acidic and mesophilic to moderately thermophilic conditions, which is consistent with the physiological environments of their source organisms. Most LAB-derived 4,6-α-GTases exhibit optimum pH values in the range of 4.0–6.0 and optimum temperatures between 30 and 50 °C (Table 2). For example, LrE81 GtfB displays highest activity at pH 4.6 and 37 °C, whereas St GtfB has the highest activity occurring at pH of 6.0 and 40 °C (İspirli, Şimşek, et al., 2019; X. Li, Fei, et al., 2018). However, variations in LAB-derived 4,6-α-GTase are observed, LfNCC2970 GtfB retains more than 80% of its activity over a pH range of 4.0–6.0 and functions at temperature range of 30–60 °C, with maximum activity occurring at 50 °C, however, its structural integrity becomes unstable above 65 °C (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, Gerwig, et al., 2017). Non-LAB-derived 4,6-α-GTases, particularly most of GtfC and GtfD enzymes, showcase broader catalytic properties. As shown in Table 2, Es255–15 GtfC from E. sibiricum, a cold-adapted bacterium retains more than 50% activity in a broad pH range of 4.0–7.5 and temperature range of 35–50 °C, with maximum activity occurring at 45 °C. However, activity decreases sharply above 55 °C (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016). Similarly, AcNCIMB8003 GtfD shows maximum activity at pH 6.5 and 60 °C, whereas PbDSM24997 GtfD exhibits maximum activity at pH 7.0 and 50 °C (Gangoiti, Lamothe, et al., 2017). Sequence comparison further suggests that compared with LAB-derived 4,6-α-GTases, non-LAB derived enzymes contain more charged residues such as lysine and arginine. These examples suggest that non-LAB enzymes may provide broader processing conditions, however, direct comparison remains difficult as studies often use different substrates, assay conditions, enzyme preparations and even units of activity.

The full potential of 4,6-α-GTases can be truly exploited if they could meet the demand of industrial applications such as baking that require elevated temperatures and longer incubation time. In order to enhance the thermostability of 4,6-α-GTases, various strategies have been explored, including screening naturally thermostable enzymes, optimization of reaction conditions, and engineering flexible regions of the protein. For example, Geobacillus sp. 12AMOR1, a thermophile bacteria found in hot springs, and B. coagulans DSM 1, a spore forming bacteria able to survive high heat and acidity, have attracted attention as it is expected that their enzymes retain activity at higher temperatures. As such, Gb GtfC shows high activity around pH 6.0 and maintains activity up to approximately 60 °C, while BcDSM1 GtfC has an activity half-life of approximately 20 min at 59 °C (X. Li et al., 2022; Su & Xu, 2014). However, rapid inactivation at higher temperatures indicates that these enzymes may still require stabilization for more demanding industrial processes.

Reaction conditions can also contribute to improved enzyme thermostability. High substrate concentration may protect enzyme from thermal inactivation by promoting substrate binding and stabilizing the active conformation (Xiang et al., 2021). As such, BcDSM1 GtfC (0.1 g/L) was able to fully convert a relatively high concentration of maltoheptaose as the substrate (115 g/L) at 66 °C (Xiang et al., 2021). Similarly, a study on cyclodextrin transferase showed that increased sago starch concentration as the substrate, to 30 g/L was more effective in maintaining enzyme activity at 70 °C than lower concentrations. This effect was attributed to adsorption of the enzyme onto starch surface which provided protection from denaturation (Muria et al., 2011). In addition, the effect of metal ions on catalytic efficiency of 4,6-α-GTases have been studied. Bai et al. (2015) reported that Ca2+ and Mn2+ slightly enhanced both hydrolytic and transferase activities of Lr121 GtfB-ΔN, whereas Cu2+, Fe2+, and Fe3+ inhibited activity, with no detectable transferase activity observed in the presence of Cu2+. However, the direct effect of metal ions on the thermostability of 4,6-α-GTases is not yet fully investigated (Bai et al., 2015). Nevertheless, studies on related starch-converting enzymes suggest that metal ions such as Ca2+ may improve thermostability by binding to specific metal-binding sites, where they help maintain structural integrity through electrostatic interactions, and stabilizing the folded conformation near the active site (Jaiswal & Jaiswal, 2024). For example, in cyclodextrin glycosyltransferases, Ca2+ increased thermal stability by 5.5-fold at 60 °C. Although, this remains to be experimentally confirmed in 4,6-α-GTases (C. Li et al., 2013).

Protein engineering has also been utilized to enhance the thermostability of 4,6-α-GTases. In accordance, site-directed mutagenesis strategy has been used to enhance the thermal resistance of enzymes by improving structural stability and rigidity in flexible regions of the enzyme. For instance, proline residues were introduced to flexible loop regions of LfNCC3057 GtfB to rigidify the polar residues to reduce loop breathing motions. This resulted in 2.08-fold higher activity of the mutant, 5 °C increase in optimum temperature, 5.76 °C increase in enzyme melting temperature as well as 11.95-fold longer half-life when compared with the wild type (Rao et al., 2023). Similarly, “the proline rule” strategy was also applied in an isoamylase from psychotropic E. sibiricum, which resulted in increased stability of the enzyme, increasing its half-life from 11 min (wild-type) to 129 min at 45 °C (Berlina et al., 2021).

Beyond process condition tolerance and thermostability that determines suitability of 4,6-α-GTases for industrial processing, their potential in starch modification also depends on their substrate affinity and the specific linkages of glycosidic linkages they synthesize.

Functional attributes and applications of 4,6-α-GTases

The nutritional properties of 4,6-α-GTases lie in their ability to modify the structure of starch and starch-derived substrates into low-caloric products with enhanced fermentability and prebiotic potentials. This makes them promising choices for various food industry and nutraceutical applications. These structural changes are mainly due to the introduction of α-(1,6) linkages into starch-derived α-glucans.

Depending on the bacterial enzyme source, substrate type, the enzyme, and reaction conditions, the proportions of α-(1,6) linkages in modified carbohydrates by 4,6-α-GTases vary widely, from as low as 7% to as high as 90% (Leemhuis et al., 2014). Regardless of this variation, nutritional and functional properties of the products are not determined solely by the presence of α-(1,6) linkages, but also by their branching patterns, molecular size, distribution and in general the organization and crystallinity of starch molecules.

The formation of α-(1,6) linkages neighboring the α-(1,4) regions can limit the accessibility of digestive α-amylases to linear starch segments. As a result, most 4,6-α-GTase treated carbohydrates are expected to exhibit lower digestibility (lower RDS, and higher SDS and RS content) (Table 3) (Gu et al., 2018; Leemhuis et al., 2014). For example, Treatment of normal, waxy and high-amylose maize starch with AcNCIMB 8003 GtfD resulted in increases in the α-(1,6) linkage content of 17.86, 11.18, and 20.84%, respectively and increased the RS content. This is likely due to the highly branched structure of the maltooligosaccharide units that limits accessibility and thus hydrolysis by digestive enzymes (Y. Yang et al., 2021). However, this relationship is not always consistent. Treatment of waxy corn starch with Gb GtfC increased the SDS content, however, it decreased the RS content. This change in RS content was attributed to the decrease in the crystalline regions due to enzyme modification (X. Li et al., 2022). Substrate structure also influences the extent of enzymatic modification. As discussed earlier, various 4,6-α-GTases show higher activity toward amylose or debranched starch than when highly branched or granular substrates such as amylopectin are used as substrates. This is partly due to steric hindrance that limits substrate accessibility to the active site which is caused by branches and the granular structure (Dong, Bai, Chen, et al., 2024). Chain length is also another factor influencing the reaction as the short maltooligosaccharides may act as substrates for some 4,6-α-GTases (Gangoiti, van Leeuwen, et al., 2016; İspirli, Yüzer, et al., 2019). Therefore, the digestibility properties may not only be dependent on the enzyme used but on the substrate type as well. For instance, Lf NCC3057 GtfB modified amylose to low molecular weight IMMP containing 72% α-(1,6) linkages that also had 64% of indigestible content (W. Yang et al., 2022). In another example, treatment of debranched waxy potato starch with BcDSM1 GtfC resulted in the end product of IMMPs with 68% α-(1,6) and 32% α-(1,4) linkages which were accompanied by increases in RS and SDS content by 70% and 17% respectively, and reduced RDS contents (Xiang et al., 2021).

The production of SDS and RS can be improved through synergistic multi-enzyme mixture strategies. For example, 4,6-α-GTases first generate the α-(1,6) rich acceptor structures which are then modified by GH70 branching sucrases that introduce α-(1,2) or α-(1,3) glucosyl linkages. Branching sucrases can catalyze the formation of α-glucans with a highly branched comb-like structures. Dextran is a good acceptor for these enzymes when sucrose is used as a donor. Given the similarity between dextran and IMMPs, IMMPs can be used as acceptors for branching enzymes (Te Poele et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the nutritional properties of these end products still depend on the final structure, including linkage type, linkage density, molecular size, and branching patterns.

The prebiotic potential of 4,6-α-GTase treated carbohydrates such as IMMPs is due to their increased resistance to digestibility in intestine and their subsequent availability as carbon sources to increase the growth of gut flora, such as Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Bifidobacterium species and improving their diversity (Kim et al., 2022). These carbohydrates can improve metabolic activity and contribute to the production of SCFAs which are strongly associated with beneficial health effects. However, the fermentation behavior of the synthesized carbohydrates is highly dependent on their α-(1,6) linkage content, as those with lower α-(1,6) contents and smaller units are more readily available for the gut flora and may be fermented more rapidly. For example, IMMP-27 (27% of α-(1,6) linkages) initiated fermentation immediately upon inoculation, whereas highly α-(1,6)-linked IMMPs (IMMP 94, 96) required a longer period of 12 h before fermentation began (Gu et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2022; Lammerts van Bueren et al., 2015; Leemhuis et al., 2014). This suggests that increased α-(1,6) linkage content may improve the resistance toward digestibility, but it also delays gut microbial fermentation.

In vivo studies have demonstrated the dietary fiber potential of 4,6-α-GTase treated carbohydrates. IMMPs have been associated with changes in cecum and colonial microbiota, whereas limited changes were observed in ileum. This suggests that IMMPs are mainly fermented in the lower gut. Increased fecal bulk has also been reported in mice diets containing high α-(1,6) linkages which further support their potential as dietary fiber ingredients (Mistry et al., 2020). In addition to their probiotic effects, IMMPs have been investigated in various studies with respect to their immunomodulatory effects. They can decrease the need for antibiotics and enhance immunostimulation through the activation of the TLR2, TLR4, and NF-κB signaling pathways and the stimulation of cytokine production (Logtenberg et al., 2021; Silva-Lagos et al., 2025). These findings indicate that 4,6-α-GTase modified carbohydrates have promise as functional food products with reduced digestibility, prebiotic properties, dietary fiber potential with immunomodulatory effects. However, these claims need further validation especially through in vivo studies.

Techno-functional properties of 4,6-α-GTase end products

Besides nutritional properties, 4,6-α-GTase modified starches are also of huge interest for food system applications as the enzymatic modification can affect starch retrogradation, rheology, texture, and storage stability. Available studies have mainly focused on wheat, potato, corn and waxy starches as well as their application in bread and gluten-free formulations (Table 3). In general, it has been shown that 4,6-α-GTases are able to affect the starchy food properties by reducing retrogradation, modifying dough or paste rheology, and storage stability. In this context, starch retrogradation is one of the main causes of quality deterioration in starch-based foods, particularly bread staling. This process involves the reassociation and recrystallization of amylose and amylopectin chains into ordered structures (X. Li, Fei, et al., 2018; Ning et al., 2025). Amylose generally retrogrades rapidly, whereas amylopectin recrystallizes more slowly during storage. By reducing amylose content, shortening amylopectin branches, and introducing α-(1,6) linkages that disturb linear chain alignment, 4,6-α-GTases can reduce the ability of starch chains to form stable double helices (X. Li, Fei, et al., 2018). St GtfB-treated wheat starch significantly differed between the control and treated samples, as the starch treated with higher amounts of the St GtfB enzyme (4 U/g) had lower retrogradation in the first and the second weeks than those treated with lower amounts of the enzyme (1 U/g) and the untreated control starch sample. Moreover, compared with the control, the modified wheat starch resulted in lower retrogradation, which could have been due to the reduced amylose content. (X. Li, Fei, et al., 2018). Similar behavior was observed in waxy corn starch treated by Gb GtfC and potato starch treated by St GtfB, which is due to the increased proportion of the shorter branch length of the amylopectin of treated starches that cannot contribute as efficiently to the formation of double helices and thus reduced retrogradation (D. Li et al., 2021).

The structural changes by the enzyme can also affect dough and bread properties such as rheology and volume. In wheat dough treated with St GtfB, a reduced amylose content, amylopectin branch lengths, and molecular weight were observed which were linked to a lower storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G"). This indicates that enzymatic treatment weakened the biomacromolecule network, resulting in improved dough behavior and higher specific volume. In the final bread sample, in comparison to control, a reduced hardness, gumminess, and chewiness were observed and the increase in hardness during storage was slower which indicates the reduced staling (D. Li et al., 2019).

The anti-staling effect has been reported in wheat bread and gluten-free bread systems with potato starch treated with 4,6-α-GTase. The treatment resulted in a better visual appearance, a finer structure and softer texture over time for both bread types. Furthermore, in regular bread, enzymatic treatment did not negatively affect crumb appearance or specific volume, however, texture was improved during storage time (Te Poele et al., 2021). A recent study compared effects of 4,6-α-GTase and 4,3-α-GTase on the retrogradation and staling of bread and buns which revealed that the enzyme-treated breads had lower enthalpies, indicating reduced staling, with 4,6-α-GTase being more effective, whereas for the buns, 4,3-α-GTase was more effective. The enzymes reduced hardness, increased stickiness in the bread, and improved chewability and elasticity. These attributes were due to changes in the amylose content, which in turn influenced the texture, with a slow increase in hardness over time and improved swallowability. Furthermore, no adverse effects on the appearance of the crumbs or their specific volumes were reported (Niçin et al., 2024).

In addition to improved anti-staling and texture properties, improved storage stability is another important determining factor in food industry applications. Isomaltodextrins produced through combined 4,6-α-GTase and branching sucrase treatment revealed improved freeze-thaw stability compared with maltodextrin as their turbidity remained relatively constant after repeated freeze-thaw cycles. These products also showed resistance to Maillard reaction conditions, acid heating treatments and digestion (Wei et al., 2023). This suggests that α-(1,6)-rich products may be suitable for applications requiring physical and chemical stability during processes. This idea is further supported by the study of Hassanein et al. (2023) where treatment of potato starch with LrE81 GtfB resulted in no significant difference in the thermal stability, crystallographic patterns or granular morphology of the treated and native starch samples even though α-(1,6) linkages increased from 2.9% to 36.8%. This makes the enzyme-treated starch with enhanced α-(1,6) linkages suitable for processing at high temperatures (Hassanein et al., 2023).

Physical state of starch is another important factor determining the effect of enzyme on its techno-functional properties. In general, compared with granular starches, gelatinized starches were more susceptible to 4,6-α-GTases. This is due to the limited access of the enzyme to the molecular network of the granular starches; however, gelatinization increased the enzyme accessibility to glucan chains (Dong, Bai, Chen, et al., 2024).

In general, it is revealed that 4,6-α-GTases are able to modify starch functionality by reducing retrogradation, changing rheological properties and improving textural characteristics. In some cases, the enzyme was also able to improve starchy product stability throughout storage time and conditions. However, these effects vary depending on the 4,6-α-GTase type, starch origin and process conditions. More research is needed to establish a definite trend in α-(1,6) linkage formation, reduced amylose content and altered techno-functional properties of starch.

Comparative functionality analysis of 4,6-α-GTase with other starch-active enzymes

A wide range of starch-active enzymes, particularly from GH13, GH77, and GH57 have been investigated and used in food industry for various applications such as baking, brewing, starch liquefaction, maltodextrin and glucose syrup production. These enzymes modify starch through reactions such as hydrolysis, transglycosylation or cyclization which in turn alters molecular weight, size and chain length distribution, branching density, crystallinity and etc. Hydrolytic starch-acting enzymes are the most commonly used ones in food industry. α-amylases (EC 3.2.1.1) are the most established and utilized starch-acting enzymes in food industry applications. They randomly hydrolyze internal α-(1,4) linkages in amylose and amylopectin which produces dextrins and maltooligosaccharides and therefore reduces viscosity. In bread baking industry, they are mostly used to enhance fermentable sugar availability, crumb softness, color, and to improve shelf-life stability (Raveendran et al., 2018; van der Maarel et al., 2002). Pullulanases (EC 3.2.1.41) and isoamylases (EC 3.2.1.68) act differently by hydrolyzing α-(1,6) branch points in amylopectin and related α-glucans. In syrup production, these debranching enzymes improve saccharification efficiency, whereas in resistant starch production, it can generate linear chains that are reassociated during cooling to form retrograded resistant starch (Xia et al., 2021). As such, pullulanases have been extensively studied as an approach to improve RS content in potato, oat, corn, chickpea and rice starches (Demirkesen-Bicak et al., 2018; X. Li, Wang, et al., 2018). For example, pullulanse treated potato starch showed an increase in RS content from 3.62% to 29.35% (Reddy et al., 2015).

In contrast, transglycosylation based enzymes rearrange or alter the structure of starch molecules. Branching enzymes (EC 2.4.1.18) cleave α-(1,4) linkages and transfer glucan segments to form new α-(1,6) branching points which produces highly branched amylopectin or clustered maltodextrins with reduced digestibility (Ban et al., 2020). Studies have shown that branching starches have been used to decrease RDS and increase SDS and RS contents of various starches such as corn, potato and tapioca (Ren et al., 2018; T. Yang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2021). For instance, branching enzyme treatment of corn starch increased α-(1,6) branching points and reduced RDS, while two-step branching enzyme treatment reduced RDS by 26.58% and increased SDS and RS by 14.79 and 11.79% respectively (Ren et al., 2020). Other transglycosylation starch-acting enzymes include cyclodextrin glycosyltransferases (EC 2.4.1.19) which convert starch chains to cyclic oligo-polysaccharides which are normally used for biocoating to enhance shelf-life stability of food products and encapsulation of flavors, aromas, bioactive compounds in food and pharmaceutical industries (Fan et al., 2025; Z. Li et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2022). 4-α-glucanotransferases (EC 2.4.1.25) are another type of enzymes with transferase activity that rearrange α-(1,4) linkages of glucan chains and have been studied in starch gels, texture improvements (Jeong et al., 2020).

The distinction between starch-active enzymes becomes visible when they are compared based on their typical processing outcomes. For instance, α-amylases and maltogenic α-amylases are established enzymes that are mostly used for starch liquefaction as they function through hydrolysis and depolymerization. However, their hydrolytic activity must be carefully monitored as excessive starch breakdown especially by bacterial thermostable α-amylases can negatively affect texture especially in bread baking industry through sticky crumb or gumminess (S. H. Park et al., 2017). In contrast, 4,6-α-GTases are of potential application when structural rearrangements are required. In food systems where excessive hydrolysis is undesirable, such as bread or starch gels, 4,6-α-GTases may offer an advantage as it modifies the starch structure without excessive chain breakdown. A similar contrast can be made with pullulanases as the enzyme is highly useful for the production of RS through debranching and enhanced retrogradation. However, this alteration may contribute to textural hardening in some starch-based food systems. As was the case in potato starch noodles. Comparatively, 4,6-α-GTases can introduce α-(1,6) linkages that disturb the alignment of α-(1,4)-linked glucans, potentially reducing retrogradation and lowering digestibility without significant negative effects on textural properties. Branching enzymes are perhaps the closest functional comparison as they also increase α-(1,6) linkages in starch structure. However, they mostly produce amylopectin-like branch points by transferring the glucan chains, whereas 4,6-α-GTases are able to produce linear α-(1,6) rich glucans such as IMMPs, or even branched α-glucans such as reuteran like carbohydrates, which improves their versatility in various applications such as dietary fibers, prebiotic supplements or in baking industry. The enhanced nutritional property is present when comparing 4,6-α-GTases with 4-α-GTases, both enzyme groups are glucanotransferases that can transfer glucan segments without excessive hydrolysis. However, 4-α-GTases mainly rearrange α-(1,4) linkages, whereas 4,6-α-GTases introduce new α-(1,6)-linked chains. This makes 4-α-GTases more useful for chain-length distribution, gel formation and texture modification, while 4,6-α-GTases provide additional nutritional advantages (Chen et al., 2023).

Future perspectives

The majority of currently characterized 4,6-α-GTases originate from a limited number of microbial sources, primarily by the Lactobacillus genus, making it important to discover new potential bacterial species and strains to meet future applications. This is particularly important because the starch-modifying enzymes in industry such as α-amylases are derived from plant origins which may limit their use as they are mostly extracted from human staple, cereal grains (S. H. Park et al., 2017). In contrast, 4,6-α-GTases are produced through microbial fermentation, which offers a more flexible route for enzyme production. However, a broader screening of food grade, thermotolerant, acid-tolerant and high yielding 4,6-α-GTase bacterial sources is still needed to identify enzymes that are suitable for food industry.

Additionally, the multidomain structure and high molecular weight of 4,6-α-GTases restrict their effective heterologous expression and soluble protein recovery, which limits their application in industry and biotechnology (Rao et al., 2022). Truncation of the N/C-terminus regions of the enzyme has been used to increase the soluble expression of 4,6-α-GTase in E. coli; however, this technique may not always be feasible if it affects substrate binding, stability or product specificity (Rao et al., 2022). Gangoiti et al. (2016), revealed that even when the C-terminus was truncated, the enzyme still accumulated in inclusion bodies (Gangoiti, Pijning, et al., 2016). However, in many other studies, truncation of the N-terminus resulted in better expression of the enzyme in E. coli and its reduced accumulation in inclusion bodies (Bai et al., 2015) . Future studies are therefore needed to improve expression strategies such as codon optimization, chaperone co-expression, food-grade microbial hosts and fermentation optimization.

Thermostability of the enzyme is another factor that determines the practical use of the 4,6-α-GTases in food industry. So far, mining of naturally thermostable enzymes and limited protein engineering including introduction of proline residues have been studied; however, many other thermostabilizing methods such as introduction of disulfide bridges, computational guided mutagenesis, direct evolution and etc. remain underutilized that have been successfully employed for other enzyme groups (Ahmad et al., 2020). In addition to protein engineering, immobilization is an underexplored strategy that could improve the industrial feasibility of 4,6-α-GTases. Enzyme immobilization through adsorption, covalent binding, entrapment, encapsulation or cross-linking can improve enzyme recovery and stability (Hill et al., 2017).

Although, current studies have indicated the potential of 4,6-α-GTases in improving the nutritional and techno-functional properties of starch-based food systems through enhancing slowly digestible and resistant carbohydrates, their application has been examined in a relatively narrow range of food matrices. The effects of 4,6-α-GTase on complex food matrices are still poorly understood and further investigations are needed. Therefore, future studies targeting the effect of 4,6-α-GTases on food matrices such as noodles, pasta, bakery products, beverages, soups, sauces, puddings and dairy-based systems are required.

Another underdeveloped direction is the combination of 4,6-α-GTases with other starch-acting enzymes. Enzyme cocktails or sequential enzyme treatments involving branching sucrases, cyclodextrin glycosyltransferases and 4-α-GTases, pullulanases, or other GH70 enzymes could be studied. For instance, debranching enzymes could increase the availability of α-(1,4) linkages before the addition of 4,6-α-GTases or branching sucrases from GH70 could further diversify the α-glucan. Such strategies may offer new variations of functional α-glucans that have not yet been explored. Moreover, the safety and regulatory status of 4,6-α-GTase is another direction that remains unexplored. For food industry applications, future studies could focus on safety of the enzyme, and allergenicity, especially if the enzyme is to be added directly to food systems.

Many studies have already focused on the mechanisms of action of 4,6-α-GTases, yet some stages of the reaction are still not very well understood, and more research is needed to gain a better insight in enzyme mechanisms with relations to catalytic behavior and product specificity. Although several crystal structures have provided valuable information, this number remains limited, thus additional three-dimensional crystallographic studies are also required for broadening our understandings of the enzyme.

In general, enhancing the applications of 4,6-α-GTases in industry would require further research regarding enzyme discovery, structural characterizations and large-scale productions. Addressing these topics will provide the necessary information for incorporating enzymes into food processing strategies to improve nutritional and techno-functional properties of products.

Conclusion

In conclusion, higher contents of SDS and RS contribute to making starch healthier by facilitating its passage to the colon and enhancing the formation of SCFAs, which are associated with preventing various diseases. The carbohydrate end products of the 4,6-α-glucanotransferase reaction mixtures are indicated to be rather indigestible by digestive enzymes, reaching the colon, as evident by various in vitro and in vivo studies. Furthermore, as observed in numerous studies, the enzyme effectively reduced the retrogradation of various starch types and starchy foods such as breads and buns due to the reduced amylose content. Although this enzyme has attracted much attention and has been extensively studied, more research is still needed to fully understand its function and for its efficient application in the food industry, including the production of prebiotic supplements and low-calorie foods.
