Quantitative transcriptome and proteome maps of classical nonclinical species
Authors: Elias Oziolor, Liang Xue, Dennis Pelletier, Darien Capunitan, Emi Kimoto, Joel D. Federspiel, Mark Sheehan, Seda Arat, Leah Newman, Gahyun Lee, Jessie Qian, Steven Kumpf, Thomas A. Lanz, Matthew Martin, Nagappan Mathialagan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The distribution of expression of target genes can provide insights that advance understanding of target availability to aid therapeutic development purposes. The increase of use of biologics and novel modalities, combined with the opportunities of alternative to in vivo experimental methods in safety evaluation of new therapeutics. A detailed understanding of expression levels in target tissues is essential to selecting nonclinical species with the highest translational potential. We quantified the relative RNA and protein levels across more than 40 normal tissues and 5 nonclinical species several tissues in human. We identified patterns of cross-species and methodology relationships that can aid in the broader understanding of nonclinical experimental design. We observed expression profile similarity across tissues that favors species similarity, except for select nervous system and immune tissues. In exploring tissue specificity, we identified ubiquitous transcripts that encode tissue-specific proteins. We also assessed discordance between RNA and protein enrichment and revealed potential sites of synthesis and action of secreted proteins. These data and their relationships are foundational assets in toxicology study design and provide insights into biological regulation in drug discovery.

Background & Summary

Gleaning the potential down-stream effects of modulating the expression of any gene is largely dependent on understanding both the distributed expression of that gene and the molecular context in which it operates across different tissues. While the passing of Modernization Act 2.0 allows for the opportunity to imagine a development process that includes fewer or no animals, the reality of drug development is that nonclinical species are still required by regulators to accurately assess potential toxicities1. The responsibility is placed on the pharmaceutical industry to develop and defend ways of reducing or refining animal use in the coming years. To assist with these efforts, pharmaceutical discovery and development can use molecular profiles like RNA and protein expression for each tissue among different species to understand fundamental biology, disease, and their corresponding therapy.

Large scale omics experiments are expensive and require specialized analysis skillsets. Thus, most efforts to develop molecular tools have focused on single modality measurements, and on single species. The scale, systematic data generation and flexibility of transcriptomics are often reasons why it is a widely chosen first option for biological interrogation and hypothesis generation2. However, there is repeated evidence that suggests that transcriptional profiles may correlate poorly with protein levels3,4. Thus, some rely on global proteomics interrogations as a more reliable resource to understand molecular mechanisms5. While these patterns are well studied in a few species, our understanding of the transcriptomic and proteomic profiles on a tissue level across all nonclinical species used in drug development remains limited. Such studies could complement transcriptomic studies and provide insights into a wide spectrum of applications like post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, tissue selection for protein targets, conservation of expression levels that enable understanding of biological function, as well as enhancing translational understanding of target biology.

Public omic datasets often focus on human6–8 and mouse9 profiles, while a few have generated cross-species transcriptional resources as well10,11. The lack of datasets or low resolution for existing data in common nonclinical species used for drug development can be a challenge for assessing translational value of early findings. There is a limited availability of transcriptomic data that covers the breadth of tissues available in human reference resources with a significant number of individuals. This gap is even broader for protein expression data. A comparable transcriptome and proteome map for nonclinical species can enable evaluation of relative levels of target expression across species and provide a valuable resource in understanding the pharmacology in nonclinical studies. To address this gap in available data and insight we aimed to profile transcript and protein expression in a wide range of tissues across five of the most common nonclinical species - cynomolgus macaque, dog (beagle), rat (Wistar Han), mouse (CD1 and C57/B6), and minipig (Gottingen). We present these data in context of the existing public resources for these nonclinical species, as well as those present for human. Our data expands the public capability to query relative transcript and protein abundance levels across human and nonclinical species, expanding the resources for target based scientific exploration and drug development.

Methods

Animal handling and sample collection

Tissue samples from pre-clinical animals (minipigs, cynomolgus monkeys, beagle dogs, Wistar Han rats, CD1 and C57BL6 mouse) were collected at Charles River Labs (CRL). All tissue collection protocols were approved by IACUC. Standard animal tissue necropsy and sample collection protocols were used by the service provider (GTN201100411 (cynomolgus monkeys), CRL20235724 (dogs, rats and mice) and CRL20351447 (minipigs). Rats and mice were euthanized by using isoflurane inhalation followed by exsanguination. Cynomolgus monkeys, minipigs and dogs were sedated with Acepromazine (1 mg/kg) and euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), IV injection followed by exsanguination. Snap frozen tissue samples were collected from 3 male and 3 female animals (minipig, cynomolgus monkeys and dogs) and for rodents, 5 male and 5 female animals were used for tissue collection. Frozen human tissue samples (3 males and 3 females) were obtained from Pfizer Tissue Bank. All tissue samples (~5 mg to 20 mg) were collected, snap frozen and stored at −80 °C until shipment to service provider (IQ proteomics, Cambridge, MA). Transcriptomics

RNA extraction and library generation

RNA was isolated from tissues using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen #74534) according to manufacturer’s protocol. RNAseq cDNA libraries were prepared from 1 μg total RNA using the Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA kit (Cat. No. RS-122-2101) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After elution, libraries were quantified using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, #Q33238) using the Qubit 1X dsDNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, #Q32853).

Bulk library sequencing

Libraries were sequenced either on an Illumina Nextseq. 500 or Nextseq. 2 K (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using a 150 cycle or 200 cycle single end flow cell, respectively to achieve a minimum read depth of 20 million paired-end reads per sample.

RNAseq quantification

The BCL files were demultiplexed using BaseSpace software with bcl2fastq function. Reads were processed to count values through a modified nf-core RNAseq pipeline (v3.14.0) executed with Nextflow (v23.10.0). The selected workflow included pseudo-alignment to respective ensembl transcriptome (v110) and quantitation with salmon (v1.10.1). Resulting counts were stored in MySQL database and linked to human orthologs through ensembl annotation and sequence similarity.

Proteomics

Sample preparation

Approximately 10 – 25 mg of sample/tube was collected for the proteome map. Tissues across all species and genders were individually lysed in 8 M urea + 1% SDS + 100 mM EPPS pH 8.0 + 1X HALT protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat. No. 78439, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Lysates were bead beat at 4 °C using a Precellys Evolution homogenizer for a total of 3 cycles: 10 second intervals at 7200 rpm with 60 second pauses.

Protein precipitation and digestion

Protein concentration in the lysates was quantified using the Pierce micro-BCA assay (Cat. No. 23235, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Proteins were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and quenched with 10 mM DTT prior to precipitation. Within each species (human, cyno, dog, or pig), all male and female tissues were equally mixed to create single 500 ug representative tissue stocks (ie., heart, bladder, and colon). The exception was mouse and rat, where female and male tissues were not mixed, but separated by sex. Total protein (~200 ug) was extracted by methanol-chloroform precipitation and digested with lysC endopeptidase (Wako Chemicals, USA) at a 1:25, enzyme/protein w/w ratio, in 100 mM EPPS pH 8.1 at room-temperature overnight. Following digestion with LysC, trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added to all digests at a 1:25, enzyme/protein w/w ratio, and incubated at 37 °C for 8 hours. The percentage of missed cleavages was monitored using several aliquots from all representative tissue types, desalted and analyzed by mass spectrometry. If the digestion metrics were met (fewer than 10% of all spectral counts corresponding to peptides with internal Lysine or Arginine), peptides were labeled with TMT. If digestion metrics failed in any tissue type, trypsin digestion was repeated for all tissues within a species.

Tandem mass tag (TMT) experimental design

In this study, we used TMT11plex which can label up to 11 samples in one experiment. We randomized tissue samples so that each TMT11plex consists of an assortment of tissues. To facilitate cross-tissue comparison and reduce the technical variation among mass-spectrometry runs, a bridge channel containing pooled reference samples created from all tissues within a species was added into each TMT11plex experiment. As a result, equal amounts of tissue samples and the bridging reference sample were multiplexed into one TMT11plex run. TMT131C was used for the same reference sample in each run. For each species, we designed four batches of TMT11plex runs (Supplemental Table S2), while some batches contain less than 10 samples, due to fewer samples needed than 10 TMT channels. In total, from 32 TMT batches, we acquired data from 277 samples. For each TMT batch, 24 fractions were created to increase proteomic profiling depth, as well as quantification accuracy. In total, there were [32*24] raw files generated from MS runs. Detailed information for each run, sample composition, and their corresponding TMT tags can be found in Table S2 sheet A.

TMT labelling of peptides

The concentration of tryptic peptides was quantified using the Pierce Fluorometric peptide assay (Cat No. 23290, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) prior to creating a single shared species-wide bridge. The species-wide shared bridge was created by removing ~5-10% of all tissues’ peptides into a separate single mix that was TMT labeled with 131C and shared across all 4 TMT plexes (per species). The remaining 90-95% of peptides were labeled by adding TMT10-plex reagents (40 ug/uL in 100% anhydrous acetonitrile; Cat. No. A34808, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) at a ratio of 4:1 (TMT:Protein, w/w), and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. A small portion (1%) of each sample and the shared bridge (131 C) were mixed together, desalted via StageTips packed with Empore C18 material12 (3 M, Maplewood, MN), vacuum dried, and analyzed via LC-MS to assess labeling efficiency and the protein loading ratio. If the proportion of unlabeled peptides exceeded 2% of total peptide identifications, labeling was repeated with a similar concentration of TMT. All labeling reactions were quenched with hydroxylamine (0.5% v/v final, Cat. No. 90115, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 15 minutes and acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to 1% (v/v) final. Samples were mixed according to total summed TMT signal observed in the labeling efficiency check such that all tissues and the bridge have equal loading. The final mixed samples were desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction (C18 Sep-pak, 50 mg cartridges; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA), and dried via vacuum centrifugation.

Peptide fractionation for total protein analysis

The dried peptides were resuspended in 0.1% TFA. Approximately 100 ug of peptide mix was subjected to orthogonal basic-pH reverse phase fractionation on a 3 × 150 mm column packed with 1.9 µm Poroshell C18 material (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), utilizing a 45 min linear gradient from 10% buffer A (5% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbornate, pH 8) to 45% buffer B (90% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8) at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min. Ninety six fractions were collected and consolidated into 24 fractions and vacuum dried. The fractions were resuspended in 0.2% formic acid, desalted using a 96-well Sep-pak tC18 plate (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA) and vacuum dried. Peptides were reconstituted in 5% formic acid/5% acetonitrile for LC-MS3 analysis.

Mass spectrometry data acquisition

All mass spectra were acquired on an Orbitrap Fusion Eclipse coupled to an EASY nanoLC-1200 (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) liquid chromatography system. Approximately 2 µg of peptides were loaded on a 75 µm capillary column packed in-house with Sepax GP-C18 resin (1.8 µm, 150 Å, Sepax) to a final length of 35 cm.

Peptides for total protein analysis were separated using a 180 minute linear gradient from 8% to 40% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data dependent mode. The scan sequence began with FTMS1 spectra: resolution = 120,000; mass range of 350- 1400 m/z; max injection time (MIT) of 50 ms; automatic gain control (AGC) target of 5e5; with dynamic exclusion for 45 seconds using a + /-10 ppm window. The 10 most intense precursor ions were selected for ion trap MS2 analysis via collisional-induced dissociation in the ion trap (normalized collision energy (NCE) = 35; MIT = 35 ms; isolation window of 0.7 Da; AGC target of 1e4). A real-time search (RTS)13 approach was utilized during data acquisition to only trigger quantitative spectra on high-confidence peptide identifications. Online spectral identification was accomplished via a custom software client that monitors spectral acquisition though a vendor supplied instrument application programming interface and assigns peptide sequences through a probabilistic model, in real-time (Orbitrap Lumos)14. The RTS client utilized a peptide database composed of in-silico predicated human, cyno, dog, mouse, rat, or pig tryptic peptides. Upon a successful database match, 10 MS2 ions were isolated via synchronous precursor selection for MS3 scans which were acquired in the Orbitrap (NCE = 55; resolution = 50,000; MIT = 110 ms; AGC target = 1.5e5; isolation windows at 1.2 Da for +2 m/z, 1.0 Da for +3 m/z or 0.8 Da for +4 to +s6 m/z.

Mass spectrometry data processing

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using the Proteome Discoverer software Version 2.5 with the SEQUEST search engine. The MS/MS spectra were searched against the corresponding UniProt sequence database (Table S2) without spliced isoforms. A 10 ppm MS1 error tolerance was used. Trypsin was set as the enzyme, allowing for 2 missed cleavages. TMT tags on lysine residues and peptide N-termini (229.162932 Da) and carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues (57.02146 Da) were set as static modifications, while oxidation of methionine residues (+15.99492 Da) and acetylation of peptide N-termini (42.011 Da) were set as variable modifications. Percolator was used as the false discovery rate calculator, and all peptides were filtered at the “Strict” Target FDR level of 0.01. Protein assembly was guided by principles of parsimony to produce the smallest set of proteins necessary to account for all observed peptides. Quantitation was performed in Proteome Discoverer with a reporter ion integration tolerance of 20 ppm for the most confident centroid within the “Reporter Ions Quantifier” node and the MS order was set to MS3. Reporter ion values were corrected for isotopic impurities using the manufacturer provided factors. Reporter abundances were based on signal intensities. Peptides were quantified by summing reporter ion intensity across all matching peptide-spectral matches, and then used to calculate protein abundasnce in each sample.

Proteomics data normalization

Reporter ion intensities per feature reported in PD’s PSMs.txt file were submitted to the MSstatsTMT workflow. When a peptide and charge combination is measured multiple times in a sample, only the maximum intensity is kept. Peptide intensities were summarized to protein intensities using Tukey’s median polish algorithm15. Protein normalization using bridge channel was applied afterwards.

Quantification for proteins

To get precursor ion quantification for each peptide feature, raw mass spectrometry data were separately processed using the MaxQuant16 software Version 1.6.7.0 with the Andromeda search engine integrated into MaxQuant environment. All the search criteria including reference database, peptide modification, FDR setting etc. were identical to the PD identification search. For protein summarization, unique peptide intensities were considered. The intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) algorithm was turned on to get absolute quantification per TMT labeled ion cluster. To maintain the comparability of a specific protein across multiple TMT batches, the iBAQ value was then proportionally allocated based on relative abundance of TMT channels for each TMT batch. As a result, weighted iBAQ were obtained for all tissue samples per species. All the downstream analysis using protein abundance was based on these protein summaries.

To quantify and explore these trends further, we calculated the pairwise Euclidian distance across all samples (Fig. S7). When clustered, these distances indeed support closer relationships within Nervous tissues, secondary to which appear to be species similarities (Fig. S7). When plotting Euclidian distances away from a single tissue in the nervous system, dog amygdala as example, we do observe lower distance to other Nervous system tissues, while other tissues appear to taper in their similarity (Fig. 2B). An expected trend emerges, where species specific similarity overwhelms system similarity in transcriptional landscape (Fig. 2B). In fact, when we test this relationship, for most tissues, the most similar tissues appear to be others within species within system, followed by within species outside system, between species within system and then finally between species outside system (Fig. 2C). However, this relationship appears to be discordant for several examples of Nervous and Immune tissues, where the dissimilarity in tissues within species between systems is higher than the one between species within system (Fig. 2D). This relationship is consistent across all species observed (Fig. S7).

Protein profiling across tissues

Proteomics data was also generated on a smaller set (n = 277) of samples. To increase the proteome coverage, each TMT 11plex sample was extensively fractionated. To facilitate cross-tissue comparison and to reduce the influence of technical variation between mass spectrometry runs, we randomized the tissue samples such that each TMT 10plex consists of an assortment of tissues and a reference sample. The overall proteomic experimental workflow was illustrated in Figure S8A.

In human, the number of proteins quantified and abundance distribution across TMT channels in each plex are comparable. (Fig. S8B,C) On average more than 8300 proteins could be quantified for each one out of four TMT batches, while 6,347 out of total 10,352 proteins were present in all 37 tissue types (Fig. S8D). These results well aligned with previous report where 6,357 out of total 12,627 human proteins could be quantified ubiquitously across tissue types8.

Across species, the number of proteins quantified in each tissue were listed in Table S3. It appears that the identification and quantification have systematic differences among species (Fig. S8E). For example, one species could have consistent higher identification than the other, which we hypothesized it is likely due to technical artifacts such as sample preparation, number of entries in the reference proteome, etc.

Distribution of RNA expression in log2TPM for both the identified and unidentified proteins were shown in Fig. S8F, matching with previous reports that the proteomic detection sensitivity is biased to higher expressed transcripts30.

Clustering of the protein levels (Z-score transformed) revealed that samples clustered by tissue types and species (Fig. S9A). Some variations between tissue types such as liver exceed that between individual species, while some other variation among species exceeds tissue types. Samples from physiologically related tissues, such as liver, cerebellum and skeletal muscles, clustered together, matching published results31 (Fig. S9A). Clustering using untransformed abundance were shown in Fig. S9A and sex ignored clustering was shown in Fig. S9B.

Identification of tissue-specific proteome and transcriptome

While RNA and Protein Tau scores correlated, there was significant variance around that relationship (Fig. S11). The tissues of each species were also categorized based on tissue specificity (TS) score proposed before8. Using the same definition as in a previous report, all proteins with shared gene names across all 6 species could be in any on the four categories: tissue specific, tissue enriched, housekeeping, undefined (Fig. 3A). For example, in human samples, we observed 400 enriched proteins and 100 tissue-specific proteins. Across species, we found a strong overlap between the genes labeled similarly across rodents (Fig. 3B). Cerebellum tissue was found to have the largest number of enriched and specific proteins, followed by testis, bone marrow, and liver (Fig. 3C). Representative profile of the house keeping/tissue specific proteins across species can vary widely, but there are patterns of high specificity in highly differentiated organs like brain or generated organ like testis (Fig. 3C). Technical Validation

We assessed quality of the transcriptomic samples through percent mapped reads to the latest at the time genomes (Ensembl v 110) and achieved over 70% median mapping for the majority of tissues, with notable lower rates for several nervous system tissues (Fig. S11). Additionally, the median genes detected per tissue were upwards of 15,000 for the majority of tissues collected. The median coefficient of variation across samples from the same tissue was also low (0.023), with all tissues falling below CV of 0.1 (Fig. 1, Fig. S12).

For the proteomics data, with peptide and protein FDR controlled at 1%, in total we identified more than 60,000 proteins across six species, with proteome coverage shown in Table 2. Out of all the identified proteins, more than 90% could be quantified, with good quality metrics in terms of TMT labeling completeness (Fig. S13A), enzymatic digestion efficiency (Fig. S13B), observed frequency of proteins across multiple tissues (Fig. S13C), protein identification across species (Fig. S13D), and identification comparison between proteomics and transcriptomics (Fig. S13E). Usage Notes (optional)

Protein degraders potential use

To demonstrate the utility of the cross-species transcriptome and proteome resource we have created, have illustrated some use cases for which this database may be applied. Given the importance of understanding tissue specificity of E3 ligases for the protein degrader modality, we sought to examine this in our transcriptomics data and determine if any genes would show a difference in tissue specificity across species as this could have an impact on species selection for toxicity and efficacy studies. In total, 634 ubiquitin ligases were quantified in at least one species in our transcriptomics dataset (Supplemental Table S4). Of those, 457 were quantified in all 6 species and were used for this analysis. We required a Tau score difference of at least 0.3 between human and any nonclinical species for a gene to be considered as having a difference in tissue specificity. This threshold resulted in 46 genes showing a tissue specificity difference and these were further clustered (Fig. 4A). From this analysis, 8 distinct clusters emerged, and the average cluster profiles of the most concerted ones are shown (Fig. 4B). This analysis revealed several clusters of interest, such as cluster 5 which would suggest dog may be a poor model for these E3 ligases as the expression in dog is more ubiquitous and thus there may be more on-target off-tissue toxicity seen in dogs than would be relevant for humans. Another example is cluster 4 where these ligases are more tissue specific in rat than in human and thus use of rat may risk underestimating any toxicities resulting from engaging these E3s. Gene Expression Profile Correlation Supports Nonclinical Study Species Selection

Target expression and localization data are important determinants of species relevance and selection for nonclinical studies. We compared two human-to-nonclinical-species expression profile correlation approaches to determine their use in species relevance support (Fig. 5); Spearman Rank on log-transformed tpm values, and Pearson correlation of Robust-Z tissue specificity scores. Robust z-scores were calculated for each gene’s log-transformed expression across tissues using the median and median absolute deviation. While both methods demonstrated utility in this application, the comparison demonstrated that the Robust-Z method increased overall correlation in higher proportions for known drug targets compared to all genes (Fig. 5A). A larger number of known drug target genes increased correlation across all nonclinical species, while few had a decrease in their relationship to human transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 5B). When a threshold of 0.3 (i.e. at least ‘low’ correlation) was applied to each method to identify “supported” genes in nonclinical species, Robust Z yielded a higher number of genes with more similar transcriptional profile from targets with approved drugs (Fig. 5C). This analysis is subject to certain constraints, including limiting comparisons to genes with a one-to-one human ortholog, ensuring data is available in both species, and manually mapping tissues for proper alignment. Sex differences across species

There are distinct sex differences in transcription for nonclinical species that are often tissue specific10. These sex differences could also predispose sex specific responses and leading to a potential covariate in toxicity testing. Differential expression based on sex reveals that the top sex differentiated tissues across our samples can be found in cyno skin and dog kidney samples, which is also corroborated by prior findings10 (Fig. S14).
