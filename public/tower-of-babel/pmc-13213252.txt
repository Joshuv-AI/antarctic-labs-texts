Micro-CT evaluation of aligner fit and thickness in 3D-printed and thermoformed aligners: Differences in relation to inner-surface offset design and tooth regions
Authors: Ho-Jin Kim, Hyo-Sang Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the adaptation and thickness of three-dimensional (3D)-printed and thermoformed aligners, comparing differences in relation to fabrication methods, inner-surface offset, and tooth region.MethodsThirty maxillary aligners were fabricated 3D-printed aligners (0.5 mm) with no offset (n = 10) or a 0.05-mm offset (n = 10) and thermoformed aligners from 0.75-mm sheets (n = 10). All aligners were fitted onto a dental model and scanned using micro-computed tomography. Gap distance and thickness were measured at five anterior and seven posterior tooth regions. Thickness ratios after fabrication were calculated. Group and tooth-region differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test, or Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests.ResultsThe no-offset 3D-printed aligners showed larger gaps (0.07–0.15 mm) than the 0.05-mm offset and thermoformed aligners (0.05–0.09 mm). Thickness was greater in 3D-printed aligners (0.68–1.08 mm) than in thermoformed aligners (0.47–0.70 mm). Thickness ratios exceeded 100% in 3D-printed aligners, particularly at the cervical and central fossa regions, whereas thermoformed aligners showed thinning (< 100%), most notably at the labio/buccocervical areas. In comparisons based on tooth region, no-offset 3D-printed aligners showed greater gaps at the buccal, buccocervical, central fossa, and palatal cusp tip regions (P < 0.001), whereas the 0.05-mm offset group exhibited no significant differences. Thermoformed aligners showed larger gaps at the palatocervical, palatal, and palatal cusp tip regions of posterior teeth (P < 0.001).ConclusionsA 0.05-mm offset in 3D-printed aligners produced gap values comparable to those obtained with thermoformed aligners and showed consistent adaptation over tooth regions. 3D-printed aligners tended to show greater thickness than their virtual design, whereas thermoformed aligners showed reduced thickness.

INTRODUCTION

Clear aligner treatment has become increasingly popular due to its superior esthetics, reduced discomfort, improved oral hygiene, and enhanced quality of life during treatment in comparison with fixed appliance treatment.1-3

More recently, clinical outcomes have gradually improved with advancements in virtual treatment-planning software, which allow precise and clinically practical stage-by-stage tooth movement sequences. The strategic use of attachments and auxiliary aligner designs, such as power ridges, bite ramps, and inclined planes, has facilitated more controlled and effective tooth movement.4,5

Despite these improvements, discrepancies between the virtually planned and clinically achieved outcomes remain a major challenge for clinicians. The overall mean accuracy of planned versus achieved tooth movement has been reported to be approximately 50%, with rotation corrections demonstrating the lowest predictability, particularly in rounded teeth such as canines, premolars, and molars.4 Another study reported that angular discrepancies in mesiodistal tipping, buccolingual tipping, and rotation exceeded 2°, and the extent of mesiodistal translation was insufficient by 0.3–0.4 mm for every 1 mm of virtually planned movement.6

One potential cause of these discrepancies is inadequate aligner adaptation to the tooth surface. Poor and uneven fit can disrupt uniform force distribution, causing unintended tooth movements that deviate from the digital plan. Thus, intimate and consistent aligner adaptation across all tooth regions is essential to minimize undesired intrusion or tipping.7,8 Aligner thickness is another important factor because it directly influences mechanical properties and, consequently, the magnitude of forces exerted on the individual tooth or specific regions.9 Regional reductions in thickness, especially at cervical areas, can lead to decreased force delivery, potentially causing a lower moment-to-force ratio and an increased risk of unintended intrusion.7,10

Improvements in aligner materials have focused on enhancing the accuracy and predictability of tooth movement. In this regard, conventional single-layer thermoformed polyethylene terephthalate glycol sheets have been replaced by multilayer materials incorporating a more elastic layer, thereby improving mechanical properties and enabling the application of a lighter and more continuous force.9,11 More recently, three-dimensional (3D)-printable aligners with shape-memory properties have been developed and applied clinically using computer-aided design systems.12-14 Previous studies have shown that thermoformed aligners undergo significant thickness reduction during fabrication,15 with greater reduction observed in the posterior teeth than in the anterior teeth and at cervical regions than at occlusal regions.10 The fit of thermoformed aligners tends to be better in anterior teeth, with smaller gaps in comparison with those in posterior teeth.15 For 3D-printed aligners, post-fabrication thickness was found to increase, with gap distances similar to those of thermoformed aligners.16,17 Aligner thickness and marginal fits may vary not only by the fabrication method (thermoforming versus 3D printing) but also in relation to the tooth position and anatomical region, even when aligners are designed with identical dimensions. However, few studies have compared the variations in thickness and aligner fit between thermoformed and 3D-printed aligners with respect to tooth position, anatomical regions, and inner-surface offsets designed to compensate for potential inner-surface thickening in 3D-printed aligners.

This study aimed to assess and compare the adaptation and thickness of 3D-printed and thermoformed aligners. The 3D-printed aligners were further compared across different inner-surface offset values. Aligner gap and thickness were analyzed between fabrication methods and between tooth regions. The null hypotheses were that no significant differences would exist between fabrication methods or between offset values in 3D-printed aligners.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample

This in vitro study was approved by the institutional review board of Kyungpook National University Dental Hospital (institutional review board approval no. KNUDH-2025-02-01-00).

A maxillary dental arch with a 2-mm tooth size-arch length discrepancy was used. The cervical undercuts of the anterior teeth had a mean depth of 2.23 ± 0.86 mm and a height of 7.82 ± 1.84 mm, and those of the posterior teeth were 0.55 ± 0.30 mm in depth and 2.28 ± 0.88 mm in height.18 The dental model was scanned using a Trios 4 intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The resulting stereolithography (STL) file was used to fabricate both 3D-printed and thermoformed aligners. Ten aligners were fabricated for each group on the basis of previous research.17,19

For the 3D-printed aligners, the STL file was imported into aligner-designing software (Direct Aligner Designer, Graphy Inc., Seoul, Korea). Then, an aligner was designed with a uniform thickness of 0.5 mm and a straight gingival line located 1 mm apical to the gingival margin of the clinical crown. Two types of 3D-printed aligners were designed on the basis of the inner-surface offset applied uniformly to the entire tooth surface: no offset and a 0.05-mm offset (Figure 1).17 Ten aligners of each offset type were oriented at a 45° angle to the build platform (Figure 2). Cone-type supports were generated using UNIZ Dental software 2.4.0.6 (UNIZ, San Diego, CA, USA) with a touch point size of 0.40 mm, head length of 2.50 mm, and diameter of 1.8 mm. Supports were placed at a low density on the external surface only to preserve internal surface integrity. Subsequently, the aligners were directly printed using a clear photocurable resin (TA-28, Graphy Inc.) on a 3D printer (NBEE, UNIZ) with a 50-μm layer thickness. After 3D printing, the aligners underwent centrifugation at 500 rpm for 6 minutes to remove uncured resin, and the supports were manually removed using a precision nipper. Subsequently, post-curing was performed for 20 minutes in an ultraviolet curing machine utilizing a 405-nm light-emitting diode source with irradiance of 1,000 W/cm2 within a nitrogen-rich chamber (Tera Harz Cure, Graphy Inc.). To enhance the polymerization of the residual monomers, the post-cured aligners were submerged in 100°C water for 1 minute and ultrasonically cleaned for 3 minutes, which was followed by a finishing process with a fine-grit rotary bur to ensure a smooth, uniform edge thickness at the support attachment areas.

For the thermoformed aligners, 10 maxillary models (S-200M, Graphy Inc.) were printed to a height of 12 mm using the same STL file. The models were then cleaned with isopropyl alcohol for 1 minute, post-cured (Tera Harz Cure) for 6 minutes, and ultrasonically cleaned for 3 minutes. Each model was used to fabricate an aligner by positive pressure to form a 0.75-mm-thick, three-layer thermoplastic sheet (CA Pro+, Scheu Dental, Iserlohn, Germany). This process involved heating at 220°C for 25 seconds, positive-pressure forming onto the dental models under 4.8-bar pressure, and air cooling for 1 minute (Biostar, Scheu Dental). The thermoformed aligners were trimmed with a straight cut line 1 mm apical to the gingival margin and gently separated from the dental model.

In total, three aligner groups were analyzed: 3D-printed with no offset (n = 10), 3D-printed with a 0.05-mm offset (n = 10), and thermoformed (n = 10).

Micro-CT scan and measurement

All aligners were fitted over a single maxillary dental model to maintain consistency. This model was 3D-printed from the same STL file and under identical 3D-printing and post-processing conditions as the 10 maxillary models used for the thermoformed group. All samples were then scanned using a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (X-eye CT, SEC Co., Ltd., Suwon, Korea) at 154 kVp, 62 mA, and a voxel size of 40 μm. Before scanning, the 3D-printed aligners were submerged in water at 70°C for 5 seconds and fitted to the model. They were subsequently air-dried at 37°C for 60 seconds, which is equivalent to the human body temperature.13,20 The thermoformed aligners were fitted onto the same model at room temperature.

The acquired scans were exported in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format and imported into CT analysis software—InVivo 6 (Anatomage Inc., San Jose, CA, USA)—for measurement of study variables. The aligner gap distance and thickness were measured at five regions of the central incisor and canine and seven regions of the second premolar and first molar (Figure 3). Thickness ratios were calculated to assess post-fabrication changes, based on the 0.5-mm digital design value for 3D-printed aligners and the 0.75-mm original sheet thickness for thermoformed aligners.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed by a single investigator (HJK). To assess method error and the reliability of the measurements, 10 samples were randomly selected from the 30 aligners and remeasured after a two-week interval. Method error, which was calculated using Dahlberg’s formula, ranged from 0.03 to 0.10 mm. Intraclass correlation coefficients were above 0.90, indicating excellent reliability.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data distribution. For normally distributed variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare differences between the aligner groups or tooth regions. When the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated, Welch’s ANOVA followed by Dunnett T3 multiple-comparison test was applied. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test and Mann–Whitney U test were used for group comparisons.

RESULTS

Comparison of the aligner gap and thickness between 3D-printed and thermoformed aligners

The gaps between the tooth surface and the 3D-printed aligners with no offset ranged from 0.07 to 0.15 mm (Table 1). In comparison with the gaps obtained with the 0.05-mm offset or the thermoformed aligners, these gaps were 0.09 mm higher at the palatocervical region of the central incisor and the incisal region of the canine (P = 0.003) and 0.08 mm higher at the buccal region of the second premolar (P < 0.001). The 3D-printed aligners with a 0.05-mm offset and the thermoformed aligners exhibited smaller gaps (0.05–0.09 mm) with no significant differences between them, except for approximately 0.04-mm differences at the labial/buccal region of the canine and second premolar and at the palatal region of the first molar.

The thickness of the 3D-printed aligners ranged from 0.68 to 1.08 mm, with no significant differences between offset groups except at the palatal and labial regions of the incisor or canine (Table 2). In comparison with the 3D-printed aligners, the thermoformed aligners were significantly thinner, measuring 0.47–0.65 mm in the anterior teeth and 0.55–0.70 mm in the posterior teeth.

Comparison of aligner gap and thickness between tooth regions

The 3D-printed aligners with a 0.05-mm offset exhibited no significant gap differences between the tooth regions (Table 3). In contrast, the no-offset aligners showed significantly greater gaps (0.11–0.13 mm) at the buccal, buccocervical, central fossa, and palatal cusp tip regions of posterior teeth than at the other regions (P < 0.001). Thermoformed aligners showed significantly larger gaps (0.08–0.13 mm) at the palatocervical, palatal, and palatal cusp tip regions of posterior teeth (P < 0.001).

For 3D-printed aligners of both offset types, thickness was the highest (0.95–1.05 mm) at the palatocervical and labiocervical regions of the anterior teeth (P < 0.001) and lowest (0.70 mm) at the incisal region. In posterior teeth, thickness was the greatest at the central fossa (1.00 mm), followed by the buccocervical/palatocervical regions, cusp tips, and buccal/palatal regions (P < 0.001). For the thermoformed aligners, the palatocervical and palatal regions were thicker than the labiocervical region in anterior teeth (P = 0.003) and thicker than the buccocervical region in posterior teeth (P < 0.001).

Ratios of fabricated aligner thickness in relation to the digitally set thickness and initial thermoplastic sheet thickness

Thickness-change ratios after fabrication (Table 4) exceeded 100% for 3D-printed aligners, indicating thickening after the printing process. In the anterior teeth, the ratios ranged from 140% at the incisal region to 200% at the palatocervical and labiocervical regions; in the posterior teeth, the ratios ranged from 150% at the buccal region to 200% at the central fossa. In contrast, the thermoformed aligners exhibited ratios below 100%, reflecting material thinning from sheet stretching during thermoforming. The anterior teeth ratios ranged from 67% at the labiocervical region to 79% at the palatal region; for the posterior teeth, the ratios ranged from 73% at the buccocervical region to 87% at the palatal region.

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the gap and thickness of aligners fabricated using recently developed materials, including a 3D-printable shape-memory polymer and a multilayer thermoplastic sheet. Comparisons were performed between the 3D printing and thermoforming methods across different tooth positions and anatomical regions, as well as in relation to the inner-surface offset values applied in 3D-printed aligners.

For the 3D-printed aligners, the thickness exceeded the digital set value of 0.5 mm by 0.2–0.5 mm, corresponding to a 140% to 200% increase (Figures 4 and 5). This discrepancy was larger than the 0.06-mm increase reported for simplified specimens in a previous study.13 Interestingly, this study demonstrated that the thickness variation was tooth- and region-dependent. The buccal, palatal, and cervical regions of the anterior teeth were thicker than the corresponding regions of the posterior teeth. Within individual teeth, the cervical and central fossa regions were thicker than the other regions. This finding contrasts with the results of a previous micro-CT study that reported smaller cervical thicknesses in comparison with occlusal or labial/palatal regions, as well as larger cervical gaps.17 One possible explanation is that the gingival margin of the aligners in this study was designed to extend 1 mm below the gingival margin to enhance cervical adaptation.21 Additionally, the observed thickness discrepancies across tooth regions may be attributable to excess resin accumulation influenced by the 45°-angled 3D printing orientation used in this study (Figure 2). This orientation likely promoted the accumulation of uncured resin in the anterior parts and on flat surfaces parallel to the build platform, such as the palatal, palatocervical, and central fossa regions. This retained resin contributed to the additional localized thickness increase after polymerization under over-penetrating light during printing or post-curing.19,22 More importantly, inner-surface thickening can interfere with proper aligner seating, since premature contact at the overthickened occlusal third and buccopalatal surfaces can compromise adaptation in adjacent areas. This interference may have been responsible for the significantly larger gaps shown by 3D-printed aligners without an offset at the palatocervical regions of the incisors and second premolars, and at the incisal region of the canines, in comparison with those with a 0.05-mm offset. Although a previous study23 highlighted the higher trueness and precision of 3D-printed aligners in comparison with the thermoformed aligners, that analysis relied on absolute error values derived from distances between bilateral landmarks rather than a comprehensive analysis of the inner surface. The current findings suggest that unintentional inner-surface thickening may compromise adaptation to the tooth surface. Notably, when a 0.05-mm inner-surface offset was applied, 3D-printed aligners demonstrated no significant difference in gap in comparison with the thermoformed aligners and maintained consistent adaptation across all measured regions. These findings highlight the importance of an optimal inner-surface offset to compensate for inherent material thickening during 3D printing and to ensure intimate aligner fit. Although the individual gap difference of 0.08–0.09 mm between the no-offset and the 0.05-mm offset or thermoformed groups did not significantly exceed the method error (0.03 to 0.10 mm), their cumulative clinical impact requires consideration. Since two-point contact is clinically critical for achieving bodily or controlled tipping, the sum of gap differences at opposing points, such as the palatocervical and incisal/labial regions, may reach approximately 0.20 mm. This cumulative gap is comparable to a typical staging tooth movement of 0.20 mm20 and exceeds the range of the method error. Therefore, the larger gaps in the 3D-printed aligner with no offset could reduce the efficiency of force delivery and result in less predictable tooth movement. Meanwhile, greater aligner thicknesses generate greater forces.20,24 The 3D-printed aligners exhibited greater thickness at the labiocervical and buccocervical regions than at the occlusal or middle third. These cervical thickenings likely support controlled tipping or bodily movement through the application of a coupling moment.21,25 Moreover, the consistent adaptation observed over the tooth regions in the 0.05-mm offset group indicates that adequate forces can be delivered even in cervical areas. This may reduce the adverse effects that are occasionally associated with thermoformed aligners, such as uncontrolled tipping or intrusive force during rotation corrections.7,8 Additionally, the shape-memory properties allow the design of 3D-printed aligners with minimal blockout in tooth undercuts, thereby maximizing surface contact and potentially enhancing tooth movement accuracy. However, the specific influence of these variations in fit and thickness on clinical outcomes remains to be elucidated. Future in vitro and in vivo studies comparing planned and achieved tooth movements are required to confirm these effects. Furthermore, while the increased thickness of the 3D-printed aligners may help reduce the risk of aligner breakage, it may affect esthetics due to a bulkier appearance.

In contrast, the thermoformed aligners exhibited a thickness reduction of 0.05–0.25 mm relative to the original thermoplastic sheet thickness of 0.75 mm. This thickness reduction was greater in the anterior teeth, likely because the greater clinical crown height required further sheet extension during pressure forming (Figure 4). In particular, the labiocervical and buccocervical regions exhibited significantly lower thickness than the other regions, which was likely attributable to the labial or buccal tooth inclination creating undercuts that necessitated further sheet extension for adequate coverage. Accordingly, regional thinning may deliver reduced orthodontic force, potentially hindering planned bodily movement or controlled tipping.26 For example, when maxillary incisors are set up with increased labial inclination to achieve bodily movement, the resulting thermoformed aligner may become thinner at the labiocervical regions due to increased sheet stretching over undercuts. This region-specific thinning can cause reduced force at the labiocervical areas but higher forces at occlusal surfaces, increasing the risk of unintended uncontrolled tipping instead of the desired bodily movement.7,8 Additionally, greater gaps at the palatocervical, palatal, and palatal cusp tip regions of the posterior teeth may result from distortion during aligner removal from the model, indicating the need to consider potential inaccuracies even after pressure forming. Thus, press-fit at the cervical area may be required to improve marginal fit and development of innovative nonuniform-thickness sheets that increase thickness at the margin of the aligners.

This study evaluated aligner gaps and thickness according to fabrication method, tooth, and anatomical regions. However, several limitations of the study require consideration. First, these findings do not account for the presence of tooth attachments, which may influence aligner fit. Second, this study did not determine the specific distribution of material thickening between the external and internal surfaces during the 3D-printing process. In addition, while each thermoformed aligner was fabricated on an individual 3D-printed model to prevent heat-induced deformation, this approach may have introduced minor model-to-model variability. Future studies should investigate the influence of attachment number, shape, and position on aligner fit and thickness, and they should assess the localized material thickening on each aligner surface. Additionally, establishing specific conditions that more accurately simulate the actual intraoral environment is crucial to effectively investigate the clinical effects of shape-memory properties in future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

The null hypotheses were rejected.

The 3D-printed aligners with a 0.05-mm offset and the thermoformed aligners showed smaller gaps than 3D-printed aligners with no offset.

The 3D-printed aligners showed a thickness increase of 0.18–0.58 mm relative to the 0.5-mm digital design, whereas thermoformed aligners demonstrated a 0.05–0.28 mm reduction from 0.75-mm sheet thickness.

In 3D-printed aligners, thickness was the greatest at the cervical and central fossa regions, whereas thermoformed aligners exhibited pronounced reduction at the labiocervical and buccocervical regions.
