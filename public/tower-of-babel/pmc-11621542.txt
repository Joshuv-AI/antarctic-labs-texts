Machine learning approach identifies inflammatory gene signature for predicting survival outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Noor N Al-Bzour, Ayah N Al-Bzour, Abdelrahman Qasaymeh, Azhar Saeed, Lujia Chen, Anwaar Saeed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, often linked to chronic inflammation. Our study aimed to probe inflammation pathways at the genetic level and pinpoint biomarkers linked to HCC patient survival.MethodsWe analyzed gene transcriptome data from 246 resectable stage I and II HCC patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). After selecting 917 inflammation-related genes (IRGs), we identified 104 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) through differential expression analysis. Two significant prognostic DEGs, S100A9 and PBK, were identified using LASSO and Cox regression, forming the basis of a risk score model. We conducted functional enrichment and immune landscape analyses, validated our findings on 170 patients from the GSE14520 dataset, and performed mutational analysis using TCGA somatic mutation data.ResultsWe analyzed 296 samples (246 HCC, 50 normal liver), showing significant survival differences between high and low-risk groups based on our risk score model. Functional enrichment analysis unveiled inflammation-associated pathways. Validation using the GSE14520 dataset confirmed our risk score’s predictive ability, and we explored clinical correlations.ConclusionOur study delineates inflammation-related genomic changes in HCC, unveiling prognostic biomarkers with potential therapeutic implications. These findings deepen our understanding of HCC molecular mechanisms and may guide personalized therapeutic approaches, ultimately improving patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81395-x.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Inflammation, Biomarkers, Prognosis, Survival, Machine-learningSubject terms: Liver cancer, Cancer genomics, Cancer microenvironment, Tumour biomarkers

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer. Liver cancers worldwide rank as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths and sixth in terms of incident cases1. HCC can be regarded as an example of inflammation-derived cancer due to chronic liver injury as many liver cancer cases (80–90%) result from chronic inflammation of the liver. This inflammation can be caused by hepatitis B, hepatitis C, alcoholic and non-alcoholic liver diseases, or cirrhosis of the liver2.

A plethora of new evidence shows the importance of inflammation in the pathogenesis of chronic diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and inflammatory bowel disease3.

Inflammation is usually viewed as a vital response to microbial invasion or tissue injury to maintain tissue homeostasis4. It can be divided according to the duration of the disease into acute inflammation and chronic inflammation. Acute inflammation is defined as the initial response to harmful stimuli and typically lasts for a few days or weeks, with granulocytes as the predominant inflammatory cells. On the other hand, chronic inflammation is characterized by the simultaneous occurrence of tissue destruction and healing. Macrophages and lymphocytes are the primary immune cells found at sites of chronic inflammation5.

A major consequence of chronic inflammation is the impairment of liver epithelial cells, causing significant cell proliferation. Concurrently, inflammation triggers the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and damages DNA, elevating the occurrence of DNA mutations. As cell proliferation rates escalate alongside DNA mutations, the likelihood of malignant transformation proportionately rises6.

There are multiple signaling pathways implicated in both the onset and advancement of inflammation; Traditional inflammatory pathways include p38 MAPK, IL-6/JAK/STAT3, and PI3K, while the Hippo pathway represents a non-classical route. Notably, the Hippo pathway has been associated with several inflammatory mediators, including FoxO1/3, TNFα, IL-6, COX2, HIF-1α, AP-1, JAK, and STAT7.

The microenvironment of HCC is special due to the underlying chronic inflammation8, which is immunosuppressive, leading to immune tolerance and facilitating tumor growth, invasion, and spread. These cancer-promoting effects result from numerous factors, including environmental signals, processes related to carcinogenesis, and the presence of different immune-suppressing cell types, inflammatory molecules, and signaling pathways9. Such changes in this microenvironment, along with other leading factors of chronic liver disease, influence the mutational rate and pattern of the genome10.

Regarding HCC biomarkers, serological parameters are the most used, such as thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, hypoproteinemia, and plasma fibrinogen11. While advancements in computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have significantly enhanced the diagnostic capabilities for HCC, the current cost of these procedures renders widespread screening impractical11. The pretreatment peripheral neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been reported as an independent predictor of survival in HCC. Additionally, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) has shown correlations with the prognosis of HCC12.

RNA sequencing is a modern deep-sequencing technique, that identifies splice variants, unannotated genes, and spliced noncoding RNAs that have not been previously recognized13. There is ample evidence indicating that mRNA-level gene signatures offer considerable potential in predicting the prognosis of HCC14.

Due to the important role of inflammation in driving HCC progression, several studies have focused on the inflammation pathway to identify potential biomarkers associated with prognosis. Of these studies, a study investigated the role of adenylate uridylate-rich element genes (AREGs) in HCC. The prognostic model that they constructed was based on CENPA, TXNRD1, RABIF, UGT2B15, and SERPINE1 selected to form the AREG-related signature, which demonstrated strong prognostic accuracy. Their results reported that the inflammation-associated AREG-related signature model can serve as a prognostic indicator for HCC15. Another study developed an inflammation risk model based on 6 genes (SSP1, ADAMTS5, EPO CXCR3, TNFRSF13C, and CRYAA) which also reports the strong association of their model with survival and immune status in HCC16.

However, inflammation-based genomic alterations are yet to be studied in HCC, which may offer valuable insight into new potential candidates for drug development. Hence, in this study, we aim to investigate the influence of the inflammation pathway at the genomic level and identify biomarkers associated with survival outcomes in HCC patients.

Methods

Data resources

We downloaded the gene transcriptome data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), for patients with HCC (n = 246) and normal samples (n = 50). We processed the clinical data of the patients and included the resectable stage I and II HCC patients. We selected only early-stage patients to ensure homogeneity within the patient population and to prevent confounding effects from advanced disease, such as metastasis and liver dysfunction. After removing the patients with incomplete survival outcomes, we included 246 patients in the downstream analysis. The clinical characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1.

We obtained the inflammation-related genes (IRGs) from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). We selected 917 IRGs with relevance score > 3 and protein-coding genes, for further analysis.

Identification of differentially expressed IRGs

Gene expression data was normalized using log2-fold change (Log2FC). Then, we applied differential expression analysis (DEA) on the gene expression data between the normal samples and the HCC samples using the “DESeq2” R package with |Log2FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.0517. We identified 1742 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and intersected the DEGs with IRGs to obtain 104 inflammation- DEGs.

Finding the independent predictors of survival

We fitted the inflammation-DEGs in a prognostic model using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) for multicollinearity reduction using “glmnet” R package. The features that were fitted are the gene expression levels of the inflammation-DEGs. The model used an L1​-norm penalty to perform variable selection and shrinkage, which allowed us to identify the genes with non-zero coefficients to be the most predictive of survival outcomes. The optimal value for the regularization parameter 𝜆 was determined using 10-fold cross-validation to minimize the mean squared error on the test folds. Finally, we obtained 9 significant prognostic DEGs18,19. Then we fitted these significant genes in univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression model to identify the independent predictors of survival and estimated their hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. The probability values were considered significant if the p-value < 0.05. We obtained two significant genes and selected them for the risk score model construction.

To assess the strength and direction of the associations between the covariates, we calculated the correlation coefficients (β). The coefficients represent the log hazard ratios obtained from the cox regression model, and these are used to quantify the relationship between each covariate and outcome variable.

The optimal cut-off point was set based on survival using the R package “cutpointr”, which is a data-driven approach that works by assessing all the possible cut-off points and selecting the one with the most significant difference in the survival outcomes, more specifically, it works by maximizing the area under the curve (AUC) for survival. The patients were then grouped into high-risk and low-risk groups. Although sensitivity analysis was not performed, the selected threshold provided a statistically significant separation of patients into high-risk and low-risk groups.

We performed survival analysis using the R packages “survival” and “survminer”20,21. We used a log-rank test to compare the difference in survival outcomes between the low-risk and high-risk groups and assessed the sensitivity and specificity of the signature using the time-dependent receiver operating characteristics analysis (ROC). We used Kaplan-Meier curves to compare and visualize the prognosis between the two groups as well.

Functional enrichment & immune landscape analysis

Gene ontology (GO) analysis was applied to the inflammation DEGs to identify the biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The gene ontology analysis was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package in R and visualized using the “topGO” R package22,23. In addition, a protein-protein interactions (PPI) network was constructed between the inflammation-DEGs using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to build the up- and down-stream interactions and relationships between proteins24.

To compare the tumor immune microenvironment across the high- and low-groups, we explored the activity of Hallmark gene set pathways and the Reactome signaling pathways using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)25, which is an unsupervised method for pathway enrichment evaluation.

Additionally, we evaluated the tumor immune microenvironment and infiltration across different immune cells using the TIMER algorithm. We used TISCH database to evaluate the expression of retrieved genes across single-cell RNA datasets (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/).

External validation of the risk score

We used the GSE14520 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)26. By including stage I and II HCC we obtained a total of 170 patients for the validation of the risk score. Using the same formula, we calculated the risk score for the patients assessed the survival outcomes using the log-rank test, and visualized the results using Kaplan-Meier Curve. In addition, we compared and analyzed the clinical characteristics of the patients across the groups using statistical tests.

Statistical analysis

We applied descriptive statistical analysis to the clinicopathological data of the HCC patients. The continuous variables were described using the mean ± standard deviation, and the categorical variables were described as frequencies/proportions. Wilcoxon rank sum test, Fisher’s exact test, and Pearson’s Chi-squared test were applied to the ordinal and categorical variables across the two groups respectively. Features with p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All data was processed and analyzed using R software (v4.2.3). The overall flow chart of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Identification of inflammation DEGs

A total of 296 samples were obtained from the TCGA database, of which 246 were HCC samples, and 50 were normal liver samples. The mRNA expression of the 296 samples was used for differential expression analysis between HCC and normal samples and resulted in 1742 HCC-DEGs, of which 827 genes were significantly upregulated, and 915 genes were significantly downregulated at adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |Log2FC|>0.5 (Fig. 2A-B). The 1742 HCC-DEGs were then intersected with the 915 IRGs to obtain inflammation-DEGs, yielding a total of 104 inflammation-DEGs, of which 70 genes were upregulated, and 34 genes were downregulated (Fig. 2C-D).

Establishment of the inflammatory prognostic score

Among the 104 inflammation-related DEGs, we obtained 9 genes by primary screening of the survival-related genes using the LASSO model which are: “ANGPT2”, “GNB3”, “IGFALS”, “IL33”, “ITGB4”, “LPL”, “PBK”, “PON1”, “S100A9”, (shown in Fig. 3A). For further screening of the genes that are considered independently associated with survival, they were then fitted in univariate Cox model and six significant candidates were further fitted in multivariate Cox models that yielded 2 significant independent predictors of survival for which p-value < 0.05, and those are S100A9 & PBK as shown in Fig. 3B in which both genes act as risk associated factors (HR > 1). After calculating the risk score based on the expression of the genes and their corresponding correlation coefficients obtained from the cox model, the optimal cut-off point was set to 2.8 based on survival. Patients were then grouped into high (n = 83) and low groups (n = 163). The low-risk group was associated with better survival outcomes in terms of overall-survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), disease-free survival (DFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) (p-value:0.00011, 0.042, 0.028, 0.015 respectively). Log-rank test was used to make the correlation and Kaplan-Meier curves were used to confirm the difference between the risk groups in the survival function, as shown in Fig. 3C-F show the association between the inflammation score and the survival outcomes.

Discussion

HCC cases have been increasing over the past decades. In addition to the common prevalence of disease occurrence, it also ranks third among tumors in terms of mortality rates27,28. Moreover, HCC has a high chance of recurrence and metastasis, which is multifactorial but primarily due to the nature of circulating tumor cells29,30, as well as circulating immune and inflammatory cells including neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets31. Hence, it is important to identify patients who are at increased risk of metastasis for optimal treatment strategies.

As inflammatory cells play a role in tumor progression, several inflammatory and immune-based prognostic tests have been established to determine survival and/or recurrence. These include cell counts such as lymphocyte count32, neutrophile-lymphocytes ratio (NLR)33, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and platelet-lymphocytes ratio (PLR)34,35. These scores offer insight into the host’s inflammatory and immune response to tumor invasion and thus may be predictive of survival.

Despite having these tests for prognosis prediction, inflammation-based genomic alterations have not been studied in HCC. In this study, we identified novel inflammatory markers that are associated with survival and prognosis in HCC. These markers may offer new insights for developing therapeutic targets for tumor eradication and suppression.

In our study, we included patients with early stages (I-II) HCC to develop an inflammation-based risk score. We identified 104 differentially expressed inflammation genes. Then, 9 prognostic genes were obtained from LASSO algorithm analysis. Among these genes, 6 genes were obtained from univariate cox analysis (“ANGPT2”, “IGFALS”, “IL33”, “PBK”, “PON1”, and “S100A9”) in which these genes were further screened using multivariate cox model to obtain 2 genes for risk score construction. Using a risk score formula, patients were divided into two groups, where in the training set, the low-risk group had better survival outcomes in terms of OS, PFS, DFS, and DSS, and in the validation set, the low-risk group had better survival outcomes in terms of OS, and DFS.

Our study showed that the genes S100A9 and PBK act as independent predictors of survival and are associated with worse outcomes. The gene S100A9 is an immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages marker gene, and it works by increasing the tumor-associated macrophages infiltration through promoting CCL2 secretion which ultimately promote the stem-cell HCC cells via the CA2+-dependent signaling within the AGER/NF-κB axis36. Moreover, studies have shown that S100A9 is associated with short survival period for cancer patients as well as development of adverse events to immunotherapy37. A study conducted by Meng et al. reported that patients with advanced HCC showed a significantly higher levels of S100A9, as well as observed events of recurrence and death, suggesting that serum S100A9 may serve as a novel predictor for prognosis in HCC patients and further helping in treatment strategy38. Several studies have investigated the role of S100A9 in HCC, however, our study examines S100A9 within the broader context of the inflammation pathway as a whole not only as a single gene. We focused on identifying inflammation-related genes that play a significant role in HCC prognosis, and through this pathway-specific approach, we identified S100A9 alongside PBK as key genes with prognostic potential.

Tumor recurrence is a strong indicator of the aggressiveness of the tumor cells. The cells that possess high invasive potential could circulate and spread to nearby tissues even in early-stage course39. S100A9 was proven to play a pivotal role in HCC proliferation and invasion by the activation of ERK signaling pathway40. Given that S100A9 is a protein released into the serum, measuring its concentration levels in the serum is a dependable method for assessing recurrence and survival outcomes, as well as for selecting the most appropriate treatment strategy41.

The gene PBK is serine/threonine kinase that belongs to the mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) family42. Recent studies have shown that PBK was highly expressed in various tumors, with increased association with poor prognosis43. A study conducted by Yang et al. confirmed that PBK is upregulated in HCC and its correlation with poor clinicopathological features. Their study showed that PBK plays a role in cytoskeletal changes such as disorganization and rearrangement of actin filaments44. Moreover, the function of PBK has been studied by Yang and colleagues in which they reported that it works by activating the FoxM1/β-Catenin pathway and hence, promoting cancer cell proliferation and migration45. Taken these roles together, PBK can be used as novel predictive biomarker in HCC.

While both PBK and S100A9 play roles in promoting HCC progression, their mechanisms of action differ. PBK primarily acts through kinase activity to regulate cell signaling pathways involved in proliferation and survival46, while S100A9 likely modulates tumor behavior through calcium binding and its interactions with other proteins, possibly influencing inflammatory and immune responses within the tumor microenvironment36,47. Thus, although both genes are associated with poor outcomes in HCC, they operate through distinct molecular mechanisms, highlighting the complexity of tumor biology and the multifaceted nature of cancer progression.

A study by Zhong et al. utilized Tas, a drug with selective neutralizing activity against S100A9, to investigate the role of S100A9 in HCC cells. They reported that the differentially expressed genes between S100A9 wild-type and S100A9 knockdown cells engaged in the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway and confirmed these findings using western blotting. Additionally, overexpression of S100A9 promoted HCC proliferation and migration48. These results support the role of S100A9 in vitro. For further exploration of the S100A9 in vivo, they generated a subcutaneous tumor model. The results showed that the knockout of S100A9 significantly delayed tumor progression whereas overexpression of the gene has markedly enhanced tumor growth. Moreover, in the nude mouse model, S100A9 wild-type HCC cells demonstrated a marked increase in lung colonization capacity compared to S100A9 null cells. This capacity was significantly reduced following treatment with Tas in mice48.

PBK knockdown or treatment with PBK inhibitors has been shown to dampen the tumorigenicity and growth in human cancers49. Yang et al. reported that PBK knockdown inhibited HCC cell migration and invasion in vitro50. The in vitro and in vivo studies show that PBK promotes oncogenic functions in HCC through activating the β-Catenin signaling pathway. Inhibiting β-Catenin through siRNAs or XAV-939 significantly reduces the malignant phenotypes associated with PBK. Additionally, PBK is identified as a downstream effector of FoxM1. Clinical samples show that PBK expression is positively correlated with both FoxM1 and nuclear β-Catenin levels. These findings suggest that PBK acts as an oncogene in HCC, and the newly identified FoxM1/PBK/β-Catenin axis could serve as a promising prognostic factor and therapeutic target for HCC50.

In our study, the risk score signature that we developed was independently predictive of survival. Furthermore, we employed an independent dataset to confirm the effectiveness of the signature in predicting outcomes, and the results were statistically significant. A study conducted by Xing et al. used a six inflammation genes risk model to predict prognosis in HCC patients51. The genes they found were SSP1, CXCR3, ADAMTS5, EPO, TNFRSF13C and CRYAA. Their risk assessment was strongly linked to reduced immune infiltration. It was observed that a higher risk score corresponded to lower survival rates. Nevertheless, the primary shortcoming of the Xing et al. study is its absence of a validation cohort for the risk model.

For the enrichment analysis between the low-risk and high-risk groups, our study aligns with enriched pathway reported by Xing et al.51. These pathways include the roles in orchestrating inflammatory and immune processes in the microenvironment of the tumor. Both studies involve the activation of various signaling cascades and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which contribute to tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. The identification of these similar pathways across different analyses underscores their importance in HCC pathogenesis and highlights potential targets for therapeutic intervention aimed at modulating the inflammatory and immune responses within the tumor microenvironment to improve patient outcomes.

KRAS is an important member of the RAS family that includes important signaling transduction pathways such as PI3K/AKT. It plays a crucial role in tumor progression and the angiogenesis process52. Our enrichment analysis showed that KRAS signaling was significantly downregulated in the low-risk group which is consistent with the literature, in which studies suggest that the evidence of KRAS pathway activation largely participates in the initiation and progression of HCC53. On the other hand, the JAK/STAT3 pathway was significantly upregulated in the high-risk group suggesting its role in worse prognosis. The activation of JAK/STAT3 pathway contributes to tumor metastasis and angiogenesis54.

In this study, we zoomed in at the genomic level to find the inflammatory biomarkers that are associated with prognosis. The strength of this study is that we focused on the resectable stages I and II to find the survival-associated biomarkers and reduce the bias by increasing the selectivity. Focusing on early stage HCC offers a more homogenous study population, reducing the variability and improving the reliability of the findings, which ultimately allows for a clearer vision for the evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy and factors influencing the long-term survival. In addition, we discovered other potential pathways that may show a correlation with the inflammatory pathway, and these need to be further investigated together.

The usage of machine learning techniques in developing our prognostic model enhances both accuracy and generalizability, as shown in our study. The effectiveness of the machine learning models, including LASSO and univariate and multivariate Cox regression, is evident in their ability to identify key prognostic factors and predict patient outcomes reliably. The pipeline that we used in our study integrates diverse data types that include both gene expression profiles and clinical parameters. This can be further applied to other malignancies to investigate the role of inflammation biomarkers influencing survival outcomes. Moreover, the external validation of the risk score increased the robustness of our study. Nevertheless, it is recommended to expand the sample size to include more women and conduct further validation with additional cohorts. For future directions, evaluating the prognostic significance of these genes to verify their dependability and applicability in a clinical setting is advised, as well as to aid in drug development. Additionally, examining the signature in advanced-stage HCC and its ability to predict responses to immunotherapy is suggested.

Unlike previous studies that combine resectable and non-resectable HCC cases, we focused exclusively on early-stage, resectable HCC patients. This enables our model to provide a more precise prognostic assessment for this specific patient population. Our methodology distinguishes itself by employing a more comprehensive selection of HCC-specific IRGs and refining these to the two key prognostic DEGs, S100A9 and PBK, which offer a more targeted risk model and reduces the risk of overfitting. Moreover, we validated our findings with an external dataset (GSE14520), confirming the model’s predictive power across diverse patient populations. Additionally, our inclusion of functional enrichment and immune landscape analyses underscores the biological and clinical relevance of our findings, providing a risk model that reflects the inflammatory and immune dynamics specific to HCC accurately.

In conclusion, our research introduced a risk score derived from the expression patterns of two genes linked to inflammation. We applied this score to a cohort of early-stage HCC patients and demonstrated its effectiveness in predicting survival rates. Furthermore, we identified additional pathways connected to inflammation, warranting deeper exploration. The identified biomarkers show promise as potential treatment targets, given their strong correlation with patient survival, thereby offering potential for enhancing outcomes and patient well-being.
