Targeting protein aggregate co-pathologies in neurodegeneration: a viable therapeutic strategy?
Authors: Silas A Buck, Sidharth S Madhavan, Laurie H Sanders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by pathological protein aggregation in the brain. Alzheimer’s disease displays amyloid-β and tau inclusions in the form of amyloid-β plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. Synucleinopathies comprise Parkinson’s disease and Dementia with Lewy bodies, which are classified by α-synuclein depositions in the form of Lewy bodies, as well as multiple system atrophy, which displays glial cytoplasmic α-synuclein inclusions. Tar DNA binding protein 43 (TDP-43) inclusions are observed in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal lobar dementia with TDP-43 inclusions. A separate subgroup of frontotemporal lobar dementias, including Pick’s disease, progressive supranuclear palsy and corticobasal degeneration, are characterized by disease-specific patterns of tau pathology and are termed primary tauopathies. Despite these classifications, it is not often appreciated that neurodegenerative diseases commonly display amyloid-β, tau, α-synuclein, and/or TDP-43 co-pathologies not typically associated with that specific disease’s pathophysiology. Additionally, in vitro and in vivo proteinopathy models show interactions between pathological forms of these proteins that increase protein aggregation and neurotoxicity, suggesting distinct mechanisms underlying co-pathologies that play a significant role in neurodegeneration. In this review, we describe the frequency of protein co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases and preclinical work demonstrating pathological protein synergies that exacerbate protein aggregation and toxicity. We also discuss granulovacuolar degeneration bodies, proteolytically active lysosomal structures that are induced by either pathological tau or α-synuclein accumulation, as an example of a shared cellular response to, and link between, distinct protein pathologies. Finally, we highlight interventional clinical trials which target multiple pathologies and/or specifically target co-pathologies in neurodegenerative diseases, noting that current preclinical and clinical research is limited and this line of investigation should be pursued more vigorously. In all, we find that protein co-pathologies are frequently observed in the brains of common neurodegenerative diseases and serve as important future therapeutic targets for combatting neurodegeneration across clinically distinct diseases.Graphical abstract Keywords: Co-pathology, Amyloid, Tau, Synuclein, TDP-43, Granulovacuolar degeneration bodies, Alzheimer’s disease, Lewy body disease, Tauopathy, Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Background

Many common and rare neurodegenerative diseases are diagnosed pathologically by the presence or absence of four proteins that accumulate in the brain with aging and neurodegenerative disease: amyloid-beta (amyloid-β), tau, alpha-synuclein (α-synuclein) and tar DNA binding protein 43 (TDP-43) [1, 2]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is pathologically defined by the presence of extracellular amyloid-β-positive plaques and intraneuronal tau neurofibrillary tangles. Synucleinopathies are pathologically characterized by α-synuclein inclusions, and can be separated into two categories: Lewy body diseases (LBD), comprising Parkinson’s disease (PD), Parkinson’s disease with dementia (PDD) and Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), which all display α-synuclein pathology in the form of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites, and multiple system atrophy (MSA), which displays glial α-synuclein inclusions. Neuronal TDP-43 inclusions are characteristic of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal lobar dementia with TDP-43 inclusions (FTLD-TDP). Neuronal and glial tau inclusions of 3R or 4R tau isoforms define the primary tauopathies Pick’s disease, progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and corticobasal degeneration (CBD). While these four protein pathologies are the most abundant, other protein pathologies have been observed in common neurodegenerative diseases. For example, Hirano bodies are eosinophilic inclusions containing actin and actin-associated proteins, and they are commonly reported in AD [3–5]. Further, rare neurodegenerative diseases, such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob, are associated with prion protein accumulation [6].

Growing evidence suggests that, despite these pathological definitions of neurodegenerative diseases, additional non-canonical protein pathologies, or co-pathologies, are also commonly found in neurodegenerative diseases as well as in the general aging population [2, 7–10]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that pathological forms of these proteins can interact with and “cross-seed” each other, meaning they can recruit and promote soluble protein into further aggregation, leading to exacerbation of neurodegeneration [7, 11]. Additionally, different protein pathologies can induce similar cellular responses. For example, granulovacuolar degeneration bodies (GVBs) are proteolytically active lysosomal structures that accumulate in neurons in response to early levels of pathological tau or α-synuclein accumulation [12]. Further, limited evidence suggests that targeting one protein pathology can reduce accumulation of protein co-pathologies, and targeting multiple pathologies simultaneously can have a compounding therapeutic effect [13–17]. While only a few clinical trials have targeted specifically co-pathologies or multiple pathologies simultaneously, early results suggest promise in addressing multiple protein pathologies in people with neurodegenerative diseases. In this review, we summarize previous findings of protein co-pathologies in common and rare neurodegenerative diseases to determine the extent to which different co-pathologies are overrepresented in different neurodegenerative diseases. We also discuss preclinical evidence investigating potential cumulative and synergistic effects of protein co-pathologies, as well as the therapeutic potential of targeting co-pathologies. We describe GVBs as an example of shared mechanisms resulting from distinct protein pathologies that could be targeted to counteract multiple protein pathologies simultaneously. Finally, we summarize current clinical trials targeting co-pathologies and/or multiple pathologies simultaneously across distinct neurodegenerative diseases. We find that co-pathologies are overrepresented in AD and DLB/neocortical LBD relative to similarly aged individuals without neurodegenerative disease or dementia diagnoses, and increased efforts should be considered to investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting protein co-pathologies in these common neurodegenerative diseases.

Amyloid-β

AD is clinically defined by the gradual onset and worsening of dementia and cognitive deficits over months to years, specifically deficits in learning and recall of recently learned information as well as language and visuospatial deficits [18]. In the brains of those living with AD, extracellular amyloid-β peptides, which result from abnormal processing of amyloid precursor protein, accumulate and form amyloid plaques, and these plaques are strongly associated with neuronal loss and cognitive decline in AD [19]. Amyloid-β plaque pathology distribution in AD is classified as proposed by Thal and colleagues [20]. In Thal phase 1 (lowest), amyloid-β pathology is limited to the neocortex, with phase 2 including the allocortex, followed by involvement of the diencephalon, striatum and basal forebrain in phase 3, the brainstem in phase 4, and finally the cerebellum in phase 5 [20]. For scoring of AD neuropathologic changes, according to National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association guidelines, Thal phases can be condensed into scores of 0 (none), 1 (Thal phase 1 or 2), 2 (phase 3) and 3 (phase 4 or 5) [21, 22]. A score of 3 (Thal phase 4 or 5), along with high tau pathology score (described in the following section), is required to be considered as having a high AD neuropathologic change [21, 22]. Higher AD neuropathologic change is correlated with worsening AD symptoms, but a high or even intermediate AD neuropathologic change is not a prerequisite for manifestation of clinical AD symptoms [10, 18, 22].

To identify studies of amyloid-β co-pathologies in neurodegenerative diseases, we performed a literature search on PubMed using the following terms (searched in the Title/Abstract only): “amyloid beta” AND (co-pathology OR Parkinson OR “dementia with Lewy bodies” OR multiple system atrophy OR “Pick’s disease” OR “progressive supranuclear palsy” OR “corticobasal degeneration” OR “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis” OR “frontotemporal lobar dementia”) NOT Review [Publication Type]. Studies not included in the original literature search were added as they were discovered, and comparable search strategies were employed for the other protein co-pathologies reviewed.

In addition to AD, other neurodegenerative diseases not typically associated with amyloid-β plaques have been found to exhibit amyloid-β as a co-pathology. In LBD, amyloid-β co-pathology is observed in 50%–90% of cases, with higher rates observed in cases with Lewy pathology in the neocortex (Fig. 1) [10, 23–25]. When sorted by clinical diagnosis, amyloid-β co-pathology is more commonly observed and of higher pathological load in DLB cases compared to PDD cases, and more common and of higher pathological load in PDD cases compared to PD cases without dementia [23, 24]. Amyloid-β co-pathology is associated with increased cortical Lewy pathology, more rapid cognitive decline and earlier mortality in LBD [23–27]. In aged individuals without dementia or without neurodegenerative disease diagnoses, amyloid-β pathology is observed in about 50%–60% of cases, suggesting elevated prevalence of amyloid-β co-pathology specifically in DLB cases and/or LBD cases with neocortical Lewy pathology [10, 28, 29]. In addition to LBD, amyloid-β co-pathology is observed in about 20% of Pick’s disease cases, 60% of PSP cases and 40% of CBD cases, as well as in 30%–40% of ALS and FTLD-TDP cases and 30%–50% of MSA cases [10, 30–32]. The observation of amyloid-β co-pathology in 60% of PSP cases is higher than might be expected, considering the classification of PSP as a primary tauopathy. However, 50%–60% of similarly aged individuals without neurodegenerative disease also display amyloid-β pathology, and the frequency of amyloid-β and other co-pathologies in PSP is positively correlated with age, suggesting aging is the primary driver of amyloid-β co-pathology in PSP [10, 30]. Nevertheless, the limited number of studies and inconsistent criteria for aged, healthy control individuals prevent conclusions regarding a potential increased prevalence of amyloid-β co-pathology in PSP compared to other primary tauopathies and similarly aged individuals. One study found amyloid-β co-pathology is associated with the primary tauopathy argyrophilic grain disease (AGD) but not Pick’s disease, PSP and CBD [33]. A separate study found that limbic amyloid-β co-pathology is associated with cognitive impairment in MSA [32].

Summary of characteristic pathologies and overrepresented co-pathologies in AD, LBD, ALS, FTLD-TDP, Pick’s disease, PSP and CBD. In AD, Lewy and TDP-43 co-pathologies are observed in roughly 50% of cases, which is greater than the rates of Lewy and TDP-43 pathologies in similarly aged individuals without dementia or without neurodegenerative disease diagnoses [8, 10, 29, 34–46]. In LBD, amyloid-β, tau and TDP-43 co-pathologies are more common specifically in clinically diagnosed DLB cases and/or LBD cases with neocortical Lewy pathology (amyloid-β: 80%–90%, tau: 50%–90%, TDP-43: 40%–50% of cases) relative to LBD cases without neocortical Lewy pathology and similarly aged individuals without dementia or other neurodegenerative diseases [7, 10, 23–25, 28, 29, 37, 39, 41, 44–54]. ALS, FTLD-TDP, Pick’s disease, PSP, CBD and MSA cases do not reliably display higher rates of co-pathologies relative to similarly aged control cases, although studies are limited and more work is needed to rule out subtle differences in co-pathology rates in these neurodegenerative diseases [10, 30–32, 55–58]

In animal models, the role of amyloid-β in mediating severity of α-synuclein pathology has been well studied (reviewed in [7, 11]). Amyloid-β interacts with and “cross-seeds” α-synuclein, facilitating α-synuclein phosphorylation, cleavage, aggregation, accumulation and associated neurotoxicity [59–69]. To investigate the therapeutic effect of targeting pathological amyloid-β accumulation in a model of amyloid-β/α-synuclein co-pathology, one study investigated the impact of immunizing amyloid precursor protein/α-synuclein transgenic mice with peptides specific to amyloid-β or α-synuclein, either separately or in combination. Both peptides were effective in ameliorating pathological amyloid-β, α-synuclein and tau accumulation, neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation and behavioral deficits, and the combined treatment with both peptides exhibited additive effects for some measurements of neuroinflammation and behavior [13]. However, the combined treatment did not have an additive effect for pathological α-synuclein accumulation [13]. In addition to α-synuclein, amyloid-β also interacts with and cross-seeds TDP-43, increasing pathological TDP-43 phosphorylation, cleavage and accumulation [14, 70]. A separate study found that increased clearance of amyloid-β by parkin decreases TDP-43 cleavage and accumulation independent of tau in an AD mouse model [14]. Interestingly, microglial activation is correlated with amyloid-β co-pathology in DLB, and transplantation of amyloid-β oligomer-activated microglia into the striatum of naïve mice induces pathological tau and α-synuclein accumulation, suggesting a mechanistic role of microglia in the appearance of amyloid-β, tau and α-synuclein co-pathology [71, 72].

While in vitro evidence of amyloid-β interaction and cross-seeding with other pathological proteins is well established, more work is needed to elucidate the therapeutic potential of targeting amyloid-β pathology in synucleinopathies, primary tauopathies and TDP-43 proteinopathies. In particular, further study using in vivo models of co-pathology are needed to test the role of amyloid-β in exacerbating other pathologies and neurodegeneration as well as the alleviating effect of inhibiting pathological amyloid-β in this context. Nevertheless, the postmortem evidence suggests amyloid-β co-pathology is more frequently observed in neocortical LBD than in other non-AD neurodegenerative diseases and similarly aged individuals without cognitive impairment, which is further substantiated by animal models demonstrating exacerbation of pathological α-synuclein accumulation by amyloid-β.

Tau

In addition to amyloid-β, intracellular tau pathology in the form of neurofibrillary tangles and dystrophic neurites is a core AD neuropathological feature, further highlighting that interplay between multiple pathologies is typical of neurodegenerative diseases. Tau pathology is also found in neurons and glia in primary tauopathies like Pick’s disease, PSP and CBD [73]. Primary tauopathies have patterns of tau pathology spread throughout the brain that are distinct to each disease, while the progression of AD tau pathology (in the form of neurofibrillary tangles) is classified into 6 stages as proposed by Braak and Braak [73–75]. In Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage I (lowest) and II, tau pathology is limited to the transentorhinal region, followed by the appearance of tau pathology in limbic regions including the hippocampus and amygdala in stages III and IV, with stages V and VI (highest) including the presence of tau pathology involving isocortical regions [74, 75]. For scoring of AD neuropathologic changes, according to National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association guidelines, Braak stages can be condensed into scores of 0 (none), 1 (Braak stage I or II), 2 (stage III or IV) and 3 (stage V or VI) [21].

Transentorhinal tau pathology (Braak stages I and II) is extremely common (present in greater than 90% of individuals) across neurodegenerative diseases as well as in similarly aged individuals without dementia [10, 28]. Therefore, for the purpose of this review across neurodegenerative diseases, moderate to severe tau pathology (Braak stages III-VI) is considered to represent significant levels of tau co-pathology. In LBD, the prevalence of tau co-pathology varies widely across clinical diagnoses. Moderate to severe tau pathology is observed in 50%–90% of DLB cases, 25%–50% of PDD cases, and 10%–15% of PD cases without dementia (Fig. 1) [23–25, 47–50]. Like in tauopathies, tau co-pathology in LBD shows a positive correlation with cognitive impairment, and the presence of both tau and α-synuclein pathologies correlates with faster progression to dementia than either pathology alone [23, 27, 35, 47, 49, 50, 76, 77]. Further, tau co-pathology in LBD is associated with shorter survival and shorter interval between onset of motor and cognitive symptoms [47]. The presence of neurofibrillary tangles in DLB is associated with reduced clinical diagnostic accuracy, and while tau pathology is associated with decreased likelihood of manifesting motor parkinsonism in DLB, substantia nigra tau pathology in the form of neurofibrillary tangles is independently associated with gait impairment in aged individuals [50, 78]. In human LBD brain tissue, neurons with neurofibrillary tangles also display α-synuclein accumulation, and tau oligomers co-localize with α-synuclein oligomers [79–81]. Intriguingly, a recent study suggests that tau pathology precedes Lewy pathology in patients with mild motor deficits, but not an official PD diagnosis, and tau pathology may drive dopaminergic neurodegeneration independently of α-synuclein [82].

In ALS and FTLD-TDP, moderate to severe tau pathology is observed in 16%–24% of cases, and MSA cases display moderate or severe tau pathology in about 10%–15% of cases [10, 32]. In aged individuals without dementia/cognitive impairment or without neurodegenerative disease diagnoses, the proportion of cases with moderate to severe tau pathology varies widely depending on the study, from less than 5% to up to 57% [10, 28, 29, 39, 83]. The high variability in rates of moderate-to-severe tau co-pathology in aged “healthy” individuals could be due to methodological differences, particularly regarding levels of cognitive impairment and age ranges used in these studies. More work is required to resolve the discrepancies in these studies and delineate the age and severity of cognitive decline at which the likelihood of moderate to severe tau pathology begins to substantially increase. Nevertheless, like amyloid-β, the evidence supports elevated prevalence of tau co-pathology in DLB cases and/or LBD cases with neocortical Lewy pathology [10].

Like amyloid-β, the role of tau in mediating severity of α-synuclein pathology has been well studied in animal models (reviewed in [7, 11]). Tau interacts with α-synuclein, promotes α-synuclein phosphorylation, fibrillization and aggregation, and enhances α-synuclein toxicity [84–90]. Importantly, in animal synucleinopathy models, multiple studies have shown that reducing tau oligomers or overall tau levels protects against neurodegeneration and behavioral deficits, while some but not all of these studies also report decreased α-synuclein pathology with tau reduction [16, 89, 91–94]. These findings are consistent with human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) consistently identifying the gene encoding tau, MAPT, as a risk locus associated with the development of PD [95–97]. In addition to α-synuclein, preclinical studies show that tau interacts with TDP-43 and exacerbates TDP-43 pathology and neurotoxicity, although work is needed to determine the therapeutic effect of tau ablation on accumulation of TDP-43 pathology [98–100]. Together, these findings implicate pathological tau accumulation in the etiology of synucleinopathies, while more limited work also links tau to TDP-43 proteinopathies. Similar to amyloid-β, development of in vivo animal models of tau co-pathology will be essential for determining the therapeutic potential of targeting pathological tau accumulation in synucleinopathies and TDP-43 proteinopathies.

α-Synuclein

Pathological depositions of α-synuclein in neurons (in the form of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites, together termed Lewy pathology) is characteristic of LBD, which include PD, PDD and DLB. Glial α-synuclein inclusions are characteristic of MSA (although Lewy pathology is observed in about 5% of MSA cases), and together with LBD, these neurodegenerative diseases are termed synucleinopathies [101]. Lewy pathology is commonly staged using the McKeith staging system, although a new, modified version of the McKeith system shows improved classification ability [102–104]. McKeith staging classifies Lewy pathology into the following categories: none, brainstem-predominant, limbic (transitional) and neocortical (diffuse), with newer stagings including olfactory-only and amygdala-predominant categories [102–104].

In addition to synucleinopathies, other neurodegenerative diseases commonly display α-synuclein co-pathology, particularly Lewy pathology. In AD, multiple studies, each investigating hundreds of AD cases, have found about 40%–60% of clinically or pathologically defined AD cases display Lewy co-pathology, covering amygdala-predominant, brainstem-predominant, limbic and neocortical Lewy co-pathology stages (Fig. 1) [10, 34–36]. Importantly, Lewy pathology is less commonly observed in similarly aged individuals without dementia or without neurodegenerative disease diagnoses (5%–25%) [10, 29, 37–40]. Interestingly, high α-synuclein load in the amygdala is associated with an increased cortical amyloid-β load, and cortical tau load is increased in cases with amygdala-predominant α-synuclein pathology but decreased in cases with brainstem-predominant or neocortical α-synuclein pathology compared to α-synuclein-negative AD cases [36]. The presence of Lewy co-pathology is correlated with earlier onset of dementia, earlier age of death, increased dementia severity and increased AD neuropathologic change in AD cases [9, 35, 105–107].

Outside of AD, Lewy co-pathology is less prevalent. Lewy co-pathology is observed in about 5%–20% of Pick’s disease, PSP and CBD cases, although it should be noted that pathologically confirmed PD cases with concomitant PSP pathology are sometimes observed [10, 30, 31, 55–57]. Additionally, Lewy pathology did not correlate with PSP and CBD clinical symptoms [30]. Lewy co-pathology is observed in about 10%–15% of ALS and FTLD-TDP cases [10], which, along with relatively low rates of amyloid-β and tau inclusions, suggests co-pathologies are not overrepresented in TDP-43 proteinopathies (Fig. 1).

Many recent studies have investigated the role of α-synuclein in exacerbating AD-like pathology in animal models (reviewed in detail in [11]). Consistent with human studies observing a correlation between Lewy co-pathology and severity of AD clinicopathological characteristics, α-synuclein promotes tau fibrillization and pathological tau and amyloid-β accumulation in synucleinopathy and co-pathology animal models [16, 84, 108–115]. Further, some Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites are immunoreactive for pathological forms of tau (although this may reflect tau surrounding Lewy bodies rather than true co-localization) [79–81, 116, 117]. Tau pathology has been observed in a PD case with the pathogenic A53T SNCA mutation (the gene encoding α-synuclein) [118]. Excitingly, α-synuclein oligomers derived from PD brain induce a unique tau oligomer strain that increases toxicity but attenuates tau fibril formation in vitro and in vivo compared to PSP-derived tau oligomers, suggesting there exist disease-specific forms of pathological tau with different toxicity levels and aggregation patterns [119]. Additionally, α-synuclein monomers, oligomers and sonicated fibrils seed TDP-43 prion-like pathogenic C-terminal domain monomers [120, 121].

Limited studies have investigated the impact of α-synuclein-targeting strategies on AD-like co-pathologies and/or the impact of combined treatment strategies targeting α-synuclein along with amyloid-β or tau. One study investigated the impact of immunizing amyloid precursor protein/α-synuclein transgenic mice with peptides specific to amyloid-β or α-synuclein, either separately or in combination. Both peptides were effective in ameliorating pathological amyloid-β, α-synuclein and tau accumulation, neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation and behavioral deficits when administered individually. Interestingly, the combined treatment with both peptides exhibited additive effects for some measurements of pathological amyloid-β accumulation, neuroinflammation and behavior, suggesting increased therapeutic potential of targeting multiple proteins in neurodegenerative diseases where more than one protein pathology is present [13]. Another study found that reducing endogenous α-synuclein in an amyloid precursor protein transgenic mouse AD model prevented the degeneration of cholinergic neurons and ameliorated behavioral deficits [15]. A separate study found that the absence of endogenous α-synuclein reduces AD lysate-derived tau seed-induced pathological tau accumulation and spreading rate in mouse brain, suggesting therapeutic potential in reducing α-synuclein protein levels in tauopathies like AD [16]. In all, these findings suggest α-synuclein co-pathology is common and may play a key role in the pathogenesis of AD, while it is less common in primary tauopathies and TDP-43 proteinopathies. Future work may further investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting α-synuclein in AD animal models, particularly the effect of reducing α-synuclein-induced toxicity on tau/amyloid-β accumulation and neurodegeneration in vivo.

TDP-43

Cytoplasmic depositions of phosphorylated and truncated forms of the intranuclear protein TDP-43 are characteristic of ALS and FTLD-TDP, and mutations in TARDBP, the gene for TDP-43, are associated with these TDP-43 proteinopathies [8]. In FTLD-TDP, four different subtypes of TDP-43 pathology are observed, comprising different amounts and distributions of neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions, dystrophic neurites and intranuclear inclusions [122–126]. Different subtypes are associated with different frontotemporal dementia variants [122–126]. Importantly, ALS and FTLD-TDP have different staging of TDP-43 pathology. In ALS, stage 1 (lowest level) displays TDP-43 pathology in the motor cortex, brainstem and spinal cord, stage 2 involves TDP-43 pathology invading the prefrontal cortex, stage 3 includes the postcentral cortex and striatum, and stage 4 (highest level) includes the anteromedial temporal lobe [127]. In behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia, stage 1 (lowest level) displays TDP-43 pathology in the basal and anterior prefrontal cortex, stage 2 involves TDP-43 pathology invading the middle frontal gyrus and insular cortex, stage 3 includes motor and parietal cortices, and stage 4 (highest level) includes the occipital cortex [128].

In AD, multiple studies across hundreds of cases show TDP-43 inclusions are observed in about 40%–70% of cases, particularly AD cases of the limbic-predominant subtype (Fig. 1, reviewed in [8]) [8, 10, 37, 41–44]. TDP-43 co-pathology in AD follows a distribution pattern that is distinct from ALS and FTLD-TDP. In AD, stage 1 (lowest level) displays TDP-43 in the amygdala, stage 2 includes TDP-43 pathology in the entorhinal cortex and subiculum, stage 3 involves the dentate gyrus and occipitotemporal cortex, stage 4 includes insular and inferior temporal cortices, stage 5 reaches the substantia nigra, and stage 6 (highest level) includes the basal ganglia and middle frontal cortex [129]. Importantly, TDP-43 inclusions are associated with the development of AD-type dementia and increased severity of AD neuropathological changes [37, 41, 130, 131]. However, tau deposition occurs before TDP-43 deposition in AD, suggesting tau, rather than TDP-43, drives early clinical presentation in AD [129, 130].

TDP-43 inclusions are also observed in up to 50% of LBD cases, with higher rates observed in cases with limbic or neocortical Lewy pathology compared to cases with brainstem-predominant Lewy pathology (reviewed in [7, 54]) [7, 10, 41, 44, 46, 51–54]. In LBD, like AD, the amygdala and entorhinal cortex are the regions most frequently positive for TDP-43 [53]. Unlike AD, studies do not reliably report an association of TDP-43 co-pathology with cognitive impairment in LBD, with one study reporting TDP-43 pathology is associated with a reduced likelihood of receiving a clinical DLB diagnosis [25, 41, 44, 46, 132, 133]. However, TDP-43 co-pathology has been associated with increased severity of α-synuclein pathology [53]. Notably, TDP-43 pathology is less commonly observed in similarly aged individuals without neurological diagnoses compared to AD and neocortical LBD, although limbic age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE) is present in 20%–50% of individuals greater than 80 years old [10, 29, 37, 45, 46].

TDP-43 co-pathology is not common in MSA as it is observed in 5%–10% of cases [10, 32]. Like α-synuclein, limited work suggests TDP-43 pathology is less common in primary tauopathies, with studies finding TDP-43 co-pathology in 0% of Pick’s disease, 6%–16% of PSP and 24%–33% of CBD cases [10, 30, 31, 58]. Further, TDP-43 co-pathology did not correlate with PSP or CBD clinical symptoms [30]. While investigations of co-pathologies in primary tauopathies are more limited than the other neurodegenerative diseases reviewed, the bulk of evidence suggests that amyloid-β, α-synuclein, and TDP-43 inclusions are not overrepresented in primary tauopathies (Fig. 1), indicating pathological tau accumulation is the primary driver of clinical symptoms and neurodegeneration in these diseases.

In vitro and in vivo models have further linked TDP-43 to AD-like neuropathological changes. TDP-43 forms toxic amyloid oligomers and co-aggregates and cross-seeds with amyloid-β [70, 98, 134]. Interestingly, in AD animal models, overexpression and depletion of TDP-43 both decrease amyloid-β plaque burden while increasing neurodegeneration [135–137]. These findings are coupled with observations of increased prefibrillar oligomeric amyloid-β, lysosomal localization of amyloid precursor protein, and increased microglial phagocytosis of amyloid-β, suggesting a complex interplay between TDP-43 and amyloid-β aggregation and clearance [135–137]. FTLD-TDP does not typically display tau pathology (in contrast to primary tauopathies that fall under the umbrella term of FTLD-Tau), but in AD models, TDP-43 overexpression has been shown to exacerbate tau pathology, and AD brains display TDP-43 and tau co-localization, with distinct tau and TDP-43 filaments within the same neuron [135, 138–140]. On the other hand, TDP-43 has been shown to suppress tau expression, and TDP-43 protein levels are inversely correlated with tau protein levels in postmortem AD brains [141]. Some of these discrepancies could be resolved by the idea that TDP-43 co-pathology may reflect TDP-43 loss of function, which increases tau expression and promotes its aggregation in AD. Future studies might consider further investigating this possibility, as well as the impact of TDP-43 loss on α-synuclein expression and aggregation.

Research in synucleinopathy models has shown that TDP-43 co-aggregates with and binds to α-synuclein, creating hybrid fibrils, although fibrils of the prion-like pathogenic C-terminal domain of TDP-43 do not seed α-synuclein monomers [120, 121, 142]. Importantly, microglia can influence the interaction between TDP-43 and pathological accumulation of other proteins. TDP-43 is a strong regulator of microglial phagocytosis, and TDP-43 depletion in microglia promotes clearance of amyloid-β [137]. Relatedly, microglial activation is positively correlated with TDP-43 and tau pathology in AD, primary tauopathies and FTLD-TDP, and TDP-43 co-localizes with α-synuclein in glial cytoplasmic inclusions in MSA cases with TDP-43 pathology [143, 144].

Much work is needed to delineate the effect of targeting TDP-43, or combinatorial therapies targeting TDP-43 along with other protein pathologies, on ameliorating amyloid-β, tau, and/or α-synuclein pathology. One study found TDP-43 depletion may have a therapeutic effect in synucleinopathies, as knockout of tdp-1/TDP-43 in A53T α-synuclein transgenic worms ameliorates motor deficits, developmental delays and dopaminergic neuron loss [17]. In all, these findings suggest TDP-43 co-pathology is common in AD and advanced stage LBD specifically, and TDP-43 appears to play a complex role in mediating amyloid-β, tau, and/or α-synuclein pathological changes in AD and LBD which may be further investigated.

Summary of neuropathological and preclinical findings

Overall, the neuropathological findings reviewed suggest that, particularly in AD and LBD, co-pathology is the norm rather than the exception. AD itself could be considered a disease derived from co-pathology, as the presence of both amyloid-β and tau inclusions is required to be considered to have AD neuropathologic change. Further, Lewy pathology is commonly observed in AD, and amyloid-β plaques and tau inclusions are commonly observed in LBD, suggesting that AD and LBD lie on a neuropathological spectrum rather than arising as a result of discrete pathological processes. Consistent with this idea, amyloid-β and tau co-pathology is more associated with DLB than PDD, and more associated with PDD than PD without dementia, indicating DLB is closer on the “AD” side of the neuropathological spectrum than PD. This reconsideration of the neuropathological underpinnings of neurodegenerative diseases has important implications for their clinical diagnosis and treatment. For example, understanding biomarkers of AD co-pathology could help the diagnosis and stratification of DLB vs. PDD. This could be useful because the “one-year rule” used to separate diagnoses of DLB and PDD, where patients with DLB have dementia that precedes or occurs within one year after the onset of motor signs of parkinsonism, does not successfully distinguish the extent of Lewy and AD pathology in these patients postmortem [47]. It is also important to consider the role genetics plays in mediating co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases. Pathogenic mutations in leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), which causes autosomal dominant PD, are associated with higher rates of tau co-pathology (about 50% of LRRK2 PD patients with the G2019S mutation) [145]. Interestingly, the AD genetic risk factor apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 is associated with increased co-pathology in ALS and neocortical LBD [10]. These findings show genetics can alter the relative prevalence and levels of co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases.

Upon review of the preclinical literature, it is apparent that in vitro work has clearly established the cross-seeding effects of different pathological proteins. However, preclinical in vivo animal models of multiple pathologies are relatively sparse, especially for the purposes of investigating the therapeutic potential of targeting co-pathologies or multiple pathologies in neurodegenerative diseases. Excitingly, a recent study established a tau, amyloid-β, and α-synuclein mixed pathology mouse model, finding that all three pathologies together exacerbate neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration compared to any of the pathologies on their own [146]. Investigation of therapeutics in mixed pathology models like this one will be crucial for developing treatments that are translationally relevant to neurodegenerative diseases where co-pathologies are common.

Lastly, the majority of neurodegenerative disease co-pathology studies describe the presence of neuronal protein pathologies. This focus on neuronal pathologies is observed despite the fact that glial protein pathology is commonly found in primary tauopathies, MSA and subtypes of TDP-43 proteinopathies [8, 73, 147]. This may be due to neuronal tau, α-synuclein, and TDP-43 pathologies having more well-defined staging classifications in neurodegenerative diseases. Future postmortem work could quantify the extent of glial protein co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases. It is also still unknown if there are differences in pathological protein interactions and cross-seeding between neurons and glial cells. Preclinical work may further investigate the role of astrocytes, microglia and oligodendrocytes in mediating cross-seeding and spread of pathological proteins in neurodegenerative disease co-pathology models.

GVBs: a link between co-pathologies

GVBs are membrane-delineated structures with a dense granule that accumulate in neurons of patients with tauopathies, particularly AD, and synucleinopathies, including PD and DLB (reviewed in-depth in [12, 148]). The connection between pathological tau or α-synuclein and GVBs has been validated by in vitro and in vivo animal proteinopathy models that demonstrate GVBs are directly induced by pathological intracellular tau or α-synuclein accumulation [108, 149, 150]. GVBs are proteolytically active structures that are hypothesized to be lysosomal in nature, as the outer GVB membrane is positive for lysosomal membrane proteins lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) and lysosomal integral membrane protein 2 (LIMP2) and the dense inner core is positive for the lysosomal protease cathepsin D [149–153]. However, the identity and origin of GVBs is still unclear, as GVBs also contain proteins across the endosome, unfolded protein response, mitophagy, apoptosis and necrosome pathways, as well as multiple tau kinases [150–152, 154–166].

The presence of GVBs in a neuron is associated with early, but not late, levels of tau and α-synuclein inclusion accumulation and maturation (i.e., GVBs are typically found in neurons with “pre-neurofibrillary tangle” or “pre-Lewy body” levels of pathological tau or α-synuclein accumulation) [108, 149–151, 157, 158, 167]. This suggests that GVBs may either 1) degrade before protein inclusions reach maturity (in the form neurofibrillary tangles or Lewy bodies), 2) protect neurons from developing mature inclusions, and/or 3) lead to cell death prior to maturation. Consistent with an association between early intraneuronal tau protein pathology and GVB accumulation, progression of GVB pathology has been classified into 5 stages, as proposed by Thal and colleagues, that closely mirror progression of Braak neurofibrillary tangle stages [167]. In stage 1 (lowest), GVBs are found in CA1 and CA2 hippocampal subfields as well as the subiculum, followed by the appearance in the entorhinal cortex and CA4 in stage 2 [167]. In stage 3, GVBs are involved in the temporal neocortex, followed by the amygdala and/or hypothalamus in stage 4, with stage 5 (highest) characterized by GVBs in the cingulate cortex as well as occasional appearance in the frontal and parietal cortices, oral raphe and pedunculopontine tegmental nuclei [167]. GVB stage is correlated with Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage, overall tau pathology burden, neuronal loss and dementia across neurodegenerative disease and similarly aged control cases [12, 155, 161, 167]. Notably, in self-reported cognitively healthy centenarians, GVBs are present in all cases and correlated with both Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage and worsening cognitive performance [168, 169].

While animal models demonstrate that pathological α-synuclein accumulation directly induces GVB formation, work in human postmortem LBD brain tissue is less clear. In the GVB staging study, GVBs were not observed in the substantia nigra, including in PD cases, and GVB stage was not significantly different in sporadic PD cases compared to controls (with all cases displaying mild to moderate Braak neurofibrillary tangle stages) [167]. However, separate studies of GVBs in LBD have found GVBs in substantia nigra neurons with Lewy pathology, with some, but not all, studies reporting GVBs surrounding mature Lewy bodies [108, 149, 157]. Additional work is required to determine the extent to which α-synuclein pathology, rather than tau co-pathology, contributes to GVB formation in LBD. Further linking GVBs to intraneuronal protein pathology is the finding that GVBs are positive for antibodies detecting phosphorylated α-synuclein, phosphorylated tau and phosphorylated TDP-43, with the phosphorylated tau finding validated by microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT) knockout mice showing no phosphorylated tau labeling of GVBs in a synucleinopathy model [12, 108, 148, 149, 151, 167]. However, phosphorylation-independent antibodies for these proteins do not typically label GVBs, and fluorescent protein-labeled tau also does not localize to GVBs [12, 148, 150, 157, 170, 171]. This could be due to the proteolytic activity of GVBs causing accumulation of specific fragments of these pathological proteins or the non-specific binding of phospho-specific antibodies to the GVB dense core [12]. Nevertheless, GVBs are associated with AD-like tau pathology and Lewy pathology, suggesting shared downstream mechanisms resulting from pathological accumulation of tau and α-synuclein.

Intriguingly, animal models show that GVBs are induced by pathological intracellular tau or α-synuclein accumulation, but not extracellular amyloid-β accumulation, further suggesting a shared downstream mechanism by which tau and α-synuclein induce GVBs [108, 149, 150, 154]. As GVBs are lysosomal structures, the answer may be through an as-yet undiscovered lysosomal stress mechanism. Indeed, pathological tau and α-synuclein accumulate in lysosomes and on lysosomal membranes, induce lysosomal stress and membrane permeabilization, and are degraded by lysosomes [172–184]. Alternatively, a recent study showed that casein kinase 1δ (Ck1δ) activity is required for GVB formation, while additional work implicates necroptosis-related proteins receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and RIPK3 [185–187]. The possibility of tau and α-synuclein inducing GVB formation through Ck1δ is further supported by the fact that Ck1δ phosphorylates tau in vitro [188, 189]. Future work could determine the mechanisms of GVB formation and function, as targeting shared mechanisms like GVBs could impact the degenerative effects of tau and α-synuclein co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases.

Clinical trial development

The clinical observational and preclinical literature resoundingly points to a substantial combination of amyloid-β, tau, Lewy, and TDP-43 pathologies within any given neurodegenerative proteinopathy. Indeed, while the clinical definition of AD requires amyloid-β and tau inclusions, and neuropathological data supports co-pathology in LBD, a person clinically diagnosed with any proteinopathy will likely display co-pathology of two or more proteins [10]. Despite this knowledge, as well as the development of targeted therapeutics towards aggregated proteins, there remains a considerable lack of therapeutics which target co-pathology. This dearth stems partially from the difficulty of discovering monotherapy agents which target multiple proteins. Additionally, combination therapies are rarely pursued due to logistical and regulatory obstacles which severely increase cost and time to approval. However, there are promising treatments under development which target co-pathologies.

To identify therapeutics in development targeting more than one protein pathology, we performed searches on clinicaltrials.gov and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, using the following search terms: (tau AND “amyloid beta”) OR (“amyloid beta” AND “alpha synuclein”) OR (tau AND “alpha synuclein”) OR (“TDP-43” AND tau) OR (“TDP-43” AND “amyloid beta”) OR (“TDP-43″ AND “alpha synuclein”) OR (multitarget OR “multi-target” OR “dual-target” OR “bispecific” OR multi protein” OR “two proteins”) AND (Alzheimer OR Parkinson OR dementia OR neurodegenerative) AND (interventional study OR intervention) NOT (observational study OR observation). Additional studies which were not captured with this search were added as they were discovered. We identified thirteen distinct treatments that are currently in Phase I-IV of clinical trials and are targeting multiple relevant protein pathologies, all of which were registered on clinicaltrials.gov (Table 1). These treatments were or are under active investigation for AD, PD, PSP, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), FTLD, CBD, and post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI), and comprise a broad range of agents, including small molecules, a protein, a peptide, and immunotherapies. Therapeutics which target co-pathology could be a single agent or a combination therapy. Of these therapeutics, seven monotherapy treatments simultaneously targeted amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein, and six treatments targeted combinations of two pathological proteins, either A) both amyloid-β and tau, B) both amyloid-β and α-synuclein, or C) both tau and α-synuclein.

The seven treatments with the broadest applicability simultaneously targeted amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein through direct or indirect mechanisms: BMS-984923, buntanetap, davunetide, dipraglurant, LM11A-31, NPT088, and phenserine (Table 1). Each of these seven treatments are monotherapies – small molecules, a protein, and a peptide – and covered a diverse range of mechanisms of action. Allosteric modulators of mGluR5 (BMS-984923 and dipraglurant), protect synapses from aberrant signaling and are under investigation for AD and PD [190–197]. Translation inhibitors (enantiomers buntanetap and phenserine) increase binding between iron-response elements and mRNA targets to reduce production of protein targets, and are under investigation for MCI, AD, and PD [198–208]. Davunetide, a peptide derived from the glial growth factor activity-dependent neurotrophic protein (ADNP), stabilizes and accelerates microtubule reassembly and is under investigation for FTLD, CBD, PSP, and MCI [209–218]. LM11A-31, a ligand of the p75 neurotrophic receptor, blocks receptor activation and subsequent neurodegeneration-inducing apoptotic signals and is under investigation for AD and PSP [219–227]. NPT088, a fusion protein consisting of a general amyloid interaction motif (GAIM) and human IgG1-Fc, utilizes this GAIM motif to break apart aggregated protein targets and prevent novel aggregation events, and is under investigation for AD [228–233]. Overall, these therapies share strategies that indirectly target pathological proteins through receptor signaling, neuronal structures, and suppressed translation, aside from direct protein targeting with NPT088. Future treatments may expand on or refine these pathways to more completely ameliorate cytotoxic effects of co-pathologies. Success of these treatments across multiple neurodegenerative diseases underscore the relevance of these pathways to neuronal health throughout aging and may suggest potential mechanistic targets in other chronic diseases of aging, besides neurodegenerative disease.

Six therapies being tested in the clinic are dual-protein targeting (Table 1). The two treatments which target solely amyloid-β and tau are immunotherapy combination treatments. One combination is of an unlisted amyloid-β monoclonal antibody matched with either the active tau immunotherapy AADVac1 or another tau immunotherapy. The second combination is of two passive immunotherapies, lecanemab (amyloid-β) matched with etalanetug (tau). Both treatments are specifically under investigation for AD [234–243]. The small molecule which targets amyloid-β and α-synuclein is GV-971. GV-971 is a mixture of acidic oligosaccharides derived from brown algae which targets the gut-brain axis to influence target protein aggregation and is under investigation for AD and PSCI [244–248]. The three small molecule treatments which target tau and α-synuclein are all inhibitors of O-GlcNAcase (FNP-223, ASN-51, ceperognastat), which limits protein O-GlcNAcylation and subsequent target protein aggregation, and are under investigation for AD and PSP [249–255]. Overall, these therapies utilize direct and indirect strategies to target co-pathologies. Future treatments may expand beyond the current roster of combination immunotherapies to include targeting amyloid-β and α-synuclein, or tau and α-synuclein, or to target additional posttranslational modifications beyond O-GlcNAcylation.

Not all co-pathology treatments are currently under active investigation. Five treatments (davunetide, dipraglurant, NPT088, ASN51, and cerperognostat) do not have active trials, with reasons ranging from difficulties in recruiting participants or termination (dipraglurant and ASN-51), to halted development following failure to meet endpoints (davunetide, NPT088, cerperognastat) [256–258]. Besides operational roadblocks, some of these outcomes may indicate that mechanisms targeted by these compounds are not sufficient to influence disease progression, or that side effects of these treatments are too prominent. The treatments BMS-984923 and FNP-223, which are under active investigation, have shared mechanisms of action with these failed or halted treatments. However, BMS-984923 has been specifically formulated to avoid adverse side effects of dipraglurant, such as vertigo. FNP-223 remains the only O-GlcNAcase under investigation and is active but not recruiting at the time of this publication. Overall, the majority of identified treatments, both under active testing or previously investigated, do not directly interact with amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein. Instead, these treatments interacted with targets upstream or downstream of protein aggregation or signaling. This approach could potentially reduce adverse side effects and improve safety, a downside of currently approved AD-immunotherapies which can cause amyloid-imaging related abnormalities (ARIA) [259–262]. For both immunotherapy combination treatment trials, it will be important to identify if the addition of a tau immunotherapy modulates the incidence of ARIA in participants.

Successful development of co-pathology-targeting treatments is promising for the amelioration of neurodegenerative diseases which exist across a spectrum of overlapping protein pathologies. Encouragingly, multiple treatments were found to be safe and tolerable across multiple trials of differing designs and phases of development. However, few of the thirteen identified treatments have progressed to Phase 3. While the eight treatments under active investigation have shown positive initial data, only buntanetap and GV-971 have progressed to Phase 3. Strategies guiding the development of combination therapies, outlined here [263, 264], may be useful in guiding the next steps for some of the remaining six early Phase treatments to progress to Phase 3 and future development of co-pathology therapies. Suggestions for combination therapy development include leveraging promising compounds from Phase 1 and Phase 2 A studies, repurposing existing treatments and agents, or going beyond antibody-only approaches to diversify mechanistic targets or reduce adverse side effects. Notably, the two immunotherapy combinations could consider the inclusion of a third agent which targets neuroinflammation, frailty, DNA damage, or mitochondrial quality, to provide wider coverage of disease mechanisms beyond protein pathologies [265]. Of the two treatments, buntanetap and GV-971, which have reached Phase 3, both have rigorous track records of safety across multiple trials of differing design. Specifically, buntanetap has been tested in both AD and PD across seven clinical trials, with five completed and two currently active. These trials have demonstrated safety and tolerability in both AD and PD populations at doses up to 80 mg once daily, as well as promising exploratory biomarker data [266, 267, 270, 271]. Buntanetap is seeking to achieve these key points with an actively recruiting 36-month safety study in PD (NCT07284784). GV-971 is approved in the People’s Republic of China for the treatment of AD, was found to be safe and tolerable in Phase 2 and Phase 3 and had significant improvements to cognition in Phase 3 [268, 269]. A global Phase 3 trial of GV-971 which included biomarker endpoints was terminated due to difficulties in recruiting participants (NCT04520412). The potential for targeting co-pathologies across neurodegenerative diseases is promising but not yet proven. Early clinical data, particularly positive signals from interventions against amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein in AD and PD, support the biological and therapeutic relevance of directly targeting these proteinopathies, warranting accelerated, well-powered trials across disorders.

Conclusion

Amyloid-β, tau, Lewy, and TDP-43 pathologies do not exist in isolation within human clinical neurodegenerative disease. These pathologies overlap widely across primary and secondary tauopathies (AD, FTLD, Pick’s disease, PSP, CBD, and AGD), synucleinopathies (LBD including PD, PDD, DLB, and MSA), and TDP-43 proteinopathies (ALS and FTLD-TDP). In this review we have highlighted the clinical observational literature within each protein pathology and the diseases which display pathological protein burden. We describe preclinical evidence supporting synergistic effects of pathological proteins on development of co-pathologies, and we highlight GVBs as an example of a neuronal response that is shared across distinct proteinopathies. Single molecules or combination treatments which target multiple protein pathologies at once hold promise for disease-modifying therapeutic development. We identified clinical trials utilizing co-pathology-targeting treatments in phase I-IV which were previously or are currently under development. There is an urgent need to improve the pipeline and translate promising molecules from the preclinical stage, as well as to identify favorable combinations of molecules for clinical development. The spectrum of neurodegenerative disease requires a delicate approach to properly integrate complex biology. The current molecules in the preclinical and clinical pipeline are encouraging candidates for meeting the ultimate goal of improving the daily experience of over 50 million adults living globally with neurodegenerative disease.
