Comparative safety of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in advanced solid tumors: a real-world cohort study with competing risk analysis
Authors: Yu-Hsiang Shih, Shao-Jing Wang, Chun-Ting Fan, Ting-Fang Lu, Yen-Fu Chen, Lou Sun, Shih-Tien Hsu, Jenn-Jhy Tseng, Chien-Hsing Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReal-world evidence comparing long-term safety between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors is limited, and the impact of competing mortality is unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX platform (2014–2024). Adults with advanced solid tumors initiating a PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor within 3 months of diagnosis were included. A 1:1 propensity score match created balanced cohorts. The primary outcome was the 1-year incidence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Introduction

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed the treatment of oncology over the past decade. By enhancing the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate malignant cells, ICIs have achieved durable clinical responses across multiple malignancies [1]. Among them, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors have emerged as cornerstones of immuno-oncology. Since their approval in 2014, these agents have conferred survival benefits in a wide range of cancers, including melanoma, non–small cell lung cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, breast cancer, endometrial cancer, and cervical cancer [1, 2].

Despite their clinical efficacy, ICIs are associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs) that can affect virtually any organ system [3]. Endocrinopathies such as thyroiditis, hypothyroidism, and adrenal insufficiency are common and often irreversible, while other irAEs including pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, nephritis, and myocarditis may be severe or life-threatening [3, 4]. Understanding the safety profiles of ICIs is therefore essential for optimizing treatment selection and long-term patient management.

PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors differ in their molecular targets and mechanisms of action. PD-1 inhibitors directly block the PD-1 receptor on activated T cells, whereas PD-L1 inhibitors bind to PD-L1 expressed on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells, while preserving PD-L2 signaling [5, 6]. These mechanistic distinctions have led to hypotheses that PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors may differ in their immunologic effects, clinical efficacy, and toxicity profiles. However, evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses comparing their safety has been inconsistent, and direct head-to-head comparisons are scarce. Moreover, trial populations may not fully reflect the complexity of patients treated in routine clinical practice, particularly those with advanced disease and substantial competing mortality.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large-scale, real-world retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX research network from 2014 to 2024. By focusing on patients with solid tumors treated with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy and excluding concurrent exposure to other immunotherapies (e.g., CTLA-4 inhibitors) or tyrosine kinase inhibitors, we sought to provide a comprehensive comparative assessment of the safety profiles of PD-1 versus PD-L1 inhibitors in routine oncology practice. Given the advanced disease stage of many patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, overall mortality represents a substantial and clinically relevant competing event that may influence the observed incidence of immune-related adverse events in real-world settings.

Methods

Data source and study period

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated health research platform that aggregates de-identified electronic health records (EHRs) from multiple healthcare organizations. The network encompasses data from more than 250 million patients across hospitals, primary care providers, and specialty clinics, primarily in the United States but also including international sites. Available data elements include demographics, diagnoses, procedures, laboratory results, and prescribed medications, with natural language processing applied to extract additional information from unstructured clinical documents. All data are rigorously standardized and de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and other applicable privacy regulations [7]. The study period extended from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2024.

Ethical approval and data protection

This study was conducted in full compliance with ethical and data protection standards. As only de-identified, anonymized data were used, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study adhered to relevant regulations, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and the General Data Protection Regulation. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB No. CE25873A).

Study population

Eligible patients were adults aged 21 to 90 years with a diagnosis of head and neck carcinoma, lung cancer, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, renal carcinoma, bladder carcinoma, endometrial cancer, cervical cancer, or melanoma. Two cohorts were defined according to the type of immune checkpoint inhibitor administered.

Cohort A comprised patients who initiated a PD-L1 inhibitor (atezolizumab, avelumab, or durvalumab) within three months of their cancer diagnosis and received treatment for a minimum of three months. Patients were excluded if they had prior exposure—defined as any time from five years before to one year after diagnosis—to any of the following agents: PD-1 inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab, toripalimab, tislelizumab), the CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab, or a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (lenvatinib, sorafenib, sunitinib, axitinib, cabozantinib, regorafenib, vandetanib, tivozanib). Additional exclusion criteria included a pre-existing diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus, thyroiditis, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, pituitary gland disorders, systemic lupus erythematosus, or rheumatoid arthritis prior to the index cancer diagnosis.

Cohort B included patients who initiated a PD-1 inhibitor (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab, toripalimab, or tislelizumab) within three months of their cancer diagnosis and received treatment for a minimum of three months. Exclusion criteria mirrored those for Cohort A, with the added specific exclusion of any prior exposure—during the same period (five years before to one year after diagnosis)—to PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, avelumab, or durvalumab), in addition to CTLA-4 inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors as listed above. Pre-existing autoimmune and endocrine disorders were also excluded identically.

Outcomes

Patients were followed for up to one year from the index date, defined as the date of the first administration of a PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor. The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which were analyzed in two categories: endocrine irAEs (including thyroiditis, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and hypopituitarism) and other irAEs (including colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, myocarditis, nephritis, and skin rash).

Given the advanced disease stage of the study population, all-cause mortality was considered a clinically relevant competing event that could preclude the occurrence or detection of irAEs. Accordingly, composite endpoints consisting of each irAE and death were evaluated in competing risk analyses to more accurately estimate the cumulative incidence of irAEs in the presence of substantial mortality risk.

Propensity score matching

To reduce potential confounding, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed between the PD-1 and PD-L1 cohorts. Matching covariates included demographic variables (age at the index date, sex, and race [White, Black or African American, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, other race, and unknown race]) and baseline comorbidities, including disorders of lipoprotein metabolism and other lipidemias, essential hypertension, cerebrovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and overweight or obesity.

All baseline characteristics, including cancer-related diagnoses, treatments, medication use, and lifestyle factors, were reassessed after matching. Chemotherapy exposure was not included as a covariate in the propensity score matching. This decision was based on the consideration that chemotherapy use is strongly driven by tumor type, disease stage, and treatment strategy, and frequently occurs concurrently with or subsequent to immune checkpoint inhibitor initiation. Including chemotherapy in the matching model could therefore introduce over-adjustment or collider bias by conditioning on post-baseline treatment decisions rather than true baseline patient characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, as appropriate. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with checkpoint inhibitor class (PD-1 vs. PD-L1) as the dependent variable, incorporating demographic and clinical covariates. Patients were matched 1:1 using a nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance.

Following matching, time-to-event analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between checkpoint inhibitor class and the risk of irAEs. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed for each irAE outcome, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were then used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for irAE risk, treating death as a censoring event.

Given the high background mortality in this advanced cancer population, competing risk analyses were additionally performed to account for death as a competing event. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated for each irAE, and subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) with 95% CIs were calculated using Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazard models, comparing PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitor users. All-cause mortality was also analyzed as an outcome using both Cox and competing risk frameworks to contextualize the magnitude of competing risk. Sex- and age-stratified competing risk analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of findings across key demographic subgroups.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Before propensity score matching, substantial imbalances were observed between patients treated with PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, particularly with respect to cancer type distribution and several clinical characteristics (Table 1). Patients receiving PD-L1 inhibitors were older on average and had markedly higher proportions of lung cancer and liver cancer, whereas patients receiving PD-1 inhibitors more frequently had head and neck, esophageal, gastric, breast, cervical, and endometrial cancers. Correspondingly, several baseline variables demonstrated large standardized mean differences (SMDs), including lung cancer (SMD = 0.496), liver cancer (SMD = 0.579), esophageal cancer (SMD = 0.367), stomach cancer (SMD = 0.341), head and neck cancer (SMD = 0.252), and nicotine dependence (SMD = 0.292), indicating substantial baseline imbalance prior to matching.

Baseline characteristics of study population

After 1:1 propensity score matching, a total of 22,672 patients were included in the matched cohort, with 11,336 patients in each treatment group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitor cohorts following matching, with all standardized mean differences below 0.1. The mean age was similar between groups (PD-L1: 66.5 ± 10.2 years; PD-1: 66.6 ± 10.8 years; SMD = 0.007), and the distributions of sex and race were comparable.

Importantly, the marked imbalances in cancer type observed before matching were effectively eliminated. The proportions of lung cancer, liver cancer, head and neck cancer, esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, and endometrial cancer were similar between the two groups after matching, with SMDs generally below 0.05. Likewise, baseline comorbidities—including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, obesity, and cerebrovascular disease—as well as prior radiation therapy, medication use, and lifestyle factors were evenly distributed between cohorts in the matched population. These findings indicate that the propensity score matching procedure achieved adequate balance and yielded a well-matched cohort suitable for subsequent comparative safety analyses.

Comparative safety of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors

In the propensity score-matched cohort of 22,672 patients (11,336 per group), the primary analysis using standard Cox proportional hazards models revealed a distinct safety profile for PD-L1 inhibitors compared to PD-1 inhibitors (Supplementary Table 2). PD-L1 inhibitor use was associated with a statistically significant lower risk of skin rash (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.56–0.78), colitis (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.71–0.87), and nephritis (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.94). No statistically significant differences were observed for endocrine irAEs (e.g., hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency), pneumonitis, hepatitis, or myocarditis.

Dominance of mortality as a competing risk

Given the advanced cancer stage of this real-world population, we performed a competing risk analysis accounting for death as a competing event. The results demonstrated that mortality was the overwhelmingly dominant outcome within the first year. The number of patients who experienced either a specific irAE or death was orders of magnitude greater than the number experiencing the irAE alone (Table 2). For instance, while only 21 and 28 patients developed thyroiditis in the PD-L1 and PD-1 groups, respectively, the composite endpoint of thyroiditis or death included 3,726 and 4,033 patients. All-cause mortality event counts alone (PD-L1: 3,710; PD-1: 4,014) far exceeded those of any individual irAE.

In this competing risk framework using the Fine-Gray model, PD-L1 inhibitor use was associated with a uniformly and significantly lower subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for every irAE analyzed, with point estimates ranging from 0.85 to 0.88 (Table 2). Notably, outcomes that showed no significant difference in the standard Cox model (e.g., all endocrine irAEs, pneumonitis, hepatitis, myocarditis) now exhibited statistically significant risk reductions favoring PD-L1 inhibitors. The sHR for all-cause mortality was 0.88 (95% CI 0.84–0.92), consistent in magnitude with the sHRs for the irAE composite endpoints.

Consistency of the competing risk pattern across subgroups

To explore whether this pattern was consistent across key patient subgroups, we conducted sex- and age-stratified competing risk analyses.

Sex-Specific Analysis: The pattern of uniformly lower sHRs for PD-L1 inhibitors was observed in both males and females, though the magnitude of association was generally more pronounced in males (Table 3). For instance, the sHR for the composite endpoint of pneumonitis or death was 0.89 (95% CI 0.84–0.94) in males and 0.95 (95% CI 0.89–1.02) in females. The sHR for all-cause mortality followed a similar trend (Males: 0.86, 95% CI 0.81–0.91; Females: 0.94, 95% CI 0.87–1.01). In contrast, the standard Cox model analysis (Supplementary Table 3) showed divergent and often non-significant sex-specific risks for individual irAEs (e.g., higher pneumonitis risk with PD-L1 inhibitors in females: HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.03–1.90).

Discussion

Our study contributes to this evolving narrative by applying a competing risk framework to these classic subgroup questions. Previous subgroup analyses have largely relied on standard regression or Cox proportional hazards models, which, as demonstrated in our primary analysis, can yield seemingly contradictory results across demographic strata (e.g., an elevated pneumonitis risk among females receiving PD-L1 inhibitors). However, our competing risk analyses indicate that once the dominant confounding effect of mortality is explicitly accounted for, much of the apparent demographic heterogeneity in immune-related adverse event (irAE) risk becomes substantially attenuated and converges toward a more coherent pattern. This observation suggests that a considerable proportion of the demographic variation observed in conventional analyses may be mediated by, or confounded with, differential survival outcomes across sex and age groups rather than reflecting intrinsic differences in immune-mediated toxicity susceptibility.

Importantly, this does not imply that standard Cox models are inappropriate per se, but rather that their underlying assumptions may be violated in populations characterized by high competing mortality. Accordingly, our findings underscore the necessity for future investigations of demographic disparities in ICI safety to rigorously account for death as a competing event in order to disentangle true toxicity risk from survival-driven effects related to treatment effectiveness or patient prognosis.

The comparative safety of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors remains an area of active investigation, with prior meta-analyses offering foundational yet sometimes inconsistent insights. Systematic reviews have characterized the overall spectrum of irAEs associated with these agents, with hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, pneumonitis, and colitis among the most commonly reported [8]. However, evidence directly comparing the toxicity profiles of the two drug classes has been mixed. Some analyses, such as the network meta-analysis by Huang et al., found no significant differences in treatment-related or immune-related adverse events between anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies across multiple tumor types [9]. In contrast, other meta-analyses, including that by Sonpavde et al., have suggested that anti-PD-L1 antibodies may be associated with a lower pooled risk of irAEs, with anti-PD-1 inhibitors linked to higher risks for specific events like rash and colitis [10]. Our large real-world cohort adds an important methodological dimension to this literature by demonstrating that the apparent comparative safety profile is highly sensitive to analytical framework and clinical context.

In standard time-to-event analyses using Cox proportional hazards models, we observed that PD-L1 inhibitors were associated with a significantly lower one-year risk of selected, clinically relevant irAEs, including skin rash, colitis, and nephritis. These findings align with prior reports suggesting differential toxicity patterns between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors and may offer practical guidance for managing common and high-burden toxicities in clinical practice.

However, the most consequential insight from our study emerged when mortality was formally incorporated as a competing risk. In this advanced cancer cohort, all-cause mortality overwhelmingly dominated the outcome landscape, with event counts far exceeding those of any individual irAE. When analyzed using Fine–Gray competing risk models, PD-L1 inhibitor use was associated with consistently lower subdistribution hazard ratios across all analyzed irAEs, including outcomes that showed no statistically significant differences in standard Cox models.

Although this pattern could superficially be interpreted as a broad safety advantage of PD-L1 inhibitors, such a uniform reduction across diverse immune-mediated toxicity pathways is unlikely to reflect a direct, class-wide protective pharmacological effect. Instead, this phenomenon is most plausibly explained by the lower observed rate of the competing event—death—in the PD-L1 inhibitor group within the first year of follow-up, as evidenced by the nearly identical magnitude of subdistribution hazard ratios for mortality and composite irAE-or-death endpoints.

This finding carries important methodological implications. It illustrates how standard survival analyses that censor at death may yield misleading inferences in populations with substantial competing mortality, implicitly assuming that patients who die are exchangeable with those who remain at risk for experiencing irAEs. The pronounced divergence between the heterogeneous results of standard Cox models and the internally coherent pattern observed under the competing risk framework underscores the limitations of conventional approaches in this setting. We therefore advocate for the routine integration of competing risk methods in real-world oncology safety research, particularly when evaluating low-incidence adverse events in populations with limited life expectancy.

The clinical interpretation of these findings must be contextualized within established efficacy paradigms. Differences in cancer type distribution between treatment groups—such as the higher prevalence of hepatocellular carcinoma and non-squamous non–small cell lung cancer in the PD-L1 inhibitor cohort—reflect real-world treatment guidelines in which PD-L1–based regimens are frontline standards [11–13]. Conversely, cancers where PD-1 inhibitors have a strong evidence base (e.g., head and neck, esophageal carcinoma, melanoma) were more common in the PD-1 inhibitor cohort [14–17]. This channeling by indication likely contributes substantially to the observed mortality signal favoring PD-L1 inhibitors. Consequently, the uniform risk reductions observed in competing risk analyses should be interpreted as a statistical reflection of aggregated efficacy and indication bias rather than definitive evidence of superior intrinsic safety.

From a clinical perspective, these findings shift the focus from isolated irAE comparisons toward an integrated evaluation of risk and benefit within the context of survival. The consistent competing risk patterns observed across sex and age subgroups—albeit with varying magnitudes—suggest that demographic differences in observed toxicity may largely reflect survival dynamics rather than true biological effect modification. In practice, treatment selection between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors will often be dictated by tumor-specific efficacy considerations, with toxicity management occurring within that predetermined therapeutic framework.

Sex-stratified safety analysis

Sex-related differences in ICI toxicity have been reported inconsistently across prior studies. Several observational analyses have suggested a higher incidence of irAEs among female patients. Unger et al., for example, reported a 66% higher risk of symptomatic irAEs in women compared with men, particularly involving hematologic, endocrine, gastrointestinal, and cardiac toxicities [18]. Similarly, another retrospective study found approximately a 1.5-fold increased irAE risk among females treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [19]. In contrast, pharmacovigilance analyses using FAERS and VigiBase reported no statistically significant sex differences in overall irAE incidence [20].

The heterogeneity of these findings may reflect differences in study design, outcome definitions, and analytical strategies, particularly with respect to competing mortality. In our study, standard Cox models suggested sex-specific associations for certain irAEs, including an apparently increased pneumonitis risk among females receiving PD-L1 inhibitors. However, once death was modeled as a competing event, the comparative safety profile between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors became largely consistent across sexes. While subdistribution hazard ratios were directionally lower in both males and females, statistical significance was primarily observed among males, with estimates in females frequently attenuated toward the null.

These findings suggest that sex may modify the observed incidence of irAEs through differential survival rather than acting as a strong biological effect modifier of comparative drug toxicity. This interpretation is supported by prior evidence demonstrating sex-based differences in ICI efficacy. A meta-analysis by Conforti et al. showed that survival benefits from ICI monotherapy were significantly greater in males than females across tumor types, a disparity not observed with chemotherapy.

.[21] In this context, the stronger competing risk signal observed in males—reflected by lower subdistribution hazard ratios for both mortality and irAEs—likely reflects greater treatment effectiveness and longer survival rather than heightened intrinsic toxicity. Competing risk models therefore provide a more accurate estimation of cumulative toxicity burden in sex-stratified analyses.

Age-stratified safety analysis

Advanced age has been associated with higher reported irAE incidence in prior pharmacovigilance and observational studies, suggesting increased susceptibility to immune-mediated toxicity among older adults (≥ 65 years) [22, 23]. However, these studies generally did not account for death as a competing event, a limitation that is particularly relevant in older populations with higher baseline mortality.

In our age-stratified competing risk analyses, the comparative safety pattern between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors remained directionally consistent across age groups, but the competing risk signal was notably stronger in older patients (65–90 years). Specifically, subdistribution hazard ratios for most endpoints were significantly below 1.0 in older patients, whereas in younger patients (21–64 years), confidence intervals frequently included 1.00, indicating less stable estimates.

These findings suggest that age primarily influences the observed incidence of irAEs through competing mortality mechanisms rather than modifying the relative toxicity between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors. Conventional Cox models may therefore overestimate age-related toxicity differences by failing to account for the higher probability of death prior to irAE occurrence in older patients. Accordingly, age-specific safety signals in real-world immunotherapy studies should be interpreted with caution unless competing risks are explicitly addressed.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although propensity score matching was carefully implemented to balance measured covariates, residual confounding by indication remains an important concern. In real-world oncology practice, treatment allocation between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors is strongly influenced by tumor type, line of therapy, performance status, metastatic burden, prior treatments, treatment intent, and clinician judgment—factors that are incompletely captured or unavailable within the TriNetX database. These same variables are also major determinants of both mortality risk and treatment tolerance. Consequently, any observed differences in mortality between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitor cohorts should not be interpreted as causal effects of the drugs themselves, but rather as potentially reflecting underlying differences in disease severity and patient fitness that could not be fully adjusted for. While matching reduced imbalance in measured baseline characteristics, unmeasured confounding related to prognosis and treatment selection cannot be eliminated and represents an inherent limitation of retrospective database studies. Second, the differential approval timelines of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors across the study period (2014–2024) warrant consideration. Cohort entry was defined by the actual date of first drug administration, thereby minimizing exposure misclassification; however, several agents were introduced later in the study window. Earlier calendar years therefore disproportionately represent first-generation agents and specific tumor indications, whereas later years include newer drugs with evolving indication profiles and potentially different patient characteristics. Changes in cancer management, supportive care, and clinical practice patterns over time may introduce calendar-time bias and affect both background mortality and the detection of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Although index-date–based cohort construction mitigates temporal misclassification, residual confounding related to evolving treatment landscapes cannot be fully excluded. Third, chemotherapy exposure was not included in the matching model. Chemotherapy use is largely determined by tumor-specific treatment strategies and may occur concurrently with or after immune checkpoint inhibitor initiation. Adjusting for chemotherapy could introduce over-adjustment or collider bias by conditioning on post-baseline treatment decisions rather than true baseline characteristics. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to treatment combinations and sequencing cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, this analysis was conducted using the TriNetX research network, which is based on routinely collected electronic health record data. While this approach enables large sample sizes and enhances real-world generalizability, it is subject to inherent data limitations. The identification of irAEs relied primarily on diagnostic coding, and the platform does not consistently provide linked laboratory values, medication administration details (such as corticosteroid treatment), or hospitalization data in a format that permits systematic multi-component adjudication. As a result, validated algorithms integrating diagnosis codes with laboratory abnormalities, prescription records, or inpatient encounters could not be applied, increasing the possibility of outcome misclassification. Fifth, adverse events may be underreported or misclassified in electronic health records, particularly for low-grade or transient irAEs, potentially leading to underestimation of true incidence. The severity or grading of irAEs could not be reliably determined, precluding analyses distinguishing mild from severe toxicities. Moreover, detailed information on treatment dosing, duration, sequencing, and adherence was not consistently available and may influence both toxicity risk and clinical outcomes. Sixth, although competing risk methods were applied to account for the high background mortality in this advanced cancer population, causes of death were not available. We were therefore unable to differentiate cancer-specific mortality from non-cancer deaths, which may be particularly relevant in older individuals. Importantly, the primary objective of this study was the comparative risk of immune-related adverse events rather than overall survival; mortality analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Finally, our findings primarily reflect an advanced, real-world population in whom mortality represents a dominant competing event. The influence of competing risks may be less pronounced in earlier-line treatment settings or in populations with longer expected survival. Additionally, the available follow-up duration may not fully capture very late-onset immune-related toxicities. Taken together, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the comparative safety profiles of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors reported in this study.

In summary, our findings indicate that the apparent comparative safety of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors in real-world advanced cancer populations is strongly influenced by competing mortality. When death is appropriately accounted for, demographic-specific toxicity signals observed in conventional Cox analyses are substantially attenuated, yielding a more coherent and consistent pattern across subgroups. These results suggest that observed differences in immune-related adverse events are more likely driven by differential survival and treatment context than by intrinsic class-wide toxicity differences, underscoring the importance of competing risk methodology for accurate interpretation of immunotherapy safety.
