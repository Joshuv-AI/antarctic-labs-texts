Psychometric evaluation of the UCLA-8 loneliness scale among structurally vulnerable older adults in rural India: implications for equity-oriented screening in primary health care
Authors: Suman Kanougiya, M Dhanush, R Dharani, S Baskarpandi, A Surendhar, B Vishali, R Swetha, V Oviya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoneliness is recognized by the World Health Organization as a major global public health concern, with well-documented consequences for mental and physical health. Yet it remains under-prioritized in public health programs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In rural India, intersecting vulnerabilities—caste, gender, and widowhood—exacerbate risks of social isolation and loneliness in later life. Despite commitments under the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE), validated screening tools are lacking in primary care.ObjectiveTo evaluate the psychometric properties of the 8-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-8) among community-dwelling older adults in rural Tamil Nadu, using Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory (IRT).MethodsA cross-sectional survey of 413 older adults (≥ 60 years) was conducted across five villages. CTT analyses assessed internal consistency, item–total correlations, and factorial structure via exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. IRT analyses applied a graded response model to estimate item discrimination, threshold parameters, and test information. Known-groups validity was examined by gender, age cohort, and living arrangement.ResultsThe UCLA-8 demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.74) and a two-factor structure distinguishing emotional from social loneliness. Emotionally salient items (e.g., “I feel alone”) showed high discrimination and strong factor loadings, whereas reverse-coded relational items (e.g., “I feel part of a group”) underperformed. IRT findings indicated optimal precision for detecting moderate-to-high loneliness but weaker sensitivity at lower levels. Loneliness scores were significantly higher among widowed women, Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe (SC/ST) elders, and those living alone.ConclusionsThe UCLA-8 shows robust psychometric validity and feasibility as a brief, equity-sensitive screening tool for rural Indian primary care. Cultural adaptation of weaker items and integration into NPHCE and Health and Wellness Centres could enable earlier detection and support for loneliness among structurally marginalized older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06637-7.Keywords: Psychometric validation; UCLA-8 loneliness scale; Social isolation; Older adults; Primary health care; India, South Asia

Introduction

Loneliness and social isolation are recognized by the World Health Organization as major global public health concerns [1]. Yet they remain insufficiently addressed in health policies and programs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Recent estimates suggest that about 16% of people worldwide—one in six—experience loneliness. Although most prevalent among adolescents and younger adults, loneliness affects people across the life course, with approximately 11.8% of older adults reporting loneliness [1]. Defined as the perceived gap between desired and actual social relationships, loneliness has been consistently linked to adverse outcomes including depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and premature mortality [2, 3]. With global population ageing accelerating, loneliness is now considered a critical social determinant of health in later life [4]. In India, the older adult population is projected to exceed 300 million by 2050, with a large proportion living in rural areas [5]. Within these settings, women, widows, and members of Scheduled Castes and Tribes (SC/ST) face heightened vulnerability to social isolation and loneliness. Widowhood, migration of adult children, disability, and economic insecurity further compound their risk of persistent loneliness and its associated mental and physical health consequences [5–7]. These vulnerabilities are exacerbated by limited community-based mental health infrastructure. Although the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE) highlights the importance of psychosocial risk assessment, standardized loneliness measurement has yet to be integrated into primary care [7].

Several theoretical perspectives provide complementary insights into the nature of loneliness in later life. Weiss’s typology distinguishes emotional loneliness, arising from the absence of close attachment figures, from social loneliness, stemming from insufficient integration into broader networks [8]. In collectivist rural Indian contexts marked by widowhood and caste-based marginalization, emotional loneliness may be particularly salient [9, 10]. Socioemotional selectivity theory adds a motivational dimension, suggesting that as people age, they increasingly prioritize emotionally meaningful relationships over broader social ties [11]. Consequently, the absence of a spouse or confidant may weigh more heavily than reduced participation in community life. Structural vulnerability frameworks further emphasize that loneliness is not merely an individual psychological state but a socially embedded outcome shaped by intersecting inequities of gender, caste, widowhood, and poverty [12]. Accurate measurement of loneliness across diverse cultural contexts requires psychometrically sound tools. The UCLA Loneliness Scale is one of the most widely used instruments for assessing loneliness across populations [13]. Its abbreviated 8-item version (UCLA-8) [14] retains strong internal consistency while offering practical brevity for use in surveys and clinical screening. However, it has not yet been validated among older adults in India. Cross-cultural studies suggest that linguistic framing, emotional expression, and social norms may influence how respondents interpret loneliness items [15, 16]. Instruments developed in Western contexts may therefore require adaptation to accurately capture the lived experiences of older adults in South Asian settings. Despite growing global interest, empirical evidence on loneliness measurement in India remains scarce—particularly from rural populations and among socially marginalized groups. Most validation studies of the UCLA Loneliness Scale have been conducted in high-income countries and rely exclusively on Classical Test Theory (CTT), which assesses internal consistency and dimensionality but assumes all items contribute equally to the underlying construct [17–19]. CTT provides sample-dependent reliability estimates and cannot assess item-level performance across the full spectrum of loneliness [20]. In contrast, Item Response Theory (IRT) offers item-level precision by modeling discrimination (how well an item differentiates between low and high loneliness) and threshold parameters (the point along the latent continuum where a respondent endorses higher response categories) [21]. To date, no published study has jointly applied both CTT and IRT to evaluate the UCLA-8 Loneliness Scale among older adults in India. This represents a critical evidence gap, as reliable and culturally valid screening tools are essential for integrating psychosocial assessments into primary health care and elder care programs such as the NPHCE. Addressing this gap, the present study provides the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the UCLA-8 among community-dwelling older adults in rural Tamil Nadu, using both CTT and IRT to examine its reliability, dimensionality, and item-level functioning. Understanding how loneliness is conceptualized is essential to interpreting its measurement and meaning among older adults. The following section outlines the theoretical perspectives that inform this study’s design and analysis, situating the UCLA-8 within broader psychological, social, and structural frameworks. Together, these perspectives guided both the selection of constructs and the interpretation of results within India’s rural ageing context.

Theoretical framework

This study is anchored in four intersecting theoretical perspectives—(i) Weiss’s typology of loneliness [22], (ii) socioemotional selectivity theory [23], (iii) the structural vulnerability framework [24], and (iv) psychometric test theory [25]—each offering complementary insights into how loneliness is experienced and measured among older adults in rural India (Fig. 1). Weiss’s model distinguishes emotional loneliness, arising from the absence of close attachment figures, and social loneliness, linked to inadequate social integration [8]. The UCLA Loneliness Scale, including its eight-item version (UCLA-8) [14], was designed to capture both dimensions through a balance of positively and negatively worded items. Socioemotional selectivity theory provides a motivational lens, suggesting that as individuals age, they increasingly invest in emotionally meaningful relationships rather than broader social interactions [11]. In rural India—particularly among widowed women—this prioritization can result in persistent emotional loneliness despite surface-level social contact. The structural vulnerability framework reframes loneliness as a socially embedded outcome shaped by gender, caste, and widowhood [12]. Older adults facing intersecting disadvantages often experience exclusion from caregiving networks, housing stability, and institutional supports, compounding their risk of chronic loneliness. Finally, psychometric test theory, drawing on Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory (IRT) [26, 27], provides analytic tools to evaluate how well the UCLA-8 measures these intertwined dimensions. CTT assesses internal consistency and item–total reliability, while IRT models item discrimination and thresholds along the latent continuum of loneliness. Together, these perspectives reinforce construct validity and ensure that the UCLA-8 sensitively detects moderate-to-severe loneliness within community-based, resource-limited settings.

Theoretical framework for the study. Figure 1 illustrates how the UCLA-8 Loneliness Scale is positioned at the intersection of four complementary perspectives. At the apex, Weiss’s emotional–social typology captures core dimensions of loneliness; the base integrates socioemotional selectivity theory (age-related motivational shifts) and structural vulnerability (gender, caste, widowhood), while psychometric test theory (CTT + IRT) provides the methodological foundation for measurement validity. Together, these frameworks link the lived experiences of loneliness among older adults in rural India to the psychometric evaluation of the UCLA-8.

Methods

Design, setting, and sample

This cross-sectional study was carried out between May and August 2024 in five rural villages of Chengalpattu district, Tamil Nadu, India. The sites were selected from a pool of 12 villages covered under the Government of India’s Unnat Bharat Abhiyan (UBA) initiative, with selection based on demographic diversity and willingness of local authorities to facilitate community access. Tamil Nadu is among the states with the fastest ageing trajectory in India: older adults constituted 13.6% of the population in 2021, and this proportion is projected to rise to 18.2% by 2031 [28]. Rural areas in particular have disproportionately high concentrations of older residents, making them important contexts for studying social and emotional wellbeing in later life. A multistage sampling strategy was employed. Within each village, a random starting point was identified, and field investigators followed a consecutive household approach. All eligible older adults (≥ 60 years) were invited to participate. Eligibility criteria were: (a) age ≥ 60 years, (b) residence in the study site for at least six months, and (c) ability to provide informed consent. In households with more than one eligible participant, one was randomly selected using a predefined procedure. Out of 512 respondents aged ≥ 55 years interviewed for the broader study, 413 older adults aged ≥ 60 years were retained for this psychometric analysis of the UCLA-8. The age cutoff of 60 years was chosen to align with United Nations and Government of India definitions of older adults. The final sample size (N = 413) exceeded minimum requirements for factor analytic models [29] and was well above the threshold estimated using Cochran’s formula with 50% prevalence, 5% precision, and 20% non-response allowance [30].

Data collection procedures

Data were collected through structured, face-to-face interviews in Tamil by a trained team of Master of Public Health (MPH) students. Interviews were conducted in semi-private household settings (e.g., verandas, side rooms) to minimize interruptions and ensure confidentiality. Responses were captured using KoboToolbox (https://www.kobotoolbox.org/) on password-protected tablets. Field investigators underwent intensive training in research ethics, rapport-building, and sensitive interviewing, with additional orientation on ageing, stigma, and mental health. Daily debriefing meetings and random back-checks were conducted to monitor data quality and protocol adherence. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. For individuals with limited literacy, trained field investigators read the consent form aloud in Tamil, ensured comprehension, and documented consent via thumb impression or oral consent in the presence of a witness. Interviews were conducted in private spaces, and participants were assured of confidentiality, the right to withdraw at any stage, and freedom to decline any question. To safeguard well-being, respondents who reported distress, abuse, or urgent psychosocial needs were provided with information on local helplines and referred to nearby primary health centres or community-based organizations where appropriate.

Measures

UCLA-8 loneliness scale

The primary measure was the 8-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-8) [14] which assesses subjective feelings of social disconnection and emotional isolation. Items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never”) to 4 (“Always”), producing a total score between 8 and 32. Higher scores indicate greater loneliness. Items 3 (“I feel part of a group of friends”) and 6 (“I am an outgoing person”) were reverse-coded so that higher values consistently represented greater loneliness. Higher scores indicate greater perceived loneliness. Items assess dimensions such as companionship, feeling left out, emotional withdrawal, and social disconnection [14].

Survey instrument and translation

The structured survey included modules on sociodemographic characteristics, health, psychological wellbeing, and social connectedness. The focal instrument for this analysis was the UCLA-8 Loneliness Scale, originally developed in the United States but widely applied in cross-cultural settings [36, 37]. To ensure appropriateness for use among older adults in rural Tamil Nadu, the scale underwent a rigorous process of cultural and linguistic adaptation. A double forward–backward translation procedure [38] was employed. The translated Tamil version was reviewed by a bilingual expert panel comprising public health researchers, psychologists, and community representatives to assess semantic equivalence and cultural resonance.

Following panel review, cognitive interviews were conducted with 12 older adults from non-study villages to evaluate item clarity, comprehension, and response consistency. Minor adjustments were introduced to simplify wording and ensure accessibility for participants with low literacy, while retaining the conceptual integrity of the original items. No items were dropped or restructured. The final Tamil version (See annexture 1) was then pilot-tested in a small sample (n = 10) to confirm feasibility and comprehension before field administration.

All supporting instruments used for convergent validity (PHQ-9, GAD-7, VASS-12, SWEMWBS, EQ-5D-5 L) were similarly administered in validated Tamil translations where available, or adapted following the same translation and review protocol [39].

Results

Sample characteristics

Supplementary Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of 413 older adults (aged ≥ 60 years), disaggregated by age group (young-old: 60–69 years; old-old: ≥70 years), gender, and living arrangement. The majority were young-old (80.9%), with a mean age of 66.3 years (SD = 4.4), compared to 82.2 years (SD = 5.6) for the old-old group (p < 0.001). Women comprised two-thirds of the sample (67.1%). The proportion of men was higher among the old-old (45.6%) compared to the young-old (29.9%; p < 0.05). Overall, 12.1% of respondents lived alone, predominantly women (88.0%). Marital status varied significantly: half of the old-old (50.6%) were not in a union, compared with 30.8% of the young-old (p < 0.05). Among women, 41.9% were not in a union, compared with only 19.9% of men (p < 0.001). Notably, 80% of those living alone were also not in a union. Caste distribution showed higher proportions of Scheduled Castes/Tribes (SC/ST) among women (37.9%) and those living alone (38.0%). While the young-old had more SC/ST representation than the old-old (37.4% vs. 24.1%), the old-old were predominantly Other Backward Classes/Most Backward Classes (OBC/MBC; 69.6%). Most participants identified as Hindu (84.0%), though non-Hindus were more common among the young-old (18.6%) and women (20.2%) compared with the old-old (8.9%) and men (9.6%) (p < 0.05 and p < 0.05, respectively). These distributions underscore how aging in rural India intersects with gender, caste, marital status, and social vulnerability—factors that shape experiences of loneliness.

Table 1 summarizes descriptive statistics and floor/ceiling effects for individual items. Mean scores ranged from 1.60 (“No one to turn to”) to 2.47 (“Feel part of group (R)”), with standard deviations between 0.94 and 1.15. Skewness and kurtosis values indicated that loneliness was positively skewed and clustered, with a minority endorsing high-frequency loneliness. Floor effects were most pronounced for Item 7 (“No one to turn to,” 65.4% “Never”), while ceiling effects (≥ 15%) appeared for Items 2 (“Feel left out,” 16.9%), 4 (“Feel isolated,” 15.5%), and 5 (“Feel lonely,” 15.3%). These patterns suggest that while many participants reported little loneliness, severe loneliness was concentrated among a subgroup. The total UCLA-8 score ranged from 8 to 32 (mean = 16.11, SD = 5.15). The distribution showed moderate positive skew (0.62) and kurtosis (2.82), indicating non-normality with clustering toward lower loneliness. Internal consistency

The UCLA-8 demonstrated good reliability. Cronbach’s α was 0.74, and McDonald’s ω was 0.75, indicating acceptable internal consistency for the eight items. The average inter-item covariance was 0.31, suggesting moderate item correlations without redundancy.

Sampling adequacy and factorability

Inspection of the correlation matrix showed positive, moderate associations among most items (e.g., r = 0.61 between “Feel lonely” and “No one to turn to”), supporting dimensional coherence. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.80, exceeding the recommended 0.70 threshold, with individual item KMOs ranging from 0.59 (“Outgoing, friendly (R)”) to 0.88 (“No one to turn to”). Bartlett’s test of sphericity was highly significant (χ²(28) = 920.3, p < 0.001), rejecting the null of an identity matrix and confirming that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The determinant of the correlation matrix (0.11) further supported factorability.

Figure 2 displays a heatmap of inter-item correlations for the UCLA-8. Stronger correlations (red cells) emerged among negatively phrased emotional items, particularly between “Feel isolated” and “Feel lonely” (r = 0.74). Weaker associations (blue/pale cells) were found between positively worded items such as “Outgoing, friendly (R)” and other items, suggesting potential multidimensionality or reduced cultural salience.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test indicated sampling adequacy (overall KMO = 0.80), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 920.3, df = 28, p < 0.001), supporting factorability of the inter-item correlations. Principal component factoring (PCF) suggested a two-factor solution based on eigenvalues > 1 (Factor 1 = 3.13, Factor 2 = 1.38), explaining 56.4% of the total variance. The scree plot (Fig. 3) also indicated a clear inflection after the second factor. In the unrotated solution, Factor 1 was defined by emotionally intense, negatively worded items (e.g., “feel lonely,” “feel isolated,” “no one to turn to”), while Factor 2 loaded more strongly on positively framed or relational items (e.g., “feel part of a group (R),” “outgoing, friendly (R)”). After varimax (orthogonal) rotation, items loneli4 (“feel isolated”), loneli5 (“feel lonely”), loneli7 (“no one to turn to”), and loneli8 (“feel alone”) loaded highly on Factor 1 (> 0.75), while loneli3 (“feel part of a group (R)”) and loneli6 (“outgoing, friendly (R)”) loaded highly on Factor 2 (> 0.73). Cross-loadings were observed for loneli1 (“lack companionship”) and loneli2 (“feel left out”), indicating overlap across the two dimensions. A promax (oblique) rotation yielded a similar two-factor pattern, with moderate correlation between factors (r = 0.31). This suggests that while a unidimensional model is statistically viable, there is empirical evidence of a two-factor structure separating negative/emotional loneliness from positive/relational connectedness (Table 2).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA): standardized loadings and model fit

The one-factor CFA model of the UCLA-8 demonstrated marginal fit (χ²(20) = 104.3, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.101, 90% CI: 0.082–0.121; CFI = 0.91; TLI = 0.87; SRMR = 0.073; CD = 0.88). Standardized loadings ranged from 0.11 to 0.88 ; Fig. 4). Items reflecting emotional isolation loaded strongly (“I feel lonely” = 0.88; “I feel isolated” = 0.82; “No one to turn to” = 0.69; “I feel alone” = 0.67), while reverse-coded or relational items loaded weakly (“Lack companionship” = 0.26; “Part of a group (R)” = 0.17; “Outgoing, friendly (R)” = 0.11). Modification indices indicated localized misfit for loneli3 and loneli6. An alternative two-factor CFA (Factor 1: Social Isolation; Factor 2: Relational Connectedness) provided clearer separation of emotional vs. relational items and showed a moderate correlation (r = 0.45; Fig. 4). Although fit indices remained modest, the two-factor structure was theoretically consistent with Weiss’s framework and EFA findings. Given sample size, bifactor or higher-order models could not be reliably tested. This indicate that the one-factor model supports UCLA-8 as a practical screening tool, while the two-factor model highlights conceptual nuance.

Convergent validity

The UCLA-8 demonstrated strong convergent validity through significant correlations with related constructs (Table S3; Fig. 7). Total UCLA-8 scores were positively associated with depression (PHQ-9; r = 0.60, p < 0.001), anxiety (GAD-7; r = 0.49, p < 0.001), and elder abuse vulnerability (VASS-12; r = 0.58, p < 0.001), consistent with the clustering of loneliness, common mental disorders, and psychosocial stressors in later life. Negative correlations were observed with psychological well-being (Warwick; r = − 0.42, p < 0.001) and health-related quality of life (EQ-5D; r = − 0.51, p < 0.001), aligning with theoretical expectations. Spearman’s rank-order correlations yielded similar patterns (ρ = 0.52 with PHQ-9; ρ = 0.46 with GAD-7; ρ = 0.53 with VASS-12; ρ = − 0.34 with Warwick; ρ = − 0.47 with EQ-5D), confirming robustness to potential non-normality. Together, these findings affirm that the UCLA-8 meaningfully converges with measures of mental health, social stress, and quality of life, supporting its construct validity in this rural South Indian context.

Discussion

Construct validity and dimensionality

The study findings indicate that emotionally charged items (e.g., “I feel alone,” “I feel left out”) aligned strongly with emotional loneliness, while relational items (e.g., “I feel part of a group of friends”) reflected social connectedness. Both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported a two-factor structure, consistent with Weiss’s framework of emotional versus social loneliness [8]. This is the first study in India to evaluate the UCLA-8 Loneliness Scale among rural older adults using both Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory (IRT). The dual-method approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of reliability, latent structure, and item functioning, identifying adaptations needed for culturally equitable geriatric screening. Emotional loneliness items showed the strongest loadings, while reverse-coded or relational items performed inconsistently—mirroring polarity effects and multidimensional clustering reported in global validations [41, 42]. These results resonate with socioemotional selectivity theory, which posits that older adults prioritize emotionally meaningful ties over broader networks [43]. Known-groups validity supported this interpretation. Widowed individuals, those living alone, and the “old-old” subgroup scored the highest. This pattern underscores that the loss of intimate bonds—rather than reduced social participation—is the main driver of loneliness in this setting [44].

IRT Findings: trait precision and item performance

IRT analysis revealed that the scale functions optimally around θ ≈ +1, supporting its use for detecting moderate-to-severe loneliness. Items such as loneli2 and loneli8 discriminated effectively, while loneli6 (“Outgoing, friendly”) contributed little information and showed irregular thresholds. These issues—also flagged in CTT—highlight the need for refinement or replacement via culturally grounded adaptation. Unlike CTT, IRT provided granular insights into which items best differentiate respondents across the loneliness continuum [45–47].

Local distinctions in a global context

Findings align with studies in conducted cross population such Polis, Brazilian, Chinese, Iranian population reporting polarity-driven or two-factor structures [2, 17, 48–51], yet our rural Tamil Nadu sample reflects distinct vulnerabilities. SC/ST caste groups, widows, and elders living alone reported loneliness rooted in systemic marginalization, not just emotional loss. This suggests that loneliness here should be conceptualized as a socially embedded outcome shaped by caste, gender, and economic exclusion [52].

Structural vulnerability and gendered loneliness

The highest loneliness scores were observed among widowed SC/ST women living alone, reflecting intersectional disadvantage [12, 52, 53]. Standardized loneliness scales risk overlooking culturally specific stressors. Incorporating local idioms of disconnection (e.g., intergenerational caregiving gaps, caste-based exclusion) into adaptations may enhance cultural validity [16, 53].

Policy and practice implications

Validated loneliness tools are urgently needed as India expands geriatric care through the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE) and Health and Wellness Centres. With refinement, the UCLA-8 could serve as a rapid screening tool for frontline providers (ASHAs, CHOs, ANMs). Embedding it into mHealth platforms, geriatric health cards, or outpatient screening could facilitate early detection and referral. However, participatory adaptation is essential—particularly for weaker items like loneli6—through methods such as cognitive interviewing, back-translation, and community co-design (Fig. 8).

Future directions

Future research should deepen qualitative exploration of loneliness, including cultural semantics of isolation and social discontinuity. Longitudinal work is needed to test whether UCLA-8 scores predict frailty, hospitalization, or depression. Developing IRT-based subscales for vulnerable subgroups (e.g., widows, SC/ST elders) may sharpen specificity. Finally, piloting in diverse South Asian contexts will clarify generalizability and inform culturally calibrated cut-offs for community and clinical use.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is the first to psychometrically validate the 8-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-8) among older adults in rural India using both Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Item Response Theory (IRT). This dual-method design enabled a rigorous assessment of reliability, factorial structure, and item-level functioning. Second, the sample included structurally vulnerable groups—widowed women, Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe (SC/ST) elders, and individuals living alone—enhancing the relevance of the findings for health equity and geriatric care in low-resource contexts. Third, the application of IRT provided granular insights into item precision and trait coverage, highlighting strengths of affectively charged items and limitations of reverse-coded items, thereby informing future adaptation. Nonetheless, limitations should be acknowledged.

The cross-sectional design limits conclusions on predictive validity (e.g., whether UCLA-8 scores anticipate health or psychosocial outcomes over time). Second, face-to-face administration may have introduced interviewer or social desirability bias for sensitive items. Third, while the tool was linguistically translated and pre-tested, cognitive interviewing and in-depth cultural validation were not conducted, which may affect subtle item interpretation. Finally, generalizability is limited to rural, Tamil-speaking older adults; replication in urban, multilingual, and higher-literacy populations is needed.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the UCLA-8 in rural South India using both CTT and IRT. The scale showed acceptable reliability, factorial validity, and strong discrimination for detecting moderate-to-severe loneliness, especially among structurally marginalized groups. Results suggest a partially multidimensional structure aligned with Weiss’s emotional and social loneliness framework, with emotional isolation items outperforming relational/reverse-coded ones. The UCLA-8 emerges as a brief, equity-sensitive tool suitable for integration into India’s NPHCE and Health and Wellness Centres. Refinement of weaker items, cultural adaptation, and future testing of predictive validity and digital applications could strengthen its scalability across South Asia.
