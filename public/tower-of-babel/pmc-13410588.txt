Effects of physical exercise on sleep quality in patients with psychiatric disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mingqi Wang, Lei Su, Lu Hao, Xiaojun Song, Jing Ma, Jing Guo, Bowen He, Mohan Hu, Runqing Wang, Yue Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs an evidence-based complementary modality, physical exercise showed significant potential in mitigating sleep disturbances across various populations.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to determine how physical exercise interventions influence the sleep quality among clinical populations diagnosed with psychiatric disorders.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across four electronic databases, MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) available from inception to March 1, 2026. Studies were included if they focused on exercise interventions in patients with psychiatric disorders, using sleep quality as the outcome. Assessment of risk of bias for the enrolled RCTs was conducted using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2. Data synthesis employed random-effects models to estimate Hedges’ g, with subsequent subgroup analyses conducted according to the FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, and Type) framework.ResultsA total of 21 RCTs (24 comparisons) with 1628 participants were analyzed and results indicated a moderate overall effect size, indicating that physical exercise contributes to a significant improvement in sleep quality (g = -0.57, 95% confidence intervals [CI]: -0.75, -0.38, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses revealed larger effect sizes for interventions characterized by a weekly frequency of ≥ 3 sessions (g = -0.60), aerobic modalities (g = -0.69), and low-to-moderate intensity (g = -0.70). Furthermore, Furthermore, larger effects were also observed for single sessions lasting ≥ 30 min (g = -0.58) and a total intervention duration under 12 weeks (g = -0.58) in patients with psychiatric disorders. Univariate meta-regression analyses revealed no significant linear moderating effects of intervention duration, frequency, volume, or total weekly exercise time on sleep quality (all P > 0.05).ConclusionOverall, physical exercise serves as a potent complementary treatment option for enhancing sleep quality among individuals with mental health conditions. Following the exploration of candidate FITT dimensions, our findings offer actionable clinical insights for incorporating tailored exercise programs as a synergistic component of holistic psychiatric rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08354-w.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are clinical syndromes characterized by impairments in emotion regulation, cognitive function, and behavioral patterns [1]. Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent and clinically impactful in individuals with psychiatric disorders. Approximately 70% of patients experience difficulties with sleep initiation or maintenance, and the prevalence of comorbid insomnia exceeds 30% [2]. Importantly, chronic sleep disturbance is an independent risk factor for cognitive decline, psychiatric relapse, premature mortality, and cardiometabolic diseases, severely worsening prognosis and quality of life [3, 4]. Despite the urgent clinical need to improve sleep in psychiatric populations, evidence-based non-pharmacological interventions remain limited and underutilized. Therefore, identifying safe, accessible, and effective strategies to improve sleep is critically needed to enhance psychiatric treatment outcomes and long-term health.

Given the profound impact of sleep on both mental and physical health, evaluating effective treatment strategies for sleep disturbances within psychiatric populations is imperative. Sleep quality is a multidimensional construct encompassing both subjective experience and objective physiological parameters [5]. Subjective sleep quality reflects self-reported perceptions of sleep satisfaction, restoration, and insomnia severity, commonly measured using validated questionnaires such as the PSQI, ISI, and sleep diaries. Objective sleep quality is assessed via physiological tools including polysomnography and actigraphy to quantify sleep efficiency, sleep architecture, and wake-sleep patterns [6]. Notably, these complementary measures are frequently combined in meta-analyses to comprehensively evaluate overall sleep improvement, as they capture distinct but clinically meaningful dimensions of sleep health [5, 7]. Although clinical guidelines prioritize cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and pharmacotherapies as first-line treatments, the implementation of CBT-I is often constrained by limited professional resources, high expenses, and low patient adherence [8]. Similarly, medications (e.g., melatonin receptor agonists) often lead to sub-optimal benefit-to-risk ratio, as their therapeutic effects are frequently offset by substantial financial costs and inherent risks of adverse reactions (e.g., daytime fatigue and cognitive impairment) [9, 10]. These clinical barriers have led to an increased interest in further add-on and alternative therapies, with physical exercise emerging as a highly accessible and evidence-based non-pharmacological intervention.

As a targeted adjunctive therapy, exercise entails a planned, systematic, and repetitive physical activity regimen aimed at enhancing health and physical fitness [11]. Exercise has been increasingly recognized as an accessible, cost-effective, and promising non-pharmacological intervention for sleep disturbances in both general and clinical populations [12, 13]. However, high-quality evidence supporting exercise for improving sleep in patients with psychiatric disorders remains limited, inconsistent, and heterogeneous across studies [7, 14, 15]. Most prior studies have combined heterogeneous exercise interventions without distinguishing the effects of modality, intensity, frequency, or duration. Furthermore, existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not sufficiently clarified the optimal exercise parameters for maximizing sleep benefits across psychiatric disorders. This lack of granular, patient-tailored guidance creates a critical barrier to clinical translation, as clinicians cannot implement evidence-based exercise prescriptions for sleep management in psychiatric settings. Therefore, a comprehensive, transdiagnostic meta-analysis is urgently needed to quantify the overall effect of exercise on sleep quality and to identify the most effective FITT (i.e., Frequency, Intensity, Time, and Type)-based exercise parameters for patients with psychiatric disorders. The present study was designed to fill this knowledge gap and provide precise, actionable recommendations for clinical practice.

Consequently, a more comprehensive analysis is required to clarify how these variables differentially shape subjective and objective sleep quality across psychiatric disorders. The present study was designed to: (1) quantify the pooled effect size of exercise interventions compared to inactive controls for enhancing sleep quality in individuals with clinically diagnosed psychiatric disorders; and (2) identify the moderating effects of key prescription parameters, including frequency, type, intensity, and duration. By investigating these variables, this study seeks to provide actionable evidence for developing precise exercise prescriptions, thereby empowering clinicians to optimize treatment strategies and improve sleep recovery in psychiatric practice.

Methods

Search strategy

Adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement, this study followed a pre-registered protocol (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024621433). We executed a comprehensive electronic search across four databases, specifically PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, encompassing all indexed publications from their respective start dates up to March 1, 2026. Database-specific search strings are detailed in Supplementary Material S1.

Study selection

Studies were selected for inclusion, specifically involving (1) Participants: adult participants (≥ 18 years) possessing a primary clinical diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), or via structured assessment tools like the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) and other equivalent verified clinical protocols; (2) Intervention: structured physical exercise interventions, including aerobic, mind-body, resistance, or multi-component exercise, which align with the principles outlined by the established guidelines [16]; (3) Comparison: eligible control arms included treatment as usual, a waiting list, or no intervention. A control arm could also receive another active treatment, such as pharmacological or psychological therapy, as long as the exercise arm received the same treatment, so that exercise was the only difference between the two arms; (4) Outcomes: assessment of sleep quality via self-reported metrics (such as the PSQI, ISI, or sleep diaries) or through objective instruments (e.g., nocturnal sleep efficiency derived from polysomnography or accelerometry-based actigraphy); and (5) Study design: peer-reviewed RCTs published in English. Research were deemed ineligible for the current analysis if they: (1) enrolled individuals presenting with untreated co-existing sleep disorders or primary insomnia disorders; (2) employed confounded interventions in which exercise was combined with an additional therapy that was not equivalently provided to the control arm, thereby preventing the isolation of exercise-specific effects; or (3) lacked extractable data or failed to clearly report outcomes. Detailed definitions for each exercise modality are provided in Supplementary Material S2.

The selection of studies was executed in a two-stage process by two independent reviewers. This involved an assessment of titles and abstracts; subsequently, a rigorous full-text review was conducted, guided by a standardized form. In the event of any disagreements, resolution was achieved through mutual consensus or by seeking the expertise of a third investigator.

Data extraction and bias assessment

Key study information, including the first author, country, year of publication, and total sample size, were independently retrieved by two investigators. We also recorded participant characteristics, such as mean age and sex, alongside clinical diagnoses. Additionally, the specific details of the exercise programs, including type, intensity, time, and frequency were documented. Sleep quality, the primary study outcome, was assessed via subjective questionnaires or objective sleep parameters, such as polysomnography or actigraphy. To address any missing or unclear data, we reached out to the primary authors through email. Any inconsistencies during the data extraction phase were resolved via mutual discussion.

The methodological quality was examined via the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) tool. This assessment addressed domains, including: (1) the randomization process, (2) adherence to the intended interventions, (3) the completeness of data, (4) measurement of outcomes, and (5) selection of reported results. Based on the RoB 2 criteria, studies were categorized as “low risk of bias” (i.e., all domains judged as low risk), “some concerns” (i.e., at least one domain with some concerns), or “high risk of bias” (i.e., at least one domain with high risk or multiple domains with some concerns). To evaluate potential reporting bias, we utilized visual inspections of funnel plots in conjunction with Egger’s linear regression model.

Data analysis

Statistical syntheses were conducted using R 4.4.3. To derive overall effect sizes (Hedges’ g, 95%), we employed a random-effects framework [17]. We used post-treatment means and standard deviations (SDs) to determine effect sizes. To ensure directional consistency across all outcome instruments, effect sizes were coded such that a negative Hedges’ g uniformly reflects improvement in sleep quality. When these were missing, we calculated them from other available statistics, such as p-values and standard errors. To prevent unit-of-analysis errors in studies with shared controls, the control condition’s sample size was partitioned. Thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were adopted to categorize pooled effect sizes as small, moderate, and large, respectively. Statistical significance was defined by a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05. Meta-analysis was restricted to outcomes with at least three independent datasets. To evaluate inter-study heterogeneity, the I2 index was employed, with values below 25%, between 25% and 50%, and exceeding 50% representing low, moderate, and substantial inconsistency, respectively [18]. The robustness of study findings was verified through leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Furthermore, potential moderation was explored via subgroup analyses. These categorical variables included modality (resistance, aerobic, mind-body, or multi-component), exercise frequency (≥ 3 or < 3 times/week), intensity (low-to-moderate or moderate-to-vigorous), single session duration (≥ 30 or < 30 min), and program duration (≥ 12 or < 12 weeks). Additionally, we explored the influence of specific psychiatric diagnoses, such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia. The impact of the assessment method (subjective vs. objective metrics) was also examined. Meta-regression models were utilized to identify factors contributing to heterogeneity, focusing on prescriptive variables such as intervention duration, frequency, volume, and or total weekly exercise time.

Results

Search results

As illustrated in the study selection flowchart (Fig. 1), systematic database searches in March 2026 yielded 7426 records. After removing duplicates, 5927 papers underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 5846 were excluded, leaving 81 articles for a detailed full-text assessment. Ultimately, 21 RCTs (representing 24 independent trials) fulfilled the eligibility requirements and were incorporated into the final quantitative analysis.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 21 studies were qualitatively synthesized (Table 1). Following the pre-specified approach of treating multi-arm trials as separate comparisons, 24 individual trials were included. The 24 identified RCTs were primarily from China (n = 7) and the United States (n = 6), followed by Australia, Ireland, and Germany (each n = 2). A total cohort of 1628 individuals was analyzed, with 846 allocated to exercise interventions and 782 serving as controls. Regarding clinical profiles, depressive disorder was the most common diagnosis (n = 9), while other studies focused on anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorder, and mixed psychosis. Subjective sleep quality was predominantly assessed via self-report instruments, most notably the PSQI (n = 14). Two studies utilized the Pittsburgh Sleep Diary and another two employed the ISI, while a single study relied on specialized subscales for sleep disturbance. Three trials relied solely on objective monitoring to quantify sleep quality. The duration of exercise program ranged from 16 to 120 min per session, implemented over periods of three to 24 weeks. Weekly intervention frequency varied across studies, spanning from a single session to daily occurrences. The prescribed exercise intervention comprised four primary types, including aerobic training (n = 10), mind-body practice (n = 5), resistance training (n = 4), and multi-component exercises (n = 3).

Methodological quality and risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for the included RCTs is detailed in Fig. 2. Approximately 24% of the studies (n = 5) were classified as having a low overall risk, whereas nine trials presented “some concerns”, largely due to randomization protocols. High risk of bias was identified in seven studies. Notably, the “missing outcome data” domain consistently showed low risk across most papers. In contrast, the most frequent methodological weakness involved the “measurement of the outcome” and “selection of reported result”. Regarding publication bias, neither the visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 3) nor Egger’s test (P = 0.327) indicated significant asymmetry, suggesting a minimal risk of reporting bias.

Meta-analysis results

Overall effects of exercise on sleep quality

A synthesis of 21 RCTs (24 individual trials) involving 1628 participants evaluated how physical exercise impacts sleep quality among adults with psychiatric conditions. Compared to control groups, exercise interventions yielded a significant improvement in sleep, characterized by a medium effect size (g: -0.57, 95% CI: -0.75, -0.38, P < 0.001, Fig. 4). Given the substantial heterogeneity observed (I2 = 61.7%), a random-effects framework was justified. Furthermore, the robustness of this outcome was supported by leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, which showed stable effect estimates (range: -0.50 to -0.63) with all CI remaining entirely below zero (Figure S8).

Effects of exercise frequency

As illustrated in Figure S1, the results indicated that interventions conducted ≥ 3 times per week resulted in more pronounced improvements (g: -0.60, 95% CI: -0.78, -0.43, P < 0.001) compared to those delivered < 3 times per week (g: -0.50, 95% CI: -0.89, -0.11, P = 0.012).

Effects of exercise intensity

The results showed that both moderate-to-vigorous exercise intensity (g: -0.37, 95% CI: -0.57, -0.17, P < 0.001) and low-to-moderate exercise intensity (g: -0.70, 95% CI: -0.99, -0.41, P < 0.001) significantly enhanced sleep quality (Figure S2).

Effects of exercise type

All four types of exercise significantly improved sleep when compared to control group. Aerobic exercise produced the largest effect size (g: -0.69, 95% CI: -1.02, -0.35, P < 0.001), followed by mind-body exercise (g: -0.67, 95% CI: -1.21, -0.14, P = 0.014), resistance exercise (g: -0.60, 95% CI: -0.92, -0.27, P < 0.001), and multicomponent exercise (g: -0.27, 95% CI: -0.50, -0.04, P = 0.024), see Figure S3.

Effects of exercise length

For the overall length of exercise intervention, both short-term (< 12 weeks) interventions (g: -0.58, 95% CI: -0.92, -0.25, P < 0.001) and long-term (≥ 12 weeks) interventions (g: -0.54, 95 CI %: -0.76, -0.32, P < 0.001) produced significant and positive effect sizes, but short-term (< 12 weeks) had a larger positive effect on improving sleep quality, as illustrated in Figure S4.

Effects of session duration

Subgroup analysis based on session duration (Figure S5) revealed that both durations significantly improved sleep quality. Specifically, exercise sessions ≥ 30 min yielded a slightly more pronounced effect (g: -0.58, 95% CI: -0.81, -0.35, P < 0.001) than those lasting < 30 min (g: -0.52, 95% CI: -0.85, -0.19, P = 0.002).

Effects of disease type

The analyses revealed significant intervention benefits for depression (g: -0.51, 95% CI: -0.71 to -0.32, P < 0.001), anxiety disorder (g: -0.43, 95% CI: -0.69 to -0.16, P = 0.0016), and schizophrenia (g: -0.87, 95% CI: -1.55 to -0.20, P = 0.011), while results for post-traumatic stress disorder, substance use disorder, and mixed populations did not reach statistical significance (Figure S6). Although substantial overall heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 61.70%, P < 0.001), primarily driven by variance within the schizophrenia and substance use disorder subgroups, inter-subgroup comparisons yielded no statistically discernible differences, suggesting a consistent intervention effect across the diverse psychiatric conditions evaluated.

Effects of sleep quality measures

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the intervention was effective for both subjective sleep quality (g: -0.60, 95% CI: -0.81, -0.38, P < 0.001, I2 = 65%) and objective sleep quality (g: -0.47, 95% CI: -0.74, -0.20, P < 0.001, I2 = 28%), with no significant difference observed between these two measurement modalities (P = 0.46), see Figure S7.

Univariate meta-regression analysis

To explore sources of the heterogeneity, univariate meta-regression was performed to examine potential linear moderating effects of intervention parameters, including duration, frequency, volume, and total weekly exercise time. Results showed that none of these continuous parameters exhibited a significant linear association with effect sizes for subjective sleep quality (all P > 0.05; Figure S9).

Discussion

The present study systematically evaluates the efficacy of exercise on sleep quality from a transdiagnostic perspective, with a particular focus on examining the potential moderating effects of key prescription parameters, including exercise type, duration, frequency, intensity, and length, as well as examining differential effects across specific psychiatric diagnoses. The main findings indicate that (1) exercise exerts a significant beneficial effect on sleep quality in individuals with psychiatric disorders; (2) while various exercise modalities are effective, aerobic exercise showed the largest effect on enhancing sleep quality; (3) exercise showed the largest effects with low-to-moderate intensity, at least three sessions per week, sessions of at least 30 min, and short-term programs (< 12 weeks); (4) exercise significantly improved sleep quality in patients with depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia, with the largest effect size in schizophrenia, although this estimate came from few studies with wide confidence intervals and should be interpreted with caution; and (5) exercise is effective across both subjective and objective outcome measures. These results generally support the transdiagnostic efficacy of exercise, suggesting it serves as a viable intervention for sleep quality across multiple psychiatric conditions. However, the strength of evidence varies by diagnosis, with the most robust effects seen in depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia.

While earlier meta-analyses have provided initial evidence that exercise generally benefits sleep quality in psychiatric populations, the present study refines this evidence by quantifying how distinct prescription parameters differentially shape sleep outcomes within a transdiagnostic framework. This parameter-specific approach provides the detailed evidence needed to translate the general benefit of exercise into specific, clinically actionable prescriptions. Based on the FITT principle, we conducted subgroup analyses on exercise variables (such as type, frequency, intensity, and duration). Our results indicated that all types of exercise can improve sleep quality, with aerobic exercise showing the highest effect size for enhancing sleep quality, followed sequentially by mind-body exercise and resistance exercise. Previous exercise interventions for insomnia have predominantly focused on aerobic exercise and mind-body exercises, and these two categories are considered the optimal exercise modalities for improving sleep quality [39]. As noted by Xie et al. [40] mind-body exercises (e.g., yoga and traditional Chinese exercises) exert a positive influence on nocturnal sleep quality. Aerobic exercise enhances sleep quality through multiple mechanisms, such as modulating cytokines [41, 42], enhancing oxidative stress resistance [43, 44], restoring neurotransmitter balance (serotonin, dopamine) [43, 45], regulating stress hormones (cortisol) and circadian rhythm hormones (melatonin) [46], and promoting sleep via post-exercise temperature drop [46, 47]. Mind-body exercises that incorporate slow, coordinated movements, deep controlled breathing, and meditation can effectively down regulate stress hormone secretion, modulate the autonomic nervous system, and reduce inflammatory markers, thereby alleviating psychological stress and promoting relaxation [48, 49]. Emerging neuroscience research indicates that mindfulness and meditation practices trigger neuroplastic adaptations in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, enhancing emotional regulation and alleviating anxiety and depression symptoms, which are core psychological stressors disrupting sleep homeostasis [13, 50]. Additionally, resistance exercise also has a regulatory effect on the sleep quality of patients with psychiatric disorders. Resistance exercise stimulates muscle contractions against external resistance, enhancing muscular hypertrophy, reducing adiposity, and improving neuromuscular function, metabolic efficiency, and bone mineral density through multifaceted physiological adaptations [51]. The underlying mechanism may involve that resistance training induces myocytes to secrete IL-6, which could improve the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines and activate hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to enhance endogenous corticosteroid secretion [52]. These coordinated responses collectively attenuate systemic low-grade inflammation and modulate neuroendocrine pathways, thereby improving sleep quality [53, 54]. Hu et al. [55] reveal that resistance exercise is more effective than aerobic exercise in multiple aspects, including improving sleep quality, increasing melatonin levels, reducing inflammatory responses, promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine synthesis, and enhancing mood. In practice, when designing exercise programs to enhance sleep quality for individuals with psychiatric disorders, exercise selection should be tailored with care, prioritizing aerobic exercise, mind - body exercise, and resistance exercise.

Beyond exercise type, our subgroup analyses suggested that the other FITT components (frequency, intensity, and duration) may also be associated with the size of the effect, although their confidence intervals overlapped and no parameter emerged as a significant moderator. The largest effects were observed at low-to-moderate intensity, for short-term duration (< 12 weeks), frequency of ≥ 3 times per week and sessions ≥ 30 min. While both extended and brief exercise protocols demonstrated significant efficacy in improving sleep among psychiatric cohorts, our findings suggest that shorter-term interventions may yield more pronounced improvements. This indicates that the therapeutic impact of physical activity may be influenced by temporal parameters, with acute or brief regimens potentially offering superior benefits. Consistent with this observation, extant meta-analytic evidence often adopts a 12-week threshold to differentiate between chronic and short-range intervention protocols, indicated that shorter exercise durations potentially yield superior improvements in sleep quality [7]. Additionally, there are also significant effect sizes for long-term exercise [56]. Therefore, prior to the formulation of exercise programs for patients suffering from psychiatric disorders, a thorough evaluation of their sleep-related concerns is imperative. The optimal prescription emerged as low to moderate-intensity exercise (≥ 3 weekly sessions, 30 min/session), aligning with WHO-recommended weekly volumes (150–300 min moderate activity) [57]. These findings are consistent in part with those recommended by the ACSM and with previous meta-analyses. For example, Xue et al. [58] established a lower limit of 440 METs-min/week for improving sleep in elderly populations, a volume that could be achieved through either 160 min of low-to-moderate activity or 60 min of vigorous-intensity exercise per week. Lei et al. [59] demonstrated that older adults engaging in ≥ 150 min of weekly Tai Chi practice exhibited significant sleep quality improvement. Collectively, our findings underscore that structured, short-term exercise regimens, characterized by moderate intensity, consistent weekly frequency, and sufficient session duration, represent an optimal intervention for improving sleep quality among psychiatric populations. These findings align with global public health guidelines and underscore the importance of evidence-based exercise prescription as a viable adjunctive strategy in mental health care. Statistical heterogeneity in the present meta-analysis was high, indicating considerable variability in the effect size estimates across studies. Despite the absence of identification of the sources of heterogeneity, the results obtained from the sensitivity analysis indicated the robustness of the findings. Our subgroup analyses suggested that effect sizes differed across FITT categories, yet the meta-regression found no significant association for the same parameters. This apparent inconsistency may reflect a difference in method rather than a true contradiction. Subgroup analysis compares pooled effects across pre-defined categories and can capture threshold or non-linear patterns, whereas meta-regression tests only for a linear dose–response trend. This analysis was also likely underpowered, as the number of trials was well below the ten or so per covariate generally recommended. We therefore regard the FITT differences as suggestive rather than confirmed, and interpret these patterns as hypotheses to be tested in larger, adequately powered trials.

The variation in treatment outcomes across different psychiatric conditions in our subgroup analysis provides critical insights into the clinical utility of exercise. Our results, specifically the robust improvements in sleep quality among participants with depression, concur with prior meta-analytic data suggesting that exercise is effective for sleep regulation [15].While the depression subgroup yielded the most stable findings due to its larger sample size, it is important to note that significant benefits were also observed for anxiety disorders and schizophrenia. The latter, despite exhibiting the largest effect size, was characterized by high internal variance. The non-significant findings observed in the substance use disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder subgroups may be attributable to limited number of studies. Consequently, these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution, and further large-scale, high-quality RCTs are warranted to provide a more definitive appraisal of exercise efficacy in these specific populations. Small sample sizes in these categories may preclude the detection of meaningful improvements. This interpretation is supported by the non-significant test for subgroup differences, which suggests that the therapeutic impact of exercise may be relatively consistent across these diverse diagnostic categories. Nevertheless, the high heterogeneity observed within the schizophrenia and substance use disorder cohorts indicates that individual responses remain variable.

A primary strength of this study is that it moves beyond simply examining whether exercise improves sleep quality to examining how it should be prescribed. Rather than pooling diverse programs into a single estimate, we separated the effects of individual exercise parameters, type, frequency, intensity, and duration (both per session and overall), within a transdiagnostic framework. This parameter-level approach yields more concrete, practically useful guidance for clinicians and is what most clearly sets the present work apart from earlier reviews. A further strength is the rigorous selection of participants, who were diagnosed using clinical criteria such as ICD-10 or DSM-5 rather than self-report screening scales. By excluding individuals identified solely through symptom questionnaires, this approach reduces the risk of false-positive inflation and ensures that the observed benefits can be attributed to clinically confirmed psychiatric populations, which strengthens the relevance of our findings to real-world practice. Finally, the combined use of subjective measures (PSQI, ISI, and sleep diaries) and objective tools (polysomnography and actigraphy) improves measurement validity and adds confidence to the conclusions.

Limitation

Despite the insights provided, several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, the inclusion of diverse psychiatric disorders introduces diagnostic heterogeneity; however, this transdiagnostic approach enhances the generalizability of our findings to real-world clinical settings. Second, the included trials exhibited substantial inter-study heterogeneity; however, the potential sources of this variability remained elusive, as the meta-regression models yielded no significant findings for the pre-specified parameters. In addition, the meta-regression was likely underpowered given the small number of trials, so the FITT-related findings should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies. The heterogeneity may also reflect unmeasured factors, such as disease severity, comorbid conditions, and medication use. Third, although we stratified the results by diagnostic category, the interpretive power of these findings is constrained by the sparse number of trials and relatively small cohorts. These statistical limitations, combined with persistent methodological variability, preclude the formulation of standardized, disorder-specific exercise protocols. Current evidence remains insufficient to define precise parameters for individual psychiatric subgroups. To advance tailored intervention frameworks, future research must prioritize methodologically rigorous, homogeneous studies that consistently document clinical characteristics.

Conclusion

Physical exercise was an effective transdiagnostic intervention for improving sleep quality in patients with psychiatric disorders, with a significant overall effect. Among the parameters examined, aerobic exercise showed the largest effect, and the most favorable outcomes were seen with low-to-moderate intensity, a frequency of at least three sessions per week, sessions of at least 30 min, and programs shorter than 12 weeks. However, given the limited number of trials, the substantial heterogeneity, and the absence of significant moderation in the meta-regression, these subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the overall benefit, clinicians may consider offering exercise as an adjunctive therapy, with the type and dose tailored to patient preference and physical capacity. Future multiarm randomized trials are needed to confirm the most effective parameters and to extend the evidence to less-studied diagnoses.
