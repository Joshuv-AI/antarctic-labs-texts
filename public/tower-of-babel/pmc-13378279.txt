Optimized iCIEF Fractionation Enables Deep Profiling of Antibody Drug Conjugate Charge Heterogeneity
Authors: Eunju Jang, Qunying Zhang, Paul M. Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibody drug conjugates (ADCs) are a novel class of therapeutic drugs that combine selectivity of an antibody moiety with the cell killing potency of cytotoxic agent. ADCs are complex molecules, and their charge variants are one of the important critical quality attributes (CQAs) that need to be monitored throughout the drug development process. Imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (iCIEF) is considered a gold standard in charge variant analysis due to its short analysis time and ease of use. For comprehensive analysis, iCIEF fractionation is a newly developed technique that can be used to enable LC–MS characterization of charge variants. Here, we describe method development for an iCIEF fractionation system, as well as further characterization using LC–MS. Fractionation conditions were optimized to provide stabilized mobilization after separation. Fractions were collected with the optimized method, and native SEC–MS results showed that fractionation enabled detection of species previously undetectable. Further mass spectrometric analysis revealed the major posttranslational modifications (PTMs) and drug load distributions for both light and heavy chains. The results indicate that iCIEF fraction collection enables a deeper understanding of ADC charge variants and will be further useful for studying other complex biomolecules.

Introduction

It has been long established that cancer is a significant global health risk, reporting nearly 20 million new cases of cancer and leading to approximately 10 million cancer deaths in 2022 [1]. Traditional small molecule chemotherapy has been used for the treatment of cancer; however, these cytotoxic agents lack selectivity toward cancer cells and can lead to severe adverse effects as unintended healthy normal cells can be damaged [2]. As a result, there has been an increased desire for to develop new targeted therapeutic agents, and antibody drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a unique class of drugs by combining selectivity of antibody moiety and the cell killing potency of cytotoxic agent [3, 4, 5]. ADCs are composed of three components, an antibody, a linker, and a payload, where the cytotoxic payload is attached to the antibody via conjugation process by the linker. ADCs may present high levels of heterogeneity because of conjugation of small molecule moieties to the already structurally complex antibodies [6]. This complexity leads to analytical challenges in monitoring critical quality attributes (CQAs) of ADCs. CQAs are physical, chemical, biological, and microbiological properties or characteristics that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution. Common CQAs of ADCs include drug‐to‐antibody ratio (DAR), drug load distribution, size variants, and charge variants, and it is important to monitor them because they can impact product quality, safety, and efficacy [7]. Charge variants are protein species that exhibit altered charge properties and can be separated into acidic, main, and basic forms [8]. Charge variants may arise from multiple bioprocessing steps, including upstream cell culture, drug conjugation processes, purification processes, and any enzymatic or chemical degradation [7, 8]. Due to their potential to affect drug efficacy, characterizing and monitoring charge variants during the drug development process are important for process control and to ensure product quality [9, 10].

Imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (iCIEF) has become a gold standard for analysis of charge variants in biotherapeutic development [11, 12]. To fully understand charge variants in a given product, either online characterization or offline characterization approach can be used [13]. iCIEF coupled to MS (iCIEF–MS) is an online characterization method, and it has emerged as a powerful tool to analyze protein charge variants with rapid and sensitive manner, often providing unambiguous identification of charge variants using intact mass measurement [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Despite the advancement in online iCIEF–MS, offline fraction collection is still required for the complete understanding of individual charge variants, such as identification and quantification of posttranslational modification (PTM) using subunit LC–MS, peptide mapping analysis, and a variety of other biophysical techniques [20, 21, 22]. Traditionally, ion exchange chromatography (IEX) has been used for the offline fractionation of charge variants. However, this approach is inefficient as it requires a comprehensive bridging study to correlate iCIEF peaks with IEX peaks because their separation principles are different. Separation of IEX depends on net surface charge, surface charge distribution, and interaction with column, whereas iCIEF separates charge variants based on their intrinsic isoelectric point (pI). Given that ADCs are quite complicated, charge variant profiles from IEX may not correlate well with those obtained from iCIEF separations [23]. Recent technology development allows direct iCIEF fractionation of complex proteins, eliminating the need for developing a bridging study for the correlation. A typical bridging study includes fractionation and purity assessment by IEX, followed by reinjection into iCIEF to determine how charge variants in IEX correspond to charge peaks in iCIEF.

This work demonstrates the feasibility of iCIEF fractionation for the characterization of charge variants for ADCs. Charge variants were fractionated using a preparative cartridge, and peaks were eluted through pressure‐based mobilization. The collected fractions were analyzed by native SEC–MS and reduced reversed‐phase LC–MS (reduced RPLC–MS) for intact and subunit‐level analysis, and peptide mapping was performed to elucidate primary structure and the PTMs on a molecular level.

Materials and Methods

Materials

ADC1 is an IgG1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) conjugated to maleimidocaproyl monomethylauristatin F (mcMMAF). Native cysteine‐based conjugation led to broad drug load distribution with average DAR of 4.

AESlytes, iminodiacetic acid (IDA), anolyte, catholyte, conditioning solution, and AD (acrylamide derivative)‐coated cartridge were obtained from Advanced Electrophoresis Solutions Ltd (Cambridge, Ontario, Canada). Pharmalyte carrier ampholytes were purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). Methylcellulose, pI markers, electrolytes, fluorescence calibration standard, and iCIEF cartridges were purchased from ProteinSimple (San Jose, CA, USA). LC/MS grade materials (acetonitrile [ACN], water, isopropyl alcohol [IPA], formic acid [FA], trifluoroacetic acid [TFA]), iodoacetamide, Zeba Spin desalting columns, guanidine, and bis‐tris buffer were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Formamide, urea, dithiothreitol (DTT), Trizma base, l‐methionine, hydrochloric acid, and trace metal‐basis ammonium acetate were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA). Tris–HCl and glycerol were purchased from Teknova (Hollister, CA, USA). Trypsin/Lys‐C mix mass spec grade was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI, USA).

ICIEF Method Development

CEInfinite C01 (Advanced Electrophoresis Solutions Ltd [AES], Cambridge, Ontario, Canada) with AES AD–coated preparative cartridge (CP00307) was used for iCIEF fraction collection. Instrumentation and configuration details are available in the paper by Chen et al. [24]. UV detection at 280 nm was conducted, and the voltage was programmed for 1000 V for 1.0 min, 2000 V for 1.0 min, and 3000 V for 10.0 min for focusing and 3000 V for 21 min for mobilization. Mobilization speed was 0.12 µL/min with mixture of 9.6 mM IDA and 2% glycerol. Milli‐Q water was used as makeup solution, and 2.4 µL/min of the solution was added to the micro tee attached to elution side of transfer capillary. For the preparation of iCIEF fractionation solution, 4% v/v HR AESlyte 8.5–9.5, 2% v/v of HR AESlyte 7–9, 10% v/v formamide, 7 mM IDA, and 2 mg/mL ADC were mixed and incubated for 5 h at 5°C prior to the fraction collection. Five fractions were collected each run, and a total of 54 runs were carried out using two cartridges—one for 18 runs and the other for 36 runs. Fraction collection was conducted at room temperature. After fraction collection, 10k MWCO membranes were used to concentrate each fraction. After sample recovery, the concentration of each fraction was measured by Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

To confirm the purity of the collected fractions, analytical iCIEF was conducted using Maurice with fluorocarbon‐coated analytical cartridge. Native fluorescence was used as detection and exposure time was set to 40 s. Master mix composition was 3 M urea, 0.5% v/v methylcellulose, 3% v/v Pharmalyte 5–8, 3% v/v Pharmalyte 8–10.5, 0.5% v/v pI marker 7.05, and 0.5% v/v pI marker 9.50. Master mix to sample mixing ratio was 3:1, and the 50 µL of final mixture was transferred to a 96‐well plate. The 96‐well plate was centrifuged at 2129 g for 3 min to remove air bubbles. Voltage was set to 1500 V for 1 min and 3000 V for 9 min. Data were acquired and processed with Waters Empower 3 software (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA).

Native SEC–MS

Each sample was diluted with 50 mM bis‐tris buffer pH 6.0 to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL, and 2.4 µg was injected for analysis. The samples were analyzed using ACQUITY UPLC Protein BEH SEC Column (200 Å, 1.7 µm dp, 2.1 i.d. × 150 mm L) on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer in positive electrospray ionization mode. Isocratic gradient where mobile phase of 100 mM ammonium acetate with 10% IPA was used at 25°C and a flow rate of 0.09 mL/min. Data were processed using Protein Metrics Byos software, v.5.8, for the analysis of the intact mass.

Reduced RPLC–MS

The samples were analyzed using a Waters BioResolve RP mAb Polyphenyl column (2.1 mm i.d. × 150 mm L, 450 Å) on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer in positive electrospray ionization mode. UV detection wavelength was 280 nm. Mobile phase A contained 0.015% TFA and 0.085% FA in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.015% TFA and 0.085% FA in ACN. Flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was set to 80°C. The elution gradient was set as 31% B at 0 min, 31% B at 2.0 min, 41% B at 15 min, 90% B at 15.5 min, 90% B at 16.0 min, 31% B at 16.5 min, and 31% B at 20.0 min. The data were processed using the Protein Metrics Byos software, v.5.8, to determine the masses of partial reduced subunits present in the sample.

Peptide Mapping

10 µg of each sample was denatured by dilution into 80 µL of denaturing buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.5 with 7.6 M guanidine HCl). Samples were then reduced at 12 mM DTT at 37°C for 30 min. Samples were alkylated at 24 mM iodoacetamide at 37°C for 30 min, protected from light. Afterwards, the samples were quenched with 1 M DTT and buffer exchanged into a digestion buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5 with 1 M urea and 20 mM l‐methionine) using 7k MWCO Zeba spin desalting columns according to the manufacturer's instruction. Then digestion was performed with Lys‐C/trypsin mix at an enzyme‐to‐protein ratio of 1:20 (w:w). After incubating the samples at 37°C for 2 h, digestion was quenched with 5% FA.

The digested samples were separated using a Waters Acquity BEH C18 column (2.1 mm i.d. × 150 mm L, 1.8 µm dp) on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system and analyzed by a Thermo Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer. Detection wavelengths were 214 and 280 nm. Mobile phase A contained 0.015% TFA and 0.085% FA in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.015% TFA and 0.085% FA in ACN. Column temperature was 55°C, and peptides were eluted at a flow rate of 0.55 mL/min. The elution gradient was set as 1% B at 0 min, 8% B at 1.4 min, 30% B at 18.4 min, 60% B at 24.2 min, 70% B at 27.2 min, 90% B at 27.3 min, 90% B at 27.6 min, 1% B at 28.0 min, 90% B at 28.3 min, 1% B at 28.7 min, 90% B at 29.2 min, 90% B at 29.6 min, 1% B at 29.7 min, and 1% B at 34.0 min. The data were processed using the Protein Metrics Byos software, v.5.8, for the peptide identification.

Results and Discussion

Preparative iCIEF Method Optimization

The first step of iCIEF fraction collection is the optimization of a preparative iCIEF method. The preparative iCIEF cartridge used for this experiment consists of an AD‐coated separation capillary with an inner diameter of 320 µm, and the separation zone is eluted to the cathodic end of the capillary through pressure‐based mobilization. A key difficulty in method optimization is reducing peak broadening during mobilization, which can be influenced by several contributing factors: (1) dispersive effects from parabolic hydrodynamic flow, (2) Joule heating, and (3) residual electroosmotic flow (EOF). First, pressure‐based mobilization produces a parabolic hydrodynamic flow profile, leading to protein band broadening due to dispersion [25]. This trend becomes more pronounced as the migration time of the focused bands in the separation capillary increases during mobilization [26]. Therefore, acidic peaks are more susceptible to peak broadening as mobilization proceeds. Second, Joule heating is more likely to occur with a larger capillary inner diameter, resulting in band broadening due to thermal effects [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Unlike a typical analytical iCIEF cartridge with a capillary inner diameter of less than 100 µm, the preparative cartridge has a larger inner diameter and may not easily dissipate heat across the capillary. Additionally, a high voltage (3000 V) is applied to the system during mobilization for refocusing. As a result, resistive Joule heating is unavoidable, leading to increased temperature and the formation of both radial and axial temperature gradients [32]. These temperature gradients may cause nonuniform electrical and velocity fields, which, in turn, lead to band broadening. Lastly, residual EOF may contribute to band broadening [33]. Typically the coating of the capillary inner surface greatly reduces EOF as well as analyte adsorption by masking silanol groups, whereas the coating can degrade over time due to several factors, including chemical composition of sample materials and physical stresses, such as pressure [34]. This may result in residual EOF in the capillary and lead to the band broadening. To address these problems, we employed two approaches to increase mobilization stability: reducing pressure‐driven dispersion effects and shortening the mobilization time. The pressure‐driven dispersion effects can be mitigated by lowering a mobilization flow rate and increasing viscosity [25]. The operational syringe pump speed for the fractionation ranges from 0.12 to 0.16 µL/min. Here, 0.12 µL/min flow rate was employed for the method. As the diffusion is inversely proportional to the viscosity of the solution, we added a small amount of glycerol (2%) to the mobilization solution to improve the stability of mobilization [25, 35]. IDA was added to the sample solution to function as an anodic stabilizer. The pH gradient is compressed due to a reduced capillary length by IDA, which shortens the mobilization time.

To evaluate the optimized method, focusing and mobilization electropherograms were compared to those obtained using the initial sample preparation method, as shown in Figure 1. The initial sample preparation method, which used 2% AESlyte 7–9, 4% AESlyte 8.5–9.5, and 10% formamide, showed good charge variant separation as presented in Figure 1A; however, the separation profile became distorted as mobilization proceeded. As shown in Figure 1B, only minimal changes in the separation profile were observed with the optimized method. These results demonstrate that optimization was successful. Using the optimized method, an auto‐fractionation protocol was developed. Sample incubation was one of the key factors in establishing the auto‐fractionation method. Without incubation, differences in overall peak elution time and slight peak shape changes were observed between early and late injections. To prevent this, the prepared sample was incubated for at least 5 h at 5°C prior to fraction collection. Samples were freshly prepared each day, and a total of 54 injections were collected over 3 days (18 injections per day).

Confirmation of Purity

With an optimized preparative iCIEF method, ADC1 charge variants were isolated into five fractions. Charge variant Peaks a, b, and c were collected in Fraction 1, Peaks d and e were collected in Fraction 2, Peaks f and g were collected in Fraction 3, Peaks h and i were collected in Fraction 4, and Peaks j and k were collected in Fraction 5 (Figure 2A).

After fraction collection, concentration of fractions was followed by purity check to confirm identities. The purity of each fraction was evaluated with Maurice analytical iCIEF with native fluorescence detection. Figure 2B illustrates the overlay of the five reinjected ADC1 fractions and the unfractionated ADC1. Table 1 lists the percentage relative peak areas of fractions. Both iCIEF electropherograms and the calculated % relative peak area demonstrated that Fractions 1, 2, 3, and 4 were enriched with the intended charge variant species, with purity level ranging between 48% and 73%. Although Fraction 5 showed a relatively lower level of peak purity compared to other four fractions on the basis of Table 1, the iCIEF profile confirmed that the intended acidic Peaks j and k were enriched in this fraction. As Peaks j and k migrate from the acidic region to the basic end, we speculated that either residual protein on the capillary wall or collection valve may coelute with these peaks and contribute to the lower percent purity observed in Fraction 5.

Native SEC–MS Analysis

To investigate potential factors contributing to charge variant formation, a comprehensive structural characterization by LC–MS analysis, including native SEC–MS, reduced RPLC–MS, and peptide mapping, was conducted on five fractions. Unfractionated ADC1 was included in all experiments as a control sample.

First, native SEC–MS was performed to monitor whether there were any differences between the five fractions at the intact level. Data were analyzed by PMI Byos, and deconvoluted masses and the peak assignment are shown in Figure 3.

Overlay of deconvoluted native SEC–MS mass spectra for unfractionated ADC1 and the five preparative iCIEF fractions. The spectra illustrate the distribution and enrichment of drug‐to‐antibody ratio (DAR) species (D0–D8) in each fraction, demonstrating effective charge‐based separation and showing detection of low‐abundance ADC species not readily observable in the unfractionated sample.

The unconjugated antibody (D0) and the conjugated ADCs with n drug molecules (Dn) were observed. The deconvoluted mass spectrum of the intact unfractionated ADC1 shows a broad drug distribution from D0 to D6, with D2 and D4 being the major forms. Fraction 1 was enriched with D0 and D2, Fraction 2 was mainly enriched with D2, Fraction 3 was mainly enriched with D4, Fraction 4 was predominantly enriched with D6, and Fraction 5 showed enrichment of D8 along with other trace molecules including D0, D2, D4, and D6. The detection of the D8 species, which was not detectable in the unfractionated sample due to low abundance, became possible through charge‐based fractionation.

Overall, the impact of drug load on ADC charge heterogeneity was demonstrated by native SEC–MS. Results indicated that the more drugs were loaded onto the molecule, the lower the molecule's pI became. Because the drug payload MMAF possesses the negatively charged carboxylic group, the higher drug load results in a more acidic pI.

Reduced RPLC–MS Analysis

Reduced RPLC–MS was performed to investigate PTMs and the number of drugs conjugated to the light and heavy chains. Both light and heavy chains were fully reduced using DTT and guanidine HCl and then analyzed by a high‐resolution mass spectrometer. Data were analyzed by PMI Byos and UV chromatograms, and deconvoluted masses are shown in Figure 4. Figure 4A shows the UV chromatogram, illustrating the separation of light and heavy chain species with different drug loads. Light chain with no drug conjugated (L0), light chain with one drug conjugated (L1), heavy chain with no drug conjugated (H0), heavy chain with one drug conjugated (H1), heavy chain with two drugs conjugated (H2), and heavy chain with three drugs conjugated (H3) were observed. For all heavy chain species, the glycoform G0F was shown as the major form with minor G1F form. Fraction 1 showed a dominant H0 peak, Fraction 2 showed prominent H0 and H1 peaks with a minor H2 peak, and Fraction 3 showed dominant L1 and H1 peaks with minor H2 and H3 peaks. UV traces of Fractions 4 and 5 in Figure 4A are very similar with slight difference. Fraction 4 was enriched with H2 and Fraction 5 was enriched with H3.

Reduced RPLC–MS analysis of preparative iCIEF fractions and unfractionated ADC1. (A) Overlay of UV chromatograms showing separation of light and heavy chain species with differing drug loads. (B–E) Deconvoluted mass spectra highlighting distribution of posttranslational modifications and drug conjugation states (H0–H3) across fractions, demonstrating enrichment patterns and the contributions of C‐terminal lysine variants to ADC1 charge heterogeneity.

Figure 4B–E shows deconvoluted masses of each heavy chain species. In the H0 deconvoluted mass spectra (Figure 3B), the predominant modification was C‐terminal lysine for Fraction 1, with the nominal mass shift of +128 Da from the main G0F peak. In the H1 deconvoluted mass spectra (Figure 3C), addition of C‐terminal lysine (+128 Da) from G0F and G1F was observed for Fraction 1, and low level of C‐terminal lysine from G0F was observed for Fraction 2 and Fraction 3. In the H2 deconvoluted mass spectra (Figure 3D), both Fractions 1 and 2 showed C‐terminal lysine variants from G0F and G1F. For the H3 mass spectra (Figure 3E), the addition of C‐terminal lysine from the main G0F peak was predominantly observed in Fraction 3. Fractions 4 and 5 showed either minimal or no C‐terminal lysine variants across heavy chain species. These reduced RPLC–MS results indicated that the main contributors to charge heterogeneity in ADC1 are the drug linker and C‐terminal lysine variants.

Peptide Mapping

Fractions were digested with trypsin/Lys‐C and subjected to peptide mapping analysis. Reduced peptide mapping enabled the identification and quantification of specific PTM differences among the five fractions. Detailed results of this analysis are presented in Table 2. Deamidation, oxidation, and C‐terminal lysine loss were mainly monitored. As shown in Table 2, there was no significant difference in deamidation levels between the fractions and the unfractionated sample. This observation suggests that the fractionation process, including sample preparation, mobilization, and peak collection, did not cause deamidation of the molecules. However, higher levels of methionine oxidation were observed in the five fractions compared to the unfractionated ADC1. Among five fractions, Fractions 3, 4, and 5 showed higher oxidation levels than Fractions 1 and 2. Previous studies have reported that HPLC UV detectors can induce photooxidation in proteins [36, 37]. Similar to these findings, we hypothesize that UV light exposure during the iCIEF fractionation may lead to generation of oxidation artifacts such as methionine oxidation. During iCIEF fraction collection, samples are mobilized through the capillary of the preparative cartridge and are exposed to UV light for detection. Thus, longer UV detection can lead to increased oxidation. Oxidation levels were generally lower in basic Fractions 1 and 2, as they eluted earlier than the main and acidic peaks and therefore experienced less UV exposure. In this experiment, increased oxidation was observed in methionine residues located in solvent‐exposed regions, which are known to be susceptible to this modification. C‐terminal lysine loss results were correlated with the reduced RPLC–MS results in Figure 4. Fraction 1, identified as the most basic, contained more C‐terminal lysine than the other fractions. In the main and acidic fractions (Fractions 3, 4, and 5), over 90% of the heavy chain C‐termini existed in a truncated form. Other PTMs, such as pyroglutamate and glycosylation, were monitored, but there was no noticeable difference observed (data not shown).

Concluding Remarks

In this work, we demonstrated the feasibility of isolating complex ADC charge variants using the iCIEF fractionation technique and characterizing these separated variants with LC–MS analysis. We emphasized the importance of optimizing sample preparation conditions to achieve stable mobilization in a pressure‐based mobilization system. The addition of an anodic stabilizer to the sample and the addition of glycerol to the mobilization solution to increase viscosity effectively reduced peak distortion and broadening during mobilization. Using the optimized iCIEF method, we successfully fractionated the complex ADC molecule, and reinjection experiments confirmed that even low‐abundance charge variants could be isolated. Furthermore, LC–MS characterization revealed the main differences between charge variants and highlighted any possible modification occurring during fractionation. ADCs are inherently complex, with charge variants attributable to PTMs on the mAb or to modifications on the linker‐payload. This iCIEF fractionation approach provides a solution for the comprehensive characterization of charge variants, helping elucidate their locations and associated modifications on these drugs.
