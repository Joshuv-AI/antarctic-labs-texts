Feasibility of Knee PROMs in Older Chinese Population: A Cross‐Sectional Comparative Study of WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS
Authors: Ziyang Dong, Yang Li, Shantao Zhang, Nan Li, Hua Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPatient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) are widely used to evaluate knee function and symptoms. WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS are commonly used knee‐specific PROMs, and their validity and reliability are well established. However, there is limited research focusing on their feasibility among older Chinese population. This study aimed to assess the feasibility of WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS among Chinese individuals aged 45 years and older, and to provide recommendations for their clinical application.MethodsQuestionnaires, including the three PROMs, informed consent forms, and demographic information, were distributed online to Chinese individuals aged 45 years and older. Study outcomes included completion time, feasibility scores, item skipping rate, PROM abandonment rate, total score of each PROM, and their intercorrelations.ResultsThe completion time for WOMAC was significantly longer than OKS, but shorter than KOOS (p < 0.001). Significantly higher feasibility score was observed in WOMAC (p = 0.001) and OKS (p < 0.001) than KOOS. Subgroup analyses showed that the older group took significantly longer to complete all PROMs than the younger group (p < 0.001). Completion time was shorter in the higher‐education group only for WOMAC (p = 0.001). Feasibility scores showed no age‐related differences; however, participants with higher education attained significantly greater scores on all three PROMs (p < 0.05). There was no significant difference in item skipping rates across the three PROMs (p > 0.05). However, the abandonment rate for KOOS and OKS was significantly higher than WOMAC (p < 0.001). Correlation analysis demonstrated strong correlations among the three PROMs, with an absolute value of the correlation coefficient larger than 0.8.ConclusionAll three PROMs demonstrated good feasibility and strong inter‐scale correlations in the older Chinese population. KOOS required the longest completion time, while WOMAC and OKS achieved higher feasibility scores. Higher educational level was associated with greater feasibility across all PROMs, whereas older age primarily prolonged completion time without significantly reducing feasibility.

Introduction

Focusing on patients' perspectives and quality of life, patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) are completed by patients themselves without the interference from clinicians or researchers, playing an essential role in capturing patients' perspectives on symptoms, function, and quality of life, thereby supporting patient‐centered care and treatment evaluation [1].

PROMs are now widely used in research and clinical practice of various diseases, including musculoskeletal disorders, cancer, gastrointestinal and neurological diseases, and mental health [2, 3]. In the field of joint surgery, PROMs such as Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and Oxford Knee Score (OKS) are commonly employed to assess knee symptoms and function for patients with knee conditions or those undergoing arthroplasty [4, 5, 6].

WOMAC, first published by Bellamy et al. [7] in 1988, is one of the most frequently used knee PROMs worldwide. KOOS was developed from WOMAC with a larger number of items by Roos et al. [8] in 1998, while OKS, a more concise score, was proposed by Dawson et al. [9] at Oxford University in 1998. These three PROMs are commonly used in knee evaluation, and their validity and reliability have been extensively tested in previous studies [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Cross‐cultural adaptations and validations, including their Chinese versions, have also been completed in recent years [12, 13, 14].

In clinical practice, however, the feasibility and application of PROMs, including knee‐specific PROMs, may be influenced by multiple factors, including respondent burden such as time required, questionnaire length, and complexity, as well as patient‐related factors such as health literacy, demographic characteristics, and socioeconomic status [15, 16, 17].

Among these factors, aging represents an important and non‐negligible determinant, particularly in the context of an aging population [18]. Previous studies have reported that the application of PROMs in older populations may be challenging, and completion often requires assistance from caregivers or healthcare providers [19, 20]. These difficulties may be related to age‐associated declines in cognitive function, which can affect patients' comprehension and response accuracy [21, 22], potentially leading to incomplete responses, increased missing data, or potential bias in PROM results. Therefore, in order to ensure the reliability and interpretability of clinical outcome assessment, the feasibility of PROMs is particularly important in older population.

There is currently limited research focusing on the feasibility of knee PROMs in the older Chinese population, an area we believe to be just as critical as their validity and reliability. Age‐related factors may influence patients' ability to engage with PROM questionnaires, potentially affecting completion rates, completion time, and questionnaire burden. In light of this, we conduct this study to evaluate the feasibility of commonly used knee PROMs, including WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS in the aged Chinese population (45 years of age and older), aiming to provide evidence to inform the selection and application of appropriate knee PROMs in clinical practice, particularly for older patients.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional observational study was approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (approval No. M2022220) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants (Figure 1).

Flowchart of study design.

Questionnaires consisting of informed consent, demographic information, and a simplified Chinese version of PROMs, including WOMAC [23], KOOS [13], and OKS [24] were distributed to aged Chinese individuals 45 years of age and older through a widely used web‐based survey platform in China. Participants were recruited from May 2022 to August 2022 through multiple channels, including posters with QR codes displayed in the hospital, invitations distributed via the department's WeChat account, and additional promotion through social media.

Participants were given the option to skip an individual item or abandon a specific PROM if they found them difficult to understand. To ensure data quality, the platform automatically monitored IP addresses and device identifiers, and suspicious duplicate entries were manually checked and removed. Questionnaires with incomplete demographic information, duplicate responses, or total completion time shorter than 2 min or longer than 1 h were excluded prior to analysis.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

1. Individuals aged 45 years or older.

2. Individuals with Chinese nationality and speak Chinese as native language.

3. Regardless of the presence or absence of knee diseases or symptoms.

Exclusion Criteria

1. Individuals who refuse to accept or sign the informed consent for any reason.

2. Individuals with insufficient cognitive or reading abilities due to below‐normal intelligence levels.

3. Individuals with severe organic diseases or mental illnesses.

Outcomes and Measures

Demographic Information

The general information collected in this study includes gender, age, education level, and the presence of knee pain. Participants were therefore categorized into middle‐aged group (45–60 years of age) and the older age group (60 years of age and older), the low education level group (below bachelor's degree) and the high education level group (bachelor's degree or above), the group with knee pain and the group without knee pain.

Study Outcomes

Completion time of each PROM in seconds was recorded, which is considered to be an important outcome as it reflects questionnaire burden. Completion time in different groups of age and education level was calculated and compared.

The feasibility score is a novel outcome in our study, provided directly by the participant. At the end of each PROM, participants were asked to rate their understanding of the items on a scale from 0 to 10, where a higher score indicates greater ease of understanding. Feasibility scores were also analyzed across different age and education level groups.

The item skipping rate of items in each PROM and the abandonment rate of each PROM were also calculated.

Item skipping rate = number of skipped items/(number of total items × number of participants finishing one PROM);

PROM Abandonment rate = number of participants who abandon certain PROM/number of all participants

The total scores of these three PROMs and their correlation were evaluated. The correlation coefficients among these three PROMs were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Variables approximately following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, those with evident non‐normality as median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Given the large sample size, which enhances the robustness of parametric tests, and the approximately normal distributions indicated by Q–Q plots, one‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA was performed to compare completion times and feasibility scores among the three PROMs based on data from participants who completed all three PROMs. Mauchly's test was used to assess sphericity, and when violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was adopted. Post‐hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment.

Subgroup comparisons in completion time and feasibility scores were conducted using independent t‐tests with all available data. Subgroup comparisons in total scores were conducted by Mann–Whitney U tests based on data without skipped items. Skipping rates were compared using Pearson's χ² tests with all available data. Abandonment rates were compared using Cochran's Q test, followed by pairwise comparisons using McNemar tests with Bonferroni correction. Correlations among total scores were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients with 95% confidence intervals using data from participants without PROM abandonment and item skipping. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Demographic Information

A total number of 1028 participants with valid demographic information were enrolled in this study, while 799 of which finish all these three PROMs in the questionnaire. The questionnaires were collected from 32 provinces, autonomous regions, or municipalities in China. 448 participants (43.58%) were male, 580 participants (56.42%) were female. 547 participants (53.21%) were 45–60 years old, and 481 participants (46.79%) were at least 60 years old. 474 participants (46.11%) had an education level below a bachelor's degree, and 554 participants (53.89%) had a bachelor's degree or above. 705 participants (68.58%) had pain of knee joint, 323 participants (31.42%) did not.

In addition, “How often is your knee painful” was the most frequently skipped item in KOOS (11.76%), and “For how long have you been able to walk before the pain from your knee becomes severe” was the most frequently skipped item in OKS (30.56%).

Total Scores and Their Correlation

In terms of total scores, WOMAC showed a very strong inverse correlation with KOOS (ρ = −0.896, 95% CI −0.910 to −0.879, p < 0.001) and with OKS (ρ = −0.857, 95% CI −0.877 to −0.835, p < 0.001), whereas KOOS correlated positively with OKS (ρ = 0.905, 95% CI 0.890–0.918, p < 0.001) (n = 668, after exclusion of participants with PROM abandonment and items skipping).

Participants with knee pain and those in the older age group both exhibited significantly higher WOMAC scores but lower KOOS and OKS scores compared with participants without knee pain (p < 0.001) (Table 7).

Discussion

Aging may lead to a decline in the feasibility of PROMs due to diminished capacity of text comprehension [22, 25]. Bernstein et al. [26] find that older patients (≥ 65 years of age) have a 23% increased likelihood of not completing assigned PROMs. Schamber et al. [27] also consider aging (over 75 years old) as a risk factor for lower completion rates. However, there is currently few research studying the effects of aging on the feasibility of PROMs in the Chinese population. Given the accelerating aging process and the large aging population in China, we conducted this study to further evaluate PROM feasibility in this demographic.

Questionnaire burden is a key factor influencing the feasibility of PROMs. An increase in questionnaire burden may make it more difficult for especially aged people to concentrate on the items and complete the questionnaire, leading to lower completion rates and longer completion time. The results of this study indicated that OKS had the shortest completion time, which represents the lightest questionnaire burden. The rank of completion time was consistent with the rank of item number of each PROM (24 items for WOMAC, 42 items for KOOS, and 12 items for OKS). In all these three PROMs, the average completion time was approximately 2–5 min which seems to be acceptable and time‐friendly. In addition, the average completion time of WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS was shorter than the time reported in previous studies, which might be associated with the convenience and accessibility of online questionnaires.

The feasibility score is an outcome ranging from 0 to 10 points. This subjective score was provided by the participants based on their understanding level of the items in each PROM. Significantly higher feasibility scores were observed in WOMAC and OKS compared to KOOS, suggesting that these two questionnaires are easier for patients to comprehend and complete, which may be partly attributed to differences in the number of items.

In terms of age, this study found that participants in the older group (≥ 60 years) required significantly longer completion time than those in the younger group (45–60 years), indicating that aging individuals may have slower reading or response speed. However, no significant difference in feasibility scores was observed among different age groups for WOMAC, KOOS, or OKS, suggesting that older participants were able to understand and complete the questionnaires as effectively as their younger counterparts.

Education level is another factor reflecting the feasibility of PROMs. The completion time of WOMAC was significantly longer in the group of lower education level, while participants with higher educational levels showed significantly higher feasibility scores across all three PROMs, indicating that educational background may play an important role in influencing questionnaire comprehension.

In this study, participants were allowed to skip specific items if they found them difficult to understand or challenging to answer. Although the overall skipping rate did not differ significantly among the three PROMs, we further analyzed the most frequently skipped item within each questionnaire. In WOMAC, the item “getting in or out of bath” was the most frequently skipped. A bathtub is relatively rare in most Chinese families, making it difficult for aged Chinese participants to answer. This reminds us that we should pay attention to not only language translation but also the conversion of lifestyle in cross‐cultural adaptation of PROMs. In KOOS and OKS, the items “how often is your knee painful” and “for how long have you been able to walk before the pain from your knee becomes severe” were reported as the most challenging to answer. Some participants don't have regular knee pain, so it may be difficult for them to determine an appropriate response regarding frequency. The readability and ease of understanding need to be improved in future PROMs to avoid any issues of ambiguity.

We also observed that some participants left entire PROMs unanswered, which we defined as the abandonment of the PROM. Our findings revealed that the abandonment rate of KOOS and OKS was significantly higher than WOMAC. This finding further supports the good feasibility and acceptability of WOMAC among elderly respondents, although the potential influence of PROM order cannot be completely excluded, as WOMAC was administered before KOOS and OKS.

All three PROMs are found to be effective in our research. Correlation analysis demonstrated strong correlations among the three PROMs, with an absolute value of the correlation coefficient larger than 0.8. Additionally, the total scores of the PROMs are consistently worse in participants in the high‐age group and the group with knee pain, suggesting that all three PROMs can sensitively reflect functional decline associated with aging and symptom severity.

This study has several limitations. First, although participants were recruited from 32 provinces across China, all responses were collected through online platforms, which may have introduced selection bias, as individuals with higher education or better digital literacy were more likely to participate. Nevertheless, online recruitment allowed broad nationwide coverage, and subgroup analyses by age and education level were conducted to assess the potential impact of this bias. Second, the feasibility score, defined as participants' ability to understand PROM items, was a novel indicator first proposed in this study, and further validation is required to confirm its sensitivity and reliability. Third, as the data were self‐reported and collected through an online survey, certain participant characteristics could not be independently verified, and the possibility of response bias or inaccuracy in recorded completion time cannot be excluded. Finally, although the three PROMs were displayed together on the same online survey page in a fixed order, participants were free to choose which questionnaire to complete first. However, most participants might tend to answer them sequentially, and thus, loss of concentration could contribute to higher abandonment rates in the later PROMs. Future studies combining online and in‐person recruitment with randomized questionnaire order are warranted to minimize bias and enhance generalizability.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the feasibility of knee‐specific PROMs among the Chinese population aged 45 years and older. All three PROMs (WOMAC, KOOS, and OKS) demonstrated good feasibility and strong inter‐scale correlations in the older Chinese population. OKS required the least completion time, while WOMAC and KOOS achieved higher feasibility scores. Higher educational level was associated with greater feasibility across all PROMs, whereas older age primarily prolonged completion time without significantly reducing feasibility.

Although these findings may appear expected, they provide important evidence within the context of the Chinese population, where variations in educational background and digital literacy may influence patients' engagement with PROMs. Understanding these factors is valuable for optimizing the use of PROMs in clinical practice, particularly when assessing outcomes in older patients. For example, shorter PROMs such as OKS may be more suitable when questionnaire burden is a concern, while more comprehensive tools such as WOMAC and KOOS may still be appropriate when detailed symptom evaluation is required.

Despite the exploratory nature of this study, the present results provide preliminary but valuable evidence regarding the feasibility of knee‐specific PROMs in the older Chinese population and may help inform the selection and application of appropriate PROMs in both clinical practice and research settings. Further research in larger and more diverse clinical populations is warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.
