On the Generalization of Adversarially Trained Quantum Classifiers
Petros Georgiou, Aaron Mark Thomas, Sharu Theresa Jose, Osvaldo Simeone

Abstract
Quantum classifiers are vulnerable to adversarial attacks that manipulate their input classical or quantum data. A promising countermeasure is adversarial training, where quantum classifiers are trained by using an attack-aware, adversarial loss function. This work establishes novel bounds on the generalization error of adversarially trained quantum classifiers when tested in the presence of perturbation-constrained adversaries. The bounds quantify the excess generalization error incurred to ensure robustness to adversarial attacks as scaling with the training sample size m m as 1 / m 1/\sqrt{m} , while yielding insights into the impact of the quantum embedding. For quantum binary classifiers employing rotation embedding , we find that, in the presence of adversarial attacks on classical inputs 𝐱 \mathbf{x} , the increase in sample complexity due to adversarial training over conventional training vanishes in the limit of high dimensional inputs 𝐱 \mathbf{x} . In contrast, when the adversary can directly attack the quantum state ρ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) \rho(\mathbf{x}) encoding the input 𝐱 \mathbf{x} , the excess generalization error depends on the choice of embedding only through its Hilbert space dimension. The results are also extended to multi-class classifiers. We validate our theoretical findings with numerical experiments.

Quantum Classifiers and Quantum Embeddings
: To elaborate, consider a quantum binary classifier that takes as input a

\mathbf{x}

determines the model class, and the specific observable

The choice of the quantum embedding

-based methods, in which information is encoded via the rotation angles of qubit gates. These methods, encompassing angle embedding, dense embedding, and their repeated variants

In contrast, with

\mathbf{x}
directly modulates the quantum amplitudes of the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})

\mathbf{x}
of dimension

scales linearly with
d
. On the flip side, the circuit implementing amplitude embedding generally requires a number of quantum gates that is exponential in the dimension

[
17
]
.

The impact of the choice of quantum embedding on the generalization of conventionally trained classifiers has been studied in
[
19
]
. This reference quantifies the generalization error via the number of encoding gates used in the circuit. In particular, for an
L

\mathcal{O}(L^{d})
. To the best of our knowledge, generalization for amplitude encoding has not been explicitly addressed in the literature.

Research Questions:
Different from classical ML models, adversaries acting against QML models can attack data either in the classical space, perturbing the input
\mathbf{x}
within an
l_{p}
-ball of some radius
\epsilon
, or in the quantum state space, perturbing the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
within a
p
-Schatten norm ball of
\epsilon
-radius
[
21
]
. Accordingly, we study the
adversarial generalization error
– defined as the difference between the adversarial training loss and the test loss – for adversarially trained QML models with angle or amplitude embeddings under classical or quantum attacks. Specifically, we address the following key questions:

•

How is the sample complexity of a QML model affected by the presence of an attacker in the ideal case in which the training process is aware of the limitations of the attacker?

•

How does the choice of the quantum embedding affect adversarial generalization?

•

What is the impact of the type of attack, classical or quantum, on adversarial generalization?

We answer these questions by providing novel probably approximately correct (PAC) bounds on the adversarial generalization error via the
adversarial Rademacher complexity
(ARC). This extends the standard Rademacher complexity (RC)-based PAC bounds presented in
[
23
]
to the adversarial setting.

Aside from our conference paper
[
24

[
25

13

26

II
Quantum Classifiers

Let
(\mathbf{x},y)
denote the (classical) data tuple of feature vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and output label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
where
\mathcal{Y}=\{+1,-1\}
for binary classification and
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
for
K
-class classification. For quantum data, the input feature is a quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
indexed by the parameter vector
\mathbf{x}
of the state, and
y
denotes the corresponding label.

A quantum classifier is described by a mapping
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
from the input feature space to the label space. When the data are classical, the classifier first maps the input vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
to a quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
in a
d_{H}
-dimensional Hilbert space via the quantum embedding
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
. This step can be skipped if the data are quantum. Note that the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
is a
density matrix
, i.e., a unit-trace, positive semi-definite,
d_{H}\times d_{H}
Hermitian matrix.

The classifier measures the embedded quantum state using a suitable Hermitian observable
A
. Throughout this work, we consider a
model class
of classifiers defined by the set of observables

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{r}

\displaystyle=\{A:A=A^{\dagger},\|A\|_{r}\leq b\},

(2)

i.e., the set of
r
-Schatten norm constrained Hermitian matrices of dimension
d_{H}\times d_{H}
. For
r\in[1,\infty)
, the Schatten norm of observable
A
is defined as
\|A\|_{r}=({\rm Tr}(|A|^{r}))^{1/r}
with
|A|=\sqrt{A^{{\dagger}}A}
, which in the limiting case of
r=\infty
, evaluates as
\|A\|_{\infty}=\max\{\lambda(|A|)\}
with
\{\lambda(|A|)\}
denoting the set of eigenvalues of
|A|
.

The quantum embedding and the measurement define a binary quantum classifier as
[
41
]

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={\rm sgn}(f(\mathbf{x})),\quad\mbox{with}\quad f(\mathbf{x})={\rm Tr}(A\rho(\mathbf{x})),

(3)

where the real-valued function
f(\mathbf{x})
computes the expectation of the Hermitian observable
A
with respect to the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
, with
{\rm Tr}(\cdot)
denoting the trace operation. This is then post-processed via the function
{\rm sgn}(a)
, which takes value
+1
when
a>0
and takes
-1
when
a<0
, to yield the class label.

For fixed quantum embedding
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
, the class
\mathcal{G}_{r}
of binary quantum classifiers can be then defined as
\mathcal{G}_{r}=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto g(\mathbf{x})={\rm sgn}(f(\mathbf{x})):f\in\mathcal{F}_{r}\}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{r}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})={\rm Tr}(A\rho(\mathbf{x})):A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\}.

(4)

More generally, a
K
-class quantum classifier can be defined via
K
observables
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
as

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}f_{k}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{with}\hskip 2.84544ptf_{k}(\mathbf{x})={\rm Tr}(A_{k}\rho(\mathbf{x}))

(5)

for
k\in[K]=\{1,\ldots,K\}
. The resulting class of quantum classifiers is
\mathcal{G}_{r}=\{\mathbf{x}\mapsto g(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}f_{k}(\mathbf{x}):f_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{r}\hskip 2.84544pt\text{for all }k\in[K]\}
.

Remark 1:
The definition of quantum classifiers above assumes a general class
\mathcal{A}_{r}
of norm-bounded observables. In practice, the observable
A
is implemented by leveraging parameterized unitary gates as in quantum neural networks. For instance, in binary classification, the observable
A
takes the form
A=UZ_{1}U^{{\dagger}}
where
U
denotes a tunable, parameterized unitary gate and
Z_{1}
denotes a (fixed) Pauli-Z measurement on the first qubit. Such parameterized observables form a subset of the set
\mathcal{A}_{r}
. ∎

Before defining the adversarial generalization error of the quantum classifiers, following Section 1 and Figure
2
, we first discuss conventional methods for embedding classical data into quantum states. As we will see, the choice of the embedding scheme is crucial for adversarial generalization.

II.1
Quantum Embedding Schemes

We now describe the quantum embeddings
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
that will be studied in this work. Pure-state embeddings are of the form
\rho(\mathbf{x})=|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle\langle\psi(\mathbf{x})|
, where
|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{H}}
is a
d_{H}
-dimensional complex vector with unit norm.

Amplitude embedding
describes the mapping

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\mapsto|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}}|i\rangle,

(6)

that encodes the normalized feature entries to the amplitudes of computational basis states
|i\rangle
for
i\in[d]
. Implementing this embedding requires
n=\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil
qubits, resulting in a
d_{H}=2^{n}=2^{\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}
-dimensional Hilbert space
[
42
]
.

Angle rotation embedding
uses
n=d
qubits to encode the
d
-dimensional input
\mathbf{x}
. Specifically, the
i
-th entry of the feature vector
\mathbf{x}
is encoded into the
i
-th qubit via a Pauli rotation. Using Pauli-Y rotations, this is achieved via the application of a
d
-qubit unitary gate
U(\mathbf{x})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}e^{-ix_{j}\sigma_{Y}}
, with
\sigma_{Y}
denoting the Pauli-Y matrix, on an initial state
|0\rangle
as
|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle=U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle
. The resulting embedded quantum state is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\mapsto|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle=U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle.

Dense rotation embedding
uses
n=d/2
qubits to encode two feature entries into each qubit via the unitary matrix
U(\mathbf{x})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d/2}e^{-ix_{2j}\sigma_{Z}}e^{-ix_{2j-1}\sigma_{Y}}
with
\sigma_{Z}
denoting Pauli-Z matrix
[
21
]
. The resulting embedded quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
can be obtained as in angle rotation embedding.

Finally,
L
-layer rotation embedding
schemes obtain the quantum state
|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle
by applying the rotation unitary
U(\mathbf{x})
followed by a fixed unitary
V^{(l)}

L
-times. This results in

\displaystyle|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle=V^{(L)}U(\mathbf{x})\ldots V^{(2)}U(\mathbf{x})V^{(1)}U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle.

(7)

Such repeated encoding schemes that facilitate data re-uploading are known to yield highly expressive quantum models
[
43
]
.

Having defined quantum embeddings and set
\mathcal{A}_{r}
of observables that define the quantum classifier, we now define the generalization error of quantum classifiers.

III
Generalization Error: Learning-Theoretic Preliminaries

Let each data tuple
(\mathbf{x},y)
be sampled from an unknown data distribution
P(\mathbf{x},y)
.
A learner observes a data set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m},y_{m})\}
of
m
examples, generated i.i.d according to the distribution
P(\mathbf{x},y)
, and uses it to learn a quantum classifier
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.

III.1
Non-Adversarial Generalization Error

In the non-adversarial setting,
the performance of the quantum classifier
g
on a data point
(\mathbf{x},y)
is measured using a non-negative loss function
\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}),y)
. The goal of the learner is to find the classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
that minimizes the
population risk
,

\displaystyle L(g)=\mathbb{E}_{P(\mathbf{x},y)}[\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}),y)],

which is the expected loss over test data tuples drawn from
P(\mathbf{x},y)
. Since this cannot be evaluated due to the unknown data distribution
P(\mathbf{x},y)
, the learner instead minimizes the
empirical
risk,

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(g)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i}),

(8)

evaluated on the observed data set
\mathcal{D}
. The difference between the population risk and empirical risk is the
generalization error
,

\displaystyle G(g)=L(g)-\widehat{L}(g),

(9)

of the classifier
g
.

Understanding the generalization error of ML models is the central question of statistical learning theory
[
44
]
and there exists different approaches to quantify it. In this work, we resort to the
probably approximately correct
(PAC) framework that characterizes the generalization error via RC as in the theorem below.

Theorem 1
.

[
44
]

Assume that the loss function
\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}),y)
is
[0,B]
-bounded. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for
\delta\in(0,1)
, with respect to the random draws of dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the generalization error
G(g)
of any classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle G(g)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})+3B\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\log\frac{2}{\delta}},\quad\mbox{where}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Bigl[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i})\Bigr]

(11)

is the empirical RC of class
\mathcal{G}_{r}
evaluated on dataset
\mathcal{D}
, with
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
denoting the vector of i.i.d Rademacher variables
\sigma_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}
taking values
\pm 1
with equal probability.

Theorem
1
shows that the Rademacher complexity of the class
\mathcal{G}_{r}
of classifiers can bound the generalization error with high probability.

III.2
Adversarial Generalization Error

We now consider the quantum classification problem in an adversarial setting. Specifically, we consider
white-box adversaries
that have access to the classifier
g(\cdot)
as well as the loss function
\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}),y)
. Depending on the data space that the adversary attacks, we identify two distinct types of adversarial attacks: classical and quantum adversarial attacks. These are depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of a quantum classifier being subject to classical (top) and quantum (bottom) adversarial attacks.

A
classical adversary
perturbs the input feature vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
to an adversarial feature
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying the constraint

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon,

(12)

such that it maximizes the loss
\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),y)
incurred by the classifier
g

[
21
]
. We call the above attack a
(p,\epsilon)
-classical attack
since the adversary performs an
l_{p}
-norm perturbation of the clean input vector within a budget of
\epsilon>0
.

In contrast, a
quantum adversary
directly perturbs the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
to an adversarial quantum state
\rho^{\prime}
, that satisfies the
p
-Schatten norm constraint

\displaystyle\|\rho(\mathbf{x})-\rho^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon,

(13)

to maximize the classifier’s loss. We call the resulting attack a
(p,\epsilon)
-quantum adversarial attack
, which assumes that the adversary has direct access to the quantum state
\rho(\mathbf{x})
.

The attacks defined above incur the following
adversarial loss
for
(p,\epsilon)
-classical attack,

\displaystyle\ell^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{x},y)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),y),\quad\mbox{and}

(14)

\displaystyle\ell^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{x},y)

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho^{\prime}:\|\rho(\mathbf{x})-\rho^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\ell(g,\rho^{\prime},y),

(15)

for the
(p,\epsilon)
-quantum attack. The superscript
C/Q
on the adversarial loss denotes if it is under classical or quantum adversary.

Assuming that the learner is aware of the presence of the adversary, their goal is to find the classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
that minimizes the classical/quantum
adversarial population risk

\displaystyle L^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{P(\mathbf{x},y)}[\ell_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q}(g,\mathbf{x},y)].

Since the adversarial population risk cannot be evaluated, the learner instead resorts to
adversarial training
via minimizing the
adversarial empirical risk
,

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i}).

The
(p,\epsilon)
-classical/quantum
adversarial generalization error
is then defined as

G_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q}(g)=L^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)-\widehat{L}^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g),

the difference between the adversarial population and adversarial empirical risks.

To characterize the adversarial generalization error, one can straightforwardly extend Theorem
1
to adversarial setting. This results in an upper bound on the adversarial generalization error
G^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)
under classical or quantum adversary via the ARC,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Biggl[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{r}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\ell^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\Biggr].

(16)

This poses the challenge of analyzing the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})
of the class
\mathcal{G}_{r}
of quantum binary or multi-class classifiers.
To this end, we resort to covering number-based analysis
[
27
,
23
]
, deriving the novel bounds on the ARC of binary classifiers defined in the next section.

IV
Adversarial Rademacher Complexity of Binary Classifiers

In this section, we study the adversarial generalization of binary quantum classifiers of the form
g(\mathbf{x})={\rm sgn}(f(\mathbf{x}))
as defined in (
3
).
Following
[
13
]
and
[
26
]
, we consider the following loss function

\displaystyle\ell(g,\rho(\mathbf{x}),y)=\phi(yf(\mathbf{x})),

(17)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,B]
is a
[0,B]
-bounded, monotonically non-increasing,
\eta

\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})
of this class of classifiers.

IV.1
Non-adversarial Rademacher Complexity Bounds

In the non-adversarial setting, the Rademacher complexity (
11
) of the class
\mathcal{G}_{r}
of binary classifiers
g(\mathbf{x})={\rm sgn}(f(\mathbf{x}))
under loss function (

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Biggl[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{r}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\phi(y_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i}))\Biggr].

Since
\phi(\cdot)
is
\eta
-Lipschitz,
\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell\circ\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\leq\eta\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{with}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Biggl[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\Biggr],

(18)

denoting the RC of the function class
\mathcal{F}_{r}
in (

The following theorem upper bounds the RC

For quantum binary classifiers, the empirical RC

m

IV.2
Adversarial Rademacher Complexity Bounds

We start by analyzing the ARC (
16
) under classical attacks.
Note that since the function
\phi(\cdot)
is monotonically non-increasing, the ARC under classical attack can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Biggl[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{r}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi\Bigl(\min_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}y_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\Bigr)\Biggr].

Upper bounding via Talagrand’s contraction inequality
[
45
]
yields,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})

\displaystyle\leq\eta\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Biggl[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}h(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\eta\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}),

(19)

where
\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}
is the adversarial function class defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{(\mathbf{x},y)\mapsto h(\mathbf{x},y)=\hskip-5.69046pt\min_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}yf(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}):f\in\mathcal{F}_{r}\Bigr\}.

Similarly, under quantum attack, we get that
\mathcal{R}(\ell^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})\leq\eta\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{Q})
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{Q}=\Bigl\{(\mathbf{x},y)\mapsto h(\mathbf{x},y)

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.69046pt\min_{\rho^{\prime}:\|\rho(\mathbf{x})-\rho^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}y{\rm Tr}(A\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle:A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}\Bigr\}.

(20)

The following theorem presents an upper bound on the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q})
of the adversarial function class
\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q}
via covering number-based analysis. The proof is deferred to Section

Theorem 3

The ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q})
of adversarial function class
\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q}
, evaluated on
m
-sample dataset
\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q})\leq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})+\frac{b\mathcal{S}^{Q/C}_{r,p,\epsilon}J(r)}{\sqrt{m}},

(21)

where
J(r)
scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{1+1/r})

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}

\displaystyle=d_{H}\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\|\rho(\mathbf{x})-\rho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|_{\frac{r}{r-1}},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{Q}

\displaystyle=d_{H}\sup_{\rho(\mathbb{x}),\rho^{\prime}:\|\rho(\mathbb{x})-\rho^{\prime}\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\|\rho(\mathbb{x})-\rho^{\prime}\|_{\frac{r}{r-1}}

(23)

Proof.

Proof can be found in Appendix

The bound in (
21
) shows that
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C/Q})
is upper bounded by the sum of the RC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})
of non-adversarial function class
\mathcal{F}_{r}
and an additional term, the
excess Rademacher complexity
due to adversarial training, scaling as
\mathcal{O}(b\mathcal{S}^{Q/C}_{r,p,\epsilon}/\sqrt{m})
. In the absence of adversarial attacks (i.e.,
\epsilon=0
), the excess RC vanishes since
\mathcal{S}^{Q/C}_{r,p,\epsilon}=0
. For
\epsilon>0
and under classical adversarial attack, the constant
\mathcal{S}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}
in (
22
) is a measure of
smoothness
of the quantum embedding
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
to classical adversarial perturbations scaled by the dimension
d_{H}

[
21
]
. Thus, if the quantum embedding chosen has good scaled adversarial smoothness, it results in a lower value of
\mathcal{S}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}
, and thus lower adversarial generalization error.
In contrast, under quantum attacks the choice of quantum embedding only determines the dimension
d_{H}
, as can be seen from (
23
).

IV.2.1
Impact of Quantum Embeddings

We now discuss some of the insights gained from the upper bound in Theorem
3
about the choice of quantum embedding on adversarial generalization. To this end, the following
proposition upper bounds the scaled excess RC
\mathcal{S}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C}
for the quantum embeddings introduced in Section
II.1
.

Proposition 1
.

The scaled excess RC
\mathcal{S}^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}
can be upper bounded under amplitude,
L
-layer rotation and dense embeddings respectively as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}2^{1+\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}\min\Big\{\frac{\epsilon\max\{1,d^{1/2-1/p}\}}{\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lVert x\rVert_{2}},1\Big\}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{(amplitude)},\\
2L(2\epsilon)^{d}d^{-d/p}\hskip 11.38092pt\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{(L-layer angle)},\\
2L(2\sqrt{2}\epsilon)^{d/2}\max\{d^{-d/4},d^{-d/2p}\}\hskip 8.5359pt\mbox{(L-layer dense)}.\end{cases}

The proposition above derives the behavior of the scaled excess RC in (
21
) under various quantum embeddings as detailed in Table
1
. This in turn can be used to study the impact of the quantum embedding on the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r,p,\epsilon}^{C})
and thus on the adversarial generalization error.

Specifically, under
classical attacks
, we have the following observations.
In the
high-dimensional
regime, where
d\gg e^{\max\{2,p\}\ln(2\sqrt{2}\epsilon)-1}
, rotation embeddings incur negligible excess RC, requiring a vanishingly small increase in sample complexity to ensure the adversarial generalization to be on par with standard non-adversarial generalization. Indeed, for rotation embeddings, the excess RC
decays at least exponentially with the data dimension
d
, vanishing in the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
. This implies that, in these regimes, one can leverage the high expressivity of repeated rotation embeddings while incurring a negligible increase in the sample complexity caused by the possible presence of an adversary.

In contrast, for quantum classifiers that employ
amplitude embedding
, the ARC has an unavoidable dimensional dependence which is at most linear in
d
.

Remark 2:
The aim of adversarial training is to minimize the adversarial population risk. This is bounded via the ARC with probability
1-\delta
as

\displaystyle L^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)\leq\widehat{L}^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}(g)+2\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G}_{r})+3B\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}

Our analysis can assess which embeddings yield low adversarial Rademacher complexity, but cannot guarantee that they also minimize the
empirical risk
. Indeed, in the non-adversarial setting, it has been shown that minimizing the empirical risk and the generalization error are competing processes with regards to the choice of quantum embedding
[
20
]
. We leave it to future work to assess how to choose an embedding which jointly minimizes the adversarial empirical risk and generalization error.∎

Finally, when the adversary employs
quantum attacks
, the excess RC scales at least linearly with the dimension of the Hilbert space. For rotation embeddings, this implies an exponential dependence on the data dimension
d
, whilst amplitude embedding based classifiers incur an additional term with at most quadratic dependence on
d
.

IV.2.2
Tighter Bounds for Noisy Embeddings

In this section, we focus on quantum adversarial attacks and derive tighter bounds on the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})
, under the following restricted class of noisy embeddings studied in
[
24
]
.

Assumption 1
.

The quantum embedding
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
is such that the minimum eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))\geq\epsilon
.

Assumption
1
is restrictive as it naturally excludes all pure quantum states.
However, it may reflect well the quantum embeddings produced by NISQ quantum devices.
Under Assumption
1
, the following theorem gives upper and lower bounds on the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a
(p,\epsilon)
-quantum adversary for
p\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
. Under Assumption
1
, the ARC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})
satisfies the inequalities

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})\leq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})\leq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}_{r})+\frac{b\epsilon\max\{1,d_{H}^{1-1/p-1/r}\}}{\sqrt{m}}.

(24)

Proof.

Proof can be found in Appendix
F
.
∎

Assumption
1
allows us to evaluate a tighter upper bound on the ARC because in this regime, the adversarial perturbations are universal within each class. In fact, the set of adversarial perturbations corresponding to input
\rho(\mathbf{x})
, defined as

P_{\succeq 0}=\{\tau:\tau+\rho(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0,\rm{Tr}(\tau)=0,\lVert\tau\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon\}

becomes equivalent to the set

P=\{\tau:\rm{Tr}(\tau)=0,\lVert\tau\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon\},

which is independent of
\mathbf{x}
. This allows us to employ Hölder’s inequality which together with the supremum over the space of observables
\mathcal{A}_{r}
allows for an exact evaluation of the excess RC. On the contrary, when Assumption
1
is not satisfied, we need to resort to a covering number analysis which gives an upper bound on the excess RC.

Theorem
4
shows that the RC
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})
under quantum attacks is at least as high as that of the non-adversarial function class
\mathcal{F}_{r}
, for a class of quantum embeddings satisfying Assumption
1
. Thus, for these embeddings, uniform convergence in the adversarial setting is provably
at least as hard
as in the non-adversarial setting.
Additionally, note that the upper bound in Theorem
4
has a tighter dimensional dependence than the one in (
21
), scaling
at most
linearly with
d_{H}
. Indeed, this dimensional dependence can be avoided if
r\geq p/(p-1).

Finally, we like to remark that finding lower bounds on
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon})
is in general challenging. For classical neural networks
[
14
]
, lower bounds on the ARC are conventionally derived by appropriately restricting the function class
\mathcal{F}^{C}_{r,p,\epsilon}
. However, a similar approach does not extend to quantum classifiers subject to classical attacks, due to the non-linearity in
\mathbb{x}
induced by the embedding
\mathbb{x}\mapsto\rho(\mathbf{x})
.
Additionally, under quantum attacks, the derivation of lower bounds must ensure that the adversarial state is a physical density matrix – a positive semi-definite, unit-trace, Hermitian matrix. The positive semi-definite condition
\rho^{\prime}\succeq 0
in turn requires the evaluation of the eigenvalues of
\rho^{\prime}
, which is a polynomial equation of order
d_{H}
. This has no general analytic solutions for
d_{H}>4
(more than 2 qubits).

VI
Extension to Multi-Class Classifiers

We now extend the previous results to study adversarial generalization of
K
-class quantum classifiers defined in (
5
). To this end, we follow the standard margin bound framework used in classical literature
[
13
,
26
]
. For
K
-class classification problems, let
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=[f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{K}(\mathbf{x})]
denote the
K
-length vector-valued function, whose
k
th entry corresponds to the score
f_{k}(x)={\rm Tr}(A_{k}\rho(\mathbf{x}))
assigned to the
k
th class.
The final predicted label is then obtained as
g(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}f_{k}(x).

Denote
M(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)=f_{y}(\mathbf{x})-\max_{{k\neq y}}f_{k}(\mathbf{x})
as the margin operator that quantifies the difference between the
y
-th class score and the best score assigned to other classes. For a training example
(\mathbf{x},y)
,
\mathbf{f}
makes correct prediction only if
M(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)>0
. We consider the following loss function
[
13
,
26
,
14
]
,

\displaystyle\ell(g,\mathbf{x},y)=\phi_{\gamma}(M(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}),y)),

(26)

where
\gamma>0
and
\phi_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
is the
1/\gamma
-Lipschitz, ramp loss defined as

\phi_{\gamma}(t)=1\mathbb{I}\{t\leq 0\}+(1-\frac{t}{\gamma})\mathbb{I}\{0<t<\gamma\},
where
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function.

The following theorem gives an upper bound on the ARC.

Theorem 5
.

The ARC of
K
-class margin-based quantum classifier can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\ell^{C/Q}_{r,p,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{2K}{\gamma}\mathcal{R}(\Pi(\mathcal{F}_{K}))+\frac{Kb\mathcal{S}^{Q/C}_{r,p,\epsilon}J^{\prime}(r)}{\gamma\sqrt{m}}

(27)

where
\mathcal{R}(\Pi(\mathcal{F}_{K}))=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\sigma}}\Bigl[\sup_{k\in[K],f_{k}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}f_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\Bigr]
can be upper bounded as in Theorem
2
and
J^{\prime}(r)
scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{1+1/r})
.

Proof.

Proof can be found in Appendix
G
.
∎

Theorem
5
extends the main inference from binary to
K
-class classification with an additional multiplicative factor of
K
on the generalization error bounds.

VIII
Conclusion and Future Works

This paper has derived novel generalization error bounds for adversarially trained quantum classifiers via an adversarial Rademacher complexity analysis. Our bounds quantify the increase in sample complexity incurred due to adversarial training against classical and quantum adversaries, while also highlighting the impact of the choice of quantum embedding. We investigated how conventional choices of quantum embedding schemes affect adversarial generalization under classical attacks and find that in the limit of high-dimensional input
\mathbf{x}
rotation embedding generalize equally well (asymptotically) to classifiers undergoing standard training.

In the case of quantum attacks we have also provided tighter bounds on the adversarial Rademacher complexity for a restricted class of embeddings, which apply to all
p
-Schatten norm attacks, improving over our previous work
[
24
]
. Finally, we derive adversarial generalization error bounds for multi-class classification, which have the same functional form as binary classification bounds, and scale linearly with the number of classes
K
.

Future works include accounting for the effect of quantum gate noise inherent in NISQ devices in adversarial generalization. Additionally, as noted in
[
48
]
, moving away from uniform convergence bounds is important for better understanding the generalization capacity of quantum models. Furthermore, analyzing the algorithmic stability of adversarial training can lead insights into the effect of optimization method on adversarial generalization.

To upper bound the ARC we will make use of the sub-additivity of the supremum operation. In particular
