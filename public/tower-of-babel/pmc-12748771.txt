Analysis of a near telomere-to-telomere genome of Phellodendron amurense reveals insights into berberine biosynthesis
Authors: Song Chen, Yue Yu, Xinyu Wang, Qiwen Guo, Wenxuan Liu, Meiqi Lu, Siyuan Li, Yuanfu Liu, Guanzheng Qu, Guohua Wang, Su Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhellodendron amurense is a Tertiary relict species with significant ecological and medicinal value. Here, we present a near telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly for this species. The assembled genome is 2.92 Gb, with a contig N50 length of 65.3 Mb, and contains 59,548 protein coding genes. Evolutionary analysis reveals that P. amurense is a paleooctoploid and the four subgenomes derived from the hybridizations of four parental lineages. We dissect the berberine biosynthesis pathway in the genome and identify the CNMT gene family (PaCNMT), which may contribute to the berberine biosynthesis in P. amurense. The PaCNMT family underwent a gene loss event within the Rutaceae lineage, followed by functional re-emergence in the P. amurense lineage through LTR-mediated gene expansion, which could have shaped the metabolic pathway in P. amurense. Our results provide insights into berberine biosynthesis in woody plants and highlight the role of LTR retrotransposons in driving the evolution of specialized metabolism.Subject terms: Genome, Transcriptomics, Genome evolution, Genome duplication, Evolutionary biology

Introduction

Phellodendron is a genus of deciduous and dioecious trees in the family Rutaceae and is native to east and northeast Asia1. This genus includes two species, P. amurense and P. chinense, and represents relics from the Tertiary period ancient tropical flora, often described as a living fossil within the botanical realm1. Phellodendron trees are characterized by distinctive features, including thick corky bark and pinnately compound leaves. The bark of Phellodendron trees contains high levels of berberine and has been traditionally used to treat inflammatory disorders, infections, and digestive issues2–4. Encased within its thick bark, Phellodendron trees contain a vibrant yellow inner layer, rich in bioactive compounds such as the prominent alkaloid berberine5,6. This yellow and bitter substance is used as a cornerstone for medicine, valued for its diverse therapeutic properties. Its intermediate product, (S)-reticuline, serves as a synthetic precursor to morphinane, protopine, and aporphines7. While the order Ranunculales is known for a range of herbaceous benzylisoquinoline alkaloid (BIA) producers, such as Coptis (berberine) and Papaver somniferum (morphine), Phellodendron stands out as a berberine-producing tree genus within the order Sapindales8. Unfortunately, Phellodendron trees are endangered in the wild due to rampant overdevelopment and the impact of climate change9. In China, the wild Phellodendron trees are listed as a Level II protected species in the List of National Key Protected Wild Plants (2021).

Despite substantial interest, assembling the genomes of Phellodendron trees presents significant challenges. The genome size of P. amurense was reported to be 2.83 Gb10, which is larger than most of the Citrus species (~400 Mb) in the Rutaceae family11, but smaller than the Sichuan pepper (Zanthoxylum species) with a genome size of 5.32 Gb12. The highly repetitive sequences constitute over 75% of the genome, considerably complicating the assembly process. The inherent complexity of this task has been testing the limits of leading-edge assembly protocols for almost a decade. The lack of high-quality reference genomes of Phellodendron trees may impede research into how ancient relict trees have adapted to climate changes over the past fifty million years.

In this study, through an interdisciplinary investigation combining genome sequencing, transcriptomics, metabolomics, phylogenetic analysis, and enzymatic assays, we elucidate the origins of the berberine biosynthesis pathway in P. amurense. Our research sheds light on the process of divergence from its sister species and provides a plausible explanation for the particularly large genome size of P. amurense. These findings underscore the value of P. amurense as both a genetic and ecological resource, emphasizing the pressing need for its conservation.

Results

Generation of a nearly gapless genome for P. amurense

Based on its C-value, the genome size of P. amurense was reported to be 2.83 Gb10. Accordingly, we generated 4.1 million High Fidelity (HiFi) reads from two Sequel II runs, amounting to 68.1 gigabases (Gb) and 24-fold coverages of the genome. The sequencing reads had an average length of 16,501 bp and an N50 length of 16,581 bp. After filtering out low-quality reads, we utilized the clean reads for a K-mer analysis to characterize the genomic features. The frequency distribution of the 21-mer revealed a distinguished peak at 22 (Supplementary Fig. 1). From the total K-mer count, we estimated the genome size of P. amurense to be 2.82 Gb, which was consistent with the reported genome size10. We also noted an inconspicuous peak at the half depth (~12×) of the main peak, indicating a relatively low level of heterozygosity within the P. amurense genome. In our further analysis, we determined the heterozygosity rates to be 0.37% and an impressive repeat rate of 74.23%, indicative of a substantial proportion of repeated sequences within the genome.

Using Hifiasm assembler13, we assembled the HiFi reads into 215 contigs, with a total length of 2.92 Gb and an N50 length of 65.3 Mb, which was more continuous than the assembly generated by Xu et al.14 (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Table 1). The inclusion of 50.16 Gb Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) sequencing data allowed us to incorporate 74 contigs (2.90 Gb, 99.3% of the total length) into chromosomal pseudomolecules (Fig. 1). This effort resulted in the generation of a near telomere-to-telomere (T2T) monoploid genome comprising 39 chromosomes. The final assembly contained only 35 gaps, and 20 chromosomes were gap-free (Table 1). Telomeres were identified at both ends of all these chromosomes (Supplementary Table 2). The number of the assembled telomeric repeats ranges from 119 to 3308 across chromosome ends, with an average of 1618 repeats.

The completeness of the assembly was assessed by the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO)15, indicating a 99.8% (1611/1614) coverage of the BUSCOs, with only 3 missing BUSCOs (Supplementary Table 3). Long terminal repeat assembly index (LAI) of the genome was 22.68, which exceeded the gold standard of 2016. The Merqury analysis yielded a quality value (QV) score of 76.55, corresponding to an estimated error rate of 2.21 × 10−8. Additionally, the Genome Continuity Inspector (GCI) score of the genome was 72.64, and thirteen chromosomes exhibited GCI scores above 98.0 (Supplementary Data 1). We also aligned the long-read sequences to the genome, achieving an alignment rate of 99.8%. The HiFi reads were evenly distributed on the chromosomes, which indicated the repetitive regions in the genome were successfully resolved (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, we assembled two haplotype genomes and analyzed their collinearity using Maximal Unique Match mer (MUMmer)17 (Supplementary Fig. 3) and Synteny and Rearrangement Identifier (SyRI)18. Comparison of the two haplotype genomes revealed 11,188,309 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 878,450 insertions, 768,672 deletions, 4932 copy number gains, 4905 copy number losses, and 919 tandem repeats. The overall heterozygosity rate between the two haplotype genomes was 0.44%, which is slightly higher than the rate estimated by the 21-mer analysis (0.37%).

Annotation of the P. amurense genome

We identified 59,548 protein-coding genes in the P. amurense genome. The number of coding genes in the P. amurense genome exceeds most of the published plant genomes but is comparable to Zanthoxylum armatum19, another member of the Rutaceae family, which has 55,335 coding genes. Assessment of the P. amurense proteome using BUSCO revealed a completeness of 98.39% (1588 out of 1614 BUSCOs) (Supplementary Table 3).

We identified 2.56 Gb repetitive elements, accounting for 87.59% of the genome. Long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were the most abundant repetitive element family, constituting 74.52% of the genome, followed by DNA transposons, which accounted for 10.65% of the genome (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 4). The annotation of repetitive elements indicated that LTR-RT insertions were the primary cause of transposable element accumulation in the P. amurense genome (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). Further investigation revealed that Gypsy and Copia LTR families occupied 31.01% and 40.85% of the genome, respectively. To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of these retrotransposons, we estimated their insertion times based on sequence divergence, which reconstructed their amplification history over the past 25 million years (Fig. 2b). The results revealed that the Copia and Gypsy elements were inserted at comparable, gradually increasing frequencies for most of the species history, but their trajectories diverged significantly approximately 1.39 million years ago. At this point, Copia insertions experienced a steep decline while Gypsy activity continued.

We identified 39 centromeric regions in the P. amurense genome. These centromeres ranged from 1.04 Mb to 5.16 Mb in length, with an average length of 2.00 Mb (Supplementary Table 5). Using the Hierarchical Centromere structure Annotation Tool (HiCAT), we identified 181 different higher-order repeat (HOR) arrays and 79 different monomers in the HORs across the genome, with a total length of 27.38 Mb. These monomers were categorized into two types: α-Sat and β-Sat, both of which are AT-rich repeats of approximately 155 base pairs. The centromeres of 35 chromosomes consist of alternating irregular sequences of both α-Sat and β-Sat monomers (Supplementary Fig. 5). The remaining four chromosomes contain only one type of satellite DNA: chromosomes 6 and 14 contain α-Sat, while chromosomes 20 and 24 contain β-Sat (Supplementary Figs. 6–8). Furthermore, some satellite units also form complex arrays of higher-order repeats (HORs), such as α-Sat1-α-Sat2 in chromosome 23 (Fig. 2c). In total, the α-Sat sequences comprise 69.46% of the satellite DNA (19.02 Mb), while the β-Sat sequences make up 30.54% (8.36 Mb). All the representative monomer sequences and their evolutionary relationships are detailed in Supplementary Table 6. Repetitive elements comprised 62.10% of the centromeric sequences. LTR retrotransposons and DNA transposons accounted for 46.64% and 15.46% of the centromeric sequences, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2c, both DNA transposons and Copia LTR retrotransposons are enriched near the centromeres but scarce elsewhere.

Subgenome phasing for the P. amurense genome

After assembly and annotation, we analyzed the evolutionary trajectory of the P. amurense genome. We constructed a phylogenetic tree using 315 single-copy genes identified from ten species (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9). The result revealed the evolutionary lineage of three genera within the Rutaceae family: Phellodendron, Zanthoxylum, and Citrus. We found a closer phylogenetic relationship between Phellodendron and Zanthoxylum, with Citrus being more distantly related. Subsequent syntenic analysis revealed that both the P. amurense and Z. armatum genomes share a 4:1 syntenic relationship with the Citrus maxima genome20 (Fig. 3b, c). In contrast, P. amurense and Z. armatum exhibited a 1:1 syntenic relationship with each other (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 10). These results indicated that the P. amurense genome may contain four subgenomes.

We then generated inter-chromosomal dot plots for the P. amurense genome, which revealed clear syntenic blocks between each chromosome and three other chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 11). However, the similarity-based method was unable to distinguish the subgenomes in P. amurense. We thus applied a subgenome-aware phylogenetic approach21 using C. maxima (2n = 2x = 18) as an outgroup to construct phylogenetic trees for each chromosome of P. amurense and assigned it to one of the four subgenomes based on their phylogenetic positions. We designated the subgenomes as A, B, C, and D. Nine out of the ten phylogenetic trees displayed a consistent topology with the structure of [D, [C, [B, A]]]. Citrus chromosome 5 simultaneously corresponds to the P. amurense groups V and VI, wherein group VI displayed the structure of [[D, C], [B, A]] (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 12).

We investigated whether the centromere positions are conserved among homoeologous chromosomes in the subgenomes of P. amurense. The centromeres positioned in the same synteny block among the homoeologous chromosomes were defined as evolutionary conserved centromeres. The results revealed that six pairs of homoeologous chromosomes, involving 9 chromosomes, exhibited positional conservation of centromeres. The remaining chromosomes, despite deriving from the same ancestral chromosome, lacked the evolutionary conservation of the centromere. Notably, the subsequent nucleotide MUMmer (NUCmer) analysis failed to identify any genomic collinearity among all 39 centromeres. In addition, the evolutionary tree of the monomer units revealed no discernible evolutionary pattern, even amongst positionally conserved centromere pairs (Supplementary Fig. 5a). We further compared the similarities between each pair of chromosomes in the centromeric regions using K-mer analysis22. The results revealed that the similarities of all the pairs were less than 65% (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

Divergence timing for the ancestral lineages of P. amurense

Ten putative ancestral chromosomes were reconstructed for P. amurense and C. maxima using IAGS23, with Melia azedarach24 as an outgroup (Fig. 4a). The results suggested that the ancestral P. amurense genome underwent at least 8 chromosomal fissions and 8 fusions after the divergence from C. maxima. In contrast, the C. maxima genome showed a simpler evolutionary history with only one fusion. Two subsequent evolutionary events further altered the chromosomal structure of the P. amurense genome (Fig. 4a, b). The first one, identified in subgenome A, was a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 6 and 22, which resulted in two rearranged chromosomes. The second event involved multiple translocations among chromosomes 7 and 28 in subgenome C, and a putative chromosome derived from ancestral chromosome 4 in subgenome D. These translocations may lead to the loss of the ancestral chromosome 4 homolog from subgenome D.

We then calculated the pairwise Ks distributions among the subgenomes of P. amurense. Consistent with the phylogenetic topology of the subgenomes, the Ks distributions formed three clusters, corresponding to these sequential divergence events, respectively (Fig. 4c). The most recent divergent event between subgenome A and B displayed a peak of Ks value of 0.172. Followed by the divergence of the subgenome C from the clade of subgenomes A and B, which displayed a mean Ks of 0.191 (A–C: 0.192; B, C: 0.190). The most ancient split, corresponding to the divergent event of subgenome D from the other lineages, produced a mean Ks of 0.213 (A–D: 0.218; B–D: 0.214; C–D: 0.207). Based on a calibrated rate of synonymous substitution, we estimated that the three divergent events occurred approximately 22.05, 24.49, and 27.31 Mya, respectively.

Since P. amurense and Z. armatum exhibited a strong 1:1 syntenic relationship (Supplementary Fig. 10), it is plausible that the allopolyploidization events (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Table 7) in the P. amurense lineages occurred before the divergence of the two genera. The Ks distribution for the orthologues of P. amurense and Z. armatum exhibited a single, distinct peak centered at approximately 0.164 (Fig. 4d), which corresponded to the divergence time of 21.03 Mya. We also employed population genomic analyses using SMC++ 25 to infer the divergent time between the two species. The examination of effective population size (Ne) indicated that a significant demographic separation began ~21.50 Mya (Fig. 4e), which is in agreement with the Ks estimation of 21.03 Mya.

Taken together, we estimated that the allopolyploidization events in the P. amurense lineage occurred after the last divergent event between parental lineages (divergence between subgenome A and B at 22.05 Mya), but before the divergence time of P. amurense and Z. armatum at 21.50 Mya.

Comparative genomic analysis of the berberine biosynthetic pathway revealed a lineage-specific expansion of PaCNMTs

Berberine is a bioactive alkaloid with various pharmacological effects7. The biosynthesis of berberine is catalyzed by a series of enzymes including (S)-norcoclaurine synthase (NCS), (S)-norcoclaurine 6-O-methyltransferase (6OMT), (S)-coclaurine N-methyltransferase (CNMT), (S)-N-methylcoclaurine 3′-hydroxylase (NMCH), 3′-hydroxy-N-methylcoclaurine 4′-O-methyltransferase (4′OMT), berberine bridge enzyme (BBE), (S)-scoulerine 9-O-methyltransferase (SOMT), (S)-canadine synthase (CAS), and (S)-tetrahydroberberine oxidase (THBO) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 2). To identify the genes responsible for this pathway in P. amurense, we conducted a genome-wide search by using functionally characterized protein sequences from Coptis chinensis, Papaver somniferum, and Aristolochia fimbriata as queries in a homology search (E-value < 1e-5). We also constructed phylogenetic trees for the gene families in seven species: Aristolochia fimbriata26 (Piperales), Citrus maxima11 (Sapindales), Coptis chinensis27 (Ranunclaceae), Nelumbo nucifera28 (Proteales), Papaver somniferum29 (Ranunclaceae), Phellodendron amurense (Sapindales), and Zanthoxylum armatum19 (Sapindales) (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Figs. 14–18). Finally, we identified 25 NCS, 7 6OMT, 7 CNMT, 7 NMCH, 10 4′OMT, 16 BBE, 8 SOMT, 4 CAS, and 11 THBO genes in the P. amurense genome (Supplementary Data 3). Genes were classified as “-like” if they exhibited low sequence similarity or contained disruptive mutations, such as exon loss and frameshifts.

Subsequently, we performed a comparative genomic analysis for these families in three species in Rutaceae, including P. amurense, Z. armatum (Sichuan pepper), and C. maxima. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, most of the gene families involved in berberine biosynthesis in the P. amurense genome exhibited synteny with C. maxima, except for the BBE and CNMT families. The BBE genes were only found in the syntenic blocks between P. amurense and Z. armatum, which was consistent with the lack of berberine in C. maxima30. It is interesting that only a PaCNMT-like gene (PaCNMT1-like1) was found in the syntenic blocks among P. amurense, Z. armatum, and C. maxima (Fig. 6c). Further analysis revealed that both the C. maxima and Z. armatum genomes contain only this CNMT-like gene, with negligible abundances (TPM < 5) across various tissues, including leaves, phloem, xylem, and roots (Supplementary Table 8).

This observation prompted us to explore the deeper evolutionary origins of the expanded CNMT family in P. amurense. We extended our synteny analysis to include other berberine-producing eudicots from distantly related orders. We first performed a comparative genomic analysis between P. amurense and C. chinensis, a medicinal plant whose berberine biosynthetic pathway has been well characterized27. However, we failed to identify any synteny for the CNMT family between the two species. We therefore extended our analysis to a more distantly related, berberine-producing species, Nelumbo nucifera (Proteales), and found a syntenic region for the PaCNMT1-like1 locus (Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition, high sequence identities were observed between the orthologous CNMT genes between P. amurense and N. nucifera (Supplementary Table 9). These results suggested that the CNMT genes may share a common ancestor from early eudicots and have undergone gene loss events during the evolution of Sapindales, followed by specific expansion events in the P. amurense lineage.

Evolutional analysis of the PaCNMT gene family

To distinguish the gene family expansion of PaCNMTs caused by specific amplification events from alloploidy, we calculated the pairwise Ks values among the four PaCNMT genes (PaCNMT1, 2, 3, and 4). We found that all the pairwise Ks values of the PaCNMTs were less than the speciational Ks of 0.164 (Supplementary Table 10). The results indicated the PaCNMT genes may be derived from specific amplifications after the speciation of P. amurense. Especially, the coding sequences of PaCNMT3 and PaCNMT4 are identical, which implies that they were the most recently duplicated genes. A syntenic analysis revealed that despite the allopolyploid nature of P. amurense, the expected homeologs of the ancestral CNMT gene were only retained in the syntenic locations of subgenome D (Supplementary Fig. 21). Consistent with this, Z. armatum also retained the single ancestral homeolog in the syntenic region of subgenome D. In contrast, PaCNMT1 and PaCNMT2 were located on loci of Chr04, while PaCNMT3 and PaCNMT4 occupied Chr14. These results indicated that the expansion of PaCNMTs was not the result of the allopolyploidization events shared with Z. armatum, but arose from more recent, lineage-specific amplifications in P. amurense. Further analysis of the nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution rate ratio (Ka/Ks) revealed that the evolution from the ancestral gene to PaCNMT1 (Ka/Ks = 0.367) and subsequently to PaCNMT2 (Ka/Ks = 0.227) was governed by strong purifying selection.

A genomic structure analysis revealed that the PaCNMTs (PaCNMT1, 2, 3, and 4) shared a conserved gene architecture with other species, comprising eight exons (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 22). The corresponding exons across these species exhibit similar lengths and phases. In contrast, PaCNMT1-like1 retained a gene structure identical to the CNMT genes but showed high divergence in its first exon. PaCNMT1-like2 suffered the loss of five exons due to the LTR insertion, and PaCNMT1-like3 contained a frameshift mutation, which rendered it non-functional.

The PaCNMT gene family in the P. amurense genome exhibited divergent expressional patterns (Fig. 7c). PaCNMT1-like1, PaCNMT1-like2, and PaCNMT1-like3 showed a relatively low expressional profile across phloem, xylem, and leaves. However, following translocation to LTR-rich regions on chromosome 4, mutations and insertions in the promoter region of PaCNMT1 may have elevated its expression level. The mRNA abundance of the subsequently generated genes, PaCNMT2, 3, and 4, was comparable to PaCNMT1 in the vascular tissues of P. amurense. We conducted a sequence analysis of PaCNMTs and their flanking 5 kb upstream and downstream regions (Fig. 6b). Our findings reveal a striking similarity in the overall genomic architecture surrounding PaCNMT2, PaCNMT3, and PaCNMT4. The downstream region of PaCNMT1 shares considerable homology with the other genes, but its upstream sequences are divergent.

PaCNMT may contribute to the production of berberine in P. amurense

Berberine has been reported to be biosynthesized mainly in the phloem and xylem of P. amurense31. We thus used RNA-seq to investigate the expressional patterns of the genes related to the berberine biosynthetic pathway in these tissues of P. amurense (Supplementary Fig. 23). The results revealed that 32 of these genes were abundant in phloem and xylem relative to leaves (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 3). The majority of these genes were highly expressed in phloem. We used liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to quantify the key alkaloids in the BIA synthesis pathway of different tissues. The identities of the putative identifications were verified by comparing their MS/MS fragmentation patterns and retention times with those from authentic compounds. Most of these alkaloids were found predominantly in the phloem, with significantly lower concentrations in the leaves. Of these, berberine was the most abundant (4.17 ± 0.04 μg/g), with concentrations 151- and 6-fold greater than in the leaves and xylem, respectively. Similarly, jatrorrhizine (1.39 ± 0.00 μg/g), magnoflorine (1.13 ± 0.01 μg/g), and phellodendrine (0.58 ± 0.01 μg/g) were also concentrated in the phloem. Comprehensive contents of the metabolites detected were listed in Supplementary Table 11.

We subsequently correlated the expression levels of berberine biosynthesis genes with the abundance of all BIA compounds identified from the metabolomics analysis. Our results demonstrated that PaCNMT2 expression showed the strongest correlation with berberine content (η = 0.97) (Supplementary Table 12). Despite sequence divergence, the predicted structure of PaCNMT2 exhibited high similarity to characterized CNMTs from other species, and the GxGxG SAM binding motif (residues 138-142) was completely conserved (Fig. 7b, d, and Supplementary Data 4). Furthermore, PaCNMT2 shares >95% sequence identity with its paralogs in PaCNMTs (Supplementary Table 13), making it an ideal representative for characterizing the conserved function of this gene family. Therefore, we performed enzymatic assays through heterologous expressing this gene in Pichia pastoris. As shown in Fig. 8a, PaCNMT2 catalyzed N-methylation of (S)-coclaurine to form (S)-N-methylcoclaurine, which exhibits the same function as CNMT in C. japonica32. Moreover, PaCNMT2 cannot methylate the (R)-coclaurine or further methylate (S)-N-methylcoclaurine to produce magnocurarine. In vitro enzyme assays on (S)-norcoclaurine and (S)-scoulerine also revealed no detectable O-methyltransferase activity of PaCNMT2. It demonstrated high catalytic selectivity and substrate specificity.

Discussion

The large size of the P. amurense genome is notable, particularly compared to other Rutaceae members like C. maxima20. A major factor contributing to the expansion of the P. amurense genome is the heterologous hybridization33 or autopolyploidization34,35 events. Phasing and evolutionary analyses indicate this event arose 22.05–21.50 Mya. However, this hypothesis has several limitations. First, the diploid progenitors were presumed extinct, preventing the direct observation of the polyploidization events. Thus, the proposed timing is an indirect inference based on genomic and Ks analyses of extant species. Second, the nearly identical Ks distributions between subgenomes make it difficult to distinguish a classic allopolyploid model from one involving hybridization between structured ancestral populations. Although our synteny and Ks results strongly suggest an allopolyploid origin for P. amurense, alternative scenarios cannot be definitively excluded.

Another factor contributing to the large genome size of P. amurense is the proliferation of LTR retrotransposons. LTR retrotransposons comprise 74.5% of the P. amurense genome, nearly three times the proportion found in C. maxima11. Similar LTR proliferation events have been observed in Ranunculaceae plant genomes, including Coptis chinensis and opium poppy29. Although this burst led to large-scale variation in chromosome structure, we observed that gene collinearity between P. amurense subgenomes remains well-preserved (Fig. 4b). We analyzed LTR insertion patterns and found that these events are more prevalent in intergenic regions, thereby avoiding widespread disruption of gene coding regions. The activity of transposable elements is also a powerful driver of evolution in centromeres. Analysis of P. amurense centromeres revealed an enrichment of LTR retrotransposons and DNA transposons. However, in contrast to Gypsy LTRs in Rhododendron molle36 and A. thaliana37, the Copia superfamily is more prevalent in P. amurense centromeres, comprising 39.14% of the centromeric sequences. This suggests that Copia LTRs may also represent a key structural element of P. amurense centromeres.

Beyond reshaping the genome, active LTR proliferation drives gene family expansion38. The formation of the unique PaCNMT gene family in P. amurense exemplifies this LTR-mediated evolution. The PaCNMT gene family underwent a recent, lineage-specific expansion in P. amurense, distinguishing the species from its relative Z. armatum. Based on long terminal repeat (LTR) remnants flanking these genes, we propose that the PaCNMT family arose through LTR-mediated translocation and duplication (Fig. 8c, d), rather than a polyploidization event. In the first step, the ortholog of pre-CNMT in the P. amurense genome, PaCNMT1-like1 (Pam.01G000547), underwent an LTR-mediated translocation to chromosome 4 (Fig. 8b). This event gave rise to the gene of PaCNMT1-like2 (Pam.04G001430), which subsequently diversified through tandem duplications, producing PaCNMT1 (Pam.04G001422) and PaCNMT2 (Pam.04G001419) (Fig. 8c). An additional duplication and LTR-facilitated translocation of PaCNMT2 to chromosome 14 gave rise to two new genes, PaCNMT3 (Pam.14G001224) and PaCNMT4 (Pam.14G001225), further expanding the PaCNMT gene family (Fig. 8d). As the two genes were duplicated recently, PaCNMT3 and PaCNMT4 nucleotide sequences are nearly identical. This entire process led to the emergence of the PaCNMT gene family, comprising four new homologous copies.

Ks analysis indicates that amplification occurred after P. amurense and Z. armatum diverged, explaining why only P. amurense possesses multiple CNMT copies. Retention of four similar CNMT paralogs under strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 0.4) suggests significant selective advantages. We propose that these duplications boost berberine biosynthesis via a gene dosage effect, providing an advantage in plant defense. This evolutionary adaptation likely underpins a distinct BIA biosynthesis pathway in P. amurense relative to related species.

Methods

Plant materials, DNA, and RNA isolation

We selected four mature P. amurense trees from the experimental field at Northeast Forestry University in Harbin, China. Young leaves from a single male tree were collected for genome sequencing and assembly. For comprehensive genomic annotation and transcriptome sequencing, we sampled various plant tissues (leaves, phloem, and xylem) from each of the four individual trees. Sampling was conducted at solar noon (12:00–13:00 local time) in July to coincide with peak alkaloid biosynthesis. Prior to processing, all samples were washed with sterile water and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at −80 °C to preserve their integrity for subsequent nucleic acid extraction.

Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh leaves using the MGIEasy™ DNA Library Prep Kit (BGI, Wuhan, CN). For PacBio assemblies, we prepared SMRTbell libraries and sequenced them using a PacBio Sequel II system. We used CCS v6.4.0 with default parameters to generate highly accurate consensus (HiFi) reads. To elucidate the genome’s spatial organization, we performed Hi-C sequencing on 2 g of flash-frozen leaf tissue in parallel with the genomic sequencing. The fixed DNA was digested with the MboI restriction enzyme, and sequencing was performed on a BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI, Wuhan, CN).

Total RNA was extracted from various plant tissues of the four individual trees using the MGIEasy™ RNA Library Prep Set (BGI, Wuhan, CN). The samples were then sent to Annoroad Gene Technology (Annoroad, Beijing, CN) for cDNA library construction following the MGISEQ user manual guidelines. Paired-end read sequencing was performed on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI, Wuhan, CN).

Genome assembly and Hi-C analysis

Before the actual assembly process, we conducted a 21-mer frequency distribution analysis using K-mer counting (KMC) v3.2.139 and further interpreted the results using GenomeScope v2.0.040. The long reads were subsequently assembled using Hifiasm v0.19.713, resulting in one primary genome and two haplotype draft contig genomes. We employed the HiC-Pro v3.1.041 to classify the Hi-C data into valid and invalid interaction pairs, retaining only the valid pairs for subsequent assembly. To assemble large contigs into chromosomal-level scaffolds, we used the Hi-C links from contig end regions rather than whole contig regions, implementing the EndHiC v1.0.042. We then generated a heatmap using HiCPlotter v0.6.643 to visualize the genomic interactions. Synteny between homologous chromosomes was determined by aligning the two haplotype assemblies with SyRI v1.6.318 and MUMmer v3.23.017.

To assess the completeness of the assembled contigs and chromosomes, we used the “embryophyta_odb10” lineage-specific profile from BUSCO v5.6.015. This profile encompasses 1,614 BUSCO groups and served as the reference database for evaluation. We also evaluated the genome evaluation by using Merqury v1.3.044 and GCI v1.0.045 with default parameters. The native range of P. amurense sourced from Plants of the World Online (POWO)46 was mapped in QGIS v3.22.947 on a Natural Earth base map.

Annotation of repetitive sequences and centromere

Repetitive sequences within the P. amurense genome were comprehensively annotated using a combination of homology-based search and de novo techniques. To conduct the homology-based search, we acquired the RepBase v2018102648 and Dfam v3.049 libraries, which serve as repositories for transposable elements (TEs). We also generated de novo TE libraries using RepeatModeler v2.0.550 and Extensive de novo TE Annotator (EDTA) v2.1.051, effectively expanding our repertoire of TE annotations. The insertion time of each LTR was also calculated by EDTA. Subsequently, we combined these four TE libraries and used them as input for RepeatMasker v4.1.552 to systematically identify and categorize repeats in the P. amurense assembly. To optimize subsequent protein-coding gene annotation, we appropriately soft-masked all repetitive regions.

We also employed QuarTeT v1.0.053 and TRASH v1.0.054 to identify putative telomere and centromeric regions. To further chart the structure of peri/centromeric regions at high resolution, we generated a genome-wide database of satellite DNA monomers using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v4.9.155 and Hierarchical Centromere structure Annotation Tool (HiCAT) v1.1.056. Centromere similarity based on K-mers was calculated using the K-mer Analysis Toolkit (KAT) v2.4.122. We then constructed a phylogenetic tree of centromeric monomer repeat sequences (α-Sat and β-Sat) using maximum likelihood analysis in RAxML-NG v1.2.057 with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Gene structure annotation

We used the MAKER pipeline v3.1.458 to predict the protein-coding genes, employing diverse methods including transcriptomic, homologous, and de novo approaches. Transcriptome-based predictions relied on RNA-seq data from three tissue samples, assembled using Trinity v2.13.259. For homology-based predictions, we downloaded protein sequences from four Sapindales species (Zanthoxylum armatum19, Citrus maxima11, Citrus sinensis11, Litchi chinensis60), four Ranunculales species (Papaver setigerum29, Papaver somniferum29, Papaver rhoeas29, Coptis chinensis27), and four extensively annotated model plants (Arabidopsis thaliana61, Populus trichocarpa62, Oryza sativa63, Vitis vinifera64). These sequences were meticulously curated to remove redundancy. In parallel, de novo prediction analyses utilizing Augustus v3.3.365, GeneMark v4.33.066, and SNAP v2013112967 were conducted on the repeat-masked genome, with model parameters trained using the available RNA-seq data. Finally, the transcript-based, homology-based, and de novo prediction results were integrated using EvidenceModeler (EVM) v2.1.068 approach, yielding a comprehensive and non-redundant gene set. Additionally, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) were annotated using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1269, barrnap v0.9.070, and cmscan from the INFERNAL package v1.1.471, which leverages the Rfam v15.0.072 database.

Functional annotation was based on the comparison with the UniProtKB database73 using an E-value of 1e-5. The results were then imported into Automated Assignment of Human Readable Descriptions (AHRD) v1.0.074 to assign appropriate Gene Ontology (GO) terms to the identified genes. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of these genes, we integrated hierarchical and phylogenetic annotations using eggnog-mapper v2.1.975, with a minimum hit e-value of 0.001. Transcription factors (TF) were predicted using the PlantRegMap76. We predicted the three-dimensional structures of PaCNMTs using AlphaFold 3 (AF3)77 via the AlphaFold server, a dedicated web portal developed by DeepMind. Full-length amino acid sequences were provided as input, from which the models exhibiting the highest per-residue confidence score (pLDDT) were selected for subsequent analysis. Resulting structures were then visualized, superimposed, and analyzed using PyMOL v2.5.078. We also defined the 2000 bp region upstream of all coding genes as the promoter region and performed cis-element identification using the PLACE database79.

Phylogenetic analysis and divergence time estimation

We used OrthoFinder v2.5.480 to identify single-copy orthologs across five Sapindales species (Phellodendron amurense, Zanthoxylum armatum19, Citrus maxima11, Citrus sinensis11, Litchi chinensis60), four Ranunculales species (Papaver setigerum29, Papaver somniferum29, Papaver rhoeas29, Coptis chinensis27), and the outgroup, Oryza sativa63. We then used Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT) v7.52081 to align the identified single-copy ortholog pairs, and extracted conserved sites using Gblocks v0.91b with default parameters. We constructed the maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using RAxML-NG v1.2.057 with 1000 bootstrap replicates. We estimated divergence times using the independent rates model in MCMCTree v4.10.582, incorporating a birth-death process with species sampling. We incorporated the Ranunculales-Sapindales split time (126.0–132.4 Mya) and constrained the divergence times for Coptis-Papaver (102.9–117.2 Mya) and Litchi-Citrus (68.0–85.4 Mya)83. We used the HKY85 + GAMMA model in MCMCTree82 with four default rate categories to model nucleotide substitution. Given the size of our data matrix, we used approximate likelihood calculations and estimated branch lengths using the baseml program of Phylogenetic Analysis by Maximum Likelihood (PAML) v4.10.684. The synonymous substitutions per site per year for Phellodendron were calculated to be 3.90e-9.

We used SMC++ v1.15.425 to reconstruct the demographic history. This method estimates changes in effective population size (Ne) over time based on the distribution of SNPs in unphased genomes. The VCF file generated by the GATK pipeline85 was the primary input for the SMC++ analysis. Reads were first aligned to the reference genome using BWA-MEM v0.7.1786. Subsequently, SNPs were identified for all individuals with the HaplotypeCaller in GATK v4.5.085.

Subgenome phasing

To determine the evolutionary history of P. amurense, we employed subgenome-aware phylogenetic approaches to phase its subgenomes21. We used the hierarchical gene lists to infer maximum likelihood trees with RAxML-NG v1.2.057. These trees were used as input for ASTRAL-Pro v1.19.087 to infer phylogeny resolving relationships at the species, subgenome, and chromosome levels. The resulting species tree was visualized and assessed using Newick utilities v1.6.088 and PhyTop v1.0.389. We also calculated the synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for genes within syntenic blocks using the YN model implemented in KaKs_Calculator v3.0.090. Ancestral genome reconstruction

Rutaceae ancestral inference followed the workflow of Yang et al.29. First, we established syntenic relationships between C. maxima11, P. amurense, and Melia azedarach24. Using BlastP v2.14.091 and MCscanX v2013111192, we identified orthologous gene pairs. We then used these gene pairs to construct a graph, enabling detection of orthologous gene groups (putative proto-genes, pPGs). To identify non-overlapping synteny blocks in these species, we applied the DRIMM-Synteny algorithm to these pPGs. Finally, we integrated the corresponding pPGs into the non-overlapping synteny blocks using processDrimm v1.0.093.

In the next stage, we reconstructed ancestral proto-chromosomes using the guided genome halving problem (GGHP) model of Inferring Ancestor Genome Structure (IAGS) v1.0.023 within the context of Rutaceae genomes. Using the reconstructed ancestral proto-chromosomes and non-overlapping syntenic blocks, we inferred the potential order of pPGs. For each syntenic block, we constructed a directed weighted pPG graph. Using a topological sorting method with a greedy strategy, we determined the potential gene orders within each graph. Based on this, we used downstream supporting scripts from IAGS to quantify shuffling events, including fissions and fusions. Using the IAGS pipeline, we analyzed the evolutionary relationships between the chromosomes of P. amurense and C. maxima and identified 10 ancestral chromosomes. Based on gene collinearity with these ancestors, we sorted the homologous chromosomes into ten groups (I–X). A major chromosomal fusion event in the evolutionary history of C. maxima that resulted in its chromosome 5 corresponding to both groups V and VI in P. amurense (Supplementary Table 14).

Annotation of the berberine biosynthesis genes

We collected berberine biosynthesis genes previously identified in Coptis chinensis27, Papaver somniferum29, and Aristolochia fimbriata26, and employed BLAST v2.14.091 to identify homologous genes in three species of the Rutaceae family, using an E-value threshold of 1e-5. Additionally, we collected a comprehensive dataset of putative CYP sequences from multiple species using the UniProtKB database94. These protein sequences were subsequently globally aligned against the proteome of P. amurense using the needle program within the EMBOSS v6.6.095 with a gap-opening penalty of 10.0 and a gap-extension penalty of 0.5. The identified P450 genes within the P. amurense genome were systematically named according to the P450 Nomenclature Protocol96, based on sequence identity thresholds of >40% for family and >55% for subfamily assignments. We then used AlphaFold 377 to predict the three-dimensional structures and structural comparison. Based on enzymatic activity previously reported by Pauli and Kutchan97 and Ikezawa et al.98, three genes predicted to encode CYP80B proteins were identified as candidates for the PaNMCH family, while four genes predicted to encode CYP719 proteins were identified as candidates for the PaCAS family. Subsequently, we constructed a phylogenetic tree for all candidate genes using RAxML-NG v1.2.057 with 1000 bootstrap replicates. We then analyzed the syntenic relationships among the three Rutaceae species (Phellodendron amurense, Citrus maxima11, and Zanthoxylum armatum19) using MCScanX v2013111192.

Gene expression analysis

For gene expression analysis, we employed the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline v3.10.199 in Nextflow v22.10.7100, which is purpose-built for RNA-seq data analysis. Raw reads were assessed for quality using FastQC v0.12.1101 and trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality bases using fastp v0.23.2102. Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using Spliced Transcripts Alignments to a Reference (STAR) v2.7.10103. Gene transcript levels were quantified using RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (RSEM) v1.3.3104 and normalized using the trimmed mean of M-value (TMM) method. We used edgeR v3.42.4105 for a comprehensive analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs with a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 and a log2 fold-change (log2FC) greater than 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Statistical significance relative to the leave groups was determined by a two-sided quasi-likelihood F-test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

LC-MS/MS analysis

For LC-MS/MS analysis, we sampled various plant tissues (leaves, phloem, and xylem) from each of the three individual trees. Samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a homogeneous powder. Approximately 0.2 g of the powdered sample was accurately weighed and placed into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. To each tube, 5 mL of 0.1% formic acid in methanol (v/v) was added. The mixture was then ultrasonicated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,220 × g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic-phase syringe filter and stored at –20 °C pending LC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection were performed using a PerkinElmer QSight 420 Triple Quad LC-MS/MS system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The separation was achieved on an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 µm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (A) and acetonitrile (B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution was as follows: 0–1 min, 20% B; 1–2 min, linear gradient from 20% to 30% B; 2–5 min, 30% B; 5–6 min, linear gradient from 30% to 90% B; 6–7 min, 90% B; 7–8 min, linear gradient from 90% to 20% B; 8–10 min, 20% B for re-equilibration. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode using Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM). The source parameters were optimized as follows: capillary voltage, 2.5 kV; source offset 50 V; source temperature, 150 °C; desolvation temperature, 500 °C; cone gas flow, 150 L/h; and desolvation gas flow, 1000 L/h. Concentrations were expressed in µg/g fresh weight (FW). Columbamine and jatrorrhizine are positional isomers with an identical MRM transition (m/z 338.2 > 323.1), making them indistinguishable by mass spectrometry alone. However, columbamine and jatrorrhizine showed different retention times at 4.18 and 4.28 min, respectively, in the liquid chromatography analysis (Supplementary Fig. 24). We used the mixture of columbamine and jatrorrhizine for further confirmation, which revealed two distinguishable peaks at the corresponding retention times. In addition, the elution order of the two compounds is consistent with the previous report106. We then proceeded to quantify them in our plant tissue samples by the peak areas at the respective retention times. Statistical analyses were performed using a two-sided Welch’s t-test implemented with SciPy v1.16.0107 in Python v3.13.5.

Heterologous expression and functional validation in Pichia pastoris of PaCNMT2

The heterologous expression experiments in yeast were conducted in collaboration with Trigoats Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). We performed PaCNMT2 enzymatic assays by heterologously expressing the gene in Pichia pastoris. Following determination of the expression strain (X-33), positive clones were amplified using 1% methanol and induced at 30 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, we performed in vitro enzyme assays using the purified protein. Enzyme assays of PaCNMT2 were performed overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Each 200 μL reaction mixture contained Gly buffer (pH 9.0), 100 μL purified protein (0.125 mg/mL), 1 mM SAM, and either (S)-coclaurine, (R)-coclaurine, (S)-norcoclaurine, or (S)-scoulerine as substrate. Each substrate was tested at three concentrations: 200 μg/mL, 400 μg/mL, and 600 μg/mL. The reaction was quenched by adding 50% (v/v) methanol.

The reaction samples were analyzed using HPLC (LC3000, Beijing ChuangXinTongHeng Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). A 20 μL aliquot of extract was injected and separated on a NuovaSil 100-5C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solutions A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: at 0 min, 5% B; at 7 min, 20% B; at 19 min, 50% B; and at 24 min, 100% B. The flow rate was 1.0 ml/min with detection at 280 nm and a total run time of 24 min.
