Refining Ky Fan’s majorization relation with linear programming
Mohammad A. Alhejji

Abstract
A separable version of Ky Fan’s majorization relation is proven for a sum of two operators that are each a tensor product of two positive semi-definite operators. In order to prove it, upper bounds are established on the relevant largest eigenvalue sums in terms of the optimal values of certain linear programs. The objective function of these linear programs is the dual of the direct sum of the spectra of the summands. The feasible sets are bounded polyhedra determined by positive numbers, called alignment terms, that quantify the overlaps between pairs of largest eigenvalue spaces of the summands. By appealing to geometric considerations, tight upper bounds are established on the alignment terms of tensor products of positive semi-definite operators. As an application, the spin alignment conjecture in quantum information theory is affirmatively resolved to the 2-letter level. Consequently, the coherent information of platypus channels is additive to the 2-letter level.



1
Introduction

Among the open problems in quantum information theory, the problem of regularized formulas sticks out like a sore thumb. Put briefly, known formulas for the optimal rates of asymptotically error-free information processing—a principal concern of the theory— are given by limits of sequences of optimizations over domains with exponentially growing dimensions. Often, little is known about the convergence rates of these sequences. It is commonly surmised that much is yet to be understood about information processing in quantum-mechanical contexts. To make progress, new analysis tools are necessary.

Let us consider the transmission of quantum information through a memoryless quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
(a linear completely positive trace-preserving map). The quantum capacity of
\mathcal{N}
is the largest rate of asymptotically error-free transmission of quantum information through
\mathcal{N}
, measured in qubits per channel use. Let
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N})
denote the quantum capacity of
\mathcal{N}
. We have one formula for
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N})
, due to Lloyd
[
17
]
, Shor
[
24
]
and Devetak
[
9
]
, in terms of the coherent information of
\mathcal{N}
. Let
\mathcal{N}^{c}
denote a complementary channel to
\mathcal{N}
defined via a Stinespring dilation (see, for example, Ch. 5 of Ref.
[
25
]
). The output of
\mathcal{N}^{c}
is conventionally called the environment of
\mathcal{N}
. The coherent information of
\mathcal{N}
is given by

\displaystyle I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N}):=\max_{\rho}H(\mathcal{N}(\rho))-H(\mathcal{N}^{c}(\rho)),

(1.1)

where
H(X):=-\tr(X \log_2 X)
is the von Neumann entropy. The maximization is over quantum states (positive semi-definite operators with unit trace) in the domain of
\mathcal{N}
. The formula we have for
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N})
is the regularized formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n})}{n}.

(1.2)

It follows from the additivity of the von Neumann entropy under tensor products that
I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n})\geq nI^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N})
. If
I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n})=nI^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N})
, then
\mathcal{N}
is said to have
additive
coherent information to the
n
-letter level. If this is the case for all
n
, then
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N})
has the manifestly computable formula
I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N})
. Unfortunately, this is generically not the case (see, for example, Sec. IV.B of Ref.
[
14
]
). A stark instance of this
non-additivity
is found in Ref.
[
7
]
, where Cubitt et al. show that for every positive integer
m
, there exists a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{m}
such that
I^{\text{(coh)}}(\mathcal{N}_{m}^{\otimes m})=0
while
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{N}_{m})>0
.

Here we are concerned with a conjecture called the spin alignment conjecture. Introduced by Leditzky et al. in Ref.
[
16
]
, it is a conjecture about the entropies of convex mixtures of quantum states that have a certain tensor product structure. It arose in the context of computing the quantum capacities of platypus channels. The particular form of these quantum channels is not relevant to us here. By a reduction in Sec. V of Ref.
[
16
]
, each platypus channel
\mathcal{M}
has an associated quantum state
M
such that the coherent information of
\mathcal{M}
is additive to the
n
-letter level if for all probability distributions
p
on the subsets of
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and all tuples of pure (rank-
1
) quantum states
(\psi_{J})_{J\subseteq[n]}
, it holds that

\displaystyle H(\sum_{J\subseteq[n]}p_{J}\psi_{J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}})\geq H(\sum_{J\subseteq[n]}p_{J}(v_{M}v_{M}^{*})^{\otimes J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}}),

(1.3)

where
v_{M}
is a unit eigenvector of
M
corresponding to its largest eigenvalue. For each subset
J\subseteq[n]
, the expression
\psi_{J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}}
denotes a quantum state that is separable across the bipartition
J\mid J^{c}
. The factor
\psi_{J}
denotes the quantum state of particles whose labels are in
J
and the tensor power
M^{\otimes J^{c}}
denotes the quantum state of particles whose labels are in the complement
J^{c}
. The spin alignment conjecture is that the inequality above holds in general. Results concerning the interplay between platypus channels and other quantum channels are shown in Ref.
[
15
]
, some of which are conditioned on this conjecture.

Described in words, the entropy of the convex mixture
\sum_{J\subseteq[n]}p_{J}\psi_{J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}}
, which may be interpreted as the quantum state of the environment of
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes n}
, is conjectured to be smallest when
(\psi_{J})_{J\subseteq[n]}
is chosen so that the particles have maximally aligned spins. The stronger conjecture that

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subseteq[n]}p_{J}\psi_{J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}}\preceq\sum_{J\subseteq[n]}p_{J}({v_{M}}{v_{M}}^{*})^{\otimes J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}},

(1.4)

where
\preceq
is the majorization symbol (see Sec.
2
for a formal definition), was pursued in Ref.
[
1
]
. As of this writing, a complete resolution of either conjecture is yet to be found.

It is instructive to consider the
n=1
case. For a self-adjoint (or Hermitian) operator
A
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
\lambda(A)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the tuple of eigenvalues of
A
arranged so
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(A)
. The relation above immediately follows from Ky Fan’s majorization relation
[
10
]
(also known as Ky Fan’s eigenvalue inequality
[
19
]
):

\displaystyle\lambda(p_{\{1\}}\psi_{\{1\}}+p_{\varnothing}M)

\displaystyle\preceq\lambda(p_{\{1\}}\psi_{\{1\}})+\lambda(p_{\varnothing}M)

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\lambda(p_{\{1\}}v_{M}v_{M}^{*})+\lambda(p_{\varnothing}M)

(1.6)

\displaystyle=\lambda(p_{\{1\}}v_{M}v_{M}^{*}+p_{\varnothing}M).

(1.7)

The last equality is crucial to this argument. More generally, for self-adjoint operators
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, the equality
\lambda(A_{1})+\lambda(A_{2})=\lambda(A_{1}+A_{2})
holds if and only if there exist orthonormal vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
such that
A_{1}w_{i}=\lambda_{i}(A_{1})w_{i}
and
A_{2}w_{i}=\lambda_{i}(A_{2})w_{i}
for each
i\in[d]
(see, for example, Ref.
[
22
]
). This invites the following definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}
denote elements of the standard orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We define an
alignment
map
↓
on the space of self-adjoint operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by the action
X\mapsto X^{\downarrow}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(X)q_{i}q_{i}^{*}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Given self-adjoint operators
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we say that they are
perfectly aligned
if there exists a unitary operator
U
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
X_{i}^{\downarrow}=UX_{i}{U}^{*}
for each
i\in[m]
.

By considering majorization as a preorder on the space of self-adjoint operators, Fan’s majorization relation may be written as follows:

\displaystyle A_{1}+A_{2}\preceq A_{1}^{\downarrow}+A_{2}^{\downarrow}.

(1.8)

This relation generalizes to conic combinations of self-adjoint operators.

The obstacle to extending the argument above to cases where
n>1
is that, in general, there may not exist a pure quantum state tuple
(\psi_{J})_{J\subseteq[n]}
such that the summands
p_{J}\psi_{J}\otimes M^{\otimes J^{c}}
are perfectly aligned. However, at the conjectured optimal tuple
({v_{M}{v_{M}}^{*}}^{\otimes J})_{J\subseteq[n]}
, the quantum state of each particle is a convex combination of the perfectly aligned operators
M
and
v_{M}v_{M}^{*}
. This suggests that local perfect alignment may yield minimum entropy when global perfect alignment is impossible.

The main contribution of this work is a formalization of this suggestion for a sum of two tensor products of two positive semi-definite operators.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
denote two positive semi-definite operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
and
C_{1},C_{2}
denote two positive semi-definite operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{C}}
. Then

\displaystyle B_{1}\otimes C_{1}+B_{2}\otimes C_{2}\preceq B_{1}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{1}^{\downarrow}+B_{2}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{2}^{\downarrow}.

(1.9)

We call this relation a
separable Fan majorization relation
. We use it to resolve the spin alignment conjecture to the
2
-letter level (Prop.
4.4
). We prove it by establishing linear programming refinements of Ky Fan’s majorization relation (Thm.
3.2
). A crucial ingredient of the proof is an observation about the geometry of subspaces that are spanned by tensor product vectors (Prop.
4.2
).

Structure of the paper

Sec.
2
contains notation and relevant mathematical background. Sec.
3
contains an estimation schema based on linear programming that refines Fan’s majorization relation. In Sec.
4
, the schema is used to establish Eq.
1.9
. The paper is concluded in Sec.

A

2
Notation and background

We refer to a set of
k
elements as a
k
-set. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x^{\downarrow}
denotes the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose coordinates are the coordinates of
x
albeit ordered (weakly) decreasingly. For
k\in[d]
, the sum of the
k
largest coordinates of
x
is denoted by
s_{k}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{\downarrow}
. For a subset
T\subseteq[d]
, we write
\mathtt{1}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote indicator function of
T
. That is, the vector that satisfies for each
i\in T,(\mathtt{1}_{T})_{i}=1
and for each
i\notin T,(\mathtt{1}_{T})_{i}=0
. A vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to majorize a vector

y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

\displaystyle s_{k}(x)\geq s_{k}(y)

(2.1)

for each
k\in[d-1]
and
s_{d}(x)=s_{d}(y)
. This statement is denoted by
y\preceq x
. Majorization is defined for self-adjoint operators spectrally (see Sec. 7 of Ref.
[
2
]
). If
A,A^{\prime}
are self-adjoint operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\lambda(A^{\prime})\preceq\lambda(A)
, then
A
is said to majorize
A^{\prime}
, and we denote that by
A^{\prime}\preceq A
. As shorthand, we write
s_{k}(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(A)
. As a relation on a real vector space, majorization is reflexive and transitive, that is, a preorder. See Ref.
[
18
]
for a more comprehensive account of majorization theory.

The statement that a vector space
W
is a subspace of a vector space
W^{\prime}
is denoted by
W\leq W^{\prime}
. We denote the dimension of a subspace
W
by
\dim(W)
and the orthogonal projection operator, or projector, onto
W
by
P_{W}
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For
k\in[d]
, Fan’s maximum principle, proven in Ref.
[
10
]
, gives an extremal formula for the sum of the
k
largest eigenvalues of
A
:

\displaystyle s_{k}(A)=\max_{\dim(W)=k}\tr(P_W A).

(2.2)

Fan’s majorization relation Eq.
1.8
is an immediate consequence of this formula. Let
\xi_{1}(A),\xi_{2}(A),\ldots,\xi_{d}(A)
denote orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that for each
i\in[d]
,
A\xi_{i}(A)=\lambda_{i}(A)\xi_{i}(A)
. These vectors and the corresponding eigenvalues make up an orthonormal spectral decomposition of
A
. For brevity, we use
\Pi_{i}(A)
to denote the rank-
1
projector
\xi_{i}(A)\xi_{i}(A)^{*}
. For each
\ell\in[d]
, we denote the linear span of the first
\ell
of these eigenvectors by

\displaystyle V_{\downarrow\ell}(A):=\langle\{\xi_{i}(A)\}_{i=1}^{\ell}\rangle.

(2.3)

We refer to
V_{\downarrow\ell}(A)
as a largest
\ell
eigenvalue space of

A
. We denote the projector onto this space by
P_{\downarrow\ell}(A)
. It is useful to express the data of such a spectral decomposition by a complete flag
[
11
]
,

\displaystyle V_{\bullet}(A):=(0\leq V_{\downarrow 1}(A)\leq V_{\downarrow 2}(A)\leq\cdots\leq V_{\downarrow d}(A)=\mathbb{C}^{d}).

(2.4)

If
\lambda(A)
contains repeated values, that is,
A
is degenerate, then
V_{\bullet}(A)
is not uniquely defined. Unless stated otherwise, we assume a choice is fixed throughout the analysis.

A linear program is an optimization problem whose objective function is linear and whose feasible set is a (convex) polyhedron. We write ‘s.t.’ as shorthand for the phrase ‘subject to’. See Ref.
[
23
]
for more details on the theory of linear programming.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a partially ordered set. A subset
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is called a downward closed set if for all
x\in T
and
y\in\mathcal{T}
,
y\leq x\implies y\in T.
The dual notion, meaning under replacing the symbol
\leq
with the symbol
\geq
, is called an upward closed set. The complement of a downward closed set is an upward closed set. For a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, its downward closure
\downarrow S:=\{y\in\mathcal{T}\mid\exists\,x\in S,y\leq x\}
is the smallest downward closed set that contains it. The upward closure
\uparrow S
is defined dually. See J.B. Nation’s lecture notes on order theory
[
20
]
.

3
Estimation schema

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be self-adjoint operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and fix
k\in[d]
. Our aim is to find upper bounds on
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})
that are generally tighter than
s_{k}(A_{1})+s_{k}(A_{2})
, the upper bound from Fan’s majorization relation Eq.
1.8
.

For a
k
-dimensional subspace
W\leq\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
i\in[d]
, we denote the overlaps

\displaystyle x^{(1)}_{W,i}:=\tr(P_W \Pi_i (A_1)),\;x^{(2)}_{W,i}:=\tr(P_W \Pi_i(A_2)),

(3.1)

and define the real vectors
x_{W}^{(1)}:=(x^{(1)}_{W,i})_{i=1}^{d},x_{W}^{(2)}:=(x^{(2)}_{W,i})_{i=1}^{d}
, and
x_{W}:=x^{(1)}_{W}\oplus x^{(2)}_{W}
. We rewrite
\tr(P_W (A_1 + A_2))
in terms of these vectors.

\displaystyle\tr(P_W (A_1 + A_2))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(A_{1})x^{(1)}_{W,i}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(A_{2})x^{(2)}_{W,i}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x_{W}.

It can be verified by inspection that
x_{W}
satisfies the linear constraints:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle x^{(1)}_{W,i},x^{(2)}_{W,i}\geq 0,\forall\,i\in[d],

(3.3)

\displaystyle x^{(1)}_{W,i},x^{(2)}_{W,i}\leq 1,\forall\,i\in[d],

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}x^{(1)}_{W,i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x^{(2)}_{W,i}=k.

We refer to these constraints as the
basic constraints
. The set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
that satisfy the basic constraints is a bounded polyhedron that we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}
. We can use this polyhedron to prove the inequality
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})\leq s_{k}(A_{1})+s_{k}(A_{2})
in a rather fancy way. Observe that

\displaystyle s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})

\displaystyle=\max_{\dim(W)=k}\tr(P_W (A_1 + A_2))

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\max_{\dim(W)=k}(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x_{W}\leq(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}\bar{x},

(3.5)

where
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is an optimal point for the linear program

\displaystyle\max\>(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x

(3.6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\>x\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}.

A routine calculation shows that
\mathtt{1}_{[k]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[k]}
is an optimal point.

To obtain sharper estimates, we need to consider more constraints. To that end, we introduce
alignment terms
.

Definition 3.1
.

For each pair
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[d]\times[d]
, we define the alignment term

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})):

\displaystyle=\max_{\dim(W)=k}\tr(P_W (P_{\da\ell_1} (A_1) + P_{\da\ell_2} (A_2)))

(3.7)

\displaystyle=s_{k}(P_{\downarrow\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+P_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(A_{2})).

(3.8)

These
d^{2}
positive numbers are meant to capture the overlaps between the largest eigenvalue spaces of
A_{1}
and the largest eigenvalue spaces of
A_{2}
. The flag pair argument
(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))
is included to emphasize the dependence of alignment terms on the assumed spectral decompositions of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. When it is clear from context, we omit it and write
\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}
.

For any
k
-dimensional subspace
W\leq\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
x_{W}
satisfies, by definition, the linear constraint

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}x_{W}\leq\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.9)

We call this an
alignment constraint
. We denote the polyhedron of points in
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}
that satisfy all alignment constraints by
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))
or simply
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
u_{k}(A_{1},A_{2})
denote the optimal value of the linear program

\displaystyle\max\>(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x

(3.10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\>x\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

Then
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})\leq u_{k}(A_{1},A_{2})
.

Proof.

The bound follows from Fan’s maximum principle Eq.
2.2
, the fact that
x_{W}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1})V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))
for every
k
-dimensional subspace
W
, and the equalities in Eq.
3.2
. ∎

Several remarks are in order.

1.

Although
\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}
is given by a maximization over
k
-dimensional subspaces, we find it is more amenable to analysis than
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})
. After all, the spectrum of a sum of two projectors is closely related to the relative position of their supports (see, for example, Ref.
[
12
]
). This is a crucial ingredient in our proof of Prop.
4.3
.

2.

If
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a polyhedron that satisfies
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))\subseteq\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}
, then the optimal value of the linear program

\displaystyle\max\>(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x

(3.11)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\>x\in\mathcal{P}.

is an upper bound on
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})
that is at most
s_{k}(A_{1})+s_{k}(A_{2})
. For example,
\mathcal{P}
could be the polyhedron of points that satisfy the basic constraints and a subset of the alignment constraints. Or
\mathcal{P}
could be equal to
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(\tilde{A_{1}}),V_{\bullet}(\tilde{A_{2}}))
where
\tilde{A_{1}},\tilde{A_{2}}
are self-adjoint operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))\leq\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(\tilde{A_{1}}),V_{\bullet}(\tilde{A_{2}}))

(3.12)

holds for each pair
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[d]\times[d]
.

3.

The upper bound
u_{k}(A_{1},A_{2})
is in fact independent of the choice of spectral decompositions of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. To see this, let us suppose that for some
t\in[d-1]
,
\lambda_{t}(A_{1})=\lambda_{t+1}(A_{1})
. Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
that acts as the identity on
\langle\{\xi_{t}(A_{1}),\xi_{t+1}(A_{1})\}\rangle^{\perp}
but is otherwise arbitrary. Let
V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1})
denote the flag

\displaystyle(0\leq UV_{\downarrow 1}(A_{1})\leq UV_{\downarrow 2}(A_{1})\leq\cdots\leq UV_{\downarrow d}(A_{1})=\mathbb{C}^{d}).

(3.13)

Notice that for all
\ell\neq t
,
UV_{\downarrow\ell}(A_{1})=V_{\downarrow\ell}(A_{1})
. This implies that for all
\ell_{1}\neq t
and
\ell_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))=\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.14)

Now, suppose that
x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))
. Let
x^{\prime(1)}
denote the vector with coordinates

\displaystyle x^{\prime(1)}_{t}

\displaystyle:=x_{t}+x_{t+1}-\min(1,x_{t}+x_{t+1})

(3.15)

\displaystyle x^{\prime(1)}_{t+1}

\displaystyle:=\min(1,x_{t}+x_{t+1})

(3.16)

and otherwise
x^{\prime(1)}_{i}:=x^{(1)}_{i}
. The objective value at
x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}
equals the objective value at
x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}
. It can be verified by inspection that
x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}
satisfies the basic constraints. For
\ell_{1}\neq t
and every
\ell_{2}
,

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

\displaystyle=(\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.19)

As for the alignment constraints associated with pairs of the form
(t,\ell_{2})
, we argue in two cases. First, if
\min(1,x_{t}+x_{t+1})=x_{t}+x_{t+1}
, then

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[t]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})=(\mathtt{1}_{[t]\setminus\{t\}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}).

(3.20)

If
t=1
, then

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[t]\setminus\{t\}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]}^{T}x^{(2)}

(3.21)

\displaystyle\leq\min(k,\ell_{2})

(3.22)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(1,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.23)

Otherwise,

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[t]\setminus\{t\}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

\displaystyle=(\mathtt{1}_{[t-1]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(t-1,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{(t-1,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))

(3.26)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(t,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.27)

Second, if
\min(1,x_{t}+x_{t+1})=1
, then

\displaystyle(\mathtt{1}_{[t]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})

\displaystyle=(\mathtt{1}_{[t+1]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}(x^{(1)}\oplus x^{(2)})-1

(3.28)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(t+1,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))-1

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{(t+1,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))-1

(3.30)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{(t,\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2})).

(3.31)

Hence,
x^{\prime(1)}\oplus x^{(2)}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V^{\prime}_{\bullet}(A_{1}),V_{\bullet}(A_{2}))
. These considerations extend to cases where there are other degeneracies in either
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
.

3.1
Total unimodularity of the constraints

A useful observation about the alignment constraints is that vectors of the form
\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]}
can be made to have the
consecutive ones property
by a single basis permutation. Let
\sigma:[d]\rightarrow[d]
denote the mirror permutation with the action
\ell\mapsto d-\ell+1
for each
\ell\in[d]
. Applying this permutation to the first
d
elements of the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, corresponding to the first summand, yields

\displaystyle\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]}\ \mapsto\mathtt{1}_{\sigma([\ell_{1}])}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]}=(0,\ldots 0,\underbrace{1,\ldots 1}_{\ell_{1}\text{times}}\mid\underbrace{1,\ldots 1,}_{\ell_{2}\text{times}}0,\ldots 0).

(3.32)

The basic constraints are also given by vectors that have the consecutive ones property.

This is significant because a matrix with the consecutive ones property, also known as an interval matrix, is
totally unimodular
. That means the determinant of every one of its square submatrices equals
+1,0,
or
-1
. This implies that if the alignment terms are integers, then the extremal points of
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}
are integral, meaning their coordinates are integers (see Ch. 19 of Ref.
[
23
]
). From the basic constraints, this means that their coordinates are equal to
0
or
1
. Since a submatrix of a totally unimodular matrix is totally unimodular, the same is true for the extremal points of any polyhedron in
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}
that is defined by a subset of the alignment constraints.

3.2
The meaning of alignment terms

We move on to discussing the meaning of alignment terms. To start, we observe that Fan’s maximum principle Eq.
2.2
implies the upper bound

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}\leq\min(\ell_{1},k)+\min(\ell_{2},k),

(3.33)

which is satisfied with equality if
P_{\downarrow\ell_{1}}(A_{1})
and
P_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(A_{2})
are perfectly aligned. We observe that Lidskii’s majorization relation (see Sec. III.4 of Ref.
[
4
]
) implies the lower bound

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}\geq\min(k,\max(0,\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-d))+\min(k,\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}).

(3.34)

When
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k
, the upper bound and lower bound coincide, implying the identity
\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
. This says that the cases where some information about the operators
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
is retained are the ones where
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}>k
.

If the upper bound Eq.
3.33
is satisfied with equality, then the alignment constraint corresponding to the pair
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
is redundant within
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)}
. On the other hand, the slackness statement
\lceil\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}\rceil<\min(\ell_{1},k)+\min(\ell_{2},k)
implies an upper bound on
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})
with a combinatorial meaning. In words,
\lceil\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}\rceil-k
is the maximum number of eigenvalue pairs whose indices are in
[\ell_{1}]
and
[\ell_{2}]
, respectively, that can appear (with coefficient
1
) together in the objective
(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x
. The remaining eigenvalue pairs have to be staggered. Notice that, per the discussion in the previous paragraph, the slackness statement implies
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}>k
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
m:=\lceil\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}\rceil-k
and suppose that it satisfies
m<\min(\ell_{1},k)+\min(\ell_{2},k)-k
. Denote the tuple of staggered eigenvalue sums

\displaystyle\mu:=(\lambda_{i}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{2}}(A_{2}))_{i=m+1}^{k}\oplus(\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i}(A_{2}))_{i=m+1}^{k}.

(3.35)

Then
s_{k}(A_{1}+A_{2})\leq s_{m}(A_{1})+s_{m}(A_{2})+s_{k-m}(\mu)
.

Proof.

By Thm.
3.2
, it suffices to show that the optimal value of the linear program

\displaystyle\max\>(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}x

(3.36)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\>x\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{(k)},\>(\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{1}]}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{[\ell_{2}]})^{T}x\leq m+k

is
s_{m}(A_{1})+s_{m}(A_{2})+s_{k-m}(\mu)
. By total unimodularity, the extremal points of the feasible set of this program are integral (see Sec.
3.1
). Hence, it suffices to maximize
(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}(\mathtt{1}_{S_{1}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{S_{2}})
over subset pairs
(S_{1},S_{2})
that satisfy
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[d],|S_{1}|=|S_{2}|=k
and
|S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]|+|S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]|\leq m+k.

Given such a
k
-set pair
(S_{1},S_{2})
, we may assume without loss of generality that
S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]=[r_{1}]
and
S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]=[r_{2}]
where
r_{1}:=|S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]|
and
r_{2}:=|S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]|
. Otherwise, the intersections
S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]
and
S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]
may be adjusted to improve the objective value. By the same token, we may assume that
S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]^{c}=\{\ell_{1}+1,\ldots,\ell_{1}+k-r_{1}\}
and
S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]^{c}=\{\ell_{2}+1,\ldots,\ell_{2}+k-r_{2}\}
. Lastly, we argue that the alignment constraint may be assumed to be satisfied with equality. If
|S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]|+|S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]|\neq m+k
, then
S_{1}
may be adjusted to improve the objective value by replacing elements in
S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]^{c}
with elements in
[\ell_{1}]
until the constraint is satisfied with equality or
S_{1}\cap[\ell_{1}]^{c}
becomes empty. If the latter happens before the former,
S_{2}
may be adjusted to improve the objective value by replacing elements in
S_{2}\cap[\ell_{2}]^{c}
with elements in
[\ell_{2}]
until the constraint is satisfied with equality. Hence, without loss of generality, we assume that
r_{1}+r_{2}=m+k
. From the fact that
\max(r_{1},r_{2})\leq k
, we conclude that
m\leq\min(r_{1},r_{2})
.

With these assumptions on
(S_{1},S_{2})
, the objective function is

\displaystyle(\lambda(A_{1})\oplus\lambda(A_{2}))^{T}(\mathtt{1}_{S_{1}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{S_{2}})

\displaystyle=s_{m}(A_{1})+s_{m}(A_{2})

(3.37)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=m+1}^{r_{1}}\lambda_{i}(A_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}-m}\lambda_{i+\ell_{2}}(A_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k-r_{1}}\lambda_{i+\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+k-r_{1}}\lambda_{i}(A_{2}).

We rewrite the sums in the second and third lines.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=m+1}^{r_{1}}\lambda_{i}(A_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}-m}\lambda_{i+\ell_{2}}(A_{2})=\sum_{i=m+1}^{r_{1}}\lambda_{i}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{2}}(A_{2}),

(3.38)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-r_{1}}\lambda_{i+\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+k-r_{1}}\lambda_{i}(A_{2})=\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+k-r_{1}}\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i}(A_{2}).

Then, by the definition of
\mu
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=m+1}^{r_{1}}(\lambda_{i}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{2}}(A_{2}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+k-r_{1}}(\lambda_{i-m+\ell_{1}}(A_{1})+\lambda_{i}(A_{2}))

(3.39)

\displaystyle\leq s_{k-m}(\mu).

Equality is realized by maximizing over
r_{1}
. ∎

3.3
Simultaneously diagonalizable summands

In this subsection, we outline a strategy for proving that the linear programming upper bound given by Thm.
3.2
is tight in the case of simultaneously diagonalizable summands. We complete the proof in App.
A
.

Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be two simultaneously diagonalizable self-adjoint operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are diagonal in the standard basis
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
. Furthermore, we take
V_{\bullet}(D_{1})
and
V_{\bullet}(D_{2})
to be complete flags corresponding to diagonal spectral decompositions of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, respectively. For each
i\in[d]
, we denote
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{1}):=q_{i}^{*}D_{1}q_{i}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{2}):=q_{i}^{*}D_{2}q_{i}
. To capture the order of the eigenvalues, we define the sets

\displaystyle\Omega^{1}_{\ell}:=\{i\in[d]\mid P_{\downarrow\ell}(D_{1})q_{i}=q_{i}\},\Omega^{2}_{\ell}:=\{i\in[d]\mid P_{\downarrow\ell}(D_{2})q_{i}=q_{i}\}.

(3.40)

for each
\ell\in[d]
. These are the sets of coordinates of the largest
\ell
values in
\tilde{\lambda}(D_{1}):=(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{1}))_{i=1}^{d}
and
\tilde{\lambda}(D_{2}):=(\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{2}))_{i=1}^{d}
, respectively. We use the two subset chains
\{\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\}_{\ell_{1}=1}^{d}
and
\{\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\}_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d}
to define two total orders,
\geq^{1}
and
\geq^{2}
, on
[d]
. For
x,x^{\prime}\in[d]
, we write
x\geq^{1}x^{\prime}
if

\displaystyle\min\{\ell\mid x\in\Omega^{1}_{\ell}\}\geq\min\{\ell\mid x^{\prime}\in\Omega^{1}_{\ell}\}.

(3.41)

Similarly, we write
x\geq^{2}x^{\prime}
if

\displaystyle\min\{\ell\mid x\in\Omega^{2}_{\ell}\}\geq\min\{\ell\mid x^{\prime}\in\Omega^{2}_{\ell}\}.

(3.42)

Observe that
x\geq^{1}x^{\prime}
implies
\tilde{\lambda}_{x}(D_{1})\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}}(D_{1})
and
x\geq^{2}x^{\prime}
implies
\tilde{\lambda}_{x}(D_{2})\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}}(D_{2})
.

Now, we compute the alignment terms of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[d]\times[d]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(D_{1}),V_{\bullet}(D_{2}))=\min(k,|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|)+\min(k,|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cup\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|).

(3.43)

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(D_{1}),V_{\bullet}(D_{2}))

\displaystyle=\max_{\dim(W)=k}\tr(P_W ( P_{\da\ell_1} (D_1) + P_{\da\ell_2} (D_2)))

(3.44)

\displaystyle=s_{k}(P_{\downarrow\ell_{1}}(D_{1})+P_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(D_{2}))

(3.45)

\displaystyle=s_{k}(\sum_{i\in\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}}q_{i}q_{i}^{*}+\sum_{i\in\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}q_{i}q_{i}^{*}).

(3.46)

If
k\leq|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
, then it equals
2k
. If
|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|<k<|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cup\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
, then it equals
2|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|+k-|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|=|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|+k
. Lastly, if
k\geq|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cup\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
, then it equals
|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}|+|\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
which equals
|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|+|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cup\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
. ∎

Since the alignment terms are integers, the coordinates of the extremal points of
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(D_{1}),V_{\bullet}(D_{2}))
are in
\{0,1\}
. Therefore, the linear programming upper bound
u_{k}(D_{1},D_{2})
equals the optimal value of the following combinatorial optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max\;(\tilde{\lambda}(D_{1})\oplus\tilde{\lambda}(D_{2}))^{T}(\mathtt{1}_{S_{1}}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{S_{2}})

(3.47)

over subsets pairs
(S_{1},S_{2})
satisfying
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[d],|S_{1}|=|S_{2}|=k
, and for all
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[d]\times[d]
,

\displaystyle|S_{1}\cap\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}|+|S_{2}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|\leq\min(k,|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|)+\min(k,|\Omega^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cup\Omega^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|).

(3.48)

We call a subset pair
(S_{1},S_{2})
that satisfies all of these constraints a
feasible pair
. Notice that the objective here differs from the one in Thm.
3.2
by a relabeling of the coordinates of the linear form.

Next, we observe that
s_{k}(D_{1}+D_{2})
equals

\displaystyle\max_{S\subseteq[d],|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}q_{i}^{*}(D_{1}+D_{2})q_{i}=\max_{S\subseteq[d],|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{1})+\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(D_{2})

(3.49)

which can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\max_{(S,S),|S|=k}\;(\tilde{\lambda}(D_{1})\oplus\tilde{\lambda}(D_{2}))^{T}(\mathtt{1}_{S}\oplus\mathtt{1}_{S}).

(3.50)

We call set pairs of the form
(S,S)

symmetric
. Therefore, to prove that
u_{k}(D_{1},D_{2})=s_{k}(D_{1}+D_{2})
, it suffices to show that for an arbitrary feasible pair
(S_{1},S_{2})
, there exists a feasible symmetric pair
(S,S)
such that the objective Eq.
3.47
at
(S,S)
is greater than or equal to the objective at
(S_{1},S_{2})
.

Theorem 3.5
.

It holds that

\displaystyle u_{k}(D_{1},D_{2})=s_{k}(D_{1}+D_{2}).

(3.51)

Proof.

Deferred to appendix App.
A
.

.

Fix two positive integers
d_{B}
and

\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}

C_{1},C_{2}

\mathbb{C}^{d_{C}}
. The goal is to prove the majorization relation

\displaystyle B_{1}\otimes C_{1}+B_{2}\otimes C_{2}\preceq B_{1}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{1}^{\downarrow}+B_{2}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{2}^{\downarrow}.

(4.1)

We start with an example showing that the assumption of positive semi-definiteness cannot be relaxed in general.

Example 4.1
.

Consider the following operators on
\mathbb{C}^{2}

\tilde{B}_{1}^{\downarrow}\otimes\tilde{C}_{1}^{\downarrow}+\tilde{B}_{2}^{\downarrow}\otimes\tilde{C}_{2}^{\downarrow}
is null, and so its spectrum is
(0,0,0,0)\not\succeq(1,1,-1,-1)
.

Ordering the spectrum

Let

\tilde{B}

\tilde{C}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(\tilde{B})\lambda_{j}(\tilde{C})\leq\lambda_{i^{\prime}}(\tilde{B})\lambda_{j^{\prime}}(\tilde{C})

i\geq i^{\prime}
and

\tilde{B}

4.2
Bounding the alignment terms

To bound the alignment term
\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(B_{1}\otimes C_{1}),V_{\bullet}(B_{2}\otimes C_{2}))
, we examine spaces spanned by product vectors indexed by elements of downward closed sets. Upon our examination, we find a generalization of a well known fact about subspace intersections. Specifically, that for any two orthonormal bases
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
and
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and for any two downward closed sets
Y,Y^{\prime}\subseteq[d]
(that is, both are intervals that start at
1
), it holds that

\displaystyle\dim(\langle\{f_{i}\}_{i\in Y}\rangle\cap\langle\{g_{i}\}_{i\in Y^{\prime}}\rangle^{\perp})\geq|Y\setminus Y^{\prime}|.

(4.7)

Let each of
\{f_{i}^{(B)}\}_{i=1}^{d_{B}}
and
\{g_{i}^{(B)}\}_{i=1}^{d_{B}}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
, and each of
\{f_{j}^{(C)}\}_{j=1}^{d_{C}}
and
\{g_{j}^{(C)}\}_{j=1}^{d_{C}}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d_{C}}
. For the sake of readability, we denote for
i\in[d_{B}]
,

\displaystyle F^{(B)}_{\downarrow i}:=\langle\{f^{(B)}_{1},\ldots,f^{(B)}_{i}\}\rangle\,,\,G^{(B)}_{\uparrow i}:=\langle\{g_{i}^{(B)},\ldots,g_{d_{B}}^{(B)}\}\rangle,

(4.8)

for
j\in[d_{C}]
,

\displaystyle F^{(C)}_{\downarrow j}:=\langle\{f^{(C)}_{1},\ldots,f^{(C)}_{j}\}\rangle\,,\,G^{(C)}_{\uparrow j}:=\langle\{g^{(C)}_{j},\ldots,g^{(C)}_{d_{C}}\}\rangle,

(4.9)

and, finally, for a subset
S\subseteq[d_{B}]\times[d_{C}]
,

\displaystyle F_{S}:=\langle\{f_{i}^{(B)}\otimes f_{j}^{(C)}\mid(i,j)\in S\}\rangle,

(4.10)

\displaystyle G_{S}:=\langle\{g_{i}^{(B)}\otimes g_{j}^{(C)}\mid(i,j)\in S\}\rangle.

(4.11)

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Upsilon
and
\Upsilon^{\prime}
be two downward closed sets in
([d_{B}]\times[d_{C}],\geq^{\times})
. Then

\displaystyle\dim(F_{\Upsilon}\cap{G_{\Upsilon^{\prime}}}^{\perp})\geq|\Upsilon\setminus\Upsilon^{\prime}|.

(4.12)

Proof.

Observe that
{G_{\Upsilon^{\prime}}}^{\perp}=G_{{\Upsilon^{\prime}}^{c}}
. Denote the difference
\Delta:=\Upsilon\setminus\Upsilon^{\prime}
. Since
\downarrow\Delta\subseteq\Upsilon
and
\uparrow\Delta\subseteq{\Upsilon^{\prime}}^{c}
,
it suffices to prove
\dim(F_{\downarrow\Delta}\cap G_{\uparrow\Delta})\geq|\Delta|.
We do this by showing that there exists a subspace whose dimension is not so large, yet the dimensions of its intersections with
F_{\downarrow\Delta}
and
G_{\uparrow\Delta}
are relatively large.

As the difference of two downward closed sets,
\Delta
is interval-like. That is, if
x,y\in\Delta
and
x\leq^{\times}z\leq^{\times}y
for some
z\in[d_{B}]\times[d_{C}]
, then
z\in\Delta
. This implies

Proposition 4.3

For each pair
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in[d_{B}d_{C}]\times[d_{B}d_{C}]

\displaystyle\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(B_{1}\otimes C_{1}),V_{\bullet}(B_{2}\otimes C_{2}))\leq\alpha_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}^{(k)}(V_{\bullet}(B_{1}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{1}^{\downarrow}),V_{\bullet}(B_{2}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{2}^{\downarrow})).

V_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(B_{2}\otimes C_{2})

contains a subspace of dimension

that is orthogonal to

. Hence, the multiplicity of

in the spectrum of

is at least
|\Upsilon^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\setminus\Upsilon^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|+|\Upsilon^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\setminus\Upsilon^{1}_{\ell_{1}}|

\displaystyle\lambda(P_{\downarrow\ell_{1}}(B_{1}\otimes C_{1})+P_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(B_{2}\otimes C_{2}))\preceq(2,\ldots,2,1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0),

|\Upsilon^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\cap\Upsilon^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|
, and the multiplicity of

|\Upsilon^{1}_{\ell_{1}}\setminus\Upsilon^{2}_{\ell_{2}}|+|\Upsilon^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\setminus\Upsilon^{1}_{\ell_{1}}|

equals
\lambda(P_{\downarrow\ell_{1}}(B_{1}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{1}^{\downarrow})+P_{\downarrow\ell_{2}}(B_{2}^{\downarrow}\otimes C_{2}^{\downarrow}))
.∎

is greater than or equal to the objective value at
(S_{1},S)
. We do so by showing that for each
i\in[k]
, there exist at least
i
elements in
S
that precede
x_{i}
in the
\geq^{1}
order.

Lemma A.7
.

For each
i\in[k]
,
|S\cap\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}|\geq i
.

Proof.

We argue by strong induction on
p:=k-i+1
. We start with the base case
p=1
. As in the proof of Lem.
A.3
, for each
i^{\prime}
, there exists
i\in[k]
such that

\displaystyle i+i^{\prime}=k+|\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}|

(A.14)

and either
r_{i^{\prime}}=1
, or
r_{i^{\prime}}>1
and
|\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}|-|\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}-1}|=1
. If
r_{i^{\prime}}=1
, then
i^{\prime}=1
and
y_{1}\in\Omega^{1}_{m_{k}}
. If
r_{i^{\prime}}>1
, then
y_{i^{\prime}}\in\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{m_{k}}
. Therefore,
S\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{m_{k}}
, and so
|S\cap\Omega^{1}_{m_{k}}|=k
.

Define
\gamma(1):=k
. For
i^{\prime}\in[k]
such that
i^{\prime}>1
, define
\gamma(i^{\prime})\in[k]
to be minimal such that

\displaystyle\gamma(i^{\prime})+i^{\prime}=k+|\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}|>k+|\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}-1}|.

(A.15)

Suppose that
p>1
and the statement is true for all elements of
[p-1]
. Let
T=\{i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{|T|}\}
be the subset of indices
i^{\prime}\in[k]
that satisfy
k-\gamma(i^{\prime})+1<p
, denoted so
i^{\prime}_{1}<\ldots<i^{\prime}_{|T|}
. Notice that
i^{\prime}_{1}=1
. Observe that for
i^{\prime}\in T^{c}
,
y_{i^{\prime}}\in\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}
implies
y_{i^{\prime}}\in\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}
.

For each
i^{\prime}\in T
, we have the equality

\displaystyle|\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}|=\gamma(i^{\prime})+i^{\prime}-k

(A.16)

and
k-\gamma(i^{\prime})+1<p
. By the inductive hypothesis,
|\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap S|\geq\gamma(i^{\prime})
.
Because of the containment
(\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap S)\cap(\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\cap S)\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}
and the inclusion-exclusion inequality

\displaystyle|(\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap S)\cap(\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\cap S)|\geq\gamma(i^{\prime})+i^{\prime}-k

(A.17)

the equality Eq.
A.16
implies

\displaystyle(\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap S)\cap(\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\cap S)=\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}},

(A.18)

which implies
\Omega^{1}_{m_{\gamma(i^{\prime})}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\subseteq S
. This in turn implies
\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\subseteq S
for all
i^{\prime}\in T
. The constraint Eq.
A.2
implies

\displaystyle|\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}\cap\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}_{|T|}}}|\geq k-p+1+i^{\prime}_{|T|}-k=i^{\prime}_{|T|}-p+1.

(A.19)

Since
({\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}}\cap{\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}_{|T|}}}})\subseteq({\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}_{|T|}}}}\cap S)
, by inclusion-exclusion

\displaystyle|({\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}}\cap{\Omega^{2}_{r_{i^{\prime}_{|T|}}}})\cap\{y_{i^{\prime}_{1}},y_{i^{\prime}_{2}},\ldots,y_{i^{\prime}_{|T|}}\}|

\displaystyle\geq i^{\prime}_{|T|}-p+1+|T|-i^{\prime}_{|T|}

(A.20)

\displaystyle=|T|-p+1

(A.21)

Together with the elements whose subscripts are in
T^{c}
, this implies

|S\cap\Omega^{1}_{m_{k-p+1}}|\geq k-|T|+|T|-p+1=k-p+1.
∎

Lemma A.8
.

The objective value at
(S,S)
is greater than or equal to the
objective value at
(S_{1},S)
.

Proof.

We relabel the elements of
S
to reflect their order according to
\geq^{1}
. Let us denote them with
x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k}
such that
x^{\prime}_{k}\geq^{1}\cdots\geq^{1}x^{\prime}_{2}\geq^{1}x^{\prime}_{1}
.

By Lem.
A.7
, for each
i\in[k]
, there exist at least
i
elements in
S
that are in
\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}
. In particular, this implies that
\{x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{i}\}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{m_{i}}
. Hence, for all
i\in[k]
,
x_{i}\geq^{1}x^{\prime}_{i}
.

The difference between objective value at
(S,S)
and the objective value at
(S_{1},S)
is
\tilde{\lambda}(D_{1})^{T}(\mathtt{1}_{S}-\mathtt{1}_{S_{1}})
which equals

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}_{i}}(D_{1})-\tilde{\lambda}_{x_{i}}(D_{1})\geq 0
. ∎
