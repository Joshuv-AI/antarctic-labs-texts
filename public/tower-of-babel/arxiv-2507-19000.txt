Edge-coloring problems with forbidden patterns and planted colors
Alexey Barsukov, Antoine Mottet, Davide Perinti

Abstract
Edge-coloring problems with forbidden patterns refer to decision problems whose input is a graph 𝔾 \mathbb{G} and where the goal is to determine whether the edges of 𝔾 \mathbb{G} can be colored (with a fixed finite set of colors) in a way that in the resulting colored graph ( 𝔾 , ξ ) (\mathbb{G},\xi) , none of a fixed set of edge-colored obstructions admits a homomorphism to ( 𝔾 , ξ ) (\mathbb{G},\xi) . In the coloring extension setting, some of the edges of 𝔾 \mathbb{G} are already colored and the goal is to find an extension of this coloring omitting the obstructions. We show that for certain sets of obstructions, there is a polynomial-time equivalence between the coloring problem and the extension problem. We also show that for natural sets of obstructions, such coloring problems exhibit a P vs. NP-complete complexity dichotomy.

Introduction

The following is a well-known instance of Ramsey’s theorem for finite graphs and 2 colors: it is impossible to color the edges of the complete graph on
R_{2}(3)=6
vertices with
r=2
colors in a way that avoids a monochromatic complete graph of size
n=3
.
More generally, Ramsey’s theorem states that for each value of
r
and
n
, there exists a number
R_{r}(n)
such that the complete graph on
R_{r}(n)
vertices cannot be colored with
r
colors while avoiding monochromatic copies of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
.
The exact values of
R_{r}(n)
are unknown even for moderate values of
r
and
n
, and even determining them computationally is hard.
In the following, the problem of deciding whether a given input graph admits an edge-coloring with
r
colors and without monochromatic
\mathbb{K}_{n}
is denoted by
\Col\bigl(\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(r)}\bigr)
, where
\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(i)}
is the colored
n
-clique whose edges are all colored with the color
i
.
The cliques in the list are called
forbidden patterns
or also
obstructions
.
Burr
[
14
]
proved that
\Col\bigl(\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(1)},\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(2)}\bigr)
is NP-hard for all
n\geq 3
.
In that field, such Ramsey-type problems have been considered for decades in the variants where the graphs
\mathbb{K}_{n}
are replaced by other graphs, mostly in the case where all obstructions are monochromatic and where every color has a single monochromatic obstruction.

1.1
The logical and computational relevance of Ramsey-type problems

Besides their combinatorial interest, problems of the form
\Col(\mathcal{F})
have appeared in connection with results in the area of ontology-mediated query answering
[
3
]
.
Consider the logic GF consisting of first-order formulas where the quantified variables must be guarded by an atomic formula.
In
[
3
, Theorems 7 and 11]
, it is established that answering union of conjunctive queries mediated by an ontology written in GF can be phrased as an instance of
\Col(\mathcal{F})
for suitably chosen obstructions
\mathcal{F}
depending on the query to be answered.
However, there, the obstructions are not necessarily monochromatic as is the case in the Ramsey setting, and obstructions are forbidden by
homomorphisms
.
Moreover, such problems are in a loose sense exactly those problems that can be described in the fragment of existential second order logic called
GMSNP
(Guarded Monotone SNP).
This logic was independently introduced by Madelaine
[
26
]
and Bienvenu, ten Cate, Lutz, and Wolter
[
3
]
.
It is an extension of Monotone Monadic SNP without inequality (MMSNP), introduced by Feder and Vardi
[
17
]
, and relates to MMSNP in the same way as
Courcelle’s MSO
2
logic relates to MSO.
Namely, second order quantifiers of arity greater than
1
are allowed in the syntax, so long as their range is guarded by the input predicates from the signature.
Then, the classes of the form
\{\mathbb{G}\mid\mathbb{G}\models\Phi\}
, where
\Phi
is a GMSNP sentence, coincide with the classes of yes-instances of the problem
\Col(\mathcal{F})
, where
\mathcal{F}
is a finite set of colored structures as above.

From the perspective of descriptive complexity it is natural to ask, for the problems that can be described in GMSNP, what complexity they can have.
An oft-mentioned open problem is whether there is a
complexity dichotomy
, i.e., if every such problem is either solvable in polynomial time or NP-complete
[
3
,
26
,
8
]
.
This happens to be true for Feder and Vardi’s logic MMSNP
[
17
]
, and the dichotomy has been proved for some other subclasses of GMSNP
[
6
,
4
,
18
]
.
Moreover, it is known that for variants of such coloring problems, for example, in the setting with forbidden
linearly ordered
graphs instead of colored graphs, there is no complexity dichotomy unless
P=NP
[
25
]
.
The same is true for the case of forbidden edge-colored subgraphs instead of forbidden homomorphic images by
[
25
, Theorem 6]
.

Moreover, problems of the form
\Col(\mathcal{F})
can all be seen as finite unions of
constraint satisfaction problems
(CSP) with countably infinite templates satisfying certain model-theoretic properties (they are so-called
reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures
).
A complexity dichotomy for such problems has been conjectured and proved to be true in many particular cases (see, e.g., the introduction of
[
29
]
for a recent account of the state-of-the-art in this area).
Thus, the complexity dichotomy for GMSNP is a consequence of the dichotomy conjectured for the CSPs mentioned here, and GMSNP gives a rich class of examples on which to put the CSP dichotomy conjecture to the test.

1.2
The complexity of vertex-coloring problems

In the version of the problem where vertex-colorings are considered instead of edge-colorings, the complexity of the problems has been well understood for many years.
Feder and Vardi
[
17
]
initially showed in their seminal paper that such problems are equivalent under randomized polynomial-time reductions to constraint satisfaction problems with finite domains; this reduction was later derandomized by Kun
[
24
]
.
Bulatov
[
13
]
and Zhuk
[
33
]
later proved that such constraint satisfaction problems obey a complexity dichotomy.
Building on Bulatov and Zhuk’s result, Bodirsky, Madelaine, and Mottet
[
8
]
obtained a new proof of the complexity dichotomy for vertex-coloring problems, using algebraic tools to provide the equivalence with finite-domain constraint satisfaction problems.
Applying the algebraic approach, a characterization of classes of obstructions
\mathcal{F}
that have a bounded treewidth duality was obtained in
[
27
]
.
An important part of the proofs above is a reduction to the so-called
precolored
setting, where some vertices of the graph given as input to the problem are already colored, and the problem is to extend this coloring in a way to avoid the obstructions.
This problem, denoted by
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
in the following, can be in principle harder than the non-precolored problem; however, it is shown in
[
8
]
that without loss of generality, the problems
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
and
\Col(\mathcal{F})
are computationally equivalent.

1.3
Contributions

In this paper, we provide new methods for investigating the complexity of such edge-coloring problems, inspired by the mentioned algebraic proof of the complexity dichotomy for vertex-coloring problems.

Reduction from
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
to
\Col(\mathcal{F})

Under certain conditions on
\mathcal{F}
, we give a polynomial-time equivalence between the coloring problem
\Col(\mathcal{F})
and the coloring extension problem
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
.
Our assumption is that, for every color
i
, there exists a partially colored graph
\mathbb{G}^{i}
with a distinguished uncolored edge
e
at great distance from any colored edge, and such that all valid extensions of this coloring of
\mathbb{G}^{i}
use the color
i
for
e
.
We call such a graph a
colored determiner
(for the color
i
).
This concept is closely related to the concept of color determiner that is standard in Ramsey theory, the difference being that a standard color determiner is a graph instead of a partially colored graph.
Thus, our assumption is
weaker
than asking for the existence of a standard color determiner.
In fact, this assumption on the obstructions is quite general, and indeed we cannot exclude that colored determiners exist for all sets of obstructions.
To support our claim that this assumption is perhaps vacuously true, we prove that this assumption holds in important cases:

•

when the class of
\mathcal{F}
-free colored graphs has the
edge-amalgamation property
and when there is a colored determiner for every color, but without the assumption of distance.
We moreover show that the edge-amalgamation property can be assumed without loss of generality (
\Cref
lemma:forcing-amalgamation);

•

when
\mathcal{F}
consists of monochromatic cycles or cliques, together with potentially all edge-colorings of
\mathbb{K}_{m}
, for some
m
.

Theorem 1

(Precoloring theorem, informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of connected finite edge-colored graphs for which colored determiners exist.
Then
\Col(\mathcal{F})
is equivalent under polynomial-time many-one reductions to
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
.

We establish
\Cref
theorem:precolored-informal by using an equivalence between the edge-coloring problems (in the precolored or non-precolored settings) and constraint satisfaction problems with an infinite constraint language.
This connection has been leveraged before in the vertex-coloring setting
[
8
]
as well as in the edge-coloring setting
[
7
,
1
]
.
This result can be found in
\Cref
sec:strategy.

Dichotomy result

For natural and interesting cases of families
\mathcal{F}
, we establish the equivalence of every edge-coloring problem
\Col(\mathcal{F})
with a constraint satisfaction problem with a finite domain.
Due to the Bulatov-Zhuk dichotomy theorem, this establishes a complexity dichotomy for a particular fragment of edge-coloring problems.

Theorem 2
.

theorem]thm:main-application
Let
m\geq 3
and
\mathcal{F}
be a family of colored graphs such that:

•

every colored graph in
\mathcal{F}
is a coloring of an odd cycle or of a clique of size
\geq 3
;

•

every colored cycle in
\mathcal{F}
is monochromatically colored;

•

if some non-monochromatic coloring of the clique
\mathbb{K}_{m}
is in
\mathcal{F}
, then
all
colorings of
\mathbb{K}_{m}
are in
\mathcal{F}
.

Then,
\Col(\mathcal{F})
is in P or NP-complete.
Moreover, the problem is (trivially) in P whenever there exists a color
i
for which all the monochromatic obstructions contain
\mathbb{K}_{m}
, and it is NP-complete otherwise.

Note that we only consider cycles of odd length and cliques with at least 3 vertices in
\Cref
thm:main-application.
This is because
\Col(\mathcal{F})
is the same problem as
\Col(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
whenever every structure in
\mathcal{F}
is homomorphically equivalent to a structure in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and conversely.
Even cycles are homomorphically equivalent to a single edge. Having a monochromatic graph of color
i
that homomorphically maps to a single edge in an obstruction set
\mathcal{F}
implies that
\Col(\mathcal{F})
is trivially equivalent to the coloring problem where the color
i
is discarded. For the same reason, our result generalizes to other settings, e.g., where we allow
\mathcal{F}
to contain colored graphs whose clique number coincides with their chromatic number.

Most of the known results concerning edge-coloring problems with forbidden patterns are in fact restricted to the case where the obstructions are monochromatic cliques (or at least 3-connected graphs), or the case where the obstructions are monochromatic cycles.
Ours is the only complexity result with mixed obstructions, also considering non-monochromatic obstructions.
As an example of a consequence of our result, we obtain the following refinement of the theorem by Burr
[
14
]
that
\Col\big(\mathbb{K}^{(1)}_{n},\mathbb{K}^{(2)}_{n}\big)
is NP-hard for all
n\geq 3
:

Corollary 3
.

For
n\geq 3
, the monochromatic
\mathbb{K}_{n}
problem is NP-hard on
\mathbb{K}_{n+1}
-free graphs.

Indeed, this problem is up to a trivial reduction the same as
\Col(\mathcal{F})
where
\mathcal{F}
contains the monochromatic colorings of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
, as well as all possible colorings of
\mathbb{K}_{n+1}
.
The hardness of this problem is then a consequence of
\Cref
thm:main-application.
The strategy of the proof is to show that for families
\mathcal{F}
as in
\Cref
thm:main-application, one can construct colored determiners and other types of gadgets introduced in Ramsey theory under the name of
signal senders
by Burr, Erdős, and Lovász
[
15
]
.
The existence of such gadgets has been confirmed in numerous cases involving monochromatic obstructions and very simple graphs (see
[
16
,
31
,
12
]
for papers dedicated to the construction of such gadgets).
Our result is also a contribution towards the study of such gadgets in the setting of homomorphically forbidden graphs, and proving their existence is the technical core of the present paper; these results can be found in
\Cref
sec:gadgets.

Inexpressibility results

Finally, we investigate the question of separation between the logics MMSNP and GMSNP, whose definable classes are of the form
\Col(\mathcal{F})
for vertex-colored and edge-colored families of structures, respectively.
It is already known that there exist families of edge-colored
structures

\mathcal{F}
for which
\Col(\mathcal{F})
is not equal to
\Col(\mathcal{G})
for
any
family of
vertex-
colored structures.
This is for example proved via a detour to ontology-mediated queries in
[
3
, Theorem 3.10, Theorem 4.2]
.
However, there does not seem to be a general understanding of such families
\mathcal{F}
and to our knowledge, no separating example was hitherto known for graphs.

We prove here that
\Col\bigl(\mathbb{K}_{3}^{(1)},\mathbb{K}_{3}^{(2)}\bigr)
and more generally
\Col\bigl(\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(r)}\bigr)
are such examples for
n\geq 3
and
r\geq 2
.
This answers an open question from
[
26
, Remark 4.19]
, where the former problem is called
Edge-No-Mono-Tri
, and also shows that
\Cref
thm:main-application does not follow from the existing dichotomy result for vertex colorings.
Our inexpressibility result in full generality is the following sufficient condition.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a finite set of edge-colored graphs with the following properties:

•

the class of
\mathcal{E}
-free edge-colored graphs is closed under amalgamation over vertices,

•

for every
K\geq 1
, there exists a graph
\mathbb{G}
that does not admit an
\mathcal{E}
-free coloring, and whose every
K
-element subgraph does.

Then there is no finite set
\mathcal{F}
of vertex-colored graphs such that
\Col(\mathcal{F})=\Col(\mathcal{E})
.

The second assumption in
\Cref
separation_gmsnp is mild and is equivalent to saying that the class of
\mathcal{E}
-free colorable graphs is not definable by a first-order sentence
[
32
]
.
This condition is clearly necessary to obtain a class that is definable in GMSNP and not in MMSNP, since any such coloring problem can be phrased with obstructions without using
any
colors.

Observe that the class of monochromatically-colored
\mathbb{K}_{n}
with
r
possible colors, for any
n\geq 3
and
r\geq 2
, satisfies the assumptions of
\Cref
separation_gmsnp.
Indeed, it is clear that the amalgamation of two colored graphs over vertices does not introduce any new copy of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
.
For the second assumption,
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
shows that there are any infinitely many critical graphs for monochromatic cliques.

1.4
Related work

Most of the related work in the area has been done in the case where the members of
\mathcal{F}
are forbidden as subgraphs.
In that setting, Burr, Nešetřil, and Rödl
[
16
]
showed the existence of signal senders and color determiners in the 2-color setting for families of monochromatic 3-connected graphs. Rödl and Siggers
[
31
]
extended this result in the setting with more colors, but where all members of
\mathcal{F}
are isomorphic.
Boyadzhiyska and Lesgourgues
[
12
]
obtained similar results in the case where
\mathcal{F}
consists of monochromatic cliques (of potentially different sizes for different colors).

3
Equivalence of uncolored and precolored problems

Colored determiners

The goal of this section is to prove that the problems
\Col(\mathcal{F})
and
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
are polynomial-time equivalent for certain families
\mathcal{F}
of edge-colored graphs.
While there always exists a reduction from
\Col(\mathcal{F})
to
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
, deciding if there is a reduction in the other direction, in general, is an open question.

Our proof relies on the existence of so-called colored determiners.
Let
\mathbb{G}
be a graph and
\xi
be a partial coloring of the edges of
\mathbb{G}
in
r
-many colors and
e\in E(\mathbb{G})\setminus\mathrm{dom}(\xi)
be an uncolored edge.
The tuple
(\mathbb{G},\xi,e)

is a
colored
i
-determiner
if

[(1)]

1.

one of the two vertices incident to
e
is not incident to any edge of
\mathrm{dom}(\xi)
,

2.

there is an
\mathcal{F}
-free extension of
\xi
,

3.

if
\gamma
is an
\mathcal{F}
-free extension of
\xi
, then
\gamma(e)=i
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, a colored determiner
(\mathbb{G},\xi,e)
is called
d
-remote
if
\dist(e,\mathrm{dom}(\xi))\geq d
.
We say that a determiner
(\mathbb{G},\xi,e)
is
\mathcal{F}
-safe
if it is
\mathcal{F}
-safe along
e
.

We prove the equivalence of
\Col(\mathcal{F})
and

\mathcal{F}

Without loss of generality, we can assume that all the colored graphs in

have domain of the form
\{1,\dots,k\}

Define

\mathbb{F}

\mathcal{F}

of

Definitions for infinite structures

A homomorphism
i\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
is an
isomorphism
if
i
is a bijection and if
i^{-1}\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{A}
is also a homomorphism.
If
\mathbb{A}
is isomorphic to an induced substructure of
\mathbb{B}
, then we say that
\mathbb{A}

embeds into

\mathbb{B}
.
An isomorphism
\alpha\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
from a structure to itself is an
automorphism
.
The set of automorphisms of a structure
\mathbb{A}
forms a group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
that acts on the sets
A^{n}
, for all
n>0
.
A
\tau
-structure
\mathbb{A}
is called
\omega
-categorical
if the domain
A
is countably infinite and if the action
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A})\curvearrowright A^{n}
has finitely many orbits, for all
n>0
.

Homogeneous Ramsey Model-complete Cores

Edge-coloring problems over connected forbidden graphs are closed under taking disjoint unions and inverse homomorphisms.
It means that each such problem can be seen as a constraint satisfaction problem on a (possibly) countably infinite domain
[
5
, Lemma 1.1.8]
.
The first step of proving the equivalence between
\Col(\mathcal{F})
and
\Ext(\mathcal{F})
is to construct such an infinite graph.
For the case where all graphs in
\mathcal{F}
are finite and connected, it is possible to construct such an infinite graph with two important properties: homogeneous and Ramsey.
Moreover, without loss of generality, one can assume that this graph is a model-complete core.

A graph is called
homogeneous
if every partial isomorphism between its induced subgraphs extends to an automorphism of the graph itself.
Homogeneous graphs are obtained as limit objects of certain classes of finite graphs.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a class of finite graphs closed under isomorphisms and taking induced subgraphs.
We say that
\mathcal{K}
has the
amalgamation
property (AP) if, for all
\mathbb{G}_{1},\mathbb{G}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
whose subgraphs induced on
I:=V(\mathbb{G}_{1})\cap V(\mathbb{G}_{2})
are identical, there exist
\mathbb{H}\in\mathcal{K}
and embeddings
e_{1}\colon\mathbb{G}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}
,
e_{2}\colon\mathbb{G}_{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}

such that
e_{1}\restriction_{I}=e_{2}\restriction_{I}.

For example, if
\mathcal{F}
consists solely of cliques, then the class of
\mathcal{F}
-free graphs has the AP.
The properties of homogeneity and amalgamation are related to each other by the following result which is one of the main tools in construction of homogeneous structures.

Theorem 24

(Fraïssé, in
[
20
]
)
.

For a class
\mathcal{K}
of finite graphs, the following are equivalent:

•

\mathcal{K}
is the set of finite induced subgraphs of a countable homogeneous graph; this graph is unique up to isomorphism and is called the
Fraïssé-limit
of
\mathcal{K}
;

•

\mathcal{K}
is closed under isomorphisms, induced subgraphs, and has the AP.

For two relational structures
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
, denote by
\binom{\mathbb{B}}{\mathbb{A}}
the set of all embeddings of
\mathbb{A}
into
\mathbb{B}
.
A family of structures
\mathcal{K}
has the
Ramsey
property (RP) if, for all
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\in\mathcal{K}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{K}
such that, for every mapping
\chi\colon\binom{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{A}}\to[n]
there exists an embedding
e\in\binom{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{B}}
such that
\chi
is constant on the set
e\circ\binom{\mathbb{B}}{\mathbb{A}}
.
A countable homogeneous structure
\mathbb{A}
is called
Ramsey
if
\mathrm{Age}(\mathbb{A})
has the Ramsey property.

Let
{G}
and
{H}
be two groups acting on sets
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
, respectively.
A mapping
f\colon V(\mathbb{A})\to V(\mathbb{B})
is
canonical
with respect to
{G}
and
{H}
if, for every tuple of elements
\bar{t}\in V(\mathbb{A})^{k}
and for every
g\in{G}
, there is
h\in{H}
such that
f(\bar{t})=h\circ f\circ g(\bar{t})
, i.e., for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, mapping
f
well-defines a mapping from the
k
-orbits of the action of
{G}
to the
k
-orbits of the action of
{H}
.
The Ramsey property and the notion of a canonical homomorphism are linked by the following result, often called
canonization lemma
.

Lemma 25

(
[
11
,
10
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be countable
\omega
-categorical structures such that
\mathbb{A}
is Ramsey, and let
\mathbb{A}^{\prime},\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
be reducts of these structures.
Then, for every homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
to
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
there exists a canonical homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
to
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
with respect to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A})
and
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{B})
.

A structure
\mathbb{A}
is a

if
\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{A})=\mathrm{Emb}(\mathbb{A})
, i.e., every endomorphism of
\mathbb{A}
is an embedding of

into itself.
Moreover,

and for every finite subset

of elements of

such that

if
\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{A})=\overline{\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A})}
.
Every

-categorical structure

has a substructure

such that

•

is homomorphically equivalent to

,

•

is a reduct of a homogeneous Ramsey structure, then so is

.

Lemma 26

(Theorem 4 in
[
28
]
)

Let

be the reduct of an

-categorical homogeneous Ramsey structure

.

]

The main idea is to transform a given class

We use the following rephrasing of their theorem.

]

\mathcal{F}

-edge-colored graph

\mathbb{F}\in\mathcal{F}

\mathbb{G}
.

is the

-edge-colored graph obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by replacing the colors of edges of
\mathbb{G}
with their
\rho
-images.

A family
\mathcal{F}
of
r
-edge-colored graphs is a
core
if every recoloring
\rho\colon[r]\to[r]
from
\mathcal{F}
to itself is surjective.

Proof.

Let

from

Lemma 29
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a core, then
(\mathbb{U}_{\mathcal{F}},\not=)
is a model-complete core.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{U}^{\prime}
be the model-complete core of
(\mathbb{U}_{\mathcal{F}},\not=)
.

As
(\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{F}},<)
is Ramsey, we know by
\Cref
lemma:canonization that there is an injective endomorphism
g
of
(\mathbb{U}_{\mathcal{F}},\not=)
which is canonical with respect to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{F}},<)
.
By canonicity,
g
induces a mapping between the orbits of edges of
(\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{F}},<)
.

Notice that either
g
preserves the linear order or inverts it.
Indeed, suppose that, for some
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]
, we have that
g(i_{1},<)=(j_{1},<)
and
g(i_{2},<)=(j_{2},>)
.
Consider a cycle defined on elements
x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{n},x_{1}
such that, for
i\in[n-1]
,
\chi(x_{i}x_{i+1})=i_{1}
, and
\chi(x_{n}x_{1})=j_{1}
.
The
g
-image of this cycle is not a substructure of
(\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{F}},<)
because it is not linearly ordered.
If some orbit
(i,<)
has empty
g
-preimage, then both orbits
(i,<)
and
(i,>)
have empty
(g\circ\gamma\circ g)
-preimage, where
\gamma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{F}})
inverts the linear order.
So, we can assume that a color disappears simultaneously for both orbits.
For some
k_{0}>0
, the mapping
g^{k_{0}}
will map
(i,<)
and
(i,>)
to orbits
(j,<)
and
(j^{\prime},>)
such that

, for all
i\in[n]
.
This means that
g^{k_{0}}
well-defines a recoloring from
\mathcal{F}
to itself which must be surjective as
\mathcal{F}
is a core.
So, for some

(\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\not=)

As

.
Hence,

and

locally invert each other

[

]

]

.
This means that
\mathbb{U}^{\prime}
and

are both
