Risk of Nephritis and Recurrence in Kimura Disease: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Caiyun Zhang, Jun Chen, Yun Hui, Huan Chen, Dequan Deng, Hong Sang, Fang Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Kimura disease (KD) presents a diagnostic challenge to clinicians because of its rarity and atypical symptoms in its early stages, and it is difficult to treat and prone to recurrence or involvement of other organs.Aims and Objectives:This study aims to investigate the possible relevance of renal involvement and recurrence by analysing the clinical presentations, laboratory results, histopathological features, therapeutic data and follow-up results of KD.Materials and Methods:A total of 27 patients diagnosed as KD in two hospitals from January 1999 to December 2021 were analysed retrospectively in this study based on the diagnosis of histopathology.Results:KD mainly affected male more than female (8:1) with the onset age ranging from 3 to 58 years (median 29.8 years). The common initial symptoms included subcutaneous soft tissue or lymph node enlargement, non-specific skin lesions and proteinuria. One patient presented cough and expectoration as the first symptoms. KD patients often had high levels of serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) and basophils, which exhibited a significantly positive correlation with renal involvement and recurrence (p < 0.05). Early mass resection could prevent the development of nephritis and decrease the risk of relapse (p < 0.05).Conclusion:KD should be noted in patients presenting with intractable and relapsing atopic skin lesions and (or) subcutaneous mass. Patients with high levels of serum IgE and blood basophils may be prone to developing KD-associated nephritis and predict a high risk of recurrence. Early surgical removal of the mass may result in a better prognosis.

Introduction

Kimura disease (KD), also known as eosinophilic lymphoid granuloma, is a rare chronic lymphoid proliferation inflammatory disease which was first described by Kim HT in 1937.[1] Later in 1948, Kimura et al.[2] carried out the definitive histologic description of this entity, so it is now commonly known as KD. KD patients is relatively common in Asian such as China, Japan or Southeast Asia, compared with non-Asian area.[345]

Clinically, KD mainly involves soft tissue, lymph node and endocrine gland, and two-thirds of patients had skin involvement.[6] KD often involves multiple organs such as the eye, epiglottis, kidney and blood.[6789] Painless subcutaneous soft mass usually affecting head and neck, marked peripheral blood eosinophilia and high level of immunoglobulin E (IgE) are common presentations.[1246] Due to rarity and atypical onset symptoms, KD often conducts a diagnostic challenge for clinicians, which can easily be confused with angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia (ALHE). These two diseases were once considered variants of the same disease, but more recent studies have shown them to be histopathological distinct with a predisposition for different age groups, gender and race. ALHE is a vascular neoplasm characterized by the proliferation of endothelial cell, which is more superficial and the absence of regional lymphadenopathy, often with normal peripheral eosinophils and serum IgE levels.[6]

The aetiology and pathogenesis of KD are still unclear, but substantial evidence suggests that T-helper type 2 (Th2) immune response contributes to the pathogenesis of KD.[81011] The CD4+ Th2 cells secrete a large number of cytokines, such as granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-5, eotaxin and rantes, trigger the production of lymphoid follicle and high IgE, which play a particular role in the pathogenesis of KD.[12]

Therapeutic choice and recurrence are another challenge for clinicians. The current therapies have some demerits and uncertain long-term effects though multiple treatment methods have been reported in KD including surgical excision, systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents, cytotoxic therapy, radiation and biological agents.[451314] Therefore, more data are needed for reference to better clarify this disease and to guide the diagnosis and treatment.

Subjects and Methods

This is a retrospective descriptive study that analysed 27 KD patients who were admitted to Jinling Hospital (20) and Nanjing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (7) between January 1999 and December 2021. The patients who had been pathologically confirmed KD were included in this study. The diagnosis was precluded underlying disorders causing eosinophilia such as allergies, parasites, collagen diseases and hematologic diseases. The clinical presentation, laboratory test, histopathology, treatment and follow-up data were reviewed and recorded by two different clinicians. The relapse of KD was defined as a complaint of swelling or pruritus after intervention and confirmed by histopathology. All indices were investigated and recorded at the time of diagnosis. Statistical analyses were implemented using SPSS 13.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), and graph was performed by GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Prism Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The data were expressed as mean and SD. Mann–Whitney U-test was used for statistical analysis of the data and Spearman’s rank test to determine the correlation between study parameters. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee conducted at Jinling Hospital on March 10, 2023.

Results

General clinical characteristics

Twenty-seven KD patients were included in this study, whose detailed clinical features were shown in Table 1. The case series were collected from different departments including nephrology (10, 37.0%), dermatology (4, 14.8%), stomatology (4, 14.8%), general surgery department (3, 11.1%) and radiotherapy (2, 7.4%). Haematology, respiratory, integrated traditional and western medicine, and ultrasonography department each had one patient (1, 3.7%). Twenty-four were men and three were women (sex ratio 8:1). The mean age at onset was 29.8 ± 14.82 years (range: 3-58 years), with incidence peaking during the third decade. The average duration of symptoms was 3.6 ± 4.36 years (range: 1 month-20 years). The onset symptoms included soft tissue enlargement (9, 33.3%), lymph node enlargement (9, 33.3%), both soft tissue and lymph node enlargement (1, 3.7%), unspecific skin lesions (5, 18.5%), proteinuria (2, 7.4%) and cough, expectoration (1, 3.7%). The skin lesions were described as itching erythema, generalized pruritic erythematous papules and subcutaneous single or multiple nodules [Figure 1 a-d]. 21 of 27 (77.8%) patients had single or multiple enlarged lymph nodes in neck (12, 44.4%), submandibular region (10, 37.0%), axilla (7, 25.9%), groin (5, 18.5%), retroauricular (2, 7.4%), retroperitoneal (1, 3.7%), supratrochlear (1, 3.7%), clavicular region (1, 3.7%) and pelvic wall (1, 3.7%). 17 of 27 (63.0%) patients had single or multiple enlarged subcutaneous soft tissue in cheek (3, 11.1%), parotid gland (3, 11.1%), head (2, 7.4%), ear (2, 7.4%), elbow (2, 7.4%), leg (2, 7.4%), forehead (1, 3.7%), submandibular gland (1, 3.7%) and arm (1, 3.7%) [Figure 1e].

Laboratory results

Eosinophilia was seen in 85.2% (23/27) patients and elevated serum IgE in 65.2% (15/23) patients. The mean eosinophil percentage and serum IgE levels were 22.4 ± 11.82% (range: 1.8–44.3%, normal: 0.4–8.0%) and 480.8 ± 466.48 IU/ml (range: 20.0–1378.0 IU/ml, normal: 0–100.0 IU/ml) respectively. Elevated blood basophil was seen in 29.6% (8/27) cases, the mean percentage was 0.9 ± 0.60% (range: 0–2.0%, normal: 0-1.0%). Urinary protein was quantified in 12 patients (12/27, 44.4%) and all showed elevated values. The mean amount of protein in 24 h urine sample was 7.3 g/24 h (normal: <0.4 g/24 h). The patients with higher peripheral blood basophils and serum IgE were prone to complicate nephritis (p < 0.05) [Figure 2].

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

The histopathological samples were collected from lymph node (17), kidney (11), soft tissue (9) or skin (5). 13/27 patients were confirmed by two different types of pathological tissue, and one patient had three biopsies (Case 12: kidney, lymph node and skin biopsy). Hyperplasia of lymphocytes and lymphoid follicles in dermis, extensive eosinophil infiltration, vascular and endothelial proliferation with pericapsular and capsular fibrosis were often seen in skin histopathology [Figure 3a and 3b]. Histopathology of lymph node and soft tissue revealed lymphatic follicular hyperplasia with interstitial small vessel hyperplasia, extensive eosinophilic infiltration and occasional eosinophilic abscesses were seen [Figure 3c and 3d]. Eosinophils and proliferation of postcapillary venules were common features (100%). Immunohistochemistry of soft tissue samples exhibited CD20, CD3, CD79a, CD43 and Ki-67 positive expression. The histopathology of renal biopsy revealed minimal change in glomerulopathy (3/11, 27.3%) and mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (8/11, 72.7%) [Figure 4]. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed 81.8% (9/11) patients with renal involvement presented IgM deposition, 36.4% (4/11) IgG and 54.5% (6/11) IgA deposition [Table 1].

Treatment and recurrence

In our research, the overall recurrence rate was 46.2% (12/26, one patient was lost due to not being treated in our hospital) during follow-up. The recurrence rates of patients with and without renal involvement were 75% (9/12) and 21.4% (3/14), respectively. 80% (4/5) patients treated with surgery alone were recurrent. All patients without renal involvement didn’t relapse after undergoing surgery and medication treatment (glucocorticoids, immunosuppressive agents, cytotoxic therapy or biological agents). Two of them initially prescribed with steroids recurred very quickly after the steroid dose tapered off and were eventually surgically removed without recurrence. Two patients presented with large soft tissue masses in bilateral cheeks had radiotherapy, then followed by masses resection. Finally, the younger one with a shorter history did not relapse, and neither of them showed any adverse reaction to radiotherapy. Case 4 who had soft mass in head and multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the submandibular region and neck was given omalizumab and surgical excision of the mass because of active hepatitis. This patient also had good response without recurrence.

Among the 12 patients with renal involvement, 60% (3/5) patients had masses resected or lymphadenectomy and systemic prednisone didn’t show relapse. Two patients had good response to cyclophosphamide initially, but the symptoms recurred after the treatment was discontinued. The most frequent relapse was observed in case 7 who was successively prescribed with mycophenolate mofetil, prednisone, cyclosporine and leflunomide. Finally, the symptoms were relieved by rituximab but still relapsed occasionally. The Spearman analysis indicated that patients with higher blood basophils and serum IgE had higher risk of recurrence (p < 0.05). The patients had masses resection exhibited a significantly negative correlation with recurrence and renal involvement (p < 0.05).

Discussion

KD is characterized by painless soft tissue or lymph node enlargement occurring predominantly in the head and neck region and often associated with salivary glands involvement.[361516] In this study, the common sites of lymph node involvement were predominant in the neck, submandibular region, axilla and groin, while soft tissue involvement was in the cheek, head, ear, elbow and leg. The glands involved in our patients were mainly salivary glands, including parotid and submandibular gland. The lacrimal gland can also be affected as reported in previous studies.[61617] 11 of 27 (40.7%) patients had lesions or masses accompanied by one or more lymph node enlargement. Three patients presented only enlarged lymph nodes without organ involvement. Our case 21 had multiple systemic lymphadenopathy confirmed by positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), including groin, neck, submandibular, clavicular region, axilla, retroperitoneal and pelvic wall. However, PET-CT was rarely performed clinically in KD due to its benign course.[18]

In our series, five patients with skin involvement had common histopathological findings, including lymphocyte proliferation and lymphoid follicles formation, vascular endothelial cell proliferation and massive eosinophil infiltration. Case 12 presented with multiple pruritic erythematous papules on the trunk and extremities for 3 years before proteinuria. She was misdiagnosed as papular-like urticaria and was prescribed antihistamine and topical corticosteroids but without remission. Cases 6, 8, 11 and 22 presented with itching erythema on forehead, elbow and waist, and subcutaneous nodule in cheek or head. Previous studies described KD as associated with pruritic conditions, such as atopy-like dermatitis, prurigo, urticaria, eczema and lichen amyloidosis.[619] These unspecific skin lesions are often easily neglected by clinicians because the mass or lymph node enlargement is undetectable in the early stage by ultrasound examination. Meanwhile, imaging examination (including ultrasound examination, CT and MRI) has a high detection rate but low specificity for masses in KD.[3] Therefore, we should proactively seek advice from senior doctors and aware of this disease in patients who have intractable and relapsing skin lesions. Sometimes a simple blood routine test can give us useful hints, meanwhile, exhaustive history, careful physical examination and skin biopsy are necessary for diagnosis.

KD is frequently associated with renal involvement, including proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome (NS) as the most common presentations.[720] In this study, nephritis was presented in 44.4% (12/27) patients, which was consistent with the previous estimation of 10%-60%.[20] 83.3% (10/12) of KD patients with renal involvement were NS. Two patients did not meet the criteria for NS because proteinuria was less than 3.5 g/d.[21] In terms of pathological types, we found two kinds of kidney diseases: minimal change glomerulopathy and mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, while other types of renal diseases have been described in KD including membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, IgA and IgM nephropathy.[62223] In this study, we found elevated serum IgE and blood basophils, and delay of the mass resection may be responsible for renal involvement. We also found that KD patients, especially those with renal involvement, often showed elevated blood CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts and CD4+ to CD8+ T cell ratio. These results suggested allergic or hypersensitivity reactions, cellular immune reaction participant in the pathogenesis of KD-associated nephritis.

There is no standard treatment for KD to date. Surgery is the mainstay of treatment, while systemic steroids, immunosuppressants (i.e. cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, thalidomide), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and radiotherapy resulted in varying degrees of efficacy and recurrence rates.[4924] Steroids are common and classic drugs for treating KD, but recurrence often occurs once the dosage tapers off.[3] The recurrence rate was 75% (3/4) for patients treated with steroids alone in this study. Radiotherapy can decrease the risk of recurrence and progression, and avoid scarring, and thus, it may be an auxiliary therapy when soft tissue is too large or for the refractory patient. The recurrence rate for patients treated with radiotherapy alone varied from 41.2% to 100%, while surgery combined with radiotherapy could significantly decrease relapse for KD in previous reports.[4] In our study, two patients were treated with surgery combined with radiotherapy, and the recurrence rate was 50%.

Some researchers have tried to treat KD with biological agents (i.e. omalizumab, rituximab, dupilumab) as monoclonal antibodies can precisely target specific cytokines and cell surface antigens.[51314] The anti-IgE antibody, omalizumab, has been reported as a successful treatment in three KD patients whose masses size and peripheral blood eosinophil counts were all decreased.[14] Omalizumab can bind to free IgE in the blood, thereby preventing IgE from binding to basophils and mast cells, inhibiting activation of inflammatory cells and preventing the release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, etc. This agent can also inhibit Th2 cytokine release, suppress eosinophils and down-regulate expression of FcRI. In this study, case 4 with active hepatitis and soft tissue enlargement and multiple lymphadenopathy was given omalizumab after soft tissue resection, which resulted in a good response without relapse in 6 years follow-up. Previous studies confirmed patients with KD-associated membranous nephropathy have demonstrated responsiveness to anti-CD20 antibodies, rituximab.[5] Case 7 had a right ear mass, multiple lymph node enlargement and mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis. He was prescribed prednisone and a variety of immunosuppressants, but all the symptoms recurred every six months to one year. Eventually, rituximab was administered and all the symptoms were alleviated, only the urinary protein began to rise five months later, while no mass or lymph node enlargement was observed in 17 months follow-up. Dupilumab is a widely used Th2 cytokine inhibitor in atopic dermatitis, which inhibits IL-4 and IL-13 signalling by blocking the shared IL-4α subunit.[25] Sporadic cases of KD treated with dupilumab were reported in recent studies. Dupilumab showed good response for patients with recurrent attacks and large masses; however, the follow-up time was only 6 to 12 months.[81113] Thus, more clinical data are needed to clarify applicable patients and long-term efficacy and safety for biological agents in KD.

Regarding to recurrence, we found high levels of serum IgE and basophils increased the risk of recurrence and early removal of mass had a lower risk of relapse. Iwai et al.[26] also found serum IgE ≥10,000 IU/mL, eosinophils 50% higher than normal level and multifocal lesions outside salivary glands were prone to recurring in KD. Lee et al.[4] recommended combination adjuvant therapy with surgery for KD patients presenting with tumour size ≥3 cm, symptom duration ≥5 years, peripheral blood eosinophilia ≥20% or serum IgE ≥10000 IU/ml, to achieve the optimal therapeutic outcome.

Significantly, the correlation between basophils and renal involvement or KD recurrence was rarely mentioned in previous literature. Basophils are known to play an important role in IgE-mediate allergic disease through the release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-4 and IL-13. IL-4-positive basophils were also reported infiltrated between the lymphoid follicles, suggesting that they may function as effector cells in those sites in the pathogenesis of KD.[27] Meanwhile, the increased blood CD4+ and CD8+ T cells indicated the immune function of T cells in the pathogenesis of KD, which have functions of secreting cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-13. Moreover, increased expression of Th2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 has been observed in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells and tissue lesions of KD.[28] These cytokines could chemoattract B cells, mast cells and eosinophils, then promote the growth and differentiation of those cells at the site of immune response. Th2 cells also might play important roles in IgE production through IL-4 and IL-5 and activation of eosinophils. In previous studies, immunohistochemistry of renal biopsy found IgE deposited along the glomerular capillary of one KD patient with NS, which might cause epithelial damage and result in proteinuria.[17] IVIG has been reported as a successful steroid-sparing agent treatment in some KD patients, which further confirmed the role of immune system in the pathogenesis of KD.[24] Because IVIG can regulate Th2 response by decreasing B-cell proliferation, neutralizing C3a and C5a, adjusting endothelial activation, anticytokine and proapoptotic antibodies which are contained. These findings indicate that the infiltrated basophils may be caused by increased cytokines production and prolong the life span of chemotaxis. Therefore, basophils are associated with KD and nephritis, which may be a risk factor for recurrence and renal involvement in KD.

Conclusion

This retrospective study indicates unspecific and refractory atopic skin lesions may be the initial presentation in the early stage of KD, and biopsy is necessary for diagnosis. KD is characterized by subcutaneous soft tissue swelling in the head and neck, eosinophilia and elevated serum IgE. This entity is more likely to be a systemic disease, high levels of serum IgE and blood basophils may underlie the development of KD-associate nephropathy and increase the risk of recurrence. For the patient with a solitary cutaneous lesion without systemic involvement, surgical excision is the first-line treatment. Combined therapy should be considered when there are multiple lesions or the possibility of recurrence is high. Biological agents are a new therapeutic method worthy of attention. Due to the limited number of patients and limitations of retrospective research, larger prospective studies of higher quality are needed to elucidate these problems in the future.
