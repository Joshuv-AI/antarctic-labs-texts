Closing the gap on gapeworm: experimental infection of Mammomonogamus ierei in domestic cats
Authors: Andreea Daniela Iani, Andrei Daniel Mihalca, Jennifer K Ketzis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMammomonogamus ierei is a nasal nematode of domestic cats, for which the life cycle and route of infection have remained unknown. Experimental oral infections have historically failed, leading to speculation regarding the involvement of an intermediate host and uncertainty about transmission pathways. The aim of this study was to determine the route of infection of M. ierei in domestic cats.MethodsTwenty kittens (6–12 weeks old) were exposed to infective third-stage larvae (L3) via six routes: per os (n = 3), oral mucosa (n = 3), nasal mucosa (n = 3), subcutaneous (n = 3), percutaneous (abdominal region) (n = 2), external auditory canal (n = 2), and an unexposed control group (n = 4). Background

Mammomonogamus is a genus of parasitic nematodes infecting mammals in tropical and subtropical regions [1]. Early classifications placed mammalian species within the genus Syngamus Leiper, 1912, as both avian and mammalian forms exhibit permanent copulation between male and female worms [2–4]. Subsequent taxonomic revision by Ryzhikov identified consistent anatomical differences between mammalian and avian species, leading to the recognition of Mammomonogamus Ryzhikov, 1948 as a separate genus [5]. Species identification within this genus relied largely on morphological characteristics, with species frequently defined in association with their hosts. Although historically grouped within Syngamidae based on biological similarities (i.e., permanent copulation) [2, 4, 5], recent phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that Mammomonogamus forms a monophyletic lineage closely related to Ancylostomatidae [6, 7].

Mammomonogamus ierei inhabits the nasal passages of domestic cats and may cause sneezing, nasal discharge, and other upper respiratory signs [8]. It is one of three species known to infect domestic cats, alongside Mammomonogamus auris (middle ear) [9] and Mammomonogamus mcgaughei (pharynx and nasal sinuses) [10]. Mammomonogamus ierei appears to be geographically restricted to the Caribbean, with St. Kitts representing one of the few locations where infections have been consistently documented in both feral and owned cats [11, 12]. Reports of M. auris originate from Asia as well as islands in the western Pacific, whereas M. mcgaughei has been reported only once, in Sri Lanka, where it was originally described [9, 10, 13].

For all Mammomonogamus species, the most significant gap in knowledge concerns the life cycle, as the route of infection remains unknown. In the original description of M. ierei, Buckley (1934) attempted experimental infections in four cats, inoculating them orally with third-stage larvae (L3). None of the cats developed patent infections during a 50-day observation period. Notably, one unexposed control cat later became copropositive, leading the author to the assumption that an intermediate host could be involved [2]. Subsequent experimental work with Mammomonogamus nasicola in sheep tested multiple oral inoculation routes, including ingestion of earthworms, which act as transport hosts for Syngamus trachea [14]. These attempts were also unsuccessful [15]. The repeated failure of experimental infections, combined with molecular evidence linking Mammomonogamus more closely to hookworms, suggests that its life cycle may resemble that of Ancylostomatidae nematodes rather than Syngamidae. Given the unknown mode of transmission and the lack of successful experimental infections, the aim of the present study was to explore direct transmission possibilities in domestic cats, considering the similarities to hookworms and the anatomical location of adult worms. Elucidating the route of infection is essential for understanding the parasite’s biology and improving control strategies, and could provide insights into the possible transmission mechanisms of the zoonotic species Mammomonogamus laryngeus and M. nasicola in ruminants and humans [15, 16].

Methods

Study animals and experimental design

A total of 20 kittens (6–12 weeks old) representing four litters were enrolled in the study. Weaned kittens were either donated by private owners seeking assistance in rehoming kittens or from the Feral Cat Project (FCP), a spay/neuter and release program. Litters from FCP originated from queens that gave birth before the designated spay day. These queens and their litter were fostered until weaning, at which point the queen was spayed and released. Parasitological examination was performed on the two queens from FCP prior to spaying, and both tested negative for M. ierei using the double-centrifugation flotation technique with Sheather’s sugar solution (specific gravity 1.28). Two of the four litters had been housed outdoors prior to enrollment, whereas the remaining two litters were raised indoors.

Acclimation and pre-study procedures

Upon arrival, kittens underwent an acclimation period during which they were clinically evaluated, and individual fecal samples were collected for parasitological examination. Fecal samples were examined using a double-centrifugation flotation technique with Sheather’s sugar solution (specific gravity 1.28) prior to anthelmintic treatment and again after treatment, before exposure. During this period, all kittens were treated to eliminate potential pre-existing parasitic infections. Animals aged ≥ 8 weeks and weighing ≥ 0.8 kg received a topical formulation containing esafoxolaner, eprinomectin, and praziquantel (NexGard® Combo, Boehringer Ingelheim) administered according to the manufacturer’s label instructions. Although no withdrawal interval is specified for this product in companion animals, a 14-day washout period was implemented to eliminate potential negative effects on parasite establishment. Kittens that did not meet the weight/age criteria were administered pyrantel pamoate (Durvet Liquid Wormer) at 20 mg/kg per os. Given its limited systemic absorption and rapid elimination, a 48-h washout was applied prior to inoculation with L3 to ensure absence of residual anthelmintic activity [17]. Fecal samples were examined prior to anthelmintic treatment and again after treatment, before exposure. All samples were negative for M. ierei at both time points for all kittens. A detailed study schedule is provided in Additional file 1.

Housing and husbandry

Kittens were housed indoors in the MAF (Multipurpose Animal Facility) at RUSVM in age-appropriate multi-level enclosures under controlled environmental conditions. Kittens were housed within their respective litter groups, except during the 24-h post-exposure period and individual fecal collections, when they were individually housed.

Kitten care and facility management followed the facility standard operating procedures (SOPs). In short, kittens received a standard commercial feline diet and potable water ad libitum; each litter had its own designated bowls and litter boxes which were cleaned twice daily. Environmental temperature, relative humidity, and a 12:12-h light–dark cycle were maintained and monitored. Routine husbandry, health observations, and monitoring of respiratory clinical signs (sneeze, cough, nasal discharge) were performed twice daily. Kittens were socialized within their litter groups for at least 30 min per day, without mixing between litters. Environmental enrichment included scratch boards, bedding, and background music. All procedures complied with RUSVM IACUC-approved protocol and SOPs.

Randomization and inoculation groups

Inoculation routes consisted of per os administration (n = 3), oral mucosa inoculation (n = 3), nasal mucosa inoculation (n = 3), subcutaneous injection (n = 3), auricular instillation into the external ear canal (n = 2), and topical application to the ventral abdominal skin (n = 2). One kitten from the subcutaneous group was additionally used to evaluate larval activity following paw-pad inoculation. Subcutaneous infection, topical application to the skin, and paw-pad inoculation were selected as means for assessing a percutaneous mode of transmission, mimicking methods used with experimental Ancylostoma spp. infections [18, 19].

Because litter origin and previous access to outdoors differed among groups, kittens were randomized within and across litters to minimize potential litter-associated effects, with the day of L3 inoculation designated as study day 0. The randomization method ensured that more than one inoculation route was represented within each litter and accounted for potential impacts of age and differences in immunity (Fig. 1). Allocation to inoculation routes was also based on age suitability for sedation, with kittens younger than 11 weeks assigned to routes that did not require sedation. Sedation was required for kittens exposed via the nasal or oral mucosa, and for paw-pad evaluation. At least one kitten in each litter was maintained as an unexposed control (Table 1).

Experimental observation period

Following inoculation (study day 0), kittens were monitored for 70 days. During this observation period, all animals remained housed in the unit under the husbandry and monitoring conditions described above.

Post-study procedures and rehoming

After completion of the study, all kittens were vaccinated and treated with NexGard® Combo. Kittens were monitored for 12 h post-treatment in an attempt to recover expelled adult worms, but no M. ierei pairs were observed. All male cats were neutered prior to adoption. Female cats were spayed either before or after adoption at no cost to the new owners. Kittens remained housed in the unit until rehoming.

Inoculum

Eggs of M. ierei were isolated from the feces of naturally infected cats using double-centrifugation flotation with Sheather’s sugar [20] followed by sieving to isolate M. ierei eggs from other parasite eggs present. Eggs were cultured at 30 ± 2 °C and 91 ± 2% relative humidity. The culture medium consisted of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with penicillin–streptomycin 10,000 U/ml (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and amphotericin B 250 µg/ml (MP Biomedicals) [21], the latter added daily to inhibit fungal growth. Cultures were aerated once daily, and larval development was monitored and photographed throughout the incubation period. Third-stage larvae consistently developed by day 9 of culture, and for experimental use were maintained for up to 15 days after culture initiation. Kittens were infected according to the assigned route. The abdominal cutaneous group received 27 L3 applied to the ventral abdomen, while all other groups were inoculated with 50 ± 5 L3. Larval numbers were determined by direct microscopic counting of three 10-µl aliquots from a homogenized suspension, and the average of these was used to determine the number of L3 per milliliter.

Inoculation methods

The inoculum was freshly prepared on the day of use and maintained under the same incubation conditions to preserve larval survival. Viability was assessed at the time of counting based on morphology and motility. Only larvae exhibiting normal morphology (Fig. 2) and active or stimulated movement upon warming were considered viable. Larvae that appeared distended, lacked visible morphological detail, and showed no movement were considered non-viable. The administered volume did not exceed 1 ml for oral inoculation and 0.5 ml for the other routes. The exact inoculated volume varied depending on the concentration of larvae in the suspension (as determined by the aliquot counts), as doses were standardized based on larval number rather than volume. For per os inoculation, the suspension was administered directly into the oral cavity using a pipette in a maximum volume of 1 ml. The pipette tip was introduced gently into the caudal portion of the oral cavity to ensure delivery as deeply as possible and to facilitate swallowing. Following administration, the kittens were examined to monitor for regurgitation or vomiting. Immediately after inoculation, a small amount of wet food was offered to promote swallowing and to reduce suspension contact with the oral mucosa. For subcutaneous inoculation, ≤ 0.5 ml of the L3 suspension was administered using a 1-ml syringe under standard aseptic conditions. For mucosal inoculation (oral or nasal), kittens were lightly sedated, and ≤ 0.5 ml of the L3 suspension was applied directly onto the mucosa to allow mucosal contact. For nasal mucosal administration, care was taken to minimize the risk of aspiration. For auricular inoculation, ≤ 0.5 ml of the L3 suspension was gently instilled into the external ear canal, and animals were observed for head shaking. For cutaneous inoculation of the ventral abdomen, cats were gently restrained in dorsal recumbency, and less than 0.5 ml of the suspension was applied to the ventral abdominal region. Animals were maintained in this position until the suspension had dried, for a maximum of 10 min. To assess larval activity upon contact with the paw-pad, five larvae suspended in 0.5 ml of water were placed directly onto the paw-pad and examined under a stereomicroscope. The kitten was lightly sedated to facilitate handling, and the procedure did not exceed 10 min.

Fecal analysis

Fecal analysis was performed using the double-centrifugation flotation technique, with a detection limit of 1 egg per gram (EPG) [20]. One gram of feces was mixed with water, strained, and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, the sediment was resuspended in Sheather’s sugar solution (specific gravity 1.28), and a second centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min was conducted with a coverslip in place.

Pooled fecal samples from each litter were collected daily. Starting 7 days post-inoculation, the samples were examined in triplicate every 7 days using the previously described method. If a pooled sample tested positive, pooled samples from the preceding 6 days were analyzed to identify the first day of a positive sample. When a litter tested positive, kittens in that litter were individually housed for fecal collection to identify the shedding kitten. Thereafter, individual fecal samples from all litter members were collected weekly to monitor whether other kittens began shedding M. ierei eggs.

All fecal samples were collected and labeled with either the litter or individual kitten identification number, date, and time, and stored at approximately 5 °C until analysis.

Molecular analysis

For molecular confirmation of species identity, genomic DNA was extracted from larval isolates using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene was performed following the protocol described by Červená et al. (2018) [6]. Reactions were carried out in a total volume of 25 µl containing 12.5 µl MyTaq™ Red Mix (Meridian Bioscience, USA), 1 µl of each primer (CoxmamF [1] and HCO2198 [22]), 3 µl of template DNA, and 7.5 µl of nuclease-free water. PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the ISOLATE II Genomic DNA Kit (Meridian Bioscience, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified amplicons were sequenced externally at Macrogen Europe (the Netherlands), and the resulting sequences were analyzed using Geneious Prime (Biomatters Ltd., New Zealand).

Results

Culture of the eggs resulted in motile L3 within 9 days (Figs. 2 and 3), and sequence analysis of larvae showed 100% nucleotide identity with the St. Kitts M. ierei adult isolate (GenBank accession MF668052.1), confirming the identity. The morphology of the L3 also corresponded to the description by Buckley (1934). The L3 had a mean body length of 351 µm (range 316.55–388.90 µm) and a mean width of 20 µm (range 15.61–24.01 µm). The larvae were covered by two molting cuticles. The outer cuticle was clearly detached from the larval body anteriorly and posteriorly, forming lateral folds during larval movement. The inner cuticle was more closely apposed to the larval body and could be difficult to observe when the larvae were not moving. Anteriorly, a small internal knob-like structure was observed on both cuticles, likely representing the remnant of the buccal vestibule from a previous larval stage. When stained with methylene blue, the inner cuticle retained from the L2–L3 molt stained more intensely than the outer cuticle retained from the L1–L2 molt. The anterior extremity was bullet-shaped, with a small buccal vestibule that continued into a long esophagus. The esophagus consisted of an elongated, cylindrical anterior portion, followed by a narrow segment that widened posteriorly to form an esophageal bulb. The esophagus measured a mean length of 133 µm (range 122.47–143.63 µm), representing approximately 38% of the total body length. The esophageal bulb had a mean width of 8 µm (range 6.91–10.54 µm). The intestine was obscured by numerous granules and continued into a clearly visible anus. The anal opening was located at a mean distance of 45 µm from the tail tip (range 43.84–47.28 µm). The tail tapered gradually to a pointed end.

With the experimental exposures, two of the 20 kittens developed patent M. ierei infections, first shedding eggs 48–51 days after inoculation (Additional file 1). Both infected kittens belonged to the subcutaneous group and had received 53 L3 that were 9 days old at the time of inoculation. These kittens remained positive for the remainder of the study. All other kittens, including controls, remained negative throughout the observation period. No detectable infections resulted from the other inoculation routes.

Larvae placed on the paw-pad were motile and exhibited undulating movement under stereomicroscopic observation. However, no clear evidence of active skin penetration was observed.

Mild respiratory signs, consisting of intermittent sneezing and occasional coughing, were observed in several kittens during the study period (Additional file 1). One kitten that subsequently developed patent M. ierei infection exhibited mild sneezing beginning on day 28, approximately 20 days prior to the onset of patency; no further respiratory signs were recorded for this kitten thereafter. The second patent kitten developed intermittent sneezing only after patency was established (days 51, 53, 62, 65, and 70).

Kittens from litter 2, originating from a household where they had been kept outdoors prior to enrollment, began shedding Trichuris felis eggs on day 11. Shedding of Platynosomum illiciens eggs in the same litter was detected between day 43 and day 49. These infections were likely acquired prior to enrollment, as the kittens were transferred to a controlled indoor study environment only at the time of acclimation. No egg shedding of T. felis or P. illiciens was observed in kittens from the remaining litters. The two kittens that developed patent M. ierei infections belonged to indoor-origin litters and did not shed Trichuris or Platynosomum eggs at any time during the study.

Discussion

This study provides the first experimental evidence identifying a route of infection for domestic cats with the nasal nematode M. ierei and represents the first report describing the mode of infection, even if experimental, for any species within the genus Mammomonogamus. Since its original description by Buckley (1934), and following failure to establish infection via oral administration of L3, the involvement of an intermediate or paratenic host has long been speculated [3, 6, 23]. The present findings demonstrate that infection can be established experimentally without the use of an intermediate host, supporting the possibility of a direct life cycle.

In avian syngamids, such as S. trachea, infection can be established through direct ingestion of L3 or eggs with L3, indicating that a direct life cycle is possible. However, infective larvae can invade and persist within earthworms and other invertebrates, which act as paratenic hosts and may enhance the transmission. Experimental studies have shown that birds fed infected earthworms develop infections more consistently and with higher establishment rates than those receiving eggs alone [14, 24]. In contrast, attempts to establish infection with M. nasicola through oral administration of larvae or inoculation via earthworms have been unsuccessful [15]. For M. ierei, however, the role of potential paratenic hosts has not yet been experimentally assessed. Although the present study demonstrates that infection can be established without the use of a paratenic host, this does not exclude the possibility that such hosts may contribute to transmission under natural conditions.

Phylogenetic analyses indicate that Mammomonogamus spp. are more closely related to hookworms, specifically Necator americanus and Ancylostoma caninum, than to avian syngamids [6, 7]. This framework provided the rationale for including experimental methods commonly used in hookworm models. Transmission of N. americanus occurs primarily through active percutaneous penetration of L3, while oral transmission is not considered a major natural route [24, 25]. In A. caninum, establishment success also depends on the route of inoculation, with the highest rates reported after paw-pad inoculation with 500 L3 (73.8%), compared with subcutaneous (49%) and percutaneous abdominal (45%) routes [18]. In that study, the abdominal hair was clipped prior to larval application, potentially facilitating larval access to the skin. For paw-pad inoculation, larvae suspended in 0.5 ml were distributed equally across all four footpads. In both cases, animals were restrained for 30 min. In contrast, in the present study, 27 L3 were applied to intact abdominal skin without hair clipping, and kittens were maintained in dorsal recumbency for no longer than 10 min. Paw-pad infection was not performed; only a brief observational assessment using five L3 applied to the paw-pad for less than 10 min was conducted to evaluate larval activity. These differences in inoculum size and exposure duration may have limited larval establishment.

Transmammary and transplacental transmission have not been investigated or demonstrated for any species of Mammomonogamus. However, given the phylogenetic relationship with hookworms, the potential of these routes of infection should be considered. Transplacental transmission of hookworms appears to be rare and not consistently confirmed, with limited evidence reported for A. caninum and no evidence for Ancylostoma tubaeforme [26, 27]. There is also no evidence of transmammary transmission for A. tubaeforme, and this form of transmission appears rare with N. americanus. In contrast, transmammary transmission is well documented with A. caninum [22, 26, 28]. In the case of A. caninum, L3 are present in milk, typically during the first week postpartum [27]. Given the rarity of transplacental and transmammary transmission of hookworms, with the exception of transmammary transmission in A. caninum, the risk of vertical transmission impacting the results of the study presented herein was considered minimal. However, to minimize potential risk of these forms affecting results, all kittens included in the study were treated with a nematocidal agent prior to experimental exposure. In addition, the results of parasitological examinations performed on the available queens were negative, including the queen of the litter from which the two kittens that later tested positive originated.

In the present study, patent M. ierei infection was achieved exclusively following subcutaneous inoculation. In contrast, oral, oral mucosal, nasal mucosal, percutaneous abdominal, and external auditory canal inoculations failed to result in egg shedding. Although subcutaneous injection does not replicate natural skin penetration, these findings suggest that successful establishment may depend on larval access to skin tissue and subsequent tissue migration, similar to that observed in hookworms [29]. Moreover, the occurrence of egg shedding 48–51 days after subcutaneous inoculation implies that larvae can migrate systemically from subcutaneous tissues to the likely site of adult establishment (nasal cavities), which is compatible with a near-natural migratory route. A comparable prepatent period had been suspected based on a previous observation of a naturally infected 12-week-old kitten (unpublished data).

Inoculation via the external auditory canal was included as an exploratory route, based on observations from the congeneric M. auris, inhabiting the middle ear of domestic cats. In M. auris, adult worms are found behind an intact tympanic membrane and can move between the middle ear, Eustachian tube, nasopharynx, and laryngopharynx [13]. These observations provided the rationale for evaluating the external auditory canal as a potential route of entry.

As originally described by Buckley (1934), M. ierei L3 retain two cuticular sheaths, a feature shared with other hookworm species, such as Bunostomum spp. [2, 24]. The timing of exsheathment and the precise larval migration route within the host remain unknown and represent important priorities for future research.

The detection of T. felis and P. illiciens eggs exclusively in kittens from outdoor-origin litters likely reflects infections acquired prior to enrollment. Given the long prepatent periods of these parasites [30, 31], the anthelmintic treatment administered during acclimation may not have eliminated immature stages. In contrast, the two kittens that developed patent M. ierei infections originated from indoor litters and did not harbor concurrent helminth infections. Although speculative, the possibility exists that differences in prior environmental conditions and background parasitism between litters influenced host immune responses. Further investigation is needed to explore this possibility.

Overall, these findings suggest that percutaneous transmission may represent a natural route of infection for M. ierei and provide experimental evidence that the life cycle of Mammomonogamus spp. differs from that of avian syngamids. Instead, it appears to align more closely with ancylostomatid nematodes.

Conclusions

This study provides the first experimental evidence of a viable route of infection for M. ierei in domestic cats. Patent infection was achieved in two of three exposed cats, following subcutaneous inoculation with L3, with a prepatent period of 48–51 days. These findings indicate that infection can occur without the involvement of an intermediate host, supporting the possibility of a direct life cycle. By advancing the understanding of transmission in M. ierei, this work provides a basis for future investigations into the biology of Mammomonogamus spp., including zoonotic species such as M. laryngeus and M. nasicola. Further studies are needed to confirm percutaneous transmission pathways and to elucidate larval migration within the host.
