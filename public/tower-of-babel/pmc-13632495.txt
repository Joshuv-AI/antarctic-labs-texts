The Swedish national guideline on immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care
Authors: S. Klemming, Vesta Seyed Alikhani, K. Breding, P. Bäcke, S. Elvermark, A. Gustafsson, Å. Hermansson, E. Jonsson, N. Kjellqvist, M. Mattisson, M. Nelander, M-C. Nilsson, A. Nyholm, S. Lilliesköld, Y. Thernström Blomqvist, E. Wilson, S. Arwehed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immediate and continuous skin-to-skin contact between a mother and her newborn is associated with numerous health benefits. Benefits for infants includes improved thermoregulation, stabilization of heart rate and respiration. Benefits for mothers include reduced maternal postpartum bleeding and improved maternal blood pressure regulation. Benefits for both include reduced stress, earlier initiation of breastfeeding, and enhanced mother–infant bonding. There is a need to scale-up the clinical implementation of skin-to-skin contact for all newborns across care settings. In particular, current practices should be reconsidered for infants born preterm, with low birth-weight, requiring specialized medical interventions, and after instrumental births/caesarean sections. This also requires reorganization of maternal and newborn care. As emphasized by the World Health Organization in its implementation strategy for scaling up kangaroo mother care (2023), the mother-newborn dyad should be regarded as an inseparable center around which health care is organized, enabling zero separation. This article presents a translation of a Swedish national guideline recommending immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact for at least the first 2 hours after birth for all newborns, followed by a mother–newborn couplet care model including continued skin-to-skin contact. In mother-newborn couplet care the mother and her newborn, regardless of medical needs or clinical complexity, are cared for together in the same physical environment to minimize separation. Implementation requires multidisciplinary collaboration across operations, clearly defined responsibilities, and access to appropriately adapted facilities, equipment, and care processes across all settings in which mother-newborn dyads receive care. The guideline is intended for healthcare professionals and managers across the continuum of maternal, obstetric, neonatal, anesthetic, surgical, and intensive care in Sweden. It applies to all mothers and newborns during the first hours after birth and throughout the subsequent hospital care when both are managed in a hospital setting. The guideline includes a scientific rationale, definitions, recommendations, implementation guidance, impact assessment and quality indicators for monitoring and evaluation. The original Swedish version of this guideline was published in 2024. It has been reported to be helpful in the implementation in Swedish hospitals. We recommend others to make similar national, regional and/or local guidelines to support implementation of these innovative, non-pharmacological interventions to improve maternal and infant health.

The original guideline was written by the author group under the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions and the National Program Area for Children’s and Adolescent’s Health. Stina Klemming prepared this translation and adjustment for publication and the co-authors from the original author group reviewed the English version. Swedish maternal and newborn care follow high standards and quality. For 30 years there has been a strong development towards humanization of care and an infant- and family-centered developmental approach. In 2022 when the World Health Organization updated the recommendations for preterm and low birth-weight infants, separations between newborns and mothers were still common in Sweden during the first hours after birth. This was especially true for newborns born preterm, with low birth-weight or in need of specialized medical interventions, and/or after instrumental births/caesarean sections as well as for mothers with peripartum complications. To promote a re-think and new practices on skin-to-skin contact and zero separation between mothers and newborns and to promote equitable and evidence-based maternal-newborn care, the National Program Area for Child and Adolescent Health initiated a project for a national guideline through the National system for knowledge-driven management within Swedish healthcare. The project was governed by the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions under the National Program Area for Children’s and Adolescent’s Health and was supported by the National Program Area for Gynecological Diseases, Pregnancy and Childbirth, and the National Program Area for Anesthesiology, Intensive Care, and Transplant medicine. The guideline was developed through a collaborative process with an author group of healthcare professionals from neonatal care, obstetrics, anesthesiology, and intensive care, and one patient representative. An extensive prepublication review was conducted through invitations to all Swedish healthcare regions and specialty societies across the care continuum, including maternal, obstetric, postpartum and postnatal, neonatal, anesthetic, surgical, and intensive care. Input was also obtained from several patient and parent organizations. The guideline was launched through an open-access webinar presenting its content and facilitating discussion on shared future directions for maternal and newborn health, including reflections on key challenges and opportunities. The original guideline published in Swedish consists of three documents; 1. The Swedish guideline for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care; 2. A scientific background; and 3. An impact assessment. In this translated version the three documents are adjusted to one.

Scope of the recommendations

This guideline addresses staff and managers in the care chain of maternal health care, obstetric care, postpartum- and postnatal care, neonatal care, anesthesia, surgery and intensive care. The recommendations apply to all newborns and their mothers during the first hours after birth and the following time when both are being cared for in a hospital setting.

The guideline intends to provide guidance for the implementation of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care. The goal is that new mothers and their newborns are cared for together regardless of medical needs and complexity and with early or immediate initiation of skin-to-skin contact. Based on current scientific knowledge, the recommendation for immediate skin-to-skin contact directly at birth applies to newborns from 28 + 0 weeks of pregnancy. To achieve the goals of the recommendations in this guideline, consensus and cooperation across operational boundaries, a clear division of responsibilities and appropriate and adapted premises, equipment and working methods are required. The guideline includes definitions, recommendations, and guidance for the implementation and development of working methods around immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care.

The recommendations were made within the framework of the National system for knowledge-driven management within Swedish healthcare and are based on other Swedish and international guidelines and scientific evidence (1–8). The recommendations are also based on existing routines in Swedish hospitals and on experiences in the multiprofessional author group. The group have representation from all healthcare regions in Sweden, from all specialty associations within the care chain, maternal healthcare, obstetric care, postpartum- and postnatal care, neonatal care and anesthesiology, surgery and intensive care, as well as from a patient representative.

In this guideline the word “mother” has been chosen to refer to the birthing parent and the person who gave birth, although not everyone identifies as a woman or a mother (9). Families come in many constellations and parenting can take many forms. In the care situation, inclusive language needs to be used based on the person’s expressions and wishes. For more information, see Recommendation: “Inclusive language” from The Swedish Federation for Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer and Intersex Rights (10).

Scientific background

The first hours and days after birth are unique and are defined as a sensitive and critical period for both mothers and newborns based on the mutual regulation of physiological, behavioral and psychological processes. All newborns and new parents have a need for - and a right to - physical and emotional closeness (8, 11–13) and separation should be avoided (14–16). Mother-newborn couplet care has the potential for positive health effects (17–31). In the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare’s guidelines for newborn care and for pregnancy, birth and the time after birth it is particularly emphasized that healthcare facilities should ensure that newborns and parents are not separated at or after birth and that immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact between a mother and a newborn is enabled to the greatest extent possible (1–3).

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact between a mother and her newborn right at birth has many scientifically proven positive effects (5, 16, 32–36). This applies regardless of birth mode (33, 37). For newborn infants, skin-to-skin contact has been shown to stimulate newborn instinctive behaviors and the initiation of breastfeeding (32, 38–41), stabilize temperature regulation (32, 42), blood sugar regulation (43), and cardiorespiratory regulation during the newborn’s physiological adaptation after birth (44, 45). Likewise, beneficial effects on the microbiota, the normal bacterial flora (46), eating behaviors (33) sleep and self-regulation (47) are described. For the mother, skin-to-skin contact has been shown to stimulate lactation (38) and uterine contraction for less postpartum bleeding (48–51), stabilize blood pressure regulation (52), and strengthen emotional attachment to the newborn (53, 54). Skin-to-skin contact and couplet care from birth have been shown to improve experiences for both mothers and newborns (21, 55) with positive effects on well-being (56, 57), reduced stress (58), anxiety and pain (22, 23, 48, 56, 59–62). Skin-to-skin contact has also been shown to reduce stress and anxiety for the other parent (21, 55, 60). A positive development for a confident parenting is seen by keeping the family together (24, 63, 64).

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care describe the place of care. The content of the care is guided by the clinical condition of the mother and the newborn and should align with existing guidelines and clinical routines in maternal and newborn care. Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact can be implemented within current recommendations for umbilical cord clamping in infants delivered after 34 weeks of gestation who do not require resuscitation (65). This approach also applies to the stabilization of transition at birth for infants born before 35 weeks of gestation (66). Evidence indicates that routine procedures, such as weighing and measuring the infant, can be deferred until after the first hours following birth (4). During this period, the maternal care can be adapted to support immediate and continuous skin-to-skin contact (17).

In this guideline, uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact for at least 2 hours is recommended. This is to allow time for the newborn’s instinctive behaviors to take place (40, 41, 67, 68) At the same time, consideration must be given to the importance of the mother emptying her bladder. The newborn’s physiological adaptation is promoted by uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact with a parent during the first six hours (42, 44, 69, 70). Continued skin-to-skin contact during the first hours, days and throughout the hospital stay and beyond has strong scientific support and should be encouraged and supported as much as possible.

In cases where interruptions in skin-to-skin contact between a mother and her newborn is needed during the first hours, studies have shown positive effects of the newborn being cared for in skin-to-skin contact with the other parent, or a person appointed by the parent (42, 44, 63). If immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact with the mother is not possible until the newborn breastfed, breast stimulation, hand expression and colostrum administration should be encouraged as soon as possible, preferably already during the first hour (71, 72). When skin-to-skin contact between the mother and the newborn resumes, the newborn instinctive behaviors are stimulated again (67, 73, 74). It is also known that both full-term and preterm neonates exhibit increased breastfeeding-specific behaviors during skin-to-skin contact even after the first hours (75).

Mother-newborn couplet care together with immediate or very early and continuous skin-to-skin contact for low birth-weight and preterm newborns has reduced the risk of serious infections in low- and middle-income countries (76), lowered infant mortality (77), and resulted in better growth (78). For low birth-weight and preterm infants, intermittent skin-to-skin contact during hospitalization has shown positive and lasting effects on brain development and organization, and consequently positive effects on behavior and long-term development (79–83).

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), skin-to-skin contact should be initiated as soon as possible after birth. For low birth-weight and preterm newborns, skin-to-skin contact is recommended immediately and as continuously as possible during the hospital stay and beyond. The recommendations on initiation of skin-to-skin contact directly at birth apply to newborns from 28 + 0 weeks of pregnancy and with a birth weight over 1,000 grams (5, 33–36, 84). For the group of critically ill newborns and newborns born extremely preterm before week 28, there is a growing number of publications supporting early skin-to-skin contact and closeness between newborns and their parents (61, 85–90).

The strength and significance of these outcomes have been studied for different groups of newborns and families and differ based on starting points such as the newborn’s age, maturity and clinical condition, the mother’s condition and the family’s wishes and needs. Many of the publications we refer to in this scientific background for the guideline “immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care” are systematic reviews with grading of the strength of the evidence. There is strong scientific support for immediate and continuous skin-to-skin contact and minimized separation between mothers and newborns after birth. Close physical contact between newborns and parents, and early positive experiences are important for development and health in the short and long term. The significance for the individual parent-newborn dyad cannot be assessed based on current scientific evidence.

Definitions

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact means that the newborn lies naked directly against the mother’s bare skin for at least the first two hours after birth in order for the newborn to be undisturbed in its instinctive breastfeeding behaviors. This guideline focuses on the first hours after birth and skin-to-skin contact is recommended to continue for as many hours per day as possible during the hospitalization. When skin-to-skin contact with the mother is not possible, the other parent, or a person appointed by the parent, can care for the newborn in skin-to-skin contact.

Mother-newborn couplet care

Couplet care means that two patients receive their care together. With mother-newborn couplet care, the new mother and the newborn receive their care in the same unit and in close physical proximity to each other. This also applies when the mother, the newborn or both need specialized care. When either the mother or the newborn is discharged, care is no longer defined as couplet care.

Patient participation, person-centered care

Healthcare professionals have a responsibility to plan for and facilitate immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care together with the family. Communication and information are based on individual conditions and needs. Dialogue with parents about previous experiences, needs, expectations, physical and psychological resources and social networks is a prerequisite for giving the family individual support and the opportunity for participation and informed consent. Personal privacy must be considered, which is especially important when patients are physically exposed.

Care of the mother and the newborn together in immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care can be seen as part of a person-centered approach. Within neonatal care, this is seen as part of the concept of infant- and family-centered developmental care.

Support and communication

All expectant parents should be informed already during pregnancy about the health benefits of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care. Parents should be informed about the newborn’s need of closeness to a parent, or a person appointed by the parent, regardless of the condition of the mother or the newborn after birth.

Language and communication difficulties are handled individually, for example with the use of an interpreter throughout the course of care. Verbal information should be supplemented with visual and/or written material in different languages. Information should be repeated and communicated via maternal health care, obstetric care, postpartum- and postnatal care, neonatal care, parent groups, national and regional healthcare websites.

Communication and support to the new parents continues to be an important focus for healthcare professionals throughout the hospital stay. If families are separated, digital solutions facilitate communication within the family and between parents and staff.

The goal of all personnel involved in care before, during and after birth should be that all new mothers and their newborns are cared for together, regardless of medical needs and complexity.

Premises and equipment

Premises need to be designed for both routine care and emergency situations and have established alarm systems for each patient. Wards and rooms of all kinds should be adapted to offer a good environment for newborns, parents and staff. This includes, among other things, ergonomics, conditions for good care hygiene and personal integrity for the mother and the newborn. Conditions for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care should be included in the planning of renovations and constructions in obstetrics, neonatology, anesthesia, surgery and intensive care.

Equipment for safe and adequate care of both mothers and newborns, both during routine care and in emergency situations, needs to be available in or adjacent to the birth room and in all care units where mothers and newborns can be cared for in couplet care.

Equipment for both the mother and the newborn should be mobile and include, among other things: medical equipment for initial stabilization, emergency situations and continued carematerials, aids and medicines for care, treatment and nursingmonitoring equipment for physiological parametersthe possibility of transferring the mother and the newborn within the hospital while maintaining skin-to-skin contact.

Assessment of the level of care

Most mothers and newborns are healthy and adaptations can be made without much difficulty to provide access to immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care after birth. Individual assessments of the level of care and place of care are based on the medical and nursing needs of the mother and the newborn.

Couplet care is planned on the unit responsible for the patient with the most complex medical needs. Care is tailored based on each patient’s medical condition together with the unit’s capacity and conditions. The assessment is repeated if a patient’s condition, or the capacity and conditions of the unit changes. If separation needs to occur, couplet care and skin-to-skin contact should be resumed as soon as possible. Parents should be continuously involved and informed about the current assessment.

To assess the level of care, tools such as postpartum/postnatal risk assessment of both the mother and the newborn can help individualize care.

Implementation

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact

Skin-to-skin contact should be initiated as soon as possible after birth. To optimize the physiological transition after birth and support breastfeeding initiation, initial skin-to-skin contact with the mother should be prioritized. If this is not possible, skin-to-skin contact should be initiated with the other parent, or with a person designated by the parent. In multiple births, both newborns are placed together with the mother if possible. Routine care of the mother and the newborn should, if possible, be postponed until after the first two hours and medically justified care, nursing and transfers should take place in uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact.

Considerations for the guideline

Risk-benefit balance for the patient

The overall assessment for the recommendations is that the benefits outweigh the risk to the mother and the newborn, regardless of mode of birth.

If couplet care of a mother and a newborn is not provided, there is a risk of repeated and in some cases prolonged separations. If couplet care is not provided, immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact during the first hours and continued skin-to-skin contact thereafter is not possible.

In case of incomplete implementation of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care, with insufficient adaptations and when requirements are not met, there is a risk of inadequate care and follow-up in both acute situations and routine care. For the mother, this means increased risk of bleeding, risk of urinary retention, inadequate treatment of conditions such as high blood pressure, pain and infections, as well as a lack of support for breastfeeding and psychological well-being. For the newborn, incomplete implementation can have consequences such as sudden unexpected postnatal collapse (7, 91), hypothermia, inadequate breathing and low blood sugar.

In this guideline, it is emphasized that in order for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care to be possible and safe, the specified requirements in organization, premises, equipment and competence need to be met. Likewise, local guidelines are needed for individualized assessment and tailoring of care to decide level of care and place of care for the mother-newborn dyad. Several Swedish hospitals have made guidelines specific for their local prerequisites (92).

For care to be safe for mothers and newborns evidence-based guidelines for obstetric and newborn care must be used according to the clinical conditions and medical needs of both patients. This includes following the recommendations for the prevention of sudden infant death syndrome when parents and newborns sleep unattended (93).

The cost-effectiveness of the measure(s)

Immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact has the potential to improve the quality of care with relatively small investments. Based on described evidence and national and international experience, health economic gains are expected through reduced morbidity for mothers and newborns with reduced need for specialized care, fewer interventions and potentially stressful procedures, as well as shorter hospital stays and long-term improved health. Through improved breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact has a number of additional cost-effective outcomes for both mothers and newborns (25, 40, 94, 95).

Couplet care has the potential to lead to reduced healthcare costs through improved care quality and increased efficiency in care (25, 26). Financial savings can be achieved through, for example, reduced need for floor space when a mother and her newborn share the same room, fewer transfers between wards, and confident parents actively participating in the care of their newborn, enabling alternative use of staff resources. Effects of implementation are followed with indicators based on target group and change, see the Impact assessment for this guideline (27, 28).

New ways of working with immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care could, based on experiences from other changes in accordance with an Infant- and Family-Centered Developmental Care approach, provide increased job satisfaction and thus continuity in staff groups (96).

Direct and indirect implementation costs

Experience from units that have been adapted to increasingly offer immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care for mothers and newborns in need of specialised care suggests that many initial additional costs are temporary. Similarly, costs for adapting premises and equipment for the maternal care in a department other than the birth unit and maternity ward are temporary investments. Providing care with immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact for newborns in need of specialised care may require one-time investments for mobile equipment. In an early implementation phase, a certain increase in personnel costs has been described. Continuous training efforts may be needed for adequate cooperation between personnel in obstetric care, postpartum- and postnatal care, neonatal care, anesthesia, surgery and intensive care.

Equity

Despite clear consensus in national and international guidelines on the importance of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care, current healthcare processes and working methods still lead to deficiencies in practice (97–100). The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child describes the child’s right not to be separated from his or her parents and that the best interests of the child shall be a primary consideration (8). When the mother gives birth by caesarean section, or needs other surgical procedures or intensive care in connection with the birth, the mother and the newborn are often separated. Separations are also common as newborns require neonatal care immediately after birth, resulting in limited opportunities for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact (101).

A survey in 2023 on couplet care in Swedish neonatal units showed that less than half of the units could offer couplet care for mothers with a newborn cared for in the neonatal unit and there was a large variation in the degree of implementation (17). It also emerged that the implementation of methods that enable immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact at birth for newborns in need of medical support had begun in many places but was still insufficiently implemented across the country.

Patients’ propensity to accept the interventions

The propensity of parents to accept the recommendations is high, because being with their newborn from birth is a natural thing. The natural place for a newborn is in skin-to-skin contact with the mother. Separation is partly caused by the way care is organized and partly by a lack of conditions to accommodate and support parents (97). Parents should be informed of the newborn’s rights not to be separated from them and their mutual need to be close to each other, regardless of the care needs of the mother and the newborn. Dialogue with parents about previous experiences, needs, expectations and physical and mental resources is important in order to provide the opportunity for informed choices and full participation in the care of a sick or preterm newborn. The family’s situation and opportunities to be together and care for their newborn skin-to-skin can be made easier by getting support and help in their situation from other family members. The family needs access to food, sanitation and an environment that enables privacy and sleep (21). There are international standards for how the care environment in the neonatal unit should be designed to accommodate this (102–105).

Feasibility

Experience shows that a stepwise implementation of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care is common (28). In existing operations, a first step may be to introduce couplet care for mothers and newborns who have a low risk assessment after birth. As experience, premises, equipment, competence and confidence develop, the care of increasingly complex patients can take place in immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and in couplet care. The goal is for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care to be offered regardless of care needs. The care is planned and provided according to the clinical situation and medical needs of the mother and the newborn. Different models for how departments and units have been organized to be able to offer couplet care has been described (17, 31). Collaborations, organization of management and staff as well as how premises are planned are based on local conditions, capacity and the organization of care. Couplet care can be carried out in healthcare environments with different architecture, and single rooms are not a strict requirement. When new wards are built, the environment should be optimized for couplet care and for a pleasant atmosphere for newborns and parents as well as for staff. In-depth information on the design of adapted care environments can be found in European and American standards (106, 107).

Sustainable implementation of couplet care and working methods for immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact for all mothers and newborns is based on cross-functional collaboration between maternal health care, obstetric care, postpartum- and postnatal care, neonatal care, anesthesia, surgery and intensive care (29, 30).

For continued care after couplet care

When either the mother or the newborn is discharged, a form of care other than couplet care takes over because it is no longer two patients who are cared for together. If the mother needs continued care, she should be able to have the newborn close and breastfeeding should be supported as far as possible. Children’s right to be with their parents or with a person or family member designated by a parent continues to apply at all times during any hospital stay.

The other parent stays at the hospital as a partner to the mother and parent to the newborn when the mother and the newborn are cared for together. Parents of hospitalized newborns and children of all ages should be offered to live with the child in a family room (1). More information on the principles of Infant- and Family-Centered Developmental Care can be found in the European standards of care for newborn health (108).

Ethical perspective

This guideline emphasizes that hospital services and staff are responsible for enabling skin-to-skin contact and couplet care as far as possible. Support must be given on the basis of a person-centered approach, taking into account the patient’s needs and resources. A clear goal is that organization, equipment and staffing should not constitute an obstacle to immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and couplet care.

This guideline’s recommendations on couplet care strengthens the patient’s right to information, participation and respect and, the family's possibility to be together. Implementation of couplet care also means that newborns are only separated from their parents if it is beneficial to the newborn and that reunification is facilitated as soon as possible. Couplet care helps to ensure maternal-newborn skin-to-skin contact early, repeatedly and even continuously.

Early, accessible and tailored information about benefits for both the mother and the newborn, is needed from healthcare providers to facilitate immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact. Every health care situation must protect the privacy and integrity of the patient, and special consideration is warranted when medical procedures require exposure of skin and intimate parts of the body. In situations where immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact between mother and newborn is not possible due to personal, physical or psychological circumstances, a supportive, non-judgmental approach is particularly important. Individual medical risk assessments of both mother and newborn make it easier for staff to tailor care and find cross-organizational common solutions for skin-to-skin contact and couplet care. In complex situations, involvement of the other parent and the family support network can enable the newborn to have skin-to-skin contact with another family member.

Discussion

The place for newborns—whether born at full term or prematurely—is in the mother’s arms, without the baby being separated from the parents. Over the past two or three decades, neonatal care has begun to rectify mistakes made a century ago; while survival rates in neonatal care improved, the experiences biologically essential for both infant, mother and family were simultaneously overlooked. As always, changes in clinical practice lag behind scientific knowledge regarding what constitutes best care.

An important core message in this guideline is that skin-to-skin contact and the concept to care for mothers and newborns together is all about the place of care. The content of care is always governed by the medical condition of each patient according to care protocols. In most cases, this does not contradict the recommendations in this guideline. Complex medical situations, especially if unexpected, can challenge other good intentions. There will be imperative situations where lifesaving interventions are prioritized. If separation is needed the best place of care with skin-to-skin contact or direct closeness between the newborn and the parent should be reinstalled as soon as possible. This is why we propose that teams always discuss the questions under “level of care and collaboration”.

Sudden Unexpected Postnatal Collapse (SUPC) can occur during early skin-to-skin contact as well as during separation (93). Continuous clinical monitoring and support of the newborn’s airway and positioning is essential, especially for fragile newborns. Ensuring the infant’s face remains visible and the nose is unobstructed is critical to preventing respiratory compromise while maintaining this vital experience for the newborn and the parent. According to our experience, the most evident risk for mother or newborn is if there is an incomplete implementation of immediate skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care were care needs might be neglected or compromised related to lack of proper organization of care or quality of care. We believe that safe care can be provided following this guideline and its recommendations for requirements and for implementation.

The original Swedish guideline on immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care was published almost two years ago. It was well received and has been reported to facilitate implementation and collaboration across units and disciplines in Swedish hospitals. We believe this was attributed to several factors: the author group had a broad and relevant representation; an extensive review process proceeded the publication; the launch webinar with open discussions; as well as a timing where several hospitals were ready for the next step in the process towards zero separation between mothers and newborns. As part of the preparations for the guideline a survey showed that in 2023, approximately one third of the Swedish neonatal units had implemented the first steps of couplet care in the neonatal unit. A follow-up on the implementation is planned in a near future. To our knowledge, many more units have made well-structured implementations following this guideline.

To change routines and mind-set in healthcare is difficult. There are no “one model fits all” and different settings have their unique opportunities and challenges. Implementation in a step-by-step strategy for different groups of patients and different conditions have been a successful way to go and teams in different parts of the world continue to publish their specific experiences. We believe that most if not all teams can and should increase collaboration across maternal and newborn care and make changes to facilitate the implementation to care for all newborns and mothers together and in skin-to-skin contact.

There are some evident changes following the publication of the Swedish version of the guideline on immediate skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care. In Sweden, we now have consensus on clear definitions of the concepts. Awareness on benefits on zero separation and needed changes for best care practice increased among decision makers, managers and healthcare professionals in the care continuum for mothers and newborns as well as in the general public following this guideline. We now have a common vision to care for all mothers and newborns together, with as little separation as possible. We strongly recommend others to write national as well as regional and local guidelines and protocols for the clinical practice of immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and mother-newborn couplet care for zero separation and the best possible well-being and outcomes for newborns, mothers and families.
