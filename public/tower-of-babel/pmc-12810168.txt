Introduction

Adenomatoid odontogenic tumor (AOT) is a benign, slow-growing neoplasm restricted to the maxillofacial region, predominantly involving the deciduous tooth-bearing areas of the mandible and maxilla. It has been described as the “master of disguise” and was originally reported by Steensland as epithelioma adamantinum in the literature.1 The term “adenomatoid odontogenic tumor” was later introduced by Philipsen and Birn in 1969,2 and subsequently recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) in its 1971 classification of odontogenic tumors.3 According to the WHO, AOT is defined as “a tumor composed of odontogenic epithelium in a variety of histoarchitectural patterns, embedded in a mature connective tissue stroma and characterized by slow but progressive growth.”1 Clinically, it is often referred to as the “two-thirds tumor,” since two-thirds of cases occur in the maxilla, two-thirds present in young females, two-thirds are associated with an unerupted tooth, and two-thirds involve canines.4 The current case highlights the application of Carnoy's solution in a pediatric patient to minimize recurrence following enucleation, curettage, and extraction of the associated tooth. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the earliest reported uses of Carnoy's solution for AOT management in children.

Case Description

A 10-year-old girl reported to the dental outpatient department with the chief complaint of a missing lower left front tooth since birth. The family history was noncontributory. No associated pain or swelling was noted. Intraoral examination revealed a missing 72 and a retained 71. Palpation of the lower left buccal vestibule revealed a hard bony lesion extending from 72 to 74. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) demonstrated a clearly demarcated, irregularly oval, unilocular hypodense area adjacent to tooth 31. The lesion measured 13.1 mm mesiodistally × 9.8 mm buccolingually × 12.8 mm superoinferiorly, with thin corticated borders, situated within the alveolar bone of the anterior mandible near the periapical region of tooth 71, slightly crossing the midline. The lesion appeared to arise from the follicular space of the impacted 72, attached at the level of the cementoenamel junction (CEJ).

The lesion in the 32 region revealed a well-circumscribed, irregularly ovoid, unilocular hypodensity of size 12.8 mm (mesiodistal) × 13.1 mm (buccolingual) × 18.3 mm (superoinferior), with thin corticated borders, situated within the alveolar bone of the anterior mandible in relation to the periapical region of 72. The lesion appeared to arise from the follicular space of the impacted 32, attached below its CEJ, enveloping the crown and root (Fig. 1). Based on the impressions from the CBCT report, the findings indicated involvement of lower teeth 72 and 32 with an odontogenic neoplasm, along with vertical impaction of teeth 31, 32, and 33. Based on radiographic evaluation, patient age, and lesion location, a differential diagnosis was considered between a calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumor (CEOT) and an AOT. The proposed treatment included enucleation of the lesion under local anesthesia, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Procedure followed for the management of AOT

Histopathological assessment of the stained sections showed cystic and solid areas of tumor cells. Solid areas revealed multinodular proliferation of spindle, cuboidal, and columnar cells in a scattered duct-like structure in a rosette-like pattern. The duct-like structures were lined with eosinophilic rings. Large amounts of calcification and eosinophilic droplets were observed between duct-like cells. Connective tissue stroma showed dense fibrosis with numerous vascular components, and extravasated RBCs were observed. All of these attributes were reflective of an AOT.

Follow-up panoramic radiographs at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively demonstrated continuous superior and inferior borders of the mandible with no evidence of radiolucent lesions or cortical defects, indicating favorable healing and bone formation at the surgical site. At the 2-year follow-up, tooth 31 had erupted, and tooth 33 was observed to be erupting into the oral cavity (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Adenomatoid odontogenic tumor is a benign odontogenic tumor that most commonly appears in individuals during the second and third decades of life.5 Characterized by its slow-growing nature, AOT often presents as painless swelling, making clinical diagnosis challenging. Early detection is crucial for timely intervention and successful management. Histologically, AOT exhibits specific features that aid in its identification. In this case, histopathological findings strongly supported the diagnosis of AOT. Notably, Carnoy's solution, a fixative, was employed to enhance the management of AOT by minimizing the risk of recurrence.

The etiology of AOT remains a subject of debate within the scientific community, although some authors admit that the tumor may originate from epithelial remnants associated with the dental lamina complex system. AOT exhibits a wide age distribution, spanning from 3 to 82 years, with a noteworthy observation that 90% of cases are diagnosed before the age of 30 years. Furthermore, the tumor displays a predilection for females, with a gender ratio of 1.9:1. Notably, AOT tends to arise predominantly from the regions containing deciduous teeth in the jaws.5

Enucleation, along with extraction of the affected tooth and subsequent curettage, is widely endorsed as an effective treatment for AOT, demonstrating favorable outcomes and low recurrence rates.6 However, it is important to note that despite the successful nature of this treatment approach, occasional recurrence cases have been documented. Notably, Chuan-Xiang and Yan reported a case of recurrent AOT, indicating the need for continued vigilance and better understanding of the factors contributing to the recurrence of this odontogenic tumor.7

The use of Carnoy's solution, known for its fixative and chemical cauterizing properties, has been shown to aid in the management of AOT by minimizing the risk of recurrence. In 1985, Voorsmit provided valuable insights into the application of Carnoy's solution. Voorsmit reported that during a period of over 5 minutes of Carnoy's solution application, hard tissue penetration extended to 1.54 mm. This observation underscores the need for judicious application, as prolonged exposure beyond 5 minutes may pose a risk of nerve injury.8 In addition to enucleation, an alternative therapeutic option for AOT includes marsupialization, which aids in minimizing lesion dimensions with preservation of nearby anatomical integrity and improved surgical outcomes with fewer complications.9 Furthermore, liquid nitrogen cryotherapy has emerged as a promising intervention, demonstrating a reduction in recurrence rates.10 However, these modalities were not utilized in this case because of the limited extent of the lesion, and cryotherapy was deemed impractical due to its high cost and unavailability to the authors.

Despite these advancements, a significant challenge in the current case was the need to extract 32 owing to its close association with the lesion and high mobility, highlighting the difficulties inherent in treating AOT. Consideration of reimplantation could have potentially improved treatment outcomes.

As the patient also presented with a constricted maxilla and retrognathic mandible, rapid palatal expansion with a jackscrew was initiated 2 years after the surgical procedure, and the patient is currently undergoing twin-block myofunctional therapy to modify the skeletal growth pattern.

Conclusion

The management of benign odontogenic tumors necessitates a strategic approach centered on prompt and timely diagnosis coupled with a conservative treatment plan. Enucleation, curettage, and extraction, complemented by the use of Carnoy's solution, have emerged as effective interventions to prevent the recurrence of these lesions. Although the recurrence rate is relatively low, the need for periodic recall and follow-up cannot be overstated. Future research endeavors may further refine these approaches and contribute to the continuous improvement of benign tumor management protocols.
