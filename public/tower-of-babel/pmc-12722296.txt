Sit to stand and timed up and go in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies
Authors: Tanya Chandra, Raisa L Silva, Shiri Keret, Runjia Li, G K Balasubramani, Ramsha Riaz, Akanksha Sharma, Jesse Wilkerson, Dana P Ascherman, Chester V Oddis, Siamak Moghadam-Kia, Rohit Aggarwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies can significantly impair physical function, of which Sit to Stand (STS) and Timed Up and Go (TUG) are quick, operator independent measures. We aimed to evaluate psychometric properties of STS and TUG compared to established core set measures of disease activity; and assess feasibility of patients self-performing these tests remotely. Data from a 6-month prospective observational study (Myositis Patient Centered Tele-Research Study -My PACER) was analyzed. Patient-reported and functional assessments were collected monthly over 6 months. A total of 120 patients (75% female, 81% White, mean age 55.5 ± 13.4 years,52% Dermatomyositis, 39% Polymyositis, 9% Necrotizing Myopathy) participated. There was strong test–retest reliability between baseline and month one for STS (r = 0.80) and TUG (r = 0.87); p < 0.01. At baseline, STS and TUG showed a very strong correlation with each other (r = − 0.75) and with most CSMs. Strong correlations were seen with Muscle Disease Activity and a validated Patient Reported Outcome measure of physical function, PROMIS-PF 20. At 6 months, STS and TUG were significantly better among patients who improved according to Total Improvement Score. STS and TUG showed good reliability, even when self-performed remotely using video instructions, with excellent construct validity and responsiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26224-5.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Rheumatology

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a heterogeneous group of systemic autoimmune disorders characterized by muscle inflammation and weakness1. Muscle strength measured by Manual Muscle Testing (MMT), is one of the key components of the core set measures (CSMs) developed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies group (IMACS) to assess disease activity2. MMT is a simple test that has demonstrated good inter- and intra-operator reliability; however, it lacks robust sensitivity to change and requires provider training. It additionally demonstrates a ceiling effect3,4. Hand-held dynamometers are also used to measure muscle strength and do not have a ceiling effect5, but require significant expertise and time. Both the MMT-8 and the hand-held dynamometer are limited to testing isometric strength (i.e. force generated without movement, related predominantly to type II muscle fiber activity). In contrast, isotonic tests measure muscle activity during movement, which is more representative of daily activities, and assess both endurance as well as maximum force (i.e., both type I and II fiber function). Therefore, isotonic tests provide a more complete assessment of muscle function6. Additionally, there is no test for assessment of skeletal muscle endurance as part of the IMACS CSMs. The only validated tool for endurance measurement is the Functional Index 3 (FI-3), which requires time and expertise, making it cumbersome for use in clinical trials and practice2.

In light of the limitations of the currently available measures, evaluation of tests that are more representative of daily activities are needed. Sit to Stand (STS) and Timed Up and Go (TUG) tests are easy-to-perform tests (predominantly testing truncal and lower extremity strength) that are operator-independent (performed by the patients themselves without any external assistance). They have been found to be reliable, valid, and responsive for myositis patients in previous studies6,7, though these cross-sectional studies enrolled a relatively small number of patients (n = 32 and 31 respectively) and had limited patient-reported outcomes (PROs) to assess the validity of STS/TUG. These tests should be evaluated in larger, longitudinal studies to generate more robust data that would support their use. With the increasing use of tele-medicine in both clinical practice and research, there is a need to evaluate characteristics of tests that can be self-performed remotely.

Our aims were therefore to evaluate the psychometric properties of STS/TUG when remotely self-performed by myositis patients and to assess feasibility of performing these tests remotely. We used data from a prospective observational cohort study that remotely recruited and assessed patients by tele-medicine using smart phone technology and wearable devices.

Methods

“Myositis Patient Centered Tele-Research” (My PACER) was a United States (U.S.) based 6-month prospective multi-center observational study in which IIM patients were recruited using two strategies:

Center-based cohort (CBC): Patients were recruited in person through clinic visits (Myositis Center; University of Pittsburgh Medical Center [UPMC] and Cedars-Sinai Medical Center). This group was further divided (equally and randomly) into two sub-cohorts based on the method of follow-up. The first was the “local cohort,” where data were collected by in-person assessments at baseline and follow-up. The second freferred to as the “remote cohort,” underwent remote tele-medicine follow-up using the study website or a mobile app.

Center-based cohort (CBC): Patients were recruited in person through clinic visits (Myositis Center; University of Pittsburgh Medical Center [UPMC] and Cedars-Sinai Medical Center). This group was further divided (equally and randomly) into two sub-cohorts based on the method of follow-up. The first was the “local cohort,” where data were collected by in-person assessments at baseline and follow-up. The second freferred to as the “remote cohort,” underwent remote tele-medicine follow-up using the study website or a mobile app.

Tele-research cohort (TRC): Patients were recruited from many U.S. sites through myositis patient-support organizations, physician referrals, and online/social media outreach. Follow-up was similar to the remote sub-cohort above.

Tele-research cohort (TRC): Patients were recruited from many U.S. sites through myositis patient-support organizations, physician referrals, and online/social media outreach. Follow-up was similar to the remote sub-cohort above.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Pittsburgh and all patients signed written informed consent prior to study participation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the IRB.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Key inclusion criteria included a rheumatologist-, dermatologist-, or neurologist-confirmed diagnosis of Dermatomyositis (DM), Polymyositis (PM), or Necrotizing Myositis (NM) in English-speaking adults living in the U.S. In addition, participants had to meet at least 4 of the 5 following criteria for PM/NM or 3 of the 5 plus rash(es) for DM: (1) muscle weakness; (2) muscle or skin biopsy consistent with myositis; (3) electromyography consistent with myositis; (4) muscle enzyme(s) elevation (i.e. creatine kinase (CK), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aldolase, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)); or (5) a myositis specific autoantibody (MSA). Patients had to be willing to participate in all study procedures for 6 months including answering all study questionnaires, performing all study procedures, and participating in audio–video conferences (tele-medicine) with the study investigator.

Exclusion Criteria included major travel plans or plans to change residency within 6 months, major surgery planned during the study, pregnancy/lactation, and any other reason deemed by the investigator to be a concern for study completion.

Study design

After subject enrollment, demographics, medication data, and disease characteristics were collected for all patients at baseline. Qualitative disease characteristics and Clinician Reported Outcomes (ClinROs) (manual muscle testing [MMT], physician-global disease activity [physician-global], extra-muscular global disease activity through the Myositis Disease Activity Assessment Tool (MDAAT)) were obtained at baseline and at the 6-month visit8. The MMTs for the local center-based cohort were obtained via in-person assessment performed by the Rheumatologist or Neurologist, while the MMTs for the non-local cohort were obtained from electronic medical records as part of standard care by their rheumatologist/neurologist/dermatologist.

PROs and other patient functional assessments were collected at monthly intervals for 6-months (Online Resource Supplementary Table 1). These included Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI)2, patient global disease activity (Patient Global)2, PROMIS PF-209, TUG, STS, and physical activity monitor data from Fitbit® (daily step count and peak cadence)10,11. All subjects completed the study procedures remotely using the study website/mobile app for the TRC and remote sub-cohort of CBC, or through paper forms/electronic tablets during in-person clinic evaluations for the local sub-cohort. Active vs. inactive disease was determined based on the treating physician’s intent to escalate immunosuppression in the previous 6 months.

Sit to Stand (STS) and Timed Up and Go (TUG) testing

STS and TUG were performed in a standardized manner in both the CBC and TRC. For the local sub-cohort of CBC, participants performed these tests at the center at baseline and at the 6-month visit. For the other visits, these tests were done remotely. In both the TRC and the remote sub-cohort of the CBC, these tests were done remotely in an unsupervised manner for all the monthly visits.

In the CBC these tests were guided by the center’s research coordinator and in the TRC these timed tests were self-performed by subjects using their mobile phone or stopwatch. Live step by step instructions given by a research coordinator to a typical center subject were the source of the YouTube video and written step by step instructions for the TRC subject performing these functional tests, so that both cohorts receive the same instructions. Both cohorts had the opportunity to ask the research coordinator any questions regarding the test procedures.

STS is the number of times a patient can stand from a seated position and sit back down in 30 s. TUG is the time in seconds needed to rise from a chair, walk 3 m, return to the chair, and sit down. At every visit, subjects were asked to complete each test twice (with average of the two readings used in our analysis) and record if they had help from another person or used their hands and/or any other aids to assist in getting up.

Hypothesis

We expected good test–retest reliability, strong construct validity with a strong correlation between other measures of physical function (such as MMT), but weak to no correlation with extra-muscular global assessment. Additionally, we expected good longitudinal responsiveness to change with improvement in STS and TUG in patients who demonstrated improvement in overall disease activity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism version 7.00 for Windows (La Jolla, California USA), R version 4.3.0 (Vienna, Austria), and SAS version 9.4 (RTP, North Carolina USA). Descriptive statistics were performed for baseline cohort characteristics.

Compliance

Paired t-test and Chi-square test were used to compare frequency of form completion and compliance between the study groups, respectively.

Reliability

Test–retest reliability of STS/TUG was assessed by using Spearman correlation of STS/TUG at baseline (visit 1) and the one-month follow up visit (visit 2). This was based on the assumption that myositis is a slow evolving condition and disease activity does not change drastically in a month12–14. Results for the whole cohort as well as the TRC vs. the local and remote sub-cohorts of CBC were evaluated. A sensitivity analysis was done for patients with different levels of disease activity (inactive vs. active) as well as disease subtypes (PM, DM, NM).

Validity

At baseline Spearman correlation was performed between STS/TUG and demographic features as well as between disease characteristics and several PROs and ClinROs to assess validity. Thresholds of strength of correlation were defined as by Cohen et al. (rho ≥ 0.5: strong, 0.3 to < 0.5: moderate, 0.1 to < 0.3: weak, < 0.1 no/negligible correlation)15.

Statistical significance of differences in median STS/TUG between various sub-groups was calculated using the Mann–Whitney test (when comparing 2 groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (when comparing more than 2 groups). Differences in categorical variables between groups were calculated using the Chi-square test.

Longitudinal validity was assessed by looking at the association of absolute change of STS and TUG and the various PROs and CLinROs using the Spearman correlation between baseline and the 6-month mark.

Additionally, repeated measures linear mixed models were utilized to evaluate the relationship of STS and TUG to PROs and ClinROs, after adjusting for fixed covariate effects (age at enrollment, gender, race, Body Mass Index (BMI), subtype of IIM, and visit number), as well as a random effect for patient.

Responsiveness

Effect size for STS and TUG was calculated based on anchors: (1) ACR/EULAR myositis response criteria 2016 (TIS) and (2) Patient Assessment of Change over 6 months16–19. Effect size was calculated via the following formula: (Mean difference between baseline and follow-up)/SD pooled. Interpretation of the magnitude of effect size was done using Cohen’s criteria with thresholds of 0.2 for small, 0.5 for moderate, and 0.8 for large effects20. The significance of change in STS and TUG over 6 months in the different response groups based on Patient Assessment of Change and ACR/EULAR clinical response groups was assessed using Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests.

Clinically Important Difference (CID) was calculated by estimating the difference of medians between groups categorized as having ‘Improvement’ vs. ‘No Improvement’, according to anchors such as Patient Assessment of Change and 2016 ACR/EULAR myositis response criteria (TIS).

Demographic characteristics of participants.

Study enrollment included 62 (52%) DM, 47 (39%) PM, and 11 (9%) NM subjects (Table 1). The presence of MSAs or MAAs (myositis associated autoantibodies) was tested in 107 subjects; while 69 (64.5%) had an MSA, 19 (17.8%) had an MAA.

Compliance with self-performed STS and TUG

Subjects were highly compliant with baseline completion of STS and TUG [114/120 (95%)]. Most subjects completed the minimum requirement of 5 of 7 STS [100 /120 (83%)] and TUG [99/120 (82.5%)] assessments. There was no significant difference in compliance between the TRC and CBC (Online Resource Supplementary Table 2). Of seven potential visits, there was no difference in the mean number of visits for TRC (6.6 ± 1.7) vs. CBC (6.3 ± 1.5) (p = 0.33).

Baseline association with demographics and clinical characteristics

The median (interquartile range [IQR]) value of STS for the entire cohort at baseline was 10.5 (8.2–13.5), while that for TUG was 9.8 (8–13.5). No significant associations were found between STS/TUG scores and age at diagnosis, age at study entry, sex, race, or ethnicity (Supplementary Table 3 and Table 4).

Median STS/TUG results were similar by clinical disease subtypes (DM, PM, NM)(Supplementary Table 4).

Thresholds of normal STS/TUG in myositis patients with normal muscle strength

Given that the majority of the patients had normal muscle strength (by MMT) (n = 70/120 (58.3%)), we derived thresholds of normal STS/TUG in myositis patients post treatment. We found that an STS of > 13 had > 90% specificity while an STS > 15 had > 95% specificity for identifying normal MMT. Similarly, a TUG of < 7.9 s had a > 90% specificity and a TUG < 6.8 had a > 95% specificity for identifying patients with a normal MMT. For patients with abnormal MMT, STS value < 7 had a > 90% specificity and an STS value < 6 had a > 95% specificity for identifying a patient with an abnormal MMT. Similarly, TUG values > 14 s had a 90% specificity for identifying patients with an abnormal MMT, and TUG of > 15 s had a 95% specificity for the same.

We also estimated average values for patients by age group for those with normal MMT and those with normal MMT plus HAQ-DI < 0.25 (n = 28/120 (23.3%)) (Supplementary Tables 5a-d). This assessment showed an overall trend of worsening STS/TUG with age, although there was a small number of patients in each sub-group.

Reliability

There was strong test–retest reliability between baseline and month 1 for both STS (rho = 0.80) and TUG (rho = 0.87). For STS, reliability was maintained regardless of the method of recruitment and follow-up (in person vs. remote). Interestingly, reliability for TUG was better in the TRC compared to the local sub-cohort of CBC (rho = 0.84 vs. 0.37 respectively). Reliability results were similar across clinical disease subtypes. Disease activity (based on physician assessment and Physician Global) did not affect the reliability of STS/TUG. Reliability assessment of STS/TUG after grouping them based on Patient Global Disease Activity was limited due to a very small number of patients with inactive disease (Table 2).

Validity

Baseline association

STS and TUG had a strong correlation with each other at baseline. Both showed a moderate to strong correlation with all CSMs, except CK and extra-muscular disease activity, demonstrating convergent and divergent validity (Table 3 and Fig. 1). In an analysis of the local sub-cohort of the CBC, MMT showed strong correlation with STS/TUG similar to that seen in the whole cohort. HAQ-DI and PROMIS-PF 20 showed the strongest correlation with both STS and TUG, indicating good validity for assessment of physical function from the patients’ perspective. Both STS and TUG showed moderate correlation with muscle disease activity (per MDAAT)8(Table 3).

Longitudinal association

There was a moderate correlation between change in PROMIS-PF 20 and change in STS (r = 0.43, p < 0.005), and a weak correlation with change in TUG (r = 0.28, p = 0.01). When evaluating absolute changes of CSMs, STS had the best correlation with CK (r = 0.33, p = 0.01). TUG, on the other hand, had the strongest correlation with the patient global assessment (r = 0.36, p < 0.001).

Linear repeated measures mixed models were used to assess the longitudinal relationships of STS/TUG with the various ClinROs, PROs, and functional assessments. Associations were strongest with PROMIS-PF 20 and HAQ-DI after controlling for age, sex, race, BMI, IIM clinical subtype, and visit number (Table 5).

Responsiveness

STS and TUG improved among patients demonstrating clinical improvement (compared to no improvement) between their baseline and 6-month visit. Similarly, both STS and TUG improved significantly among patients with at least minimal improvement (2016 ACR/EULAR myositis response criteria), while higher TIS scores correlated with greater improvement in STS/TUG. We found a significant improvement in the TUG in subjects with a HAQ-DI improvement of at least 0.22, but this was not observed with the STS (Table 7)21.

Discussion

Functional limitation is common in myositis patients and is due to many causes that include muscle weakness, impaired endurance, and fatigue2,22–25. Thus reliable, valid and responsive measures of muscle strength and function are critical in patient care and clinical trials.

Currently, we rely on the MMT to measure muscle strength, but this is examiner- and expertise-dependent and is only semi-quantitative3,4. Moreover, current measures of isometric strength (MMT and quantitative dynamometry) are not ideal measures of the isotonic strength required to complete activities of daily living, and they fail to measure endurance. Even though endurance can be measured by FI-3, the test is cumbersome and requires expertise2.

The STS/TUG tests present several advantages over traditional strength tests. They are simple, objective, and operator-independent; making them suitable for in person clinic-based as well as remote (tele-medicine) assessments. The metrics are continuous over time and offer insights into disease progression and treatment effectiveness, making STS/TUG valuable tools for assessing lower extremity muscle function in IIM patients. Prior studies have shown both tests to be reliable, valid, and responsive and our study further adds to this data given its significantly larger cohort, the longitudinal measurement of a large number of PROs, assessment of clinically important differences of both STS/TUG as well as inclusion of data from wearable devices6,7.

The current study involved a large cohort (n = 120) which was largely representative of the patients with IIM in the US in terms of age and gender. Unfortunately most clinical studies have an under-representation of non-Caucasian patients in the US and this was the same for our study21–26. Longitudinal assessments and high compliance rates for STS/TUG, independent of recruitment method or myositis subtype. STS/TUG showed excellent test–retest reliability when tested 1 month apart, regardless of recruitment method or myositis subtype. Cut-offs for normal were ascertained, with > 90% and > 95% specificity for both STS/TUG, providing useful parameters for longitudinal evaluation of IIM patients.

This study established the construct convergent and divergent validity of STS/TUG by correlating them with clinical and patient-reported outcome measures. Notably, while STS/TUG correlated well with MMT values, they weakly correlated with the serum CK, suggesting that CK levels might not accurately represent functional status. This result is somewhat expected in DM patients who were a majority in this cohort, as CK level does not correlate with muscle disease activity in these patients27–30. Additionally, there could be an element of muscle atrophy contributing to muscle weakness which can also contribute to lack of association of muscle enzymes with STS/TUG.

The longitudinal association of STS/TUG over the study period was strong for PROs such as HAQ-DI, patient global, PROMIS-PF 20, and the MMT, suggesting that these tools offer a comprehensive evaluation of both objective strength and the patient’s functional status. This was further supported by the correlation seen with STS in patients demonstrating an improvement in the TIS and patient assessment of change. The same was true regarding the TUG in relation to a TIS improvement and a HAQ-DI improvement ≥ 0.22 compared to baseline. These results demonstrate that STS/TUG are measures of physical function that are responsive to change and improve concordantly with other outcome measures.

The strengths of this study lie in its large sample size, successful implementation of remote assessments, and high compliance rates. These factors highlight the feasibility of using tele-medicine for functional assessment of myositis patients, decreasing the need on-site assessments. The tele-medicine principles used in this study will allow a wider network of patients to be recruited by a single center, contrasting with traditional in-person evaluations in which patient travel can be a limiting factor—ultimately, disenfranchising some participants.

A limitation of our study is the limited representation of non-White and Hispanic patients, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations. This underrepresentation is a known issue in rare disease research and underscores the need for future studies to proactively recruit more diverse cohorts31–35. Importantly, the tele-medicine and remote research methods used in this study may help address this gap by increasing accessibility and reducing participation barriers for geographically or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Future research should include intentional outreach to underrepresented populations to confirm the validity and applicability of STS/TUG measures across racial and ethnic groups.

The lack of assessment of technological literacy could be a potential limitation of this study (although some degree of comfort with technology was inherently required for this largely remote study) as well as lack of a structured assessment of the patient’s environmental set up (e.g., type of chair, flooring, space constraints) We must also consider the lack of quality control of STS/TUG measurements given that remote measurements were not supervised. However, the study coordinators provided written and video-based training on these tests, and there was excellent test–retest reliability for both STS/TUG in the local sub-cohort of the CBC where the baseline measurements were done in clinic under supervision of research coordinators and remotely one month later. Moreover, high reliability and validity and lack of aberrant results suggest that tests were performed correctly by patients remotely. The use of medical records to assess MMT for the remotely monitored patients (rather than the standardized assessment of the MMT at an expert myositis center) is another limitation of this study. However sensitivity analysis done involving only the local sub-cohort of the CBC, showed no difference from results seen in the whole cohort, suggesting that the method of ascertainment of MMT might not have had as much of an impact on the accuracy of the results.

The large number of patients with normal muscle strength in this study affects generalizability of these results to weaker patients. Future studies must include weaker patients to accurately ascertain the meaningfulness and accuracy of the STS/TUG in subjects with more active disease. A notable limitation of our study is that use of hands and/or assistive devices was permitted during STS and TUG testing, without strict criteria to determine necessity. While this was intended to accommodate patient safety and feasibility during remote self-assessments, it may have introduced variability or inflated performance metrics in some cases. Future studies should implement more stringent, standardized guidelines to restrict assistive aid use to only those with documented functional need. Moreover, studies should explore the development of adjustment methods or scoring penalties to account for aid usage, which would improve the consistency, validity, and comparability of STS/TUG results across patients and time points.

Another potential limitation of the STS and TUG is that both may be affected by non-muscular pathology such as skeletal or pulmonary disease related to their IIM but also pathology unrelated to IIM such as osteoarthritis6. Our baseline analyses did not show any correlation of skeletal and pulmonary disease activity and STS/TUG, however there was a longitudinal association of STS with skeletal disease activity with worsening skeletal disease activity being associated with a worse STS. Given the lack of baseline association, there is an overall lack of clarity about the effect of these parameters on STS/TUG in this study and this is something that merits further investigation in subsequent studies. Given that we wanted to study STS/TUG as holistic measures of functional status in myositis patients, and not just muscle strength, the contribution of other -non muscular parameters to the performance of these tests might even be desirable to some extent. Furthermore, both tests only assess lower extremity and axial strength/function, while upper extremity strength is not addressed by these tests. This can be overcome by incorporating a ‘1 kg arm lift,’ a test which has been shown to be reliable and valid in two previous, albeit small, IIM studies6,36.

Conclusion

Our study establishes that the STS and TUG tests are reliable, valid, and responsive measures of physical function in patients with myositis, albeit with some limitations. Their simplicity, objectivity, and feasibility for administration make them valuable tools for assessing muscle function, especially in the context of tele-medicine and remote research.
