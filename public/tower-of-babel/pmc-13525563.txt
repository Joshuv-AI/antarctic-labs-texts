Listening across frequencies: Capturing seizures of all patients to advance antiseizure medication development
Authors: Stéphane Auvin, Jacqueline French
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeizure frequency has been the primary endpoint in epilepsy trials, with enrollment usually requiring ≥4 seizures per month. This threshold is more and more misaligned with clinical reality, as the availability of more treatment options has reduced baseline seizure burden, with a risk of excluding a proportion of patients from trials. Although cognitive and developmental outcomes are gaining prominence, seizure control remains essential due to its impact on morbidity and mortality. We have tools to study all types of seizure frequency: Time‐to‐event designs, including time‐to‐pre‐randomization seizure count or time to the Nth seizure, offer a validated alternative by enabling inclusion across a wider range of seizure frequencies while maintaining methodological rigor. Future trial designs should study cognitive outcomes, while studies focusing on anti‐seizure efficacy remain critical.

Change in seizure frequency has long been a cornerstone of clinical trial design in epilepsy, serving both as a primary efficacy endpoint, and consequently a key inclusion criterion. This approach, rooted in methodological convenience and regulatory precedent, has facilitated drug development over decades. Minimum seizure frequency for enrollment is usually set at ≥4 seizures per month. Although this was a reasonable bar in the past, the development of new effective antiseizure medications has reduced the number of subjects who qualify, as demonstrated in both adults and children. In adults, among treatment‐resistant patients, only 42% of focal epilepsy patients met this bar, 1 and in individuals with Dravet syndrome, only 25% had ≥4 countable seizures in a 4‐week period up to age 8 years, based on data from 77 individuals. 2 This suggests a potential discordance between conventional trial eligibility criteria and real‐world seizure frequency, indicating that a substantial proportion of patients, possibly in the range of 50%–75%, may be excluded from randomized controlled trials based on seizure frequency alone. Further data are needed to better refine these estimates.

The commonly used threshold of four seizures per month is primarily driven by the need to ensure sufficient event capture and statistical power. In conventional epilepsy trials, this has allowed designs with prospective baseline periods and fixed blinded maintenance phases typically lasting 12–20 weeks. When baseline seizure frequency is lower, longer observation periods are required to capture a comparable number of events, extending trial duration. This progressive lengthening raises important concerns, including prolonged exposure to placebo, reduced feasibility of recruitment and retention, and substantial increases in trial cost.

As available treatments have expanded and clinical management has improved, fewer patients present with sufficiently high baseline seizure burdens to qualify for traditional trial designs. The increasing availability of approved antiseizure medications makes it more challenging to demonstrate the efficacy of new antiseizure medications within the same population. 3

In response to the challenges of enrolling patients based on seizure frequency, some authors have suggested shifting the focus of clinical trials toward cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes. This is a critical question for patients and families, given the nature of developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, in which developmental impairment is a core feature of the disease, and is further supported by the growing expectation that emerging disease‐modifying therapies, such as antisense oligonucleotides and gene therapies, may positively influence developmental outcomes. However, seizure control and neurodevelopmental improvement should be considered distinct yet complementary objectivses rather than interchangeable ones. The need for effective antiseizure treatments remains, as epilepsy continues to carry significant morbidity and mortality. 4 As long as patients continue to experience generalized tonic–clonic seizures, they remain at risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, underscoring the need to continue developing effective antiseizure medications even when seizure frequency is low.

Alternative methodological approaches for both higher and lower seizure frequency are available to explore the antiseizure effect of a therapeutic intervention without using the “conservative” threshold of four seizures per month.

For patients with low baseline seizure frequencies, time‐to‐event designs represent a particularly relevant and increasingly validated method in epilepsy clinical trials. Rather than relying on fixed‐duration maintenance periods and minimum seizure thresholds, these approaches define individualized endpoints based on the occurrence of a prespecified number of seizures (e.g., time to the nth seizure or time to reach a baseline seizure count), allowing the follow‐up duration to adapt to each patient's intrinsic seizure rate. This facilitates inclusion across a broader spectrum of patients, including those with infrequent events who would otherwise be excluded, while maintaining statistical robustness and potentially reducing prolonged exposure to placebo. However, these designs may also introduce challenges related to variability in seizure occurrence and potential implications for the precision of treatment effect estimates.

Time‐to‐event designs, particularly the time‐to‐prerandomization seizure, provide a strong alternative to conventional fixed‐duration epilepsy trials. Time‐to‐prerandomization seizure count allows each participant to remain in the blinded phase until the postrandomization seizure count reaches that participant's own prerandomization monthly seizure count. This gives the opportunity to have an individualized time exposure to a placebo, but it could also be a method that helps to deal with low seizure frequency. In a reanalysis of 11 double‐blind placebo‐controlled trials across focal and generalized epilepsies, time‐to‐prerandomization seizure count reproduced the primary efficacy conclusions of 10 of the 11 original trials. 5 This design shortened the blinded treatment period by at least 312 participant‐years, avoiding roughly 142 000 seizures during the otherwise continued observation period. In addition to being a tool to evaluate low seizure frequency, this design is a more patient‐centered design for future antiseizure medication trials.

Conversely, in populations with higher seizure frequencies, alternative adaptations of time‐to‐event concepts have also been proposed to address feasibility and ethical concerns. In infants and young children with focal seizures, innovative designs such as “time to nth seizure” combined with variable baseline durations allow broader inclusion, reduce the need for repeated video‐electroencephalography hospitalizations, and minimize exposure to placebo or ineffective treatments. 6 These approaches, developed through international consensus, illustrate how trial methodology can be adapted to the specific constraints of the population while maintaining rigorous assessment of efficacy.

The clinical trial design with a conservative seizure frequency is becoming challenging for some epilepsies, including Dravet syndrome. But this should not lead to abandoning seizure control as an objective. Effective control of seizures remains essential, not only for quality of life benefits, but also because of its direct impact on the mortality risk of the patients. At the same time, there is a clear need to address cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, particularly with the emergence of disease‐modifying therapies. These priorities should not be viewed as competing, but as complementary. Ideally, future clinical trial designs should evolve to better integrate both dimensions (a significant challenge indeed), enabling continued progress in antiseizure medication development while also advancing interventions that meaningfully impact developmental trajectories.
