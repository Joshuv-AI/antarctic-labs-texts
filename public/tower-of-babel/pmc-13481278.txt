Contrast-enhanced ultrasound-guided biopsy and perfusion monitoring for bladder involvement in relapsed multiple myeloma: a case report
Authors: Yanyan Ge, Bin Xu, Zhengmiao Wang, Yixuan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveExtramedullary involvement of the urinary bladder by plasma cell myeloma is extremely rare. This report presents a case to explore the clinical application value of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in the diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring of this condition.MethodsA 54-year-old man with high-risk multiple myeloma (IgAλ type, R-ISS stage II, 1q21 amplification) developed newly-onset urinary frequency during systemic therapy. Grayscale ultrasound, color Doppler ultrasound, and CEUS were performed, followed by CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy. After pathological confirmation, salvage therapy was administered, and follow-up CEUS was performed to evaluate treatment response.ResultsGrayscale ultrasound showed diffuse thickening of the bladder wall (maximum thickness approximately 2.5 cm) with abundant color Doppler flow signals. CEUS observed a perfusion pattern of “rapid arterial uptake, slow washout, and hyperenhancement”: the muscular layer demonstrated early enhancement at 11 seconds with centrifugal (serosal-to-mucosal) filling, and marked contrast agent retention was observed during the venous phase. CEUS-guided biopsy and subsequent immunohistochemistry confirmed monoclonal plasma cell myeloma infiltration of the bladder (strongly positive for CD38, kappa-negative, lambda-positive). Follow-up CEUS after salvage therapy demonstrated a reduction in bladder wall thickness to 0.72 cm with delayed arterial enhancement.ConclusionCEUS-guided transabdominal biopsy targeting the muscular layer may be considered in patients with suspected bladder plasmacytoma and high-risk features for transurethral resection to avoid false negatives from mucosal sampling. Furthermore, CEUS provides a nephrotoxicity-free modality for monitoring treatment response.

Introduction

Extramedullary involvement of the urinary bladder by plasma cell myeloma is extremely rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature (1–3). Its imaging features overlap with those of primary bladder tumors, chronic cystitis, and tuberculosis, posing a diagnostic challenge (4). Conventional imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) mainly provide morphological information and have limited ability to differentiate neoplastic from inflammatory or infectious lesions when perfusion patterns are similar (5). Furthermore, multiple myeloma patients frequently present with renal impairment, limiting the use of iodinated contrast or gadolinium. In contrast, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) enables real-time, dynamic assessment of microvascular perfusion, which can help identify potential enhancement patterns and guide targeted biopsy of the most representative areas (6). To our knowledge, the CEUS perfusion pattern of bladder plasmacytoma and its role in serial treatment monitoring have not been systematically characterized. Herein, we report a case of multiple myeloma with bladder infiltration diagnosed by CEUS-guided biopsy and monitored by follow-up CEUS after salvage therapy.

It is also important to distinguish solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma (SEP), which occurs in patients without prior multiple myeloma, from extramedullary involvement in established systemic multiple myeloma. The present patient had pre-existing high-risk multiple myeloma (IgAλ, R-ISS stage II, 1q21 amplification), therefore, the bladder lesion represented extramedullary relapse rather than SEP. This distinction carries prognostic significance, as extramedullary relapse in multiple myeloma is associated with more aggressive biology and worse outcomes.

Case description

A 54-year-old man (weight 72 kg) initially presented in 2023 with low back pain and was diagnosed with high-risk multiple myeloma (IgAλ type, R-ISS stage II, with 1q21 amplification). He had no family history of hematologic malignancies or hereditary disorders, denied tobacco use, and reported no significant occupational or environmental exposures. There was no prior history of chronic bladder disease or urological procedures.

In August 2025, while on maintenance therapy, he developed new-onset urinary frequency (12 to 15 times per day), including 3 to 4 episodes of nocturia, without dysuria, hematuria, or urinary incontinence. His Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 2 (ambulatory, capable of self-care but unable to carry out any work activities) at relapse. Physical examination revealed mild suprapubic tenderness on deep palpation; bimanual examination was negative for palpable pelvic masses. No palpable abdominal mass, abdominal distension, or costovertebral angle tenderness was noted.

Laboratory findings

Laboratory evaluation at relapse in August 2025 revealed that basic urine cytology and urine culture both had negative results. The CBC revealed decreased Hb of 90 g/L and Hct of 25.4%, thrombocytopenia (platelets 65 × 109/L), lymphopenia (0.6 × 109/L), and neutropenia (1.53 × 109/L). The reticulocyte count was also low (0.023 × 10¹²/L), together with low MCV (82.0 fL). The biological examination also demonstrated abnormal findings, including elevated direct bilirubin (9.77 umol/L), LDH (254 U/L), α-Hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (213 U/L), Urea (20.38 mmol/L), creatinine (380 μmol/L), and Uric acid (641 μmol/L). The estimated GFR was also low, with only 14.8ml/min.

Further serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) revealed increased serum M-protein level of 16.5%, γ globulin of 22.6%, and β2-globulin of 4.2% (reference range 3.2–6.5%). The Immunofixation Electrophoresis showed positive IgA and λ light chain, with IgM, IgG, and κ light chain of negative results. Quantitative Immunoglobulins showed increased globulin A (6.03g/L) and λ light chain (2.64 g/L), while decreased globulin M (<0.210 g/L) and serum κ/λ ratio (0.72).

The urine light chain protein analysis also revealed both increased κ and λ light chain (277.0 and 33.6 mg/L), with a decreased urine κ/λ ratio (0.12). Twenty-four-hour urine total protein quantification was 0.95 g/24h; urine protein electrophoresis was not performed to quantify M-protein specifically. Bone marrow aspiration showed 0.5% mature plasma cells.

Flow cytometry identified 5.17% clonal plasma cells with the immunophenotype CD27+, CD38+, CD56+, CD138+, CD229+, CD269+, cLambda+, CD19−. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed 1q21 amplification as the sole cytogenetic abnormality; no del(17p), t(4;14), t(14;16), or other high-risk alterations were detected.

Imaging and pathological findings

Due to severe renal impairment, pelvic MRI and contrast-enhanced CT were not performed; PET/CT was also declined for financial and radiation concerns. Whole-body bone scintigraphy demonstrated increased uptake in the T10 vertebra, L5 vertebra, sacrum, and bilateral iliac bones, consistent with known myeloma-related bone disease. While bone scintigraphy may underestimate purely lytic lesions, the findings supported the diagnosis of relapsed multiple myeloma and were sufficient for staging. Bone marrow aspiration showed clonal plasma cells ≥10%, confirming active disease and prompting evaluation of his new urinary symptoms.

Grayscale ultrasound revealed diffuse, non-uniform thickening of the anterior bladder wall, measuring up to 2.5 cm at its thickest point (Figure 1A), with abundant, disorganized intramural blood flow on color Doppler imaging (Figure 1B). Abdominal CT (non-contrast) confirmed bladder wall thickening with irregular margins, which could not exclude a mass lesion. The combination of negative urine cytology and culture, non-specific CT findings, and definitive histopathological confirmation via CEUS-guided biopsy effectively excluded common alternative etiologies, including urothelial carcinoma, chronic cystitis, tuberculosis, and lymphoma.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) examination was performed using a VINNO ULTIMUS 9E Super (Suzhou, China) ultrasound system equipped with a C5–1 convex probe (frequency 1–5 MHz). Contrast-specific mode was used with a mechanical index of 0.08, and time-gain compensation was optimized to enhance bladder wall signals.

A fixed dose of 1.6 mL sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (SonoVue, Bracco, Italy) was administered, which corresponded to approximately 0.022 mL/kg in this 72-kg patient, consistent with our institutional protocol for bladder lesions. CEUS was performed according to the institution’s bladder imaging protocol. A fixed dose of 1.6 mL was used independent of body weight, consistent with the institutional protocol for bladder lesions. In the early arterial phase (11 seconds), the muscular layer was enhanced first, demonstrating centrifugal filling from the serosal to the mucosal side, with heterogeneous hyper-enhancement and tortuous tumor vessels (Figure 1C), and the mucosal layer was enhanced seconds later at 14 seconds. During the venous phase (60 seconds), both layers showed marked contrast agent retention (“slow washout”) with patchy, geographic residual enhancement (Figure 1D). Layer differentiation was based on anatomical landmarks (muscular layer = hypoechoic, deep/superficial; mucosal layer = slightly hyperechoic, superficial/deep) and confirmed by two senior sonographers through independent review with inter-observer agreement. However, although the VINNO ULTIMUS 9E system is equipped with offline quantitative analysis software (VINNO Q-lab) capable of generating time-intensity curve (TIC) curves and extracting perfusion parameters (arrival time, time-to-peak, peak intensity, wash-out time), post-hoc TIC reconstruction was not completed.

Given the CEUS finding of predominant muscular-layer infiltration with relative mucosal sparing, transurethral resection biopsy (TURBT) risked sampling only superficial mucosa and yielding false-negative results. Furthermore, the patient presented multiple high-risk factors for TURBT: (i) severe renal impairment (serum creatinine 380 μmol/L, eGFR ~14.8 mL/min), increasing perioperative risk; (ii) thrombocytopenia (platelet count 65 ×109/L), elevating bleeding risk; and (iii) poor performance status (ECOG 2) after multiple prior treatment lines, limiting tolerance to general anesthesia. Following multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion involving ultrasound, urology, and hematology, percutaneous transabdominal CEUS-guided biopsy was selected as the optimal approach.

The procedure was performed with the patient in a supine position after voluntary bladder emptying (residual urine ~30 mL), ensuring the anterior bladder wall and lesion remained in close apposition to the abdominal wall. Under real-time CEUS guidance, the biopsy needle was advanced through a paramedian puncture site approximately 2 cm above the pubic symphysis, aligned with the hypervascular muscular hotspot identified at 11 seconds. The needle was angled 65° to the skin surface, directed toward the anterior bladder wall target; real-time CEUS confirmed needle tip placement within the most intensely enhanced muscular layer area, avoiding intervening bowel loops.

Local anesthesia was achieved with 1% lidocaine (~10 mL) via sequential infiltration: intradermal wheal formation followed by deep tracking along the anticipated needle path to the preperitoneal space. An 18-gauge automatic biopsy gun (Bard Maxcore, USA; needle length 16 cm, throw distance 2.2 cm) was used to obtain three tissue cores. Each core measured approximately 1.5 cm at retrieval (subsequently shrinking to 1.2 cm in formalin fixation), with a total specimen volume of 1.2 × 0.3 × 0.1 cm.

Post-procedure monitoring included 2 hours of bed rest with a compression dressing at the puncture site, followed by an additional 2 hours of observation in the ultrasound suite (total 4 hours). Vital signs, gross hematuria, abdominal pain, and peritoneal irritation signs were monitored. No complications occurred: no gross hematuria within 4 hours, normal voiding, and no fever or worsening abdominal pain. Telephone follow-up the next day confirmed absence of delayed bleeding or infection. No urinary extravasation was observed.

Pathological examination revealed: gross specimens consisted of three gray-white tissue cores, with a total volume of 1.2 × 0.3 × 0.1 cm, entirely embedded. Microscopic examination showed diffuse infiltration of the bladder wall by atypical plasma cells with eccentric nuclei, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and occasional prominent nucleoli. Immunohistochemistry results: CK (AE1/AE3)(-), CD38(+) (Figure 2A), CD138(+), lambda light chain restriction (positive) (Figure 2B), and kappa light chain negative (Figure 2C). The proliferation index assessed by Ki-67 staining was approximately 70% (Figure 2D). Therapeutic interventions

The patient was diagnosed with multiple myeloma (IgAλ, DS stage IIIA, R-ISS stage II, 1q21 amplification) in November 2023. He initially received one cycle of VCD (bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, dexamethasone) from November 17, 2023, which relieved his low back pain. From December 8, 2023, the regimen was switched to VRD (bortezomib, lenalidomide, dexamethasone). After the fourth cycle of VRD (completed in March 2024), the patient achieved a very good partial response (VGPR). He subsequently received intermittent cycles of VRD, RD (lenalidomide, dexamethasone), and VR (bortezomib, lenalidomide) combined with bone-protective therapy (denosumab). From December 2024, he was maintained on lenalidomide monotherapy.

Bone marrow aspiration showed 0.5% mature plasma cells. Imaging confirmed bladder wall thickening. Following CEUS-guided biopsy confirmation of bladder extramedullary plasmacytoma, salvage therapy with KPCD (carfilzomib, pomalidomide, cyclophosphamide, dexamethasone) was initiated on September 13, 2025. Bilateral nephrostomy tubes were placed for obstructive uropathy. The KPCD regimen was administered as follows: carfilzomib 34 mg/m² intravenously on days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15, 16; pomalidomide 4 mg orally on days 1-21; cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m² intravenously on days 1, 8, and 15. The KPCD cycle lasted 28 days. Treatment intensification was based on progressive extramedullary disease (bladder involvement), aggressive pathology (Ki-67 70%, 1q21+), renal impairment, and the patient’s refusal of daratumumab due to financial constraints.

Follow-up and outcomes

After one cycle of KPCD, urinary frequency resolved, and spontaneous urination returned, the serum M-protein by SPEP also converted to negative by both SPEP (0% M-protein fraction) and immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE, negative for IgA, IgG, IgM, κ, and λ bands). Serum free light chain κ/λ ratio normalized to 0.7175. Hemoglobin recovered to 107 g/L, platelet count normalized to 181 ×109/L, and serum creatinine decreased to 63 μmol/L. Urine free light chain excretion remained mildly elevated (κ 7.75 mg/L, λ 10.80 mg/L), likely reflecting persistent tubular proteinuria from prior renal injury rather than residual tumor secretion. According to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) 2016 criteria, the patient achieved a very good partial response (VGPR) or better. Formal complete response (CR) could not be assigned because mild bladder wall thickening persisted (0.72 cm), and dedicated imaging response criteria for extramedullary plasmacytoma are not established. Follow-up imaging included: (i) CEUS at 1 month, showing bladder wall thickness reduction from 2.5 cm to 0.72 cm with delayed arterial enhancement (13 vs. 11 seconds), indicating anemia diminished tumor perfusion (Figure 3); and (ii) conventional grayscale ultrasound at 1.5 months (wall thickness 0.67 cm) and 2 months (wall thickness 0.45 cm), demonstrating progressive anatomical improvement.

Follow-up imaging one month after salvage therapy. (A) A grayscale ultrasound image shows a marked reduction in bladder wall thickness to 0.72 cm (arrow). (B) CEUS in the arterial phase at 13 seconds demonstrates delayed enhancement compared with pretreatment (11 seconds), indicating diminished tumor perfusion.

The patient completed the first cycle without dose interruption; only grade 1 fatigue and nausea occurred, with no cardiotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, or significant hematologic toxicity (e.g., neutropenia). At the 7-month follow-up, the patient maintains sustained symptom relief, and continuous monitoring is ongoing.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that the CEUS examination was well tolerated and appreciated the absence of radiation and the lack of need for iodinated contrast, given his concerns about kidney function. He stated, “The contrast-enhanced ultrasound was quick and painless. I was relieved that it didn’t affect my kidneys.” After one cycle of KPCD, he noted a rapid improvement in his urinary symptoms: “Within two weeks, I could sleep through most of the night without waking up to urinate. I feel much better and can go out during the day without constantly looking for a bathroom.” The patient expressed satisfaction with the follow-up ultrasound monitoring, as it allowed him to avoid repeated invasive procedures.

Discussion

Bladder extramedullary plasmacytoma is an exceptionally rare manifestation of plasma cell myeloma, with only scattered case reports in the literature (1–3). This case provides three key insights into the diagnostic and therapeutic monitoring value of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS).

First, the CEUS perfusion pattern may serve as a notable diagnostic clue. Han et al. had proven that CEUS showed good performance for the assessment of muscle invasion in bladder cancer (7). Unlike typical urothelial carcinoma, which often presents with early rapid hyper-enhancement followed by rapid washout, or chronic cystitis showing diffuse inflammatory perfusion, our CEUS findings added a real-time functional dimension. It revealed an atypical muscular-layer-first enhancement with marked venous retention. As López et al. emphasized, distinguishing bladder plasmacytoma from anaplastic transitional cell carcinoma on routine histology is challenging (2); thus, identifying this perfusion pattern can effectively guide the clinician toward the correct diagnosis and targeted biopsy, significantly differentiating it from common bladder lesions such as urothelial carcinoma, chronic cystitis, and tuberculosis (4). A recent review by Kufel et al. further corroborates the high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of CEUS for bladder neoplasms, reinforcing its emerging role as a versatile adjunct to conventional imaging. This case underscores the broader imperative to integrate advanced functional imaging modalities like CEUS with image-guided targeted biopsy into the diagnostic workup of rare extramedullary myeloma sites, where anatomical overlap with primary malignancies and inflammatory conditions frequently confounds standard morphological assessment and necessitates precision sampling strategies beyond conventional endoscopic approaches (8).

Second, biopsy targeting the muscular layer based on CEUS results is important (9). In the case series by Gupta and Epstein, plasma cell neoplasms of the bladder often showed intact mucosa with infiltration confined to the lamina propria or muscularis propria (10). Transurethral resection may therefore yield false-negative results if only superficial tissue is sampled. CEUS identifies hypervascular hotspots during the early arterial phase, directing the needle to viable, diagnostically productive tissue and reducing false-negative results from blind or mucosal sampling. The high Ki-67 proliferation index (70%) in our case correlates with aggressive biological behavior, consistent with the poor prognosis associated with extramedullary disease (11, 12).

Third, CEUS offers functional monitoring beyond grayscale imaging. In the present case, follow-up CEUS demonstrated delayed arterial onset (13 vs. 11 s) and reduced enhancement intensity, indicating diminished tumor perfusion before complete anatomical normalization. These functional biomarkers may suggest treatment efficacy. In the series by Gupta and Epstein (10), none of the nine patients with bladder plasma cell neoplasms progressed to multiple myeloma during follow-up (1–127 months), suggesting a relatively favorable prognosis for isolated lesions. However, it must be emphasized that our patient presented with extramedullary relapse of pre-existing systemic multiple myeloma, distinct from SEP. Extramedullary relapse in this context typically could be associated with aggressive disease biology and worse prognosis than isolated bladder plasmacytoma.

Fourth, CEUS offers specific advantages in multiple myeloma patients with renal impairment. Multiple myeloma frequently causes renal impairment (13). Conventional CT requires iodinated contrast media, which carry a risk of contrast-induced nephropathy. CEUS microbubbles are excreted via the respiratory tract and do not undergo renal metabolism, making it the optimal imaging modality for high-risk myeloma patients with obstructive uropathy. In the present case, the patient exhibited elevated serum creatinine (380 μmol/L) at relapse, and the application of CEUS alleviated the patient’s concerns regarding kidney injury and improved adherence.

Finally, while we acknowledge that this recommendation is based on a single case, the clinical rationale for preferring CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy over TURBT in this specific scenario is robust: (i) CEUS precisely identified the muscular layer as the diagnostic target, which TURBT could not reliably access; (ii) the patient’s severe renal impairment (eGFR ~14.8 mL/min), thrombocytopenia (65 ×109/L), and poor performance status (ECOG 2) constituted contraindications to general anesthesia and endoscopic surgery; and (iii) MDT consensus supported the percutaneous approach as safer and diagnostically superior in this high-risk setting. We do not advocate universal replacement of TURBT with CEUS-guided biopsy; rather, we propose that CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy may be considered in selected patients with high-risk features and CEUS-identified deep-layer infiltration.

To our knowledge, approximately 30 bladder plasmacytoma cases have been reported in previous studies (4, 11, 14). Among these cases, most were diagnosed by transurethral resection or cystectomy. Our case adds several novel contributions: (i) to our knowledge, this is among the first descriptions of the CEUS perfusion pattern of bladder plasmacytoma with muscular layer priority; (ii) a clear rationale for CEUS-guided biopsy over transurethral cystoscopy; (iii) serial imaging follow-up combining CEUS at1 month with conventional ultrasound at 1.5 and 2 months, providing both functional and anatomical response assessment; and (iv) integration of serological and functional renal parameters. These features provide a more comprehensive framework for diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring than previously available.

However, this case report still had several limitations: First, it is based on a single patient, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, while CEUS demonstrated a observable perfusion pattern in this case, further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these imaging features across diverse populations and disease stages. Third, although CEUS-guided biopsy provided definitive histopathological confirmation, the technique’s reproducibility and accuracy may vary depending on operator experience and equipment availability. Fourth, the absence of quantitative TIC parameters limits the objectivity of perfusion assessment; future studies should incorporate offline quantitative CEUS analysis to validate the semi-quantitative observations reported here. Fifth, whole-body bone scintigraphy was used instead of the more sensitive whole-body MRI or PET/CT for skeletal assessment, which may have underestimated the extent of lytic bone disease. Finally, the absence of comparative imaging modalities such as contrast-enhanced MRI or CT in this case precludes direct assessment of CEUS performance relative to other advanced imaging techniques; however, these omissions were clinically justified by severe renal impairment and patient-specific factors.

Conclusion (take-away lesson)

For multiple myeloma patients presenting with new-onset urinary symptoms and suspected bladder extramedullary involvement, CEUS could be performed for the early identification of muscular layer invasion. CEUS-guided biopsy targeting the hypervascular muscular hotspot is a potential sampling approach, and repeat CEUS provides a non-invasive, nephrotoxicity-free method for follow-up treatment response evaluation.
