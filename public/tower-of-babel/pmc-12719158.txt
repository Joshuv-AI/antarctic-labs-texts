Bacteria-inoculated biochar boosts copper immobilization and carbon sequestration via microbial restructuring and pH regulation
Authors: Leizhen Rao, Lingya Kong, Lina Wang, Lu Yang, Ying Liu, Shaopo Deng, Jing Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntensive agriculture aggravates soil acidification and heavy metal contamination. Biochar and its composites are cost-effective, carbon-rich amendments that can influence both heavy metal and soil carbon cycling, yet their synergistic mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, a combination of the diffusive gradient in thin films (DGT) technique, Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS), and high-throughput sequencing was applied to investigate the effects of various biochar-based amendments: plain biochar, bacteria-inoculated biochar, and biochar combined with oyster shell powder on copper (Cu) stabilization and carbon dynamics in acidic orchard soils. All amendments significantly raised soil pH (by 1.03–2.54 ​units) and organic matter content, while reducing bioavailable Cu (DGT-Cu) by 60%–73%. Bacteria-inoculated biochar exhibited superior performance, lowering microbial metabolic quotient (qCO2) by 44%, enriching functional microbial taxa (e.g., Bacillus spp., by 7-fold), and enhancing soil enzyme activity. These shifts coincided with an 8% decrease in recalcitrant compounds (e.g., lignin, tannin) and a 6% increase in labile protein/lipid fractions of dissolved organic matter (DOM), indicating accelerated microbial turnover of stable carbon pools. Statistical analyses indicated that Cu bioavailability was primarily controlled by soil properties (e.g., pH), while qCO2 was mainly driven by microbial traits like community structure and microbial biomass carbon. Notably, pH also indirectly shapes microbial processes. This study reveals a mechanistic linkage between Cu immobilization and carbon sequestration, highlighting the dual regulatory role of soil properties and microbial processes. These findings provide new insights into the ecological restoration and sustainable management of contaminated agricultural soils.Keywords: Bacteria-inoculated biochar, Cu immobilization, Carbon sequestration, DGT, FT-ICR MS

Introduction

Heavy metal contamination poses a serious threat to soil health, ecosystems, and human well-being worldwide [1]. In agricultural systems, the intensive use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides under modernized practices has accelerated the enrichment of heavy metals in soils, often accompanied by progressive acidification [2]. For example, Bordeaux mixture, a Cu-rich broad-spectrum fungicide widely applied in orchard management, can substantially elevate soil Cu concentrations over long-term use, increasing them from natural background levels to several tens or even hundreds of mg/kg. In coastal orchards of China, decades of Cu-containing fungicides application have raised soil Cu concentrations to 85.77 ​mg/kg, far above the local background level of 24.0 ​mg/kg [3]. In extreme cases, Cu concentrations in vineyard surface soils have been reported to reach 3200 ​mg/kg in certain regions of Brazil [4]. In acidic soils, such Cu becomes highly bioavailable, increasing phytotoxicity, reducing yields, and disrupting microbial communities essential for nutrient cycling and soil fertility [5]. Beyond these direct impacts, heavy metals impair soil carbon sequestration, a critical process in climate change mitigation [6]. In a subtropical paddy field, heavy metal pollution reduced soil organic carbon by up to 12% in the rice season and 11% in the wheat season, primarily due to suppressed plant growth and microbial activity [[6], [7], [8]]. Therefore, mitigating heavy metal contamination in agricultural soil, particularly in orchards, is critical for sustaining soil productivity and achieving climate goals.

Among available remediation approaches, immobilization is especially appealing because it stabilizes metals without disrupting agricultural use. Soil amendments such as biochar, lime, and oyster shell powder can effectively reduce metal bioavailability. Biochar, a carbon-rich material produced by pyrolyzing biomass, has received particular attention due to its high pH, porous structure, and adsorption capacity [9,10]. It immobilizes metals via complexation, cation exchange, and electrostatic interactions while simultaneously improving soil pH and organic content [11]. For example, biochar has been shown to reduce the chemically extractable (a proxy for bioavailability) fractions of Cd, Zn, Pb, and Cu in contaminated soils by approximately 88%, 62%, 85%, and 42%, respectively [12,13]. Using the diffusive gradients in thin films (DGT) technique, Fang et al. reported that biochar application decreased the accumulation and bioavailability of Cd, Cu, Ni, and Pb in the rice rhizosphere [14]. Modified biochars, such as those enhanced with phosphorus, have also been shown to lower Cd availability and uptake in tomato fruits [15]. Furthermore, pairing biochar with metal-tolerant microbes (e.g., Pseudomonas or Bacillus) can improve remediation efficiency through adsorption and precipitation, while enhancing soil enzyme activity, stimulating phytohormone production, and promoting plant growth [13,16]. For instance, a Bacillus-loaded biochar developed by Huang et al. reduced exchangeable Cd in co-contaminated soil by 50.96% and improved both the growth and quality of water spinach, thereby reducing the transfer of Cd into edible plant tissues [17].

Beyond stabilizing heavy metals, biochar can also enhance soil carbon sequestration owing to its stable, decomposition-resistant structure. By storing carbon and enriching soil organic matter, biochar supports soil fertility, structure, and long-term carbon retention. Xia et al. reported that integrating biomass pyrolysis with electricity generation and biochar application could reduce greenhouse gas emissions from China's staple crop production from 666.5 to −37.9 ​Tg CO2-eq/yr, largely due to increased soil carbon sequestration, fossil fuel displacement, and reductions in CH4 and N2O emissions [18]. Synergistic effects of biochar on both metal immobilization and carbon fixation are increasingly recognized. For instance, clay mineral-modified biochar inhibited the migration and transformation of Pb, Cd, and Cr while reducing soil carbon emissions [19]. Biochar can also modify the composition and properties of dissolved organic matter (DOM), including its aromaticity and humic- or protein-like fractions, thereby influencing microbial community and activity, which in turn modulates soil biogeochemical processes [20]. Recently, Sun et al. demonstrated that functional microbes supported by biochar's porous structure further enhance carbon stabilization [21]. This dual capacity for heavy metal immobilization and carbon retention positions biochar as a promising tool for soil protection and climate change mitigation.

However, significant gaps remain in understanding how biochar influences microbial communities and how such shifts mediate the molecular transformation of DOM, ultimately affecting long-term carbon dynamics under heavy metal stress. Microorganisms are pivotal in driving detoxification, soil structure formation, and organic matter decomposition, and thus regulate carbon sequestration through DOM turnover [22,23]. Yet, most studies still rely on bulk indicators, such as microbial biomass, enzyme activity, or total organic carbon, without resolving the molecular pathways by which microbial-driven DOM dynamics contribute to carbon stabilization [24]. Recent advances in ultrahigh-resolution Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) now allow detailed characterization of DOM molecular composition and transformation pathways, offering new opportunities to elucidate its role in ecosystem processes [25,26]. For instance, Si et al. used FT-ICR MS to show that biochar amendments alleviated the negative impacts of N addition by promoting the formation of N-rich, labile DOM and stimulating N cycling [27]. These findings highlight the potential of FT-ICR MS to reveal mechanisms of amendment-induced processes that underpin both remediation and carbon management.

In this study, we investigate the efficacy of biochar-based amendments, including biochar, bacteria-inoculated biochar, oyster shell powder, and their composites in immobilizing Cu in acidic orchard soils, while simultaneously assessing their impact on carbon dynamics. We hypothesize that biochar-based amendments, particularly bacteria-inoculated biochar, achieve a synergistic effect on Cu immobilization and carbon sequestration by modifying soil properties and microbial processes. By integrating DGT for labile Cu quantification, 16S rRNA sequencing for microbial profiling, and FT-ICR MS for soil DOM characterization, this work aims to provide novel strategies for the remediation of contaminated soils and enhanced carbon management.

Materials and methods

Soil samples and experimental design

Agricultural soil was collected from an orchard on the Shandong Peninsula where Bordeaux mixture had been applied for 20 years, with samples obtained from the 0–20 ​cm surface layer [28]. The soil contained 281.4 ​mg/kg of Cu and exhibited the following baseline physicochemical properties: pH (H2O) 4.65, SOM 19.1 ​g/kg, CEC 12.8 ​cmol (+)/kg, total nitrogen 1.90 ​g/kg, TP 1.65 ​g/kg, TK 17.95 ​g/kg, bicarbonate content 0.05 ​g/kg, and sand, silt, and clay contents of 67.3%, 22.9%, and 9.8%, respectively. Soil samples were air-dried at room temperature, with large stones and plant residues manually removed. The soil was then ground and sieved through a 10-mesh nylon sieve, and stored in self-sealing bags for analysis of soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC), particle size distribution, and heavy metal concentrations in diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (DTPA)- and DGT-extractable fractions.

Biochar and bacteria-inoculated biochar were prepared following Tu et al. [13]. The bacterial strain used for inoculation was identified as Pseudomonas frederiksbergensis, a Cu-tolerant species isolated from historically contaminated orchard soil. Details about isolation and characterization procedures and the oyster shell material can be found in the supplementary information. Pot experiments were conducted with 1.25 ​kg of soil per pot under five treatments: biochar at 20 ​g/kg (B), bacteria-inoculated biochar at 20 ​g/kg (BN), a combination of oyster shell powder (10 ​g/kg) and biochar (10 ​g/kg) (BO), oyster shell powder at 20 ​g/kg (OS), and a control group (CK). After mixing the amendments with the soil, the soil moisture content was adjusted to 40% of field capacity. The pots were placed in an incubation chamber with constant temperature and humidity, with periodic water additions to maintain soil moisture. Recognizing that most stabilization experiments to date have been of relatively short duration (i.e., several days to months), we designed a one-year incubation experiment to systematically assess the long-term stabilization performance of biochar-based materials and corresponding controls. Samples collected after the one-year incubation were analyzed for soil chemical properties and microbial community structure, with three replicates for each treatment and control group.

Analysis of the soil properties, respiration, microbial biomass, and enzyme activity

For consistency, all soil samples were subjected to the same preparation and analytical procedures. Soil respiration was measured following the method of Li et al. [29]. MBC was quantified via chloroform fumigation extraction with a potassium sulfate solution, following Vance et al. [30], with a 15 ​g air-dried soil sample. The qCO2 was calculated as the ratio of basal respiration carbon to MBC [31]. Soil enzyme activities were assessed using commercial kits, including N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminidase (S-NAG) and β-glucosidase (S-β-GC). One unit of enzyme activity (U/g soil) was defined as the amount of enzyme that produced 1 ​μmol of p-nitrophenol per day per gram of soil [13].

Analysis and determination of labile Cu content in soil samples

To comprehensively assess Cu bioavailability in soil, this study employed both the DTPA and DGT methods for Cu quantification. DTPA extraction was selected owing to its rapidity and broad applicability across diverse soil types and conditions. The procedure outlined by Tu et al. was followed for the DTPA method [13].

DGT, a passive soil sampling technique, emulates plant root uptake processes and provides time-integrated measurements of bioavailable metal concentrations [32]. This approach is also suitable for evaluating changes in heavy metal bioavailability induced by the incorporation of stabilization amendments, including biochar, into soils [14]. For the DGT method, a standard circular DGT device (Vision ES&T Ltd., China) consists of a plastic base, a Chelex-100 resin binding gel layer, a diffusive hydrogel layer, a 0.14-mm-thick hydrophilic polyethersulfone membrane (0.45 ​μm pore size), and a plastic cap with a circular exposure window of 2.54 ​cm2. When deployed in soil, labile metal cations diffuse through the diffusive layer. They are irreversibly bound to the resin, establishing a concentration gradient that drives further diffusion from the soil solution and solid phase. This process provides an integrated measure of metal bioavailability. Detailed procedures for DGT device fabrication and deployment have been described previously [33]. For the detailed procedure, please refer to the supplementary information.

DNA extraction and Illumina MiSeq sequencing analysis

Soil DNA was extracted using the FastDNA SPIN Kit (MPbio, USA) per the manufacturer's instructions. Bacterial 16S rRNA was sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) for high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. The sequence reads were deposited in the NCBI SRA database under the BioProject ID: PRJNA1302097. α-, β-diversity analyses (e.g., Shannon, Chao1, Simpson, and PCoA) were performed using the free online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com).

DOM components and molecular composition analysis

Briefly, the extraction of DOM was conducted using soil samples that had been passed through a 10-mesh sieve. The extraction process was performed at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:5 in ultrapure water [35]. For the detailed procedure, please refer to the supplementary information. DOC was measured by a Shimadzu TOC-VCPH TOC analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan), and the filtered supernatant was used for optical property analysis via UV-Vis spectrophotometry (GENESYS 10 ​S, Thermo Scientific) and 3D-EEM fluorescence spectroscopy (F-7000, Hitachi). Parallel factor analysis (PARAFAC) was applied to 3D-EEM data to identify fluorescent components of DOM with different sources and chemical properties. The aromatics index (SUVA254), fluorescence index (FI), and humification index (HIX) of DOM were also assessed [35]. We consider the molecular diversity and component content of DOM as indicators of its chemodiversity.

The molecular composition of soil DOM extracts was analyzed using FT-ICR MS (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA), following the methods of Zhou et al. [36]. Molecules were classified into eight categories: lignin-like (LG, O/C ​= ​0.1–0.67, H/C ​= ​0.7–1.5), proteins-like (PT, O/C ​= ​0.3–0.55, H/C ​= ​1.5–2.2), lipids-like (LP, O/C ​= ​0–0.3, H/C ​= ​1.5–2.0), condensed aromatics-like (CA, O/C ​= ​0–0.6, H/C ​= ​0.2–0.7), tannin-like (TN, O/C ​= ​0.67–1.2, H/C ​= ​0.5–1.5), unsaturated hydrocarbons-like (UH, O/C ​= ​0–0.1, H/C ​= ​0.7–1.5), amino sugars-like (AS, O/C ​= ​0.55–0.67, H/C ​= ​1.5–2.2), and carbohydrates-like (CH, O/C ​= ​0.67–1.2, H/C ​= ​1.5–2.0) [37].

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (v4.2.2). Dunnett's test was used to compare the four treatments with the control group, while two-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate the interaction effects of soil amendments, both with a significance level of α ​= ​0.05. SourceTracker2 was employed to analyze the shared taxa proportions among soil microbial communities, comparing each as a source against the others as sinks. The “edgeR” package was used to calculate OTU abundance differences, and the “ggplot2” package for visualization. DOM molecular mass was recorded to three decimal places, and common DOM molecules across all samples were identified and correlated with dominant bacteria using Spearman's coefficient. Multiple linear regression and variance partitioning were used to assess the key drivers of qCO2 and DGT-Cu. The distance-based linear model (DISTLM) analysis was performed to examine the relationships between microbial community structure and soil properties. Heatmaps were generated with the “pheatmap” package, and simple figures were created using Origin 2025. All data were presented as mean ​± ​standard error.

Results and discussion

Effects of soil amendments on soil physicochemical properties, DGT-Cu concentration, and qCO2

The application of soil amendments led to marked alterations in soil physicochemical properties (Table 1, Table S1). All treatments increased soil pH, SOM, and DOC contents, while slightly decreasing CEC. Among them, the OS and BO treatments exhibited the greatest pH enhancement, increasing by 55% and 47%, respectively, compared to CK. This highlights the strong alkalinity of oyster shell powder in neutralizing soil acidity. In contrast, the B and BN treatments caused more moderate pH increases, less than half that of the OS and BO treatments. Concerning SOM, all biochar-containing treatments (B, BN, BO) significantly elevated SOM content by 68%, 62%, and 35%, respectively, underscoring biochar's major contribution to organic matter input. In comparison, the OS treatment slightly reduced SOM, likely due to its inorganic nature. Notably, the BO treatment resulted in the highest DOC content among all treatments, suggesting that combining biochar with oyster shell powder may enhance the release of soluble organic carbon. These results indicate that different amendments exert distinct effects on soil properties: biochar primarily improves SOM, oyster shell raises pH, and their combination enhances DOC accumulation.

DGT measurements further revealed significant differences in Cu bioavailability across treatments (Fig. 1a). The BO group achieved the greatest reduction in DGT-Cu (39.83 ​μg/L), representing less than one-third of the concentration in the control. The OS group performed similarly (40.84 ​μg/L), with no statistical difference from BO, indicating that oyster shell powder plays a dominant role in Cu immobilization. The B and BN treatments also reduced Cu bioavailability (to 57.37 ​μg/L and 58.80 ​μg/L, respectively), though to a lesser extent than in the BO and OS groups. Two-way ANOVA (based on CK, B, OS, and BO treatments) confirmed a significant interaction between biochar and oyster shell (p ​< ​0.001), suggesting that their combination reduces Cu bioavailability through both physical adsorption and chemical precipitation. These findings align with previous studies, which have shown enhanced heavy metal immobilization compared to individual amendments through combinations of organic and inorganic amendments [38].

DGT-Cu concentration (a) and qCO2 (b) from unamended and amended soil samples. Asterisk (∗∗∗) indicates significant differences compared to unamended samples at p ​< ​0.001.

In contrast to their substantial effects on Cu bioavailability, the amendments showed more variable impacts on soil microbial respiration, as indicated by the qCO2 values (Fig. 1b). Only the BN group significantly reduced qCO2, from 3.18 to 1.77 ​mg CO2–C/(mg MBC·day), suggesting improved soil microbial carbon efficiency, potentially due to mitigated Cu toxicity and enhanced microbial function [39,40]. The B, BO, and OS treatments did not significantly affect qCO2 compared to the control. Two-way ANOVA (based on CK, B, OS, and BO treatments) revealed no significant interaction between biochar and oyster shell on qCO2 (p ​= ​0.66), and the effect of oyster shell alone was minimal. The greater reduction in qCO2 observed in the BN treatment, compared to B, suggests that microbial inoculation enhanced carbon use efficiency by reshaping microbial communities. Luo et al. similarly reported that modified biochar reshaped the soil microbial community by increasing the relative abundance of heavy metal-tolerant and metabolically active taxa, enhancing key extracellular enzyme activities, and improving functional diversity, thereby reducing qCO2 under heavy metal stress [41].

Overall, each amendment showed distinct strengths. The BO group was most effective for Cu immobilization, benefiting from the combined action of biochar and oyster shell. The BN group improved microbial metabolic efficiency and moderately reduced Cu availability, supporting dual goals of remediation and carbon management. Therefore, both bacteria-inoculated biochar (BN) and biochar-oyster shell composites (BO) provide practical value for contaminated soil remediation. The selection between these amendments can be optimized based on the specific remediation objectives (e.g., heavy metal immobilization or enhancement of microbial activity).

Effects of soil amendments on the characteristics and molecular diversity of soil DOM

DOM, a key component of soil organic matter, plays a crucial role in microbial metabolism and carbon sequestration within the soil matrix [42]. In this study, we employed 3D-EEM (Fig. 2, Fig. S1) and FT-ICR MS to assess how different amendments influence the composition and molecular diversity of soil DOM. These changes are ecologically significant, as aromatic, stable DOM fractions contribute to long-term carbon storage, while labile fractions fuel microbial activity and nutrient cycling [43,44]. Understanding these molecular shifts provides critical insights into how amendment strategies affect both soil remediation and sustainability.

Fluorescence spectroscopy results (Table S1) showed clear differences among treatments. The SUVA254 value, an indicator of DOM aromaticity, increased by 38% in OS treatment compared to the control, suggesting substantial enrichment of aromatic compounds. In contrast, the increased SUVA254 for the biochar treatments (B and BN) was roughly half of that in OS, while the BO treatment showed minimal change. This indicates that oyster shell powder preserves more aromatic organic matter. Similarly, the spectral ratio (SR), which inversely correlates with molecular size and complexity, decreased across all treatments, implying an overall increase in DOM molecular weight and complexity. The fluorescence index (FI) and humification index (HIX) both declined after amendment application. Biochar-based treatments (B and BN) led to more pronounced reductions (6% and 19%, respectively) than the OS and BO treatments (2% and 15%), suggesting a potential shift in DOM sources and a decrease in humic content.

PARAFAC analysis resolved three fluorescent components: C1 (Ex/Em ​= ​235, 310/420 ​nm) represented fulvic acid-like substances [45,46]. C2 (Ex/Em ​= ​265, 390/500 ​nm) corresponded to terrestrial humic-like substances [47,48]. Both C1 and C2 possess higher molecular weights and greater aromaticity, rendering them less susceptible to microbial degradation. C3 (Ex/Em ​= ​255, 360/440 ​nm) reflected UVA-humic-like materials, likely derived from microbial metabolism [49,50]. In comparison to C1 and C2, C3 has a smaller molecular weight and is likely derived from microbial decomposition of plant material and microbial metabolites [51,52]. Humic acids (C2 and C3) dominated DOM composition (59.3%–64.1%), with C1 being less prevalent. Biochar application increased C2 content (terrestrial humic acid), while oyster shell favored C3 (UVA-humic acid) (Fig. 2d). Zhang et al. reported that biochar application significantly increased the humic acid components of DOM in agricultural soils, as also observed in the B and BN treatment groups in the present study [53]. Interestingly, the BN group showed a higher proportion of C3 than biochar alone, suggesting that microbial inoculation promoted the conversion of plant-derived DOM into microbially derived humic-like substances. This enhanced microbial processing is supported by the elevated microbial respiration (MR) and microbial biomass carbon (MBC) observed in the BN treatment, indicating greater microbial metabolic activity and biomass accumulation (Fig. S5a–b).

FT-ICR MS analysis further revealed significant enhancement in DOM chemodiversity across treatments (ADONIS: R2 ​= ​1, p ​= ​0.001; Fig. S2). Van Krevelen diagrams showed that lignin- and tannin-like compounds dominated DOM profiles (72%–81%) (Fig. 3a). The application of biochar (B group), oyster shell (OS group), and their composite treatment (BO group) increased the relative abundance of lignin-like components and reduced labile components (proteins/lipids), indicating increased DOM stability. However, the BN group produced a distinct pattern: recalcitrant components (lignin, tannins, and condensed aromatic compounds) decreased by 7%, 17%, and 4%, respectively, while labile components (proteins/lipids, amino sugars, and unsaturated hydrocarbons) increased by 63%, 23%, and 55% (Fig. 3b–c). This shift suggests that the BN group enhanced the degradation of stable DOM and the formation of bioavailable, energy-rich compounds. Molecular-level analysis (Fig. 3d) confirmed that high O/C, low H/C compounds (e.g., lignin- and tannin-like) declined, while low O/C, high H/C compounds (e.g., protein/lipids and amino sugars-like) increased, further indicating enhanced biological activity. These results align with Ling et al. who found that biochar additions increased labile DOM fractions by promoting microbial activity [54]. This mechanism may also apply to the BN group, where bacteria-inoculated biochar appeared to modulate DOM composition through the specific responsive microbial taxa. This is because microbial inoculants do not substantially alter overall microbial diversity but may modulate the native microbial community by selectively inhibiting or promoting the growth of specific indigenous taxa [55].

The observed DOM shifts may result from three primary mechanisms: 1) Adsorption and release: Biochar's porous structure adsorbs small molecules and gradually releases larger humic substances, enhancing humification and aromaticity [56]. 2) Microbial regulation: Microbes reshaped via bacteria-inoculated biochar (BN group) enhance the decomposition of recalcitrant components and promote the formation of labile components, thereby significantly improving the biological activity of DOM. 3) Physicochemical changes: Oyster shell powder modifies the soil's pH and CEC, thus affecting the solubility and distribution of DOM components [57]. These findings suggest that both biochar and oyster shell treatments enhance the humification and stability of DOM, potentially influencing soil carbon sequestration and the mobility of heavy metals by increasing the proportion of aromatic and recalcitrant components. In summary, biochar and oyster shell primarily enhance DOM stability through humification and aromatic enrichment, whereas bacteria-inoculated biochar enhances DOM bioactivity and turnover. Overall, the mechanisms associated with different amendments are distinct, and their effects may further impact the long-term stability and functionality of soil ecosystems.

Response of soil bacterial community composition to amendment application

Soil amendments significantly reshaped the composition of the microbial community. Compared to CK, the OS and BO treatments induced substantial changes in 2968 and 2827 OTUs, respectively, whereas the B and BN treatments altered 2193 and 1834 OTUs (Fig. S3). These results suggest that oyster shell powder alone and its combination with biochar had a more pronounced effect on bacterial communities, likely due to the strong pH-buffering effect of oyster shell and the high porosity and surface reactivity of biochar [58]. Alpha-diversity analysis showed significant increases in the Chao1 index across all treatments, reflecting enhanced microbial richness. Notably, the B and OS treatments exhibited higher Chao1 values than BN and BO, implying that the composite treatments had limited additive effects on richness. Greater microbial richness (higher Chao1) may contribute to soil carbon turnover by broadening the functional potential for organic matter decomposition. Hu et al. demonstrated that increasing the Chao1 index from 600 to 1178 markedly enhanced microbial utilization of rice-straw saccharification residues and reduced CO2 emissions [59]. However, richness alone may not necessarily enhance metal immobilization, which tends to depend more on the abundance of specific functional taxa and their associated genes [60]. This likely explains the absence of a consistent correlation between Chao1 values and Cu stabilization across treatments. Furthermore, only B and OS treatments significantly improved the Shannon index, while BN and BO treatments showed no notable change (Fig. 4a), possibly due to microbial competition in the BN group or the complex chemical environment in the BO group. Xu et al. similarly observed that biochar application selectively altered bacterial community structure [61]. Specifically, it significantly increased richness (Chao1 index: +7.9%; OTU richness: +4.1%) without affecting the Shannon diversity index, which implies a predominant influence on community richness over evenness.

PCoA revealed distinct clustering of microbial communities among treatments (Fig. 4b), with the BO group aligning more closely with OS and the B group with BN (SourceTracker2, Table S2). This pattern suggests that in each composite treatment, the dominant influence was derived from either oyster shell or biochar, respectively. Zhang et al. observed similar clustering between lime and lime-biochar treatments [62]. These clustering further implies that strong pH effects from inorganic amendments can exert a primary influence on microbial community structure. DISTLM analysis identified soil pH (81.4%), the C3 component of DOM (12.0%), and lignin-like compounds (3.3%) as key abiotic drivers shaping community composition. Zhang et al. [53] also found that humic components of DOM were key in shaping bacterial communities under organic fertilizer treatments. The importance of humic DOM fractions in structuring bacterial communities highlights that amendments shape microbiomes largely through their impacts on soil physicochemical conditions rather than by direct microbial introduction.

At the phylum level, dominant bacterial groups (accounting for 60%–74% of total abundance) included Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Proteobacteria, and Acidobacteria (Fig. 4c). In CK, Actinobacteria were most abundant; however, their relative abundance declined under all amendment treatments, while Acidobacteria increased. This shift likely reflects changes in soil pH and DOM composition induced by the amendments: the pH elevation, particularly in oyster shell–based treatments, may have reduced the competitive advantage of Actinobacteria, whereas the enrichment of DOM, especially aromatic and humic fractions, created conditions favorable for Acidobacteria. Similar patterns were reported by Wang et al. who observed Acidobacteria enrichment and Actinobacteria decline in biochar-treated acidic soils [63]. Thus, the observed community restructuring appears to be an indirect consequence of physicochemical changes rather than a direct amendment effect.

At the genus level, norank_o__Gaiellales and Bacillus were widespread across treatments, but Bacillus was selectively enriched in the BN group, while norank_o__Gaiellales declined (Fig. 4d). Additionally, genera such as Tumebacillus, norank_o__C0119, and Gemmatimonas were significantly enriched in BN. In contrast, other treatments had a limited impact on low-abundance genera. These findings suggest that different amendments can influence the structure of the soil microbial community by selectively enriching specific functional groups, such as Bacillus in the BN group. Xu et al. similarly reported Bacillus enrichment under biochar-incubated treatments, where these taxa reduced heavy metal mobility and bioavailability through microbially induced calcite precipitation (MICP) [64]. This mechanism is consistent with the patterns observed in the BN group of the present study. Bacillus species are well known for facilitating MICP, a process capable of immobilizing heavy metals (e.g., Ni2+) via co-precipitation or surface adsorption onto CaCO3 crystals [65]. Although MICP was not directly quantified here, its potential involvement cannot be ruled out, as previous studies have shown that urease-driven MICP effectively immobilizes Cu2+, suggesting a possible analogous role in the BN group [66]. Future work integrating mineralogical characterization and Ca–Cu interaction modeling is warranted to verify this hypothesis.

Interestingly, the dominant genera enriched in the BN treatment, such as Bacillus, Tumebacillus, and norank_c__KD4-96, were not entirely consistent with the originally inoculated strains. This discrepancy suggests that the introduced bacteria may not have persistently colonized the soil, but instead indirectly promoted the restructuring and functional enhancement of the indigenous microbial community. Similar phenomena have been reported in previous studies. For example, Li et al. noted that microbial inoculants often exert their effects through community reassembly rather than direct colonization [67]. Accordingly, this study emphasizes the regulatory influence of inoculants on the broader soil microbiome rather than the performance of the introduced strains per se. Future studies should explore these interaction mechanisms more deeply using metagenomics, functional gene tracking, or stable isotope probing approaches.

Overall, the amendment application significantly altered the soil bacterial community, enhancing species richness and shifting the relative abundances of key bacterial phyla and genera. These changes were primarily mediated through modifications to pH and DOM composition. The enrichment of taxa such as Bacillus and Acidobacteria highlights the ecological importance of microbial functional traits in driving biogeochemical processes. Linking microbial community shifts to functional outcomes is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying amendment-induced soil restoration.

Correlation analysis of soil environmental factors with DGT-Cu and qCO2

Variation in soil environmental factors exerts a profound influence on both heavy metal bioavailability (DGT-Cu) and the microbial respiration efficiency (qCO2). DGT-Cu serves as an indicator of Cu bioavailability in soil, while qCO2, defined as the specific rate of microbial respiration, reflects the metabolic efficiency of the microbial community. We used statistical modeling to identify the key drivers of DGT-Cu and qCO2 variation, and incorporated molecular-level DOM characterization to probe potential underlying mechanisms.

Correlation analysis revealed strong negative associations between DGT-Cu and pH (r ​= ​−0.88), DOC (r ​= ​−0.89), and SUVA254 (r ​= ​−0.87) (Fig. 5a), suggesting that elevated pH, greater organic carbon content, and more aromatic DOM collectively contribute to the reduction of Cu bioavailability. These findings are consistent with Song et al. who reported that biochar-mediated increases in pH and DOC significantly reduced the bioavailability of Cu and Zn [68]. DGT-Cu was positively correlated with CEC (r ​= ​0.58), indicating that high CEC may facilitate Cu adsorption and retention in soils [69]. Subsequent MLR and VPA analyses confirmed that pH, CEC, and DOC are the dominant predictors of Cu bioavailability, jointly explaining 98.7% of the variance (R2 ​= ​0.987, p ​< ​0.001) (Fig. 5b). In contrast, microbial variables, such as MBC and microbial β-diversity (PCoA2), explained only about 17% of the variance, indicating that abiotic processes primarily govern short-term Cu immobilization. This observation aligns with results from Zhou et al. who found that pH and DOC explained more than 90% of the variation in heavy metal bioavailability, highlighting the overriding role of soil chemistry [70].

In contrast, qCO2 was predominantly regulated by microbial attributes, supplemented by soil chemical properties (R2 ​= ​0.947, p ​< ​0.001) (Fig. 5c). Specifically, qCO2 was closely related to microbial community features (e.g., PCoA2, r ​= ​0.80; Chao1 index, r ​= ​−0.78) and decreased with increasing MBC and SOM (r ​= ​−0.88, p ​< ​0.001). This suggests that greater microbial biomass and SOM levels improve microbial efficiency, resulting in lower CO2 emissions per unit of microbial biomass. Similar findings were reported by Gao et al. who observed that biochar amendments improved microbial efficiency and reduced qCO2 in forest soils by alleviating nutrient limitations and reducing metabolic stress [71].

Molecular-level DOM analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between qCO2 and labile DOM components such as amino sugars and carbohydrates, while positive correlations were observed with tannins (Fig. S4). Of the 615 DOM molecules significantly correlated with qCO2, 75.8% of lignin-like molecules showed positive correlation, while 91.4% of protein/lipid-like molecules showed negative correlation. This supports the notion proposed by Zhou et al. that DOM structure governs its biodegradability, thereby shaping microbial activity [70]. This may imply that DOM composition modulates CO2 emissions through its influence on microbial metabolism and substrate degradability. In line with our findings, Xia et al. reported that amino sugars and carbohydrates were rapidly metabolized by microbes under various fertilization regimes, which may explain their negative relationship with qCO2 in this study [72].

The amendment application also altered interactions between dominant bacterial genera and specific DOM molecules. Fig. 6a shows significant Spearman correlations between the top 20 genera and DOM compounds, while Fig. 6b classifies genera into functional quadrants based on their positive or negative associations with labile and recalcitrant DOM fractions. Notably, 55% (11/20) of dominant genera, including Bacillus, norank_c__Gitt-GS-136, and norank_c__KD4-96, showed negative correlations with recalcitrant compounds and were enriched in the BN treatment. While correlation does not directly imply causation, some observed relationships may reflect underlying metabolic linkages. For instance, Bacillus species are known to produce peroxidases and laccases capable of degrading lignin- and tannin-like compounds [73,74]. Their enrichment in the BN group, along with negative correlations with recalcitrant DOM, supports the hypothesis that these taxa contribute to enhanced DOM transformation and microbial efficiency. Hu et al. reported similar patterns, interpreting negative bacteria-DOM correlations as indicative of active microbial degradation, where the presence of specific bacteria corresponds to the depletion of specific DOM molecules [37]. Yet, correlation may stem from simple co-occurrences resulting from indirect environmental filtering, for example, shifts in DOM composition under different redox or pH conditions can simultaneously influence microbial community structure and DOM profiles [75]. To disentangle these correlations, future studies using time-resolved sampling, 13C-labeled DOM substrates, and multi-omics approaches (e.g., metagenomics and metatranscriptomics) are needed to elucidate causal pathways linking microbes to DOM turnover.

In summary, soil physicochemical properties, especially pH, DOC, and CEC, play a dominant role in governing Cu bioavailability (98.7% variance explained), while microbial characteristics are more critical for regulating qCO2. Although the statistical analyses provide strong evidence for these relationships, functional validation is needed, including quantification of key metabolic enzymes and transcriptomic markers. The molecular characteristics of DOM offer valuable mechanistic insights into microbial metabolism and respiratory efficiency. Long-term monitoring of microbial community dynamics and DOM molecular composition is essential for fully understanding the impacts of amendments, such as biochar, on soil ecosystem function.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the effects of biochar-based amendments, including biochar, bacteria-inoculated biochar, oyster shell powder, and their combinations, on Cu bioavailability and carbon dynamics in acidic orchard soils. DGT analyses demonstrated that all treatments significantly reduced bioavailable Cu by 60%–73% and raised soil pH by 1.03–2.54 units, with interaction observed between biochar and oyster shell powder. Beyond heavy metal stabilization, these amendments also substantially influenced the soil carbon processes. In particular, bacteria-inoculated biochar reduced qCO2 by 44%, indicating enhanced microbial carbon use efficiency and synergistic effects in Cu immobilization and carbon sequestration. These effects were accompanied by altered enzymatic profiles, such as increased S-NAG and decreased S-β-GC, and shifts in the microbial community, particularly a seven-fold enrichment of Bacillus. This enrichment may favor biosynthetic over catabolic pathways and was associated with Cu immobilization in our study. FT-ICR MS further demonstrated that bacteria-inoculated biochar altered DOM composition by reducing recalcitrant compounds (e.g., lignin- and tannin-like) by 8% and increasing labile fractions (e.g., proteins/lipids) by 6%. These molecular transformations suggest improved DOM bioavailability and microbial-mediated conversion of organic matter into more stable forms, contributing to long-term carbon retention. Overall, our findings demonstrate that heavy metal immobilization and carbon sequestration are co-regulated processes, synergistically driven by shifts in soil pH and microbial community structure. In future soil remediation practices, regulating soil pH and introducing specific functional microbial communities could achieve a combined effect on heavy metal pollution and carbon cycling, thereby improving remediation efficiency and enhancing soil carbon sink capacity. This integrated approach not only offers novel pathways for soil quality enhancement and pollution control but also establishes the theoretical and technical foundation for achieving carbon-neutral agriculture.
