PAC-Bayesian Optimal Control with Stability and Generalization Guarantees
Mahrokh Ghoddousi Boroujeni, Clara Lucía Galimberti, Andreas Krause, Giancarlo Ferrari-Trecate

Abstract
Stochastic Nonlinear Optimal Control (SNOC) seeks to minimize a cost function that accounts for random disturbances acting on a nonlinear dynamical system. Since the expectation over all disturbances is generally intractable, a common surrogate is the empirical cost, obtained by averaging over a finite dataset of sampled noise realizations. This substitution, however, introduces the challenge of guaranteeing performance under unseen disturbances. The issue is particularly severe when the dataset is limited, as the trained controllers may overfit, leading to substantial gaps between their empirical cost and the deployment cost. In this work, we develop a PAC-Bayesian framework that establishes rigorous generalization bounds for SNOC. Building on these bounds, we propose a principled controller design method that balances empirical performance and prior knowledge. To ensure tractability, we derive computationally efficient relaxations of the bounds and employ approximate inference methods. Our framework further leverages expressive neural controller parameterizations, guaranteeing closed-loop stability. Through simulated examples, we highlight how prior knowledge can be incorporated into control design and how more reliable controllers can be synthesized for cooperative robotics.



I
Introduction

Stochastic Nonlinear Optimal Control (SNOC) concerns minimizing a cost function that accounts for stochastic uncertainties affecting nonlinear systems, such as process noise and disturbances.
Beyond the linear quadratic Gaussian setting, this problem is generally intractable. It is often replaced by minimizing the average cost over a dataset of sampled uncertainties, i.e., the empirical cost. This substitution raises the fundamental challenge of deriving generalization bounds, namely, upper bounds on the true (intractable) cost in terms of its empirical (computable) counterpart.

A closely related problem has been extensively studied in supervised machine learning within the Probably Approximately Correct (PAC)-Bayesian framework
[
2
]
. PAC-Bayesian analysis provides high-probability generalization bounds for predictors sampled from an arbitrary posterior distribution, either in expectation or for a single draw. The calculation of these bounds typically comprises the empirical cost and a discrepancy measure between the posterior and a prior distribution.

Classic PAC-Bayesian bounds for supervised learning require
aggregated predictors
, obtained by averaging all predictors according to the posterior. Transferring this idea to SNOC would imply aggregating control actions across all possible controllers weighted by the posterior, which is computationally infeasible.
Instead, we adopt a less commonly used variant of PAC-Bayesian bounds that applies to
randomized predictors

[
2
]
, i.e., single predictors drawn from the posterior. In the SNOC context, this enables the establishment of generalization guarantees for a
single controller
sampled from the posterior, which is far more practical in control applications. The proposed PAC-Bayesian bound complements existing approaches to certifying learned controllers, such as those relying on mixed-integer programming or satisfiability modulo theory techniques
[
8
,
32
]
.
A discussion of related work on PAC-Bayesian control can be found in
Appendix
-A2
.

Beyond generalization, the sampled controller must achieve low cost and guarantee closed-loop stability, which is essential in control theory. This can be accomplished by defining prior and posterior distributions over families of controllers that are expressive enough to achieve high performance on complex tasks, yet restricted to ensure stability. To this end, we leverage the Performance-Boosting framework
[
15
,
14
]
, which provides broad classes of nonlinear neural network (NN)-based controllers with closed-loop stability guarantees. It further enables the optimization of general cost functions, which can reflect diverse closed-loop specifications, from minimizing the L
2
-gain of the closed-loop system to encoding safety. Importantly, it provides an unconstrained parametrization of all and only stabilizing controllers (in the
\ell_{p}
sense) for a given
\ell_{p}
-stable nonlinear system. Since this framework guarantees closed-loop stability for any parameter choice, any posterior distribution over this parameter space assigns probability mass exclusively to stabilizing controllers.

I-A

Contributions

We propose a PAC-Bayesian framework for SNOC that advances both the theoretical foundations and the algorithmic methodology of control design.
On the theoretical side, we
(i)
establish generalization certificates for SNOC and
(ii)
derive a computationally efficient, albeit looser, variant that constitutes the first tractable PAC-Bayesian bound for SNOC, enabling scalability to realistic control problems.
On the algorithmic side, we
(iii)
develop a control policy learning procedure that allows sampling controllers from an optimal posterior distribution constructed to minimize the generalization bound in expectation,
(iv)
implement highly expressive neural controllers with built-in stability guarantees
[
14
]
, and
(v)
approximate the optimal posterior to make controller sampling numerically tractable.
We validate our framework in two examples. First, we show how coarse prior knowledge can be incorporated into control design using a toy example. Second, we demonstrate its potential in a cooperative robotic task.

This paper builds upon our conference version
[
5
]
, where we introduced the first PAC-Bayesian approach for SNOC. The present work extends those results by deriving both upper and lower bounds on the true cost, introducing novel tractable bounds, and broadening the controller architectures to include State-Space Models (SSMs)
[
18
]
alongside Recurrent Equilibrium Networks (RENs)
[
30
]
. For approximating the posterior distribution, we incorporate normalizing flows
[
31
]
in addition to Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD)
[
23
]
. Furthermore, we propose constructing a data-driven prior distribution that helps in tightening the bounds. Finally, we enhance performance with a bootstrapping strategy. The advantages of these extensions are validated numerically.

Stability is a fundamental property of closed-loop systems, ensuring that bounded noise does not result in uncontrolled growth of the state

Definition 1

Definition 2
(
\ell_{p}
-stable mappings)

A mapping from a sequence to another sequence is called

-stable if it transforms every
\ell_{p}

\ell_{p}

Using these definitions, we can formalize

-stability for closed-loop systems. Recall that a closed-loop system, as per (
1

2

Definition 3
(
\ell_{p}
-stable closed-loop systems)

A closed-loop system is called
\ell_{p}
-stable if its rollout map

is an
\ell_{p}
-stable mapping.

Intuitively, this means that the system’s energy remains bounded whenever the input noise energy is bounded
[
9
]

II-C

Stable stochastic optimal control

Having ensured that the controller
K_{t}^{\theta}
is stabilizing for any parameter choice, we optimize its parameters
\theta
based on a
finite-horizon (FH) cost

L(x_{T:0},u_{T:0})
over a horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
. Since trajectories satisfy
(x_{T:0},u_{T:0})=r^{\theta}_{T:0}(w_{T:0})
, we equivalently write the FH cost as
L(\theta,w_{T:0})
. The function
L:\Theta\times\mathbb{W}^{T+1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is assumed to be nonnegative and piecewise differentiable.

As the noise sequence
w_{T:0}
is stochastic, we define the
true cost
as the expectation of the FH cost under the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{T:0}\coloneqq(\mathcal{D}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{T})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta,\mathcal{D}_{T:0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E_{w_{T:0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{T:0}}L(\theta,w_{T:0}).

(4)

By averaging over noise realizations,
\mathcal{L}
provides a robust measure of the controller’s performance.

Computing
\mathcal{L}
in (
4
) is generally intractable as it involves a high-dimensional expectation over
w_{T:0}
. For nonlinear systems and policies, there is no closed form, and an accurate Monte Carlo estimation can demand prohibitively many rollouts even when
\mathcal{D}_{T:0}
is known. Instead, SNOC methods typically optimize the
empirical cost
based on observed data

\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\bigl\{w_{T:0}^{i}\bigr\}_{s=1}^{S}
,
where
S\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of sampled noise sequences. The empirical cost is defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}L(\theta,w_{T:0}^{s}),

(5)

which averages the FH cost over the noise sequences in
\mathbb{S}
. Unlike
\mathcal{L}
,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
can be efficiently computed and optimized, leading to the following
empirical SNOC problem:

\displaystyle\text{Empirical SNOC}:~\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\;

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S}),

(6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle r^{\theta}_{\infty:0}\text{ is }\ell_{p}\text{-stable}.

(6b)

This formulation minimizes
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})
while ensuring stability according to
Definition 3
. We refer to its solution as the
empirical controller
.

III
Inference in controller space

A critical drawback of the empirical SNOC approach in (
6
) is that the trained controller may perform significantly worse when confronted with out-of-sample noise sequences than those in its training dataset
\mathbb{S}
. Formally, the true cost
\mathcal{L}
can substantially exceed the empirical cost

\hat{\mathcal{L}}
, a phenomenon corresponding to
overfitting
in supervised learning. In this section, we overcome this limitation by establishing a generalization bound on the true cost and using it to propose a controller design algorithm.

We adopt a probabilistic view over controllers: a
prior
distribution
\mathcal{P}
is specified over the parameter space
\Theta
, independently of the dataset
\mathbb{S}
, and a data-dependent
posterior

\mathcal{Q}

is formed after observing
\mathbb{S}
. In our setting, both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are supported on stabilizing controllers (cf.
Section II-B
),
so any
\theta\sim\mathcal{Q}
is stabilizing by design.

While the prior and posterior terms resemble Bayesian terminology, they are not necessarily linked by Bayes’ law. In classical Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution depends on the observed data exclusively through the likelihood, which measures how probable the data is under a given model
[
22
]
. In our context, this would mean evaluating the likelihood of observing a set of noise sequences
\mathbb{S}
given a controller
K^{\theta}
. However, since the process noise
w_{t}
is independent of the controller choice, the resulting posterior would be uninformative for control design. Instead, the posterior must reflect how good a controller
K^{\theta}
is on the dataset
\mathbb{S}
, which we quantify via the empirical cost
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})
. In other words, controllers achieving lower cost on
\mathbb{S}
should be assigned higher posterior probability relative to their prior. The PAC-Bayesian framework enables precisely this type of data dependence, by constructing posteriors that trade off empirical performance and proximity to the prior.

Later, in
Corollary 1
, we exploit this flexibility to define the
optimal Gibbs posterior

\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, which minimizes the bound in expectation. In practice, we approximate
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
using scalable variational methods such as SVGD or normalizing flows, as detailed in
Section IV-A
.

III-A

PAC-Bayes generalization bounds for SNOC

We begin by bounding the performance gap between true and empirical costs using the PAC-Bayes framework.
Originally developed for supervised learning (e.g.,
[
2
]
), where the goal is to train predictors with guarantees on unseen data, this approach provides high-probability bounds on generalization performance. In the SNOC setting, we reinterpret these tools by shifting the focus from predictors to controllers trained on datasets of noise sequences.
Table I
summarizes the correspondence between supervised learning and SNOC. By aligning these correspondences, we effectively extend PAC-Bayesian bounds from supervised learning to the SNOC framework.

III-B

Optimal posterior

III-B
1
Defining the optimal posterior

As highlighted earlier in this section,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are not necessarily linked by Bayes’ law. The flexibility in choosing
\mathcal{Q}
can be exploited to align the posterior more closely with the SNOC cost
L
. Ideally, for a given
\theta
sampled from
\mathcal{Q}
, one would choose
\mathcal{Q}
to minimize the upper bound on
\mathcal{L}
in (
8
). However, this leads to a circular dependency, since
\mathcal{Q}
must be fixed before sampling
\theta
. To resolve this, we select
\mathcal{Q}
by minimizing the expected upper bound over draws of the controller parameters, leading to the following SNOC problem:

\displaystyle\text{PAC-SNOC}:~\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\;

\displaystyle\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal{Q}}\Bigl[\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{d\mathcal{Q}}{d\mathcal{P}}(\theta)\Bigr],

(10a)

s.t.

\displaystyle r^{\theta}_{\infty:0}\text{ is }\ell_{p}\text{-stable}\quad\forall\,\theta\sim\mathcal{Q},

(10b)

where constant terms in (
8
) have been dropped. The constraint (
10b
) requires that every controller
\theta
sampled from the posterior distribution
\mathcal{Q}
stabilizes the closed-loop system in the sense of
Definition 3
.
We refer to the solution of (
10
) as the

optimal posterior
distribution and denote it by
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.

The constraint (
10b
) imposes closed-loop stability for every
\theta
sampled from
\mathcal{Q}
. This is enforced by defining
\mathcal{Q}
over the stabilizing controller family introduced in

10a
), ignoring (

Corollary 1
(Lemma 1.1.3 in
[
7
]
)

Given a dataset
\mathbb{S}
, a prior distribution
\mathcal{P}
chosen independently of
\mathbb{S}
, and a parameter

, the solution of (
10
) is the Gibbs distribution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\theta)\,e^{-\lambda\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})}/Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S}),

(11)

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\E_{\theta\sim\mathcal{P}}e^{-\lambda\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})},

(12)

where
Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S})
, known as the

function
, normalizes
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.

Computing the partition function

is notoriously difficult, as it requires integration over
\theta\sim\mathcal{P}

[
20

. This challenge is addressed further in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta,\mathcal{D}_{T:0})

\displaystyle\geq 2\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S})-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{\lambda C^{2}}{8S},

(14)

hold individually with probability at least
1-\delta
and jointly with probability at least
1-2\delta
. The probability is over simultaneously sampling
\mathbb{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{T:0}^{S}
and
\theta\sim\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.

The proof follows by substituting (
11
) into (
8
) and (
9
).
These bounds, specialized to
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, are tighter than the general PAC-Bayes bounds in
Theorem 1
, which hold for any
\mathcal{Q}
, and no longer depend explicitly on the posterior distribution.

III-B
3
Selecting the Gibbs posterior parameter

The probability mass assigned to each controller
\theta
by the optimal posterior distribution depends on two terms: the empirical cost
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})
and the prior probability
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
. The trade-off between these two terms is modulated by the parameter
\lambda
. Equivalently, this can be seen from the objective (
10a
), which balances minimizing the expected empirical cost against encouraging similarity between posterior and prior, measured by the KL divergence.

In the extremes,
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
collapses to a degenerate distribution at the minimizer(s) of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, and converges to the prior
\mathcal{P}
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. The prior can thus be chosen to incorporate existing knowledge or to regularize the posterior complexity, thereby mitigating overfitting (see
Section V
for examples).

The bounds in
Corollary 2
become tighter as the right-hand sides decrease, motivating the tuning of the free parameter
\lambda
. However, this is not straightforward since both (
13
) and (
14
) depend on the partition function (
12
), which is generally intractable. Under
Assumption 1
, the partition function satisfies
Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S})\in[e^{-\lambda C},1]
, which allows us to derive a tractable but looser relaxation of these bounds. Based on this relaxation, the following result provides a principled choice for
\lambda
.

Proposition 1

Consider the relaxation of (
13
)–(
14
) obtained by bounding the partition function.
The value of
\lambda>0
that simultaneously minimizes the relaxed upper bound and maximizes the relaxed lower bound is:

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}=\sqrt{8\,S\,\ln(1/\delta)}/C.

(15)

Given the prior
\mathcal{P}
and the dataset
\mathbb{S}
, the Gibbs distribution
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
with the parameter
\lambda^{*}
defined in (
11
) and (
15
) fully specifies a posterior distribution, requiring no additional free parameters. This construction provides a principled balance between fitting the data and incorporating prior knowledge.

. In the second stage, the remaining data
\mathbb{S}_{2}=\mathbb{S}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{1}

. When the Gibbs parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are chosen according to
Proposition 2
, the resulting posterior coincides with that of the single-stage procedure, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathcal{Q}^{*}
.

III-C

Two-stage inference

The bounds in
Corollary 2
depend on the prior
\mathcal{P}
through the partition function
Z_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P},\mathbb{S})
. This term becomes smaller, and thus the bounds become tighter, when
\mathcal{P}
assigns higher probability mass to controllers with lower costs. Designing such priors manually is challenging, so we aim to learn them from data. However,
Corollary 1
requires
\mathcal{P}
to be independent of the dataset
\mathbb{S}
.

To address this, we split the dataset into two disjoint subsets,
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}_{1}\cup\mathbb{S}_{2}
with
\mathbb{S}_{1}\cap\mathbb{S}_{2}=\varnothing
, and adopt a two-stage procedure. In the first stage, we use
\mathbb{S}_{1}
together with the data-independent prior
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{P}
to infer a posterior
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
. This posterior then serves as a data-dependent prior for the second stage,
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, which uses
\mathbb{S}_{2}
to infer the posterior
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. The process is illustrated in
Figure 1
. Posterior inference in both stages follows the procedure in
Corollary 1
.

To ensure that the two-stage and original single-stage procedures have the same performance, we require that they obtain the same posterior, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathcal{Q}^{*}
. The following result establishes conditions under which this equivalence holds.

Proposition 2

Let
\mathbb{S}_{1}
and
\mathbb{S}_{2}
be subsets of the dataset
\mathbb{S}
with sizes
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
\mathbb{S}_{1}\cup\mathbb{S}_{2}=\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{S}_{1}\cap\mathbb{S}_{2}=\varnothing
. Define the posterior distributions in the two stages as Gibbs distributions with parameters
\lambda_{1}=\frac{S_{1}}{S}\,\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=\frac{S_{2}}{S}\,\lambda
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}(\theta)\,e^{-{\lambda_{1}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S}_{1})}/Z_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathbb{S}_{1})\;,\quad\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{P},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}(\theta)\,e^{-{\lambda_{2}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S}_{2})}/Z_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathcal{P}_{2},\mathbb{S}_{2})\;,\quad\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}.

Then,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
coincides with the optimal posterior
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
from
Corollary 1
, corresponding to the prior
\mathcal{P}
and the full dataset
\mathbb{S}
.

The proof is given in
Appendix
-B1
. Since
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, the two-stage procedure achieves the same performance as the single-stage approach. However, it offers an additional advantage: the bounds obtained by applying
Corollary 2
in the second stage—with prior
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, parameter
\lambda_{2}
, and dataset
\mathbb{S}_{2}
—can be tighter than those obtained in the single-stage case with prior
\mathcal{P}
, parameter
\lambda
, and dataset
\mathbb{S}
. This improvement is confirmed empirically in
Section V
.

IV
Approximate Inference

We introduced the optimal posterior in
Corollary 1
and its associated generalization bounds in
Corollary 2
. However, their direct use is limited by the partition function
Z_{\lambda}
in (
12
), which is generally intractable due to the expectation over
\theta\sim\mathcal{P}
. In this section, we develop approximation methods that make these results practically applicable.
We first approximate
\mathcal{Q}
with a tractable distribution from which controllers
\theta
can be efficiently sampled. We then approximate the function
Z_{\lambda}
to obtain computable bounds on the true cost.
An overview of the relationships between the theoretical components of our framework is illustrated in
Figure 2
.

).

IV-A

Approximating the optimal posterior

Given
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathbb{S}
, the optimal posterior
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
is only computable via (
11
) up to the normalization constant
Z_{\lambda}
. Hence, direct sampling from
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
is infeasible. To address this, we approximate
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
with a tractable distribution from which we can sample the controller parameters
\theta
.
We employ two approximation techniques: SVGD
[
23
]
and normalizing flows
[
31
]
.

SVGD approximates
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
using a set of
k\in\mathbb{N}
particles denoted by
{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}}
. In our framework, each particle corresponds to the parameters of a controller, i.e.,
\phi_{\kappa}\in\Theta
for
\kappa\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. SVGD initializes these particles by sampling from the prior distribution and iteratively updates them to align their density with the probability mass of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
through gradient descent.
After training, the approximated distribution is a uniform distribution over the particles, making sampling equivalent to randomly picking a particle with equal probability. A one-dimensional illustration of SVGD particles before and after training is provided in
Figure 3
.
We refer to
[
23
]
for further details.

Fig. 3:
Approximating a one-dimensional target distribution (red) over a variable
x
using SVGD (top) and normalizing flows (bottom). In SVGD, particles (cyan crosses) are initialized uniformly and move toward high-density regions after training. In normalizing flows, both the Gaussian base distribution (magenta) and the transformations are trainable, allowing the transformed distribution (cyan) to better align with the target after training.

Although SVGD can approximate complex distributions with arbitrary precision given sufficient particles
[
23
]
, its computational cost grows linearly with the number of particles. To balance accuracy with efficiency, we use normalizing flows
[
31
]
. This approach transforms an initial simple density, called the
base
distribution, into a complex one through parameterized invertible mappings. The parameters of these invertible mappings and the base distribution are updated through gradient descent to align the resulting transformed distribution with
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
. A one-dimensional example of normalizing flows before and after training is provided in
Figure 3
.
We empirically compare SVGD and normalizing flows in
Section V
.

IV-B

Approximating the bound

Next, we tackle the intractability of the upper bound for the optimal posterior in
Corollary 2
, which arises due to its dependence on the partition function,
Z_{\lambda}
. To address this, we approximate
\ln Z_{\lambda}
via Monte Carlo methods and account for potential overestimation using McDiarmid’s inequality
[
26
]
. This ensures a valid, albeit looser, upper bound, as established in the following corollary.

Corollary 3

Let
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N_{P}}
be
N_{P}\in\mathbb{N}
independent samples from the prior
\mathcal{P}
, and define the Monte Carlo estimate:

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N_{P}},\mathbb{S})=\frac{1}{N_{P}}\sum_{n_{P}=1}^{N_{P}}e^{-\lambda\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{n_{P}},\mathbb{S})}.

(16)

If the optimal posterior
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
from (
11
)-(
12
) is used in
Theorem 1
, as per
Corollary 2
, the inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\theta,\mathcal{D}_{T:0})\leq-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\hat{Z}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N_{P}},\mathbb{S})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda C^{2}}{8S}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{e^{\lambda C}-1}{N_{P}}\Bigr)\sqrt{\frac{N_{P}}{2}\ln\bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}\bigr)},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\theta,\mathcal{D}_{T:0})\geq 2\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\hat{Z}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N_{P}},\mathbb{S})-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda C^{2}}{8S}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{e^{\lambda C}-1}{N_{P}}\Bigr)\sqrt{\frac{N_{P}}{2}\ln\bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}\bigr)},

(18)

hold individually with probability at least
1-2\,\delta
and jointly with probability at least
1-3\,\delta
. The probability is over simultaneously sampling
\mathbb{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{T:0}^{S}
,
\theta\sim\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, and
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N_{P}}\sim\mathcal{P}^{N_{P}}
.

The proof is provided in
subsubsection -B2
.

The last term on the right-hand side of (
17
) and (
18
) reflects the additional conservatism introduced by the approximation. This term decreases as the number of samples
N_{P}
increases, ultimately vanishing as
N_{P}\to\infty
. Moreover, since
\ln\hat{Z}_{\lambda}
is an unbiased estimator of
\ln Z_{\lambda}
,
Corollary 2
and
Corollary 3
are consistent and coincide in the limit
N_{P}\to\infty
.

A related approximation is provided in
[
5
]
by bounding the difference between
Z_{\lambda}
and
\hat{Z}_{\lambda}
for sufficiently large
N_{P}
. In contrast, we bound the deviation between
\ln Z_{\lambda}
and
\ln\hat{Z}_{\lambda}
. Compared to
[
5
]
, our relaxed bound decays faster with
N_{P}
and holds for any
N_{P}
, as demonstrated in our experiments in
Section V-B
.

Experiments

We evaluate the proposed method on two systems:
(i) a scalar Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) system, and
(ii) a multi-agent navigation task in which two planar robots must reach predefined target locations while avoiding collisions.
For both systems, the posterior distribution over controller parameters is given by the optimal Gibbs distribution
\mathbf{Q}^{*}
with parameter
\lambda^{*}
, as defined in (
11
) and (
15
), respectively. In all experiments, we apply the cost transformation in (
7
) with
C=1
, and set
\gamma
as discussed in
Section III-A
.

The source code for all experiments is available at
https://www.github.com/DecodEPFL/PAC-SNOC
.
The implementation is based on PyTorch
[
28
]
modules and supports batched computations for efficient GPU usage.
All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA H100 GPU, with each run restricted to
40\%
of the available GPU resources.

V-A

LTI system

System

We consider a scalar, stable LTI system to illustrate the control design procedure and the derived PAC-Bayesian bounds. The system is given by:

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=\bar{x}+w_{0},

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=a\,x_{t-1}+b\,u_{t-1}+w_{t},\quad t=1,2,\ldots,

with
a=0.8
,
b=0.1
, and
\bar{x}=2
. At each time step, the noise
w_{t}
is drawn from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{w},\sigma_{w}^{2})
with
\mu_{w}=0.3
and
\sigma_{w}^{2}=0.09
. The distribution of
w_{t}
is assumed unknown; instead, we are given
S
independent noise sequences of length
T=10
. The value of
S
is varied to study its influence on the performance.

Controller

We utilize the affine state-feedback controller
u_{t}=-(k\,x_{t}+\beta)
, where
k
and
\beta
denote the controller gain and bias. The vector
\theta=[k,\beta]^{\top}
represents the controller parameters. Closed-loop stability corresponds to the constraint
k\in(-2,18)
, which we enforce during optimization by projecting
k
into this interval at each update. Because the system is scalar and LTI, this simple projection step alone ensures stability, obviating the need for more elaborate stability certification methods such as those in
Section II-B
.
The controller parameters are optimized to minimize the quadratic FH cost
L(x_{T:0},u_{T:0})=\sum_{t=0}^{T}5\,x_{t}^{2}+0.003\,u_{t}^{2}
.

Baselines

We compare our methodology against two alternatives. The first is an
empirical
controller obtained by minimizing
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})
as defined in (
5
). The second is a
benchmark
(ideal) controller that assumes exact knowledge of the noise mean
\mu_{w}
, enabling the optimal choice
\beta=\mu_{w}/b
. The gain
k
of the benchmark controller is optimized by minimizing the empirical cost over a large dataset of
1024
noise sequences. While this controller achieves strong performance, it relies on both accurate knowledge of
\mu_{w}
and a substantial amount of data, which limits its practicality. We nonetheless include it as a best-case competitor.

Prior distribution

The prior distribution over the controller parameters
\theta=[k,\beta]^{\top}
must be selected independently of the dataset
\mathbb{S}
. We choose independent priors for
k
and
\beta
. Specifically, we set the prior for
k
as a Gaussian distribution centered at the infinite-horizon LQR (IH-LQR) gain with variance
1.0
. Although the IH-LQR gain is not necessarily optimal for the finite horizon
T=10
, it provides a reasonable, data-independent choice.
We select the prior for
\beta
to reflect available knowledge of the noise mean
\mu_{w}
. We consider two cases:
(i) when it is only known that
\mu_{w}\in[-0.5,0.5]
, we set
\beta\sim\mathcal{U}(-0.5/b,0.5/b)=\mathcal{U}(-5,5)
;
(ii) when
\mu_{w}
is known with some uncertainty, we set
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{w}/b,1.5^{2})=\mathcal{N}(3,1.5^{2})
.
We refer to these priors as
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
, respectively.

Posterior distribution

Since both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
are bivariate in the controller parameters
(k,\beta)
, we approximate their Probability Density Functions (PDFs) by discretizing a rectangular grid in the
(k,\beta)
-plane that contains more than
95\%
of the probability mass. In two dimensions, such gridding is not computationally expensive; therefore, we adopt this approach instead of more complex sampling-based approximations such as SVGD or normalizing flows. Figure
4
shows
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{Q}^{*}

S=512
.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{U}}
and

Evaluating the performance

For
S=8
, the posterior distributions associated with both
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}}
are concentrated near the benchmark controller, whereas the empirical controller lies farther away. This suggests that controllers sampled from the posterior are more likely to resemble the benchmark controller, potentially yielding superior out-of-sample performance compared to the empirical controller. With
S
increased to
512

A comparison of the two rows of Figure

System

We consider two robots coordinately passing through a narrow valley and reaching a target position while avoiding both obstacles and mutual collisions—see Figure
7
. Each robot is modeled as a point-mass double integrator subject to nonlinear drag forces. The overall system state is
x\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
, comprising the positions and velocities of both robots in the Cartesian coordinates, and the control input
u\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
represents the forces applied to each robot along the two coordinate directions. Each robot is prestabilized by a proportional controller that drives it toward the desired end position but lacks explicit collision-avoidance logic, which can result in collisions and degraded performance. This experiment is adapted from
[
15
]
, where additional details on the system setup are provided.

The noise
w_{t}
in (
1
) affects only the initial condition, i.e.,
w_{t}=0
for
t>0
, with
w_{0}
drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution of variance
\sigma_{w}^{2}=0.2^{2}
. The value of
\sigma_{w}
is assumed to be unknown. The dataset consists of
S=32
independent noise sequences over a horizon of
T=100
, with nonzero values only at
t=0
.

Controller

Given the complexity of the task, we employ NN-based controllers following the stability-preserving architecture described in
Section II-B
. The mapping
\mathcal{M}_{\infty:0}^{\theta}
, which is required to be
\ell_{p}
-stable, is implemented in two NN variants: one based on RENs and the other on SSMs. The dimensions of the REN and SSM are chosen so that each contains approximately
900
parameters, enabling a fair performance comparison. Further details on these models are provided in
Appendix
-C
.

The controller parameters are optimized to minimize an FH cost comprising a quadratic term plus collision- and obstacle-avoidance penalties:

L(x_{t},u_{t})=\sum_{t=0}^{T}(\delta x_{t})^{\top}Q(\delta x_{t})+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}+l_{ca}(d_{t})+l_{oa}(x_{t}),

where
\delta x_{t}=x_{t}-x_{\text{target}}
,
x_{\text{target}}
is the desired state with target positions and zero velocities, and
d_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is the distance between robots at time
t
. The collision-avoidance term
l_{ca}(d_{t})
is zero if
d_{t}>D
for a safe distance
D>0
, and otherwise
l_{ca}(d_{t})=(d_{t}+\nu)^{-2}
with
\nu>0
a small positive constant. The obstacle-avoidance term
l_{oa}
is a function of the distance between the robots and each obstacle.
The exact formulation of the cost function is provided in
[
15
]
.

Baselines

We compare our method against the empirical approach, which minimizes
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\mathbb{S})
for each controller architecture. No benchmark approach is considered in this experiment, as the large number of controller parameters would require an impractically large dataset to train an ideal high-performing controller, and manually incorporating knowledge of the noise distribution into the controller design procedure is challenging.

Prior distribution

We use a zero-mean spherical Gaussian prior on the parameters
\theta
of the controller—whether implemented as a REN or as an SSM— which imposes L
2
-regularization over
\theta

[
4
]
. This choice is motivated by the well-established effectiveness of L
2
-regularization in mitigating overfitting in supervised learning. The variance of the prior is fine-tuned via Bayesian optimization (see
Appendix
-D
).

Posterior distribution

We approximate the optimal Gibbs posterior distribution
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
, corresponding to
\lambda=\lambda^{*}

Performance enhancement using bootstrapping

Since our framework produces a distribution over controllers, we can sample multiple candidates and select the best one to enhance performance. To preserve the validity of the bounds, note that
Corollary 2
holds with probability
1-\delta
for a single controller sampled from
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
. If
N_{Q}\in\mathbb{N}
controllers are sampled, the probability that the bounds hold for all of them is
1-N_{Q}\delta
, and thus also
1-N_{Q}\delta
for the selected best controller. Consequently, by setting
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/N_{Q}
, sampling
N_{Q}
controllers, and choosing the best one, the bounds continue to hold with probability
1-\delta
for the selected controller.

The next key question is how to select the best controller. While choosing the one with the lowest training cost is natural, it risks overfitting. Instead, we propose a bootstrap-based method. Bootstrapping is a resampling technique that repeatedly draws samples with replacement from the training dataset to estimate a statistic and its confidence interval
[
21
]
. We use this approach to estimate the out-of-sample cost of each candidate controller, thereby guiding the selection toward the one expected to generalize best.

We empirically validate this approach using REN-based controllers with
S=32
,
\delta=0.1
, and the Normalizing Flows approximation method. Specifically, we sample
N_{Q}=10
controllers from the approximated posterior distribution corresponding to
\delta^{\prime}=0.1/10=0.01
. We then compare the best controller obtained through bootstrapping against
100
controllers sampled directly from the posterior distribution with
\delta=0.1
in terms of the test cost. The bootstrapping-based controller achieves an
8\%
improvement over the average cost of the sampled controller and a
20\%
improvement over the worst sampled controller. This demonstrates that bootstrapping robustifies our approach by avoiding poor-performing controllers, even though the probability of encountering such cases is low.

Scalability

Finally, we train a PAC-SNOC REN-based controller with approximately
10^{4}
parameters using SVGD approximation with a single particle for
1000
epochs. The resulting test cost improves by
7\%
compared to the results in Table
II
, and the training time is
2583\pm 330
seconds, demonstrating that our framework effectively scales to large controllers.

VI
Conclusions

This paper introduced a PAC-Bayesian framework for SNOC that provides generalization guarantees by relating the true cost of a controller to its empirical performance on finite datasets.
On the algorithmic side, we developed a principled methodology for learning control policies by sampling from an optimal posterior distribution, ensuring closed-loop stability through neural architectures with built-in guarantees. The framework naturally incorporates prior knowledge via the prior distribution and remains computationally feasible for complex tasks thanks to modern approximate inference techniques.
Experiments on synthetic and robotic tasks confirmed these benefits, showing improved generalization. Future work includes extending the analysis to infinite-horizon settings, studying dataset-size dependence more rigorously, and leveraging existing controllers for prior design.

Learning stabilizing controllers

In general, stability can be imposed in an SNOC problem (

The first one is to resort to

[
3

However, enforcing such constraints often becomes a computational bottleneck in complex applications. This issue has been highlighted in
[

10^{2}

The second possibility is to use
unconstrained optimization approaches searching within classes of control policies with built-in stability guarantees
[
34
,
16
,
15
,
14
,
17
]
.
These methods allow learning controllers through optimization algorithms based on gradient descent, without imposing further constraints to ensure stability.
Additionally, unconstrained approaches usually have a much lower computational burden than constrained ones
[
15
,
14
,
17
]
.
In
Section IV
, we show that unconstrained approaches also enable the use of algorithms for sampling the posterior, such as SVGD.
Moreover, in

-A
2
PAC-Bayesian control

PAC-Bayesian bounds have not been widely applied to SNOC problems, with the exception of
[
24
]
, which studied them in a different context, focusing on zero-shot generalization across new environments. Other related works employ PAC-Bayesian analysis for learning control policies in Markov Decision Processes
[
12
,
13
]
.
However, these approaches typically assume finite action or state spaces, which are not well-suited for SNOC.

A key reason for the limited adoption of classical PAC-Bayesian bounds in control is that they integrate actions from all possible controllers under the posterior, which is computationally prohibitive and impractical, as discussed in
Section I
.
We address this challenge by extending PAC-Bayesian bounds for randomized predictors to SNOC, enabling tractable analysis in settings with infinite and continuous state and action spaces.

In
[

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\theta}:\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{t+1}\\
\zeta_{t}\\

\sigma(\zeta_{t})\\
\hat{w}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
\xi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\xi}
is an internal state initialized at zero, and
\zeta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\zeta}}
is an internal variable. The activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is applied elementwise and must be piecewise differentiable with first derivatives in

. The mapping
\Omega:\mathbb{R}^{{N_{\theta}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{(N_{\xi}+N_{\zeta}+{N_{u}})\times(N_{\xi}+N_{\zeta}+{N_{x}})}
, provided in
[
14
]
, ensures that
\zeta_{t}
is uniquely defined at every
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and that (
24
) is
\ell_{2}
-stable for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
, i.e.,
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
. Together, (
3
) and (
24

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
.

In

, we use RENs with
(N_{\xi},N_{\zeta})=(8,8)

{N_{\theta}}=864

(N_{\xi},N_{\zeta})=(32,32)

For the empirical approach, the REN parameters are initialized by sampling from a Gaussian distribution with a zero mean and a diagonal covariance matrix

SSM

As discussed in
Section II-B
, the only
requirement on
\mathcal{M}_{\infty:0}^{\theta}
is that it is
\ell_{p}
-stable.
This requirement can be satisfied by a recently proposed class of Structured
State-Space Models (SSMs), namely the L2RU architecture
[
25
]
.

The L2RU architecture consists of a sequence of
state-space layers
, where each layer is a sequence of an
\ell_{2}
-bounded discrete-time LTI system with a Lipschitz-bounded nonlinearity. Linear pre- and post-processing modules are also applied to the input and output. Crucially, the L2RU parametrization ensures a prescribed input-output
\ell_{2}
-bound for any choice of parameters across all modules. This design guarantees input-output stability without the requirement of satisfying explicit constraints during optimization, so that one has
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
.

In
Section V-B
, we instantiate the controller with two state-space layers, each with a hidden state of dimension
8
. The nonlinearities are implemented as Coupling Layers
[
10
,
27
]
with hidden layers of dimension
8
. This configuration results in a dynamical neural controller with
{N_{\theta}}=896
parameters that guarantees closed-loop stability for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
.

When training with the empirical approach, the parameters of SSMs are initialized following the procedure in
[
18
,
25
]
, where the eigenvalues of each state-space layer are sampled uniformly at random inside a disk centered at the origin with outer radius
1
and a non-zero inner radius. This prevents eigenvalues with small modulus, which may induce oscillations in the SSM output
[
18
]
. The inner radius is treated as a tunable hyperparameter, as discussed in
Appendix
-D
. Similar to RENs, explicit initialization is not required when training with PAC-SNOC since the SSM parameters are sampled directly from the initial distribution over controllers.

-C
3
SVGD

As discussed in
Section IV
, Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD)
[
23
]
approximates the optimal posterior distribution
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
as a discrete distribution over a set of particles
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Each particle
\phi_{i}\in\Theta
represents the parameters of a controller and is initialized by sampling from the prior distribution
\mathcal{P}
.
At each iteration, the particles are updated according to:

\displaystyle\phi_{i}\leftarrow

\displaystyle\phi_{i}+\eta\,\varphi(\phi_{i}),

\displaystyle\varphi(\phi)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big[\kappa(\phi_{j},\phi)\nabla_{\phi_{j}}\log\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\phi_{j})+\nabla_{\phi_{j}}\kappa(\phi_{j},\phi)\Big],

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate and
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
is a positive definite kernel that encourages diversity among the particles by repelling them from one another.
We employ the RBF kernel with bandwidth selected using the median heuristic
[
23
]
.

Importantly, the gradient
\nabla_{\phi}\log\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\phi)
requires only the unnormalized density of
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
in (
11
), thus avoiding computation of the partition function
Z_{\lambda}
. After convergence, the approximate posterior is given by the uniform distribution over the
k
particles. The learning rate is treated as a hyperparameter, tuned as described in
Appendix
-D
.

-C
4
Normalizing flows

Normalizing flows
[
31
]
provide an alternative to SVGD for approximating the optimal posterior
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
by learning a parametric distribution with tractable density evaluation and sampling. The key idea is to begin from a simple base distribution
p_{0}(z)
over
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, typically a multivariate Gaussian, and map it to a complex distribution through a sequence of invertible transformations. Formally, letting
z_{0}\sim p_{0}(z)
and
z_{L}=f_{L}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}(z_{0})
, the resulting density is obtained via the change-of-variables formula:

\log q(z_{L})=\log p_{0}(z_{0})-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\log\Big|\det\nabla f_{\ell}(z_{\ell-1})\Big|.

In our framework, the transformed variable
z_{L}
corresponds to the controller parameters, i.e.,
z_{L}\equiv\theta
. The flow parameters, along with the mean and covariance of the base distribution, are optimized by minimizing the KL divergence between the induced distribution
q
and the target Gibbs posterior
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
. Training is performed via stochastic gradient descent, with the learning rate tuned as a hyperparameter following
Appendix
-D
.

We use
L=16
transformations and define each
f_{\ell}
as a
planar flow

[
31
]
,

f(z)=z+u\,h(w^{\top}z+b),

where
u,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
, and
h
is the hyperbolic tangent function. Planar flows offer a balance between flexibility and computation, as their Jacobian determinant is available in closed form.
We used the open-source NormFlows package
[
33
]
in our code.

We observed that convergence is sensitive to the choice of base distribution. To address this, we adopt a data-driven initialization: we first train controllers using the empirical method on a single fixed data point with five different random seeds, then center the base distribution at the mean of the resulting parameters. The covariance is chosen as a diagonal matrix, where the diagonal entries are obtained by scaling the sample variance of these parameters by a constant factor greater than one. This scaling factor is treated as a hyperparameter and tuned as described in
Appendix
-D
.

-D

Hyperparameter tuning

In our framework, hyperparameters include the standard deviation of the Gaussian initialization for RENs, the inner radius of the eigenvalue initialization disk for SSMs, the scaling constant of the base distribution covariance for training normalizing flows, the standard deviation of the prior distribution for PAC-SNOC, and the learning rate for all methods.

We tune these hyperparameters using the open-source
Optuna
package
[
1
]
, which performs automated hyperparameter search via Bayesian optimization. The objective function is defined as the validation cost over a held-out subset of
25\%
of training trajectories, and the best-performing hyperparameter configuration over
10
trials is selected. For reproducibility, we specify the search ranges and the final selected hyperparameters in the codebase accompanying this paper.

When using the two-stage approach, the number of data points used in each stage can also be tuned. For this, we performed a grid search aimed at minimizing the constant terms in
Corollary 3
, since these constants are generally dominant. From the three values yielding the smallest constant terms, we then computed the full bound and selected the configuration with the tightest result. Note that computing the full bound requires two rounds of posterior inference, which makes a direct grid search over the bound in
Corollary 3
impractical. This motivates our procedure, where the grid search is first performed over the constant term only, and then the best candidates are fully evaluated. In principle, Bayesian optimization could also be used for this purpose, though we did not explore this option.

Giancarlo Ferrari Trecate

(SM’12) received a Ph.D. in Electronic and Computer Engineering from the Università Degli Studi di Pavia in 1999. Since September 2016, he has been a Professor at EPFL, Lausanne, Switzerland. In the spring of 1998, he was a Visiting Researcher at the Neural Computing Research Group, University of Birmingham, UK. In the fall of 1998, he joined the Automatic Control Laboratory, ETH, Zurich, Switzerland, as a Postdoctoral Fellow. He was appointed Oberassistent at ETH in 2000. In 2002, he joined INRIA, Rocquencourt, France, as a Research Fellow. From March to October 2005, he was a researcher at the Politecnico di Milano, Italy. From 2005 to August 2016, he was Associate Professor at the Dipartimento di Ingegneria Industriale e dell’Informazione of the Università degli Studi di Pavia.
His research interests include scalable control, machine learning, microgrids, networked control, and hybrid systems.
Giancarlo Ferrari Trecate is the founder and current chair of the Swiss chapter of the IEEE Control Systems Society. He is Senior Editor of the IEEE Transactions on Control Systems Technology and has served on the editorial boards of Automatica and Nonlinear Analysis: Hybrid Systems.
