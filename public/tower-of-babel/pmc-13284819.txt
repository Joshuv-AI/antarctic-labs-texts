To the Editor:

We read with great interest the review article by Morelli et al. [1] on diagnostic approaches to COPD. They provide a valuable synthesis of evolving diagnostic paradigms. They highlight the strengths of our recently published multidimensional diagnostic schema that incorporates lung imaging into the diagnostic paradigm [2]. They categorise the feasibility of implementation of this schema as moderate, citing the need for computed tomography (CT) imaging of the chest. This is a common misconception regarding the new multidimensional diagnostic schema that we would like to clarify. Although this concern may hold validity for large-scale epidemiological studies or for clinical trials, it is not a departure from current clinical practice. According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report, a diagnosis of COPD is made when respiratory symptoms are present and spirometric airflow obstruction is confirmed [3]. The new schema may be applied when lung imaging is available, or is acquired for further characterisation in cases of diagnostic uncertainty [2]. Therefore, the addition of lung imaging is not “mandatory” but an extension of the current diagnostic framework. The GOLD document also calls for structured assessment of symptoms for decision making related to treatment but symptoms are often categorised in practice as present or absent [3]. The current diagnostic framework is dominated by ascertainment of symptoms and risk factor-based probability of COPD. Chest CT imaging is more accessible than spirometry in many regions of the world, especially in primary care settings [4]. The new diagnostic schema's true innovation lies in operationalising what clinicians already do intuitively: integrating symptoms, imaging and lung function into a comprehensive diagnostic assessment. By establishing explicit thresholds for minor criteria and recognising preobstruction COPD, the framework provides diagnostic clarity in scenarios where the GOLD criteria fall short, particularly in symptomatic patients with preserved spirometry who have structural lung disease.

We commend the authors for highlighting the critical need for multidimensional assessment beyond spirometry alone. The limitations of the forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity ratio as a sole diagnostic criterion are increasingly recognised, and frameworks incorporating symptoms, imaging, and functional impairment represent essential progress toward earlier detection and more personalised COPD care. Rather than being less feasible, the multidimensional schema may be more aligned with real-world clinical practice than purely spirometry-based approaches.
