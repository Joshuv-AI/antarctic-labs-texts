RAPID perfusion CT in acute ischemic stroke: an educational pictorial review of common patterns, pearls, and pitfalls

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid processing of Perfusion and Diffusion (RAPID) software for CT perfusion (CTP) is a valuable tool for the evaluation of acute ischemic stroke and therapeutic decision-making in extended therapeutic windows. However, automated maps should not be interpreted in isolation. A structured, pattern-based approach, integrating qualitative visual assessment with automated quantitative output and clinical information to refine therapeutic selection, remains essential to mitigate pitfalls and optimize patient care. Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability throughout the world. During the hyperacute phase, rapid identification of irreversibly infarcted and hypoperfused but potentially salvageable tissue is critical for selection of patients who may benefit from reperfusion therapy. In the past decade, imaging has evolved from a purely time-based strategy to a “tissue-clock” paradigm, where treatment decisions are driven by advanced imaging hemodynamic biomarkers, rather than time of symptom onset [1].

CT perfusion (CTP) represents a central part of this approach, through quantification of cerebral hemodynamics, it allows discrimination between infarct core and penumbra and, thus, provides a more accurate estimate of tissue viability than non-contrast CT (NCCT) alone [2, 3].

This ability is especially important for patients presenting outside conventional treatment time windows, wake-up strokes, and complex clinical conditions in which initial CT findings are equivocal or non-specific [4, 5]. Implementation of automated post-processing systems such as RAPID has contributed substantially to the general application of perfusion imaging to the acute stroke work-up. With standardized quantitative maps and summary results based on predefined thresholds, including infarct core derived from cerebral blood flow (CBF) and volumes and ratios of mismatch, RAPID provides rapid and reproducible results [6, 7].

These results were critical for major randomized controlled trials in which the benefit of treatment selection based on perfusion imaging was demonstrated. In particular, the clinical importance of selection for mechanical thrombectomy based on mismatch and for extended time windows was established by the DEFUSE 3 and DAWN trials, whereas the application of perfusion-directed thrombolysis for selected patients outside conventional time limits was supported by the EXTEND trial [4, 5, 8].

Despite these advances, perfusion imaging is not without limitations. Automated map interpretation depends on acquisition quality, threshold selection, patient motion, characteristics of contrast bolus, and physiological variability. In addition, the estimated infarct core or penumbra may differ from MRI results or may not fully reflect the final infarct, especially in cases of early reperfusion, collateral variability, or technical artifacts [7, 9, 10]. For these reasons, although automated software improves standardization of workflow, interpretation of images still requires radiological expertise and integration with clinical and vascular imaging results.

The objective of this article is to provide an educational pictorial review based on selected illustrative cases of the most common imaging patterns, interpretative pitfalls, and technical features of CTP with RAPID software in acute ischemic stroke. With correlation to NCCT, perfusion maps, quantitative RAPID results, and, when available, MRI, the goal of this review is to offer a practical approach for identifying typical and atypical perfusion situations appropriate for daily clinical practice.

Educational case selection

The imaging examples presented in this pictorial review were selected from the institutional imaging archive to illustrate representative CT perfusion patterns, common interpretative pitfalls, and technical limitations encountered during RAPID software analysis in patients with suspected acute ischemic stroke. Cases were chosen exclusively for their educational value and their ability to demonstrate specific imaging concepts relevant to daily neuroradiological practice.

The purpose of this review is to offer a practical and educational visual guide to the interpretation of RAPID-derived perfusion maps. The selected examples highlight clinically relevant scenarios frequently encountered in acute stroke imaging, including classical target mismatch profiles, large mismatch with minimal infarct core, extensive infarct core patterns, intermediate mismatch configurations, threshold-dependent variations in Tmax interpretation, motion-related artifacts, discrepancies between CT perfusion and MRI findings, anatomical limitations in deep gray matter and posterior circulation territories, and common stroke mimickers.

Principles of RAPID CT perfusion interpretation

Interpretation of CT perfusion studies with RAPID software should not be limited to automated summary volumes and must always be combined with NCCT, CT angiography, clinical findings, and evaluation of image quality [6, 7]. Although RAPID provides standardized quantitative results, accurate interpretation requires appreciation of the physiological basis and limitations of each perfusion parameter [6, 7].

Infarct core

Estimates of infarct core volume were obtained for regions of cerebral blood flow less than 30% of that measured in the corresponding contralateral hemisphere. This criterion has been extensively validated as a surrogate measure of irreversible ischemic injury and is widely used in major clinical trials of image-directed methods of reperfusion [2, 4, 5]. However, automated infarct core estimates should not be considered as absolute representations of biological reality, because they may be influenced by variations in acquisition protocols, patterns of collateral circulation, degrees of reperfusion, and technical artifacts [7]. As a result, automated infarct core estimates may under- or overestimate the final infarct extent on follow-up imaging [7, 11].

Hypoperfused tissue and Tmax

The degree of hypoperfusion was primarily quantified with maps of time to maximum cerebral blood flow. Application of a threshold of greater than 6 s provided the most widely accepted indication of critically hypoperfused tissue at risk for infarction and formed the basis for most contemporary approaches to treatment based on patterns of mismatch [3, 6]. Use of lower thresholds included regions of benign oligemia, whereas progressively higher limits reflected increasingly severe impairments of cerebral perfusion. Thus, interpretation of maps of time to maximum cerebral blood flow should emphasize overall patterns and severity of delays in cerebral perfusion rather than depend solely on a single value for the duration of hypoperfusion [3, 7].

Core–penumbra mismatch and collateral circulation

The mismatch concept reflects the contrast between irreversibly damaged tissue and hypoperfused but potentially salvageable brain parenchyma. Patients with relatively small infarct cores and large volumes of hypoperfused tissue exhibit favorable patterns of mismatch and are most likely to benefit from reperfusion therapy [4, 5, 12], especially in extended intervals of treatment. The importance of selecting patients based on patterns of tissue viability was confirmed by major studies in which treatment decisions based on estimates of tissue viability produced greater clinical benefit than conventional criteria of time of treatment [4, 5, 12, 13].

Collateral perfusion is a major determinant of the rate of progression of infarction and of patterns of tissue viability after arterial occlusion. Robust collateral pathways maintain brain parenchymal viability despite severe perfusion delays, whereas poor collateralization accelerates core expansion and leads to worse clinical outcomes [14, 15]. As a result, interpretation of patterns of mismatch must always consider the magnitude and severity of abnormalities of Tmax, combined with findings of vascular imaging and the overall clinical situation. The hypoperfusion intensity ratio (HIR) represents an indirect index of collateral efficiency and the severity of hypoperfusion. Lower ratios reflect better collateral status, slower rates of infarct progression, and improved clinical outcome, whereas higher ratios indicate a greater burden of critically hypoperfused tissue and a higher risk of infarct expansion [16]. Therefore, this parameter should augment traditional indices of mismatch and provide additional information concerning the vulnerability of tissue to hypoperfusion and the adequacy of collateral perfusion.

Practical interpretation workflow

A complete strategy for rapid interpretation should include: (1) assessment of established signs of cerebral ischemia on NCCT; (2) identification of arterial occlusion on CTA; (3) quantitation of volume of infarct core on maps of cerebral blood flow; (4) determination of the volume of hypoperfused brain on maps of time to maximum; (5) evaluation of patterns of mismatch and of features of collateral perfusion; and (6) confirmation of adequacy of image quality and absence of potential artifacts of technical origin [6, 7, 13].

Automated results must always be interpreted in the context of the overall clinical and imaging findings and never considered in isolation [7, 10, 12]. Special attention should be given to artifacts of motion, abnormalities of the contrast bolus, limitations of anatomic coverage (e.g., for the posterior circulation and deep gray matter structures) and potential discordance between findings of perfusion imaging and subsequent MRI results [7, 10, 12]. As a result, RAPID represents a tool for assisting, rather than replacing, the expertise of the radiologist and of the multidisciplinary clinical team [6, 7, 15].

Technical and algorithmic limitations of RAPID

Beyond transient artifacts and pitfalls, RAPID operates under intrinsic algorithmic limitations. Fixed hemodynamic cutoffs (CBF < 30% and Tmax > 6 s) may not reflect the full biological continuum of ischemic injury in all patients. Furthermore, software performance remains limited in posterior fossa ischemia due to restricted Z-axis coverage and beam-hardening artifacts, as well as in small lacunar or deep gray matter infarcts where partial volume averaging obscures true perfusion deficits.

The therapeutic “Sweet spot”—classical target core–penumbra mismatch

The prototypical pattern of perfusion in acute ischemic stroke is the presence of a large mismatch ratio, sustained by a small infarct core with an extensive region of penumbra with delayed perfusion (Fig. 2). This mismatch profile confirms the presence of at-risk, but not yet irreversibly damaged tissue (penumbra), and is therefore one of the most clinically relevant situations for acute stroke imaging. In fact, it represents the biological substrate for treatment responsiveness established by DEFUSE and DAWN trials for reperfusion therapy [4, 5, 8]. The “slow progressor”—large mismatch with minimal infarct core

This situation reflects a very an extremely high mismatch ratio and is consistent with robust collateral circulation that prevents or significantly delays the conversion of penumbra into irreversible infarction [14]. The impact of Tmax threshold stratification on penumbral estimation

The interpretation of automated perfusion maps is closely related to the hemodynamic thresholds applied during post-processing. Variation in Tmax cutoffs may lead to significant discrepancies in the estimated volume of hypoperfused tissue. While permissive thresholds (e.g., Tmax > 4 s) may encompass areas of benign oligoemia, thereby overestimating the volume of tissue at risk, more stringent thresholds (Tmax > 10 s) isolate regions of critical hypoperfusion and are often indicative of failing collateral circulation [2, 3, 14].

This technical specificity is essential because automated software appears to provide an impression of objective accuracy but results in maps that are still dependent on the choice of thresholds. The “Warning Zone”—intermediate perfusion patterns (moderate mismatch)

A large proportion of patients do not conform to either of the clearly favorable or unfavorable profiles outlined above but instead exhibit intermediate perfusion profiles with moderate infarct core and variable peripheral hypoperfusion. These cases are among the most challenging encountered in routine practice, as the automated mismatch metrics may hover near the traditional inclusion thresholds. In this scenario, the decision for reperfusion therapy cannot rely solely on the software output but must also integrate other parameters such as severity of symptoms, location of vascular occlusion, involvement of eloquent areas, status of collateral perfusion, age, time of onset, and perfusion parameters [13].

This case (Fig. 6) underscores a fundamental tenet of modern stroke imaging: while RAPID provides the data, the radiologist needs to combine imaging results with the overall clinical situation. Diagnostic traps—motion artifacts and technical mimics

Despite the sophistication of automated algorithms, image quality remains the ultimate arbiter of diagnostic reliability. Motion artifacts are the “Achilles’ heel” of CTP, being among the most common causes of misleading maps with the generation of patchy, non-physiological perfusion defects that can mimic or exaggerate a true ischemic penumbra. These “pseudo-mismatch” patterns often manifest as irregular, curvilinear abnormalities that ignore established vascular territories. In addition, technical factors including poor timing of contrast bolus, incomplete acquisition, and noise influence both visual maps and numerical results [7, 10].

Critically, motion-induced noise not only creates false positives; it can also lead to a dangerous underestimation of the infarct core. As illustrated in Fig. 7, abrupt patient movement can distort the deconvolution process to the point of masking established ischemic changes. Discrepancy between CTP and MRI

While automated CT perfusion analysis provides vital guidance during the hyperacute triage of stroke, its findings do not always align with subsequent MRI. Recognizing the pathophysiological and timing-related reasons for CTP–MRI discordance is essential to avoid misinterpreting transient, evolving, or technical perfusion changes. For instance, a focal CTP abnormality may not always translate into restricted diffusion on MRI; such discrepancies often reflect transient ischemic attacks (TIA), early spontaneous reperfusion, or technical overestimation of the ischemic burden [9, 12, 18]. Conversely, the “ghost infarct core” phenomenon—where a large CBF-defined core on CTP significantly overestimates the final infarct volume seen on MRI—remains a critical pitfall in hyperacute imaging.

This modality gap, illustrated in Fig. 8, underscores the importance of regarding automated perfusion software as a decision-support tool rather than an absolute truth. Variations in imaging timing and collateral dynamics can create a “moving target” effect, where the physiological state of the brain evolves between the CT and MRI acquisitions. Infarct core estimation in deep gray matter is inherently confounded by high baseline attenuation, dense vascularization, and susceptibility to partial volume effects. These factors often render the fixed CBF < 30% threshold unreliable, potentially leading to spurious volumetric assessments in the hyperacute phase [7, 18].

The posterior fossa represents an even greater diagnostic challenge, where technical and physiological constraints converge. The brainstem and cerebellum are frequently affected by limited Z-axis coverage, beam-hardening artifacts from the petrous bone, and a lower signal-to-noise ratio. Furthermore, vertebrobasilar ischemia may produce only marginal Tmax delays, even in clinically catastrophic presentations, making the thresholds derived from anterior circulation data largely inapplicable [19–21].

As illustrated in this final composite analysis, a “negative” or “subtle” RAPID map in these territories must never override clinical judgment. When ischemia is suspected in the basal ganglia or posterior fossa, the radiologist must pivot to a multimodal integration of NCCT, CTA, and clinical findings. In these anatomical blind spots, MRI with DWI remains the indispensable arbiter, as CTP—and by extension RAPID—is prone to significant underestimation of the true ischemic burden [20].

Stroke mimickers

Stroke mimickers make up nearly half of suspected stroke admissions. They can present as functional disorders or symptoms of neurological or medical conditions. Despite careful assessment, many patients still receive unnecessary thrombolysis and intensive care. Accurate diagnosis is crucial, as recurrences are common [22]. Accurate diagnosis is important. CT perfusion could help in these events also when the results of software are negative.

Undetected seizure with post-ictal deficits and a Todd’s paresis, in which the patient presents with transient postictal focal weakness, is one of the most common presentations of a stroke mimic. Discussion

CTP analyzed with RAPID is now an essential component of modern stroke imaging and has contributed greatly to the shift from a rigid time-based treatment strategy to a tissue-clock paradigm. This evolution is grounded in landmark randomized trials demonstrating superior outcomes for patients selected via clinical-imaging mismatch and perfusion-based tissue viability, extending the therapeutic window for thrombectomy and thrombolysis up to 24 and 9 h, respectively [8].

Extensive literature evaluates the diagnostic performance of RAPID CTP, demonstrating high sensitivity (85–92%) and specificity (88–95%) for identifying core and penumbra when compared to expert radiological readings and follow-up MRI DWI. While its automated algorithms offer high inter-software reproducibility, diagnostic variability can still arise from patient-specific parameters such as contrast delivery dynamics, severe motion, and baseline vessel tortuosity [8].

Technically, CBF less than 30% represents one of the most widely accepted thresholds for estimating the infarct core. [2, 3, 23]. However, this threshold does not represent absolute biological truth, but it is more of an operational definition, based on validation studies. This metric is influenced by acquisition protocol, scanner characteristics, method of deconvolution, and patient hemodynamics [7]. Consequently, quantitative outputs must be contextualized within a broader radiological framework.

The progression of an infarct is fundamentally determined by the status of collateral circulation, which is indirectly reflected in RAPID variables, including Tmax delay and the hypoperfusion index [14]. Although RAPID provides standardized results, automated software may produce a “false sense of certainty” if technical confounders, such as motion artifacts, contrast bolus kinetics, or truncated acquisitions, are not meticulously scrutinized [10, 18].

A pivotal evolution in acute stroke management involves the treatment of patients with large infarct cores. Traditionally viewed as a contraindication to intervention, recent trials (e.g., SELECT2, TENSION) indicate that selected patients with extensive baseline injury may still experience significant clinical benefit from mechanical thrombectomy [17].

This emerging evidence challenges rigid exclusion based solely on a numerical volume, emphasizing that the “salvageability” of tissue is a dynamic biological continuum rather than a binary metric [24, 25].

Variations in quantitative estimates across different commercial software packages stem primarily from differences in mathematical deconvolution algorithms (e.g., delay-insensitive vs standard singular value decomposition), automated selection criteria for arterial input functions (AIF) and venous output functions (VOF), and subtle differences in thresholding logic [15].

In clinical practice, these technical discrepancies can lead to significant volume shifts (up to 15–20 mL), potentially reclassifying patients near trial inclusion cutoffs (such as a 70 mL core cutoff) and altering treatment decisions. Therefore, the radiologist should always review raw source data, correlate perfusion abnormalities with NCCT and CTA findings, and integrate the overall clinical status.

Furthermore, recent trials and meta-analyses (such as PRACTISE) have questioned whether mandatory CTP offers a tangible clinical benefit over high-quality NCCT and CTA alone in early therapeutic windows or unselected patient populations. While CTP remains indispensable for late-window selection and wake-up strokes, relying exclusively on CTP may unnecessarily delay reperfusion or exclude eligible candidates in straightforward early-window scenarios.

Conversely, CTP remains a high-value tool in patients with mild clinical deficits (low NIHSS), where a “nontrivial” proportion may still harbor substantial at-risk tissue that warrants intervention.

Conclusions

CT perfusion processed with RAPID software is a transformative decision-support tool designed to assist, rather than replace, the radiologist by streamlining the neuroradiological workflow, facilitating precise, tissue-based decision-making in acute stroke. By providing standardized hemodynamic metrics, it serves as a powerful adjunct for both radiologists and clinicians, particularly in extended therapeutic windows.

However, automated outputs must never be used as stand-alone criteria for treatment decisions, but should serve as objective data to be integrated into the broader clinical, morphological, and vascular picture under expert radiological oversight. Crucially, automated maps must be meticulously adjudicated to identify technical artifacts, threshold dependencies, and anatomical limitations. Ultimately, the most effective workflow synergizes technological speed with human diagnostic acumen, ensuring that therapeutic decisions remain firmly grounded in biological reality rather than algorithmic outputs. This review serves as a practical guide to mastering these imaging patterns, ultimately empowering neuroradiologists to minimize interpretive errors and deliver precise, individualized care for patients with acute ischemic stroke.
