Acetyltransferase NAT10 promotes aggressiveness of colorectal cancer cells via ac4C acetylation of SERPINA1 mRNA
Authors: Weicong Fan, Xinlei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundN-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) mediated N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification is a significant RNA modification in various tumors. This study aimed to investigate the underlying mechanism of NAT10 modulating the progression of colorectal cancer via mRNA ac4C modification.MethodsAberrant expressed NAT10 in colorectal cancer was identified from GSE37182 dataset. The ac4C modification levels in colorectal cancer cells were accessed by dot blotting assay, and the NAT10 levels were accessed by RT-qPCR. Cell viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration of colorectal cancer cells were evaluated to determine the regulatory role of NAT10 on cell aggressiveness. The interaction between NAT10 and Serine protease inhibitor A1 (SERPINA1) was verified using RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and ac4C RNA immunoprecipitation (ac4C RIP), and Luciferase reporter assay. Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains a leading cause of global cancer-related morbidity and mortality. A significant proportion of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, presenting with malignant features such as local invasion and distant metastasis, which complicates treatment and worsens prognosis [1–3]. Therefore, it is particularly important to study the suppression of the occurrence and development of colorectal cancer and to provide new therapeutic targets and tumor markers for colorectal cancer.

Epigenetic modifications, which regulate gene expression without altering DNA sequence, play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of colorectal cancer [4]. Among these, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) is an acetylation modification of N4 cytosine, and N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) is the only protein identified that has both an acetylase domain and a RNA binding domain, so it is considered to be a RNA ac4C modifying enzyme [5, 6]. It has been reported that NAT10 expression is significantly increased in colorectal cancer, and plays a role in the development and proliferation of colorectal cancer cancer cells by regulating the ac4C modification of FSP1 [7, 8]. Moreover, NAT10 is involved in colorectal cancer metastasis which could be regulated by GSK-3β [9]. These findings led us to hypothesize that NAT10 may function as an oncogenic driver in colorectal cancer through ac4C-dependent modification of key transcripts.

Serine protease inhibitor A1 (SERPINA1) gene encoding alpha-1-antitrypsin (AAT) protein is involved in the pathophysiology of various tumors [10–12]. It was reported that the SERPINA1 levels in the serum of CRC patients were higher than those in healthy groups [13, 14]. SERPINA1 regulated by CEBPB promotes the development of colorectal cancer by activating STAT3 pathway [15]. Despite these associations, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling SERPINA1 expression in colorectal cancer, particularly through epigenetic RNA modifications, remain largely unexplored.

This study aimed to investigate whether the oncogenic effects of NAT10 in colorectal cancer are mediated through ac4C modification and subsequent regulation of SERPINA1. We confirmed the upregulation of both NAT10 and ac4C in colorectal cancer. Functional experiments showed that NAT10 knockdown suppressed cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Mechanistically, NAT10 directly binds to and enhances ac4C modification of SERPINA1 mRNA, increasing its expression. These findings were corroborated in vivo, where pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 attenuated tumor growth and reduced SERPINA1 levels. Our work reveals the NAT10/ac4C/SERPINA1 axis as a crucial driver of colorectal cancer, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

The GSE37182 dataset, encompassing gene expression data from 90 colorectal tumor and 82 normal tissue samples profiled on the Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 expression beadchip, was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This dataset has been widely employed in recent studies to identify critical genes and dissect tumor-specific pathways in colorectal cancer [16, 17]. Samples within the dataset were assigned to two defined groups, “Tumor” and “Control,” based on their metadata. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the interactive online tool GEO2R with the default limma-based statistical package, applying an adjusted p-value threshold of < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|) of ≥ 1.0. The final list of DEGs meeting these thresholds was downloaded and categorized as either upregulated or downregulated in colorectal cancer tissues.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the expression levels of NAT10 and all other genes across all samples in the dataset. A volcano plot was generated to visualize positive and negative correlators. Subsequently, these NAT10-correlated genes were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the DAVID bioinformatics database. A hypergeometric test was applied, and pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The correlation between NAT10 and SERPINA1 expression was specifically analyzed and visualized using a scatter plot with a fitted regression line, and the Pearson correlation coefficient and its statistical significance were calculated to confirm their association within the GSE37182 cohort.

The expression of NAT10 and SERPINA1 in colorectal cancer were predicted by online database Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). The potential acetylation sites on SERPINA1 mRNA was predicted by an online tool PACES (http://rnanut.net/paces/).

Cell culture and treatment

The human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW480, along with the normal humanintestinal epithelium cell line NCM460, were used in this study. These cells were purchased from the ZYBio (ZY-C6439H; Shanghai, China), American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CCL-228; Manassas, VA, USA), and SUNNCELL (SNL-519; Wuhan, Hubei, China), respectively. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (HyClone; South Logan, UT, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco; Grand Island, NY, USA), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Gene knockdown, overexpression, and pharmacological inhibition

The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) plasmids targeting NAT10 (shNAT10) and MISSION® pLKO.1-puro Non-Target shRNA Control Plasmid DNA (shNC) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). For transfection, shRNA plasmids (at a final concentration of 30 nM) were mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and incubated for 20–30 min at room temperature. The complexes were then added to colorectal cancer cells seeded in 6-well plates at approximately 60–70% confluence.

For SERPINA1 overexpression, its full-length cDNA was cloned into lentiviral vector pHBLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-mCherry-T2A-Puromycin (HANBI, Shanghai, China) to over-express SERPINA1, empty vector was used as the negative control. Lentivirus and packaging vectors were transfected into 293T cells. Two days later, the supernatant containing lentivirus was collected after centrifugation. Colorectal cancer cells were then incubated with lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) overnight. The culture medium was changed 24 h later, and fluorescence photos were taken 48 h later to check the transfection efficiency, and RNA or protein was extracted from the cells for detection.

As for pharmacologically NAT10 inhibition, HCT116 and SW480 cells were treated with Remodelin (S7641; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) at a concentration of 20 μM for 48 h [18].

Cell counting kit (CCK)−8 assay

CCK-8 detection kit (BB-4202-500 T; BestBio, Nanjing, China) was used to detect the cell viability. 100 μL of cell suspension was seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2.5 × 104/well. 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and the plate was incubated for another 2 h. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

5‐Ethynyl‐2’‐deoxyuridine incorporation assay

BeyoClick™ EdU‐555 Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, China) was used after following the manufacturer’s protocol to determine cell proliferation. A total of 3 × 104 cells were cultured for 24 h in 96‐well plates. After a 2‐ h incubation with 20 μM EdU solution, colorectal cancer cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde then secured using Apollo Dye Solution and DAPI. Cells were then photographed and counted under a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Transwell assay

HCT116 and SW480 cells in each group were collected, and cell suspension with concentration of 2.5 × 105 cell/mL was prepared by serum-free medium. Cell suspension was inoculated into the upper chamber of Transwell chamber at 200 μL per well, and serum medium was added to the lower chamber with 600 μL per well. The cells were incubated for 24 h, fixed with paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. The number of migrating cells was observed by MD1000 optical microscope (Leica, Shanghai). Moreover, invaded cells were captured with the help of matrigel (Corning, USA). Matrigel dissolved at 4℃ overnight was diluted with serum-free culture solution at a volume dilution ratio of 1:9, and 40 μL of diluted matrigel was evenly laid on the cell membrane. Other experimental steps were the same as cell migration experiment.

ac4C detection by dot blotting assay

Total RNA was extracted from NCM460, HCT116 and SW480 cells by TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically. For the dot blot assay, 2 μg of total RNA per sample was denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, then immediately placed on ice. The denatured RNA was manually spotted onto a positively charged nylon membrane (Amersham Hybond-N +, GE Healthcare) using a dot blot manifold. After air-drying, the RNA was immobilized onto the membrane by ultraviolet cross-linking. Then the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit monoclonal ac4C antibody (ab252215; 1/200; Abcam; Cambridge, MA, USA). After extensive washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Signal was developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio-Rad) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (Bio-Rad). Signal intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH).

Quantitative real-time PCR

According to the instructions of the PrimeScript™ real time-PCR Kit (Takara; Tokyo, Japan), mRNA was extracted and transcribed into cDNA, and then PCR reaction was carried out with the help of FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Applied Biosystems). The reaction procedure was 2 min in 50℃, 10 min in 95℃, 15 s in 95℃, and 1 min in 60℃, with a total of 40 cycles. The reference gene in this experiment was beta-actin. Primer sequences are as follows (5'—> 3'): NAT10: F ATAGCAGCCACAAACATTCGC, R ACACACATGCCGAAGGTATTG; SERPINA1: f GAGCTGCTTATCCGCTTCTTC, R GGGGCGTACCACATGATCC.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RIP assay was performed according to the protocol of the Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA). Briefly, the supernatant of HCT116 cell lysates were collected after RIPA lysate treatment and centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 4℃). A portion of the cell lysate was saved as “Input” control. The remaining lysate was incubated with magnetic beads pre-conjugated with either anti-NAT10 antibody (ab194297, 1/2000; Abcam; Cambridge, MA, USA) or normal rabbit IgG (ab133470, 1/1000; Abcam) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After extensive washing, the RNA–protein complexes were eluted from the beads and treated with Proteinase K to digest the proteins. Co-precipitated RNA was then extracted and purified. The enrichment of specific target RNAs in the immunoprecipitates relative to the IgG control was quantified by RT-qPCR.

ac4C RNA immunoprecipitation (ac4C RIP)

The ac4C-RIP assay was performed to specifically enrich RNA fragments containing ac4C modifications. The anti-ac4C antibody (ab252215, 1/200, Abcam) was first covalently conjugated to magnetic beads. Total RNA extracted from HCT116 cells was fragmented to an average size of 100–500 nucleotides using RNA fragmentation buffer. The fragmented RNA was then incubated with the ac4C antibody-conjugated beads (or control IgG-conjugated beads) in RIP immunoprecipitation buffer overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the bound RNA was eluted, purified, and analyzed by RT-qPCR to determine the relative ac4C enrichment levels on SERPINA1 mRNA.

mRNA stability assay

HCT116 cells stably expressing shNC or shNAT10 together with vector, WT-SERPINA1, or Mut-SERPINA1 were inoculated in 12-well plates overnight and then treated with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (MedChemExpress) at 0, 4, 8 and 12 h. Total RNA was then isolated and the results were analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assay

Potential ac4C modification sites within the SERPINA1 mRNA (nucleotides 866–880), predicted by the PACES algorithm, were validated using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. DNA fragments corresponding to the wild-type (WT) SERPINA1 3’UTR and a mutant (Mut) version with the predicted ac4C motif deleted or point-mutated were synthesized and independently cloned into the multiple cloning site downstream of the firefly luciferase gene in the pGM-CMV-Luc vector (Yeasen Biotech). For the assay, HCT116 cells were seeded in 24-well plates. After 24 h, cells in each well were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with 900 ng of either the WT or Mut reporter plasmid, 40 ng of the Renilla luciferase control plasmid (pRL-TK, Promega), and 100 nM of either shNC or shNAT10. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity for each well to control for transfection efficiency. The normalized relative luciferase activity (Firefly/Renilla) in shNAT10 groups was then compared to that in the shNC control group for each reporter construct.

ac4C-site mutant SERPINA1 experiments

To determine whether the oncogenic function of SERPINA1 is dependent on its ac4C modification, a mutant SERPINA1 (Mut) construct was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted ac4C site (nucleotides 866–880) in the pHBLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-mCherry-T2A-Puromycin vector. HCT116 and SW480 cells were first transfected with shNC or shNAT10 plasmids (pLKO.1-puro) using Lipofectamine 2000 and selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 72 h to establish stable NAT10 knockdown cell lines. These cells were then infected with lentivirus expressing empty vector, WT-SERPINA1, or Mut-SERPINA1 in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL). After 48 h, mCherry fluorescence was verified by microscopy to confirm transduction efficiency.

Animal experiment

The animal study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medcona Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) (approval number: MDKN-2025-491). All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Female BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were purchased from Medcona Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle. To establish xenograft tumors, HCT116 cells (5 × 106) suspended in 100 μL of a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel (Corning) were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank of each mouse. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 50–100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned into two groups (n = 6 per group). Mice in the treatment group received Remodelin (S7641, Selleck Chemicals), prepared in a vehicle of 5% DMSO + 30% PEG300 + 65% PBS, administered via oral gavage at a dose of 100 mg/kg body weight daily for 4 consecutive weeks [18, 19]. Control group mice received an equal volume of the vehicle alone. The calculation of tumor volume was determined by using the formula tumor volume (mm3) = (length × width2)/2 every week. At the experimental endpoint, all mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were then excised, weighed, photographed, and processed for subsequent histological or molecular analysis.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis

The exfoliated tumors of the mice were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4 °C, followed by routine dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. For histological evaluation, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a commercial kit (Shanghai GEFAN Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against SERPINA1 (PA5-87677, 1/100, Invitrogen). Following incubation with an appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody, signals were visualized using a 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. All stained sections were scanned using a digital slide scanner.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was applied for data analysis. The results were expressed by mean ± SD. The independent sample T-test was used for the comparison between the two groups, and the one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA were used for the comparison between multiple groups. p < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

NAT10 expression and ac4C modification are up-regulated in colorectal cancer

Initial analysis of the GSE37182 dataset identified 382 significantly up-regulated and 265 significantly down-regulated DEGs in tumor tissues compared to controls (Fig. 1A). Analysis further indicated that NAT10, a key regulator of ac4C modification, was significantly elevated in the colorectal cancer group (Fig. 1B). Consistent with this, data from the GEPIA database showed that NAT10 mRNA expression was significantly higher in 275 colorectal cancer samples compared to 349 normal control samples (Fig. 1C). Experimentally, the total ac4C acetylation was significantly increased in the colorectal cancer cells compared with that in normal human intestinal epithelial cell line NCM460, as determined by dot blotting assay (Fig. 1D). NAT10 stimulates SERPINA1 expression via ac4 C modification

To explore the molecular mechanism, we performed correlation analysis using the GSE37182 dataset, identifying 3222 genes positively correlated and 3701 genes negatively correlated with NAT10 expression (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these genes revealed significant involvement in pathways related to malignant tumor growth, including cell proliferation and cell migration (Fig. 3B). SERPINA1, a gene enriched in both these pathways, showed a significant positive correlation with NAT10 expression (Fig. 3C). Data from the GEPIA database also confirmed that SERPINA1 expression was significantly elevated in 275 colorectal cancer samples compared to 349 normal controls (Fig. 3D). Given the reported pro-tumorigenic role of SERPINA1 in colorectal cancer [20], we investigated whether NAT10-mediated aggressiveness involves ac4C modification of SERPINA1. As shown in Fig. 3A, the abundance of ac4C modifications on SERPINA1 mRNA was monitored by ac4C RIP followed PCR method, and the results suggested that inhibition of NAT10 significantly down-regulated the ac4C enrichment of SERPINA1 compared with the shNC group (Fig. 3E). The RIP followed PCR experiment results suggested that there is a notable enrichment of NAT10 in conjunction with SERPINA1 mRNA compared with the IgG groups (Fig. 3F). Given that SERPINA1 expression was altered in conjunction with changes in NAT10 expression, we wondered whether SERPINA1 regulation by NAT10 was ac4C dependent. PACES algorithm identified a potential ac4C modification site (nucleotides 866–880) within the SERPINA1 gene sequence (Fig. 3G). Subsequently, the WT SERPINA1 containing the potential ac4C-modified regions and WT SERPINA1 without the potential ac4C-modified regions were constructed, and the relative luciferase activity of WT group was significantly suppressed in HCT116 cells with NAT10 knockdown. Conversely, the MUT group did not show any significant changes in this regard (Fig. 3H). Additionally, an mRNA stability assay revealed that WT-SERPINA1 exhibited a markedly longer half-life than Mut-SERPINA1. ac4C modification is required for SERPINA1-mediated oncogenic function

Afterwards, SERPINA1 expression was transiently over-expressed in both HCT116 and SW480 cells (Fig. 4A). To establish direct causality between ac4C modification and the malignant phenotype, we generated an ac4C-site mutant SERPINA1 and performed rescue experiments. In the shNC background, overexpression of either WT-SERPINA1 or Mut-SERPINA1 similarly enhanced cell viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration compared to the vector control (Fig. 4B–I), confirming that the Mut protein itself retains biological activity. Critically, in the shNAT10 background, WT-SERPINA1 partially reversed the suppressive effects of NAT10 knockdown, whereas Mut-SERPINA1 failed to rescue the inhibited aggressive phenotypes and remained comparable to the vector control (Fig. 4B–I). Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that acetyl-transferase NAT10, a key writer of the RNA modification ac4C, is highly expressed in colorectal cancer cells. Inhibition of NAT10 potently suppresses cell viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration of colorectal cancer cells in vitro and attenuates tumorigenesis of colorectal cancer cells in vivo. Mechanistically, we identified SERPINA1 as a novel downstream target, whose mRNA stability is enhanced through direct binding and ac4C modification by NAT10. The oncogenic function of this axis was confirmed by rescue experiments, where overexpression of SERPINA1 partially reversed the anti-tumor effects of NAT10 inhibition. To sum up, our results unveil the NAT10/ac4C/SERPINA1 pathway as a critical driver in colorectal cancer progression, positioning SERPINA1 as a potential predictive biomarker and therapeutic target.

The ac4C modification, catalyzed primarily by NAT10 in humans, is an important acetylation modification in eukaryotic genes reported in recent years. Initially characterized in tRNA and rRNA, ac4C is now established as a functional mRNA modification that enhances transcript stability and translation efficiency, thereby expanding the repertoire of epigenetic regulation in cancer [21, 22]. NAT10 can play a key role in promoting mRNA stability and increasing translation efficiency by modifying specific sequences of target mRNA in the ac4C manner [23, 24]. NAT10 influences diverse cellular processes including DNA damage response, autophagy, and cell cycle progression. Its dysregulation is frequently observed in malignancies [25–27]. In tumor cells, NAT10 is usually highly expressed, such as in hepatocellular carcinoma, where NAT10 is absent in the nucleolar and highly expressed in the cytoplasm and cell membrane, promoting cancer cell invasion and migration [28]. High expression of NAT10 inhibits the degradation of p53 by Mdm2 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells and promotes the proliferation of cells with mutant p53 [29]. In colorectal cancer, elevated NAT10 expression and its subcellular redistribution have been linked to increased invasiveness and poor prognosis, highlighting its tumorigenic role [9]. These studies indicate that the role of NAT10 is extensive and complex in tumor micro-environment. Our study corroborates these findings, showing that NAT10 is essential for the aggressive phenotype of colorectal cancer cells.

The oncogenic mechanism of NAT10 often involves the ac4C modification of specific mRNA targets to promote cancer hallmarks. For example, NAT10 mediates ac4C acetylation of COL5A1 mRNA to promote metastasis and EMT of gastric cancer [30]. NAT10 mediates the acetylation of BCL9L, SOX4 and AKT1 mRNA ac4C acetylation to promote the development of bladder cancer [23]. In line with this paradigm, our work identifies SERPINA1 as a functionally critical target of NAT10 in colorectal cancer. While SERPINA1 has been implicated as a biomarker in colorectal cancer [31], the regulatory mechanism governing its overexpression was unclear. We provide evidence that NAT10 binds to SERPINA1 mRNA and mediates its ac4C modification, thereby stabilizing the transcript and contributing to tumor aggressiveness. This establishes a direct mechanistic link between the NAT10/ac4C axis and a known oncogene in colorectal cancer pathogenesis.

From a translational perspective, the NAT10-specific inhibitor Remodelin presents a promising therapeutic avenue [32]. Originally identified for its role in ameliorating cellular senescence in progeria, our study shows that Remodelin effectively curbs the malignant behaviors of colorectal cancer cells by inhibiting NAT10, both in culture and in animal models. This repurposing of Remodelin underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting the NAT10/ac4C axis in colorectal cancer.

While this study elucidates a novel NAT10/ac4C/SERPINA1 axis, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the functional validation was primarily conducted in two cell lines and a xenograft model. Future investigations involving patient-derived organoids or genetically engineered mouse models would strengthen the clinical relevance of the findings. Secondly, the correlation between NAT10/SERPINA1 expression and clinical outcomes in this study was derived from public databases, which provide information on mRNA abundance rather than the actual level of ac4C modification. High expression of NAT10 and SERPINA1 in these datasets serves as a proxy indicator but does not directly reflect the epitranscriptomic status of individual patients. Therefore, the clinical significance of the NAT10/ac4C axis remains to be validated by direct quantification of ac4C modification levels in a cohort of clinical CRC tissues versus matched normal tissues. Thirdly, the global landscape of ac4C modification in colorectal cancer and the full spectrum of NAT10 target mRNAs remain to be comprehensively mapped via techniques like ac4C-seq. Future work should also aim to validate SERPINA1 as a circulating biomarker and explore synergistic combination therapies targeting this pathway. Additionally, although Remodelin is an established NAT10 chemical probe, recent reports indicate potential off-target effects. While our pharmacological and genetic data were concordant, future validation using a chemically distinct NAT10 inhibitor is warranted to fully consolidate these findings.
