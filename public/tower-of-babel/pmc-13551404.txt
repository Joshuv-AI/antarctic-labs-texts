Gold and Nickel Release From Gold‐Plated Earrings Under Cysteine‐Modified Artificial Sweat Conditions
Authors: Mikkel Bak Jensen, Malin Glindvad Ahlström, Morten S. Jellesen, Saman Nikpour, René Daniel Pütz, Yolanda Hedberg, Jeanne Duus Johansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundContact allergy to gold is frequently observed in patch testing, although with low clinical relevance. Nickel allergy, in contrast, is common, clinically relevant, and nickel release from jewellery remains a regulatory concern. Standardised artificial sweat tests show no detectable gold release from gold‐containing jewellery.ObjectivesTo assess surface composition and the release of nickel and gold from gold‐plated earrings under clinically relevant biochemical conditions.MethodsTen pairs of low‐cost gold‐plated earrings were analysed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy‐dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and X‐ray fluorescence. Metal release was assessed using a cysteine‐modified artificial sweat solution at 30°C for 168 h. Gold and nickel concentrations were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry.ResultsNickel release was detected in 10/10 earrings (median: 2.1 μg/cm2, range: 0.00032–10.0) frequently exceeding the EU regulatory limit. Gold release was detected in 4/10 earrings (median: 0.00092 μg/cm2, range: 0.00041–0.21). Gold was identified by cross‐sectional SEM/EDS in a subset of components (6/20) but was not detected by top‐down XRF analysis.ConclusionNickel release from gold‐plated earrings was widespread and often exceeded regulatory limits, indicating that gold plating does not reliably prevent nickel exposure. Gold release occurred in some earrings, but the clinical relevance remains uncertain.

Introduction

Contact allergy to gold is frequently observed in patch testing. Recent epidemiological data report positive patch test reactions in up to 14.1% of dermatitis patients [1]. In contrast, only a small proportion of these reactions are considered clinically relevant, ranging between 21.3% and 28.3% [2, 3]. Nickel allergy is common, with a prevalence of 17.5% in patients with dermatitis, and approximately 55.5% of the reactions considered clinically relevant [4]. Under standard conditions, metallic gold is expected to release only small amounts of ions, if any, due to its thermodynamic stability in water and air environments. Experimental studies using standardised artificial sweat have consistently reported no detectable gold release from gold‐containing jewellery materials [4]. Similar findings have been reported in later studies applying comparable test conditions [5, 6].

More recent experimental work has demonstrated that gold release may occur under specific chemical conditions. In particular, sulphur‐containing amino acids, such as cysteine, have been shown to promote gold dissolution in vitro [5]. When cysteine‐containing solutions are used, measurable gold release from gold items has been reported, whereas no release is detected in artificial sweat alone [5].

Earrings are a relevant source of gold exposure, as they are intended for prolonged and direct skin contact [7, 8]. However, data on the surface composition, elemental content and potential gold release from gold‐plated earrings, particularly low‐cost consumer products, is limited.

The aim of this study was to characterise the surface and bulk elemental composition of gold‐plated earrings and to assess whether gold release occurs under conditions simulating the biochemical environment of human skin contact.

Methods

Sample Collection

Ten pairs of earrings were purchased in February 2025 from an international online marketplace. The earrings were selected to represent widely available low‐cost consumer products intended for prolonged skin contact. All products were marketed by the manufacturer as gold‐plated, with reported gold contents ranging from 18 to 24 karat (K). The selection strategy was based on the presence of a gold‐plated surface, irrespective of the underlying material. Elemental Composition of Ear Needle and Decorative Part

Surface and bulk elemental composition of piercing components was assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy‐dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) prior to release testing. A total of 20 components were cut into cross‐sections and embedded for analysis of both ear needle and decorative part.

Following initial surface examination, selected components were sectioned using a Struers Accutom‐50 Precision Automatic Saw, embedded in an epoxy mounting material, prepared for cross‐sectional analysis. The embedded samples were ground sequentially using silicon carbide papers of decreasing grit size (320, 500, 1000, 2000 and 4000), each with a force of 5 N for 3 min using a Struers Rotopol‐22 polishing machine. The polished cross‐sections were subsequently analysed by SEM/EDS (ZEISS Sigma 6300) with a 25 kV acceleration voltage and 5000× magnification using EDS line scan (ZEISS SmartEDX) to assess elemental composition.

X‐Ray Fluorescence (XRF)

Elemental composition of the earring components was assessed by XRF. Measurements were performed using a top‐down approach, providing information on surface‐near elemental composition without cross‐sectional preparation of the samples. A benchtop XRF analyser (MAXXI 6, Oxford Instruments, Germany) was used to screen all components for the presence of nickel and gold. Measurements were performed at 45.0 kV and 0.053 mA with an acquisition time of 15 s. The estimated limit of detection (LOD) was approximately 0.01–0.05 weight percent (wt%).

SEM/EDS provides high spatial resolution with a relatively shallow information depth, typically below approximately 2 μm at 25 kV, depending on the material and beam conditions, allowing for localised surface or near‐surface compositional analysis. In contrast, XRF probes a larger subsurface volume rather than immediate surface, with an effective information depth generally ranging from approximately 10 μm up to 100–200 μm for heavier elements, thereby providing more bulk‐representative compositional information. Together, these techniques complement each other by enabling differentiation between surface and subsurface composition, while also highlighting that thin surface coatings may be detected by SEM/EDS but remain undetected or diluted in XRF measurements.

Release Test

Cleaning

Earrings were cleaned by wiping several times with ethanol‐dampened Chem‐Wipes to remove any surface contaminants. A calliper estimated their surface area, which ranged from 2 to 13 cm2.

The pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0 ± 0.2 using 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution. This pH was chosen because gold is more soluble at pH 8 than at a more acidic pH [9]. The solution was deaerated by purging argon through the solution before adding the L‐Cysteine. This prevented some of the expected self‐oxidation of cysteine to cystine.

The artificial sweat test solution was modified from the standardised artificial sweat solution according to ISO 105‐E04, by adding 0.05 g/L of L‐cysteine, which is 10 times lower concentrated than the L‐histidine, which is a relevant ratio for human sweat [10, 11]. The reason for the addition of L‐cysteine is prior knowledge of its accelerating effect on gold dissolution [5, 12]. The solvent was ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm2 resistivity, Millipore Reference, Canada). All chemicals were of analytical grade, except the acids (hydrochloric acid and nitric acid) used to prepare the standards and to digest the samples, which were of ultrapure grade (puriss. p.a.).

Test Procedure

Without any disassembly, two replicates for each earring type were fully immersed in the prepared solution (2–24 mL, depending on its dimensions), ensuring that all parts were in contact with the liquid. The samples were then placed in an incubator at 30°C for 168 h. The duration and temperature were based on the EN1811 standard. Similarly, blank solutions (solutions without any earrings) were also placed in the incubator. These blank solutions were used to check for background contamination and to prepare standards for ICP‐MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP Q). After the immersion test, 1 or 2 mL of ultrapure water (based on surface area) was used to rinse the samples, and this water was added to the test solution. Then, 100 μL of freshly prepared aqua regia (3:1 of HCl 37%, Sigma Aldrich and 67% nitric acid puriss. p.a. grade, Sigma‐Aldrich, respectively) was added to 3 mL of each test solution. The solutions were then microwave‐digested (Multiwave 7000, Anton Paar, Canada) at 220°C for 20 min to allow temperature ramping, followed by 10 min at 220°C and 140 bar. After digestion, another 500 μL of aqua regia was added to the solution, and the final volume was adjusted to 10 mL using ultrapure water. Dilution factors were accounted for, and the chloride concentration was kept constant in all standards, quality controls and solution samples, as it affects the plasma temperature of ICP‐MS. The limits of detection (LOD, based on three times the standard deviation of the blank) were 0.02 μg/L Ni and 0.08 μg/L Au, and the limits of quantification (LOQ = 10 × LOD) were 0.2 μg/L Ni and 0.8 μg/L Au, respectively. If the sample concentration exceeded the calibration range, the sample was diluted and reanalysed. Samples were also reanalysed if the corresponding quality control deviated more than 20% from the expected value. An internal Sc standard was used for drift control.

The amount of released metals was calculated using Equation (1). (1) Amount of released metalsμgcm2=cSampleμgL−cBlankμgL×DF×VLAcm2 where csample and cblank are the sample concentration and blank concentration, respectively. The specimens' exposure volume V varied, as did the area A.

To avoid contamination from the surroundings, all containers and materials in contact with solutions were acid‐cleaned in 10% HNO3 for at least 24 h, then rinsed with ultrapure water, and air‐dried.

Statistical Analysis

All descriptive statistics were performed in RStudio (version 2025.05.0, Build 496).

Results

Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy Dispersive X‐Ray Spectroscopy

Gold was detected by SEM/EDS in 10 out of 20 analysed components (50%). Among gold‐positive components, the median surface gold content was 3.18 wt% (range: 0.672–36.7 wt%). Nickel was detected in 17 out of 20 components (85%), with a median nickel content of 27.3 wt% among nickel‐positive components (range: 0.08–95.5 wt%). High nickel contents were predominantly observed in components consistent with stainless steel‐based bulk materials. Individual SEM/EDS results for all components are presented in Table 2 and Data S1. Thin nickel interlayers were found in seven out of 12 examined (cross‐sectioned) components. Gold‐containing surface layers were found in five out of 12 examined components, of which three components had thicker, micrometre‐thick layers (IDs 4.1, 4.2 and 9.2).

Nickel was detected in 13 out of 20 components (65%), with a median nickel content of 20.8 wt% among nickel‐positive components (range: 5.7–58.0 wt%). Discussion

Nickel release was detected from all gold‐plated earrings and frequently exceeded the EU regulatory limit, indicating that gold plating does not reliably prevent nickel exposure. Our investigation also suggests that a nickel interlayer is commonly applied. Gold release was detected from four out of 10 gold‐plated earrings using a modified artificial sweat solution containing L‐cysteine. Gold release levels were low, with a maximum of 0.21 μg/cm2 over 168 h, corresponding to approximately 50 μg/L in the test solution. In contrast, previous studies applying standardised artificial sweat without sulphur‐containing compounds have consistently reported no detectable gold release from gold‐containing jewellery, with reported detection limits below 0.01 μg/cm2 [4, 5]. The present findings support experimental evidence that sulphur‐containing amino acids promote gold dissolution and suggest that standard artificial sweat tests may underestimate gold exposure from jewellery. Concomitant nickel and gold allergy has been reported in patients with contact dermatitis, including jewellery‐related cases, with co‐sensitisation observed in approximately 10%–39% of patients [2, 13]. However, the clinical implication cannot be determined from the present studies.

Gold allergy is common in patch testing, although clinical relevance is generally considered low [1]. Clinical studies have shown that exposure to gold jewellery can provoke dermatitis in a subset of gold‐allergic individuals [7]. The detection of gold release under biochemically relevant conditions provides a plausible exposure pathway that may contribute to sensitisation or elicitation in selected individuals. However, the clinical relevance of the low levels of gold release observed in this study cannot be determined.

The modified test solution was based on prior experimental work demonstrating a pronounced effect of cysteine on gold dissolution. Svedman et al. reported no gold release in artificial sweat alone, whereas substantial gold release occurred when cysteine was added, with release levels exceeding those observed in the present study [5]. Using a lower cysteine concentration, reflecting the relative composition of human sweat, we observed measurable gold release from consumer earrings marketed as gold‐plated. These findings indicate that even low concentrations of sulphur‐containing compounds may be sufficient to induce gold release under clinically relevant biochemical conditions. The modified test solution differs from the EN 1811 artificial sweat solution in several ways; (1) it is pH 8 instead of pH 6.5, which is expected to result in higher gold but lower nickel release compared to EN 1811 [9]; and (2) it contains two amino acids (histidine and cysteine) but no urea and no lactic acid. The latter is important for gold, since the sulphur‐containing cysteine is expected to significantly increase the release of gold compared to the other substances. It forms a gold‐cysteine complex [14], which results in a chemical dissolution process [5].

Surface analysis detected gold by cross‐sectional SEM/EDS analysis in a subset of components, whereas gold was not detected by top‐down XRF analysis. This discrepancy is most likely explained by very thin gold coatings below the detection limits of these surface analytical techniques. Importantly, gold release occurred from samples in which gold layer thickness were below detectable levels, indicating that undetectable gold does not exclude the potential for gold ion release.

The findings are consistent with the presence of ultra‐thin ‘gold‐flash’ coatings, commonly used in low‐cost jewellery. Such coatings are often discontinuous and may be only a few nanometres thick, which can place them below the detection limits of techniques such as XRF and challenge their cross‐sectional visualisation even in SEM/EDS. In addition, these thin layers may not provide an effective barrier to underlying nickel‐containing substrates, thereby contributing to the observed nickel release.

Nickel was detected by surface analysis in the majority of components and was released from all earrings. Based on manufacturer‐reported information, only earrings described as nickel, steel or stainless steel would be expected to contain nickel, whereas titanium alloy, copper, silver and gold should not. According to this classification, only 1 of the 10 manufacturer material descriptions would predict the presence of nickel. Nevertheless, nickel was released from all earrings and detected in 15 of 20 analysed components, often because of a nickel interlayer necessary to plate the gold or golden‐looking coating on. Nickel release exceeded gold release in all samples and surpassed the EU regulatory limit of 0.2 μg/cm2/week for nickel release in six out of 10 samples [15]. The observed nickel concentrations would be expected to yield a positive dimethylglyoxime spot test result [16], which might be helpful for patient guidance. This is concerning and supports ongoing evidence that earrings and piercings remain key contributors to nickel contact allergy [8, 17].

In contrast to gold, surface detection of nickel corresponded well with measured nickel release. For gold, the lack of detection by top‐down XRF despite measurable release suggests that very thin gold layers could fall below the detection limits of this method. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that gold or silver‐plated jewellery does not reliably prevent nickel release, and that product information may not accurately reflect material composition [18].

This reflects analytical uncertainty and the fact that consumers cannot readily assess plating quality.

The present study was designed as a proof‐of‐concept focusing on low‐cost jewellery for gold ion‐release testing. Future studies should apply the same experimental approach to higher‐quality gold‐plated and solid gold jewellery to assess whether gold release occurs under comparable conditions and whether release patterns differ according to material quality and alloy composition.

Overall, nickel release was considerable and exceeded gold release in all samples. The sample with the highest gold release showed comparatively low nickel release, despite minimal surface‐detectable gold. In contrast, surface detection of nickel corresponded well with measured nickel release.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has limitations. The sample size was small and restricted to low‐cost consumer products. Release testing was performed under static in vitro conditions and does not account for mechanical wear, intermittent exposure or interindividual variation in skin chemistry. In addition, very thin gold coatings may be difficult to detect by SEM/EDS. Thin gold layers may not be visible in cross‐sectional analyses, even at high magnifications, and top‐down XRF analysis may underestimate or entirely miss the presence of thin gold layers.

Conclusion

Gold‐plated earrings released nickel in all samples, frequently exceeding the EU regulatory limit, including products marketed as nickel‐free or based on materials such as titanium alloy. Gold release was detected in a subset of earrings under cysteine‐modified artificial sweat conditions, but the clinical relevance of the low release levels remains uncertain.
