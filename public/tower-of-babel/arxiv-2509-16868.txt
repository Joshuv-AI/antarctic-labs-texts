On some log-concavity properties of the Alexander-Conway and Links-Gould invariants Thanks: Key words and phrases: Links–Gould invariant, Alexander polynomial, alternating links, unimodality, log-concavity, Lorentzian polynomials.
Matthew Harper, Ben-Michael Kohli, Jiebo Song, Guillaume Tahar

Abstract
. The Links–Gould invariant LG ⁡ ( L , t 0 , t 1 ) \mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{1}) of a link L L is a two-variable quantum generalization of the Alexander–Conway polynomial Δ L ​ ( t ) \Delta_{L}(t) and has been shown to share some of its most geometric features in several recent works. Here we suggest that LG \mathrm{LG} likely shares another of the Alexander polynomial’s most distinctive - and mysterious - properties: for alternating links, the coefficients of the Links-Gould polynomial alternate and appear to form a log-concave two-indexed sequence with no internal zeros. The former was observed by Ishii for knots with up to 10 crossings. We further conjecture that they satisfy a bidimensional property of unimodality, thereby replicating a long-standing conjecture of Fox (1962) regarding the Alexander polynomial, and a subsequent refinement by Stoimenow. We also point out that the Stoimenow conjecture reflects a more structural phenomenon: after a suitable normalization, the Alexander polynomial of an alternating link appears to be a Lorentzian polynomial. We give compelling experimental and computational evidence for these different properties.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Alexander and Links-Gould polynomials of links

The Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{L}
is an isotopy invariant of links
L
valued in
\mathbbm{Z}[t^{\pm 1}]
and defined up to units. It was first defined in 1928 by James Waddell Alexander
[
Ale28
]
and was the first polynomial invariant of knots. Remarkably, the Alexander polynomial remained the only known polynomial link invariant until Vaughan Jones discovered his eponymous polynomial half a century later
[
Jon85
]
. Designed to distinguish links up to isotopy, the Alexander polynomial also relates to some fundamental geometric properties of knots and links. For example, the degree of
\Delta_{L}(t)
is a lower bound for the 3-genus of
L

[
Sei35
]
, and the Alexander polynomial of a fibered knot is monic
[
Neu65
,
Rap60
,
Sta61
]
. The Alexander invariant is also relevant to study the four-dimensional properties of a knot. For example, for a topologically slice knot
K
,
\Delta_{K}(t)
takes a particular form known as the Fox-Milnor condition
[
FM66
]
.

It is also worth noting that
\Delta_{L}(t)
can be constructed as a quantum link invariant, that is, a Reshetikhin–Turaev-type invariant where it occurs in its palindromic normalization as the Alexander–Conway polynomial. It arises in this way by considering representations of either: the Hopf algebra
U_{q}{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}
at fourth roots of unity, or the Hopf superalgebra
U_{q}\mathfrak{gl}(1|1)
, see
[
Mur92
,
Vir06
]
. In the latter perspective, the Alexander polynomial is the first in the family of Links–Gould invariants
\mathrm{LG}^{m,n}(L;t_{0},t_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}[t_{0}^{\pm 1},t_{1}^{\pm 1}]
obtained from the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction applied to representations of the Hopf superalgebras
U_{q}\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, see
[
LG92
,
DWLK99
,
DW01
]
. The simplest case after the Alexander polynomial is derived from
U_{q}\mathfrak{gl}(2|1)
. We shall denote the associated invariant
\mathrm{LG}^{2,1}
by
\mathrm{LG}
and refer to it as the Links–Gould polynomial.

Although
\mathrm{LG}
is not yet known to have a classical geometric interpretation that would relate it even more closely to the Alexander polynomial, recent results show that it reproduces some of the Alexander polynomial’s most distinctive characteristics. Specifically, the degree of the Links–Gould polynomial gives a lower bound for the genus of a knot by work of two of the authors
[
KT
]
. Also, the
\mathrm{LG}
invariant of a fibered link is
\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1}]
-monic following the recent work of López-Neumann and van der Veen
[
LNvdV
]
. These two properties imply and improve upon similar statements for the Alexander polynomial. This is because
\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{1})
specializes to
\Delta_{L}(t)
in at least two different ways
[
DWIL05
,
Koh16
,
KPM17
]
:

\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},-t_{0}^{-1})=\Delta_{L}(t_{0}^{2})\,,\qquad\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{0}^{-1})=\Delta_{L}(t_{0})^{2}.

(1)

It is therefore reasonable to imagine that other properties of the Alexander polynomial should have their counterparts for the Links-Gould invariant.

1.2.
The Alexander polynomial of alternating links

A class where the Alexander polynomial has an interesting behavior is that of alternating links: the Alexander polynomial of an alternating link is alternating with no internal zeros by works of Crowell
[
Cro59
]
and Murasugi
[
Mur58
]
. This means that if we consider the Conway normalization of
\Delta_{L}(t)
, we can write:

\Delta_{L}(t)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n}a_{k}t^{k},\quad\text{with }a_{l}\cdot a_{l+1}<0\text{ for all }l\in[\![-(n-1),n-1]\!]\,.

Moreover, Fox conjectured that the absolute values of the coefficients of the Alexander polynomial of an alternating link form a unimodal sequence
[
Fox61
]
, i.e.

|a_{-n}|\leq|a_{-n+1}|\leq\cdots\leq|a_{k}|=|a_{k+1}|=\cdots=|a_{m}|\geq|a_{m+1}|\geq\cdots\geq|a_{n}|\text{, }-n\leq k\leq m\leq n\,.

Stoimenow refined the statement by conjecturing that the sequence should be log-concave with no internal zeros
[
Sto05
]
. This in turn would imply that the sequence is trapezoidal:

|a_{-n}|<|a_{-n+1}|<\cdots<|a_{k}|=|a_{k+1}|=\cdots=|a_{m}|>|a_{m+1}|>\cdots>|a_{n}|\text{, }-n\leq k\leq m\leq n\,.

Different forms of the Fox conjecture have been proved over the years for many infinite families of knots and links, see
[
Par78
,
Har79
,
Mur85
,
OS03
,
Jon10
,
Sto16
,
AC21
,
HMV24
,
AJK
,
KMP
]
. A summary of these studies and their scope can be found in
[
AJK
]
. It also includes several recent works
[
AC21
,
HMV24
,
AJK
,
KMP
]
. The general conjecture, however, remains unsolved to this day.

1.3.
Similar properties for the Links-Gould polynomial of alternating links?

In his early foundational studies on the Links-Gould invariant, Ishii conjectured that
\mathrm{LG}
displays the same “alternating” behavior as the Alexander polynomial on alternating knots.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
Ish06
]
)
.

The LG polynomial
\mathrm{LG}(K;t_{0},t_{1})=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}t_{0}^{i}t_{1}^{j}
of an alternating knot
K
is “alternating”:

a_{ij}a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\geq 0\quad\text{if }i+j-i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}\text{ is even, and}\quad a_{ij}a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\leq 0\quad\text{otherwise}
.

Ishii verified Conjecture
1.1
for all prime knots with up to
10
crossings. Using the fact that
\mathrm{LG}
is equivalent by a change of variables to the Garoufalidis–Kashaev
V_{1}
-polynomial of links
[
GK
,
GHK+c
,
GHK+a
]
, the computations of Garoufalidis–Li for
V_{1}
show that the Ishii conjecture is true for all prime knots with up to
16
crossings, see
[
GL
]
.

In this work we conjecture that once again the Links-Gould invariant
\mathrm{LG}
and the Alexander polynomial follow similar patterns. First, Conjecture
1.1
can be made more precise, echoing the behavior of the Alexander polynomial.

Definition 1.2
.

A two-indexed sequence
(a_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
is said to have
no interior zeros
if

\text{Conv}(\text{Supp}(a_{ij}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\text{Supp}(a_{ij})\,,

where
\text{Supp}(a_{ij})=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,a_{ij}\neq 0\}
and
\text{Conv}(X)
is the convex hull of a subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Conjecture 1.3

(strong version of the Ishii conjecture)
.

The LG polynomial
\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{1})=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}t_{0}^{i}t_{1}^{j}
of an
alternating link

L
with
\mu
components is “alternating” with

a_{ij}=(-1)^{\mu+i+j+1}\,|a_{ij}|

for all
i,j
. Moreover,
(a_{ij})
has no interior zeros.

Remark 1.4
.

Conjecture
1.3
is
not
true for other two-variable generalizations of the Alexander polynomial, i.e. for the two variable polynomial invariant
\Delta_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}
derived from representations of
U_{q}\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
at a fourth root of unity, studied by one of the authors
[
Har
, Figure 10]
.

We further propose that
\mathrm{LG}
should satisfy bidimensional properties of log-concavity and unimodality, thereby generalizing, at least in spirit, the Fox and Stoimenow conjectures.

Definition 1.5
.

A sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
log-concave
if, for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:
a_{i-1}\,a_{i+1}\leq a_{i}^{2}
.

Remark 1.6
.

Log-concavity, as well as its generalizations we will define hereafter, is invariant under multiplication by a monomial.

Definition 1.7
.

We will refer to a bidimensional sequence
(a_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
as
2d log-concave
if for every
i,j,k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
:

a_{i+k,j+l}\,a_{i-k,j-l}\leq a_{ij}^{2}\,.

We understand 2d log-concavity as being indicative of the sequence being log-concave “in all possible directions”. In particular if a sequence is 2d log-concave, its restriction to any row, column or diagonal in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a log-concave singly-indexed sequence.

Conjecture 1.8

(2d log-concavity)
.

For any alternating link
L
, the absolute values of the coefficients of
\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{1})
form a 2d log-concave sequence (with no interior zeros).

Definition 1.9
.

We say that a two-indexed sequence
(a_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
unimodal
if, for any
K\geq 0
, the two-indexed sequence
(a_{ij}\,\mathds{1}_{|a_{ij}|\geq K})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
has no interior zeros.

Conjecture 1.10

(unimodality)
.

For any alternating link
L
, the 2d sequence of absolute values of the coefficients of
\mathrm{LG}(L;t_{0},t_{1})
is unimodal.

We will give evidence in support of these assumptions. Specifically, we show that Conjectures
1.3
,
1.8
, and
1.10
hold for all alternating prime knots with up to 12 crossings, as well as for several infinite families of alternating links.

Note that despite the obvious similarities between Conjectures
1.3
,
1.8
, and
1.10
and comparable statements for the Alexander polynomial, these conjectures are not straightforward generalizations of the well-known properties for
\Delta_{L}(t)
. None of the specializations from Equation (
1
) allow the properties of the Alexander polynomial on alternating links to be understood as consequences of the properties of the Links–Gould polynomial. This was pointed out in
[
Koh18
]
about the Ishii conjecture, and remains true here. This suggests that unlike the genus bounds and fiberedness criteria, these properties for alternating links are more likely the trace of a deep structural behavior common to both link invariants.

Let us point out that in our journey to understand whether the
\mathrm{LG}
polynomial is more closely related to the Jones polynomial or to the Alexander polynomial, these new hypothetical properties of the Links-Gould polynomial reinforce its proximity with
\Delta_{L}(t)
. Indeed, the Jones polynomial of an alternating link is alternating
[
Thi87
]
, however, it is not always unimodal. Consider, for example,

V_{8_{10}}(t)=-t^{-6}+2t^{-5}-4t^{-4}+5t^{-3}-4t^{-2}+5t^{-1}-3+2t-t^{2}.

1.4.
A strengthening of the Fox-Stoimenow conjecture for the Alexander polynomial

In recent work, Hafner, Mészáros, and Vidinas proved the Fox–Stoimenow conjecture for special alternating links
[
HMV24
]
by showing that a well chosen normalization and homogenization of the Alexander polynomial is Lorentzian, a property introduced and studied by Brändén and Huh
[
BH20
]
. Observe that being Lorentzian is a very strong assumption and, as we will see, it is not satisfied by the
\mathrm{LG}
invariant for any obvious choice of variables, not even for all
special
alternating links. However, in the case of
\Delta_{L}(t)
, we show that for
any
alternating link, the Stoimenow extension of the Fox conjecture is equivalent to the divided power normalization of the Alexander polynomial being Lorentzian. We refer to the Definitions
3.5
and
3.8
for precise definitions of the (divided power) normalization and Lorentzian property.

Proposition 1.11

(Lorentzian property for Alexander)
.

The homogenized Alexander polynomial of an alternating link
\mathrm{Homog}(\Delta_{L}(-t))
is denormalized Lorentzian if and only if the absolute values of the coefficients of the Alexander polynomial of the link form a log-concave sequence (with no internal zeros).

That the Alexander polynomial is denormalized Lorenztian follows from the structure of
\Delta_{L}(t)
for
L
an alternating link and from Proposition
1.11
, which is an application of the following elementary algebraic result.

Proposition 1.12
.

For
P(t)
a
one-variable
polynomial with nonnegative real coefficients, its sequence of coefficients is log-concave if and only if
Q(t,z):=\mathrm{Homog}(P)
is denormalized Lorentzian.

Thus, following Stoimenow
[
Sto05
]
the Alexander polynomial satisfies the Lorentzian property for all alternating knots with up to 16 crossings, and this is conjecturally true for all alternating links.

We conclude this introduction with a question.

Question 1.13
.

Is the homogenized Links–Gould polynomial of alternating links denormalized Lorentzian after a proper change of variables?

3.
Log-concavity, unimodality, Lorentzian polynomials: properties, examples, counter-examples

3.1.
Log-concavity, unimodality

First we give some elementary properties of log-concave sequences that will be helpful in the next section. The following definitions, as mentioned in the introduction, are recalled below.

We say that a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
log-concave
if, for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

a_{i-1}\,a_{i+1}\leq a_{i}^{2}\,.

Likewise, we refer to a bidimensional sequence
(a_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
as
2d log-concave
if for every
i,j,k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
:

a_{i+k,j+l}\,a_{i-k,j-l}\leq a_{ij}^{2}\,.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
a log-concave sequence of non-negative real numbers with no internal zeros, the sequence
(a_{i}/a_{i-1})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is non-increasing.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a log-concave sequence of non-negative real numbers with no internal zeros, then the sequence is trapezoidal and, in particular, it is unimodal.

Proof.

The sequence
(r_{i})=(a_{i}/a_{i-1})
is non-increasing on the interval where it is well-defined. So for some
-\infty\leq p\leq q\leq+\infty
one can write:

r_{i}>1\iff i\leq p\,,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad r_{i}<1\iff i\geq q\,.

This precisely means that
(a_{i})
is trapezoidal and therefore unimodal.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Corollary
3.2
is not true if one does not assume that the sequence does not have internal zeros. Indeed, a sequence can have multiple local maxima and still be log-concave, as long as the “bumps” are far apart enough. Consider for example:

(a_{i})=(\ldots,0,1,0,0,1,0,0,\ldots)\,.

Corollary 3.4
.

[Generalized log-concavity inequality]
Suppose
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a log-concave sequence of non-negative real numbers with no internal zeros. For all
i,k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

a_{i-k}\,a_{i+k}\leq a_{i}^{2}\,.

Proof.

If any of the three terms is zero, then the inequality obviously holds, because the sequence has no internal zeros. So we can suppose that all three terms are nonzero. In that case, the inequality we wish to prove can be written as follows:

\frac{a_{i+k}}{a_{i}}\,\leq\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i-k}}\,.

But we know that the sequence
(a_{i}/a_{i-1})
is non-increasing and thus:

b_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i-k}}=\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i-1}}\times\frac{a_{i-1}}{a_{i-2}}\times\ldots\times\frac{a_{i-k+1}}{a_{i-k}}

is the product of
k
positive non-increasing sequences. So
(b_{i})
is a non-increasing sequence and we have:

b_{i+k}\leq b_{i}\,,\quad\text{ i.e. }\quad\frac{a_{i+k}}{a_{i}}\,\leq\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i-k}}\,.\qed

3.2.
Lorentzian polynomials

We define the
d-th discrete simplex

\Delta_{m}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
by

\Delta_{m}^{d}=\{{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}~|~\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=d\,\}}\,.

For any
1\leq i\leq m
, we denote by
\epsilon_{i}
the unit vector in the i-th coordinate.

The notation
w^{\alpha}
stands for
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\partial^{\alpha}=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
. We also introduce
\alpha!=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}!
.

Definition 3.5
.

An
m
-variable homogeneous polynomial
P
of degree
d
with nonnegative real coefficients is
strictly Lorentzian
if for any
\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d-2}
,
\partial^{\alpha}P
is a nondegenerate quadratic form of signature
(1,m-1)
.

Definition 3.6
.

A subset
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
is
\mathrm{M}
-convex
if for any index
i
and any
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma
whose
i
-th coordinates satisfy
\alpha_{i}>\beta_{i}
, there exists an index
j
such that

\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}\,,\quad\alpha-\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{j}\in\Sigma\,,\quad\beta-\epsilon_{j}+\epsilon_{i}\in\Sigma\,.

The set of degree
d
homogeneous polynomials in
m
variables with nonnegative coefficients and an
\mathrm{M}
-convex support is denoted by
\mathrm{M}_{m}^{d}
.

Definition 3.7
.

A homogeneous real polynomial is
Lorentzian
if it is the limit of strictly Lorentzian polynomials of the same degree and in the same number of variables.

Brändén and Huh
[
BH20
]
prove that in the space of degree
d
homogeneous polynomials in
m
variables with real coefficients, the set of Lorentzian polynomials
\mathrm{L}_{m}^{d}
can be described completely:
\mathrm{L}_{m}^{2}
is the set of degree
2
homogeneous polynomials with nonnegative coefficients and at most one positive eigenvalue. For
d>2
, we have

\mathrm{L}_{m}^{d}=\{P\in\mathrm{M}_{m}^{d}:\partial^{\alpha}P\in\mathrm{L}_{m}^{2}\text{ for any $\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d-2}$}\}\,.

Definition 3.8
.

Given a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
m
variables
P=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}}c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha}
, we define its
normalization

\mathcal{N}(P)
by setting

\mathcal{N}(P)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}}\frac{c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\,.

As shown in Proposition 4.4 of
[
BH20
]
, we have the following.

Proposition 3.9
.

Given a homogeneous polynomial
P=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}}c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha}
, if
\mathcal{N}(P)
is Lorentzian, then the coefficients of
P
satisfy

c_{\alpha+\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}}c_{\alpha-\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{j}}\leq c_{\alpha}^{2}
.

Let us state a few stability properties satisfied by Lorentzian polynomials.

Proposition 3.10

(
[
BH20
, Corollary 3.8]
)
.

Given two homogeneous polynomials
P
and
Q
, if
\mathcal{N}(P)
and
\mathcal{N}(Q)
are Lorentzian, then
\mathcal{N}(PQ)
is also Lorentzian.

Note that the converse does not hold. For example, let
f=6x^{3}+2xy^{2}+6ay^{3}
with
\mathcal{N}(f)=x^{3}+xy^{2}+ay^{3}
. Then
\mathcal{N}(f^{2})
is Lorentzian, but for
a\in(-\frac{\sqrt{6}}{18},0)
,
\mathcal{N}(f)
is not Lorentzian.

Proposition 3.11
.

If
P(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
is a homogeneous polynomial and
w_{k}^{-1}P(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
is also a polynomial, then
\mathcal{N}(P)
is Lorentzian if and only if
\mathcal{N}(w_{k}^{-1}P)
is Lorentzian.

Proof.

The reverse implication follows from Proposition
3.10
. To prove the forward implication, assume
\mathcal{N}(P)
is Lorentzian and write
P=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}}c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha}
. Then

\mathcal{N}(w_{k}^{-1}P)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}}\frac{c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha-\epsilon_{k}}}{(\alpha-\epsilon_{k})!}\,.

By assumption
c_{\alpha}=0
whenever
\alpha_{k}=0
, thus the above sum is well-defined. Equivalently, we assume that
\alpha\geq e_{k}
. Note that
\mathcal{N}(w_{k}^{-1}P)
is of degree
d-1
. Fix
\gamma\in\Delta_{m}^{d-3}
. Then

{\partial^{\gamma}}[\mathcal{N}(w_{k}^{-1}P)]=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\Delta_{m}^{d}\\
\alpha\geq\gamma+\epsilon_{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{\alpha}w^{\alpha-\epsilon_{k}-\gamma}}{(\alpha-\epsilon_{k}-\gamma)!}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\Delta_{m}^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}+\beta}w^{\beta}}{\beta!}

is a degree two polynomial. The matrix entries of the Hessian
\tilde{H}^{\gamma}
of
{\partial^{\gamma}}[\mathcal{N}(P)]
are
(\tilde{H}^{\gamma})_{ij}=c_{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}+\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{j}}
.

A similar computation shows that the Hessian
H^{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}}
of
\partial^{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}}[\mathcal{N}(P)]
satisfies
H^{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}}=\tilde{H}^{\gamma}
. Since
H^{\gamma+\epsilon_{k}}
has at most one positive eigenvalue, so does
\tilde{H}^{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{N}(w_{k}^{-1}P)
is Lorenztian.
∎

In addition, the next result has been proved as Lemma 4.8 in
[
BLP23
]
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Set
P(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
a homogeneous polynomial and
Q(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m-1}):=P(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m-1},w_{m-1})
. If
\mathcal{N}(P)
is Lorentzian, then so is
\mathcal{N}(Q)
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let

.

A homogeneous polynomial

Proof of Proposition
1.12
.

We write

P(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}\,t^{k}\,,\quad x_{k}\geq 0\,,\quad x_{d}\neq 0\,.

To translate the Lorentzian condition in terms of coefficients of the polynomial, we can now compute the partial derivatives of

Q(t,z)=\mathcal{N}\circ\mathrm{Homog}(P)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{d}\frac{c_{k}\,t^{k}z^{d-k}}{k!(d-k)!}\,.

Set
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
0\leq a\leq d-2
and
a+b=d-2
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{a}Q}{\partial t^{a}}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=a}^{d}\frac{c_{k}}{(k-a)!(d-k)!}\,t^{k-a}z^{d-k}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{a+b}Q}{\partial z^{b}\partial t^{a}}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=a}^{a+2}\frac{c_{k}}{(k-a)!(a+2-k)!}\,t^{k-a}z^{a+2-k}=\frac{c_{a}}{2}z^{2}+c_{a+1}tz+\frac{c_{a+2}}{2}t^{2}\,.

The Hessian matrix for
\frac{\partial^{a+b}Q}{\partial z^{b}\partial t^{a}}(t,z)
relative to the derivatives
\partial z,\partial t
is:

\begin{pmatrix}c_{a}&c_{a+1}\\
c_{a+1}&c_{a+2}\end{pmatrix}

with characteristic polynomial and discriminant

\displaystyle\chi_{a}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-(c_{a}+c_{a+2})\lambda+c_{a}c_{a+2}-c_{a+1}^{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Delta_{a}=(c_{a}-c_{a+2})^{2}+4c_{a+1}^{2}\geq 0\,.

The Lorentzian property for
Q(t,z)
means that the smaller root of
\chi_{a}
is nonpositive. In other words,
c_{a}+c_{a+2}-\sqrt{\Delta_{a}}\leq 0
for any
0\leq a\leq d-2
. Since all the
c_{i}
are nonnegative, this last inequality is equivalent to
c_{a}c_{a+2}\leq c_{a+1}^{2}
, which exactly means that the sequence
(c_{i})
of coefficients of
P
is log-concave. This proves Proposition
1.12
.
∎

Here we consider various choices of variables in the presentation for the Links–Gould polynomial of the trefoil. For each choice, we exhibit a choice of derivative and corresponding quadratic form whose Hessian has more than one positive eigenvalue.

\displaystyle\mathrm{LG}(\mathsf{3_{1}})(t_{0},t_{1})
