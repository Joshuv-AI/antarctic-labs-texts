Identification of severe eosinophilic asthma in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps who are awaiting sinus surgery
Authors: Drew Davies, Alexander Royston, Elizabeth Hudson, James Davidson, Edward Chisholm, Dinesh Shrikrishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is a common comorbidity within severe asthma populations; its presence is associated with increased severity of disease and eosinophilic inflammation that may require biologic therapies.ObjectiveWe sought to identify potential patients with severe eosinophilic asthma in a population of patients with CRSwNP who were awaiting functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) for nasal polyps.MethodsWe conducted a service evaluation of patients with CRSwNP who were awaiting FESS in a district general hospital. We screened electronic records for eosinophil levels, previous asthma diagnosis, symptoms, and medical treatment.ResultsOf the 709 patients on the otolaryngology waiting list, 123 patients with CRSwNP were awaiting FESS. Of those patients, 74 had previous eosinophilia (serum eosinophil count ≥ 0.3 x109/L), 32% were female, and their mean age was 53.5 years (SD 15 years). Of those 74 patients, 36 had a preexisting diagnosis of asthma, of whom 61% were at step 4 or 5 of the Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines, with 18% receiving 2 or more courses of oral steroids in the previous 12 months, 41% unknown to local asthma services, and 27% receiving biologic therapy for asthma. In addition, of those with eosinophilia with no previous asthma diagnosis who were awaiting FESS for nasal polyps (38 of 74), 26% had documented symptoms of breathlessness or wheeze.ConclusionWe have identified patients with CRSwNP awaiting nasal polyp surgery who have eosinophillic asthma that may benefit from treatment optimization and review in a severe asthma service. Proactive identification of patients with severe asthma and comorbid CRSwNP who are currently awaiting FESS may widen access to asthma optimization and biologic medications.

Introduction

Biologic medication has revolutionised the care of patients with severe asthma over the past decade. However, access to these medications for eligible patients remains a challenge, particularly owing to geographic barriers1 and long waiting times to access speciality services.2 In the United Kingdom, only 16% of patients with asthma who are eligible for a biologic receive one, with a wide regional variability of 2% to 29%.1 A previous cohort study identified a hidden population of patients with severe asthma in primary care, with 8% having potential severe asthma, of whom 72% were unknown to local specialist services.2 It is therefore important that we actively identify this hidden population of patients to optimize their disease.

Previous screening programs have focused on identifying severe asthma within primary care cohorts; however, patients with comorbid chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps (CRSwNP) are a population that to our knowledge, has not previously been screened for severe asthma and biologic uptake. Patients diagnosed with asthma and comorbid CRSwNP are more likely to have severe asthma with higher levels of airway inflammation and worse asthma control.3 These patients have an elevated type 2 inflammatory signal, with raised levels of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and eosinophils.4 This inflammation can be targeted by biologics designed to treat severe asthma, with raised eosinophil counts in these patients associated with an improved response to treatment and better outcomes.5 Patients with comorbid severe asthma and nasal polyp disease who are currently under the care of otolaryngology services may therefore represent an eligible cohort for targeted therapies.

We sought to identify potential severe eosinophilic asthma in patients with CRSwNP who are awaiting surgery in a secondary care setting who may benefit from targeted biologic therapy for their airway disease. We collaborated with our local otolaryngology department at Musgrove Park Hospital, a severe asthma center in the Southwest of the United Kingdom, which provides both otolaryngology and severe asthma services for Somerset (population ∼550,000 in 2026).

We conducted a service evaluation of patients with CRSwNP who were awaiting functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) for nasal polyps at our severe asthma center. We screened the hospital records of 709 patients on the active otolaryngology surgery waiting list. We included all patients aged 18 years or older who were awaiting FESS for CRSwNP and identified those with a documented eosinophil level of 0.3 × 109/L in the past year. This cutoff was chosen because it is part of the eligibility criteria for prescription of anti–IL-5 biologic therapy for patients with severe asthma in the United Kingdom.6 We used electronic patient records to establish the number of patients with a diagnosis of asthma and documented their medication history and oral steroid use. These records included primary care referral letters to otolaryngology as well as local hospital clinic letters. We documented comorbidities, laboratory parameters, medications, and previous contact with respiratory services. We also screened (via key word searches) the records of all patients who did not have a diagnosis of asthma for symptoms of dyspnea, cough, or wheeze.

Results and discussion

We identified 123 patients who were awaiting FESS for CRSwNP. A total of 74 patients had a previous documented eosinophil level of 0.3 × 109/L or higher. Of the 74 patients with raised eosinophil levels, 36 had a coexisting diagnosis of asthma, with 64% (23 of 36) having receive 1 or more courses of steroids in the previous 12 months. Of those 36 patients, 22 (61%) were receiving treatment for their asthma at Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA)7 treatment step 4 or 5, with 18% having received 2 or more courses of oral steroids in the previous 12 months, indicating potential severe asthma (Table II). Of the 22 patients at treatment GINA step 4 or 5, 9 (41%) were unknown to our severe asthma service and 6 (27%) were already taking a biologic medication for their asthma. This service evaluation identified a cohort of patients awaiting nasal polyp surgery who had potential severe eosinophilic asthma and comorbid CRSwNP, highlighting a population that would benefit from treatment optimization and consideration for biologic therapy. In the United Kingdom, National Institute for Clinical Excellence reimbursement for ear, nose, and throat services to commence biologic therapy for patients with refractory CRSwNP has only recently been agreed to.8 This contrasts with respiratory services, for which National Institute for Clinical Excellence reimbursement has been in place for several years for those patients with severe asthma who have comorbid CRSwNP.6 There is a need for integrated pathways to identify this patient cohort and ensure that they are reviewed in a multidisciplinary severe asthma setting.

A high proportion of patients with a diagnosis of asthma were at GINA treatment step 4 or 5 and had received oral steroids. Although our methodology did not allow us to quantify exacerbations directly by utilizing oral steroid use as a proxy, we can infer a significant proportion of patients with potential severe disease. On the basis of the current UK guidelines, patients with severe asthma requiring 3 or more courses of steroids would be eligible for biologics. The fact that our data showed a relatively low number of patients receiving 2 or more steroid courses may reflect an underreporting of symptoms, thus highlighting the need for asthma specialist input and education.

We have also identified patients who had documented respiratory symptoms alongside elevated eosinophil counts and nasal polyp disease but no formal asthma diagnosis. In our study, only 12% of patients had testing for fractional exhaled nitric oxide level performed previously, and only 32% had spirometry recorded, irrespective of previous asthma diagnosis. A baseline elevated type 2 inflammatory signal has been found to be associated with an increased risk of future asthma exacerbations,9 and these patients may therefore represent a high-risk undiagnosed population. This highlights a need to encourage routine respiratory symptom evaluation and fractional exhaled nitric oxide level testing in otolaryngology clinic assessments in patients with CRSwNP.

A limitation of this study is the use of retrospective primary care data, where it can be unclear if prescribed inhaled therapy is being taken as directed and patients could be undertreated for their asthma exacerbations. In addition, by focusing on type 2–high profile patients, who have historically benefited the most from biologic therapy, we potentially missed patients with type 2–low asthma who may also benefit from treatment optimization. Furthermore, we could not differentiate whether oral steroid courses were being administered for exacerbations of CRSwNP or for asthma, although in this case the underlying pathology is similar and access to biologic treatment is important in treating both eosinophilic conditions.

Collaboration between respiratory and otolaryngology multidisciplinary teams is vital to ensure that patients with comorbid CRSwNP and severe asthma have both their lung and nasal pathology appropriately reviewed and treated. Screening for asthma symptoms in this population is important, as their asthma is often more severe and poorly controlled. Promotion of assessment of asthma symptoms in otolaryngology clinics and creation of pathways with active identification of cases for referral to specialist asthma clinics could help in integration of services to better identify and treat this cohort of patients. These patients may well be suitable for biologic medications, and cross-specialty working will help facilitate wider access to these life-changing treatments.
