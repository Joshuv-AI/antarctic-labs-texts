Fel’s Conjecture on Syzygies of Numerical Semigroups
Evan Chen †, Chris Cummins *, GSM *, Dejan Grubisic *, Leopold Haller *, Letong Hong ⋄ \diamond, Andranik Kurghinyan *, Kenny Lau †*, Hugh Leather *, Seewoo Lee †, Aram Markosyan *, Ken Ono †, Manooshree Patel *, Gaurang Pendharkar *, Vedant Rathi *, Alex Schneidman *, Volker Seeker *, Shubho Sengupta ⋄ \diamond, Ishan Sinha *, Jimmy Xin *, Jujian Zhang †*

Abstract
. Let S = ⟨ d 1 , … , d m ⟩ S=\langle d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\rangle be a numerical semigroup and k ⁡ [ S ] k[S] its semigroup ring. The Hilbert numerator of k ⁡ [ S ] k[S] determines normalized alternating syzygy power sums K p ​ ( S ) K_{p}(S) encoding alternating power sums of syzygy degrees. Fel conjectured an explicit formula for K p ​ ( S ) K_{p}(S) , for all p ≥ 0 p\geq 0 , in terms of the gap power sums G r ​ ( S ) = ∑ g ∉ S g r G_{r}(S)=\sum_{g\notin S}g^{r} and universal symmetric polynomials T n T_{n} evaluated at the generator power sums σ k = ∑ i d i k \sigma_{k}=\sum_{i}d_{i}^{k} (and δ k = ( σ k − 1 ) / 2 k \delta_{k}=(\sigma_{k}-1)/2^{k} ). We prove Fel’s conjecture via exponential generating functions and coefficient extraction, isolating the universal identities for T n T_{n} needed for the derivation. The argument is fully formalized in Lean/Mathlib, and was produced automatically by AxiomProver from a natural-language statement of the conjecture.

1.
Introduction

Numerical semigroups form a classical meeting point of
additive number theory and commutative algebra.
Throughout this paper, let

S\coloneq\left\langle d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\right\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

denote a semigroup with
m\geq 1
generators (
\gcd(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})=1
), and let

\Delta\coloneq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus S

denote the gap set (finite by definition).
We always assume
0\in S
.
The semigroup ring

k[S]\cong k[t^{d_{1}},\dots,t^{d_{m}}]

is a one-dimensional graded domain whose Hilbert series records
fine arithmetic data of
S
through
\Delta
.
The interplay between gaps, Hilbert series, and graded free resolutions
links numerical semigroups to topics such as Frobenius-type problems,
restricted partition functions, and toric/monomial methods.
We refer the reader to

[
9
]
for background on numerical semigroups and

[
8
]
for commutative-algebraic and combinatorial aspects
of semigroup rings and their resolutions.

In recent work, Fel
[
5
,
7
]
introduced alternating power sum invariants
built from the degrees of syzygy in the Hilbert numerator of
k[S]
,
and conjectured a uniform closed formula (
1.6
below)
expressing certain normalized combinations
K_{p}(S)
in terms of gap power sums

(1)

G_{r}(S)\coloneq\sum_{g\in\Delta}g^{r}

and universal symmetric polynomials
T_{n}
.
In this paper, we prove Fel’s conjecture for all
p\geq 0

by a direct formal-power-series argument.

1.1.
Hilbert series of numerical semigroups, and alternating syzygy

We begin by recalling the relevant mathematical objects related to
k[S]
.

Definition 1.1
.

The
Hilbert series
for
S
is defined as the ordinary generating
function
H_{S}(z)\coloneq\sum_{s\in S}z^{s}
.
It can be expressed as a rational function

H_{S}(z)=\frac{Q_{S}(z)}{\prod_{1}^{m}(1-z^{d_{i}})}

for some polynomial
Q_{S}(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
, called the
Hilbert numerator
of
S
.

The Hilbert numerator
Q_{S}
takes a specific form: it can be written as
an alternating sum of monomials

(2)

Q_{S}(z)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{\beta_{1}}z^{C_{1,j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{\beta_{2}}z^{C_{2,j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{\beta_{3}}z^{C_{3,j}}+\dots+(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\beta_{m-1}}z^{C_{m-1,j}}

where
\beta_{i}>0
are called the
partial Betti numbers

and the
C_{i,j}
are called the
degrees of syzygies
,
as defined in
[
5
, §2]
.
(We note in
loc. cit.
that there are additional constraints on
\beta_{i}

and
C_{i,j}
, but they are not needed for the subsequent
Definition

1.2
.)

The degrees of syzygy allow for the statement of several surprising results.
For example, define the alternating power sums of syzygy degrees as follows.

Definition 1.2
.

For
r\geq 0
define the sum of the alternating
r
th powers by:

\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\beta_{i}}(-1)^{i}C_{i,j}^{r}.

Definition 1.3
.

Additionally, define the shorthand

\pi_{m}\coloneq d_{1}\dots d_{m}.

One of the main theorems of
[
5
]
computes
\mathbb{C}_{r}
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
5
, Theorem 1]
)
.

We have
\mathbb{C}_{0}(S)=1
,
\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)=0
for
1\leq r\leq m-2
, and

\mathbb{C}_{m-1}(S)=(-1)^{m}(m-1)!\pi_{m}.

1.2.
Fel’s sequence
T_{n}
of universal symmetric polynomials

What about
\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)
for
r\geq m
?
In
[
7
]
, these
\mathbb{C}_{n}(S)
were reparametrized in terms of
a certain explicit invariant denoted
K_{p}(S)
.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
7
, §3]
)
.

There exists an invariant
K_{p}(S)
such that for every
p\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{C}_{m+p}(S)=(-1)^{m}\pi_{m}\cdot\frac{(m+p)!}{p!}\cdot K_{p}(S).

This invariant
K_{p}
is a linear combination of the
G_{0}
,
G_{1}
, …,
G_{p}

defined by (
1
).

The quantity
G_{i}
are called the
genera
or
gap power sums
of
S
.
(In particular,
G_{0}=|\Delta|
is called the genus of
S
.)
In
[
7
, equation (23)]
, several
K_{p}
are established by direct calculation.
Let
\sigma_{k}\coloneq\sum_{1}^{m}d_{i}^{k}
and
\delta_{k}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k}-1}{2^{k}}
;
then the formula for
0\leq p\leq 3
are:

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=G_{0}+\delta_{1}

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle=G_{1}+\tfrac{\sigma_{1}}{2}G_{0}+\tfrac{3\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}}{6}

\displaystyle K_{2}

\displaystyle=G_{2}+\sigma_{1}G_{1}+\tfrac{3\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}}{12}G_{0}+\tfrac{\delta_{1}(\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2})}{3}

\displaystyle K_{3}

\displaystyle=G_{3}+\tfrac{3\sigma_{1}}{2}G_{2}+\tfrac{3\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}}{12}G_{1}+\tfrac{\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2})}{8}G_{0}+\tfrac{15\delta_{1}^{4}+30\delta_{1}^{2}\delta_{2}+5\delta_{2}^{2}-2\delta_{4}}{60}.

These formulas for
K_{p}
have been written in a suggestive form,
where a special sequence of polynomials

T_{0}(\sigma)=1,\quad T_{1}(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2},\quad T_{2}(\sigma)=\frac{3\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}}{12},\quad T_{3}(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2})}{8},\;\dots

seem to repeat in the formulas.
These polynomials are defined in
[
6
]

and we describe these
T_{n}
fully in
Definition

2.1
and
Definition

2.2
.
Throughout, we refer to them as
universal symmetric polynomials
.
Fel conjectured that the
T_{n}
describe
K_{p}(S)
fully:

Conjecture 1.6

(
[
7
, Conjecture 1]
)
.

For every
p\geq 0
, we have

K_{p}(S)=\sum_{r=0}^{p}\binom{p}{r}T_{p-r}(\sigma)G_{r}(S)+\frac{2^{p+1}}{p+1}T_{p+1}(\delta).

Two additional conjectures about
T_{n}

(not directly related to semigroups
per se
)
are also stated in
[
6
]
,
which we describe in the next section.

1.3.
The reappearance of
T_{n}
in relation to Ramanujan’s
U_{2n}(q)
and other contexts

This section provides some historical background
showing three appearances of the universal symmetric polynomials
T_{n}

outside the context of numerical semigroups, following
[
1
]
.
This theory will not be used in our proof itself of our main theorem,
but establishes the contexts in which
T_{n}
appears elsewhere in mathematics.

1.3.1.
Restricted partitions

For positive integers
d_{1}
, …,
d_{m}
, let
W(s,d_{\bullet})

denote the restricted partition function
(that is, the number of integer partitions of
s\geq 0
into
d_{i}
).
Sylvester
[
10
]
decomposes
W(s,d_{\bullet})

as the sum of so-called
Sylvester waves

W_{q}
:

W(s,d_{\bullet})=\sum_{\exists i:q\mid d_{i}}W_{q}(s,d_{\bullet}).

In particular, the
first Sylvester wave

W_{1}
is of special interest:
it has the form (see
[
5
, (3.16) and (7.1)]
)

W_{1}(s,d_{\bullet})=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}f_{r}(d_{\bullet})s^{m-1-r}\quad f_{r}(d_{\bullet})\coloneq\left(\sigma_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{i}d_{i}\right)^{r},

with each
\mathcal{B}_{i}
denoting a Bernoulli umbra,
meaning
(\mathcal{B}_{i}d_{i})^{n}\coloneq B_{n}d_{i}^{n}
.

However, it turns out that
f_{r}
are actually given up to sign changes by
the universal symmetric polynomials
T_{n}
:

Theorem 1.7

(
[
6
, Conjecture 1]
or
[
1
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

For
n\geq 2
, we have

f_{n}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})=T_{n}(\sigma_{1},-\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\dots,\sigma_{n-1},-\sigma_{n})

where only the signs
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{n}
are flipped on the right hand side.

1.3.2.
Recursion via zig-zag numbers

Fel also observed that the
T_{n}
seem to satisfy a recursion relation
related to the tangent/zig-zag numbers
A_{2j+1}
,
satisfying

\sec x+\tan x=\sum_{j}A_{j}\cdot x^{j}/j!.

Namely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.8

(
[
6
, Conjecture 2]
or
[
1
, Theorem 1.7]
)
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have

\frac{T_{2n+1}}{T_{1}^{2n+1}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}A_{2j+1}\binom{2n+1}{2j+1}\frac{T_{2n-2j}}{T_{1}^{2n-2j}}.

1.3.3.
Ramanujan’s
q
-series

Recently a new striking appearance of
T_{n}
appeared in quasimodular forms,
which led to the resolution of the preceding two conjectures.
Consider Ramanujan’s
q
-series

\displaystyle U_{2n}(q)

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{1^{2n+1}-3^{2n+1}q+5^{2n+1}q^{3}-7^{2n+1}q^{6}+\dots}{1-3q+5q^{3}-7q^{6}+\dots}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}(2k+1)^{2n+1}q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}}{\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}(2k+1)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}}

which is a weight
2n
quasimodular form
[
3
]
.
As in
[
2
, §3]
, introduce the generating function

\Omega(X)\coloneq\sum_{n\geq 0}U_{2n}(q)\cdot\frac{X^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}=\frac{\sinh X}{X}\prod_{j\geq 1}\left(1-\frac{4q^{j}\sinh^{2}X}{(1-q^{j})^{2}}\right)

and define
Y_{n}(q)
according to

\sum_{n\geq 0}Y_{n}(q)\cdot\frac{X^{n}}{n!}=\exp\left(\frac{\theta(q)^{2}X}{2}\right)\Omega\left(\frac{X}{2}\right)

where
\theta(q)\coloneq\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-q^{2k})(1+q^{2k-1})^{2}

is the weight
1/2
theta function.
Then we can describe
Y_{n}
with the following proposition.

Proposition 1.9

(
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

We have a formula

Y_{n}(q)=\frac{1}{2^{n}(n+1)}\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{n+1}{2k+1}\theta(q)^{2n-4k}U_{2k}(q).

Each
Y_{n}
then has a canonical symmetric function avatar; it is defined by

\widetilde{Y}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\coloneq\frac{1}{2^{n}(n+1)}\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{n+1}{2k+1}\sigma_{1}^{n-2k}\Psi(U_{2k}(q))

where
\Psi
is a symmetric function representation
defined in
[
1
, equation (1.9)]
,
extended with
\Psi(\theta(q)^{2})=\sigma_{1}
.
These avatars turn out to coincide again with Fel’s universal symmetric polynomials:

Theorem 1.10

(
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

For integers
n,k\geq 1
,

T_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\widetilde{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},0,0,0,\dots).

In particular, this interpretation was used to prove
both
Theorem

1.7
and
Theorem

1.8
.

Background

Here we recall the relevant nuts and bolts that are required to prove Fel’s Conjecture.

2.1.
Syzygy degrees

On the side of syzygy degrees, we content ourselves to note that
K_{p}(S)

was given a complete definition in
Section

1
.
Indeed, we have

•

The Hilbert numerator
Q_{S}(z)
is given by
Definition

1.1
.

•

By combining (
2
) and
Definition

1.2
,
we may write
\mathbb{C}_{r}
directly in terms of the coefficients of
Q_{S}
:

(3)

\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{r}\cdot[z^{n}]\,(1-Q_{S}(z))

This definition is somewhat ad hoc and perhaps not the “morally correct” one
because it does not refer to partial Betti numbers
\beta_{i}
at all;
however, for the purposes of proving
Theorem

1.11
it is sufficient.

•

Finally,
Theorem

1.4
can be rearranged to say

(4)

K_{p}(S)=\frac{\mathbb{C}_{m+p}(S)}{(-1)^{m}\pi_{m}\cdot\frac{(m+p)!}{p!}}.

The rest of the section is dedicated to describing
T_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

in terms of
x_{i}
,
\sigma_{k}
, and
\delta_{k}
.

2.2.
Universal symmetric polynomials, in terms of
x_{i}

It remains to give a full definition of the universal symmetric polynomials
T_{n}
.
Fel’s original definition
[
6
, §2]
begins by defining

\displaystyle P_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}x_{i}^{n}-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}+x_{j})^{n}+\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq m}(x_{i}+x_{j}+x_{k})^{n}

\displaystyle-\dots+(-1)^{m}(x_{1}+\dots+x_{m})^{n}

for any integers
m\leq n
, and then setting

(5)

T_{n-m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\coloneq\frac{P_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})}{x_{1}\dots x_{m}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{m+1}n!}{(n-m)!}}.

We prefer to rewrite (
5
) more compactly as an exponential generating function.

Definition 2.1
.

Define the generating function

A(t)\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{e^{x_{i}t}-1}{x_{i}t}\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]].

Then we have

T_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\coloneq n!\cdot[t^{n}]\,A(t).

To see that this definition matches (
5
), it suffices to note that

\displaystyle(-1)^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(e^{x_{i}t}-1)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-e^{x_{i}t})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}e^{x_{i}t}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}e^{(x_{i}+x_{j})t}

\displaystyle-\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq m}e^{(x_{i}+x_{j}+x_{k})t}+\dots

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}e^{(x_{1}+\dots+x_{m})t}.

Hence the coefficient of
t^{n}
in the numerator of
A(t)
in
Definition

2.1

equals

(-1)^{m+1}n!\cdot P_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

for
n\geq m
.
Adjusting by the denominator of
x_{1}\dots x_{m}\cdot t^{m}
gives (
5
).

2.3.
Universal symmetric polynomials, in terms of
\sigma_{k}

It is clear that
T_{n}
is symmetric in the
x_{i}
’s and of degree
n
.
Hence we will rewrite it in the power basis
\sigma_{k}\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{k}
.
For example, rather than writing

\displaystyle T_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}+\dots+x_{m}}{2}

\displaystyle T_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{3(x_{1}+\dots+x_{m})^{2}+(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{m}^{2})}{12}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

we can write the more compact

\displaystyle T_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}

\displaystyle T_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}}{12}

viewing
T_{i}
as a polynomial in
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{i})
.
This has the advantage that it suppresses the dependence on
m
from the definition.
Further examples for
n\leq 7
are recorded in
Section

3.1
.

2.4.
Universal symmetric polynomials, in terms of
\delta_{k}

Fel’s conjecture involves replacing
T_{n}(\sigma)
with
T_{n}(\delta)
,
by replacing each
\sigma_{k}
with
\delta_{k}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k}-1}{2^{k}}
.
We’ll also write an exponential generating function for
T_{n}(\delta)
:

Definition 2.2
.

Retaining
A(t)
from
Definition

2.1
, define

B(t)\coloneq\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}A(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]].

Then we have

T_{n}(\delta)\coloneq\frac{n!}{2^{n}}\cdot[t^{n}]\,B(t).

To see why
B(t)
works,
introduce symbols
y_{1}
, …,
y_{m}
satisfying

(6)

x_{1}^{k}+x_{2}^{k}+\dots+x_{m}^{k}=y_{1}^{k}+\dots+y_{m}^{k}+1\qquad k\geq 1.

(Strictly speaking, we should really use infinitely many variables,
so that
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
, … are all independent.
Alternatively one may fix
n
, choose
m>n
, and work modulo
t^{m+1}
.)
Then applying
Definition

2.1
and (
6
) means

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{e^{y_{i}t-1}}{y_{i}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})}{n!}t^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T_{n}(\sigma-1)}{n!}t^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{e^{y_{i}t-1}}{y_{i}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T_{n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m},1)}{n!}t^{n}=\sum\frac{T_{n}(\sigma)}{n!}t^{n}=A(t).

Here
\sigma-1
stands for
(\sigma_{1}-1,\sigma_{2}-1,\dots)
.
Hence, we conclude that

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{T_{n}(\sigma-1)}{n!}t^{n}=\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}A(t)=B(t).

Finally, it is clear from homogeneity that

T_{n}(\delta)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}T_{n}(\sigma-1)
, as needed.

3.2.
Worked example
S=\left\langle 3,5\right\rangle

The semigroup
S=\left\langle d_{1},d_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle 3,5\right\rangle

has gap set

\Delta=\{1,2,4,7\}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle G_{r}

\displaystyle=1+2^{r}+4^{r}+7^{r}

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=3^{k}+5^{k}

\displaystyle\delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{3^{k}+5^{k}-1}{2^{k}}.

We can compute the Hilbert series as

H_{S}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}-(z+z^{2}+z^{4}+z^{7})=\frac{1-z^{15}}{(1-z^{3})(1-z^{5})}.

This means the Hilbert numerator is
Q_{S}(z)=1-z^{15}
and we obtain

\mathbb{C}_{n}(S)=15^{n}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle K_{p}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{C}_{2+p}(S)}{(-1)^{2}\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot(p+1)(p+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{15^{p+2}}{15(p+1)(p+2)}=\frac{15^{p+1}}{(p+1)(p+2)}.

Remark 3.1
.

In general when
m=2
, the “obvious” patterns above hold:
given
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1

we generally have
Q_{S}(z)=1-z^{d_{1}d_{2}}

and

K_{p}(S)=\frac{(d_{1}d_{2})^{p+1}}{(p+1)(p+2)}.

We note this also follows from
[
7
, equation (63)]
.

4.
Proof of
Theorem

1.11

Fix the semigroup
S=\left\langle d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\right\rangle
above.
The main idea of the proof is to convert all the data into exponential generating functions.
Then purely algebra manipulation will be enough to imply the desired result.
This has already been done for
A(t)
and
B(t)
,
but the
G_{r}(S)
and
\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)
have not been translated yet.
In general we will use the letter
t
for the variable
in an exponential generating function and
z
for an ordinary generating function,
so we can summarize the strategy as replacing
z
with
e^{t}
whenever possible.

For example, here is how to work with
\mathbb{C}_{n}(S)
with an exponential generating function.

Lemma 4.1
.

The exponential generating function for
\mathbb{C}_{n}(S)
is
1-Q_{S}
. That is,

\mathbb{C}_{n}(S)=n!\cdot[t^{n}](1-Q_{S}(e^{t})).

Proof.

By comparison with (
3
).
Suppose we write
1-Q_{S}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}z^{k}
, so that

\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^{r}\cdot c_{k}.

In that case, we have

\displaystyle 1-Q_{S}(e^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}e^{kt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}k^{n}\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}\cdot k^{n}\right)\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{C}_{r}(S)\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.\qed

Similarly, we can convert
G_{r}(S)
into an exponential generating function as follows.

Definition 4.2
.

The
gap polynomial

\Phi_{S}(z)
is defined as

\Phi_{S}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}-H_{S}(z)=\sum_{g\in\Delta}z^{g}.

Lemma 4.3
.

The exponential generating function for
G_{n}(S)
is given by
\Phi_{S}(e^{t})
, that is

\Phi_{S}(e^{t})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G_{n}(S)\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

Proof.

By direct computation, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{S}(e^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\Delta}(e^{t})^{g}=\sum_{g\in\Delta}e^{tg}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\Delta}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(gt)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{g\in\Delta}\frac{(gt)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n\geq 0}G_{n}(S)\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\qed.

Definition 4.4
.

The
product polynomial

P_{S}(z)
is the denominator in our definition of
Q_{S}(z)
, that is

P_{S}(z)\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-z^{d_{i}}).

Hence, we have
H_{S}(z)=\frac{Q_{S}(z)}{P_{S}(z)}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

P_{S}(e^{t})=(-1)^{m}\pi_{m}\cdot t^{m}\cdot A(t).

Proof.

By definition

P_{S}(e^{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-e^{d_{i}t}\right)

so this follows by comparison to
Definition

2.1
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

We have

\frac{P_{S}(e^{t})}{1-e^{t}}=(-1)^{m+1}\pi_{m}\cdot t^{m-1}\cdot B(t).

Proof.

In the same way as
Lemma

4.5

this follows by comparison to
Definition

2.2
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

We have

1-Q_{S}(e^{t})=1+(-1)^{m}\pi_{m}\cdot\left(t^{m-1}\cdot B(t)+t^{m}\cdot A(t)\cdot\Phi_{S}(e^{t})\right).

Proof.

We start from the identity

\Phi_{S}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}-H_{S}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}-\frac{Q_{S}(z)}{P_{S}(z)}

so that

Q_{S}(z)=\frac{P_{S}(z)}{1-z}-\Phi_{S}(z)P_{S}(z)

and

1-Q_{S}(z)=1-\frac{P_{S}(z)}{1-z}+\Phi_{S}(z)P_{S}(z).

Substituting
z=e^{t}
at applying
Lemma

4.6
,
and
Lemma

4.5
to rewrite
P_{S}(e^{t})
in terms of
A(t)
and
B(t)

completes the proof.
∎

Now choose
n=m+p\geq 1
for any
p\geq 0
.
We now proceed to extract the
t^{m+p}
coefficient of
Lemma

4.7
.
On the left-hand side, using
Lemma

4.1
we get

(7)

[t^{m+p}](1-Q_{S}(e^{t}))=\frac{\mathbb{C}_{m+p}(S)}{(m+p)!}.

We compare this with the following two calculations.

Lemma 4.8
.

We have

p!\cdot[t^{p+1}]B(t)=\frac{2^{p+1}}{p+1}T_{p+1}(\delta).

Proof.

From
Definition

2.2
, we have

[t^{p+1}]B(t)=\frac{2^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}T_{p+1}(\delta)

which gives the desired conclusion by multiplying by
p!
.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

We have

p!\cdot[t^{p}]A(t)\cdot\Phi_{S}(e^{t})=\sum_{r=0}^{p}\binom{p}{r}T_{p-r}(\sigma)\cdot G_{r}(S).

Proof.

Compute

\displaystyle[t^{p}]A(t)\cdot\Phi_{S}(e^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{p}[t^{p-r}]A(t)\cdot[t^{r}]\Phi_{S}(e^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{p}\frac{T_{p-r}(\sigma)}{(p-r)!}\cdot\frac{G_{r}(S)}{r!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{r=0}^{p}\binom{p}{r}T_{p-r}(\sigma)G_{r}(S).\qed

Hence, if we compare the
[t^{m+p}]
coefficients
by combining (
7
)
with the sum of
Lemma

4.8
and
Lemma

4.9

in
Lemma

4.7
,
we obtain that

(8)

\frac{\mathbb{C}_{m+p}(S)}{(m+p)!}=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{p!}\pi_{m}\cdot\left(\frac{2^{p+1}}{p+1}T_{p+1}(\delta)+\sum_{r=0}^{p}\binom{p}{r}T_{p-r}(\sigma)G_{r}(S)\right).

Dividing out by
\frac{(-1)^{m}}{p!}\pi_{m}
gives
1.6
, as desired,
completing the proof of Theorem
1.11
.

Appendix

AxiomProver is an AI system for mathematical research via formal proof that is currently under development.
As an early test case in this effort, we present this case study,
treating
1.6
as an end-to-end formalization target.
We chose it because we believed that its proof could be driven largely
without requiring a full formal development of numerical semigroups,
minimal resolutions, symmetric functions, and related machinery,
placing it within reach of today’s Mathlib.
We also chose the problem because it has gained renewed relevance given the
recent reemergence of the polynomials
T_{n}
.

This paper confirms that expectation: the formal statement and proof
are fully formalized in Lean/Mathlib (see
[
4
,
11
]
) and were produced
by AxiomProver from a natural-language statement of
1.6
.
The following section makes
precise what “produced” means, and describes our process.

Process

The formal proofs provided in this work were developed and verified using Lean
4.26.0
.
Compatibility with earlier or later versions is not guaranteed
due to the evolving nature of the Lean 4 compiler and its core libraries.
The relevant files are all posted in the following repository:

https://github.com/AxiomMath/fel-polynomial

The input files were

•

Fel_Conjecture.tex
, a natural-language statement of the problem;

•

a
task.md
that contains the single line

1. State and prove Fel’s conjecture in Lean.

•

a configuration file
.environment
that contains the single line

lean-4.26.0

which specifies to AxiomProver which version of Lean should be used.

Given these three input files,
AxiomProver autonomously provided the following output files:

•

problem.lean
, a Lean 4.26.0 formalization of the problem statement; and

•

solution.lean
, a complete Lean 4.26.0 formalization of the proof.

The repository also contains an ancillary file
examples.sage
that
provides the examples in the earlier Section
3
.
This file was written by hand and is unrelated to the formalization process
(in particular, it was not provided to AxiomProver as part of the input).

After AxiomProver generated a solution, the human authors wrote this paper
(without the use of AI) for human readers.
Indeed, a research paper is a narrative designed to communicate ideas to humans,
whereas a Lean files are designed to satisfy a computer kernel.

Further commentary

We offer further commentary and perspective for these results.

(1) This work represents a case where an AI system (i.e. AxiomProver)
is given a specified and self-contained natural language problem statement,
which it in turn solves and verifies in Lean.
We have already known for a while that AI can often
solve and formalize competition problems from Putnam and IMO.
This paper is an early example of the end-to-end formalization of research mathematics.

(2) We specifically selected an open problem that we believed was
within reach of the current Mathlib repository.
We didn’t want to worry about gaps in Mathlib preventing the formalization.
