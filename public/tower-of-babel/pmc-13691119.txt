Impact of extramedullary plasmacytoma on outcomes in patients with relapsed refractory multiple myeloma treated with talquetamab: U.S. Myeloma Immunotherapy Consortium

ABSTRACT
Talquetamab, a GPRC5D-targeting bispecific antibody, shows promising activity in relapsed-refractory multiple myeloma. However, real-world outcomes in patients with extramedullary disease (EMD) remain poorly described. We studied 360 patients treated with talquetamab at 15 U.S. centers by Dec 2024. Soft tissue plasmacytomas (STP) were classified as EMD (not contiguous with bone) or paraskeletal disease (PSD; contiguous with bone). If both were present, patients were assigned to the EMD category. Of those, 97 (27%) had EMD, 22 (6%) had PSD, and 241 (67%) had No-STP. Median follow-up was 12.8 months. Overall response rates were 68% in EMD, 63% in PSD, and 65% in No-STP (p = 0.8). Median progression-free survival was 4.3, 4.5, and 7.8 months, respectively (p = 0.009), and median overall survival was 10.3 months, 13.0 months, and not reached (p = 0.070). These findings demonstrate that EMD and PSD are associated with preserved response rates but shorter PFS and OS with talquetamab. While systemic markers of tumor burden and inflammation—particularly lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and ferritin—emerged as independent predictors of inferior PFS, elevated LDH also retained independent prognostic significance for OS. While EMD was associated with shorter PFS and OS on univariate analysis, it did not retain independent prognostic significance on multivariable analysis, suggesting that inferior outcomes are driven by aggressive disease biology rather than by lesion anatomy alone. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation. Talquetamab retains clinical utility in this high-risk population with EMD, though outcomes are suboptimal with a need for further refinement of treatment approaches.

Introduction

Extramedullary disease (EMD) represents an aggressive MM subtype in which malignant plasma cells proliferate independently of the bone marrow, often within an immunosuppressive microenvironment that may impair T-cell activity [1–4]. Historically, soft tissue plasmacytoma (STP) involvement was grouped together with bone-connected soft tissue plasmacytomas, also known as paraskeletal disease (PSD), whereas true EMD arises via hematogenous spread without contiguous bone involvement. Given their distinct biology and clinical behavior, distinguishing these entities is clinically important [5, 6].

Hereafter, EMD denotes true EMD (with or without concurrent PSD); PSD alone is analyzed separately.

The adverse prognosis of EMD is driven by complex biological mechanisms including loss of bone marrow adhesion molecules (particularly CXCR4 dysregulation) [7, 8], and MAPK pathway mutations [8, 9], and high-risk genomic features such as duplication 1q and deletion 17p [10]. These alterations are accompanied by specialized immune evasion mechanisms, including upregulation of inhibitory molecules on cytotoxic T and NK cells despite persistent MHC class I expression, which are particularly relevant in the context of T-cell–redirecting therapies [3]. Spatial transcriptomics of EMD samples has demonstrated exhausted T-cells colocalizing with plasma cells while functional CD8 + T-cells and M1 macrophages were segregated to tumor-free regions [1]. A finding that directly informs the anticipated limitations of T-cell–engaging bispecific antibodies in this setting. Additionally, EMD has reduced expression of therapeutic targets, including CD38, SLAMF7, GPRC5D, FCRH5, which could contribute to diminished efficacy of immunotherapy [8]. Despite these biological challenges, the clinical landscape of EMD has evolved considerably.

A meta-analysis of outcomes in patients with EMD receiving non-T-cell-directed therapies showed that they were 87% less likely to respond to standard-of-care regimens and twice as likely to progress or die compared with patients without EMD [11]. This established the poor prognostic context against which T-cell-redirecting therapies were subsequently evaluated. In the era of T-cell-redirecting therapies, outcomes in EMD have shown meaningful heterogeneity depending on the agent and the specific STP subtype. Our prior work evaluating the B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed T-cell-engaging bispecific antibody, teclistamab, in relapsed/refractory MM (RRMM) demonstrated differing outcomes between patients with PSD and EMD, highlighting the heterogeneity within STP [12]. Talquetamab, a first-in-class GPRC5D-directed bispecific antibody, represents a mechanistically distinct approach and an opportunity to evaluate whether targeting a different antigen can overcome some of the resistance mechanisms observed with BCMA-directed therapy in EMD. The post-hoc analysis of MonumenTAL-1 trial, evaluating talquetamab in the RRMM setting, reported lower overall response rates (ORR) in patient with EMD vs without EMD (48% vs 82% at 0.4 mg/kg weekly dose; 41% vs 80% at 0.8 mg/kg every 2 weeks dose) [13]. The median PFS was also shorter: 4.6 months (95% CI, 2.8–5.6) vs 9.2 months (95% CI, 7.0–11.8) in the 0.4 mg/Kg cohort, and 3.4 months (95% CI, 2.1–5.4) vs 16.9 months (95% CI, 11.3–NE) at 0.8 mg/kg cohort [13]. Notably, MonumenTAL-1 grouped PSD with the non-EMD comparator arm, limiting the interpretation of outcomes in the PSD subgroup.

Because contemporary real-world cohorts increasingly include patients with more complex treatment histories, higher rates of prior BCMA-directed therapy, and longer survival that permits ongoing genomic evolution over time, [14–16] they represent a fundamentally different clinical context than the original trial populations. Accordingly, evaluating outcomes in this setting may yield insights that differ meaningfully from earlier studies. Accordingly, we conducted this multicenter real-world analysis to evaluate the outcomes of patients with EMD and PSD receiving talquetamab.

Methods

This was a retrospective multicenter study, evaluating patients with RRMM at 15 medical centers in the U.S. Myeloma Immunotherapy Consortium, who received talquetamab by Dec 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 1—Consort Diagram). Each center obtained independent Institutional Review Board approval and informed consent in accordance with institutional requirements. Patients included in the study were classified into three groups based on center-reported data and the presence of STP. Patients without STP were classified as No-STP. Lesions contiguous with bone, including extraosseous extension into adjacent soft tissue, were classified as PSD, whereas lesions arising in soft tissue separate from bone were classified as EMD [5].

Patients presenting with both PSD and EMD were grouped as EMD, reflecting its adverse prognostic impact. Cytogenetic risk was classified using a modified version of the 2025 International Myeloma Society (IMS)/International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) consensus criteria [17], adapted for real-world data availability. Patients were assigned to one of three groups: (1) standard-risk, (2) High-risk, defined per consensus criteria as t(4;14), t(14;16), or t(14;20) in combination with gain/amp(1q) or del(1p); or concomitant gain/amp(1q) and del(1p); with or without concurrent del(17p), and (3) Indeterminate-risk, a real-world adaptation category including cases with isolated del(17p) or isolated 1p abnormalities when full high-risk definitions could not be determined due to missing TP53 mutation status, variant allele fraction, or 1p allelic status. Response was evaluated by the treating investigators according to IMWG criteria [18]. As done previously [12, 19], given the retrospective nature of this study, not all IMWG criteria were applied strictly. For example, confirmatory testing for complete responses (CR) was not mandatory. If imaging was unavailable at the time of best hematologic response, the response was determined based on hematologic criteria alone (35/119; 29% of all patients with STP). For patients with EMD or PSD, both hematologic and radiographic responses were assessed when data were available. Radiographic response was based on the imaging modalities reported by each center, acknowledging variability in imaging schedules and techniques across institutions. Increases in plasmacytomas observed during Cycle 1 alone were not considered disease progression, to avoid misclassifying tumor flare events [20, 21]. Patients who died before response assessment were considered hematologic non-responders.

Statistical analysis

A total of 463 patients received talquetamab: 360 as standalone talquetamab (i.e., administered as an independent line of therapy without planned transition to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy) and 103 as bridging therapy prior to CAR T-cell therapy. Given substantial baseline differences between the bridging and non-bridging cohorts (Supplementary Table 1) and the confounding effect of subsequent CAR T therapy, the analysis is restricted to the standalone talquetamab cohort (n = 360). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval from talquetamab initiation to progression or death, whichever occurred first; patients alive without progression were censored at last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from talquetamab initiation to death, with surviving patients censored at last follow-up. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared across subgroups with the log-rank test. Associations between pre-specified covariates and outcomes were assessed using univariate Cox proportional hazards models. These included demographic and disease-related factors, such as disease burden, age at diagnosis, age at talquetamab step-up dosing (SUD), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at talquetamab, functionally high risk relapses (defined as the progression within 18 months of initial therapy), plasma cell leukemia (PCL) at talquetamab (≥5% circulating plasma cells within ±30 days of initiation), cytogenetic abnormalities per Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) at any time, prior BCMA-directed therapy, triple-class and penta-refractory status, number of prior anti-myeloma regimens, and time from diagnosis to talquetamab. Laboratory values at talquetamab SUD included platelet count, absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), ferritin (log-transformed), lactate dehydrogenate (LDH) (per 100 U/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP) (per 10 mg/L). Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model. All statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS (V29.0) and GraphPad Prism (V10). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate whether attenuation of the EMD association in multivariable models could reflect collinearity with systemic laboratory markers of disease burden and host reserve, we examined relationships between EMD status and baseline laboratory covariates included in the models (ferritin, LDH, CRP, platelet count, and ALC). Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) [22]. In addition, the strength of association between EMD status and each continuous laboratory marker was summarized using eta-squared from one-way ANOVA models.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 360 patients, 97 (27%) had EMD, 22 (6%) had PSD, and 241 (67%) had No-STP at the time of talquetamab initiation. In the EMD cohort, 67% (n = 65) had visceral involvement and 72% (n = 70) presented with more than one EMD lesion at talquetamab. Half of the patients with EMD had known concurrent PSD disease (n = 49, 50.5%), while the presence of PSD along with EMD is unknown for 3% (n = 3) of patients. In the PSD cohort, 59% (n = 13) had 2 or more disease sites. Among patients with STP, 36% (n = 43) received targeted radiotherapy (XRT) within 2 weeks prior to or concurrent with talquetamab (EMD: 38; PSD: 5); detailed XRT outcomes are reported in the EMD Subgroup Analysis section.

The median age of the entire cohort was 66 years (range, 38-89), and 84% (n = 301) had received prior BCMA-directed therapy. The median time from MM diagnosis to talquetamab was 5.96 years (interquartile range (IQR), 3.3-9.5). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1, Supplementary Table 2. Overall, the three STP groups were generally comparable, with some notable differences. Safety

Adverse effects are summarized in Table 2. The incidence and severity of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) were similar across the EMD, PSD and No-STP groups (any grade: 61% vs 54.5% vs 58.5%, p = 0.8; grade ≥2: 14% vs 27% vs 16%, p = 0.3). Median time to CRS onset and duration were comparable. In contrast, the incidence and severity of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) differed across the groups, with a higher rate in PSD (any-grade 41%) than in EMD (16%) or No-STP (11%) (p < 0.001). Grade ≥2 ICANS occurred more frequently in PSD (27%) than in EMD (6%) or No-STP (5%) (p < 0.001). Among patients in the EMD group who developed ICANS, 62.5% (n = 10) had concurrent PSD. However, the rate of grade ≥2 ICANS was comparable between patients with EMD alone and those with EMD + PSD (n = 3 in each group). We conducted a univariate analysis to assess the impact of clinical and disease-related factors on PFS and OS. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. EMD subgroup analysis

Within the EMD cohort, multivariable analysis identified poor performance status (ECOG ≥ 2), elevated LDH, and higher ferritin at the time of talquetamab as independent predictors of inferior PFS, while prior BCMA-directed therapy was associated with improved outcomes. For OS, older age at diagnosis, ECOG ≥ 2, and elevated LDH remained independently associated with worse outcomes, whereas prior BCMA-directed therapy remained protective (Supplementary Table 4/Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, EMD-specific disease characteristics, including visceral vs non-visceral involvement, number of EMD lesions, concurrent PSD, and prior radiation to EMD sites, were not independently associated with PFS or OS.

Of the patients with STP (EMD or PSD), 43 received targeted XRT within 2 weeks prior to or while receiving talquetamab (EMD: 38 patients, 39%; PSD: 5 patients, 23%). Within the EMD cohort receiving XRT, 13 patients had isolated EMD, 23 had concurrent PSD, and PSD status were unclear in 2 patients. Granular data regarding irradiated sites and the number of treated lesions were not available.

Response to XRT was evaluable in 51% (22/43) of treated patients, including 53% (20/38) in the EMD group and 2 of 5 patients with PSD. Among evaluable EMD patients, the XRT response rate was 70% (14/20). The duration of response to XRT was not available.

PFS appeared longer among EMD patients achieving a response to XRT; however, this observation is descriptive only and should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size, rapidly diminishing numbers at risk, and potential guarantee-time and selection biases (Supplementary Fig. 2). No formal inferential comparisons were available.

Discussion

This multicenter real-world retrospective study is, to our knowledge, one of the largest and most comprehensive analyses to date evaluating the impact of STP (EMD and PSD) on outcomes among patients with RRMM treated with talquetamab as standalone therapy in the current FDA-approved late-relapse setting [23]. Although EMD was associated with inferior PFS and OS in univariable analyses, it was not independently associated with survival outcomes after adjustment for clinical and laboratory covariates. Similarly, within the EMD subgroup, anatomic features such as visceral involvement, lesion number, and concurrent PSD were not independently prognostic. Instead, elevated LDH, higher ferritin, and poor performance status emerged as the dominant predictors of adverse outcomes. These markers likely reflect not only tumor burden but also the degree of systemic inflammation and immune microenvironment hostility, both of which may impair the efficacy of T-cell–redirecting therapies. This interpretation is supported by preclinical data demonstrating that bispecific antibodies exhibit reduced activity in settings of high tumor burden due to unfavorable effector-to-target ratios, regulatory T-cell–driven immunosuppression, and accelerated T-cell exhaustion [24, 25]. Together, these findings suggest that, in the setting of T-cell–redirecting therapy, outcomes in patients with EMD may be driven more by the inflammatory and immunosuppressive milieu associated with aggressive disease biology than by lesion anatomy alone. Collinearity analyses supported this interpretation, indicating that attenuation of the EMD effect after adjustment was not attributable to problematic overlap among covariates.

Within the EMD subgroup, elevated ferritin at the time of talquetamab independently predicted inferior PFS, while elevated LDH, a marker of tumor burden and rapid cellular turnover, was independently associated with shorter PFS and OS. The EMD group also demonstrated higher cumulative infection frequency; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution given differences in treatment duration, number of cycles received, and prior therapy exposure across groups, which may independently affect immune reconstitution and infection susceptibility. Additionally, the lack of granular data on IVIG dosing frequency, cumulative exposure, and timing relative to infections limits our ability to assess the adequacy of immunoglobulin replacement across groups. Whether infection burden in EMD reflects true host vulnerability related to aggressive disease biology, treatment-related immunosuppression, or both remain uncertain and warrants prospective evaluation. Collectively, these findings suggest that poorer outcomes with talquetamab in EMD reflect unfavorable tumor–immune dynamics, biologically aggressive disease, and potential intrinsic therapy resistance to GPRC5D-directed therapy—factors that are not mutually exclusive and may act synergistically to limit the efficacy of T-cell–redirecting therapy in this setting. Interventions that modulate the tumor–immune environment, such as immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) [26] or CD38-targeted antibodies [27, 28]. could potentially influence these outcomes. In addition, pro-inflammatory pathways, including IL-6 signaling [3, 8], have been implicated in EMD proliferation and survival. Whether targeting these pathways could enhance the T-cell–redirecting therapy efficacy warrants further investigation.

The observation of improved PFS and OS with talquetamab in EMD patients with prior BCMA-directed therapy should be interpreted with caution; the underlying mechanisms cannot be determined from our data. However, several non-mutually exclusive biological explanations may be hypothesized. BCMA-directed therapies eliminate BCMA-expressing clones, selecting for BCMA-low or BCMA-negative subclones [29–31]. Since BCMA serves as a critical survival receptor mediating APRIL-driven NF-κB and PI3K-AKT signaling [32]. clones that lose BCMA may have reduced survival fitness, potentially rendering them more susceptible to subsequent non-BCMA-directed therapies such as talquetamab. Additionally, prior BCMA-directed therapy likely achieves tumor debulking, and preclinical evidence demonstrates that T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies are more effective in low-tumor-burden settings due to favorable effector-to-target ratios and reduced T-cell exhaustion [33]. Consistent with the possibility that prior immune activation may prime subsequent non-BCMA responses, mezigdomide demonstrated a numerically higher ORR in BCMA-exposed patients (50%) compared with the overall cohort (41%) in the CC-92480-MM-001 trial [34]. Collectively, these observations suggest that prior BCMA-directed therapy may paradoxically create a disease state more responsive to subsequent non-BCMA therapies through selective pressure, tumor debulking, or immune microenvironment remodeling—mechanisms particularly relevant in EMD, where the tumor-immune milieu plays a dominant role. Prospective validation with serial antigen profiling and immune correlative studies is needed.

Our findings align with prior work demonstrating substantial heterogeneity within the STP category. A single-center retrospective study reported that STP (EMD + PSD) did not uniformly predict inferior OS, with adverse prognostic impact observed mainly in PCL or secondary STP arising in the RRMM setting [35]. Another analysis showed that the prognostic relevance of STP varies by subtype, particularly EMD, and is influenced by coexisting high-risk features such as elevated LDH and multiple high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities [36]. Additional studies have further highlighted the heterogeneity of EMD, with outcomes differing between de novo and secondary EMD presentations in RRMM [37].

More broadly, prior studies have shown that EMD is associated with inferior outcomes with conventional therapy and with several T-cell-redirecting therapies, including talquetamab [38–40], teclistamab [12, 41, 42], and BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy [43–45], although the magnitude and independence of this effect vary across cohorts.

In this context, our results more closely resemble recent real-world elranatamab data [46], in which systemic markers such as LDH retained prognostic significance, whereas EMD itself did not. A prior real-world talquetamab study reported EMD in 34% of patients evaluated [47]; however, dedicated analyses of STP subtypes and multivariable modeling were not reported, representing a gap that the current study addresses.

The apparent discrepancy with our prior teclistamab analysis [12], in which EMD retained independent prognostic significance, likely reflects differences in multivariable model specification. In the current study, LDH and ferritin were included as covariates— variables not systematically incorporated in the prior analysis. After adjustment for these systemic markers, along with performance status, EMD did not retain independent prognostic significance. These findings suggest that the prognostic impact attributed to EMD may be largely driven by underlying systemic disease biology rather than the presence of extramedullary involvement alone.

Dual-targeting approaches, with combination of BCMA- and GPRC5D-directed bispecific antibodies have been evaluated in the RedirectTT-1 trial. In the phase 1b-2 dose-escalation and dose-expansion cohort, an ORR of 61% was observed in patients with EMD [48]. The subsequent phase 2 expansion cohort reported an ORR of 79%; 12-month PFS of 61%; and 12-month OS of 74%. Notably, the phase 2 trial excluded patients with prior BCMA- or GPRC5D-directed bispecific antibody exposure, limiting direct comparison with real-world cohorts. Whether dual-targeting offers incremental benefit over single-agent T-cell–redirecting therapy in EMD, particularly in patients stratified by systemic risk markers, warrants prospective evaluation with standardized EMD assessment, including lesion size, number, and metabolic activity.

CAR T-cell therapy, particularly ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel), has also shown promising activity in this setting. In CARTITUDE-1, patients with EMD achieved 100% response, although durability was shorter (EMD: PFS, 12.9 months; DOR, 13.8 months vs. NR in the overall population) [43]. Real-world studies of cilta-cel have shown a broadly similar pattern, with meaningful activity in EMD but inferior outcomes relative to patients without EMD involvement [44]. Notably, however, long-term follow-up from CARTITIDE-1 at 61.3 months showed that one-third of treated patients (32/97) remained alive and progression-free for ≥5 years after a single cilta-cel infusion without maintenance therapy. Among patients who remained progression-free ≥5 years (n = 32) vs those who progressed earlier (n = 46), the prevalence of EMD was similar (12.5% vs 13%), suggesting that long-term remission may still be achievable in selected patients with EMD [49].

These observations raise the possibility that a high EMD burden combined with systemic inflammation creates a tumor-immune environment that is less permissive for T-cell cytotoxicity. Strategies to reduce EMD burden or modulate inflammation before or during therapy may therefore improve outcomes. Local cytoreductive approaches, such as radiation therapy, may favorably alter the tumor–immune microenvironment in this context. The role of XRT in EMD patients receiving talquetamab could not be formally evaluated in our cohort due to small sample size and methodological constraints; a prospective trial addressing this question is ongoing (NCT06572605).

In our study, the higher rate of grade ≥2 ICANS in PSD compared to EMD and No-STP is a notable observation that was also seen with teclistamab in a similar real-world cohort [12], though based on small event numbers in both studies. Within the EMD group, the majority of patients who developed ICANS had concurrent PSD (62.5% any-grade, 50% grade ≥2), suggesting a possible contribution of the PSD component to ICANS risk. This may reflect higher overall tumor burden or more advanced disease biology inherent to PSD, or alternatively, the anatomical proximity of paraskeletal lesions to neural structures—potentially facilitating CNS access and lowering the threshold for neurotoxicity. The analysis was not adjusted for potential confounders, including disease burden, prior therapies, or concomitant CNS-active medications, and confirmation in larger, prospectively characterized cohorts with detailed anatomical and correlative biomarker data is needed.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and heterogeneity in prior therapies, together with relatively limited follow-up, may affect the generalizability and durability of survival estimates. Nonuniform baseline bone marrow evaluation and incomplete laboratory data limited precise characterization of disease burden. Additionally, inflammatory biomarkers were not systematically available. Baseline imaging prior to talquetamab initiation was not consistently available, and patients without imaging could not be fully characterized, raising the possibility of misclassification and potential enrichment of higher-risk disease in No-STP subgroup. Additionally, response assessment was not standardized, as imaging, timing of evaluations, and criteria for response were determined by treating physicians rather than protocol-defined schedules. Consequently, radiologic response and lesion-specific assessments may be inconsistent and not fully concordant with IMWG criteria or comparable to clinical trial data, where serial imaging is recommended. In addition, imaging data for plasmacytoma characterization were limited, and granular details such as lesion size, exact number, and spatial distribution were not uniformly captured, restricting deeper evaluation of EMD burden and its impact on outcomes. Accordingly, response rates should be interpreted with caution, and time-to-event endpoints such as PFS and OS may provide more reliable measures of clinical benefit in this real-world cohort. Furthermore, intervening therapies immediately preceding talquetamab were not uniformly captured, limiting assessment of the impact of immediate BCMA-directed therapy exposure or other non-BCMA therapies on outcomes. Heterogeneity in talquetamab dosing across treating providers further represents an important limitation that may reduce statistical power for subgroup analyses.

In this multicenter real-world cohort of patients with RRMM treated with talquetamab, EMD was associated with inferior outcomes in univariable analysis but was not independently prognostic after adjustment for clinical and laboratory factors. These data suggest that, in the era of T-cell-redirecting therapies, outcomes in EMD appear primarily influenced by systemic tumor burden and host factors, with intrinsic disease biology likely also contributing. Talquetamab, therefore, remains a reasonable option in this high-risk population, although complementary strategies aimed at cytoreduction, inflammation modulation, combination therapy with either more than one target or with T-cell modulators like IMiDs or CELMoDs, and supportive care may be needed to optimize outcomes.
