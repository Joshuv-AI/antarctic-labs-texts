Data-driven identification and semi-automated quantification of molecular targets for tumour-imaging of colorectal liver metastases and primary colorectal tumours
Authors: Mats I Warmerdam, Nidal Amenchar, Feline Hutten, A Stijn L P Crobach, Okker D Bijlstra, Nada Badr, J Sven D Mieog, Ronald van Vlierberghe, Alexander L Vahrmeijer, Peter J K Kuppen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate pre- and intraoperative assessment of disease extent is crucial for curative treatment of colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) and primary colorectal cancer (pCRC). Tumour-targeted nuclear imaging can enhance pre- and postoperative tumour staging, while tumour-specific fluorescence-guided surgery can improve intraoperative visualization. However, validated targets remain limited, particularly for CRLM. This study aimed to identify and validate novel molecular targets for CRLM using a data-driven approach. Additional objectives included pCRC target expression and evaluating target expression in neoadjuvant-treated patients.ResultsUsing a data-driven RNA-based discovery approach (Euretos), candidate targets for colorectal liver metastases were identified. Of these, the six highest-ranking targets—CEACAM5, EPCAM, CEACAM6, MUC13, FXYD3, and CDH17—were selected for validation through immunohistochemistry (IHC). Semi-automated image analysis quantified IHC staining intensity (0-100) for tumour epithelium and background on a per-pixel basis. A patient’s staining pattern was regarded as positive if mean tumour epithelium scored positive (>25), background negative (<25) and tumour epithelium >25 points higher than background, referred to as relative positive expression. The proportion of CRLM samples showing relative positive expression was 79% for CEACAM5, 45% for EPCAM, 80% for CEACAM6, 24% for MUC13, 58% for FXYD3, and 22% for CDH17. CEACAM5/CEACAM6 combined positivity reached 90% (either one positive), showing that targeting both markers enables molecular imaging in nearly the entire population. Staining intensities were similar in pCRC epithelium (P >0.05). Neoadjuvant-treated CRLM exhibited higher expression for all targets.ConclusionUsing a novel data-driven approach, six potential imaging targets were successfully identified and validated. CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 emerged as strong targets that, regardless of neoadjuvant therapy, covered nearly the entire CRLM population—supporting their further probe development and clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01354-z.Keywords: Molecular imaging, Nuclear imaging, Fluorescence-guided surgery, Colorectal liver metastases, Colorectal cancer, Neoadjuvant treatment

Introduction

Approximately 35% to 60% of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients develop colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), with approximately 25% eligible for potentially curative surgical resection [1]. Accurate staging using ultrasound, CT, MRI, or PET is essential for treatment stratification. However, despite thorough assessments, 7–15% of patients with CRLM scheduled for curative resection are found to have unresectable disease (R2) during surgery [2, 3]. In addition, 60–70% of patients who undergo curative liver resection will exhibit other metastases in the follow-up - lesions that might have been present but undetected during preoperative staging [4–7]. These findings suggest that pre-operative cancer staging may not be sufficiently accurate and sensitive. Besides pre-operative difficulties in tumour identification, intraoperative challenges remain as well, with 14–34% of resections resulting in tumour-positive resection margins (R1), associated with recurrent liver disease and worse survival outcomes [8–10]. Tumour-targeted molecular imaging can help address these challenges by providing high-contrast visualization of tumour cells, intraoperatively through targeted fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) and pre- and postoperatively via tumour-targeted nuclear imaging.

Molecular tracers typically used include antibodies, peptides, or similar agents that selectively bind to proteins overexpressed on cancer cells [11, 12]. When conjugated to fluorescent dyes in the near-infrared (NIR) range (700–900 nm), these molecular tracers enable precise, real-time intraoperative visualization of malignant tissue [11], while combined with a radionuclide, they aid in cancer staging via PET or SPECT imaging [12].

Research on optimal targets for molecular imaging in CRLM remains limited. Ideal targets must meet specific criteria, including homogeneous upregulation within tumour epithelium (TE), minimal expression in adjacent normal tissues, membrane localization, accessible extracellular domains for peptide or antibody binding, and consistent expression across patients [13–15]. Additionally, these targets should be overexpressed in both neoadjuvant-treated (NAT) and untreated tumours (no NAT). To further enhance their clinical utility in CRLM, targets should also be overexpressed in primary CRC (pCRC), enabling their application in synchronous resections. As usually no biopsy is taken from CRLM, preferably, target expression in CRLM can be reliably predicted beforehand from previous pCRC biopsy or resected tissue. Lastly, the ideal target should be overexpressed in all CRLM patients; if not, combining complementary targets should achieve this [14, 15].

Typically, the identification and validation within a researcher’s tissue of interest rely on known targets from other tissues, existing literature, or targets associated with readily available agents. While practical, this approach may result in potentially ideal targets remaining unknown. To identify new CRLM and pCRC targets in an entirely unbiased manner, we employed a novel, data-driven method that integrates transcriptomic data and RNA expression profiling [16]. The obtained targets (i.e. high tumour expression, while low in surrounding tissue) were assessed against three additional selection criteria, as described under ‘Methods’. The highest ranking targets that met al.l criteria were then screened in an initial immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of a pilot CRC cohort. Targets demonstrating potential were then validated by IHC in the full cohort and quantified using a semi-automated image analysis method, with validation prioritized for CRLM and secondarily for pCRC.

Methods

Patient and tissue selection

Patients that underwent surgery for both CRLM and pCRC at Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC, Leiden, The Netherlands) between 2010 and 2022 were retrospectively included. One representative formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue block from each pCRC and CRLM was collected from the Dept. of Pathology. For patients with more than three resected CRLM, three were randomly included in the analysis, totalling a maximum of four tumours (3 CRLM, 1 pCRC) per patient. Clinicopathological data were retrospectively collected from electronic hospital records. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board at LUMC, Leiden, The Netherlands (reference number B20.052).

Data-driven target selection

A list of the highest-ranking targets was obtained using the target selection tool of Euretos (Utrecht, the Netherlands), a data-driven selection platform that integrates over 275 public life sciences data and textual sources, providing a comprehensive knowledge base where multi-omics data is linked to literature, experimental, and clinical evidence [16]. This platform includes integrated gene RNA expression data, standardized as transcripts per million (TPM), across various tissue types. Using the Euretos “Single Target for Cancer algorithm” tool, the TPM count of the tissue of interest (CRLM) was evaluated in relation to that of the healthy surrounding tissue (liver parenchyma, LP). Since this tool did not offer CRLM as tissue of interest, adenocarcinoma of rectum or colon (i.e. pCRC) was used as tissue of interest and compared with LP. A key requirement for specific biomarkers is that the desired tissue should have higher expression compared to surrounding tissue. Therefore, pCRC boundaries were set at > 50 TPM, while LP was set at < 5 TPM. Within Euretos, expression data on CRC were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset and expression values for surrounding healthy LP were extracted from The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [16]. To ensure that we only acquired relevant genes that are predicted to code for cell membrane proteins, the surfaceome gene set was used. This gene set comprises of 4673 genes based on the experimentally derived Cell Surface Protein Atlas (CSPA) and bioinformatically predicted sets of cell surface proteins [17–20]. By running the tool with the aforementioned study-specific input, a list was obtained ranking the TPM expression from high to low, based on absolute TPM difference between target tissue of interest (pCRC) versus background (LP).

Additional target criteria

The highest-ranked targets identified using Euretos were further assessed based on three additional criteria: (1) The encoded protein must predominantly have a membranous localization according to the Human Protein Atlas [21]; (2) The target protein expression in LP should be marked as ‘low’ or ‘absent’ in the Human Protein Atlas; (3) the target has to either demonstrate promising cell membrane protein expression in CRLM/pCRC literature or lack relevant literature. Highest-ranked targets meeting all three criteria were selected for IHC validation, with a maximum of seven targets selected. If, following initial IHC screening of a pilot cohort, a target demonstrated limited TE expression, it was excluded from further extensive IHC analysis in the full cohort. To exclude a target, agreement was required from two experienced observers (PK and MW). The targets that showed promising expression in this first initial screening were then selected for comprehensive IHC validation on the entire cohort.

Immunohistochemistry

In order to facilitate semi-automized quantification using AxioVision software, an IHC double staining was performed as has been previously described [22]. TE expression of the tumour target of interest was stained with blue chromogen (Vector Blue), while extracellular matrix, comprising of stromal tissue and also blood vessels, was stained with brown chromogen. Tissue Sect. (4 μm) were cut from FFPE tissue blocks, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Type of antigen retrieval depended on the antibody protocol. CEACAM5, EPCAM, and FXYD3 required heat-induced AR at 95 °C for 10 min in PT Link (Dako, Glostrup, Germany) with low pH (6.0), while CEACAM6, MUC13 and CDH17 required a similar AR at high pH (9.0). No antigen retrieval was performed for TSPAN1. Distinct antibody mixes were prepared for each staining, combining primary mouse antibodies against the tumour marker of interest with rabbit antibodies against extracellular matrix markers (collagen I, collagen VIa, elastin), diluted in 1% BSA in PBS. All antibody types, concentrations and incubation protocols are listed in supplementary Table 1. Sections were incubated overnight at room temperature with the antibody mix. The next day, sections were treated with anti-rabbit HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (Rabbit envision, Dako) and were then developed with the brown chromogen substrate kit (Dako), colouring extracellular matrix brown. Next, the sections were incubated with AP-labelled secondary anti-mouse antibodies (BrightVision; ImmunoLogic, Arnhem, Netherlands) and developed using Vector Blue Substrate (Vector Laboratories, Newark, USA), colouring TE blue, according to the expression level of the marker of interest. Potential batch effects were controlled by ensuring that all tissue sections were exposed to the chromogens for identical durations. Specifically, slides were incubated with DAB for 10 min and with VBlue for 20 min, with timing carefully monitored during the staining procedure. After staining and hardening of the mounting medium, all slides were stored in the dark to prevent light-induced fading. Each marker was stained in a single session, eliminating batch effects within markers. Haematoxylin counterstaining was omitted. Sections were dehydrated and mounted with Aquatex (Merck, Germany).

Semi-automated quantitative analysis

H&E-stained tissue sections, sequential to the IHC-stained sections, were used to define three regions of interest (ROIs) within each tumour (CRLM and pCRC) and one ROI in the adjacent LP or mucosa, provided sufficient tissue was available; otherwise, fewer ROIs were selected.

Slides with the double-stained tissue sections were digitized using a panoramic scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) and imported into CaseViewer (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) for manual annotation of the ROIs, based on the H&E annotations. These ROIs were subsequently imported into AxioVision digital imaging software (Rel. 4.8.2, Carl Zeiss AG), a software tool that allows for per-pixel quantification of staining intensity on a scale from 0 (black) to 255 (white). To ensure accuracy, necrosis and artefacts were manually excluded. The software automatically removed pixels with an intensity of 240–255, as these represented white, non-tissue areas. TE tissue was identified by excluding brown-stained extracellular matrix, based on color properties (hue, luminosity, saturation). Then, for each ROI, the software automatically calculated the average pixel intensity of the CRLM TE, pCRC TE, LP, and mucosa. A detailed description and graphic representation of the semi-automated image analysis workflow are provided in the supplementary Fig. 1.

To standardize staining intensity, raw pixel values (x, ranging from 0 to 240) were converted to a scale from 0 (no staining) to 100 (maximum staining), referred to as the staining intensity score (y), using the formula: y = (α – x)/(α – β) * 100. The lower reference point (α), representing no staining, was defined as the average pixel value in TE on negative control slides (no primary antibody), and was fixed for all targets. The upper reference point (β), representing maximum staining, was the average pixel value from at least four TE areas scored as 3 (maximum staining) by the pathologist using a 0–3 scale, performed for each target separately. For all six targets, the determined fixed values of α (lower reference point) and β (upper reference point) can be found in supplementary Table 2. X is the average pixel value of the tissue of interest that needed to be converted. Staining intensity scores (y) below 0 were set to 0, and scores above 100 were capped at 100. For instance, if the average intensity value (x) of CRLM TE within a patient was 180, with α of 220 and β of 120, this resulted in a staining intensity score (y) of (220–170)/(220–120) *100 = 50. A score of 0–25 was regarded weak, 25–50 as moderate, 50–75 as strong and 75–100 as very strong. An absolute and a relative method were used to determine whether tissue staining was positive or negative. ‘Absolute positive staining’ was defined as a staining intensity score >25 (i.e. at least moderate staining) and ‘absolute strong positive staining’ as a staining score >50, regardless of background staining intensity. In contrast, the stricter ‘relative positive staining’ was defined by three criteria: (1) absolute positive staining in tumour epithelium (>25), (2) absolute negative staining in the background (<25), and (3) a staining intensity in TE at least 25 points higher than the background. To be regarded as positive on this score, all three criteria had to be met.

To ensure that every patient with CRLM can be effectively targeted, the complementarity of molecular target expression was assessed using two scores. The ‘dual target complementarity score’ was calculated by assessing the proportion of patients with relative positive staining in a combination of two individual targets. To be considered complementary, at least one of the two combined targets must exhibit relative positive staining. Secondly, the ‘cumulative target complementarity score’ was assessed to evaluate how sequentially adding targets increases overall patient coverage, starting with highest scoring target on relative positive staining and iteratively adding the most complementary targets one by one, based on the number of additional patients identified at each step.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), Excell for Microsoft 365 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized patient clinical characteristics. Baseline characteristics were compared using the Chi-square test for categorical variables, the unpaired t-test for normally distributed continuous data, or the Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric data. Staining intensity differences between normal tissues (LP, mucosa) and TE (CRLM, pCRC) were evaluated using paired t-tests (for parametric data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (for nonparametric data). Differences in CRLM staining intensity scores by NAT status were analysed with independent t-tests (parametric data) or Mann–Whitney U tests (nonparametric data). Pearson (parametric data) or Spearman rank (non-parametric data) correlation coefficients were calculated. Values between ± 0.50 and ± 1 indicate a strong correlation, ± 0.30 to ± 0.49 a moderate correlation, and below ± 0.29 a weak correlation. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Identification and selection of target proteins and initial immunohistochemical evaluation

To identify promising upregulated genes that encode membranous proteins, the Euretos’ “Single Target for Cancer algorithm” tool was used. 136 of 4673 surfaceome genes complied to our criteria (TPM LP<5, CRC>50) and were automatically ranked from high to low based on absolute TPM difference (Fig. 1). The top 20 genes and their expression are shown in supplementary Table 3. The top nine genes were selected and scored based on the three predefined additional target selection criteria as described in Methods. Following these criteria, Claudin-4 (CLDN4) was excluded due to prior literature indicating low expression, especially in metastatic lesions (criterion 3) [23], while FAM3D was excluded because it is secretory and extracellular rather than membranous (criterion 1) [16, 21]. Of the seven remaining genes that met al.l three additional target selection criteria, TSPAN1 showed insufficient TE expression in the pilot IHC evaluation (data not shown) and thus did not advance to the next step of IHC validation in the full cohort. CEACAM5, EPCAM, CEACAM6, MUC13, CDH17, and FXYD3 yielded promising initial results (data not shown) and were therefore selected for IHC evaluation on the entire cohort.

Expression in colorectal liver metastases and surrounding liver parenchyma

The primary objective was to identify highly expressed targets in CRLM relative to the surrounding LP. To this end, staining intensity scores (y) were calculated for all six targets in CRLM TE versus LP. The boxplots in Fig. 3a illustrate that all six targets were significantly overexpressed in CRLM TE compared to LP (P < 0.001 for all targets). Staining in LP was absent for CEACAM5, EPCAM, and FXYD3, whereas CEACAM6, MUC13, and CDH17 showed average staining scores of 13.0, 17.8, and 30.4 out of 100, respectively. Absolute positive staining in CRLM TE (>25 out of 100) was observed in 96% of patients for CEACAM6, 94% for CDH17, 82% for CEACAM5, 73% for MUC13, 61% for FXYD3, and 49% for EPCAM (Fig. 3b). Of these, CDH17 and CEACAM6 exhibited the highest number of patients with absolute strong positive staining (>50 out of 100), 73% and 55% respectively, illustrated in supplementary Fig. 2a. Unfortunately, CDH17 and MUC13 showed positive staining in LP in 69% and 34% of cases, respectively. This resulted in relative positive staining (absolute TE staining >25, LP staining <25 and TE-LP>25) in 80% of patients for CEACAM6, 79% for CEACAM5, 58% for FXYD3, 45% for EPCAM, 24% for MUC13 and 22% for CDH17 (Fig. 3c).

Expression in primary colorectal cancer and surrounding mucosa

To further enhance clinical utility, targets preferably also exhibit overexpression in pCRC. Figure 4a demonstrates that all targets were expressed in the normal mucosa; however, CEACAM5 (P < 0.001), CEACAM6 (P < 0.001), MUC13 (p = 0.007), and CDH17 (p = 0.003) showed significant overexpression in pCRC compared to mucosa. Absolute positive TE staining (>25) was most frequent for CDH17 (96%), followed by CEACAM6 (95%), MUC13 (80%), CEACAM5 (64%), FXYD3 (60%), and EPCAM (33%) (Fig. 4b). Of these, CDH17 and CEACAM6 exhibited the highest number of patients with strong positive staining (>50), 79% and 64% respectively, illustrated in supplementary Fig. 2b. Due to expression in the mucosa, relative positive stainings were lower, with 58% positive score for CEACAM5, 56% for CEACAM6, 30% for MUC13, 14% for FXYD3, 12% for EPCAM, and 9% for CDH17 (Fig. 4c).

pCRC expression as predictor for CRLM expression

As target expression in CRLM can preferably be reliably predicted from previous pCRC obtained tissue, we assessed whether pCRC TE expression corresponded with CRLM TE expression. The scatterplots presented in supplementary Fig. 3 demonstrate a positive correlation for all targets; however, only CEACAM5, CEACAM6, MUC13, and FXYD3 exhibited statistically significant associations. Of those, CEACAM5 (ρ = 0,585, P < 0.001), CEACAM6 (r = 0.623, P < 0.001), and MUC13 (ρ = 0.573, P < 0.001) showed a strong correlation (correlation > 0.5), suggesting that pCRC expression of these targets is predictive for expression level in CRLM. For all targets there was no statistically significant difference (paired and unpaired tests) in staining intensity score between CRLM and pCRC.

Dual target complementarity score

To evaluate pairwise complementarity, the ‘dual target complementarity score’ was calculated by assessing the proportion of patients positive for CEACAM6 in combination with a second individual target. To be considered complementary, at least one of the two combined targets must exhibit relative positive staining. CEACAM6 was selected as the anchor target, as it showed the highest percentage of CRLM patients with relative positive staining in this cohort. CEACAM6 alone covered 80% (42/53) of CRLM patients. When combined with CEACAM5 or EPCAM, this increased to 89%; to 91% when combined with FXYD3; to 81% with MUC13; and 85% with CDH17. These results highlight that certain target combinations can substantially enhance patient coverage compared to single targets alone.

Cumulative target complementarity score

To assess the incremental value of each additional target, the ‘cumulative target complementarity score’ was determined by sequentially combining targets, starting with CEACAM6, and calculating the increase in total patient coverage at each step. CEACAM6 alone demonstrates relative positive staining in CRLM TE in 80% of patients. When CEACAM5 was added, coverage increased to 89%. Including EPCAM as a third marker raised coverage further to 94%. With the addition of FXYD3, coverage reached 96%. The inclusion of CDH17 or MUC13 did not further increase the coverage.

Discussion

Targeted molecular imaging agents capable of binding specifically to tumour cells hold promise for addressing critical challenges in patients with CRLM. Tumour-targeted nuclear imaging can enhance pre- and postoperative tumour staging, while tumour-specific fluorescence-guided surgery can improve intraoperative visualization. Preferably the targets involved are overexpressed in CRLM TE, as well as pCRC TE and still abundant after systemic therapy.

In this study, we employed an unbiased, data-driven approach to identify potential membrane-bound molecular targets based on RNA expression, and after applying additional criteria, we validated the protein expression of the remaining six highest-ranked targets—CEACAM5, EPCAM, CEACAM6, MUC13, FXYD3, and CDH17—using IHC double staining and semi-automated per-pixel image analysis. All six targets showed significant higher protein expression in CRLM TE compared to LP. Additionally, all targets, except EPCAM, showed absolute positive staining (> 25) in CRLM TE in more than 60% of patients, with CEACAM6 and CDH17 even demonstrating absolute strong positive staining (>50) in the majority of patients. However, when applying stricter criteria—requiring absolute positive staining in CRLM TE, the absence of staining in LP and at least 25 points higher staining in CRLM TE compared to LP (in this article named relative positive staining)—CEACAM5, CEACAM6, and, to a lesser extent, FXYD3 were identified as the most promising targets, while CDH17 lost its potential, due to high expression in LP. A similar pattern was observed for pCRC, with more than 60% of patients showing absolute positive staining (>25) in pCRC TE for all targets, except EPCAM. Similarly, as for CRLM, CEACAM6 and CDH17 exhibited the biggest population with absolute strong positive staining (>50). Due to significant expression in mucosal tissue however, the proportion of pCRC patients showing relative positive staining was lower than that seen in CRLM. Nevertheless, CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 emerged as strong targets for pCRC imaging, paralleling their efficacy for CRLM. Importantly, this study revealed that neoadjuvant-treated tumours exhibited similar or even significantly higher expression of all six targets, which is particularly relevant as the increasing effectiveness of neoadjuvant therapies expands the pool of patients eligible for curative CRLM surgery [24]. Notably, we showed that targets complement each other, making adequate targeting of nearly the entire CRLM and pCRC population possible.

CEACAM5 is a long-known and well-established generic target in gastrointestinal cancers [25]. This study underscores its potential as a target for nuclear imaging and fluorescence-guided surgery. In CRC patients, CEACAM5-targeting agents have been used for diagnostics with PET imaging, for treatment with CEA-targeted immunotherapy, and for intraoperative visualization using fluorescent probes [26–36]. The CEACAM5-targeted antibody conjugated to the NIR fluorescent dye BM104 called SGM-101 has been evaluated in Phase I-III trials, demonstrating precise intraoperative fluorescence imaging of CEACAM5-positive malignant tissue, leading to clinically relevant changes in the surgical approach [28, 29, 31, 33–35]. Currently, SGM-101 is being evaluated for the intra-operative delineation of CRLM in a dual dye approach, combined with indocyanine green (NCT05965817). Indocyanine green is a NIR fluorescent agent that, after intravenous injection, accumulates around liver metastases, creating a characteristic fluorescent rim [37, 38]. While the recent multicentre MIMIC-trial showed that intra-operative CRLM visualization with indocyanine green helped identify additional metastases in 10% of patients and was able to reduce the number of unintended R1-resections, it has limitations [9]. Its non-tumour cell targeting nature can lead to the resection of false positive lesions (fluorescent, but benign) and margins (fluorescent margins, but R0), particularly in patients who have undergone neoadjuvant treatment [9, 37, 39]. Moreover, it does not enable visualization of pCRC, malignant lymph nodes, or other abdominal metastases, limiting its utility in tumour imaging. These limitations underscore the need for more specific tumour-targeted fluorescent tracers.

Another member of the CEACAM family validated in this study is CEACAM6 [40, 41]. In our cohort, absolute positive staining was observed in approximately 95% of CRLM and pCRC, notably higher than the ~ 55% reported in literature [42, 43]. This might be attributed to the fact that our cohort comprised of nearly 90% pathologically confirmed high stage colorectal carcinomas, which was shown to be correlated to higher expression [40, 42, 43]. Unlike CEACAM5, there is limited research on CEACAM6 in CRLM. Its overexpression in other gastrointestinal tumours, such as pancreatic, gastric, and non-small cell lung cancer, in combination with the results in this study highlights its broad oncological relevance [40, 41]. Recent studies have shown success in targeting CEACAM6 using an antibody conjugated to IRDye-800CW for visualizing tumours in gastric carcinoma murine models and an antibody-radioimmunotheranostic conjugate using [89Zr] and [177Lu] in PDAC mice models [44, 45].

The epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM), showed absolute positive staining in 50% of CRLM and 33% of pCRC samples in our study, much lower than reported literature values of 57%−100% in pCRC and 83% in CRLM [46, 47]. EPCAM was successfully targeted in two studies using antibody conjugates with fluorescent dyes, radionuclides, or both in orthotopic colorectal cancer mouse models, suggesting its viability as a molecular imaging target [48–50].

MUC13 is a member of the mucin family of glycoproteins, involved in gel-like secretions [51]. Consistent with this study, Walsh et al. noticed a predominantly apical staining pattern with basolateral staining primarily observed in poorly differentiated pCRCs [52]. In our study, over 70% of patients exhibited absolute positive MUC13 expression in both CRLM and pCRC, with the majority showing moderate expression (scores 25–50). The discrepancy between our findings and those of Gupta et al., who reported strong staining in 100% of pCRC and CRLM, likely arises from methodological differences [53]. Our per-pixel scoring analysis accounted for both apical and basolateral membranes, whereas their subjective scoring approach assigned full scores despite the absence of basolateral staining. Our objective scoring method most likely provides a more accurate assessment of MUC13’s suitability as a target for molecular imaging. No specific molecular imaging agents targeting MUC13 have been reported yet.

FXYD domain-containing ion transporter 3 (FXYD3) belongs to a family of small membrane proteins that modulate Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity [54]. In our study, FXYD3 expression in CRLM and pCRC was consistent with prior findings, with absolute positive staining observed in 62% of CRLM aligning with the 71% reported by Bhimani et al. [55], and 60% in pCRC, within the previously reported range of 18%−71% [54–56]. Unfortunately, its small extracellular domain (40 amino acids) significantly restricts its potential for development of peptide-based targeting agents [57]. No specific molecular imaging agents targeting FXYD3 have been reported yet.

Cadherin-17 (CDH17), also known as liver-intestine cadherin, is a calcium-dependent glycoprotein that functions as a proton-dependent transporter, facilitating peptide absorption in the gastrointestinal tract [58]. While its name suggests expression in the liver, this observation was derived from studies in rats, with reports indicating its absence in human LP [58–60]. Our study did show CDH17 expression in LP, albeit at significantly lower levels compared to CRLM TE. Although CDH17 expression in CRLM has not been extensively studied, our findings are consistent with previous research that reports strong and consistent CDH17 staining in up to 100% of pCRC tumours, making it an excellent target in pCRC surgery [58–60]. Moreover, CDH17 is an attractive target due to its large extracellular domain and relatively few glycosylation [61]. Over recent years, CDH17 has been successfully targeted in various cancer murine models [62–65]. In CRC, CDH17-targeted nanobodies conjugated to IRDye800CW were used for NIR fluorescence tumour imaging and targeted immunotoxin delivery using pseudomonas endotoxin, yielding excellent results [63]. Similar success was seen when the same nanobody and endotoxin were applied in gastric cancer [65]. Additionally, CDH17 has been successfully targeted for PET tumour imaging in gastric cancer and lymph nodes using targeted antibodies [64] and in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [62].

This study demonstrates several methodological strengths, making it more robust than many traditional target identification approaches. First, in contrast to commonly used strategies that rely on previously reported or clinically established cancer markers, our novel approach employs a systematic, unbiased data-driven workflow that integrates transcriptome-wide selection (Euretos) with additional predefined and reproducible target selection criteria. By starting from large-scale expression data rather than existing literature or known therapeutic targets, this method minimizes selection bias and enables the discovery of both established and previously overlooked biomarkers relevant to colorectal cancer. Secondly, in contrast to traditional subjective scoring of epithelial expression, our semi-automated quantification method further strengthens the study by eliminating subjective scoring bias, increasing reproducibility, and enabling the detection of more subtle staining differences. However, despite its strengths, some limitations exist. The target selection tool of Euretos ranks potential targets based on transcript per million (TPM) rather than protein expression, even though RNA levels do not always correlate with protein abundance [66, 67]. Moreover, the thresholds for RNA expression in LP (< 5 TPM in this study) and pCRC (> 50) are inherently arbitrary and influence the final target list. This variability emphasizes the importance of validating identified targets to ensure their clinical relevance. Other known gastrointestinal markers such as αvβ6, EGFR, and MUC1 did not qualify using this approach. αvβ6 and EGFR showed insufficient TPM in CRC (< 50), with EGFR also having excessive LP TPM (> 5). While MUC1 was eligible, it ranked 26th and did not qualify among the highest-ranked targets. Importantly, none of these markers have demonstrated to outperform the targets validated in this study [68–70].

For effective molecular imaging, it is crucial that the surrounding tissue of the tumour does not express the target, or at a low level. In our study, nearly all targets were absent in LP, with only CDH17 and MUC13 displaying expression. This may limit their applicability for CRLM molecular imaging. For pCRC imaging, the colorectal mucosa, unfortunately, exhibited varying degrees of expression for most targets. However, due to the thin and smooth nature of the mucosal wall compared to bulkier tumours, this expression is unlikely to interfere significantly with NIR fluorescence or radionuclear imaging. Additionally, during intra-operative fluorescence-guided imaging of pCRC, the thick colorectal wall effectively separates the abdominal cavity from the mucosal lining, blocking the NIR fluorescence signal, thus minimizing interference from the agent bound to the mucosal target. Therefore, all targets in this study, and especially the overall highest and strongest expressed targets CEACAM6, CDH17, may qualify as a very effective target for pCRC imaging. For intraluminal endoscopic colorectal imaging however, high mucosal expression could pose a challenge to generate contrast between malignant tissue and mucosa. Therefore, only CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 are well-suited for this application, as they were the only targets with a high percentage of relative positive staining.

From this study, several key insights can be drawn regarding the identification and potential development of targeted imaging agents. Firstly, Euretos proved to be an effective tool for identifying tumour targets. Of the top nine ranked targets, six targets yielded promising results. Secondly, in line with several other studies, this research shows that target expression is usually not reduced after systemic therapy; rather, this study showed that most targets were even significantly higher expressed in neoadjuvant treated patients [15, 71, 72]. Whether this increase is causally related to the treatment is unknown. The observed difference may, in part, be attributed to selection bias, as patients with more advanced disease are more likely to receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and the severity of disease is known to correlate with target expression. However, for tumour-targeted imaging, causality is not a primary concern; rather, the absolute expression patterns within different subpopulations are of greater importance. Moreover, this study demonstrates that targets can be complementary and that using a selection of these targets, it is possible to adequately image nearly the whole CRLM population. CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 demonstrated relative positive expression in approximately 80% of CRLM cases each, increasing to nearly 90% when combined, underscoring their complementarity. When considering all six targets collectively, 96% of the patient population was covered, with only two patients negative for all markers. Notably, the cumulative coverage was primarily achieved through CEACAM6, CEACAM5, EPCAM, and FXYD3, as MUC13 and CDH17 did not identify additional patients beyond those already covered. These findings support four clinical strategies: (1) administering a single tracer against CEACAM5 or CEACAM6 as a broadly applicable approach, accepting that approximately one in five patients may not achieve sufficient tumour-to-background contrast, or (2) targeting multiple targets simultaneously, for example using multiple tracers or bispecific tracers, to maximize target coverage across the population, or (3) selecting a single, patient-specific tracer based on prior histological data—such as from the primary rectal tumour—to guide the most effective targeting. This approach proved especially feasible for CEACAM5, CEACAM6, and MUC13, whose expression in pCRC strongly predicted expression in CRLM. Lastly, this study provides a foundation for developing targeted agents against the studied targets for tumour imaging, radio nuclear-based, NIR fluorescence-based, or both. As noted, CEACAM5, CEACAM6, EPCAM, and CDH17 have already been explored preclinically with fluorescent and radiolabelled agents, with CEACAM5 being the only one translated to patients thus far. Our findings confirm that, in addition to CEACAM5, particularly CEACAM6 is a strong candidate for further development of targeting agents, mainly due to their consistent high TE expression among all CRLM and pCRC patients. Although CDH17’s high expression in LP reduces its suitability for CRLM imaging, the consistent and strong expression of CDH17 in pCRC TE makes it a promising target for primary colorectal cancer imaging too.

Conclusion

This study identified targets using an unbiased, data-driven approach and validated six of these through objective, quantitative methods. All six targets exhibited significantly higher expression in CRLM compared to LP, regardless of NAT, and showed similar expression in pCRC. Considering expression patterns in TE, LP, and mucosa, especially CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 emerge as excellent candidates for targeted imaging in both CRLM and pCRC, covering nearly the entire population. While CDH17 demonstrates strong potential for pCRC imaging, its expression in LP limits its suitability for CRLM imaging. Altogether, this study establishes a foundation for the development of tumour-targeted tracers for molecular imaging of CRLM and pCRC.
