Complex interplay of astrogliosis and pathology in preclinical Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Alexandra Gogola, Davneet Minhas, Brian Lopresti, Beth Snitz, Alexandria C Reese, Cristy Matan, Minjie Wu, Xuemei Zeng, Anum Saeed, Steven Reis, Howard Aizenstein, Milos D Ikonomovic, Oscar Lopez, Thomas Karikari, Ann Cohen, Victor L Villemagne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe evaluated the effects of astrogliosis on Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology, co‐pathology, and symptoms.METHODSCross‐sectional data from 187 non‐demented participants were processed to obtain measures of SMBT‐1 standard uptake value ration (SUVR), plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), β‐amyloid (Aβ), Centiloids (CL), tau (CenTauR), white matter hyperintensities (WMH), hippocampal volume (HV), and cognition. Linear regressions evaluated pairwise associations, linear regression interaction terms indicated moderating association, and causal mediation analysis evaluated mediating associations.RESULTSAstrogliosis was associated with CL (p < 0.01), neurodegeneration (0.05 < p < 0.1), and global cognition (p < 0.01). Moderation analyses revealed SMBT‐1 SUVR had an attenuating interaction with CL in respect to CenTauR (β = −0.13, p = 0.0006) and memory impairment (β = 0.064, p = 0.05). Plasma GFAP had an attenuating interaction with CL in respect to CenTauR (β = −0.19, p = 0.00001).DISCUSSIONReactive astrogliosis is an early and important player in the AD development pathway by having an early attenuating effect against Aβ. More work is needed to further understand these complex associations.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, astrogliosis, SMBT‐1, tau, β‐amyloid

BACKGROUND

The Alzheimer's Association reports that, as of 2024, Alzheimer's disease (AD) affects approximately 11% of older adults in the United States, totaling nearly 7 million individuals, and by 2060 it is projected that this number will grow to 13.8 million. 1 Despite the prevalence of AD and its significant economic burden, a complete understanding of the mechanisms driving AD pathogenesis and progression remains elusive. Diagnostic challenges remain, particularly for early disease detection, and disease‐modifying immunotherapies targeting β‐amyloid (Aβ) have shown a modest clinical response despite successfully reducing Aβ plaque burden. 2 , 3

The rationale for anti‐Aβ treatment approaches is focused on the Aβ cascade hypothesis, 4 a view supported by an abundance of both biochemical and genetic evidence. 5 , 6 However, mounting evidence suggests that this model is neither universal nor complete. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Aβ assessment is best at identifying individuals with preclinical AD, the stage of AD when cognitively unimpaired individuals present with elevated levels of Aβ deposition. 11 , 12 However, Aβ load is poorly associated with AD‐related cognitive impairment and decline, and what associations do exist are usually mediated by tau deposition and neurodegeneration. 13 , 14 Conversely, intracellular tau deposits, the second pathological hallmark of AD, is more strongly associated with neurodegeneration, the severity of cognitive impairment, and progression to dementia. 15

Neuroinflammation is a crucial contributor to the development of AD. As such, it has recently been included in the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association's (NIA‐AA) revised guidelines for diagnosing and staging AD as a non‐disease‐specific feature in its pathogenesis. 16 While neuroinflammation in the brain is mainly mediated by both microglia and astrocytes, 17 and both types of reactive gliosis are commonly found alongside Aβ and tau in AD, 18 astrogliosis has been the primary focus of recent work in the preclinical and prodromal stages of the AD pathway. 19 Reactive astrogliosis is a diverse spectrum of molecular, morphological, and functional changes in astrocytes as they respond to central nervous system (CNS) injury and 20 , 21 is an early neuroinflammatory event in several neurodegenerative conditions 22 such as AD. 23 At this time, much of the existing in vivo research into the influence of astrogliosis on the AD pathway have been done using glial fibrillary acidic protein (plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]), a fluid biomarker of reactive astrogliosis in either plasma or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 and the only biomarker of inflammation currently recognized in the updated NIA‐AA staging guidelines. 16 These studies have shown that plasma GFAP is a useful indicator of early AD pathology and can predict progression to AD. 24 , 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 However, like most neuroinflammatory markers, plasma GFAP has limited specificity for AD, as elevated levels occur in a number of other disorders 31 . Further, plasma GFAP yields a “global” measure of astrogliosis and cannot provide regional brain information that can be associated with regional Aβ and/or tau indices. A potential alternative is the relatively new positron emission tomography (PET) radiotracer, (S)‐(2‐methylpyrid‐5‐yl)‐6‐[(3‐18F‐fluoro‐2‐hydroxy)propoxy]quinoline (SMBT‐1) that is highly selective for the monoamine oxidase‐B (MAO‐B) commonly overexpressed by reactive astrocytes and has already shown compelling results across the AD‐spectrum. 32 , 33 , 34 While some previous studies in humans have postulated that neuroinflammation drives the accumulation of tau, 35 , 36 which is significantly associated with neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment, 37 , 38 other studies, conducted in animal models, have suggested that astrocytes can have a protective effect over Aβ and tau accumulation. 39 , 40 However, the mechanisms by which astrogliosis influences AD development and progression, and the timing of those mechanisms, are still poorly understood.

In the present study, we sought to investigate the impact of reactive astrogliosis in early stages of AD development by directly comparing indices of SMBT‐1 and plasma GFAP in respect to early AD pathology and symptoms. Specifically, we evaluated the associations between reactive astrogliosis, AD pathology (Aβ, and tau), cerebrovascular disease (CVD), neurodegeneration, and cognition in non‐demented individuals. We also evaluated the potential moderating and mediating associations between these biomarkers in aging early stages of AD development.

METHODS

Participants

We evaluated 187 adults without dementia enrolled in either the Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) study 41 or the Neuroimaging Core at the University of Pittsburgh. Our final cohort included individuals who were non‐Hispanic White (NHW), Black and/or African American (AA), or Hispanic (163 NHW, 22 AA, 2 Hispanic) ranging in age from 55 to 90 years (70.0 ± 7.9 years) who were diagnosed as having either normal cognition (CU) or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by consensus at the University of Pittsburgh AD Research Center.29 The studies were approved by the Human Use Subcommittee of the Radioactive Drug Research Committees and the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pittsburgh. Participants gave written informed consent, and underwent Aβ and SMBT‐1 PET imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and cognitive assessments. A subset of 139 participants had tau PET imaging, and a subset of 126 participants had blood sample collection.

Cognitive assessments

Participants underwent neuropsychological tests and received consensus clinical diagnoses as part of the respective study protocols. Neuropsychological test results quantified global cognition, executive function, and memory using the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes (CDR SoB), Digit Symbol scores, and Craft Story Delayed Recall, respectively.

Imaging acquisition and processing

MRI

Sagittal T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition with gradient echo (MPRAGE) and T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MR images were acquired using a 3.0T Siemens Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare). MPRAGE MR images were processed with FreeSurfer (FS) v7.1.1 to generate a brain parcellation atlas for sampling the PET images 42 , 43 and FS v8 to determine the hippocampal volume (HV) and intracranial volume (ICV). FLAIR MRI images were processed as previously described to measure white matter hyperintensities (WMH) volumes as a metric of CVD. 44 , 45

PET

Aβ PET imaging was performed using 11C‐Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB), which was produced under an approved United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Investigational New Drug (IND) application (IND#72200). Tau PET imaging was performed using either 18F‐Flortaucipir (FTP) or 18F‐MK‐6240 (MK), which were prepared as previously described. 46 Precursors were provided by Avid Radiopharmaceuticals and Cerveau Technologies, Inc., respectively, under existing agreements. SMBT‐1 (IND #166977) was produced as previously described. 47 All PET images were collected on a Biograph mCT (TrueV) (Siemens Healthcare) and reconstructed as described elsewhere. 42 All PET images were motion‐corrected as needed based on visual determination and registered to their corresponding MR images. Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB) PET images were collected and summed 50–70 min post‐injection, 48 FTP images were collected and summed 80–100 min post‐injection, 49 , 50 MK images were collected and summed 90–110 min post‐injection and SMBT‐1 images were collected and summed 70–90 min post‐injection. 47 FS‐identified cerebellar gray matter was used as the reference region to convert the PiB and SMBT‐1 images from standard uptake values to standardized uptake values ratios (SUVRs). 47 , 48 PiB SUVR images were transformed into PiB Centiloid (CL) images using a site‐specific validated transformation. 51 FTP and MK SUVR images were transformed into FTP and MK CenTauR (CTR) images by co‐registering the participants’ MR and PET images to the tissue probability map (TPM) included within SPM12, using an eroded cerebellar cortex CenTauR mask as the reference region, and then applying the previously determined CenTauR transformation equations. 52

PET outcome images were sampled using the FS brain parcellation atlases derived from the corresponding MRIs. Radiotracer‐specific composite regions of interest were combined via volume‐weighting for each PET image. PiB Centiloid images were sampled with a global region, comprised of anterior cingulate, anterior ventral striatum, superior frontal, orbitofrontal, insula, lateral temporal (inferior and middle temporal), parietal, posterior cingulate, precuneus. 48 FTP and MK CTR images were sampled using the meta temporal region of the CenTauR template. 53 SMBT‐1 SUVR images were sampled in regions of early Aβ accumulation like the banks of the superior temporal sulcus (Bankssts) the supramarginal gyrus and the posterior cingulate; as well as a lateral temporal composite (a region of early cortical tau accumulation) comprised of inferior temporal, middle temporal, superior temporal, and temporal pole.

Plasma biomarker acquisition and analysis

Plasma GFAP was collected and analyzed using a NUcleic acid‐linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA), specifically the NULISAseq CNS disease panel as described elsewhere. 54

Statistical analysis

All statistical models were assessed in R. 55 Initial comparison of Aβ status (A status), and regional SMBT‐1/GFAP status was done using a CL of 20. 56 Rough preliminary SMBT‐1 and GFAP thresholds using Gaussian Mixture Model posterior probabilities relative to CU A− participants, assuming two components. Regional SMBT‐1 SUVR thresholds were as follows: 1.67 SUVR in Bankssts, 1.49 SUVR in supramarginal, 1.80 SUVR in posterior cingulate, and 1.63 SUVR in lateral temporal. The plasma GFAP positivity threshold was 12.89 NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) units. Applying these thresholds, participants were split into four groups, A− (SMBT‐1/GFAP)−, A− (SMBT‐1/GFAP)+, A+ (SMBT‐1/GFAP)−, and A+ (SMBT‐1/GFAP)+ then compared pairwise via Mann–Whitney U tests.

Simple linear regressions were used to evaluate age, sex, race, education, and tau tracer type as potential confounders to include in each of the subsequent models. Linear regressions, including the appropriate confounder(s), assessed pairwise associations between the following continuous variables: SMBT‐1 SUVRs, plasma GFAP, CL, CTR, WMHs, HV, and cognitive measures.

Moderation analysis was done by first including interaction terms between pairs of the variables included in the simple regressions. In cases where significant interactions were detected between the continuous measures or the inclusion of the interaction term eliminated the significance of a pairwise regression, moderating effects were investigated further by binarizing the potential moderating variable with Gaussian Mixture Model posterior probability thresholds and utilizing R's emmeans package 57 to estimate the marginal means within each group and calculate the significance of the contrasts. Any instance with a contrast significance of ≤ 0.1 was considered a true moderating effect. Mediation analysis was done with R's mediation package 58 .

In running and evaluating the models, it was assumed that, in the progression to neurodegeneration, WMH change preceded CL, SMBT‐1 SUVR, and plasma GFAP change; CL, SMBT‐1, and plasma GFAP change preceded CTR change; CTR change preceded HV change; and all other changes preceded change in cognitive measures. Due to the still unconfirmed causal direction between CL and SMBT‐1/GFAP, CL associations with SMBT‐1 and plasma GFAP were evaluated in both directions, and standardized β‐values compared, to evaluate possible directionality. Models including HV or WMH as an outcome included ICV as a covariate, whereas all models including HV or WMH as inputs first adjusted each for ICV. All statistics were run first for the CU‐only cohort and then with the MCI participants included as a non‐demented cohort, and all β‐values are reported relative to each metric's native units unless specifically denoted as being standardized for comparison.

Non‐demented cohort

The addition of the MCI participants did not appreciably impact the proportions between CL and astrogliosis (Figure S5). In the Bankssts SMBT‐1 evaluation, 49.5% of individuals were A− SMBT‐1−, 29.1% were A− SMBT‐1+, 12.1% were A+ SMBT‐1− and 9.3% were A+ SMBT‐1+. Similar proportions were also observed in the other SMBT‐1 regions explored. When using plasma GFAP as a biomarker for astrogliosis, 43.2% of individuals were A− plasma GFAP−, 36.0% were A− plasma GFAP+, 8.0% were A+ plasma GFAP− and 12.8% were A+ plasma GFAP+.

Analysis of CenTauR, WMH, HV, Digit Symbol, and craft story delayed recall relative to the A ± Bankssts SMBT‐1 ± groups with the addition of the MCI participants revealed similar differences as the CU group with the addition of Digit Symbol being significantly lower in the A+ SMBT‐1+ group compared to the A− SMBT‐1−, A− SMBT‐1+ and A+ SMBT‐1− groups, while HV was a trend‐level lower in A+ SMBT‐1+ compared to the A+ SMBT‐1− group. When assessing the same variables relative to the A ± plasma GFAP ± groups, HV had a trend‐level difference between the A− plasma GFAP− and A− plasma GFAP+ groups. It should also be noted that in this cohort, the WMH differences were preserved in the plasma GFAP comparisons, but disappeared in the Bankssts comparisons (Figure S6).

Analysis of linear correlations between plasma GFAP and region SMBT‐1 SUVRs revealed trend‐level (0.05 < p < 0.1) associations of plasma GFAP with Bankssts SMBT‐1 SUVR (p = 0.056) and posterior cingulate SMBT‐1 SUVR (p = 0.076) (Figure S7).

DISCUSSION

Neuroinflammation's recent inclusion as a non‐AD‐specific feature in the revised NIA‐AA guidelines for diagnosing and staging AD 16 necessitates further investigation of its role in the AD development pathway. Since reactive astrogliosis occurs early in the disease, 20 , 32 we investigated the prevalence of SMBT‐1 binding, a biomarker of reactive astrogliosis, with respect to AD pathology, CVD, neurodegeneration, and cognition. We assessed pairwise associations between each metric, explored potential moderating and mediating associations, and compared SMBT‐1 and plasma GFAP.

Analysis of the relationship between SMBT‐1/GFAP status and CL status in CU revealed that while most discordant cases were SMBT‐1‐, plasma GFAP+ (∼85%), very few were perithreshold, suggesting that the discordances were not primarily attributable to uncertainties in biomarker thresholds, and while both reflect astrogliosis and somewhat overlap, they might not represent the same type of astrogliosis. When analysis of the association between SMBT‐1/GFAP status and CL status in CU was expanded to the other metrics, we found that CenTauR was significantly elevated and Craft Delayed Recall was significantly decreased in the A+ SMBT‐1− groups compared to the others, suggesting of a potential “protective” effect of astrogliosis at the earliest stages of the AD pathway. Similar findings were not observed in the plasma GFAP analysis, but WMHs were significantly higher in the A+ plasma GFAP− group compared to the others.

The results from analysis of A ± (SMBT‐1/GFAP ) ± categorization and the associations between A ± (SMBT‐1/GFAP ) ± and other metrics, taken in conjunction with the results from the analysis of linear correlations between plasma GFAP and regional SMBT‐1 SUVRs revealing no significant or trend‐level associations, further suggest that, at the early stages of AD, plasma GFAP and SMBT‐1 are not assessing the same type of astrogliosis.

The CU participants’ regressions were in accordance with previous work. Vascular damage was associated with global cognition 59 , 60 ; Aβ was associated with tau 5 , 6 , 61 and cognitive impairment 62 , 63 ; tau was associated with cognitive impairment 64 , 65 , 66 ; and neurodegeneration was associated with cognitive impairment. 67 , 68 , 69 Additionally, we found that Aβ was significantly associated with reactive astrogliosis, which was in agreement with preliminary human studies. 70 Investigation of standardized β values between astrogliosis and CL demonstrated that plasma GFAP had the strongest association with CL (0.32), followed by Bankssts (0.24) but did not show a difference in the directionality of the associations, providing no substantial cross‐sectional evidence of which is a stronger determinant of the other.

The moderation and mediation analyses in the CU participants yielded significant moderations. Bankssts SMBT‐1 SUVR had an attenuating interaction with CL in respect to CenTauR and Craft Story Delayed Recall. At low levels of SMBT‐1, CL, and CenTauR had a significant positive association. Conversely, at high levels of Bankssts SMBT‐1, there was essentially no association between CL and CenTauR. Similarly, at low levels of Bankssts SMBT‐1, CL, and Craft Story Delayed Recall had a significant negative association and that association was not present at high levels of SMBT‐1 SUVR. These moderations suggest that, as Aβ begins to accumulate, the presence of reactive astrogliosis may help delay the downstream accumulation of tau, indicating an attenuating effect, whereas the absence of reactive astrogliosis allows tau accumulation to begin sooner. However, these moderations were only present in the analysis using SMBT‐1, plasma GFAP did not have the same moderating effect. We did find that, when plasma GFAP was positive, CL had a strong positive association with CenTauR that was no longer significant when plasma GFAP was negative, but the moderation itself was not significant, providing further support that, in asymptomatic individuals, plasma GFAP does not characterize the same kind of astrogliosis as SMBT‐1.

Inclusion of the MCI participants in the secondary analysis reinforced the results found in the CU‐only cohort and introduced new associations that provide insight into AD progression. In the full cohort analysis of A ± astrogliosis ± groups, HV and Digit Symbol were both lowest in the A+ SMBT‐1+ groups, and HV was reduced in the A− plasma GFAP+ group, providing the first evidence that astrogliosis starts having a detrimental effect on neurodegeneration and cognition. With the addition of the MCI participants, the WMH differences were preserved in only the plasma GFAP comparisons,, again suggesting a potential link between plasma GFAP and vascular damage. SMBT‐1 PET and plasma GFAP started to be correlated when MCI participants were included in the analysis, agreeing with earlier work that included participants along the AD spectrum, from the A− CU to A+ AD, 71 and supporting the concept that these two measures start to reflect a similar type of “detrimental” astrogliosis as AD progresses. This suggests either a potential time lag between SMBT‐1 and plasma GFAP becoming abnormal where plasma GFAP only starts to increase as disease progresses 72 , 73 or they actually reflect different kinds of reactive astrocytes. 20 , 74 Likely both, since reactive astrogliosis is not a single entity; it encompasses multiple and heterogeneous phenotypes and stages. Moreover, while SMBT‐1 only assesses central reactive astrocytes that overexpress MAO‐B. Plasma GFAP, while supposedly reflecting central reactive astrogliosis, might also have a peripheral origin. 74 , 75

In the linear regressions, all previous CU cohort associations held with the addition of Aβ having an association with global cognition and astrogliosis (Bankssts SMBT‐1 SUVR, plasma GFAP), having associations with HV and CDR SoB. Analysis of the standardized β values between astrogliosis and HV, CDR SoB demonstrated that, conversely to the patterns with CL, SMBT‐1 has the strongest association with each (Table S2), further suggesting that astrogliosis, particularly as indicated by regional brain pathology, has a detrimental effect as impairment progresses.

In the MCI‐included moderation and mediation analyses, all previous CU‐only associations held, with the addition of a significant attenuating moderating effect of plasma GFAP on the association between CL and CenTauR, similar to the effect of Bankssts SMBT‐1. The persistence of the moderating SMBT‐1 effect with the addition of the MCI individuals, suggests that the attenuating effect of astrogliosis on amyloid with respect to tau accumulation observed in CU individuals is not immediately diminished as signs of cognitive impairment manifest. The appearance of the moderating effect of plasma GFAP with MCI participant inclusion suggests that global astrogliosis indexes parallel regional SMBT‐1 only in symptomatic disease stages. As such, plasma GFAP may have limited sensitivity to astrogliosis in preclinical disease stages, whereas regional measures of astrogliosis, such as those indexed by SMBT‐1 are potentially more sensitive to emerging Aβ pathology.

This study is not without limitations. This is a cross‐sectional, multi‐cohort population‐based study of individuals without dementia. Our results are representative of the community from which the participants were drawn but may not be broadly generalizable and the selection of a cohort without dementia limits the amount of pathology present, potentially reducing the number of significant associations and interactions detected and, while this cohort consisted of multiple races/ethnicities and race/ethnicity was significantly associated with SMBT‐1 SUVR, 87.2% were NHW, making meaningful evaluation of the influence of race/ethnicity impossible. More work is needed in larger, more diverse cohorts to confirm this association and evaluate the possible underlying biological, environmental, or methodological factors. Additionally, participants did not necessarily have every evaluated metric available, such as tau PET or plasma GFAP, where we were only able to evaluate a subset of the whole cohort. This meant that not all statistical tests were equally powered, potentially causing the presence of otherwise significant associations to be missed. Furthermore, there were only 15 MCI participants, and no longitudinal data yet, necessitating the combination with CU in a non‐demented cohort for analysis and limiting our ability to determine the impact of the presentation of symptoms. The preliminary thresholds for SMBT‐1 and GFAP displayed moderate levels of variability, likely due to the limited sample size. Evaluation of larger datasets will be needed to determine more robust thresholds. Finally, this work relied on the evaluation of mediating and moderating associations but was conducted in a cross‐sectional dataset. Longitudinal data will need to be collected to determine how changes in each of the metrics impacts the others for the most accurate assessment.

In CU participants, astrogliosis is associated with Aβ. SMBT‐1‐assessed regional astrogliosis acts as an attenuating moderator between Aβ and tau and memory impairment, whereas plasma GFAP‐assessed astrogliosis does not. Plasma GFAP and SMBT‐1 have no significant association. When including MCI individuals in the evaluations, astrogliosis is additionally associated with neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. Adding MCI participants also presents and reveals a similar moderating effect of plasma GFAP on the association between CL and CenTauR, and presents a significant association between plasma GFAP and regional SMBT‐1. These findings suggest that reactive astrogliosis is an early and important player in the AD development pathway by having an attenuating effect against Aβ, limiting the subsequent accumulation of tau, but contributing to downstream neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. They also indicate that SMBT‐1 and plasma GFAP are not necessarily representing the same type of astrogliosis, with regional SMBT‐1 as a potential earlier indicator. More work is needed to further understand these complex associations, identify how these associations change as pathology accumulates, and determine their temporal associations.
