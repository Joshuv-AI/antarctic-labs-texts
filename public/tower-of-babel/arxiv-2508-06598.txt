On pairs of triangular numbers whose product is a perfect square and pairs of intervals of successive integers with equal sums of squares


Abstract
A number N N is a triangular number if it can be written as N = t ⁡ ( t + 1 ) / 2 N=t(t+1)/2 for some nonnegative integer number t t . A triangular number N N is called square if it is a perfect square, that is, N = d 2 N=d^{2} for some integer number d d . Observation 1

Without loss of generality (wlog)

(n-m)^{2}+(n-m+1)^{2}+\dots+(n+k)^{2}=(n+k+j+1)^{2}+(n+k+j+2)^{2}+\dots+(n+2k+j)^{2}

Proof.

Equation (

j+1

In general, two intervals might intersect.
In this case, we just delete

Clearly, we obtain an equivalent reduced equality
that corresponds to disjoint intervals.
Furthermore, in this new equality

Furthermore,

Equation (

In general, (*) might fail.

Obviously, in this case (

Which triangular numbers are perfect squares?

The question is answered by the following lemma.

Lemma 2

(see also
[
10
]
)
.

For
i=0,1,\ldots

t_{i}=2x_{i}^{2}
when

is even and
t_{i}=(x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}
when
i

Proof.

Assume
i
is even. Then

t_{i}(t_{i}+1)/2=x_{i}^{2}(2x_{i}^{2}+1)
, and by (
13
),

\begin{split}t_{i}(t_{i}+1)/2&=\frac{1}{8}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)^{2}\left(2\cdot\frac{1}{8}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)^{2}+1\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{8}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}+(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{32}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{2i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{2i}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Assume
i
is odd. Then

t_{i}(t_{i}+1)/2=(x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}\left((x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}+1\right)/2
. By (
13

\begin{split}x_{i-1}+x_{i}&=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\left(\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{i}}{1+\sqrt{2}}-\frac{(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}}{1-\sqrt{2}}+(1+\sqrt{2})^{i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\left(\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{1+\sqrt{2}}(1+\sqrt{2})^{i}-\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{1-\sqrt{2}}(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right)\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}\cdot\sqrt{2}+(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\cdot\sqrt{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{i}+(1-\sqrt{2})^{i}\right),\end{split}

(x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{2i}+(1-\sqrt{2})^{2i}-2\right),

so that

t_{i}(t_{i}+1)/2=\frac{1}{2}(x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}((x_{i-1}+x_{i})^{2}+1)=\frac{1}{32}\left((1+\sqrt{2})^{2i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{2i}\right)^{2}.

Thus, in both cases,

\sqrt{t_{i}(t_{i}+1)/2}=\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2i}-(1-\sqrt{2})^{2i}}{4\sqrt{2}}.

(14)

4.5
Main family of identities in case
m=0

In this case we want to characterize all pairs
(k,j)

such that the product

\Pi(k,j)=k(k+1)(k+j)(k+j+1)=4[k(k+1)/2][(k+j)(k+j+1)/2],

that is, the product of two triangular numbers
(times 4) is a perfect square.

Obviously, the product of any two triangular numbers that are perfect squares is
a perfect square. There exist other perfect square quadratic numbers.
Here we produce a large family of them and conjecture that there are no more.
This is an essential generalization of the Euler’s result.

4.5.1

4.7
Generalizations and extensions

We aim to characterize positive integer
s
-vectors

\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})

such that one of the following holds:

(i)

The product
\Pi_{s}=\prod_{r=1}^{s}k_{r}(k_{r}+1)
is a perfect square; or

(ii)

2\Pi_{s}
is a perfect square.

For
s=1
, this problem was resolved by Euler in 1778. It is easy to see that no solution of type (i) exists in this case. Indeed, the product
k(k+1)
cannot be a perfect square because
k
and
k+1
are consecutive integers and hence coprime, so they cannot both be perfect squares simultaneously.

For
s=2
, a large family of solutions of type (i) was constructed in the present section. Moreover, according to the last conjecture, this family is believed to be complete.

In contrast to the case
s=1
, both types of solutions exist when
s=2
. For example, the identity

2\cdot(3\cdot 4)\cdot(24\cdot 25)=(2^{3}\cdot 3\cdot 5)^{2}=120^{2}

shows that
(3,24)
is a solution of type (ii).

Now, consider
s\geq 2
and an
s
-vector

\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})

which is a solution of either type (i) or (ii) and contains an entry equal to 2 or 3. Replace one such entry: change
2\mapsto 3
or
3\mapsto 2
. It is easy to verify that the resulting vector
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}
will yield a solution of the *opposite* type: (i) and (ii) are interchanged.

For instance, starting from the previous type (ii) solution

(3\cdot 4)(24\cdot 25)=\frac{120^{2}}{2},

we replace
3\cdot 4
with
2\cdot 3
to obtain the product

(2\cdot 3)(24\cdot 25)=(3\cdot 4\cdot 5)^{2}=60^{2},

which is a solution of type (i).

Each Euler solution of type (ii) for
s=1
,

2k(k+1)=d^{2},

generates a solution of type (ii) with
s=2
and
j=1
as follows:

(2k(2k+1))\cdot((2k+1)(2k+2))=2[d(2k+1)]^{2}.

There also exist solutions of type (ii) with
s=2
that do not arise from the above two families. For example:

\displaystyle 2\cdot(3\cdot 4)(24\cdot 25)

\displaystyle=(2^{3}\cdot 3\cdot 5)^{2}=120^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(4\cdot 5)(9\cdot 10)

\displaystyle=(2^{2}\cdot 3\cdot 5)^{2}=60^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(5\cdot 6)(15\cdot 16)

\displaystyle=(2^{3}\cdot 3\cdot 5)^{2}=120^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(6\cdot 7)(27\cdot 28)

\displaystyle=(2^{2}\cdot 3^{2}\cdot 7)^{2}=252^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(7\cdot 8)(63\cdot 64)

\displaystyle=(2^{5}\cdot 3\cdot 7)^{2}=777^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(9\cdot 10)(80\cdot 81)

\displaystyle=(2^{3}\cdot 3^{3}\cdot 5)^{2}=1080^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(11\cdot 12)(32\cdot 33)

\displaystyle=(2^{4}\cdot 3\cdot 11)^{2}=528^{2},

\displaystyle 2\cdot(15\cdot 16)(120\cdot 121)

\displaystyle=(2^{4}\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 11)^{2}=2640^{2}.

Thus, characterizing all solutions of type (i) or type (ii) remains an open problem for
s\geq 2
.
