Abstract
. In classical percolation theory, the van den Berg-Kesten (BK) inequality is a fundamental tool that shows that disjoint events induce negative conditionings on each other. The inequality also holds in the context of last passage percolation (LPP), which is the zero temperature limit of polymer models and an important subclass in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class. Recently, an analog of the BK inequality was discovered in the context of zero temperature line ensembles and the scaling limit of LPP, where it was used to study upper tail probabilities of the weight and the scaling limit of geodesics under such upper tail conditionings. However, while it has become apparent that such an inequality in the positive temperature setting would have a number of applications, it seems likely that a direct generalization of the zero temperature inequality would not hold. In this work we prove a version of the BK inequality for the KPZ line ensemble and the continuum directed random polymer. We do so by working with the log gamma polymer, making use of its integrability and the geometric RSK correspondence. Our inequality serves as a key input in analyzing the KPZ line ensemble and proving sharp upper tail estimates of the KPZ equation in [ GH22 ] , and proving convergence of the continuum directed random polymer to Brownian bridge under the upper tail event in [ GHZ23 ] . The crucial role of integrability in the validity of such an inequality is highlighted via a counter-example for a non-integrable model.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Background on the zero temperature BK inequality

The van den Berg-Kesten (BK)
[
VDBK85
,
Rei00
]
inequality is a well-known and important tool in classical Bernoulli percolation on
\mathbb Z^{d}
. It says that the probability of two events occurring “disjointly” (i.e., their occurrence can be verified by inspecting the values of the i.i.d. random variables associated to disjoint subsets of the graph) is upper bounded by the product of the individual events’ probabilities, i.e., they are negatively correlated.

While in its original form it was stated in the case that the random variables are Bernoulli-distributed, it also extends to the case of general distributions on
\mathbb R

[
AGH18
]
. This has made it a useful tool also in the study of last passage percolation (in which one considers the maximum energies of directed paths passing through an i.i.d. random field, where the energy of a path is the sum of the variables it passes through) on
\mathbb Z^{2}

[
BGHH22
,
BHS22
,
BBF23
,
Sch23
,
ABGS25
]
, an important subclass of models in the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality (KPZ) class.

Another important tool in the study of the KPZ universality class are
line ensembles
, or collections of random interacting functions admitting a local Gibbs property. Two central examples are the parabolic Airy line ensemble
[
CH14
]
and the KPZ line ensemble
[
CH16
]
. The parabolic Airy line ensemble is a zero temperature object associated to a limiting LPP model known as the directed landscape, while the KPZ line ensemble is a positive temperature object associated to a limiting polymer model known as the continuum directed random polymer (CDRP). Both line ensembles have curves indexed by
\mathbb N
, where the
k
lowest indexed curves together encode information about the energy of
k

disjoint
paths for every
k
.

This connection to disjoint paths suggests that the above line ensembles should enjoy a version of the BK inequality. This was proven for the case of the parabolic Airy line ensemble
[
GH22
]
(though its proof operates at the level of the line ensemble directly). The setting of the KPZ line ensemble is significantly more subtle, as will become apparent shortly, and proving a version of the BK inequality in that setting is the goal of this paper.

Let us briefly state the inequality proved for the parabolic Airy line ensemble
(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\ldots)
in
[
GH22
]
. Let
a<b
,
\mathcal{C}([a,b],\mathbb R)
be the space of real-valued continuous functions on
[a,b]
, and
\mathsf{A}
be an increasing Borel measurable subset of
\mathcal{C}([a,b],\mathbb R)
, where increasing means that if
f\in\mathsf{A}
and
g\in\mathcal{C}([a,b],\mathbb R)
satisfies
g\geq f
pointwise, then
g\in\mathsf{A}
. The version of the BK inequality from
[
GH22
]
says that, almost surely,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\bm{\cdot})|_{[a,b]}\in\mathsf{A}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{P}_{1}\right)\leq\mathbb P\left(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\bm{\cdot})|_{[a,b]}\in\mathsf{A}\right);

in words, conditioned on the first curve, the second curve is stochastically dominated by an unconditioned copy of the first curve. This inequality was used in
[
GH22
]
to derive sharp estimates on upper tail probabilities of the first curve (in fact, this argument was given for a general class of line ensembles satisfying certain assumptions, including a much weakened form of (
1.1
)).

Remark 1.1
.

We point out that, while the classical BK inequality suggests an inequality of the type of (
1.1
) for the parabolic Airy line ensemble, it does not imply it. This is due to the fact that generic events of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
encode energies of collections of two disjoint paths as a whole, and not of each path individually. However, certain special events can be related to events of the energies of the individual paths, for which, indeed, (
1.1
) is implied by the classical BK inequality.

1.2.
The BK inequality in positive temperature

Given its great utility in LPP and other zero temperature contexts, it is of interest to obtain a generalization of the BK inequality in positive temperature settings, such as polymer models and their associated line ensembles.
The curves of the positive temperature analog of
\mathcal{P}
, the KPZ line ensemble, which we will denote
\mathfrak{h}_{t}=(\mathfrak{h}_{t,1},\mathfrak{h}_{t,2},\ldots)
, are associated to the free energy of polymers or collections of disjoint polymers in a continuum polymer model. Here, informally, the free energy is the logarithm of the partition function, which is the integral over all directed paths of the exponential of the path’s energy. Note the contrast with LPP, where one considers the single path with the maximum energy. Our goal is to obtain an inequality like (
1.1
) with
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
in place of
\mathcal{P}
.

The distinction just noted already poses a conceptual obstacle to obtaining a form of the BK inequality in the positive temperature context. Indeed, the weights of paths in LPP models are determined by just the portion of the environment they pass through, which works well with providing disjoint “certificates” of events occurring as needed in the classical BK inequality. The free energy in a polymer model, however, necessarily involves all the randomness in the environment, and so, for instance, while the largeness of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
can be tied to the existence of two disjoint paths of large energies, such a simple disjoint certificate is not available for the largeness of
\mathfrak{h}_{t,2}
.
Thus no naive or immediate generalization of the BK inequality from LPP is possible. This issue can be understood as fundamentally arising from entropy.

Nevertheless, in this work we prove a form of the BK inequality for the free energy of the CDRP and the KPZ line ensemble. In the next section we introduce our main objects more precisely and in Section
1.4
we state our main results.

Remark 1.2
.

As we will see, the inequality we prove for the KPZ line ensemble differs slightly from (
1.1
), due to the complications arising from the entropy phenomenon in positive temperature. We will expand more on this point after the statements of the results are given.

1.3.
Continuum directed random polymer & KPZ line ensemble

In this section we define the CDRP’s disjoint polymer partition functions and the KPZ line ensemble. We will not need the polymer measure for the CDRP.
For all of these objects, we take the inverse temperature
\beta=1

1.3.1.
The CDRP partition function, multiplicative stochastic heat equation, and its multi-line extension

p_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\exp(-x^{2}/t)

In

1.3.2.
Multi-point partition function with distinct endpoints

For any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}),\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\in\Lambda_{n}
, and
s<t
, we define

(1.5)

\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t):=\det[\mathcal{Z}(x_{i},s;y_{j},t)]_{i,j=1}^{n}.

Then from the continuity of
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{1}
, we have that
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t)
is almost surely continuous in all the variables.

\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t)
should be understood as the partition function of
n
disjoint paths, one each from
(x_{i},s)
to
(y_{i},t)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where the starting points are
distinct
(as otherwise the determinant above is zero, reflecting that the entropy of disjoint paths with common endpoints is zero). From this perspective (
1.5
) can be understood as a form of the Lindström-Gessel-Viennot lemma (see Lemma
2.7
ahead) in the continuum. To go from
\mathcal{K}_{n}
to
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
(i.e., the case of common starting and ending points), one must first normalize
\mathcal{K}_{n}
by a factor that matches the entropy contribution and then take a limit of the distinct points. This normalization factor is explicit and given by the product of the Vandermonde determinants of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
(see (
3.1
) ahead).

1.3.3.
KPZ line ensemble

An important tool in the study of the KPZ equation and the free energy of the CDRP is the KPZ
t
line ensemble from
[
CH16
]
and its associated Gibbs property.
To be concise, at this stage we simply state some relevant facts connecting these objects, with references given in Section
3
.

For any
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb R
, we denote

(1.6)

\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}(x):=\log\mathcal{Z}(0,0;x,t)+\frac{t}{12},\quad\mathfrak{h}_{t,2}(x):=\log\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{2}(0,0;x,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(0,0;x,t)}+\frac{t}{12}.

We note that
\mathcal{H}(x,t):=\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}(x)
solves the KPZ equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{4}\partial_{x}^{2}\mathcal{H}+\tfrac{1}{4}(\partial_{x}\mathcal{H})^{2}+\dot{W}

in a formal sense, i.e.,
\mathcal{Z}(x,t)=\exp(\mathcal{H}(x,t))
solves the multiplicative SHE (
1.2
).

Thanks to the scaling in defining
\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
has the parabolic decay of
-x^{2}/t
. More precisely,
x\mapsto\smash{\mathfrak{h}^{\beta}_{t,1}(x)}+x^{2}/t
is stationary (which can be deduced from the shear invariance of
\mathcal{Z}
, Lemma
3.1
).
We also denote

(1.7)

\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(x):=t^{-1/3}\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}(t^{2/3}x),\quad\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,2}(x):=t^{-1/3}\mathfrak{h}_{t,2}(t^{2/3}x).

As a result of this scaling,
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}
and
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,2}
have the parabolic decay of
-x^{2}
independent of
t
. This is also the scaling under which the
t\to\infty
limit is the parabolic Airy line ensemble.

1.4.
Main results

For any
a<b
, a Borel measurable subset
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}([a,b,\mathbb R])
is called
increasing
, if for any
f\in\mathsf{A}
and
g\in\mathcal{C}([a,b,\mathbb R])
such that
g\geq f
pointwise,
g\in\mathsf{A}
holds. For a function
f
and real number
\lambda
, we define
f-\lambda
to be the function
x\mapsto f(x)-\lambda
. Our first main result is the following inequality.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist
C,c,L_{0}>0
such that the following holds. Let
y\in\mathbb R
,
K\geq 0
, and
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}([0,K],\mathbb R)
be an increasing Borel measurable set. For any
t>0
,
L\geq L_{0}(t^{-1/6}\vee 1)
, and
M>C(L+y^{2})^{3/4}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb P\Bigl(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,2}|_{[y,y+K]}-Ct^{-1/3}\log M\in\mathsf{A}\ \Big|\ \hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(y)=L\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb P\left(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}|_{[y,y+K]}\in\mathsf{A}\right)+3(K+1)t^{2/3}\exp(-cM^{2}).

The same also holds when the conditioning is replaced by
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(y)\geq L
. The same also holds under both conditionings when the processes are considered on
[y-K,y]
rather than
[y,y+K]
.

In words, (
1.1
) holds for the KPZ line ensemble as well if we condition on the first curve on a single point
y
and consider the second curve on either side of
y
, up to a logarithmic shift of the second curve and an error of
\exp(-cL^{2})
. The
C\log M
term (which depends on
L
through the lower bound on
M
) can be understood as a quantitative instantiation of the role of entropy mentioned above in Section
1.2
, as can be seen in the proof. While we do expect some sort of a shift to be needed for a form of the BK inequality to hold for the KPZ line ensemble, we do not know whether a logarithmic shift is the true behavior. One also obtains a version of the BK inequality for the Airy line ensemble (i.e., zero temperature) by taking, e.g.,
M=Lt
and taking
t\to\infty
; see Remark
4.5
. The lower bounds on
L
and
M
are not strictly necessary, but are assumed to obtain a cleaner form of the error bound.

Theorem
1.3
is actually a fairly immediate consequence of a slightly more technical result which we state next and which will be proven in Section
4
. In the following, for two real-valued functions
f
,
g
defined on a common domain
\mathcal{D}
, we write
f\geq g
as shorthand for
f(x)\geq g(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. For
f
defined on
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
,
f|_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
denotes the restriction of
f
to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Finally, a.e.
f\in\mathcal{C}(I,\mathbb R)
for an interval
I
means all functions
f
in a probability
1
set with respect to the law of Brownian motion on
I
with a normally distributed starting point.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exist
C,c>0
such that the following holds. Let
y\in\mathbb R
,
K,R\geq 0
, and
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}([0,K],\mathbb R)
be an increasing Borel measurable set. For any
t>0
,
M>0
, and a.e.
f\in\mathcal{C}([y-R,y],\mathbb R)
,

(1.8)

\begin{split}&\mathbb P\Bigl(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,2}|_{[y,y+K]}-Ct^{-1/3}\log M\in\mathsf{A}\ \Big|\ \hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}|_{[y-R,y]}=f\Bigr)\\
&\leq\mathbb P\left(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}|_{[y,y+K]}\in\mathsf{A}\right)+\frac{3(K+1)t^{2/3}\exp(-cM^{2})}{\mathbb P(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}|_{[y-R,y]}\geq f)}.\end{split}

The same also holds when the conditioning is replaced by
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}|_{[y-R,y]}\geq f
, in which case we may relax the continuity assumption and allow any
f:[y-R,y]\to\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\}
which is upper semicontinuous.

All of the previous also hold under both conditionings when
+K
is replaced by
-K
and
-R
by
+R
simultaneously.

Observe that the error term looks different in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

to Theorem

) as well as one we prove for the continuum directed random polymer (Theorem
1.5
) as the BK inequality. In that sense the term can be thought of as referring to a class of inequalities which, broadly, control the behavior of the second curve conditional on the first curve in terms of an unconditioned copy of the first curve.

1.5.
Applications

The BK inequality and variants of it have already proven to be very useful in studies involving line ensembles in the KPZ universality class. Here we briefly discuss its role in two works,
[
GH22
]
and
[
GHZ23
]
.

The first work,
[
GH22
]
, studies upper tail behavior of the first curve of line ensembles satisfying certain natural assumptions like correlation inequalities by making use of probabilistic resampling ideas. The results are of two types: the first type obtains sharp upper and lower bounds on the probability of upper tail events, while the second type describes the limit shape or fluctuation behavior of the line ensembles when conditioned on such events. In fact, the latter is an ingredient of the proofs of the former. To establish the fluctuation behavior of the top curve under an upper tail conditioning, one needs to know that the conditioning does not cause the second curve to rise too high (e.g., in zero temperature, this would force the first curve to move up as well, affecting the fluctuations). The BK inequality is a natural tool which provides exactly such a guarantee, and (in fact, a much weaker version) is one of the assumptions imposed on the line ensembles for the framework of
[
GH22
]
to apply; the arguments of the paper are not sensitive to the precise form of the inequality. Further, the BK inequality (
1.1
) was proved in
[
GH22
]
for the parabolic Airy line ensemble, but a (weaker) version for the KPZ line ensemble was only conjectured (in an earlier version).

Our Theorem
1.3
is sufficient to meet the requirements of the framework developed in
[
GH22
]
. Consequently, the KPZ line ensemble results, including the sharp upper tail estimates for the KPZ equation, also follow (as now formally recorded in
[
GH22
]
in the latest arXiv version).
More precisely, the earlier conjectured inequality in
[
GH22
]
stated that the KPZ line ensemble satisfies a form of the BK inequality with a shift that is logarithmic in the size of the interval over which the first curve is conditioned on, rather than a function of the values of the first curve as proved in Theorem
1.4
. Based on the entropic source of the shift as discussed above, we believe that conjecture is too strong to be true as stated. In any case, on a practical level, Theorem
1.4
would suffice for taking the place of such an inequality in most arguments (as was the case in
[
GH22
]
itself).

The second work,
[
GHZ23
]
, also studies upper tail behavior, but of certain path measures associated to the first curve of the line ensembles. More precisely, in zero temperature, the first curve of the parabolic Airy line ensemble records the weight of a geodesic in the directed landscape started at
(0,0)
as the endpoint varies along a horizontal line, while in positive temperature, the first curve of the KPZ line ensemble records the free energy of a polymer starting at
(0,0)
as its endpoint varies in the same way. In both cases we have an associated path measure, namely the law of the geodesic and the annealed polymer measure, respectively.
[
GHZ23
]
established that, when conditioned on the first curve of the respective line ensembles to be large and taking the largeness parameter to
\infty
, the path measures (appropriately rescaled) in both cases converge to a Brownian bridge. Theorem
1.3
is a crucial input to the proofs, and is used in a number of locations for similar reasons as in
[
GH22
]
.

We note that chronologically it is somewhat unusual that this paper appears subsequent to the above mentioned papers
[
GH22
]
or
[
GHZ23
]
for which it serves as an input. This is due to the fact that one of the roles of this paper is to fix an earlier incorrect formulation and proof of the BK inequality of the KPZ line ensemble that those papers relied on.
We also emphasize that the arguments in this paper have no dependencies that create a circular argument with the previous papers. In particular, this paper is self-contained except for the use of some basic properties and estimates for the objects of interest.

2.1.
The log-gamma polymer model

The log-gamma model was introduced in
[
Sep12b
]
, and is defined as follows. Take
\theta>0
, and let
\{X_{\mathbf{v}}\}_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb Z^{2}}
be independent random variables with inverse-gamma distribution of parameter
\theta
; i.e., with probability density given by

(2.1)

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}x^{-\theta-1}\exp(-x^{-1})\quad\text{for }x>0.

For any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb Z^{2}
with
\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
in each coordinate, and
k\in\mathbb N
, we define the
k
disjoint polymer partition function

(2.2)

T_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\sum_{(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{\mathbf{w}\in\gamma_{j}}X_{\mathbf{w}},

where the summation is over all collections of
k

vertex-disjoint
up-right paths
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})
, with each
\gamma_{i}
from
\mathbf{u}+(i-1,0)
to
\mathbf{v}+(-k+i,0)
. (By convention,
T_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=0
if no such collection of paths exists.)

We will often need a slight generalization of the previous definition. Take any
2k
vertices
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb Z^{2}
, such that for each
i,j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,
\mathbf{u}_{i}\leq\mathbf{v}_{j}
in each coordinate, and any up-right path from
\mathbf{u}_{i}
to
\mathbf{v}_{j}
and any up-right path from
\mathbf{u}_{j}
to
\mathbf{v}_{i}
must intersect (e.g., all the
\mathbf{u}_{i}
lie on the same horizontal line in order and the same for the
\mathbf{v}_{j}
). We define the
k
disjoint polymer partition function

(2.3)

T([\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{k}],[\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}]):=\sum_{(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{\mathbf{w}\in\gamma_{j}}X_{\mathbf{w}},

where the summation is over all collections of
k

vertex-disjoint
up-right paths
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})
, with each
\gamma_{i}
from
\mathbf{u}_{i}
to
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
In particular, it is again taken to be zero if no such collection of paths exists.

Our main result for the log-gamma polymer is the following prelimiting version of Theorem
1.5
. It will be proven in Section
2.5
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\theta>0
. For any integers
m,n\geq 2
,
f:\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket\to\mathbb R_{+}
, and increasing Borel measurable set
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathbb R^{(m-1)(n-1)}
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb P\left(\Bigl\{f(m)^{-1}T([(1,1),(a,1)],[(m,n),(m,b)])\Bigr\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,m\rrbracket,b\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\in\mathsf{A}\;\Big|\;T_{1}((1,1),(\bm{\cdot},n))|_{\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}=f\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb P\left(\Bigl\{T_{1}((a-1,1),(m-1,b))\Bigr\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,m\rrbracket,b\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\in\mathsf{A}\right).\end{split}

Remark 2.2
.

We point out that Theorem
2.1
holds when the pair of starting points and the pair of ending points are adjacent (i.e.,
a=2
and
b=n-1
), in which case

T([(1,1),(a,1)],[(m,n),(m,b)])/T_{1}((1,1),(m,n))

is precisely the second curve of the discrete log-gamma line ensemble (associated to system size
n
). Thus one might expect to be able to take a limit of Theorem
2.1
in this special case directly to the KPZ line ensemble and obtain the BK inequality without the logarithmic shift and probability error term present in Theorem
1.4
.

However, the issue is that the righthand side of Theorem
2.1
involves the first curve of the log-gamma diffusion associated to system size
n-1
. Typically, due to entropy considerations, the first curve of the
(n-1)
-system line ensemble is
\log n
higher than the second curve of the
n
-system. More precisely, this is essentially because the probability of two random walks of length
n
started at a unit order separation have probability of order
n^{-1/2}
of remaining disjoint for their lifetimes. This logarithmic shift is seen on a technical level in the fact that the centering terms required to take the limit to the KPZ line ensemble differs by
\log n
between the two system sizes (as can be read off of Lemma
4.1
ahead). As a result, the limiting inequality obtained is trivial.

The crucial aspect of Theorem
2.1
is that it allows points to be separate, which avoid this issue. The strategy is thus to take the limit when the endpoints are separate on the diffusive scale (so the underlying random walks have unit order probability of remaining disjoint) and to make use of modulus of continuity properties in the limit in order to make the points coincide. This will be done in Sections
3
and
4
.

Notice that Theorem
2.1
allows the second endpoint to vary on the vertical line
\{(m,b):b\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket\}
. In the scaling limit to the CDRP, this corresponds to the endpoint varying temporally. However, it will be more convenient to allow the point to vary spatially, as these partition functions are what is captured by the KPZ line ensemble. For this, we record the following simple corollary that instead allows the second endpoint to vary on the horizontal line of index
n-1
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\theta>0
. For any integers
b,m,n\geq 2
with
b<m
and function
f:\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket\to\mathbb R
, the following holds. Let
\{\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
be sampled from the conditional law of

\{T([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}\text{ given }T_{1}((1,1),(\bm{\cdot},n))|_{\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket}=f.

Then, there exists a coupling of the law of
\{\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
and that of
\{T_{1}((a,1),(b^{\prime},n-1))\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
such that, for all
a\in\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket
and
b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket
,

\displaystyle f(b)^{-1}\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\leq T_{1}((a-1,1),(b^{\prime}-1,n-1)).

Proof.

The proof idea is to first use the coupling from Theorem
2.1
to obtain a pair of environments such that the associated partition functions on the right boundary have the correct marginal distribution (one conditional on
T_{1}
and the other not) and are dominated in the way specified there. Then, one augments both environments (i.e., extending the coupling) by inserting columns to the right of
x=m
of the same i.i.d. inverse gamma random variables in both systems. This will imply a domination of the partition functions to the
(n-1)
st
line by a recursion. The details are below.

By Theorem
2.1
and Strassen’s theorem on stochastic domination (see, e.g.,
[
Lin99
]
), it follows that there exists a coupling between
\{\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b,\ell)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}
and
\{T_{1}((a-1,1),(b-1,\ell))\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}
such that, almost surely, for all
a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle f(b)^{-1}\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b,\ell)])\leq T_{1}((a-1,1),(b-1,\ell)).

Next consider an i.i.d. collection
\{X_{(i,j)}\}_{i\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}
of inverse gamma random variables. For
b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket
, define

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\tilde{T}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b,\ell)])\cdot\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\gamma:(b+1,\ell)\to(b^{\prime},n-1)}\prod_{(i,j)\in\gamma}X_{(i,j)},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}((a-1,1),(b^{\prime}-1,n-1))

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}T_{1}((a-1,1),(b-1,\ell))\cdot\sum_{\gamma:(b+1,\ell)\to(b^{\prime},n-1)}\prod_{(i,j)\in\gamma}X_{(i,j)}.

Observe that, by (
2.4
),

(2.5)

\displaystyle f(b)^{-1}\tilde{\tau}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\leq\tau_{1}((a-1,1),(b^{\prime}-1,n-1))

holds for all
a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket
and
b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket
.

It follows immediately from the definition (
2.3
) of
T
that
\{\tilde{\tau}([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
has the law of

\{T([(1,1),(a,1)],[(b,n),(b^{\prime},n-1)])\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}\text{ conditioned on }T_{1}((1,1),(\bm{\cdot},n))|_{\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket}=f.

Similarly,
\tau_{1}((a-1,1),(b^{\prime}-1,n-1))_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
has the law of
\{T_{1}((a-1,1),(b^{\prime}-1,n-1))\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,b\rrbracket,b^{\prime}\in\llbracket b+1,m\rrbracket}
.
This along with (

Remark 2.5

(Non-validity of Theorem
2.1
for Bernoulli distributions)
.

We point out that Theorem
2.1
is not true for general distributions, and thus the integrability of the log-gamma polymer model seems to play a crucial role in its validity. Indeed, consider the case of
m=n=2
with
\{X_{\mathbf{v}}\}_{\mathbf{v}\in\llbracket 1,2\rrbracket^{2}}
being i.i.d. Bernoulli(
p
) random variables for any
p\in(0,1)
(i.e.,
\mathbb P(X_{\mathbf{v}}=1)=1-\mathbb P(X_{\mathbf{v}}=0)=p
). For notational convenience, write
\smash{T^{(2)}_{1}}=T_{1}((1,1),(2,2))
,
\smash{T^{(1)}_{1}}=T_{1}((1,1),(1,1))
(the superscripts indicating the system size), and
\smash{T_{2}^{(2)}}=T_{2}((1,1),(2,2))
. Then observe that

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{1}

\displaystyle=X_{(1,1)},

\displaystyle T_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=X_{(1,1)}X_{(2,2)}\left(X_{(1,2)}+X_{(2,1)}\right),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle T_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=X_{(1,1)}X_{(1,2)}X_{(2,1)}X_{(2,2)}.

If we condition on
T_{1}^{(2)}=2
, we force
X_{(1,1)}=X_{(1,2)}=X_{(2,1)}=X_{(2,2)}=1
. So
T_{2}^{(2)}/T_{1}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}
almost surely. Thus it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(2^{-1}T_{2}^{(2)}\geq t\mid T_{1}^{(2)}=2\right)=1\quad\text{ for all }t\leq\frac{1}{2}.

On the other hand, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb P\Bigl(T_{1}^{(1)}\geq t\Bigr)\leq\mathbb P\Bigl(T_{1}^{(1)}\geq 1\Bigr)=p<1,

as
T_{1}^{(1)}=X_{(1,1)}
is a Bernoulli(
p
) random variable. Thus the main display of Theorem
2.1
does not hold when
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, for any
p\in(0,1)
.

Though in this example there was complete freezing of all the vertex variables, this is not necessary. Indeed, one can consider vertex weights distributed as i.i.d. random variables uniform on
[0,1]
. Then
T^{(2)}_{1}\leq 2
almost surely again. If one fixes
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and conditions on
T^{(2)}_{1}\geq 2-\delta
, it follows that
\min(X_{(1,1)},X_{(2,2)}))\geq 1-\frac{1}{3}\delta
and
\min(X_{(1,2)},X_{(2,1)})\geq 1-\frac{2}{3}\delta
almost surely. So, under the same conditioning,
T^{(2)}_{2}\in[1-2\delta,1]
almost surely, since
(1-\frac{1}{3}\delta)^{2}(1-\frac{2}{3}\delta)^{2}\geq 1-2\delta
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(\frac{T_{2}^{(2)}}{T^{(2)}_{1}}\geq t\ \Big|\ T_{1}^{(2)}\geq 2-\delta\right)=1\quad\text{ for all }t\leq\frac{1}{2}-\delta.

But
\mathbb P(T_{1}^{(1)}\geq t)=1-t<1
for all
t\in(0,1]
, yielding that the inequality of Theorem
2.1
does not hold whenever
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2}-\delta]
. Thus violations to the inequality also exist among continuous distribution and in the absence of complete freezing.

2.2.
Geometric RSK for general polymer models on

In this section we recall some basic facts about the geometric RSK (gRSK) correspondence in the context of general polymer models on

that will be needed in the proof of Theorem

are defined as in (

The gRSK correspondence can be defined via either row/column insertion algorithms (see e.g.,

by

\displaystyle Z_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=T_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}),\text{ and }Z_{j}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\frac{T_{j}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})}{T_{j-1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})}\text{ for $j\geq 2$}

(with the convention of taking

when

Remark 2.6
.

We note that
Z_{j}
for

is not quite a discrete analog of

as defined in (

). The difference is that the former is the ratio of partition functions for
j
and

disjoint polymers while the latter
is
the partition function for
j
disjoint polymers. This discrepancy arises from differing notation in two parts of the literature (
[
COSZ14
]
for

and
[

]
for
\mathcal{Z}_{j}
) and we have chosen to be consistent with both.

For a fixed starting point such as
(1,1)
, we view
\smash{\{Z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}}
with the ending point varying as a line ensemble, with
Z_{j}
the values of the
j
th
line. Indeed, taking appropriate scaling limits of this line ensemble results in the KPZ or parabolic Airy line ensembles (see
[
Wu23c
]
along with
[

]
, and
[

, respectively).

Disjoint polymer partition functions can be expressed as a determinant, a fact which will be very useful for us. The first equality in the following is an immediate consequence of the Lindström-Gessel-Viennot lemma
[
GV89

and the second is by definition (

Lemma 2.7

Remark 2.9

We make a few simple observations about the nature of paths in this joint line ensemble to familiarize the reader; see Figure

Proposition 2.10

(Extended invariance identity)
.

Let

and

with

(here,

\displaystyle T\left([(a_{1},1),\ldots,(a_{k},1)],[(b_{1},n),\ldots,(b_{\ell},n),(m,b_{\ell+1}),\ldots,(m,b_{k})]\right)

\displaystyle=S\left([(a_{1},1)^{\shortuparrow},\ldots,(a_{k},1)^{\shortuparrow}],[(b_{1},n),\ldots,(b_{\ell},n),(m,b_{\ell+1})^{\shortrightarrow},\ldots,(m,b_{k})^{\shortrightarrow}]\right).

The conditions on

and

are merely to ensure planar ordering of the points in the primal environment so as to ensure that the partition function is non-zero. Observe that the same condition also ensures the planar ordering of the corresponding points in the line ensemble, thus guaranteeing that that partition function is also non-zero.

\begin{split}&F^{m,n}_{a,b}(\{Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}):=S([(a,1)^{\shortuparrow}],[(m,b)^{\shortrightarrow}]).\end{split}

For any integers
1\leq a\leq m
and
1\leq b\leq n

Observe that the (

Remark 2.14

In a simple case, the terms appearing in Lemma

\displaystyle=T([(1,1),(2,1)],[(m,n),(m,b)])+T_{1}((1,1),(m,b))\cdot T_{1}((2,1),(m,n)),

which is equivalent to the determinant expression from Lemma

2.4.
Density formulas for the log-gamma polymer

Recall the definitions of the index sets
J[m,n]
,
J_{1}[m,n]
, and
J_{2}[m,n]
from (
2.7
) and of
Z_{j}^{m,n}
from (
2.8
) and (
2.9
). In this subsection and the next, these quantities are associated with the case where the underlying i.i.d. noise is given by inverse-gamma(
\theta)
random variables (recall (

)).

As mentioned, the log-gamma model is an integrable polymer model. Here, this means that we have explicit formulas for the joint distribution of a family of partition functions. We state this next, which is a consequence of results from
[
COSZ14
]
. It will be proved just below.

Lemma 2.15
.

The joint probability density of
\{Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}
at
\{z_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}
equals

(2.22)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{Z}_{m,n}}\exp\Bigg(-\sum_{(i,j)\in J[m,n];i\leq m-1}\frac{z_{j}(i)}{z_{j}(i+1)}-\sum_{(i,j)\in J[m,n];i\leq m-1;j\leq n-1}\frac{z_{j+1}(i+1)}{z_{j}(i)}\Bigg)\\
\times\exp\Bigg(-\sum_{(i,j)\in J[m,n];i\geq m+2}\frac{z_{j}(i)}{z_{j}(i-1)}-\sum_{(i,j)\in J[m,n];i\geq m+2;j\leq n-1}\frac{z_{j+1}(i-1)}{z_{j}(i)}\Bigg)\\
\times\prod_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}z_{j}(i)^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{\min\{m,n\}}z_{j}(m)^{-\theta},\end{split}

under the constraint
z_{j}(m)=z_{j}(m+1)
for
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(m,n)\rrbracket
, where
\mathfrak{Z}_{m,n}
is the renormalization constant.

Before giving the proof of Lemma
2.15
, we make a
simple but important observation about the explicit density function given in (
2.22
), thus relating the law of
\{Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}
with that of
\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}
.

We first note that
J[m-1,n-1]
is naturally embedded inside
J[m,n]\setminus\{(i,j)\in J[m,n]:j=1\}
and that therefore we can separate out the terms corresponding to entries of
J[m-1,n-1]
in (
2.22
) to see the relation between the laws of
\{Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m,n]}
and
\{Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}
. This also allows us to do so after conditioning on the values of
Z^{m,n}_{1}(\bm{\cdot})
.

For integers
m,n\geq 2
and any
g:\llbracket 1,m+n\rrbracket\to\mathbb R
, we define
\Gamma_{g}:\mathbb R_{+}^{J[m-1,n-1]}\to\mathbb R_{+}
as

(2.23)

\Gamma_{g}(\{z_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}):=\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathfrak{Z}}_{m,n,g}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{z_{1}(i)}{g(i)}-\sum_{i=m}^{m+n-2}\frac{z_{1}(i)}{g(i+2)}\right),

where
\tilde{\mathfrak{Z}}_{m,n,g}
is taken such that
\mathbb E\big[\Gamma_{g}(\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]})\big]=1
.

Performing the separation of the terms corresponding to
J[m-1,n-1]
after conditioning on the values of
Z^{m,n}_{1}(\mathbf{\cdot})
leads to the following statement, whose proof is by inspection and omitted.

Corollary 2.16
.

For
g:\llbracket 1,m+n\rrbracket\to\mathbb R
such that
g(m)=g(m+1)
, the law of
\{Z^{m,n}_{j+1}(i+1)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}
, conditional on
Z^{m,n}_{1}(\bm{\cdot})=g
, equals the (unconditioned) law of
\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}
reweighted by
\Gamma_{g}(\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]})
.

Proof of Lemma
2.15
.

Denote
\hat{Z}^{m,n}_{j}(i)=Z^{m,n}_{j}(i)=Z_{j}((1,1),(i,n))

equals (
2.22

2.5.
BK inequality for log-gamma polymers

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
2.1
. We start with a negative association statement.
To state it, recall the definition of
\Gamma_{g}
from (
2.23
).

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be an increasing event of
\{T_{1}((a,1),(m-1,b))\}_{a\in\llbracket 1,m-1\rrbracket,b\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}

For any
g:\llbracket 1,m+n-1\rrbracket\to\mathbb R_{+}
,
the random variable

(2.24)

\Gamma_{g}\left(\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}\right)

and
\mathsf{A}
are negatively associated; i.e., (
2.24

\mathsf{A}
is stochastically dominated by (unconditioned) (
2.24
).

Proof.

Consider both
\Gamma_{g}\left(\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}\right)
and
\mathds{1}[\mathsf{A}]
as functions of the i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{i,j}\}_{i\in\llbracket 1,m-1\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}

Since
T_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
is increasing in
\{X_{i,j}\}_{i\in\llbracket 1,m-1\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}
, for any
\mathbf{u}\leq\mathbf{v}
in each coordinate, we have that
\Gamma_{g}\left(\{Z^{m-1,n-1}_{j}(i)\}_{(i,j)\in J[m-1,n-1]}\right)
is a decreasing function (recall (
2.23
)) and

∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

It suffices to show that, for any

(2.25)

\mathbb P\left(\left\{\frac{T([(1,1),(a,1)],[(m,n),(m,b)])}{T_{1}((1,1),(m,n))}\right\}_{a\in\llbracket 2,m\rrbracket,b\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\in\mathsf{A}\;\Big|\;Z_{1}^{m,n}=g\right)\\

Inputs and tools for continuum objects

Having established a form of the BK inequality for disjoint polymers in Theorem

, our next task is to take the continuum limit to establish Theorem

1.5
(as well as of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

.
We start by stating some distributional symmetries of
\mathcal{Z}
, namely shift, shear, and reflection invariances.

Lemma 3.1

(Theorem 3.1,
[
AKQ14a
]
or Proposition 2.3,
[
AJRAS22
]
)
.

\mathcal{Z}
has the same distribution as

(1)

(Shift and shear)
(x,s;y,t)\mapsto\mathcal{Z}(x+\nu s+\alpha,s+\eta;y+\nu t+\alpha,t+\eta)\exp(\nu^{2}(t-s)+2\nu(y-x))
, for any
\nu,\alpha,\eta\in\mathbb R
; and

(2)

(Reflection)
(x,s;y,t)\mapsto\mathcal{Z}(-x,s;-y,t)
, and
(x,s;y,t)\mapsto\mathcal{Z}(y,-t;x,-s)

Further, for any disjoint time intervals
\{(s_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
, the functions
\mathcal{Z}(\cdot,s_{i};\cdot,t_{i})
are independent. Also, with probability one
\mathcal{Z}(x,s;y,t)>0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb R
and

.
Finally, (1) and (2) also hold for
\mathcal{K}
by (

).

3.1.
Properties of multi-point partition functions

Next we turn to a rescaled version
\mathcal{M}_{n}
of the multi-layer partition function
\mathcal{K}_{n}
from (
1.5
).

The rescaling to go from
\mathcal{K}_{n}
to
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is by the Vandermonde determinants of the endpoints, and can be regarded as an entropy factor; this is necessary to relate
\mathcal{K}_{n}
to
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
(as in (
1.4
)), where all the endpoints are equal.
We define
\mathcal{M}_{n}:\{(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y};t):\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathring{\Lambda}_{n},s,t\in\mathbb R,s<t\}\to\mathbb R
by

(3.1)

\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t):=\frac{\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t)}{\Delta(\mathbf{x})\Delta(\mathbf{y})}=\det[\mathcal{Z}(x_{i},s;y_{j},t)]_{i,j=1}^{k}\Delta(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\Delta(\mathbf{y})^{-1}

where
\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})
.

3.1.1.
Positivity and implications.

Using the Karlin-McGregor theorem, it is straightforward to deduce that
\mathcal{M}
is non-negative, as shown in
[
OW16
, Proposition 5.5]
.
The simultaneous strict inequality is proved in
[
LW20
, Theorem 1.4]
, and also in
[
AJRAS22
, Theorem 2.17]
with a different method.

Lemma 3.2
.

Almost surely, for any
s<t
,
n\in\mathbb N
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathring{\Lambda}_{n}
, it holds that
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbf{x},s;\mathbf{y},t)>0
.

The
k=2
case of
Lemma

3.2
will be useful later, and we write it out explicitly: for any
s<t
,
x_{1}<x_{2}
, and
y_{1}<y_{2}
,

(3.2)

\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{1},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{2},t)>\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{2},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{1},t).

This is a positive temperature analog of the quadrangle inequality in zero temperature (see e.g.,
[
DOV22
,
BGH21
]
).

This positivity yields an inequality relating
\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
\mathcal{Z}
which will be useful later. Here and in the remainder of the paper we adopt a slightly different notation for
\mathcal{K}_{2}
than in Section
1
, namely, we write
\mathcal{K}_{2}([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{1},t),(y_{2},t)])
rather than
\mathcal{K}_{2}((x_{1},x_{2}),s;(y_{1},y_{2}),t)
. This is in order to be more consistent with the notation from Section
2
and as it is slightly clearer when dealing with just two points.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following holds almost surely. For any
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
,
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq y_{3}
, and
s<t
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2}([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{1},t),(y_{2},t)])}{\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{2},t)}<\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2}([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{1},t),(y_{3},t)])}{\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{3},t)}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2}([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{2},t),(y_{3},t)])}{\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{2},t)}<\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2}([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{1},t),(y_{3},t)])}{\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{1},t)}.

Proof.

We give the proof for the first inequality as the second is analogous. Using (
1.5
), expanding, and rearranging, we must show that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{1},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{2},t)-\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{2},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{1},t)\Bigr)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{3},t)

\displaystyle<\Bigl(\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{1},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{3},t)-\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{3},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{1},t)\Bigr)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{2},t).

Cancelling and rearranging, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{2},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{3},s;y_{3},t)-\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{3},t)\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},s;y_{2},t)>0.

This is precisely (
3.2
).
∎

4.1.
Scalings for log gamma

Recall from Section

that

and, for
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb Z^{2}
which are ordered

component-wise, let

be the partition function as defined in (

\mathbb R^{4}_{\shortuparrow}
from Section

be defined by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{n}(x,s;y,t)=Z^{n}_{1}\left(ns+2n^{1/2}x,ns;nt+2n^{1/2}y,nt\right)\cdot n^{1/2}\cdot\left(2^{-1}(2n^{1/2}-1)\right)^{2n(t-s)+2n^{1/2}(y-x)}

for all

such that the arguments of

The following lemma was essentially proven in
[
AKQ14b
]
, and a similar statement was given in
[
Wu23c
, Proposition 5.10]
, so we will only outline its proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathcal{Z}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
weakly in the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets of
\mathbb R^{4}_{\uparrow}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof outline.

[
AKQ14b
, Theorem 2.7]
asserts the desired convergence for the full space-time field in a general polymer model where the vertex weight distributions have mean zero and variance one, which is not the case for the (rescaled) inverse gamma random variables we have.
[
Wu23c
, Proposition 5.10]
obtains the same statement for the marginal where
x=s=0
in the case of rescaled inverse gamma random variables by observing that the argument of
[
AKQ14b
]
also holds if the variance converges to

appropriately quickly (as
n\to\infty

and so the same observation applied to the full convergence result of
[
AKQ14b
, Theorem 2.7]
gives our result.
∎

Next we define the object which will converge to
\mathcal{K}_{2}
(recall its definition from (
1.5
)). First, let

\mathbb R^{6}_{\shortuparrow,\leq}:=\Bigl\{(x_{1},x_{2},s;y_{1},y_{2},t):t>s\geq 0;(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in\Lambda_{2}\Bigr\}.

Define
\mathcal{K}_{2}^{n}:\mathbb R^{6}_{\shortuparrow,\leq}\to\mathbb R
by

(4.2)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2}\left([(x_{1},s),(x_{2},s)];[(y_{1},t),(y_{2},t)]\right)\\
&=T\left([(ns+2n^{1/2}x_{1},ns),(ns+2n^{1/2}x_{2},ns)];[(nt+2n^{1/2}y_{1},nt),(nt+2n^{1/2}y_{2},nt-1)]\right)\\
&\qquad\times n\times(2^{-1}(2n^{1/2}-1))^{4n(t-s)-1+2n^{1/2}(y_{1}+y_{2}-x_{1}-x_{2})}\end{split}

for all
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2},s,t)
such that
t\geq s+\frac{1}{n}
and all the arguments of

are integers, and for all other
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2},s,t)
in the domain by linear interpolation.

Here we use
nt-1
in the second coordinate instead of

as in the first so as to match what appears in Corollary
2.3
. It is because of this extra

term that the constant rescaling factor in (
4.2
) (i.e., the final line) contains an extra

in the exponent compared to the square of the analogous rescaling factor in (

), as can be seen by performing the substitutions

and

in (

by

differs by a

factor, modulo the just mentioned deterministic modification in the scaling. Thus, as we see next, in the limit the distinction is not present.

In the following we regard
\mathcal{K}_{2}^{n}
as a continuous function defined on all of
\mathbb R^{6}_{\shortuparrow,\leq}
by embedding the above definition and extending to the remainder of the domain in an arbitrary manner that respects continuity.

Lemma 4.2
.

As
n\to\infty
, in the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets of
\mathbb R^{4}_{\shortuparrow}\times\mathbb R^{6}_{\shortuparrow,\leq}
,

\smash{(\mathcal{Z}^{n},\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K}_{2})}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2}
are the same continuous function of
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{n}
by (
1.5
) and Lemma
2.7
, respectively, it suffices to prove that both the marginals of
(\mathcal{Z}^{n},\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2})
converge to the correct limits. That
\smash{\mathcal{Z}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{Z}}
is Lemma
4.1
. So we turn to the convergence of

.

First, by Lemma
2.7
, we can express
\mathcal{K}_{2}^{n}
as a determinant of a matrix whose entries are given by

evaluated at the appropriate points. By the joint convergence of

after rescaling to
\mathcal{Z}
as given in Lemma

, we obtain that the limit as

of this determinant is exactly the one appearing in the definition (

) of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
as a determinant of
\mathcal{Z}
(note that the required rescaling for the factors of

matches what is present by the discussion after (

∎

4.2.
Proof of the limiting BK inequality for disjoint polymers in the CDRP

Here we give the proof of Theorem
1.5
. We start with two basic but useful facts about weak convergence.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S
be a metric space with Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}
. Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
and
X
be random elements taking values in
S
with
X_{n}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}}X
. Let
\mathsf{E}\in\mathcal{S}
be a continuity set for
X
, i.e.,
\mathbb P(X\in\partial\mathsf{E})=0
, where
\partial\mathsf{E}=\bar{\mathsf{E}}\setminus\mathsf{E}^{\circ}
is its topological boundary, and suppose
\mathbb P(X\in\mathsf{E})>0
. Then
X_{n}
conditioned on
X_{n}\in\mathsf{E}
converges in distribution to
X
conditioned on
X\in\mathsf{E}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that, for any bounded continuous function
f:S\to\mathbb R
, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb E[f(X_{n})\mid X_{n}\in\mathsf{E}]\to\mathbb E[f(X)\mid X\in\mathsf{E}].

Write
\mathbb E[f(X_{n})\mid X_{n}\in\mathsf{E}]=\mathbb E[f(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{X_{n}\in\mathsf{E}}]/\mathbb P(X_{n}\in\mathsf{E})
. Then what we must show follows from the Portmanteau and continuous mapping theorems since
\mathsf{E}
is a continuity set for
X
. First, the Portmanteau theorem implies
\mathbb P(X_{n}\in\mathsf{E})\to\mathbb P(X\in\mathsf{E})
, and, since
x\mapsto f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{x\in\mathsf{E}}
is almost surely continuous at
X
, the continuous mapping theorem implies
\mathbb E[f(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{X_{n}\in\mathsf{E}}]\to\mathbb E[f(X)\mathbbm{1}_{X\in\mathsf{E}}]
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Recall we wish to bound the conditional probability

(4.3)

\mathbb P\left(\log\mathcal{K}_{2}((x_{1},x_{2}),s;(y_{1},\bm{\cdot}),t)|_{[y_{1},y_{1}+K]}-\log\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;y_{1},t)\in\mathsf{A}\mid\log\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{[y_{1}-R,y_{1}]}\right).

(The case where
+K
and
-R
are simultaneously replaced by
-K
and
+R
is immediate after recalling the distributional symmetry of the processes under reflection around the origin, Lemma
3.1
.) By spatial and temporal translation invariance (Lemma
3.1
) of
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
, it suffices to prove the case
x_{1}=s=0
. By shear invariance of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
and
\mathcal{Z}
(again Lemma
3.1
), it further suffices to take
y_{1}=0
.

Let
\{\mathcal{U}_{m}\}
be an increasing sequence of finite sets, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}_{1}\subset\mathcal{U}_{2}\subset\ldots
such that
\cup_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{U}_{m}
is dense in
[-R,0]
. Note that if we consider the conditional probability above in (
4.3
) but conditioned on
\log\mathcal{Z}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}
rather than
\log\mathcal{Z}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{[-R,0]}
, then this sequence of conditional probabilities forms a martingale (in
n
) whose limit is (
4.3
) (due to the denseness assumption on
\{\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
and the continuity of
\mathcal{Z}
). So it suffices to prove the desired bound on the conditional probability conditioned on the values of
\log\mathcal{Z}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)
on a finite set; this is useful as we can avoid technicalities regarding the convergence of probabilities conditioned on equaling a given element in the space of continuous functions and instead work in finite-dimensional settings.

Now we turn to prove the desired bound when conditioned on the values of
\log\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},s;\bm{\cdot},t)
on a finite set
\mathcal{U}\subset[-R,0]
. Let
g:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb R
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty]
fixed, let
\mu_{n}^{\varepsilon}
be the law of

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2}([(0,0),(x_{2},0)];[(0,t),(\bm{\cdot},t-\frac{1}{n})])|_{[0,K]}}{\mathcal{Z}^{n}(0,0;0,t)}

\displaystyle\text{conditioned on}\quad\mathcal{Z}^{n}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{\mathcal{U}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{U}}[g(x),g(x)+\varepsilon]

and
\nu_{n}
be the law of
\mathcal{Z}^{n}(x_{2},0;\bm{\cdot},t-\tfrac{1}{n})
. Let
\mu^{\varepsilon}
be the law of

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{K}_{2}([(0,0),(x_{2},0)];[(0,t),(\bm{\cdot},t)])|_{[0,K]}}{\mathcal{Z}(0,0;0,t)}\quad\text{conditioned on}\quad\mathcal{Z}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{\mathcal{U}}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{U}}[g(x),g(x)+\varepsilon],

and
\nu
be the law of
\mathcal{Z}(x_{2},0;\bm{\cdot},t)
.

Next we wish to take the weak limit as
n\to\infty
and make use of Lemma
4.3
, whose assumptions we now verify. First, by Lemma
4.2
we know that
\smash{(\mathcal{Z}^{n},\mathcal{K}^{n}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K}_{2})}
. Second,
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
are atomless (the latter following from the former by the definition (
1.5
)) and
\mathcal{Z}(0,0;\bm{\cdot},t)|_{\mathcal{U}}
has full support, which follows from the Brownian Gibbs property of the KPZ line ensemble (see Section
A.1
or
[
CH16
]
) or by
[
CHN21
, Theorem 1.2]
. So Lemma
4.3
yields that, as
n\to\infty
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mu^{\varepsilon}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mu^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\nu.

Now we wish to apply Corollary
2.3
. We make substitutions for the coordinates and quantities appearing in that statement so as to put things in the correct scaling. First, replace

by
2n^{1/2}x_{2}
,
b
by

, and

by

by

yield that there exists a coupling of

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{n}(x)\leq Y_{n}(x).

Combining (

and

for all

.

Setting

for all

for all

and for any increasing Borel measurable set

\displaystyle\mathbb P\Biggl(\hskip-22.76228pt\parbox[c]{230.46748pt}{\centering$\bigl(\log\mathcal{K}([(0,0)(x_{2},0)];[(0,t),(\bm{\cdot},t)])|_{[0,K]}$\\

Taking

.

4.3.

Here we give the proofs of Theorems

(equivalently,

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

. There exist

such that, for

,

,

, and

, for all

5.
Generalizations

In this section we discuss how our method actually extends to other multi-point partition functions. In Section

\begin{split}&\mathbb P\left(\Big\{\sum_{j=w+1}^{w+i}\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,j}|_{[y,y+K]}-Ct^{-1/3}\log M\Big\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathsf{A}\ \bigg|\ \{\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,j}|_{[y-R,y]}\}_{j=1}^{w}=\mathbf{f}\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb P\left(\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{i}\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,j}|_{[y,y+K]}\Big\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathsf{A}\right)+\frac{3(K+1)t^{2/3}\exp(-cM^{2})}{\mathbb P(\{\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,j}|_{[y-R,y]}\}_{j=1}^{w}\geq\mathbf{f})}.\end{split}

where
C,c>0
are constants that may depend on
w,n
.
We also expect the same to hold when the conditioning is replaced by
\{\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,j}|_{[y-R,y]}\}_{j=1}^{w}\geq f
, in which case we may relax the continuity assumption and allow
\mathbf{f}:[y-R,y]\to(\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\})^{w}
to be upper semicontinuous.

Since the sum of the top
m
lines of the KPZ line ensemble at a given point is the same as
\mathcal{M}_{m}
evaluated when all
m
starting points coincide and all
m
ending points coincide, the main input required to generalize
1.4
to (
5.2
) assuming (
5.1
) would be a quantitative regularity estimate for
\mathcal{M}_{m}
(in order to relate it with
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{m}
, which is in this context the analog of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
in the
m=2
case) as the starting and ending points vary, essentially a higher-indexed version of
Lemma

4.4
. For the
m=2
case handled in the earlier sections, we were able to make do with the identity
Lemma

3.6
and regularity estimates for the KPZ equation, but such an identity does not seem to generalize to higher indexed curves in a way that is useful for this purpose. We do not pursue obtaining quantitative regularity estimates directly in this paper.

A.1.
Line ensembles and Gibbs properties

W start the following Gibbs properties of
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
given
\mathfrak{h}_{t,2}
.
For any
a<b
, denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}([a,b])
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
on
\mathbb R\setminus(a,b)
, and
\mathfrak{h}_{t,2}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Take any
a<b
and
t>0
.
Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}([a,b])
,
for (1) law of
\mathfrak{h}^{\beta}_{t,1}
in
[a,b]
, (2) the rate
2
Brownian bridge connecting
\smash{\mathfrak{h}^{\beta}_{t,1}(a)}
and
\smash{\mathfrak{h}^{\beta}_{t,1}(b)}
, the former is absolutely continuous with respect to the latter, with Radon-Nikodym derivative (for a path
B
) proportional to
W(B,\mathfrak{h}^{\beta}_{t,2})
, where

(A.1)

W(f,g)=\exp\Big(-2\int_{a}^{b}\exp(f(x)-g(x))dx\Big)

This Gibbs property was first introduced and proven for the KPZ line ensemble in
[
CH16
]
. The connection between the KPZ
t
line ensemble and CDRP was formally established in
[
Nic21
]
.
The form of the Gibbs property presented here is from
[
GH22
, Proposition 2.6, Theorem 2.8]
.

A useful consequence of the Gibbs property is the monotonicity in boundary data recorded below.

Lemma A.2

(Monotonicity in boundary data)
.

Fix
a<b
, real numbers
\smash{w^{*},z^{*}}\in\mathbb R
and measurable functions
\smash{g^{*}}:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\}
for
*\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
such that
\smash{w^{\downarrow}\leq w^{\uparrow}}
,
\smash{z^{\downarrow}\leq z^{\uparrow}}
, and, for all
s\in(a,b)
,
\smash{g^{\downarrow}(s)\leq g^{\uparrow}(s)}
.

For
*\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
, let
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}
be a process on
[a,b]
such that
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}(a)=\smash{w^{*}}
and
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}(b)=\smash{z^{*}}
, with Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to Brownian bridge given by
W(\mathcal{Q}^{*},g)
for
\beta=1
.
Then there exists a coupling of the laws of
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{\uparrow}}
and
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{\downarrow}}
such that almost surely
\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{\downarrow}}(s)\leq\smash{\mathcal{Q}^{\uparrow}_{j}(s)}
for all
s\in(a,b)
.

The positive temperature (
\beta=1
) statements are Lemmas 2.6 and 2.7 of
[
CH16
]
. See also
[
Dim22
]
for a more detailed proof.

The following is a useful correlation inequality saying the line ensemble is positively associated.

Lemma A.3

(FKG inequality,
[
GH22
, Theorem 2.7]
)
.

For any
t>0
and
a<b
, and any pair of increasing events
A
and
B
in the space of all real continuous functions on
[a,b]
,

\mathbb P\left(\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{[a,b]}\in A,\,\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{[a,b]}\in B\right)\geq\mathbb P\left(\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{[a,b]}\in A\right)\cdot\mathbb P\left(\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{[a,b]}\in B\right).

Finally, we need the following estimate on the one-point lower tail of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}
.

Proposition A.4
.

There exist
c,L_{0}>0
such that, for any
0<t\leq 1
and

\mathbb P\left(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)<-L\right)\leq\exp(-cL^{2}t^{1/6}).

If we instead assume

for some
t_{0}>0

\mathbb P\left(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)<-L\right)<\exp(-cL^{5/2}),

These two estimates can be deduced from

A.2.
Proof of monotonicity in conditioning

Here we prove Lemma
3.8
.

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

[
GH22
, Theorem 2.7]
states that the law of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
conditional on
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}(x_{j})=y_{j}^{\shortuparrow}
stochastically dominates the same conditional on
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}(x_{j})=y_{j}^{\shortdownarrow}
for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathbb R
and
y_{1}^{*},\ldots,y_{m}^{*}\in\mathbb R
for
*\in\{\shortuparrow,\shortdownarrow\}
such that
\smash{y_{j}^{\shortuparrow}\geq y_{j}^{\shortdownarrow}}
for all
j\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
. Thus the case where
\mathcal{D}
is a finite set is already established.

Now consider the case where
\mathcal{D}
is an interval. Let
\{\mathcal{D}_{m}\}
be an increasing sequence of finite subsets of
\mathcal{D}
such that
\cup_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}_{m}
is a dense subset of
\mathcal{D}
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb N
and any event
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
of the first
k
curves of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
on
\mathcal{D}
, it holds that

(A.2)

\mathbb P\left(\mathfrak{h}_{t}|_{\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times\mathcal{D}}\in\mathsf{A}\ \Big|\ \mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{\mathcal{D}_{m}}\right)

is a martingale in
m
. By Doob’s martingale convergence theorem and the continuity of
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
, this sequence has an almost sure limit, which equals

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(\mathfrak{h}_{t}|_{\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times\mathcal{D}}\in\mathsf{A}\ \Big|\ \mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{\mathcal{D}}\right).

Let us rephrase this in terms of the probability kernels underlying the conditional probabilities. For each
m\in\mathbb N
, let
\mu_{m}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathbb R^{m}\to[0,1]
be the probability kernel associated to the regular conditional probability in (
A.2
), and
\mu:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)\to[0,1]
be the one associated to (
A.3
), where
\mathcal{B}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{C}(\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
. So, what we have obtained by the martingale argument is that, for each fixed
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{B}
, almost surely,

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(\mathsf{A},\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{\mathcal{D}_{m}})\to\mu(\mathsf{A},\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}|_{\mathcal{D}}).

Since
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
has full support in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
(by the absolute continuity to Brownian motion implied by the Brownian Gibbs property enjoyed by
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
; see Lemma
A.1
and
[
CH16
]
), it follows that there exists
\Omega_{\mathsf{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
which has probability 1 under the Brownian measure in the statement of the lemma such that, for all
f\in\Omega_{\mathsf{A}}
,
\mu_{m}(\mathsf{A},f|_{\mathcal{D}_{m}})\to\mu(\mathsf{A},f|_{\mathcal{D}}).

Now let
\mathsf{A}
be an increasing event and let
f,g\in\Omega_{\mathsf{A}}
be such that
f(x)\geq g(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
. Then by the finite case already established,
\mu_{m}(\mathsf{A},f|_{\mathcal{D}_{m}})\geq\mu_{m}(\mathsf{A},g|_{\mathcal{D}_{m}})
. Taking
m\to\infty
yields
\mu(\mathsf{A},f)\geq\mu(\mathsf{A},g)
.

It remains to extend to all increasing events. This is accomplished by an approximation argument (see
[
Bar05
, Lemma 6]
) from a countable generating set, using the continuity of
\mathfrak{h}_{t,1}
and that
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
is a Polish space. That is, we guarantee
\mu(\mathsf{A},f)\geq\mu(\mathsf{A},g)
holds for a suitable countable collection of increasing
\mathsf{A}
for all
f,g\in\Omega
for a probability one
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb R)
, and this implies the same inequality for all increasing
\mathsf{A}
.
∎

A.3.
Proof of the lower bound on the upper tail

Here we prove Proposition
3.9
.

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

For an
M
to be chosen, consider the favourable event

\mathsf{Fav}_{t}=\left\{\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(-L^{1/2})\geq-L-M\right\}\cap\left\{\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(L^{1/2})\geq-L-M\right\}.

By stationarity and positive association (Lemma
A.3
) of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(x)+x^{2}
,

\mathbb P(\mathsf{Fav}_{t})\geq\mathbb P(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)>-M)^{2}.

By Proposition
A.4
, if
M>(t^{-1/6}\vee 1)L_{0}
, then
\mathbb P(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)>-M)\geq 1-\exp(-cM)
. So by setting
M=C(t^{-1/6}\vee 1)
for a sufficiently large constant
C
guarantees that
\mathbb P(\mathsf{Fav}_{t})\geq 1/2
for all
t>0
.

Consider the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(1,[-L^{1/2},L^{1/2}])
.
The Brownian Gibbs property says that the distribution of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}
on
[-L^{1/2},L^{1/2}]
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}
, is that of a rate two Brownian bridge tilted by the Radon-Nikodym derivative
W_{H_{t}}/Z_{H_{t}}
associated to the conditioned boundary data. By monotonicity (Lemma
A.2
), on
\mathsf{Fav}_{t}
, this Brownian bridge stochastically dominates the rate two Brownian bridge
B
from
(-L^{1/2},-L-M)
to
(L^{1/2},-L-M)
with no lower boundary condition.

Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb P\big(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)\geq L\big)\geq\mathbb E\Big[\mathbb P\big(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)\geq L\mid\mathcal{F}\big)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{Fav}}\Big]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb P\left(B(0)\geq L\right).

Now
B(0)
is a normal random variable with mean
-L-M
and variance
L^{1/2}.
Thus by a standard lower bound on the normal probability (see, e.g.,
[
GH22
, Lemma 2.10]
), it holds on
\mathsf{Fav}_{t}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb P\big(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{t,1}(0)\geq L\big)\geq cL^{-3/4}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(L+L+M)^{2}}{2L^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-5L^{3/2}),

the last inequality for
L>M
. This completes the proof.
∎

The proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
3.11
.

First we prove the two-point tail estimate that there exists
c,C>0
such that for any

and

,

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(|\mathfrak{h}(x_{1},0;y_{1},t)-\mathfrak{h}(x_{2},0;y_{2},t)|\geq M\|(x_{1},y_{1})-(x_{2},y_{2})\|^{1/2}\right)\leq C\exp(-cM^{2}).

From (

3.11

[
DV21

To prove (
A.5

(A.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb P\left(|\mathfrak{h}(x,0;y_{1},1)-\mathfrak{h}(x,0;y_{2},1)|\geq M|y_{1}-y_{2}|^{1/2}\right)\leq C\exp(-cM^{2}).

Then, by (i) the triangle inequality, (ii) since
|a|+|b|\leq 2^{p-1}(a^{4}+b^{4})^{1/4}
for any
a,b\in\mathbb R
, and (iii) since

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{h}(x_{1},0;y_{1},1)-\mathfrak{h}(x_{2},0;y_{2},1)|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|\mathfrak{h}(x_{1},0;y_{1},1)-\mathfrak{h}(x_{1},0;y_{2},1)|+|\mathfrak{h}(y_{2},0;x_{1},1)-\mathfrak{h}(y_{2},0;x_{2},1)|

\displaystyle\leq M\left(|y_{1}-y_{2}|^{1/2}+|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{1/2}\right)\leq\tilde{C}K\|(x_{1},y_{1})-(x_{2},y_{2})\|^{1/2},

where the penultimate inequality holds on an event of probability at least

by applying (
A.6

A.5
) and completes the proof of Lemma

∎
