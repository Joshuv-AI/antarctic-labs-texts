Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody disorder (MOGAD) optic perineuritis following severe COVID19 infection
Authors: Miguel Leitão, Pamela Davila Siliezar, Noor Laylani, Andrew G Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe the case of bilateral myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody disorder (MOGAD) perineuritis following severe COVID19 pneumonia.ObservationsA 56-year-old man presents with bilateral vision loss (BCVA OU of counting fingers) following severe COVID19 infection. Neuroimaging revealed bilateral perineuritis, with MOG-IgG antibody positive (1:20), confirmed by cell-based assay, elevated ESR and CRP (42 mm/h and 8.2 mg/dL, respectively). The patient was started on IV methylprednisolone with significant improvement in visual field testing bilaterally, followed by slow steroid taper. After 6 months, repeat MOG-IgG antibody was negative (seronegative conversion) and inflammatory parameters (ESR and CRP) were within normal limits.Conclusions and importanceCOVID-19 has been previously associated with MOGAD optic perineuritis, mostly with higher antibody titers. This case suggests a new pathophysiological hypothesis in which concomitant cytokine storm in severe COVID-19 disrupts the blood-brain-barrier, leading to the entry of even lower MOG-IgG titers to the central nervous system (CNS) and exacerbate severe visual loss. Clinicians should be aware of the association of COVID-19 and MOGAD.

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody disorder (MOGAD) is an inflammatory disorder of the central nervous system (CNS). Although the precise pathogenesis of MOGAD continues to expand, recent evidence suggests humoral mechanisms such as autoantibody binding to surface myelin and consequent complement activation and/or cellular cytotoxicity as the mechanism for perivenous and confluent white matter demyelination. Interestingly, there have been prior reported cases of MOGAD following viral infections, such as for severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) coronavirus disease (COVID-19).1

We report a case of bilateral MOGAD perineuritis following COVID19 pneumonia. Based upon our review of the English language ophthalmic literature, we believe that this case is novel and unique.

A 56-year-old Hispanic male presented with bilateral loss of vision after severe COVID-19 in July 2020. Past medical history was significant for prior syphilis treated with penicillin with positive Treponemal antibody but seronegative rapid plasma reagin (RPR) following treatment. The remainder of the past surgical, social, and family history were noncontributory. He was on no medications but required 8L of oxygen for severe post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis. A few days following the COVID-19 diagnosis, the patient reported acute, painless, progressive loss of vision in both eyes (OU). He required 4 months of hospitalization and developed secondary pulmonary fibrosis and severe chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure.

On neuro-ophthalmic examination, the visual acuity was counting fingers (CF) OU. Anterior segment, intraocular pressure, motility, and external examinations were unremarkable. Pupils were poorly reactive OU (grade +2), with no relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). Fundoscopy revealed bilateral optic disc pallor, cup-to-disc ratio of 0.4, peripapillary atrophy and there were no vascular or macular findings. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging showed global retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning OU and no evident macular abnormalities (see Supplemental Material). Automated perimetry (Humphrey visual field (HVF) V stimulus testing) showed diffuse generalized depression OU, though with increased false negative errors and long testing time.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and orbit showed bilateral optic nerve sheath enhancement (Fig. 1) consistent with optic perineuritis. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed opening pressure 12 cm H2O, CSF white blood cell count of 3 cells/μL (0–5 cells/μL), CSF glucose of 72 mg/dL (40–70 mg/dL), and CSF protein 40 mg/dL (12–60 mg/dL). Chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes with extensive fibrotic interstitial lung disease following COVID-19 pneumonia. Endobronchial ultrasound with transbronchial needle aspiration showed no evidence of noncaseating granuloma or malignancy. Serum complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, urine analysis, angiotensin converting enzyme, tuberculosis interferon gamma release assay, lysozyme, ANA, pANCA, cANCA, Lyme titer, and HIV testing were unremarkable. Syphilis serologies showed non-reactive RPR, reactive TPPA and negative CSF VDRL. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 42 mm/h and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 8.2 mg/dL.

Coronal post-contrast T1 fat-saturated MRI showing hyperintense signal around the optic sheath bilaterally (arrows), suggestive of bilateral optic perineuritis.

Aquaporin 4 titer for neuromyelitis optica (NMO) was negative. Myelin oligodendrocytic glycoprotein (MOG) antibody was positive at a titer of 1:20 which was confirmed by cell-based assay.

The patient was started on methylprednisolone 1000 mg once-daily for a total of 3 days. After 5 days, although BCVA remained at CF OU, there was a significant improvement in visual field testing bilaterally, again with increased false negative errors and long testing time. The patient was discharged on a tapering dose of oral prednisone. After 6 months of treatment, repeat MOG antibody was negative.

COVID-19-induced MOGAD has been reported previously, as summarized in Table 1 (Supplemental Material). In most previous case reports, patients developed MOGAD optic neuritis with high antibody titers at least 1 week after developing mild COVID-19 symptoms (ie. cough, shortness of breath). Proposed immunological mechanisms involve molecular mimicry elicited B-cell response, due to homology between host epitopes and a viral epitope, or autoreactive T-cells triggered by viral antigens, leading to the stimulation of plasma cells to produce autoantibodies. For example, the carbohydrate molecular mimicry between the bacterial lipooligosaccharide and human GM1 ganglioside have been proposed for the association between infection from C. jejuni and development of Guillain-Barré syndrome.2 In addition, there have been reported cases of MOGAD with high anti-MOG titers between 1 and 2 weeks following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination, which is sufficient time for the aforementioned immunological mechanisms.3

Our patient presented with a distinctive clinical presentation for MOGAD optic perineuritis in the setting of severe COVID-19 pneumonia. Although the initial titers for anti-MOG antibodies (1:20) were low, the secondary cytokine release syndrome related to the severe COVID-19 pneumonia in 2020 may have caused significant disruption of the blood brain barrier (BBB) leading to the entry of anti-MOG antibodies into the CNS, causing MOGAD optic perineuritis and visual loss.

The mechanism of disruption of the BBB in severe COVID-19 may be caused by a combination of 3 factors: release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1B, IL-6, TNF, and type 1 IFN; metabolic dysfunction and apoptosis of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMEC); and delivery of viral particles (such as S1) to the BMEC. Consequently, even low serum titer of anti-MOG antibodies may cross the disrupted BBB and cause significant antibody and/or cell-mediated immune injury of neural tissue.4,5 Whether the patient was already anti-MOG positive before the COVID-19 infection or became seropositive through molecular mimicry remains to be answered, as the patient hadn't been tested before and his prior medical history was noncontributory.

In summary, COVID-19 has been associated with MOGAD optic perineuritis. We hypothesize that concomitant cytokine storm in COVID-19 could exacerbate severe visual loss in this setting. Clinicians should be aware of the association of COVID-19 and MOGAD.
