Sublinear iterations can suffice even for DDPMs
Matthew S. Zhang, Stephen Huan, Jerry Huang, Nicholas M. Boffi, Sitan Chen, Sinho Chewi

Abstract
SDE-based methods such as denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) have shown remarkable success in real-world sample generation tasks. Prior analyses of DDPMs have been focused on the exponential Euler discretization, showing guarantees that generally depend at least linearly on the dimension or initial Fisher information. Inspired by works in log-concave sampling ( 59 ) , we analyze an integrator – the denoising diffusion randomized midpoint method (DDRaM) – that leverages an additional randomized midpoint to better approximate the SDE. Using a recently-developed analytic framework called the “shifted composition rule”, we show that this algorithm enjoys favorable discretization properties under appropriate smoothness assumptions, with sublinear O ~ ​ ( d ) \widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d}) score evaluations needed to ensure convergence. This is the first sublinear complexity bound for pure DDPM sampling — prior works which obtained such bounds worked instead with ODE-based sampling and had to make modifications to the sampler which deviate from how they are used in practice. We also provide experimental validation of the advantages of our method, showing that it performs well in practice with pre-trained image synthesis models.

On the smoothness assumption.
The main caveat relative to the prior
O(d)

This is well out of scope of this work, the focus of which is instead on bringing our theoretical understanding of DDPMs closer to what is known for DDIMs.

Comparison to prior work

Below we describe relevant prior work in the theoretical study of diffusion models.

Discretization analyses for DDPMs.

Discretization analyses for DDIMs.

As mentioned above, all known diffusion-based sampling guarantees achieving sublinear complexity are based on DDIM sampling.
15

O(L^{2}\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon)
for ODE-based samplers under the assumption that the true scores and the score estimates are

Under the same assumptions,

The randomized midpoint method was first introduced by

4
Technical overview

We first discuss the difficulties inherent in analyzing (
RMD
).
The original analysis of
59
, which inspired almost all subsequent analyses of randomized midpoint, is based on a coupling argument in
W_{2}
.
However,
all state-of-the-art analyses for diffusion models work in
\TV
or
\KL
.
When we try to apply the former to the latter, we therefore arrive at a fundamental incongruity.
Indeed,
W_{2}
analyses of diffusion models often incur exponential accumulation of errors, unless overly restrictive assumptions such as strong log-concavity are imposed on
\pi_{0}
, e.g.,
11
;
24
;
25
;
69
.
One way in which existing works achieving sublinear complexity have circumvented this is to introduce
corrector steps
which periodically inject randomness into the dynamics to essentially convert
W_{2}
bounds into
\KL
bounds
(
15
;
29
;
36
;
45
)
. This is an option that we cannot afford in this work, as our goal is to simply analyze a discretization of the vanilla DDPM reverse process without further algorithmic modifications.

In light of this, how can we analyze randomized midpoint discretization in
\TV
or
\KL
?
There are two main challenges.
The first is that standard approaches, such as Girsanov’s theorem, do not readily apply to (
RMD
), because natural interpolations of (
RMD
) are not Markovian: the intermediate point
X^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}
“sees into the future” for times
t\leq t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}
.

The second challenge is that the analysis should be fairly sharp in order to see a tangible benefit from (
RMD
). Indeed, the intuition behind (
RMD
) is that the use of a randomized step size to define
X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}
effectively “debiases” the algorithm.
This is formalized via the notions of weak and strong errors
(
54
)
.
Consider a single iteration on
[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, with the random variables
X_{t_{k+1}}^{\mathsf{alg}}
and
X_{t_{k+1}}
obtained by solving (
RMD
) and (
rev-OU
) respectively, from the same initial condition
X_{t_{k}}^{\mathsf{alg}}=X_{t_{k}}=x
.
The weak and strong errors are defined as follows:

Weak error:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{E}X^{\mathsf{alg}}_{t_{k+1}}-\mathbb{E}X_{t_{k+1}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)\,,

Strong error:

\displaystyle\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_{k+1}}\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{strong}}(x)\,.

These two notions loosely capture the squared “bias” and “variance” of the discretization scheme at a single step.
When the weak error is substantially smaller than the strong error—as is the case for (
RMD
)---then one can prove improved discretization bounds, basically because stochastic fluctuations cancel out à la the central limit theorem.
3
3

3

A simple analogy is that the sum of
N
i.i.d. random variables, each with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
, has size roughly
N\mu+N^{1/2}\sigma
; think of
\mu
as the weak error and
\sigma
as the strong error.

Unfortunately, as can be seen from the definitions, the weak and strong errors are most easily controlled via coupling methods, which are most easily handled in
W_{2}
.

In summary, we require an analysis framework that works in
\TV
or
\KL
, which is flexible enough to handle discretizations without Markovian interpolations, and which can witness the benefits of smaller weak errors.

Shifted composition.

In the literature on log-concave sampling, in which the randomized midpoint discretization first arose, obtaining
\KL
guarantees was also a longstanding challenge until very recently.
A series of papers
(
3
;
4
;
2
)
has developed a new framework, known as
shifted composition
, which satisfies our desiderata above.
We therefore aim to adapt it to the setting of diffusion models.

Briefly, the idea behind shifted composition is that in order to control the
\KL
divergence between two processes (taken to be the algorithm and the “ideal” process it approximates), we can introduce a third process—called the auxiliary process—which is initialized at one of the two processes but is
shifted
to hit the second process at a terminal time.
The hitting condition ensures that the
\KL
divergence between the two original processes at the terminal time is controlled by the
\KL
between the first process and the auxiliary process.
Due to the definition of the auxiliary process, this latter
\KL
can be controlled in terms of a distance recursion which incorporates the weak and strong errors.

Adaptation to diffusion models.

Although shifted composition is well-suited for our needs, we stress that there are additional technical challenges in the diffusion model setting.
Namely, under Assumption
3
, the Lipschitz constant is changing with time; moreover, Theorem
3
uses a non-uniform step size schedule. Accommodating these complications requires an extension of the original shifted composition framework; see Appendix
A
for details.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that stochastic diffusion model samplers can break the
O(d)
complexity barrier given the right discretization and a natural Lipschitz assumption for the score estimator. Empirically, we find the randomized midpoint performs well in a variety of settings, outperforming both Euler and Heun for both stochastic SDE and deterministic ODE sampling.
Several interesting lines of exploration remain for future work.
First, it may be possible to combine our analysis with works that establish discretization guarantees depending only on the
intrinsic
dimension.
Second, it would be quite interesting if Assumptions
3
on the score estimator could be removed, thereby providing an analysis under the minimal assumptions of
5
;
19
.
This may be challenging, as it seems incompatible with our proof technique.
Another possible avenue would be to replace our Lipschitz conditions with a relaxed Lipschitz condition, similarly to
36
, which would imply substantially better guarantees for Gaussian mixtures.

A.2
Review of the shifted composition framework

The shifted composition framework for proving discretization bounds for Markov processes, developed in the sequence of works
3
;
4
;
2
, allows for the translation of Wasserstein/coupling-based errors to
\KL
guarantees. They also allow the user to account for the difference between “weak” and “strong” errors. Suppose first that the following assumptions hold.

Assumption 4

(Wasserstein regularity results)
.

Suppose that we have a sequence of kernels
(P_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(P_{n}^{\mathsf{alg}})_{n\geq 0}
for which the following properties hold. Namely, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
X^{\mathsf{alg}}\sim\delta_{x}P_{n}^{\mathsf{alg}}
,
Y\sim\delta_{y}P_{n}
,
Y^{\mathsf{alg}}\sim\delta_{y}P_{n}^{\mathsf{alg}}
be coupled. Then, for functions
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{weak}},\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{strong}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, constants
L,\gamma\geq 0
, assume that the following hold:

1.

Weak error:

\lVert\mathbb{E}Y^{\mathsf{alg}}-\mathbb{E}Y\rVert\leq\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{weak}}(y)
.

2.

Strong error:

\lVert Y^{\mathsf{alg}}-Y\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{strong}}(y)
for some coupling of
(Y^{\mathsf{alg}},Y)
.

3.

Wasserstein Lipschitzness:

\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-Y^{\mathsf{alg}}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq L\,\lVert x-y\rVert
for some coupling of
(X^{\mathsf{alg}},Y^{\mathsf{alg}})
.

4.

Coupling:

\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-x-(Y^{\mathsf{alg}}-y)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\gamma\,\lVert x-y\rVert
for some coupling of
(X^{\mathsf{alg}},Y^{\mathsf{alg}})
.

Without loss of generality in this work, assume
L\geq 1
.

Additionally, some conditions on the
\KL
divergence are necessary to obtain guarantees.

Assumption 5

(KL regularity)
.

With the same notation as Assumption
4
, assume that the following holds: for a parameter
c\geq 0
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\KL(\delta_{x}P_{n}^{\mathsf{alg}}\mathbin{\|}\delta_{y}P_{n}^{\mathsf{alg}})\leq c\,\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}
.

Then, the following guarantee holds.

Lemma 6
.

Under Assumptions
4
and
5
, if
L\geq 1+\frac{\log N}{N}
, we have for some measure
\pi^{\mathrm{approx}}
and any initial measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that, defining
\bar{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}}\coloneqq P_{1}^{\mathsf{alg}}\dotsm P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}}
and
\bar{P}_{k}\coloneqq P_{1}\dotsm P_{k}

\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\mathrm{approx}}\mathbin{\|}\pi\bar{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{N})\lesssim c\,\bigl(\{(L-1)N\vee\log N\}\,\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{strong}}^{2}+(N-1)\,(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{weak}}^{2}+\gamma\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{strong}}^{2})\bigr)\,.

Furthermore, we have for
N\geq 2L/(L-1)

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\pi^{\mathrm{approx}},\pi\bar{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{N})\lesssim\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{strong}}^{2}+\log\bigl(\frac{L}{L-1}\bigr)\,(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{weak}}^{2}+\gamma\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{strong}}^{2})\,.

Here,

The proof of this theorem is accomplished by considering, if
(X^{\mathsf{alg}}_{t_{k}})_{k\in[N]},(Y_{t_{k}})_{k\in[N]}

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{0}^{\mathsf{aux}}\coloneqq Y_{0}\,,\qquad\tilde{Y}_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\coloneqq Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}}+\upeta_{n}\,(Y_{t_{n}}-Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}})\,,\qquad Y_{t_{n+1}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\sim P^{\mathsf{alg}}_{n+1}(\tilde{Y}_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}},\cdot)\,,

for an appropriate sequence of shifts

, and then judiciously applying Assumptions

and

. Note that the framework above does not account for the case where the constants vary between the different indices of the kernels

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl\lVert\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(X^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}})-\nabla\log\frac{\pi_{T-t}}{\upgamma}(X_{t})\bigr)\,e^{t-t_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}^{2}h_{k}^{2}\,\lVert X^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}-X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}\rVert^{2}\,.

We then split the first term into

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lVert\nabla\log\pi_{T-t}(X_{t})-\mathsf{s}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}})\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathsf{G}^{2}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k},t}(x)+\mathsf{F}^{2}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}(x)\,.

For the second term, we can reuse (
A.1
). This gives our desired bound.
∎

The following lemma follows from applying the Lipschitz property of the estimator, as well as the bound (
A.1
) that we previously derived.

Lemma 8

(Score estimator bounds)
.

We have, for
X_{t}^{+}(x)
obtained from (
RMD
) conditional on
X_{t_{k-1}}^{\mathsf{alg}}=x
, for
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lVert\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t}(X_{t}^{+}(x))\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}^{2}h_{k}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t}\bigl(\mathsf{G}_{t_{k-1},s}^{2}(x)+\mathsf{F}_{t_{k-1}}^{2}(x)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathsf{G}_{t_{k-1},t}^{2}(x)+\mathsf{F}_{t}^{2}(x)+\bigl\lVert\nabla\log\frac{\pi_{T-{t_{k-1}}}}{\upgamma}(x)\bigr\rVert^{2}\,.

Recall that the local errors
(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{weak}}_{k})^{2}
,
(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{k})^{2}
are simply the pointwise local errors from Lemma
7
, averaged over
x\sim\pi_{T-t_{k-1}}
.

Lemma 9

(Local errors)
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following errors, taking
1-\varrho_{k}\asymp h_{k}^{r}
for some power
r\geq 2
at each step (treated as an absolute constant), with
h_{k}\ll 1/\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}
always,

(a)

Weak error:

\displaystyle(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{weak}}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{k}^{2}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}+{\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}^{2}h_{k}^{4}}\,(\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}-\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k-1}})+h_{k}^{2+2r}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}\,.

(b)

Strong error:

\displaystyle(\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{strong}}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{k}^{2}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}+h_{k}^{2}\,(\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}-\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k-1}})\,.

Note that the main difference between the two errors is the additional error term
h_{k}^{2}\,(\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}-\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k-1}})
in the strong error.

Proof.

To bound these in expectation, assuming that
X\sim\pi_{T-t_{k-1}}
, we have from Lemma
5
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{k-1}\leq s\leq t\leq t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{T-t_{k-1}}}[\mathsf{G}_{s,t}^{2}(X)]\leq\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}-\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k-1}}\,.

Here, we note that
t\mapsto\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t}
is monotonically increasing along the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{T-t_{k-1}}}[\mathsf{F}_{t}^{2}(X)]\lesssim\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,.

Substituting these into Lemma
7
, and using Lemma
8
concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Verifying the assumptions of shifted composition

Next, we check the hypotheses of the shifted composition local error framework (see Appendix
A.2
).

Lemma 10

(Properties of (
RMD
))
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the Markov kernels
P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}}
corresponding to (
RMD
) satisfy the following properties, with the same definitions as Lemma
7
.
Let
Y^{\mathsf{alg}}
denote the output of (
RMD
) starting from
y
.
Assume that
h_{k}\ll 1/\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}
, and define
\mathsf{p}_{k}\coloneqq{\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}h_{k}}
.

(a)

Wasserstein Lipschitzness:

\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-Y^{\mathsf{alg}}\rVert_{L^{2}}-\lVert x-y\rVert\lesssim\mathsf{p}_{k}\,\lVert x-y\rVert
.

(b)

Coupling:

\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-Y^{\mathsf{alg}}-(x-y)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\mathsf{p}_{k}\,\lVert x-y\rVert
.

(c)

Regularity:
Let
\varrho_{k}\in[0,1)
be a parameter which is arbitrarily close to
1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\KL(\delta_{x}P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}}\mathbin{\|}\delta_{y}P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}})\lesssim\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{h_{k}}\log\frac{1}{1-\varrho_{k}}\,.

Proof.

(a)

This follows from (b).

(b)

Fixing
\tau_{k}
and synchronously coupling the Brownian motions, we have

\displaystyle\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-Y^{\mathsf{alg}}-(x-y)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,\lVert x-y\rVert

\displaystyle\qquad{}+2\,(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+})-\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(Y_{t_{k-1}}^{+})\rVert

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,\lVert x-y\rVert+2\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}\,(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,\lVert X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}-Y_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}\rVert\,.

As for the second term, we can bound it again via synchronous coupling:

\displaystyle\lVert X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}-Y_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert e^{-\tau_{k}}\,(x-y)+2\,(1-e^{-\tau_{k}})\,(\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}}(x)-\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}}(y))\rVert

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}h_{k})\,\lVert x-y\rVert\lesssim\lVert x-y\rVert\,.

(c)

We apply a familiar trick from
4
where we compute the conditional
\KL
given
\tau_{k}
, and then integrate. It is for this reason that we need to truncate our random variable
\tau_{k}
. Condition on
\omega_{k}\coloneqq\{\tau_{k},(B_{t})_{t\leq t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{x}P_{k\mid\omega_{k}}^{\mathsf{alg}}=\mathcal{N}\bigl(e^{-h_{k}}x+2\,(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(X^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}})+\zeta_{k,1},\,(1-e^{-2(h_{k}-\tau_{k})})\,I_{d}\bigr)\,,

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{k,1}=\sqrt{2}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}e^{s-t_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\,.

Using the formula for the
\KL
divergence between two Gaussians, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\lVert e^{-h_{k}}\,(x-y)+2\,(1-e^{-h_{k}})\,(\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(X^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}})-\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}_{T-t_{k-1}-\tau_{k}}(Y^{+}_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}))\rVert^{2}}{2\,(1-e^{-2(h_{k}-\tau_{k})})}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{1}{1-e^{-2(h_{k}-\tau_{k})}}\,\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}+\frac{\beta_{t_{k}}^{2}h_{k}^{2}}{1-e^{-2(h_{k}-\tau_{k})}}\,\lVert X_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}-Y_{t_{k-1}+\tau_{k}}^{+}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{1-e^{-2(h_{k}-\tau_{k})}}\,.

Linearizing the denominator for
h_{k}\lesssim 1
and
\tau_{k}\in[0,\varrho_{k}h_{k}]
for some parameter
\varrho_{k}
approaching
1
,

\displaystyle\KL(\delta_{x}P_{k\mid\omega_{k}}^{\mathsf{alg}}\mathbin{\|}\delta_{y}P_{k\mid\omega_{k}}^{\mathsf{alg}})\lesssim\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{h_{k}-\tau_{k}}\,.

Taking expectations and using joint convexity, we find

\displaystyle\KL(\delta_{x}P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}}\mathbin{\|}\delta_{y}P_{k}^{\mathsf{alg}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{h_{k}}\log\frac{1}{1-\varrho_{k}}\,.

∎

A.5
Integral computations

Now, we need a bespoke version of the original local error recursion from
4
which holds for the time-varying step sizes considered in this work.
We consider the following step size choice, which satisfies
h_{k}\ll 1/\tilde{\beta}_{t_{k}}
.

\displaystyle h_{k}\coloneqq\frac{C_{h}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\,\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})\,T}}\min\{1,T-t_{k}\}\asymp\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})\,T}}\cdot(1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})})\,.

(A.2)

Here,
C_{h}\asymp 1
is an absolute constant. Let us briefly justify this. When
T-t_{k}\leq 1/\tilde{\beta}_{0}
, then
\frac{1}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\asymp\frac{1}{T-t_{k}}
. Otherwise,
\frac{1}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\asymp 1
.
We also select the shift

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=\frac{C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{1-e^{-2(T-t)}}\,,

where again
C_{\eta}\asymp 1
.

The following proof is heavily based on the argument of
4
. Although we briefly describe the high-level idea in the subsequent proof, a detailed discussion of the shifted composition framework is beyond the scope of this paper and we refer to
4
.

Lemma 11
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, with the choice of step-size given in (
A.2
) and for
T\geq 1
and
t_{N}\in(T-\frac{1}{6},T)
, there exists a probability measure
\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}
such that

\displaystyle\KL(\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{alg}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\KL(\pi_{T}\mathbin{\|}\upgamma)+\Bigl(T+\frac{1}{T}\log\frac{1}{T-t_{N}}\Bigr)\,\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\log\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})\,T}}{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}\,(T-t_{N})}\,.

Furthermore, if we consider
d_{N}^{2}=\lVert Y^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t_{N}}-Y_{t_{N}}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
where
Y_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\sim\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}
and
Y_{t_{N}}\sim\pi_{t_{N}}
, then

\displaystyle d_{N}^{2}\lesssim\Bigl((T-t_{N})^{2}+\frac{T-t_{N}}{T}\Bigr)\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}}\,.

Proof.

The idea is to define an auxiliary process
(Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}})_{n\leq N}
with
Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\sim\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}
.
The auxiliary process is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{0}^{\mathsf{aux}}\sim\pi_{T}\,,\qquad\tilde{Y}_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\coloneqq Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}}+\upeta_{n}\,(Y_{t_{n}}-Y_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}})\,,\qquad Y_{t_{n+1}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\sim P^{\mathsf{alg}}_{n+1}(\tilde{Y}_{t_{n}}^{\mathsf{aux}},\cdot)\,.

Here,
\upeta_{n}\coloneqq\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\eta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
, and
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denotes (
rev-OU
).
In other words, the auxiliary process follows the (
RMD
) algorithm (i.e., using an estimated score and time discretization), but we interleave steps which shift the auxiliary process toward the true reverse process.

By the KL chain rule,

\displaystyle\KL(\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{aux}}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t_{N}}^{\mathsf{alg}})

\displaystyle\leq\KL(\pi_{T}\mathbin{\|}\upgamma)+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{T}}\KL(\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{aux}}_{x}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{x})\,,

where
\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{aux}}_{x}
,
\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{x}
denote path measures started from
x
.

Define
d_{n}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\lVert Y^{\mathsf{aux}}_{n}-Y_{t_{n}}\rVert^{2}]
and note that
d_{0}=0
. We compute the KL divergence between the auxiliary process and the algorithm using the shifted composition technique and Lemma
10
; see
4
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{T}}\KL(\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{aux}}_{x}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{x})\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\upeta_{k}^{2}d_{k}^{2}}{h_{k}}\log\frac{1}{h_{k}}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{N}h_{k}\eta_{kh}^{2}d_{k}^{2}\log\frac{1}{h_{k}}\,.

The next step is to simplify the computation by approximating the sum by an integral, as was done in
2
.
In this proof, we reserve the
mathtt
font for continuous-time interpolations of discrete quantities appearing in this proof.
Thus,
\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}
interpolates
d_{n}^{2}
, i.e.,
\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}\coloneqq d_{t_{n}}^{2}
where
t_{n}\leq t\leq t_{n+1}
. Similarly,
\mathtt{h}_{t}
is defined similarly to
h_{k}
in (
A.2
), replacing
t_{k}
with
t
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_{T}}\KL(\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{aux}}_{x}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}_{x})\lesssim\int_{0}^{t_{N}}\eta_{t}^{2}\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\mathtt{h}_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

We next write down a recursion for
d_{n}^{2}
. This is the usual local error recursion, see
4
.

\displaystyle d_{n}^{2}\leq(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})^{2}\,(1-\upeta_{n})^{2}\,d_{n-1}^{2}+2\,(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{weak}}+\mathsf{p}_{n}\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{strong}})\,(1-\upeta_{n})\,d_{n-1}+(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}\,.

Here, we invoke Lemma
10
, the conclusion of which involves hidden universal constants. By redefining
\mathsf{p}_{n}
(so that
\mathsf{p}_{n}=O(\tilde{\beta}_{t_{n}}h_{n})
), we write the above recursion without any universal constants, which simplifies the following computations.

Applying Young’s inequality on the middle term, we find that

\displaystyle d_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})\,(1-\upeta_{n})\,d_{n-1}^{2}+O\Bigl(\frac{(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{weak}}+\mathsf{p}_{n}\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}}{(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})\,(1-\upeta_{n})-(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})^{2}\,(1-\upeta_{n})^{2}}+(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}\Bigr)\,.

To simplify the denominator, let us make the ansatz (which we will verify later) that
\mathsf{p}_{n},\upeta_{n}\ll 1
and
(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})\,(1-\upeta_{n})<1
.
This then yields the following recursion, noting that
d_{0}^{2}=0
is assumed:

\displaystyle d_{n}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl(\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1+\mathsf{p}_{j})\,(1-\upeta_{j})\Bigr)\,\Bigl(\frac{(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{\mathrm{weak}})^{2}}{\upeta_{k}-\mathsf{p}_{k}}+(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}\Bigr)\,.

In such a case, given our choice of step size and shift, defining

\displaystyle\mathtt{p}_{t}\asymp\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\mathtt{h}_{t}}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\,,\qquad\mathtt{h}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{C_{h}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}}\,,

so that naturally
\mathsf{p}_{k}=\mathtt{p}_{t_{k}}
,
h_{k}=\mathtt{h}_{t_{k}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\upeta_{k}-\mathsf{p}_{k}\asymp\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\mathtt{h}_{t_{k}}}{1-e^{-2(T-t_{k})}}\asymp\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}}{\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}}\,,

under our choices as well.
We indeed have
(1+\mathsf{p}_{n})\,(1-\upeta_{n})<1
if we choose
C_{\eta}
to be a sufficiently large absolute constant, and
\mathsf{p}_{n},\upeta_{n}\ll 1
.

Furthermore, define the following:

\displaystyle(\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}\coloneqq\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}^{2}_{0}\,(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}\,\partial_{t}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t}\,,

\displaystyle(\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{weak}})^{2}\coloneqq\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{4}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}^{2}_{0}\,(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})^{2}T^{2}}\,\partial_{t}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t}\,.

These are obtained by taking the local errors from Lemma
9
, dividing by a factor of
h_{k}
(which is helpful when converting from the summation to the integral approximation), and taking the continuous-time interpolation.
Here, the contribution from the
h_{k}^{2r}
term can be seen to be negligible, taking
r\geq 4
sufficiently large and bounding
\mathsf{g}^{2}
using Lemma
4
. Note that the finite difference
\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k}}-\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t_{k-1}}
converts into a derivative. Finally, we have for absolute constants
\bar{c},c
that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1+\mathsf{p}_{j})\,(1-\upeta_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\bigl(c\tilde{\beta}_{t_{j}}h_{j}-\frac{C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}h_{j}}{c\,(1-e^{-2(T-t_{j})})}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{n}}\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{1-e^{-2(T-t)}}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\,,

so long as we choose
C_{\eta}
to be a sufficiently large absolute constant.
We then substitute this into

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Bigl(-\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{1-e^{-2(T-r)}}\,\mathrm{d}r\Bigr)\,\Bigl((\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{strong}})^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}}{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}}\,(\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{weak}})^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

If we substitute in the definitions of
\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{weak}}
,
\mathtt{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{strong}}
,

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Bigl(-\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{1-e^{-2(T-r)}}\,\mathrm{d}r\Bigr)\,\Bigl(\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,\frac{1-e^{-2(T-s)}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-s)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}\,(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}\,\partial_{s}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{s}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}\,\Bigl(\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,\frac{1-e^{-2(T-s)}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-s)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}\,(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}\,\partial_{s}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{s}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Let us now simplify some of these integrals.
First, for
K\coloneqq\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}\gg 1
, and using the change of variables
v=e^{-2(T-s)}
,
\mathrm{d}v=2v\,\mathrm{d}s
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}(1-e^{-2(T-s)})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=(e^{2(T-t)}-1)^{K}\int_{0}^{t}(v^{-1}-1)^{-K}\,v\,(v^{-1}-1)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{(e^{2(T-t)}-1)^{K}}{2}\int_{e^{-2T}}^{e^{-2(T-t)}}\bigl(\frac{v}{1-v}\bigr)^{K-1}\mathrm{d}v\,.

Next, let
\omega\coloneqq e^{1/K}
.

\displaystyle\int_{e^{-2T}}^{e^{-2(T-t)}}\bigl(\frac{v}{1-v}\bigr)^{K-1}\mathrm{d}v=\sum_{j\geq 0}\int_{\omega^{j}\leq(1-v)/(1-e^{-2(T-t)})\leq\omega^{j+1}}\bigl(\frac{v}{1-v}\bigr)^{K-1}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}\leq\frac{1}{(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{1}{\omega^{(K-1)j}}\int_{\omega^{j}\leq(1-v)/(1-e^{-2(T-t)})\leq\omega^{j+1}}v^{K-1}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad{}\leq\frac{1}{(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-2(K-1)(T-t)}}{\omega^{(K-1)j}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})\,\omega^{j}\,(\omega-1)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\frac{e^{-2(K-1)(T-t)}}{K\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{1-e^{-2(T-t)}}{\omega^{(K-2)j}}\lesssim\frac{e^{-2(K-1)(T-t)}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})}{K\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\,.

On the other hand, a naïve bound is

\displaystyle\int_{e^{-2T}}^{e^{-2(T-t)}}\bigl(\frac{v}{1-v}\bigr)^{K-1}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{e^{-2T}}^{e^{-2(T-t)}}v^{K-1}\,\mathrm{d}v}{(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\leq\frac{e^{-2K(T-t)}}{K\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{K-1}}\,.

Using the naïve bound for
T-t\gtrsim 1
, and the refined bound for
T-t\lesssim 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}(1-e^{-2(T-s)})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}}\,.

On the other hand, integrating by parts, letting

\displaystyle f(s,t)\coloneqq\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-s)})^{2}=e^{-4(T-s)}\,\frac{(e^{2(T-t)}-1)^{K}}{(e^{2(T-s)}-1)^{K-2}}

which is increasing in
s
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-s)})^{2}\,\partial_{s}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(t,t)\,\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t}-f(0,t)\,\mathsf{g}^{2}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}f(s,t)\,\mathsf{g}^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq f(t,t)\,\mathsf{g}^{2}_{t}\,.

Together with Lemma
4
, it yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\frac{e^{2(T-t)}-1}{e^{2(T-s)}-1}\Bigr)^{\frac{\bar{c}C_{\eta}\tilde{\beta}_{0}}{2}}\,(1-e^{-2(T-s)})^{2}\,\partial_{s}\mathsf{g}^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim d\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}\,.

Finally, this all implies that

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\operatorname{score}}^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{2}\,(d+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2})T}\,[d\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})+\mathtt{M}_{2}^{2}\,(1-e^{-2(T-t)})^{2}]\,.

Examples satisfying Assumption
3

We provide some examples of distributions where Assumption

holds for the true scores, i.e., for
\mathsf{s}_{t}=\nabla\log\pi_{t}
.
The following examples all come from the literature on quantitative Lipschitz estimates of Kim–Milman maps (i.e., flow map for the probability flow ODE) which were originally used to establish log-Sobolev inequalities. For completeness, we provide derivations below.

•

Log-concave measures.
Let
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V)
with

.
Then, Assumption
3
holds with
\tilde{\beta}_{0}\leq 1
.

•

Lipschitz perturbations of strongly log-concave measures.
Let
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V-W)
, where
V
is
\alpha
-strongly convex (
\alpha>0
) and
W
is
L
-Lipschitz.
Then, Assumption
3
holds with
\tilde{\beta}_{0}\leq L^{2}/\alpha\vee 1
.

•

Semi-log-concave over compact sets.
Let
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V)
over a compact set with diameter at most
R
, and such that

for some
\alpha<0

Then, Assumption

holds with

Gaussian convolutions of compactly supported measures.
Let
\pi_{0}=\nu*\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

\nu
has compact support, of diameter at most

holds with
\tilde{\beta}_{0}\lesssim 1\vee R^{2}

Strongly log-concave outside a ball.
Let
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V)

satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{\lVert x-y\rVert=r}\frac{\langle\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(y),x-y\rangle}{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}\geq\begin{cases}\alpha-\beta\,,&\lVert x-y\rVert\leq R\,,\\
\alpha\,,&\lVert x-y\rVert>R\,,\end{cases}

We remark that in all of these examples except the first, the log-Sobolev constant of

scales exponentially in

This implies that, given access to an accurate score estimator, diffusion models are far superior to standard MCMC methods such as the Langevin diffusion.

We also provide one instance showing the failure of Assumption

Two point masses.
Consider

Log-concave measures.

Let
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V)
where

is strongly log-concave. The conditional distribution of

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{t}(x)=-\frac{I_{d}}{1-e^{-2t}}+\frac{e^{-2t}}{(1-e^{-2t})^{2}}\operatorname{cov}(X_{0}^{\rightarrow}\mid X_{t}^{\rightarrow}=x)\,.

(B.1)

Now, the reverse conditional measure has the form

\displaystyle\pi_{0\mid t}(x\mid y)\propto\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\lVert y-e^{-t}x\rVert^{2}}{2\,(1-e^{-2t})}-V(x)\Bigr)\,,

so that

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{0\mid t}(x\mid y)=\frac{e^{-2t}}{1-e^{-2t}}\,I_{d}+\nabla^{2}V(x)\succeq\frac{e^{-2t}}{1-e^{-2t}}\,I_{d}\,.

(B.2)

The Brascamp–Lieb inequality
(
9
)
then allows us to bound the covariance by the inverse of the matrix above. Thus, after some algebra,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\Bigl(\nabla^{2}\log\frac{\pi_{t}}{\upgamma}\Bigr)=\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\log\pi_{t}+I_{d})\leq 1\,.

The minimum eigenvalue can be lower bounded in (
B.1
) by taking the covariance to be zero, which shows that
\tilde{\beta}_{0}=1
is sufficient.

Lipschitz perturbations of strongly log-concave measures.

Next, suppose
\pi_{0}\propto\exp(-V-W)

is
\alpha

where

\displaystyle\nu_{\tau,y}(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{2\tau}+\frac{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x)\,.

Following the argument of

\displaystyle\lVert\cov_{\nu_{\tau,y}}\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}\leq(\sqrt{\lVert\cov_{\tilde{\nu}_{\tau,y}}\rVert}+W_{2}(\nu_{\tau,y},\tilde{\nu}_{\tau,y}))^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{\tau,y}(x)\propto\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{2\tau}+\frac{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{2}-V(x)\Bigr)\,.

Using Brascamp–Lieb, the first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\lVert\cov_{\tilde{\nu}_{\tau,y}}\rVert\leq\alpha-1+\frac{1}{\tau}\,.

On the other hand, by the

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{t,y},\tilde{\nu}_{t,y})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}^{2}(\tilde{\nu}_{t,y})\,\mathsf{FI}(\nu_{t,y}\mathbin{\|}\tilde{\nu}_{t,y})\,.

C.2
Variants of the randomized midpoint

Our starting point is the semi-linear SDE (
SDE
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=(\lambda(t)X_{t}+f_{t}(X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,.

(C.1)

From the intuition that a linear SDE of the form
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\lambda(t)X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}
admits a closed-form solution, we use the ODE integrating factor
\omega(t)\coloneqq\exp(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda)
as an ansatz. By Itô’s rule,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(\omega(t)X_{t})

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\omega(t))\,X_{t}+\omega(t)\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=-\lambda(t)\,\omega(t)X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\omega(t)\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\lambda(t)X_{t}+f_{t}(X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\omega(t)\,f_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\omega(t)\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

where we have successfully removed the linear term.
Integrating both sides from some starting time
t_{0}
to
t_{0}+h
, we have the integral representation

\displaystyle\omega(t_{0}+h)X_{t_{0}+h}-\omega(t_{0})X_{t_{0}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}{\omega(t)}\,f_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}{\omega(t)}\,g(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

\displaystyle X_{t_{0}+h}=\frac{\omega(t_{0})}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}X_{t_{0}}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,f_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,.

(INT)

In order to approximate (
INT
) we perform a two-step discretization scheme. First, we draw a random time
\tau
from the density proportional to
t\mapsto\mathsf{1}_{[t_{0},t_{0}+h]}(t)\,\omega(t)
which serves as our midpoint. Defining
\Omega(t)\coloneqq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega
, explicitly

\displaystyle\tau\sim p(\tau)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\omega(t)}{\Omega(t_{0}+h)-\Omega(t_{0})}\,,&t_{0}\leq\tau\leq t_{0}+h\,;\\
0\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

We can use the plug-in estimator

to obtain an unbiased estimate to the integral in the fashion of Monte Carlo quadrature. Unfortunately we do not know the value of

, necessitating a second approximation. We use an Euler scheme, assuming that the function is constant on the interval and taking the left endpoint (which we do know). Thus, we have

\displaystyle X^{+}_{t_{0}+\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(t_{0})}{\omega(t_{0}+\tau)}\,X_{t_{0}}+\frac{\Omega(t_{0}+\tau)}{\omega(t_{0}+\tau)}\,f_{t_{0}}(X_{t_{0}})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+\tau)}\,g(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

\displaystyle X_{t_{0}+h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(t_{0})}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,X_{t_{0}}+\frac{\Omega(t_{0}+h)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,f_{t_{0}+\tau}(X_{t_{0}+\tau})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,.

It remains to treat the noise terms. Define the stochastic process

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\omega(t^{\prime})}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t^{\prime}}\,.

Clearly
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=0
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\omega(t^{\prime})^{2}}{\omega(t_{0}+h)^{2}}\,g(t^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,,

by an application of Itô’s rule. We will compute

\displaystyle Y_{t_{0}+h}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{t_{0}+\tau}^{t_{0}+h}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(t_{0}+\tau)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+\tau)}\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\int_{t_{0}+\tau}^{t_{0}+h}\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega(t_{0}+h)}\,g(t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}

) and collecting linear terms, resulting in
\lambda(t)=\dot{c}(t)/c(t)+\dot{\sigma}(t)/\sigma(t)
. We can also account for the skip connection in the denoiser itself, resulting in the choice of

\displaystyle\lambda(t)=\frac{\dot{c}(t)}{c(t)}+(1-c_{\text{skip}}(t))\,\frac{\dot{\sigma}(t)}{\sigma(t)}\,,

where
c_{\text{skip}}(t)
is the skip connection in the denoiser
D(\cdot;\sigma)
(see Table 1 of
39
). In particular, we consider
c_{\text{skip}}(t)=\sigma_{\text{data}}^{2}/(\sigma(t)^{2}+\sigma_{\text{data}}^{2})
for
\sigma_{\text{data}}=0.5
.

In our experiments we use the relative score for the OU and VP processes, the non-relative score for the EDM process, and the skip connection for the VE process.
