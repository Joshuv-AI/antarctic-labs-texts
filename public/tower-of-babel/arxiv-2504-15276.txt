Improved Algorithms for Quantum MaxCut via Partially Entangled Matchings
Anuj Apte, Eunou Lee, Kunal Marwaha, Ojas Parekh, James Sud

Abstract
We introduce a 0.611 0.611 -approximation algorithm for Quantum MaxCut and a 1 + 5 4 ≈ 0.809 \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}\approx 0.809 -approximation algorithm for the EPR Hamiltonian of [ Kin23 ] . A novel ingredient in both of these algorithms is to partially entangle pairs of qubits associated to edges in a matching, while preserving the direction of their single-qubit Bloch vectors. This allows us to interpolate between product states and matching-based states with a tunable parameter.



1
Introduction

Given a graph
G(V,E,w)
with positive edge weights
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a
2
-local Hamiltonian term
h
, define the
n
-qubit Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E(G)}w_{ij}\cdot h_{ij}\,,

where
h_{ij}
is the local term
h
applied on qubits
(i,j)
. For two particular local terms
h
, we are interested in the problem of computing the the maximum energy of
H_{G}
, which we denote as
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})
, for any
G
. This is not an easy task in general: deciding if
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})
is above some threshold with inverse polynomial accuracy is known to be
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-hard
[
PM15
]
.

In the first problem, we choose the local term

\displaystyle h^{QMC}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{i}I_{j}-X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j}-Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)=2\ket{\psi_{-}}_{ij}\bra{\psi_{-}}_{ij}\,,

where
\ket{\psi_{-}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{01}-\ket{10}\right)
is the singlet state. This problem has recently been studied under the name
Quantum MaxCut
(QMC)
[
GP19
]
. In the statistical mechanics literature, Hamiltonians
H_{G}
defined by
h^{QMC}
are instances of the zero-field quantum Heisenberg XXX
1/2
model. The decision version of QMC is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-hard
[
PM15
]
.

In the second problem, we choose the local term

\displaystyle h^{EPR}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{i}I_{j}+X_{i}X_{j}-Y_{i}Y_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)=2\ket{\phi_{+}}_{ij}\bra{\phi_{+}}_{ij}\,,

where
\ket{\phi_{+}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\right)
. This problem, named
EPR
by
[
Kin23
]
, is thought to be easier than QMC, since Hamiltonians
H_{G}
defined by
h^{EPR}
are
stoquastic
(sign-problem free)
[
PM15
,
CM16
]
. In fact, it is not yet clear if EPR can be solved in polynomial time.

In lieu of exactly computing
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})
, we may try to
approximate
this value. In both problems, the local term
h
is positive semidefinite, so
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})\geq 0
. We judge an approximation by its
approximation ratio
. Suppose we can find efficiently computable functions
\ell,u
such that for all graphs
G
,

\displaystyle 0\leq\ell(G)\leq\lambda_{max}(H_{G})\leq u(G)\,.

Then, the approximation ratio
\alpha
is at least

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\min_{G}\frac{\ell(G)}{\lambda_{max}(H_{G})}\geq\min_{G}\frac{\ell(G)}{u(G)}\,.

In most works, the upper bound
u
is determined by solving a semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxation of the maximization problem
[
BH14
,
GP19
,
PT21
,
Tak+23
,
Wat+24
,
Hub+24
]
.
The source of such SDP relaxations are generally hierarchies of SDPs that provide increasingly better upper bounds
u
at the cost of solving larger-sized SDPs. The quantum moment-SOS hierarchy, typically based on Pauli operators, is widely employed for quantum local Hamiltonian problems. This hierarchy is an instance of the NPA hierarchy
[
NPA08
]
and is also known as the quantum Lasserre hierarchy
[
PT21
]
. The second level of the quantum moment-SOS hierarchy is necessary for good approximations, since critical monogamy-of-entanglement properties begin to emerge at that level
[
PT21
,
PT22
]
.

The lower bound
\ell
is usually determined by an algorithm that prepares a state with this energy. Several approximation algorithms have been proposed for QMC and EPR, using product states
[
GP19
,
PT21
,
PT22
,
Kin23
]
, matchings
[
AGM20
,
PT21
,
PT22
,
LP24
,
Jor+24
,
GSS25
]
, and short variational circuits
[
AGM20
,
Lee22
,
Kin23
,
JN25
,
GSS25
]
.
During the preparation of this manuscript, the best known approximation ratios for these problems were improved to
\alpha\geq 0.603
for QMC
[
GSS25
]
and
\alpha\geq\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}\approx 0.809
for EPR
[
JN25
]
.

We provide a new algorithm for both problems. For each algorithm, we start from a good product state. We then choose a matching in
G
and
partially
rotate matched qubits towards an entangled state. Our novel contribution is to perform this rotation such that individual single-qubit Bloch vectors are preserved, up to a rescaling of magnitude. This allows us to evaluate the energy of our entangled state in terms of the energy of the product state. For EPR, we use a
fractional
matching and provide a circuit-based algorithm. For QMC, we explicitly describe a tensor-product of single and two-qubit states, which interpolates between the product state and an
integer
matching-based state with a single tunable parameter. For QMC, we additionally introduce the technique of choosing a maximum-weight matching with respect to
rescaled
edge weights. One interpretation of this approach is that we widen the search space of our algorithm to include matchings other than the maximum-weight matching of
G
. Combining these techniques allows us to achieve state-of-the-art approximation ratios on each problem:

Theorem 1
.

For EPR,
\alpha\geq\frac{\varphi}{2}\approx 0.809
,
where
\varphi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.618
is the golden ratio.

Theorem 2
.

For QMC,
\alpha\geq 0.611
.

Remark 1
.

Theorem

1
was shown simultaneously and independently by
[
JN25
]
using a different algorithm. The algorithm of
[
JN25
]
also
partially
entangles edges while preserving single-qubit marginals. However, we believe both our algorithm and analysis are simpler.
Theorem

2
requires an improved upper bound in addition to new algorithmic techniques. The technique of finding a matching with respect to rescaled edge weights was simultaneously and independently used by
[
GSS25
]
; however, they use this as part of improved approximation algorithm for QMC on triangle-free graphs rather than general graphs. Our QMC approximation does rely on a strengthening by
[
GSS25
]
of a class of upper bounds first used in the work
[
LP24
]
. Without this strengthened bound, we still achieve an approximation ratio
\alpha\geq 0.610
.

Our approximation algorithm finds a quantum state
\rho_{G}
such that
\mathrm{Tr}\left(H_{G}\rho_{G}\right)\geq\ell(G)
. For the EPR Hamiltonian, the analysis of our algorithm is optimal: there exist graphs
G
(such as the single-edge graph) where

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(G)}{u(G)}\leq\frac{\bra{\psi_{G}}H_{G}\ket{\psi_{G}}}{\lambda_{max}(G)}=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}\,.

For QMC, however, it is possible that a better analysis of this algorithm (particularly of
u(G)
) would give a larger approximation ratio.

In the remainder of this work, we introduce necessary notation, describe each algorithm, and prove
Theorems

1
and
2
.
We defer some technical lemmas to the appendix.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Graph theory

Let
G(V,E,w)
denote a graph with vertex set
V
, edge set
E
, and positive edge weights
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In this work, we always take
V={\left[n\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. We let
W_{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}
. When
G
is inferred by context, we simply write
W
. Define
N(v)
as the set of neighbors of a vertex
v\in V
. For convenience, we index edges by either
e
or
(i,j)
, or simply
ij
in subscripts.

A fractional
matching
of a graph
G(V,E,w)
is a function
m:E(G)\to[0,1]
that assigns a value to each edge in
G
, such that the sum over values
\sum_{j\in N(i)}m_{ij}
is at most
1
for any vertex
i
. If
m_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
for all edges
(i,j)
, we say that
m
is an
integral
matching. For an integral matching
m
, we say an edge
(i,j)
is in the matching if
m_{ij}=1
.
The
weight

\textrm{Wt}(m)
of a matching
m
is defined as
\textrm{Wt}(m)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E(G)}w_{ij}\,m_{ij}
. We define
\textnormal{FM}_{G}
(or
\textnormal{M}_{G}
) to be the maximum total weight of any fractional (or integral) matching of
G
. For convenience, we sometimes let
\textnormal{M}_{G}
also denote the set of edges in the maximum-weight integral matching of
G
.
When the graph is clear from context we drop the subscript
G
. Optimal integral and fractional matchings can be computed in polynomial time, for example with linear programming
[
Edm65
]
.

2.2
Quantum computation

We refer to
\vec{\sigma}=\left(X,Y,Z\right)
as the canonical Pauli matrices. As such, we can define the
Bloch vector

\vec{B}(\rho)
of any
1
-qubit density matrix
\rho
as the vector
(b_{x},b_{y},b_{z})
in the unit sphere
\textrm{S}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+\vec{B}(\rho)\cdot\vec{\sigma})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}(I+b_{x}X+b_{y}Y+b_{z}Z)\,.

Pure states correspond to unit Bloch vectors.
Let
\ket{\psi_{-}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{01}-\ket{10}\right)
be the
singlet state
, and
\ket{\phi_{+}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\right)
be the
EPR state
.

2.3
Previous algorithms

In our work, we use two previously known algorithms as subroutines:

1.

The algorithm
\mathrm{PROD}
takes a graph
G(V,E,w)
as an input, and outputs a good product state
\rho_{PROD}
. For EPR, we define
\rho_{PROD}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\bra{0}^{\otimes n}
.
For QMC, we define
\mathrm{PROD}
to be the output of the
[
GP19
]
rounding algorithm applied to the second level of the quantum moment-SOS hierarchy, as in
[
PT22
]
and
[
LP24
]
.
This is the only part of our algorithm for QMC that relies on an SDP. As described in
[
PT22
, Sec 2.2]
, the output from the SDP can be interpreted as a
pseudo-density matrix
. It is called a
pseudo
-density matrix because it satisfies only some of the constraints of a valid density matrix.

2.

The algorithm
\mathrm{MATCH}
takes a graph
G(V,E,w)
as an input, and outputs a product of 2-qubit states
\rho_{MATCH}
. It was first formally proposed in
[
LP24
]
. The algorithm first finds a maximum-weight integral matching
\textnormal{M}_{G}
. For EPR (and for QMC), it outputs the tensor product of
\ket{\phi_{+}}_{ij}\bra{\phi_{+}}_{ij}
(and
\ket{\psi_{-}}_{ij}\bra{\psi_{-}}_{ij}
for QMC) for every pair of vertices
(i,j)
in the matching, and the maximally mixed state for every vertex
i
not in the matching. This obtains energy
2
on matched edges and
1/2
on unmatched edges, for a total of
(3\textnormal{M}_{G}+W_{G})/2
.

3
Our Algorithms

To motivate our algorithms, consider the following simple graph
G
:

a
b
c

This graph is bipartite, so QMC and EPR are equivalent under local rotations
[
Kin23
]
. We thus focus on EPR for simplicity. It is easy to compute that
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})=3
. The optimal product state
\rho_{PROD}
is
\rho_{0}^{\otimes 3}
, where
\rho_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\ket{0}\bra{0}
, and achieves energy
2
. The algorithm
\mathrm{MATCH}
computes the maximum matching
\textnormal{M}_{G}=\left\{(a,b)\right\}
and returns the state
\rho_{MATCH}
as described in
Section

2
. This state gains energy
2
on edge
(i,j)
and
1/2
on edge
(b,c)
, achieving total energy
5/2
. Thus, the algorithm of
[
LP24
]
, which returns the better of
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
, achieves
5/6\approx 0.833
of the optimal energy.

Our algorithms do better by interpolating between
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
, rather than taking the better of the two. For our example
G
, the unitary

\displaystyle U=e^{i\theta\left(\frac{X_{a}-Y_{a}}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\frac{X_{b}-Y_{b}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}\,,

takes
\rho_{PROD}
to
\rho^{\prime}
, a tensor product of a two-qubit state
\rho_{ab}
and a single-qubit state
\rho_{c}
. The parameter
\theta
sets the entanglement for
\rho_{ab}
: when
\theta=0
,
\rho_{ab}
is a product state; when
\theta=\pi/4
,
\rho_{ab}
is fully rotated into the EPR state. As such, we view this approach as smoothly interpolating between
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
in superposition. It can be easily verified that the energy obtained by
\rho^{\prime}
on edge
(a,b)
is
\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{ab}\rho^{\prime}\right]}=1+2\cos\theta\sin\theta
, and the single qubit marginals of
a
and
b
are given by

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Tr}_{bc}{\left[\rho^{\prime}\right]}=\cos{2\theta}\rho_{0}+2\sin^{2}{\theta}I=\rho^{\prime}_{b}\,.

In particular, note that the single qubit marginals are simply rescaled and shifted by the identity. This fact aids in the analysis: we can still evaluate the energy of
(b,c)
in terms of
\rho_{PROD}
, even though the edge is not in our matching:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{bc}\rho^{\prime}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{bc}\left(\rho^{\prime}_{b}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{c}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{bc}\left(\rho^{\prime}_{b}\otimes\rho_{0}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{bc}\left(\left(\cos{2\theta}\rho_{0}+2\sin^{2}{\theta}\,\left(I/2\right)\right)\otimes\rho_{0}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}{\left[h^{EPR}_{bc}\left(\cos{2\theta}\rho_{PROD}+\sin^{2}{\theta}\,I\otimes\rho_{0}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\cos{2\theta}+\sin^{2}\theta=\cos^{2}\theta\,.

Thus the total energy is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left[(h^{EPR}_{ab}+h^{EPR}_{bc})\rho^{\prime}\right]}=1+2\cos\theta\sin\theta+\cos^{2}\theta\,.

The parameter
\theta
can be optimized over; in this example, taking
\theta\approx.554
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left[(h^{EPR}_{ab}+h^{EPR}_{bc})\rho^{\prime}\right]}\approx 2.618,

outperforming both
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
.

In this example, the analysis simplifies nicely because the initial product state is the symmetric state
\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}
, and because the graph is bipartite. For EPR on general graphs, we provide a circuit-based algorithm that prepares an entangled state based on a
fractional
matching in the graph. This state still works by
partially
entangling edges according to the matching while preserving single-qubit marginals, but it does not have a simple interpretation in terms of
\rho_{MATCH}
. We leave open the possibility that a
(\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4})
-approximation algorithm can be achieved with a tensor product of one and two-qubit states that simply interpolates between
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
. For QMC, our algorithm does indeed interpolate between
\rho_{PROD}
and
\rho_{MATCH}
. However the algorithm and analysis become slightly more complicated. Crucial to our analysis is the following lemma:

Lemma 1

(Energy obtained by QMC algorithm on matched and unmatched edges)
.

Given two
1
-qubit pure states
\rho_{i},\rho_{j}
and a real parameter
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, there exists a
2
-qubit pure state
\rho_{ij}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left[{h^{QMC}\rho_{ij}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\sin{\theta})\left(1-\vec{B}(\rho_{i})\cdot\vec{B}(\rho_{j})\right)}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\vec{B}(\mathrm{Tr}_{j}{\left[\rho_{ij}\right]})

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta}\cdot\vec{B}(\rho_{i})\,,

\displaystyle\vec{B}(\mathrm{Tr}_{i}{\left[\rho_{ij}\right]})

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta}\cdot\vec{B}(\rho_{j})\,.

The last two constraints in
Lemma

1
indicate that the Bloch vectors of the single-qubit marginals of
\rho_{ij}
are rescaled Bloch vectors of
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
, respectively. This is analogous to the argument in our example, where the marginals are rescaled and shifted by the identity. This again allows us to compute the energy of unmatched edges in terms of
\rho_{PROD}
. The parameter
\theta\,
again sets the entanglement in
\rho_{ij}
: when
\theta=0
,
\rho_{ij}
is a product state; when
\theta=\pi/2
,
\rho_{ij}
is maximally entangled.
The proof of
Lemma

1
is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

3.1
EPR

Using the intuition from our example, we introduce the following algorithm for EPR:

Algorithm 1

(Fractional matching algorithm for EPR)
.

Given a weighted graph
G(V,E,w)
:

1.

Define the
1
-qubit Hamiltonian

\displaystyle P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(X-Y)\,,

and the angle

\displaystyle\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{\ln\left(\varphi\right)}{2}\approx 0.240\,,

(1)

where
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is the golden ratio.

2.

Find a fractional matching
(m_{uv})_{(u,v)\in E}
of maximum weight (e.g., via linear programming).

3.

Output the state

\displaystyle\ket{\chi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}e^{i\gamma_{uv}P_{u}P_{v}}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\,,

(2)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{uv}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1}\exp{\left[-\theta\cdot m_{uv}\right]}\ \in\ [0,\pi/2]\,.

(3)

The unitary
e^{i\gamma P_{u}P_{v}}
rotates the state
\ket{00}_{ij}
towards the Bell state
\ket{\phi^{+}}
; this circuit was proposed in the approximation algorithm of
[
Kin23
]
.

3.2
QMC

Our algorithm for QMC relies on the following subroutine, which we call
\mathrm{PMATCH}
since it places partially entangled 2-qubit states on the edges of a matching, while
\mathrm{MATCH}
places maximally entangled states:

Algorithm 2

(
\mathrm{PMATCH}
)
.

Given a weighted graph
G(V,E,w)
and a real parameter
\theta\in{\left[0,\pi/2\right]}
:

1.

Prepare the state
\rho_{PROD}=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}\rho_{i}
by running the
\mathrm{PROD}
algorithm on QMC from
Section

2
.

2.

For each edge
(i,j)\in E
, compute
t_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\vec{B}(\rho_{i})\cdot\vec{B}(\rho_{j})
from
\rho_{PROD}
.

3.

Find a maximum-weight integral matching
\widetilde{M}\subseteq E
on the
reweighted
graph
G(V,E^{\prime},\widetilde{w})
, where

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{ij}=w_{ij}\cdot\left(\frac{\sin{\theta}\left(1-t_{ij}\left(1+\sin{\theta}\right)\right)}{2}\right)^{+}\,,

(4)

and
E^{\prime}=\{(i,j)\in E\ |\ \widetilde{w}_{ij}>0\}
. Here, we use the notation
(\cdot)^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\max(0,\cdot)
from
[
LP24
]
.

4.

Let
\rho_{PMATCH}\left(\theta\right)
be the state starting from
\rho_{PROD}
, but for each edge
(i,j)
in
\widetilde{M}
, replace
\rho_{i}\otimes\rho_{j}
with the state
\rho_{ij}
described in
Lemma

1
, parametrized by
\rho_{i},\rho_{j},\theta
.

5.

Output the state
\rho_{PMATCH}\left(\theta\right)
.

Our algorithm for QMC chooses the better of the algorithms
\mathrm{PMATCH}
and
\mathrm{MATCH}
:

Algorithm 3

(Combined algorithm for QMC)
.

Given a weighted graph
G(V,E,w)
:

1.

Prepare the state
\rho_{MATCH}
by running the
\mathrm{MATCH}
algorithm from
Section

2
.

2.

Prepare the state
\rho_{PMATCH}\left(\theta\right)
by running
\mathrm{PMATCH}
with parameter
\theta=1.286
.

3.

Output the state out of
\rho_{MATCH}
and
\rho_{PMATCH}(\theta)
obtaining larger energy on the QMC Hamiltonian

.

4
Analysis

We upper-bound the maximum energy
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})
of QMC and EPR Hamiltonians using a quantifiable
monogamy of entanglement
:

Lemma 2

(Monogamy of Entanglement, e.g.
[
LP24
, Lemma 1]
)
.

For both EPR and QMC, and for all graphs
G(V,E,w)
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}(H_{G})\leq W_{G}+\textnormal{FM}_{G}\,.

For QMC, our analysis improves with better upper bounds on
\lambda_{max}(H_{G})
. We use an inequality of the form
W_{G}+\textnormal{M}_{G}/d
; this first appears in
[
LP24
, Lemma 4]
with the constant
d=4/5
. By a detailed analysis and numerical verification on graphs with up to
13
vertices, this constant was recently improved to
14/15
in
[
GSS25
]
:

Lemma 3

(Strengthened monogamy of entanglement,
[
GSS25
, Lemma 3.10]
)
.

For QMC, and for all graphs
G(V,E,w)
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}(H_{G})\leq W_{G}+\frac{\textnormal{M}_{G}}{d}\,,

where
d=\frac{14}{15}
.

EPR

. Given the state in

\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
instead of
\varphi
as in the main text. This change is solely for ease of readability.

Proof.

In terms of
c
, the statement we would like to prove is

\displaystyle\min_{x\in[0,1]}{\left[\frac{1}{2(1+x)}\left(1+c^{-(1-x)}+2\sqrt{1-c^{-x}}\cdot c^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-x)}\right)\right]}=\frac{c}{2}.

The golden ratio
c
obeys the identities
c^{2}=c+1
and
c^{3}=2c+1
. Note that the minimum value of
Eq.

12
is at most
\frac{c}{2}
. This is because when
x=0
, the left-hand side is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1+c^{-1}+0\right)=\frac{1}{2c}(c+1)=\frac{c^{2}}{2c}=\frac{c}{2}\,.

Thus it suffices to show that for all
x\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle 1+c^{-(1-x)}+2\sqrt{1-c^{-x}}\cdot c^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-x)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}c(1+x)\,.

We repeatedly transform this inequality to find a simpler, equivalent inequality:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle c+c^{x}+2\sqrt{1-c^{-x}}\cdot c^{\frac{1}{2}(x+1)}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad c^{2}(1+x)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle c+(c^{x}-1)+2\sqrt{c}\cdot\sqrt{c^{x}-1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad(c+1)(1+x)-1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\sqrt{c}+\sqrt{c^{x}-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad cx+x+c

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sqrt{c}+\sqrt{c^{x}-1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad\sqrt{c^{2}x+c}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sqrt{c^{x}-1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad\sqrt{c}\cdot(\sqrt{cx+1}-1)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle c^{x}-1

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad c\cdot(cx+1+1-2\sqrt{cx+1})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 2c\sqrt{cx+1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad c^{2}x+2c+1-c^{x}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sqrt{4cx+4}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad cx+c^{2}-c^{(x-1)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 4cx+4

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad(cx+c^{2})^{2}+c^{(2x-2)}-2c^{(x-1)}(cx+c^{2})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\geq}}\quad c^{2}(x+c)^{2}-4cx-4+c^{(2x-2)}-2c^{x}(x+c)\,.

Let
f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}c^{2}(x+c)^{2}-4cx-4+c^{(2x-2)}-2c^{x}(x+c)
. We will complete the proof by showing that
f(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in[0,1]
. We do this by proving that
f
is convex in the region
x\in[0,1]
, so its maximum value is attained at either
x=0
or
x=1
. We then verify the inequality at each boundary point. Consider the second derivative of
f
with respect to
x
:

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)=2c^{2}+(2\ln c)^{2}c^{(2x-2)}-2c^{x}\ln c\cdot\left((x+c)\ln c+2\right)\,.

We substitute in the variable
z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}c^{x}
for
z\in[1,c]
. Then
g(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}f^{\prime\prime}(\log_{c}x)
is equal to

\displaystyle g(z)=2c^{2}+z^{2}\cdot(4c^{-2}\ln^{2}c)-z\cdot 2\ln c\cdot((c+\log_{c}z)\ln c+2)\,.

To show
f
is convex, we want to show
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,1]
(or equivalently,
g(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in[1,c]
). We do so by showing that
g
is decreasing on the interval
[1,c]
, and
g(c)\geq 0
. Consider the first derivative of
g
with respect to
z
:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=z\cdot(8c^{-2}\ln^{2}c)-4\ln c-2c\ln^{2}c-2\ln^{2}c\cdot\left(\log_{c}z+\frac{1}{\ln c}\right)\,.

Note that
g^{\prime}
is itself convex. Using the higher-order derivatives of
g
,

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=8c^{-2}\ln^{2}c-\frac{1}{z\ln c}\cdot 2\ln^{2}c=8c^{-2}\ln^{2}c-\frac{2\ln c}{z}\,,

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ln c}{z^{2}}\,,

observe that
(g^{\prime})^{\prime\prime}=g^{\prime\prime\prime}\geq 0
for all
z
. So
g^{\prime}
achieves its maximum value at a boundary point (either
z=1
or
z=c
). When
z=1
, we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=8c^{-2}\ln^{2}c-4\ln c-2c\ln^{2}c-2\ln^{2}c\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\ln c}\right)

\displaystyle=(\ln c)\cdot(8c^{-2}\ln c-6-2c\ln c)

\displaystyle\leq(\ln c)\cdot(-6+\frac{8\ln c}{c+1})

\displaystyle\leq(\ln c)\cdot(-6+\frac{8\ln e}{2})

\displaystyle<0\,.

When
z=c
, we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle=8c^{-1}\ln^{2}c-4\ln c-2c\ln^{2}c-2\ln^{2}c\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{\ln c}\right)

\displaystyle=(\ln c)\cdot(8c^{-1}\ln c-6-2(c+1)\ln c)

\displaystyle\leq(\ln c)\cdot(-6+\frac{8\ln c}{c})

\displaystyle\leq(\ln c)\cdot(-6+\frac{8\ln e}{1.6})

\displaystyle<0\,.

Since both of these values are negative,
g^{\prime}
is negative on the interval
[1,c]
. So
g
is decreasing on the interval
[1,c]
.
To finish the claim that
g\geq 0
on the interval
[1,c]
, we check that
g(c)\geq 0
:

\displaystyle g(c)

\displaystyle=2c^{2}+4\ln^{2}c-2c\ln c\cdot((c+1)\ln c+2)

\displaystyle=2c^{2}-4c\ln c+4\ln^{2}c-2c^{3}\ln^{2}c

\displaystyle=2(c-\ln c)^{2}+2\ln^{2}c-2(2c+1)\ln^{2}c

\displaystyle=2(c-\ln c)^{2}-4c\ln^{2}c\,.

Note that
1.6<c<2
and
2\ln c=\ln c^{2}=\ln(c+1)<\ln 2.62<1
. Then we can lower-bound
g(c)
as

\displaystyle g(c)\geq 2(1.6-0.5)^{2}-4\cdot 2\cdot 0.5^{2}=2.42-2>0\,.

Finally, we verify that the boundary points of
f
are at most
0
. When
x=0
, we have

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=c^{4}-4+c^{-2}-2c

\displaystyle=c^{-2}(c^{6}-2c^{3}-4c^{2}+1)

\displaystyle=c^{-2}((c^{3}-1)^{2}-4c^{2})

\displaystyle=c^{-2}((2c)^{2}-4c^{2})

\displaystyle=0\,.

When
x=1
, we have

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=c^{2}(c+1)^{2}-4c-4+1-2c(c+1)

\displaystyle=c^{6}-2c^{3}-4c-3

\displaystyle=(c^{3}-1)^{2}-4(c+1)

\displaystyle=(2c)^{2}-4c^{2}

\displaystyle=0\,.

Since the maximum value of a convex function is attained on its boundary, we have that for all
x\in[0,1]
,
f(x)\leq\max_{x\in[0,1]}f(x)=\max(f(0),f(1))=0
.
∎
