Renal-clearable fluorescent probe for early detection of pancreatic cancer in living murine models
Authors: Minrong Huang, Zhe Zhou, Tong Zhang, Xiaofeng Xia, Wei Chen, Erfei Wang, Feiyi Wang, Jun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a highly aggressive and lethal malignancy with extremely poor patient survival rates. The lack of biomarkers and deep tissue detection barriers has presented long-standing and unsolved issues, severely hindering the early diagnosis of PC even in living rodent models. Here, we report a renal-clearable leucine aminopeptidase (LAP)–activatable fluorescence probe, overturning the probe’s pharmacokinetics from the liver to kidney pathway (90% of the injection dosage). This strategy reduces interference in the liver and enables LAP as a biomarker for PC detection in murine models. After in situ response with LAP in the pancreas, renal-clearable fluorogenic fragments could be efficiently excreted into the urine, thus addressing the depth barriers of PC. Our strategy has resolved the lack of biomarkers and deep tissue detection barriers in early PC detection in murine models, which would greatly expand the bioanalytical toolbox for PC identification, further enabling the efficient screening of PC drugs at the animal level and the exploration of diagnostic methods for PC.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is an extremely lethal disease worldwide, with an overall 5-year survival rate lower than 10% (1–3). Despite the poor prognosis of PC, its early detection has been shown to substantially improve patient survival. Nevertheless, because of the subtle early symptoms and potential misdiagnosis on imaging scans, the early diagnosis of PC faces great challenges. The common clinical imaging modalities for PC diagnosis, such as computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, generally display limited specificity and can only detect tumors with a diameter of over 1 cm (4–7). Therefore, current diagnostic technologies fail to offer satisfying precision for the critical diagnosis of PC or subserve patient stratification for an optimal treatment modality. Even in some rodent models, it is difficult to achieve identification of PC, which makes it difficult to conduct efficient screening of PC drugs at the animal level and develop early treatment techniques for PC.

Fluorescence molecular imaging has revolutionized in vivo disease diagnosis, allowing for the noninvasive and real-time monitoring of disease onset and progression in living animals (8–30). However, the lack of biomarkers and deep tissue detection barriers has severely hindered the practicability of fluorescent probes in the early diagnosis of PC (31–34). For instance, while leucine aminopeptidase (LAP) is overexpressed in the plasma of patients with PC compared to those with pancreatitis, it is also expressed in liver diseases (liver inflammation, injury, etc.) and serves as an inevitable interference. Now, the diagnosis of PC is faced with the dilemma of lack of reliable biomarkers. Carbohydrate antigen 19-9, which is widely used in clinical practice, has deadly limitations such as high false positive rate and low diagnosis rate (0.5 to 0.9%) (32). LAP, as a potential biomarker, has shown important research value in the diagnosis of PC (35). Moreover, the limited penetration depth of optical signals greatly hampers the specific detection of PC, even in some tumor animal models, which are located deep within the body where numerous organs and blood vessels converge (36–40). Therefore, it is essential to formulate a molecular design strategy that overcomes the long-standing and unsolved issues of the specificity and depth barriers, which will pave a reliable pathway for the early detection of PC in living animal models.

Here, we describe a molecular rational design strategy to create a renal-clearable LAP-activatable fluorescence probe for the early detection of PC in living murine models (Fig. 1), relying on the chromene-O and cyclodextrin (CD) unit for enhancing LAP-response sensitivity and fully overturning the pharmacokinetics from the liver to kidney pathway, respectively. Hence, our probe had resolved the challenge of the lack of biomarkers and deep tissue detection barriers, which have presented long-standing and unsolved issues, severely hindering the early diagnosis of PC in murine models. In vivo and ex vivo imaging provides solid evidence that probe CyCD-O-Leu (dark state) specifically and in situ reacts with LAP in a PC mouse tumor to its activated form (CyCD-O-OH, bright state). Then, because of the high renal clearance (90% of the injection dosage), CyCD-O-OH can be efficiently transported to the bladder and excreted into the urine. As demonstrated, we show that this strategy has well addressed the essential issues and depth barriers in early detection of PC in murine models: (i) renal-clearable pathway for reducing the interference of active substances in the liver; (ii) accumulation of in situ PC-activated fluorescent indicator in urine for highly sensitive diagnosis. By virtue of harnessing this strategy, PC can be specifically and noninvasively identified within only 5 days in living mice. We anticipate that our strategy will greatly facilitate the efficient detection of PC in living mice, further enabling the efficient drug development of PC drugs at the animal level.

RESULTS

Optimizing NIR fluorescent response sensitivity to LAP via probe rational design

We made full use of the “step-by-step” strategy to develop a renal-clearable and LAP-responsive fluorescent probe (Fig. 1A). First, we “inserted” a classical self-elimination group between the fluorophore and LAP sensing unit, aiming to enhance the LAP combination ability. Subsequently, to optimize the NIR fluorescence response sensitivity, we synthesized three semicyanine fluorophores and compared their optical properties (fig. S1). It was found that replacing the chrome-O unit with chrome-S unit had the best response to LAP while ensuring stability (table S1 and fig. S2). Last, we focused on introducing the CD building block for fully reversing the probe’s pharmacokinetics from the liver to kidney pathway. By virtue of harnessing this strategy, the probe CyCD-O-Leu was prepared, with Cy-O-Leu and Cy-S-Leu as references.

To validate that replacing chromene-S with chromene-O unit could enhance the response sensitivity, we systematically investigated the LAP-triggered NIR fluorescence responses of Cy-O-Leu and Cy-S-Leu (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2 (A and D) and fig. S3, Cy-O-Leu (5 μΜ) showed two absorption peaks at 606 and 654 nm and was initially nonfluorescent. In response to LAP (10 U/liter), a new absorption peak (at around 670 nm) appeared (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, the probe exhibited a fluorescent enhancement (sevenfold) at 715 nm (Fig. 2D), confirming the successful uncaging of the self-elimination group–linked LAP sensing unit. In contrast, Cy-S-Leu (with chromene-S unit) showed only a relatively slight NIR fluorescence increase in the presence of LAP (Fig. 2, B and E, and fig. S4). This result confirmed that the probe with chromene-O unit had much higher sensitivity in off-on sensing of LAP.

Furthermore, β-CD was used to modify Cy-O-Leu, and the resulting probe CyCD-O-Leu exhibited a similar LAP-triggered NIR fluorescence response with Cy-O-Leu (Fig. 2, C, F, and G, and fig. S5), verifying that the CD structure did not interfere with LAP recognition. As shown in Fig. 2G, the fluorescence intensity (at 715 nm) increased linearly with the levels of LAP activity (0 to 0.5 U/liter), and the detection limit was calculated as 0.038 U/liter. In addition, we also confirmed the time stability (fig. S6) and the applicability under physiological conditions (including different temperatures and pH values; fig. S7). Also, the enzyme catalysis kinetics for CyCD-O-Leu with LAP was determined (fig. S8), and the Michaels constant (Km) and maximum of initial reaction rate (Vmax) were found to be 10.42 μΜ and 2.1959 μM/s, respectively, indicating the strong affinity between the probe and LAP.

To investigate the specificity of CyCD-O-Leu for LAP detection, we examined the fluorescent response of the probe to LAP as well as some potential interferents, such as biologically essential metal ions, reactive oxygen species, biomolecules, and related enzyme species. As shown in Fig. 2 (H and I), only the incubation with LAP resulted in an off-on NIR fluorescence enhancement. For gaining insight into the activation of the sensing mechanism, we carried out HR-MS, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to analyze the reaction products of the probe with LAP (figs. S9 to S12). These analyses clearly confirmed that LAP could cause the cleavage of the amide linkage between the peptide substrate and the self-eliminating linker, followed by spontaneous 1,6-elimination to release the free dye CyCD-O-OH (“uncaged” state), which emits in the NIR region. Together, these results demonstrated the excellent sensitivity and selectivity of CyCD-O-Leu for NIR lighting-up LAP.

NIR lighting-up endogenous LAP in living cells

Encouraged by the excellent sensitivity and selectivity of probe CyCD-O-Leu toward LAP, we further investigated the feasibility of CyCD-O-Leu for lighting up LAP in living cells. Before the intracellular sensing, the probe’s biocompatibility was confirmed within HPDE6-C7 (human normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line) and BxPC-3 (human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell line) cells. Also, the probe’s negligible cytotoxicity for HPDE6-C7 and BxPC-3 cells was also confirmed by cytotoxicity assays in figs. S13 and S14. To study the time-dependent NIR fluorescence response, we incubated BxPC-3 cells with CyCD-O-Leu for 90 min. As shown in Fig. 3A, the probes displayed a NIR fluorescence enhancement within only 30 min, indicating a rapid response toward LAP in living cells. To further confirm the probe’s selectivity, we pretreated BxPC-3 cells with LAP inhibitor (bestatin). These cells (with bestatin) showed an obvious fluorescence decrease (fig. S15), further supporting that the probe can be selectively activated by endogenous LAP and generate NIR fluorescence signals. Furthermore, CyCD-O-Leu was used to detect LAP activity in normal cell lines (HPDE6-C7) and different cancer cell lines (Fig. 3C). Following a 30-min incubation, BxPC-3 cells, as human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells, exhibited obvious NIR fluorescence enhancement.

Clarifying the renal-clearable pharmacokinetics in living mice

The liver, due to its substantial spatial presence in the body and potential obstruction of the pancreas, plays a crucial role in probe metabolism, often resulting in diagnostic inaccuracies in disease assessment (41–43). Particularly for PC, LAP is overexpressed in the plasma of PC models as well as expressed in liver diseases, serving as an inevitable interference (44–47). In contrast, the kidneys, being relatively compact and prominently discernible, impose minimal hindrance at the disease locus. This attribute affords a notable decline in signal disruption from neighboring organs, facilitating the in situ imaging of the lesion site and mitigating the incidence of erroneous positive signals. CD are small cyclic oligosaccharides with nanoscale outer diameters, abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl groups on the exterior surface, and a hydrophobic inner cavity. These features allow CD to improve the aqueous solubility and renal-clearable behavior of conjugated optical agents without markedly increasing their overall molecular size. In particular, the small size and high hydrophilicity of CD favor glomerular filtration and urinary excretion while reducing nonspecific tissue retention. Therefore, CD can serve as a renal-clearable and pharmacokinetics-modulating moiety in optical probe design (48, 49).

To further assess the biosafety profile of the CD-linked probe, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was also performed. Analysis of tissue sections from the pancreas and five additional major organs revealed that CyCD-O-Leu exhibited minimal biological toxicity, with negligible impact on murine organs (fig. S16). Then, we investigated the probes’ biodistribution by using the uncaged derivatives CyCD-O-OH (with CD group) and Cy-O-OH (without CD group). After intravenous injection of normal mice, Cy-O-OH’s fluorescence rapidly and primarily appeared in the liver site within 30 min and reached the maximum at 90 min (Fig. 4A). In contrast, CD-linked CyCD-O-OH was rapidly metabolized through the kidney and into the bladder immediately following injection, with minimal interaction with other organs. To gain insight into the in vivo metabolic pathway, the isolated organs are collected for fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 4B, the administration of Cy-O-OH mainly dispersed in the liver site. Conversely, injection of the CD-functionalized fluorophore CyCD-O-OH only resulted in light fluorescence in the kidneys. Then, urine samples (24 hours postintravenous injection) were collected, which demonstrated the high renal clearance efficiency of CyCD-O-OH (90% of the injection dosage, higher than that of Cy-O-OH; fig. S17). These results demonstrated the key role of the CD group: (i) Probe without CD was cleared by the reticuloendothelial system, which can lead to probe accumulation in the liver; (ii) renal-clearable CyCD-O-OH (with CD) is rapidly excreted from the body via the kidney-bladder-urine pathway. Therefore, we reasonably assumed that the renal-clearable pharmacokinetics of our probe could reduce interference from active substances in the mice’s liver (such as the LAP in the liver).

Identifying PC from normal tissue: Making LAP a PC biomarker in living murine models

To explore the use of CyCD-O-Leu for differentiating PC and normal pancreatic tissue, we established two mouse models including control group and PC group. H&E staining results showed that (Fig. 5A) compared with the control group, the pancreatic tissue from the PC group exhibited a reduction in the volume of pancreatic parenchyma and pancreatic lobules (black arrow), and a large area of tumor cells was loosely arranged, with a high nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, nuclear atypia, and irregular shape (red arrow). These results clearly confirmed the successful establishment of PC murine models.

To validate the distinction between LAP levels in PC and normal pancreas, we carried out protein immune trace (WB) analysis on pancreatic tissue samples. As shown in Fig. 5 (B and C), the WB gray value of the PC group exhibited a higher magnitude than that of the control group, suggesting the potential for LAP detection in differentiating PC from normal mice. The immunohistochemical staining on pancreas tissue slices in PC versus control pancreas also showed that the LAP activity is specific to PC cells (fig. S18). Subsequently, an intravenous injection of CyCD-O-Leu (300 μM, 200 μl) was administered to the aforementioned mouse groups. As shown in Fig. 5D, CyCD-O-Leu exhibited bright NIR fluorescence in the pancreatic site of PC mice. In contrast, the pancreas of normal mice showed extremely weak fluorescence. Ex vivo imaging of extracted organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and pancreas) revealed that the fluorescence signal in the pancreas of PC mice was higher (around seven-fold) in comparison to normal mice (Fig. 5E and figs. S19 and S20). Furthermore, the genetic KPC mouse model was conducted for testing the efficacy of probe CyCD-O-Leu in KPC mice, whose expression of the KPC mutant genes and tumor growth was detected using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technology and high-resolution ultrasound imaging experiments (figs. S21 and S22). CyCD-O-Leu in KPC mice exhibited similar NIR fluorescence signals to those in orthotopic PC mice (fig. S23). Collectively, these findings provide solid evidence supporting the utilization of our probe for differentiating PC from normal mice via in vivo and in situ LAP sensing.

To further confirm that our renal-clearable probe could well reduce interference from liver activation, we administered Cy-O-Leu (without CD unit, liver-pharmacokinetics probe) to mice afflicted with drug-induced liver injury (DILI; which also could induce increased LAP expression in mice liver) (42) and to healthy mice. As shown in fig. S24, the study demonstrated the activation of Cy-O-Leu in the livers of healthy mice or DILI mice. In contrast, CyCD-O-Leu (with CD unit, renal-clearable probe) showed almost no fluorescence in the liver site (fig. S25). These results highlighted that our renal-clearable probe could well reduce interference from the liver’s activation through high renal clearance efficiency (90%), thus enabling LAP to serve as a feasible biomarker for PC detection in murine models.

Early detection of PC in living mice within 5 days: Ex vivo urinalysis for overcoming the depth detection barrier

Given that CyCD-O-OH (uncaged fluorophore) can be rapidly excreted from the body via the kidney-bladder-urine pathway (Figs. 3 and 4), we envisioned that our probe could in situ interact with LAP in the PC site and produce CyCD-O-OH, which are then excreted into urine for the remote detection of PC through urinalysis. This artificial urinary method may effectively improve the early detection of PC than in vivo imaging, since optical signals’ shallow penetration depth has impeded the use for detecting the pancreas (located deep within the body).

To explore the potential for distinguishing early or advanced PC, a simulated in situ PC tumor model was established by simultaneously injecting BxPC-3 cells into the pancreases of six groups of mice (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, CyCD-O-Leu was injected intravenously into different groups of PC mice. In vivo imaging results show that only the pancreas of PC mice on day 28 presented obvious fluorescence enhancement, while it is difficult to detect the fluorescence signal in the early stage of PC mice on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 due to the small size of the pancreas and the deep concealment (Figs. 6, B and D, and fig. S26). In contrast, the imaging results of the ex vivo pancreas exhibited fluorescence change of the pancreas at different PC stages. In particular, the fluorescence signals in the excisional pancreas showed obvious enhancement at day 7, which was much earlier than that of in vivo imaging. These results provided solid evidence that, by using in vivo imaging, the optical signals’ shallow penetration depth greatly limited the early detection of PC, inspiring us to evaluate the artificial serous and urinary method for early noninvasive detection of PC in living mice.

Given that the serum and urine concentrations of LAP are typically elevated in PC (35), serum and urine analyses using probe CyCD-O-Leu were conducted to detect PC mice at different stages. As illustrated in Figs. 5F and 6E, only serum samples (e1) from advanced PC mice exhibited an obvious fluorescence enhancement. Consequently, we prepared two types of urine samples: (i) Urine 1 samples from PC mice without any treatment were collected and then incubated with CyCD-O-Leu. (ii) Urine 2 samples from PC mice were collected after intravenous injection of CyCD-O-Leu. In day 5, the fluorescence intensity of urine 1 samples remained almost unchanged (compared with day 1), while that of urine 2 samples showed a fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 6, E and F). This result strongly indicated that (i) direct urine testing did not trigger an obvious fluorescence response; (ii) the probe needed to in situ interact with LAP in PC and the product CyCD-O-OH was then excreted into urine, which would induce a remarkable fluorescence enhancement in urine. Simultaneously, HPLC analysis of urine 2 samples from PC mice at different stages was also conducted to monitor the content of reaction product CyCD-O-OH, due to the efficient renal clearance of CyCD-O-Leu, and the experiment results show that CyCD-O-OH in mice urine increased from 3.2 to 33.8% within 5 days, indicating a notable alteration in LAP levels with cancer induction time (Fig. 6G). These comprehensive findings demonstrated that CyCD-O-Leu well addressed the depth barriers of PC in living mice, serving as an effective biosensor for the early identification of PC within only 5 days, much earlier than that of the traditional in vivo imaging method.

DISCUSSION

This work was focused on the long-standing challenges in the early identification of PC in living animal models: lacking biomarkers for identifying PC and deep tissue detection barriers. This breakthrough is enabled by overturning the probe’s pharmacokinetics from the liver to kidney pathway and thus allows (i) renal-clearable pathway for decreasing interference from active substances in the liver and enabling LAP to serve as a biomarker for PC in living mice; (ii) accumulating of in situ PC-activated fluorescent indicator in urine for highly sensitive ex vivo urinalysis and well addressing the depth barriers of PC in living mice.

With the in vivo and ex vivo imaging, we confirmed that probe CyCD-O-Leu was in situ activated by LAP in PC tumor of murine models, and the renal-clearable fluorogenic fragments (90% of the injection dosage) could be efficiently transported to the bladder and excreted into the urine. This strategy has allowed for identifying PC from normal pancreas tissue and realized sensitive detection of deep PC tumors in murine models. On the basis of the artificial urinary analysis, we showed that PC could be noninvasively identified within only 5 days in living mice, much earlier than that of traditional in vivo imaging methods. Our strategy has resolved the lack of biomarkers and deep tissue detection barriers for early PC detection in murine models, which would greatly expand the bioanalytical toolbox for the identification of PC, further enabling the efficient screening of PC drugs at the animal level and the development of early treatment techniques for PC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Apparatus and reagents

All reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers and directly used without further treatment. 1H-NMR and 13C-NMR spectra for all compounds were recorded on a Bruker AV 400 spectrometer. An Agilent 1260-6224 TOF liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry spectrometer was used to acquire high-resolution mass spectra. The MALDI-TOF mass spectra were recorded on a Bruker ultraextreme MALDI-TOF/TOF. Ultraviolet (UV) absorption spectra were obtained with a SHIMADZU UV-1800 spectrometer. Fluorescence emission spectra were obtained using an Agilent Cary Eclipse spectrometer. High-performance liquid chromatograms were acquired on SHIMADZU LC-16 chromatography. Confocal fluorescence imaging was conducted with a Zeiss LSM 900 microscope. NIRF imaging in living mice was conducted on VISQUE InVivo Smart-LF systems. The stained images with H&E were obtained via an Olympus IX51 microscope.

LAP, aptotinin, cellulase, lipase, sulfatase, α-chymotrypsin, tyrosinase, γ-glutamyltransferase, and arginase were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and Hoechst 33342 were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology.

Cells and animals

All the cells used were purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology. The mice were purchased from Wuhan Shulaibao Biotechnology and Shanghai Model Organisms. The animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Hubei University (ethical approval number: 20240098). All animal experiments follow the international welfare guidelines and the “3Rs” principles.

Preparation of sample solution

Cy-O-Leu, Cy-S-Leu, and CyCD-O-Leu were separately dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a 10 mM stock solution. LAP was diluted in tris buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20 and heated to 37°C. In a selective study, stock solutions of all analytes were prepared in DI water.

Determination of the limit of detection

The limit of detection was determined from the fluorescence titration assay. The fluorescence intensity of CyCD-O-Leu (5 μM) was measured after incubation with various concentration of LAP. The SD of blank measurement was calculated by the fluorescent intensity of CyCD-O-Leu without LAP. To gain the slop, the fluorescent intensity at 715 nm was calculated by the following equationDetection limit=3σ/kwhere σ was the SD of blank measurement. k was the slope of the plot of fluorescence intensity against LAP concentration.

Determination of the fluorescence quantum yield

Fluorescence quantum yields for Cy-O-OH, Cy-S-OH, and Bcy-O-OH were determined by using ICG (Φf = 0.13 in DMSO) as a fluorescence standard. The quantum yield was calculated using the following equationΦF(X)=ΦF(S) (ASFX/AXFS) (nX/nS)2where ΦF is the fluorescence quantum yield, A is the absorbance at the excitation wavelength, F is the area under the corrected emission curve, and n is the refractive index of the solvents used. Subscripts S and X refer to the standard and to the unknown, respectively.

Kinetic assay

Various concentrations of CyCD-O-Leu (1 to 20 μM) were incubated with LAP (10 U/liter) at 37°C for 30 min in tris buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20. For CyCD-O-Leu, fluorescence spectra of the solution were measured for quantification analyses. Then, initial reaction velocity (micromolar per minute) was calculated, plotted against the concentration of CyCD-O-Leu, and fitted to a Michaelis-Menten curve. The kinetic parameters were calculated by the Michaelis-Menten equation: V = Vmax × [S]/(Km + [S]), where V is the reaction rate. [S] is the concentration of CyCD-O-Leu.

Selectivity study

CyCD-O-Leu was in incubated with various analytes in tris buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20 at 37°C for 60 min. The concentrations of all analytes were as follows: ZnCl2 (100 μM), CaCl2 (100 μM), MgCl2 (100 μM), ClO− (25 μM), H2O2 (100 μM), glutathione (100 μM), Cys (100 μM), Hcy (100 μM), aprotinin (40 U/liter), cellulase (2 μg/ml), lipase (2 μg/ml), sulfatase (2 μg/ml), α-chymotrypsin (2 μg/ml), tyrosinase (40 U/liter), γ-glutamyltransferase (40 U/liter), arginase (40 U/liter), and LAP (10 U/liter).

Tandem cleavage study

In the absence or presence of LAP (10 U/liter), Cy-O-Leu, Cy-S-Leu, and CyCD-O-Leu were incubated in tris buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20 at 37°C for 30 min. Each mixture was subjected to HPCL, mass spectrometry, or MALDI-TOF analysis. HPCL chromatograms were acquired with 0.1% HCOOH-supplemented methanol and water as the eluent (methanol/water) and 600 nm as the detection wavelength.

Cell culture

HPDE6-C7 cells, Hela cells, and HepG2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin antibiotics. 4T1 cells, BxPC-3 cells, and PANC02 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptavidin. Capan-1 cells were cultured in IMDM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptavidin. All the cells were maintained in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% humidified air at 37°C. The medium was changed every other day. Cells were for passage when the cell confluence reached more than 80%.

Cytotoxicity assay

CCK-8 assay was conducted to evaluate cytotoxicity of CyCD-O-Leu against HPDE6-C7 and BxPC-3 cells. Two kinds of liver cancer cells (1 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and then allowed to adhere for 24 hours. After removing the culture medium, the cells were incubated with various concentrations of CyCD-O-Leu (0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μM) at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 24 hours. The 10% CCK-8 solution prepared with serum-free DMEM or RPMI 1640 was evenly added to each well and incubated for another 4 hours. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The cell viability value without CyCD-O-Leu treatment was normalized to 100%. The cell viability was calculated by the following equationCell viability=(ODsample−ODblank)/(ODcontrol−ODblank)where ODsample was the optical density of cells incubated with CyCD-O-Leu and CCK-8. ODcontrol was the optical density of cells incubated with CCK-8. ODblank was the optical density of CCK-8.

Confocal fluorescence imaging in cells

For cell imaging, cells were seeded onto a coagulation petri dish (35 mm) at 2 × 104 cells per well in a 1-ml medium. CyCD-O-Leu (5 μM) was added to the dishes and incubated at 37°C. For the inhibiting study, cells were pretreated with inobestin (0 to 100 μΜ) for 30 min and then incubated with CyCD-O-Leu (5 μM) for 30 min. After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (1×, pH 7.4). Fluorescence images were captured on a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. Excitation/emission wavelengths were λex = 405 nm and λem = 420 to 480 nm for Hoechst 33342 and λex = 640 nm and λem = 650 to 700 nm for CyCD-O-Leu.

Renal clearance analysis

The living mice were intravenously injected with CyCD-O-Leu (300 μM, 200 μl) and placed in metabolic cages. The excreted urine was collected at 24 hours postinjection, centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and filtered by a 0.22-μm syringe filter. The excreted probes were quantified using UV-Vis absorption spectrometer. The renal clearance efficiency (%) = ne/ni*100%, where ne and ni were denoted as the total amount of excreted probes and the amount of injected probes, respectively.

Histological study

The organs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in ethanol solution, and embedded in paraffin before 10-μm sectioning. Histology samples were stained by H&E under standard protocols. Images were acquired using a panoramic slice scanner by Hungary.

Western blot analysis

The tissue blocks were washed two to three times with precooled PBS to remove blood stains, cut into small pieces, and placed in a homogenization tube. Two 4-mm homogenate beads were added, 10 times the tissue volume of lysate was added (various protease inhibitors were added within a few minutes before use), and the homogenization program was set for homogenization. (If the protein concentration needs to be increased, then the volume of the lysis liquid can be reduced by an appropriate amount.) The homogenized tubes were removed, the lysate was placed on ice for 30 min, and the tissue was shaken every 5 min to ensure complete lysis. The Western blot’s result was analyzed by AIWBwell.

Immunohistochemical staining

The dissected tissues are embedded and then sectioned, followed by an immunofluorescence experiment. First, antigen retrieval is performed, and then serum blocking is carried out. One and two antibodies are added for incubation, followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole staining for nuclear detection and probe CyCD-O-Leu for response. Last, the slides are sealed, and images are captured.

Establishment of murine models

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Regulation on the Administration of Laboratory Animals of Hubei Province and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Hubei University. Balb/c-nu mice (8 weeks old) were fed in a temperature-controlled (22° ± 3°C) room with 12-hour dark/light cycles.

DILI murine model: To establish DILI in 8-week-old BALB/c mice, paracetamol (APAP) was dissolved in normal saline under heating and given a single intraperitoneal injection of 300 mg/kg to BALB/c mice.

PC murine model: To establish PC in 8-week-old BALB/c mice, BxPC-3 cells (5 × 107 cells per mouse) were suspended in 0.1 ml of 50% (v/v) mixture of Matrigel in precooling PBS (pH 7.4). Each mouse was injected into the pancreas with cell-Matrigel mixture (0.1 ml).

KPC murine model: To establish the genetic KPC model, the male mice with the Kras-LSL-G12D and Trp53-R172H genes and the female mice with the Pdx1-Cre-Tg gene were mated to generate KPC mice with all three genes: Kras-LSL-G12D, Trp53-R172H, and Pdx1-Cre-Tg. The expression of the KPC mutant genes was detected using PCR technology to confirm their expression at the molecular level. The tumor growth was monitored using high-resolution ultrasound, and relevant imaging experiments were conducted after 14 weeks of nurturing, when the pancreatic tumors had formed.

Mouse imaging

Fluorescence images were captured using an InVivo Smart-LF system with a Cy5.5 filter and a HyperRed lamp (excitation at 630 to 680 nm and emission at 690 to 740 nm). Fluorescence intensity was quantified by CleVue software.
