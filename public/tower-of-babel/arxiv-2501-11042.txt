A nodally bound-preserving finite element method for hyperbolic convection-reaction problems
Ben S. Ashby, Abdalaziz Hamdan, Tristan Pryer

Abstract
. In this article, we present a numerical approach to ensure the preservation of physical bounds on the solutions to linear and nonlinear hyperbolic convection-reaction problems at the discrete level. We provide a rigorous framework for error analysis, formulating the discrete problem as a variational inequality and demonstrate optimal convergence rates in a natural norm. We summarise extensive numerical experiments validating the effectiveness of the proposed methods in preserving physical bounds and preventing unphysical oscillations, even in challenging scenarios involving highly nonlinear reaction terms.



1.
Introduction

Ensuring that numerical approximations respect certain physical bounds
presents a significant challenge across various fields in
computational modelling. Many important PDE models rely on solutions
that adhere to these bounds to maintain physical validity. For
instance, in phase-field modelling, solutions must strictly preserve
global maxima and minima
[
WGZ22
]
, while in
incompressible flow simulations, the velocity field is required to
remain divergence-free
[
SL18
]
. Similarly, in
applications such as kinetic equations, dose deposition modelling for
external beam radiotherapy

[
ACH+24
]
, or nuclear
engineering
[
CEL+24
]
, maintaining physical accuracy is
critical to ensure reliable and safe operations, particularly when
computational constraints limit the achievable numerical
fidelity. These bounds, derived from fundamental physical principles,
become especially important when the solution serves as input to
subsequent models, where unphysical values could propagate errors and
lead to erroneous predictions downstream.

A common manifestation of the failure of numerical solutions to
satisfy these bounds is the oscillation of the solution around layers
or irregularities in the PDE solution. For example, singularly
perturbed elliptic problems often exhibit boundary layers, while
hyperbolic problems can support discontinuities, which can lead to
under- and over-shoots numerically (see §
6
). Even
many stabilised numerical methods can exhibit unphysical oscillations
that overshoot these maxima and minima, as illustrated in Figure

3(b)
.

The aim of the method presented in this work is to prevent spurious
numerical behaviour from violating maximum principles. We propose a
finite element method that enforces physical bounds at nodal points by
discretising the problem as a variational inequality. This ensures
that the solution lies within a finite-dimensional, closed and convex
subset of the natural solution space for the PDE. We provide bounds on
the approximation error of the proposed bound-preserving finite
element method, demonstrating optimal convergence rates for a scalar
hyperbolic equation. This formulation enables rigorous error analysis
and yields a simple implementation whilst also offering flexibility in
computational mesh selection. Our analysis focusses on
convection-reaction equations, a class of problems where bound
preservation is particularly important due to the challenges posed by
sharp gradients, boundary layers and discontinuities. Moreover, we
extend our approach to nonlinear problems, leveraging appropriate
quasi-norms to establish best-approximation results within the
framework of variational formulations.

Standard finite element methods often fail to respect physical bounds
without imposing additional constraints or modifications. For example,
the most popular finite element methods for incompressible flows often
fail to ensure pointwise divergence-free solutions, and proving energy
stability in dissipative systems is similarly challenging (see, e.g.,

[
GMP14
,
CJ21
]
). This
was formalised early on in
[
CR73
]
, which demonstrated that
piecewise linear finite elements respect such bounds only under
specific mesh conditions.

A variety of methods have been proposed to address these challenges. A
notable approach is the development of methods satisfying the

Discrete Maximum Principle (DMP)
, particularly for
convection-dominated convection-diffusion problems (see

[
MH85
,
XZ99
,
BE05
,
Kuz07
,
BJK17
,
BJK23
,
WY24
]
, among
others). These methods often involve nonlinear stabilisation, where
additional nonlinear terms are introduced to ensure that the discrete
solution does not violate the maximum principle. Despite their
effectiveness, these methods frequently rely on piecewise linear
elements and extending them to higher-order elements introduces
stricter mesh conditions and additional analytical complexities.

Related approaches include nodally bound-preserving stabilised methods

[
BGPV24
,
ABP24
,
BPT24
]
,
where the discrete solution is shown to satisfy a variational
inequality. The idea of enforcing bounds by formulating the problem as
a variational inequality has also appeared in

[
CN17
,
KS24b
,
KS24a
]
. These
methods often involve solving nonlinear problems, a necessity
highlighted by the Godunov barrier theorem, which asserts that
achieving high-order accuracy while preserving monotonicity or bounds
is generally impossible with linear methods. Consequently,
nonlinearity is an inherent feature of any method aiming to respect
maximum principles whilst retaining higher-order accuracy.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: In
§
2
we introduce a model hyperbolic problem and
discuss its solution, regularity and approximation by the finite
element method. We then conduct an error analysis in
§
3
. Following in §
4
, we
provide a best approximation result for a hyperbolic problem with
singular nonlinearity in the reaction term. Implementation details
including methods to obtain an approximation to the finite element
solution are given in §
5
. Finally,
numerical experiments are presented in §
6
.

2.
Problem formulation & discretisation

In what follows,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, is assumed to be
a Lipschitz domain, ensuring that a unit outward normal vector

\bm{n}
is defined almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega
. For any
measurable subset
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, let
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\omega)
denote
the space of
p
-th power (Lebesgue) integrable functions over

\omega
, equipped with the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\omega)}
. The

\operatorname{L}^{2}
inner product over
\omega
is written as

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{\omega}:=\int_{\omega}uv\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, with the
convention that the subscript is omitted if
\omega=\Omega
.

The standard Sobolev space
\operatorname{W}^{k,p}(\omega)
is defined as the
space of functions in
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\omega)
whose weak derivatives of
order at most
k
are also in
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\omega)
. As is customary,

\operatorname{W}^{k,2}(\omega)
is denoted by
\operatorname{H}^{k}(\omega)
.

For
\bm{b}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we introduce
the linear hyperbolic problem given by

(1)

\begin{split}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u+cu&=f\quad\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u&=g\quad\text{on}\,\,\Gamma_{-},\end{split}

where
\Gamma_{-}
is the inflow boundary defined by
the flow field
\bm{b}
, that is,

\Gamma_{-}=\{\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega:\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x})<0\},

and

(2)

\Gamma_{+}=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{-}.

The analysis of this linear problem is significantly more complicated
than for advection-diffusion-reaction problems, as the smoothing
properties enjoyed by elliptic operators are not present here. For
example, even when
\bm{b}
is smooth, discontinuities can propagate
along streamlines, leading to solutions which are quite irregular. We
elaborate further on this point in Remark

2.2
.

We now proceed to discuss questions of existence, uniqueness and
regularity of solutions to (
1
). Let
\bm{b}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
and
c\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
and suppose there exists

\mu>0
such that

(3)

c(x)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{b}(\bm{x})\geqslant\mu\text{ for almost every }\bm{x}\in\Omega.

We define the function space

(4)

\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)=\{v\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v+cv\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega),\,\,(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})v=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\Gamma_{-}\},

where boundary values are interpreted in the trace sense. We refer to

[
SP22
, §3.1]
for a justification of sufficient
boundary regularity so that the boundary condition included in this
definition is meaningful. The space
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
is a
Hilbert space when equipped with norm

(5)

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)}=\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v+cv\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

allowing us to give the variational formulation of problem
(
1
): find
u\in\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
such that

(6)

a(u,v)=l(v)\quad\forall v\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega),

where

(7)

a(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w+cw\right)v,

and

(8)

l(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv.

The following result derived from Green’s formula will be frequently used, and is stated
here for convenience.

(9)

\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right)w=\int_{\partial\Omega}vw(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})-\int_{\Omega}v\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right)-\int_{\Omega}vw\operatorname{div}(\bm{b}).

We now present the main existence result, which assumes ‘good’ behaviour of
the advection field
\bm{b}

Proposition 2.1

(Existence & uniqueness of solutions
[
SP22
]
)
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain, and assume that the vector field
\bm{b}

lies in
C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
, with all vector components strictly positive. We additionally
assume
c\in C(\bar{\Omega})
. We assume additionally that
g
can be extended to
\Omega

such that the result lies in
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
. For
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, there
exists a unique solution
u\in\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
to problem
(
6
).

Remark 2.2

(Further remarks on regularity)
.

The conditions for the solution to problem
(
6
) to have full

\operatorname{H}^{1}(\Omega)
-regularity are strong, requiring
f\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
in addition to higher regularity on
\bm{b}
(see

[
Rau72
]
). However, in special cases, less regular
solutions can still be shown to exist. For example, as discussed in

[
HGR08
, Part III, 1.1]
, in the special case where
\Omega

is the unit square and the components of
\bm{b}
are bounded away
from zero, there is a unique solution that lies in
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
even
when
g\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{-})
, meaning problem
(
1
) supports discontinuous solutions.

More generally, as shown in
[
HGR08
, Theorem 3.1]
, the
problem admits a unique solution in
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
provided
g
has an
extension in
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)\cap\operatorname{L}^{q}(\Omega)
for some
q\geqslant 2
. Similar
results on the regularity of solutions can be found in

[
SP22
,
Ber12
,
GT10
]
.

Of course, the numerical analysis of such cases is complicated by
this lack of regularity. Indeed, it is precisely in these
scenarios-where the boundary data or the solution exhibits low
regularity-that spurious behaviour is frequently observed upon
discretisation with many numerical methods.

Remark 2.3

(Problems satisfying a priori bounds)
.

For some choices of
\bm{b}
, problem (
1
)
can be solved analytically using the method of characteristics. One
such case is when
\bm{b}
has well-defined, non-intersecting
streamlines with no stationary points. These conditions ensure that
any
\bm{x}\in\Omega
lies on a single characteristic curve which
originates at the inflow boundary, and therefore
u
is the solution
of an ODE along this characteristic with initial data given by the
inflow boundary condition. Assuming further that
c\geqslant 0
and that
the boundary data
g\in\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{-})
, we have for example
that if
f=0
almost everywhere,

(10)

u(\bm{x})\leqslant\left\|g\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{-})}.

2.1.
Finite element discretisation

Let
u
be the solution of (
6
) and,
without loss of generality, we assume that
u
satisfies the bound

u(\bm{x})\in[0,1]
for almost every
\bm{x}\in\Omega
. We assume
that the domain
\Omega
is subdivided into a conforming, shape-regular
triangulation
\mathscr{T}
, namely,
\mathscr{T}
is a finite family of sets such that

(1)

K\in\mathscr{T}{}
implies
K
is an open simplex or box,

(2)

for any
K,J\in\mathscr{T}{}
we have that
\overline{K}\cap\overline{J}
is a full
lower-dimensional simplex (i.e., it is either
\emptyset
, a vertex,
an edge or the whole of
\overline{K}
and
\overline{J}
) of both

\overline{K}
and
\overline{J}
and

(3)

\bigcup_{K\in\mathscr{T}{}}\overline{K}=\overline{\Omega}
.

We additionally assume that the elements align with transitions
between inflow and outflow boundaries. The finite element space
\mathbb{V}

is defined to be

(11)

\mathbb{V}:=\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{R}(K)\,\,\forall K\in\mathscr{T},\,v_{h}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{-}\},

where
\mathcal{R}(K)
is either
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
, the space of
polynomials of degree
k
, or
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)
, the space of
polynomials of total degree
k
, depending upon the triangulation. For
an element
K\in\mathscr{T}
, we denote the diameter of
K
by
h_{K}
, with
h:=\max_{K\in\mathscr{T}}h_{k}
. Finally, let
\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{N}
be
the union of the set of interior degrees of freedom and those that lie
on the outflow boundary.

We define the convex subset
K_{h}
of

\mathbb{V}
by restricting the nodal values of function in
\mathbb{V}
, that is

(12)

K_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}:v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\in[0,1],\,\,i=1,\dots,N\}.

Remark 2.4

(Preservation of the bound at the degrees of freedom)
.

For polynomial degree,
k=1
, the space
K_{h}
consists of precisely
the finite element functions which satisfy the upper and lower bound
pointwise since between nodes the functions are (bi)linearly
interpolated. For polynomial degree,
k=2
, or higher, this is not
the case, and
K_{h}
is the set of finite element functions which are

nodally
bound preserving. This property was investigated in

[
BGPV24
]
for second order elliptic problems,
where the authors obtain their approximations via a nonlinear
stabilised method rather than a variational inequality. There, the
bound preserving approximation was shown to be equivalent to the
solution of a discrete variational inequality.

Let
\delta_{K}
,
K\in\mathscr{T}
, be positive real numbers and let
a_{h}

denote the bilinear form associated with the SUPG stabilisation method

[
BH82
,
JNP84
]
, defined as

(13)

a_{h}(w_{h},v_{h})=\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w_{h}+cv_{h}\right)v_{h}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w_{h}+cw_{h}\right)\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v_{h}.

The numbers
\delta_{K}
are the SUPG parameters which determine the
local degree of stabilisation. The bilinear form
a_{h}
has an
associated norm for
w\in\operatorname{H}_{-}

(14)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}:=\mu\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}+|w|^{2}_{\Gamma^{+}},

where

|w|^{2}_{\Gamma^{+}}:=\int_{\Gamma^{+}}(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})w^{2}.

Then the finite element discretisation is to find
u_{h}\in K_{h}
such that

(15)

a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h}-u_{h})\geqslant l_{h}(v_{h}-u_{h})\quad\forall v_{h}\in K_{h},

where

l_{h}(v_{h}):=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}f\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\right).

3.
Error analysis

In this section we prove the main result of this work: an error bound for

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u-u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
, where
u_{h}
is a bound-preserving approximation to
u\in\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
. The key idea is that we can combine the weak forms of the
variational problem (
6
) and the discrete

inequality
(
15
) and use the coercivity and
continuity of
a_{h}
and the consistency of the SUPG formulation to derive a best
approximation result. Consistency is the subject of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(Consistency of the SUPG method)
.

Let
u
be the solution of problem
(
6
). Then for any
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
,

l_{h}(v_{h})-a_{h}(u,v_{h})=0.

Proof.

Note that

(16)

\begin{split}a_{h}(u,v_{h})&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u+cu\right)v_{h}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u+cu\right)\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}f\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v_{h}=l_{h}(v_{h}),\end{split}

by (
6
).
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Continuity & coercivity properties of
a_{h}
)
.

Assume that, for all
K\in\mathscr{T}
, the SUPG parameters
\delta_{K}
are chosen so
that

(17)

0\leqslant\delta_{K}\leqslant\frac{\mu}{\left\|c\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(K)}}.

Then the bilinear form
a_{h}
is coercive over
\operatorname{H}_{-}

with respect to the norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
, that is, for
w\in\operatorname{H}_{-}

(18)

a_{h}(w,w)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}.

Furthermore, for
w\in\operatorname{H}_{-}
we define the norm

(19)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}^{2}:={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}^{-1}\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}.

Then, for
w,v\in\operatorname{H}_{-}

a_{h}
satisfies the
continuity result

(20)

a_{h}(w,v)\leqslant C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|v\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

Proof.

We first show coercivity. Using (
9
) yields

(21)

\begin{split}a_{h}(w,w)\geqslant\inf_{x\in\Omega}\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{b}\right)\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})\lvert w\rvert^{2}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\left\|\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\\
-\left\lvert\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}cw(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w)\right\rvert,\end{split}

where only the final term on the right hand side remains to be estimated. To
this end, we observe that, on any
K\in\mathscr{T}
,

(22)

\left\lvert\int_{K}\delta_{K}cw(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w)\right\rvert\leqslant\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|c\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(K)}\left\|w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\left\|\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}.

We now split this product using Young’s inequality and note the
choice of
\delta_{K}
, given in (
17
) to see that

(23)

\begin{split}\left\lvert\int_{K}\delta_{K}cw(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w)\right\rvert&\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\delta_{K}\left\|c\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(K)}\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu\left\|w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|^{2}_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)},\end{split}

from which the result follows. To show continuity, we again use Green’s
formula and bound

(24)

\begin{split}a_{h}(w,v)=&\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w+cw\right)v+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w+cw\right)\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(c-\operatorname{div}\bm{b}\right)wv-\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right)w+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})wv\\
&+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\int_{K}\left(\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right)\left(\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right)+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\delta_{K}\int_{K}cw\left(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right).\end{split}

An application of Cauchy-Schwarz results in

(25)

\begin{split}\lvert a_{h}(w,v)\rvert&\leqslant\left\|c-\operatorname{div}\bm{b}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left\|w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|v\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\left\|\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\left\|\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{K}w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\\
&\qquad+\lvert w\rvert_{\Gamma_{+}}\lvert v\rvert_{\Gamma_{+}}+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\left\|\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\left\|\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{K\in\mathscr{T}}\left\|c\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(K)}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\left\|w\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}\left\|\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}_{K}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(K)}.\end{split}

Finally, let
M=\max\left\{1,\left\|c-\operatorname{div}\bm{b}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)},\left\|c\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\max_{K}\delta_{K}^{1/\penalty 2}\right\}
. Then applying a discrete
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
25
) gives

(26)

\lvert a_{h}(w,v)\rvert\leqslant 2\frac{M}{\mu}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|v\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

as required.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Best approximation)
.

Let
u\in\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)
be the solution of
(
6
) and let
u_{h}\in K_{h}
be the
solution of (
15
). Then we have the
following best approximation result:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u-u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leqslant C\inf_{v_{h}\in K_{h}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u-v_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{*}.

Proof.

We begin our estimation noting that, by coercivity of
a_{h}
with
respect to the norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
,

(27)

\frac{1}{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|u-u_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leqslant a_{h}(u-u_{h},u-u_{h}).

By consistency, shown in Lemma
3.1
, we have

(28)

a_{h}(u,u_{h}-v_{h})=l_{h}(u_{h}-v_{h}),

and since
u_{h}
satisfies the discrete problem (
15
)
we have

(29)

a_{h}(u_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})\leqslant l_{h}(u_{h}-v_{h}).

Equations (
28
) and
(
29
) together imply that

a_{h}(u-u_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})\geqslant 0.

The inequality (
27
) therefore leads to

Remark 3.4

4.
Extension to the case of nonlinear reaction

We now consider the extension of the analysis of the previous sections
to the problem

(35)

\begin{split}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u+|u|^{p-2}u&=f\quad\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u&=0\quad\text{on}\,\,\Gamma_{-},\end{split}

where
1<p\leqslant 2
. The problem therefore has nonlinear reaction
which is singular as
u\to 0

positivity of solutions becomes very important.

Define (cf. Equation (

\operatorname{H}_{-,p}(\Omega)=\{v\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v+|v|^{p-2}v\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega),\,\,(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n})v=0\,\,\text{on}\,\,\Gamma_{-}\}.

Then the weak form of problem (

) is to find
u\in\operatorname{H}_{-,p}(\Omega)
such that

(36)

a(u,v)+b(u;u,v)=l(v)\quad\forall v\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega),

where

and

are as defined in (
7
) and
(
8
) (the former with

), and where the semilinear form

b(u;v,w)=\int_{\Omega}\lvert u\rvert^{p-2}vw\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Remark 4.1

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

The existence and uniqueness of solutions to this problem, in the
presence of diffusion, can be established using monotone operator
theory, as detailed in
[
RR06
, Chapter
10]
. While the proof techniques for the
corresponding linear problem do not straightforwardly generalise to
the nonlinear case, they can be adapted through fixed-point arguments.

Classical error analysis in the
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\Omega)
norm often leads to
suboptimal estimates for

. To address this issue, quasi-norms
were introduced in
[

as a tool for achieving
optimal convergence rates. We define the following quasi-norm, for
fixed

(38)

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{(w,p)}:=\int_{\Omega}\lvert v\rvert^{2}\left(\lvert v\rvert+\lvert w\rvert\right)^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

4.1.
Preliminary results

Error analysis in quasi-norms requires technical lemmata which are stated here for
convenience. The key results are strong monotonicity and boundedness properties of the
form

with respect to the quasi-norm, given in Lemma

and Lemma

Lemma 4.2
.

(

Let

and

\left\lvert|x|^{p-2}x-\lvert y\rvert^{p-2}y\right\rvert\leqslant C_{1}\left(\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert,

(40)

\left(|x|^{p-2}x-\lvert y\rvert^{p-2}y\right)\left(x-y\right)\geqslant C_{2}\left(\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2},

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists

such that, for all

b(u;u,u-v)-b(v;v,u-v)\geqslant C_{C}\left\|u-v\right\|^{2}_{(u,p)}.

Proof.

We first note that

(42)

\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert=\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y-x+x\rvert\leqslant 2\lvert x\rvert+\lvert x-y\rvert,

We therefore we have, noting that

(43)

\begin{split}\left(|x|^{p-2}x-\lvert y\rvert^{p-2}y\right)\left(x-y\right)&\geqslant C_{2}\left(\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\geqslant 2^{p-2}C_{2}\left(\lvert x\rvert+\lvert x-y\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2},\end{split}

and so the result follows with
C_{C}=2^{p-2}C_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For
1<p<2
, there exists
C_{B}>0
such
that for any

and any

Proof.

For real numbers

and

see that

(45)

\begin{split}\left\lvert|x|^{p-2}x-\lvert y\rvert^{p-2}y\right\rvert|w|&\leqslant C_{1}\left(\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert|w|\\
&\leqslant C^{\prime}_{1}\left(\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert\right)^{p-2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert|w|,\end{split}

which can be further bounded by invoking

∎

5.
Overview of active-set techniques and applications

The variational inequality (
15
), posed over
the finite-dimensional space
K_{h}
, is nonlinear and must be solved
using an iterative method. In this section, we describe solution
techniques for this class of problems. Throughout, we denote a vector

\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as non-negative, written
\bm{\xi}\geqslant 0
,
if all its components satisfy
(\bm{\xi})_{i}\geqslant 0
.

The problem (
15
) can be viewed as a specific
instance of the general variational inequality

(58)

\left\langle G(u),v-u\right\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall v\in K,

where
G:V\to V
is a mapping on a Banach space
V
and
K
is a
closed and convex subset of
V
. In the discrete setting, we introduce
a discrete operator
\bar{G}_{h}:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
and reformulate
(
15
) as: find
u_{h}\in K_{h}
such that:

(59)

\left\langle\bar{G}_{h}(u_{h}),v_{h}-u_{h}\right\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall v_{h}\in K_{h}.

An equivalent reformulation involves a mapping
G_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N
denotes the dimension of the finite element
space. This mapping is defined using the finite element system matrix
and right-hand side. Specifically, let
\bm{A}_{h}
represent the
assembled system matrix such that

(60)

\left(\bm{A}_{h}\right)_{ij}=a_{h}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i}),

with
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
the nodal basis functions. Similarly, let

(61)

\left(\bm{F}_{h}\right)_{i}=l_{h}(\varphi_{i}).

If
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector of degrees of freedom
corresponding to a finite element function
v_{h}
, given by:

(62)

v_{h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{V}_{i}\varphi_{i}(\bm{x}),

then the mapping
G_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is given by:

(63)

G_{h}(\bm{V})=\bm{A}_{h}\bm{V}-\bm{F}_{h}.

We also define the discrete set

(64)

K_{h,N}:=\{\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:0\leqslant\bm{V}_{i}\leqslant 1,\,i=1,\dots,N\}.

The discrete variational inequality (
15
) can
now be reformulated algebraically as: Find
\bm{U}\in K_{h,N}

such that

(65)

G_{h}(\bm{U})\cdot(\bm{V}-\bm{U})\geqslant 0\quad\forall\bm{V}\in K_{h,N}.

Definition 5.1

(Projection Operators)
.

Let
P:\mathbb{V}\to K_{h}
denote the orthogonal projection onto the
closed convex subset
K_{h}
. Assuming
K_{h}
is a box as defined in
(
12
), the projection
P
has a simple
explicit form. For
v_{h}\in K_{h}
, we define:

(66)

P(v_{h}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\left\{0,\min\left\{v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i}),1\right\}\right\}\varphi_{i}.

Similarly, we define the projection in the Euclidean setting,
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to K_{h,N}
, as:

(67)

\Pi(\bm{X})_{i}:=\max\left\{0,\min\left\{\bm{X}_{i},1\right\}\right\}.

5.1.
Projection Methods

For discretisations of many elliptic variational inequalities, a
common approach combines iterative schemes, such as Richardson
iteration or successive over-relaxation (SOR), with a projection
step. These methods ensure that the iterates remain within the convex
set and are relatively straightforward to implement. The underlying
principle for such methods is that the discrete problem can often be
reformulated as a minimisation problem, allowing the application of
various well-established optimisation algorithms.

However, when the finite element system matrix is not symmetric, as is
the case in this problem, these classical methods generally fail to
converge. This limitation necessitates the use of projection methods
that are robust under broader assumptions. One such method, introduced
in
[
Alf94
]
, iteratively projects the solution back
onto the feasible set and is defined as follows. Given an initial
guess
u_{h}^{0}\in K_{h}
, the method updates the iterate using:

(68)

u_{h}^{j+1}=P\left(u_{h}^{j}-\gamma\bar{G}_{h}(u_{h}^{j})\right),

where
\gamma>0
is a step size parameter. Iteration is terminated
when the stopping criterion
\left\|u_{h}^{j+1}-u_{h}^{j}\right\|<\texttt{TOL}
is satisfied, for a given tolerance
TOL
.

The projection method, as detailed in
[
Alf94
]
,
converges to a solution under the assumptions that the operator is
monotone and Lipschitz continuous, without requiring
differentiability. While this method is simple and guarantees
convergence, it often requires many iterations to achieve acceptable
accuracy. Consequently, for large-scale problems or those demanding
high efficiency, more advanced and scalable methods are necessary to
enhance convergence rates and reduce computational costs.

5.2.
Reduced-Space Active Set Method

The numerical results presented in this work were obtained using the
finite element discretisation package Firedrake

[
HKM+23
]
, which leverages PETSc

[
BGMS98
]
for solving the variational inequalities
introduced in Sections
2
and

4
. Among the various solvers available in PETSc, we
employed the reduced-space active-set method

[
BM06
]
. This method is particularly well-suited for
parallel implementations and guarantees that the computed solution
satisfies the imposed constraints. Although no formal convergence
proofs were provided in
[
BM06
]
, the method has
demonstrated efficiency and robustness in numerous test cases,
especially in applications involving monotone operators, which aligns
with the scope of this work.

Let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Define the active and inactive sets
associated with
\bm{X}
as follows:

(69)

\displaystyle A_{0}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle:=\left\{i:\bm{X}_{i}=0\text{ and }G_{h}(\bm{X})_{i}>0\right\},

(70)

\displaystyle A_{1}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle:=\left\{i:\bm{X}_{i}=1\text{ and }G_{h}(\bm{X})_{i}\leqslant 0\right\},

(71)

\displaystyle A(\bm{X})

\displaystyle:=A_{0}(\bm{X})\cup A_{1}(\bm{X}),

(72)

\displaystyle I(\bm{X})

\displaystyle:=\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus A(\bm{X}),

where
G_{h}(\bm{X})_{i}
denotes the
i^{\text{th}}
component of the
nonlinear residual evaluated at
\bm{X}
. The set
A(\bm{X})

contains the indices of degrees of freedom where the box constraints
are active, while
I(\bm{X})
denotes the inactive set.

In the reduced-space active set method, starting from an initial guess

\bm{X}^{0}
, the degrees of freedom corresponding to
A(\bm{X}^{j})

are fixed at each iteration. A linear system, derived from the
linearisation of
G_{h}
, is solved over the inactive set
I(\bm{X}^{j})

to compute an update
\delta\bm{X}^{j}
, analogous to Newton’s
method. Specifically, let
G_{h}^{I}(\bm{X}^{j})
denote the reduced
residual vector containing only the entries indexed by
I(\bm{X}^{j})
,
and let
J^{I}
be the Jacobian of
G_{h}^{I}
restricted to the inactive
set. The update is then decomposed as

(73)

\delta\bm{X}^{j}=\delta^{I}\bm{X}^{j}\oplus\delta^{A}\bm{X}^{j},

where
\delta^{A}\bm{X}^{j}=\bm{0}
, and
\delta^{I}\bm{X}^{j}
satisfies

(74)

J^{I}\delta^{I}\bm{X}^{j}=-G_{h}^{I}(\bm{X}^{j}).

The updated solution is then computed as

(75)

\bm{X}^{j+1}=\Pi\left(\bm{X}^{j}+\alpha\delta\bm{X}^{j}\right),

where
\Pi
denotes the projection onto the constrained space

K_{h,\,N}
, as defined in (
67
). A line search is used
to determine the step size
\alpha
, employing either backtracking
or a secant-based algorithm provided by PETSc. For further details on
the selection of
\alpha
, we refer the reader to

[
BM06
,
ZYK+21
]
.

Importantly, due to the projection in (
75
),
all iterates satisfy the box constraints, i.e.,
\bm{X}^{j}\in K_{h,\,N}
for all
j
. The algorithm terminates when either the
residual or the relative reduction in the residual falls below a
specified tolerance, set to
10^{-8}
in this work.

6.3.
Example 3: A discontinuous pure advection problem

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the bound preserving method for a problem with low
regularity and where violation of the maximum principle is expected when standard finite
element methods are used, we consider a pure advection problem with discontinuous data

spurious under- and over-shoots as the discontinuity is propagated by the velocity
field. The advection field is chosen to be

(80)

\bm{b}_{3}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}(-y,x),

\Gamma^{+}=\{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega:x=1\}\cup\{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega:y=0\}.

Boundary data is prescribed as follows:

(81)

g_{3}(x,y):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x<\frac{1}{3}$},\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if $\frac{1}{3}\leqslant x<\frac{2}{3}$},\\
1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

resulting in a discontinuous piecewise constant solution having
\operatorname{H}_{-}(\Omega)

regularity but no better, illustrated in

7.
Conclusions and Further Work

In this work, we introduced a method for preserving bounds on
numerical solutions to partial differential equations by reformulating
the standard discrete weak form as a discrete variational
inequality. The finite element method was analysed for both a linear
first-order problem and a first-order problem with a nonlinear
reaction term. The SUPG method was adapted to this bound-preserving
framework, and the expected convergence rates were successfully
retained.

While certain aspects of the analysis are tailored to the specific
problems considered, the proposed framework is broadly applicable and
can, in principle, be extended to any problem admitting a variational
formulation. This flexibility enables the application of the method to
a wide range of partial differential equations using existing finite
element techniques.

The approach outlined here shows particular promise for problems
involving singular coefficients or where strict preservation of bounds
is essential to ensure physical validity. Future work will explore the
extension of this framework to kinetic equations

[
ACH+24
,
PPT24
]
,
where ensuring positivity of solutions is important physically, and to
models of non-Newtonian fluids
[
AP25
]
, which
feature complex nonlinearities and require positive preserving schemes
to ensure well-posedness. Key to all these applications is the
efficient solution and computational scalability of this class of
method.
