Magnetically guided biohybrid microrobots with multimodal locomotion for barrier penetration and multifunctional tumor treatment
Authors: Yizhi Gong, Mengqi Fan, Zhuluni Fang, Immihan Ceren Yasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Penetrating biological and physical barriers within the body is essential for microrobots to access target sites and achieve effective therapeutic outcomes. However, synthetic microrobots exhibit limited deformability and dynamicity, which are required to navigate tight and complex microenvironments. Here, by leveraging the soft, deformable body of Euglena gracilis, we develop a novel biohybrid microrobot platform that integrates magnetic architectures for controlled propulsion, deformation, and multi-modal locomotion. This design not only preserves the natural motility of microalgae but also leverages their intrinsic therapeutic properties, including chlorophyll-dependent photodynamic therapy (PDT) and immune modulation through E. gracilis natural products. Our biohybrid microrobots navigate through dense three-dimensional biological matrices and around tumor spheroids, exhibiting targeted delivery to tumor regions under both magnetic control and autonomous tumor tropic behavior. This multi-functional platform combines adaptive locomotion, controllable and chemotactic guidance, offering a new paradigm for precision medicine without the need for exogenous drug loading, and has the potential to become a versatile future solution for tumor targeting and dynamic, adaptive treatment in complex medical environments.

Introduction

Overcoming physical and biological barriers is paramount for advancing microrobotic targeted therapies1,2. Therefore, there is a critical need for developing microrobots capable of adaptively navigating the complex and constrained environments of the human body, including traversing long distances and penetrating narrow, delicate regions without compromising their structural integrity. Soft and deformable microrobots can fulfill this requirement by exhibiting the flexibility to squeeze through tight spaces and dynamically change their shape to overcome physical barriers. By endowing microrobots with such deformation capabilities, the risk of damaging surrounding tissue is also minimized, thereby enhancing safety and efficacy.

Extensive efforts have been devoted to developing biohybrid microrobots, integrating living organisms, such as algae, bacteria, and cells, including sperm cells and immune cells, with the synthetic components to utilize the inherent properties of living systems3–7. These microrobots leverage the autonomous movement and sensing capabilities of such organisms and cells to improve the motility and adaptability for targeted therapies1,8,9. Among various microorganisms, microalgae have shown great potential as active biological motors in a range of biomedical and environmental applications. They offer unique benefits, such as sustained autonomous movements, phototaxis ability, and autofluorescence properties that facilitate imaging and tracking10,11. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (CR) is a widely utilized microalgae with advantages including efficient phototactic movement, high biocompatibility, and the ability to produce oxygen through photosynthesis4,12,13. However, the widely used CR is not a soft organism and cannot demonstrate body deformations due to its rigid cell wall. At the same time, it also lacks many of the natural nutrients found in other microalgae species14–16. On the other hand, immune cells exhibit shape deformation, including nuclear and cytoskeletal remodeling, which enables them to penetrate tight physical and biological barriers during migration, yet their migration speeds are almost an order of magnitude smaller than that of microalgae17,18.

In this regard, the locomotion abilities of E. gracilis, characterized by its soft, deformable body and multi-modal movement patterns, as well as its high nutritional value and food potential, present an attractive source for developing medical biohybrid microrobots. E. gracilis naturally exhibits flexible body deformations and multiple movement modes such as flagellar swimming and euglenoid movement, enabling it to navigate complex microenvironments19,20. Flagellar swimming involves the beating of a long flagellum that generates spiral waves, propelling the organism forward and causing it to rotate along its axis. This mode allows E. gracilis to swim through fluids efficiently. Additionally, E. gracilis exhibits a process referred to as euglenoid movement (i.e., metaboly). It is characterized by large, coordinated body deformations facilitated by waves of contraction and expansion along the flexible pellicle, which is a protein layer supported by microtubules spiraling around the cell. These peristaltic-like shape changes allow E. gracilis to move in complex microenvironments such as viscous or confined environments, where flagellar motion alone would be insufficient21,22. These locomotion strategies give E. gracilis exceptional adaptability and represent a rich source of inspiration for creating microrobots capable of navigating challenging biological settings. Previously, light-controlled E. gracilis-based microrobot was demonstrated for biomedical applications23,24. However, light control has a limited application space within the body due to issues with penetration depth, presenting a challenge for long-distance navigation and control of microrobots25. On the contrary, magnetic field-based control provides deeper tissue penetration, non-invasive and precise remote actuation, and greater compatibility with complex biological environments, making it a more versatile and effective method for guiding microrobots in biomedical applications26. Meanwhile, compared to other biological organisms, E. gracilis possesses significant nutritional added value, especially paramylon, which is a polysaccharide and has been previously proven to have medical functions such as immune regulation15,27,28.

Herein, we report multifunctional, magnetically controllable E. gracilis-based biohybrids, mEbots, with adaptive and multimodal locomotion capabilities for targeted photodynamic therapy and immunomodulation (Fig. 1). The direction of mEbots’ locomotion can be precisely controlled by an externally applied magnetic field in the swimming mode, whereas switching to metaboly can be induced by the environmental changes, such as viscosity and confinement. Our mEbots exhibit robust swimming performance in diverse environments and metaboly with shape deformation for navigating viscous environments and fibrous 3D collagen matrices. With such features of controlled swimming and deformability, mEbots are directed to the colorectal tumor to execute photodynamic therapy with high efficiency. Hybridization of E. gracilis’ intrinsic multimodal locomotion abilities with magnetic control creates a versatile platform for developing microrobots capable of multitasking and multimodal locomotion for navigating complex environments.

Result

Formation and characterization of mEbots

To create biohybrid microrobots with multimodal locomotion capabilities for overcoming physical and biological barriers, we utilized E. gracilis due to its flagellar swimming and metaboly properties. E. gracilis metaboly is a specialized, adaptable mode of shape-changing movement that provides propulsion and allows the microalgae to navigate complex environments, assisting in crawling and escape strategies when normal flagellar swimming fails21,22. It refers to the ability to change and deform their body shapes through coordinated and large-amplitude body deformations. These deformations result from the sliding of pellicle strips, a protein layer supported by microtubules spiraling around the cell (Fig. S1A–C). Since the deforming function of E. gracilis requires sliding between adjacent pellicular strips, single spherical magnetic particles larger than the width of a single strip will impede their movement. Guided by the pellicle’s characteristic dimensions we observed (Fig. S1C), we designed a nanoscale magnetic particle network that preserves metaboly while providing sufficient magnetic moment to steer the biohybrids. After choosing a specific magnetic particle morphology, we employed a biotin-streptavidin conjugation strategy (Fig. 2a). Initially, amine-functionalized magnetic iron nanoparticles (MNPs, 200 nm) were modified with the low-molecular-weight chitosan4,10. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis confirmed the chitosan modification of small-sized spherical magnetic nanoparticles into an interconnected network suitable for attachment to E. gracilis (Fig. 2b). Chitosan also presents reactive sites for further conjugation, enabling the introduction of fluorescent tags and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)–biotin. Consistent with successful biotinylation, the zeta potential of the chitosan-modified MNPs shifted from 34.7 ± 3.54 mV to 52.67 ± 5.8 mV (Fig. S1D). When assembled into chained/networked architectures, these nanoparticles exhibit soft-ferromagnetic behavior with collective magnetic moments and enhanced magnetization29. In alignment with this, magnetic characterization of the chitosan-MNPs network by vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) demonstrated soft-magnetic behavior with a saturation magnetization of 54.5 emu/g, remanence of 5.5 emu/g, and a coercivity of 61.8 mT (Fig. S1E). E. gracilis cells were also biotinylated with Sulfo-NHS-biotin, and Cy3-streptavidin non-covalent interaction with biotin confirmed successful surface modification (Fig. 2c, d). To minimize chemical stress on the cells, we employed water-soluble reagents and performed conjugations on vigorously growing cultures (days 3–5 of the cycle; Fig. S1F). Then, through biotin-streptavidin non-covalent interaction, we assembled the biotinylated MNP-chitosan network and streptavidin-labeled microalgae, as shown in Fig. 2e. Due to the streptavidin’s tetramer structure, bulk mixing leads to crosslinking of multiple cells. To overcome this aggregation challenge, we employed a droplet microfluidic workflow that encapsulates single streptavidin-modified cells while supplying an adequate amount of biotinylated MNP-chitosan network inside each droplet by tuning flow rates and pressures. Although this approach yields a relatively high fraction of empty droplets, it reliably suppresses multicell packaging and improves the uniformity of the mEbots (Fig. 2f). The emulsification/demulsification sequence caused negligible damage to the mEbot (Fig. S2, movie S1), and quantitative analyses confirmed a significant improvement in conjugation quality and yield with the microfluidic method, from approximately 40% to 70% mEbot generation rate (Fig. 2g). The confocal fluorescence microscope and the label-free high-resolution microscope demonstrated the interface between the MNP-chitosan network and the surface of the microalgae. It is also important to note that due to limitations in contrast, focal plane, and the use of monochrome cameras, optical microscopy could not fully reveal the coverage of particles on mEbot. We therefore employed SEM at high magnification to demonstrate how the particle network adheres to the pellicle surface of mEbot. Additionally, high-magnification imaging with a color camera further confirmed the distribution of particles across the mEbot surface. These findings collectively help elucidate how the particle network integrates into the pellicle without compromising the mobility and deformability of mEbot (Figs. 2h and S2B, C). Furthermore, to better reveal the amount of the loaded MNPs, we utilized Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) on the same batch of mEbots to assess the per-cell iron content, which is found to be 0.2272 ± 0.0255 pg/cell.

Motion characterization and magnetic guidance of mEbots

We first tested and compared the swimming behavior of the bare microalgae, and mEbots, in the presence and absence of external magnetic control. Upon modification, mEbots exhibited a mean speed of around 65 ± 21.5 μm/s, showing a decrease in swimming speed compared to bare microalgae swimming with a speed of 78 ± 34.9 μm/s (Fig. 3a). This decrease can be attributed to increased hydrodynamic drag on the body due to the surface-attached nanoparticles and the mechanical effects of the conjugation process on mEbots’ metabolism. We then generated a uniform 15 mT magnetic field along one axis to navigate the mEbots. We have achieved directional control along the same axis and measured the mEbot’s swimming speed as 43 ± 17.1 μm/s, with markedly reduced stochastic trajectories, demonstrating stable magnetic control. The speed distribution of magnetically controlled mEbots shifted toward lower values compared to free-swimming E. gracilis and mEbot, indicating a certain response latency under magnetic control (Fig. 3a). It is important to note that only 2D projections of the motion were analyzed, because motion along the z-axis is not captured during the video acquisition. We also examined the mobility of mEbots in media with varying composition and pH to assess their adaptability (Figs. 3b, c and S3). The results showed that mEbots maintained motility across different solutions, sustaining measurable speeds under all tested conditions, thereby indicating a degree of physicochemical tolerance. Our observations suggest that osmotic pressure, composition, and viscosity of the solution have a subtle but significant impact on mEbot movement. In PBS or artificial colonic fluid (ACF), where osmotic pressure is high, the movement of mEbots is limited to ~40 μm/s and 25 μm/s, respectively, with relatively small speed fluctuations. However, in DMEM, which is rich in nutrients such as sugars, mEbots exhibit highly irregular movements. They exhibited both a dormant state and extremely rapid movements with a peak speed of around 110 μm/s. We also tested the mEbot swimming in various pH environments. Aligning with the previous research24, mEbot exhibited swimming over a pH range of 4 to 10, with a peak activity near pH 7.5, and a movement speed of around 75 μm/s. mEbot peak activity at pH 7.5 mirrors wild-type Euglena gracilis optimal growth and motility range (pH 6–8), where flagellar beat frequency and amplitude are maximized. This neutral optimum likely reflects the optimal function of pH-sensitive enzymes in the flagellar ATP synthase and dynein motors powering the 10–15 Hz beat pattern essential for swimming velocity.

All tested media-maintained pH within the physiological range for E. gracilis (7.0–7.4), excluding pH as a confounding factor. In low-viscosity media (around 1.0 cP), mEbots exhibited the highest speeds in DMEM (containing 4.5 g/L glucose), which were around 20% greater than in sugar-free AF-6 despite comparable ionic strengths, suggesting that exogenous carbohydrates enhance flagellar beating frequency via accelerated ATP production. Conversely, exposure to high ionic strength media (PBS, 0.156 M) induced a modest (around 12%) reduction in swimming speed relative to AF-6 medium. This attenuation likely stems from ionic disturbance to the ATP-dependent flagellar motor: elevated ion concentrations may dissipate transmembrane proton gradients essential for mitochondrial ATP synthesis, thereby limiting the energy supply to flagellar motor24. Critically, viscosity emerged as the dominant determinant of locomotion. When viscosity exceeded around 2.0 cP (30% Ficoll), mEbot velocity declined by >60% and the propulsion mode transitioned from sustained directional swimming to intermittent tumbling (Fig. 3h, i). These results establish a viscosity threshold for effective mEbot navigation in biological environments and highlight the necessity of energy-rich media for sustained motility.

After characterizing the swimming speeds of mEbots in various environments, we have demonstrated precise magnetic navigation over predefined trajectories. Under programmed magnetic guidance, mEbots were steered on a square-shaped trajectory, where they performed repeated sharp turns with high repeatability and acceptable response times (Fig. 3d). Time-lapse trajectories demonstrated discrete, stepwise heading control through successive corners. It is worth noting that the experimental range for the magnetic control steering was confined to a circular pool approximately 10 mm in diameter inside the center of the working space, and the magnetic field distribution here was approximately a unidirectional and uniform field (Fig. S3B, movie S2). In its natural state, E. gracilis exhibits diverse swimming patterns with stochastic trajectories, which are associated with its light-sensing functionality and result in a biased Lévy-walk–like motion along spatial light gradients10,30. However, under directional magnetic fields, mEbot yielded stable typical spiral trajectories (Fig. 3e). In the absence of magnetic control, tracks were tortuous with frequent reorientations, while the magnetic guidance produced directed, persistent spirals with reduced dispersion, demonstrating robust external steering (Fig. 3f, g). To further confirm that mEbot’s flagellar motility (not the magnetic pulling force) drives the motion, we performed control experiments using glutaraldehyde-fixed mEbots, as shown in Movie S2. After fixation, mEbots lost their ability to swim via flagellar beating. Under these conditions, when a rotating magnetic field was applied, the fixed mEbots were completely unable to move and could only rotate due to the magnetic torque exerted on their body. This clearly demonstrates that the magnetic field serves solely directional control and alignment during the swimming mode, rather than exerting pulling forces. For all experiments, the region of interest (ROI) was confined to a reasonably small area within the uniform magnetic field, specifically, for example, the central region of the confocal dish, spanning no more than 5 mm in diameter, as confirmed by magnetic field mapping in Fig. S3B.

Barrier penetration ability is pivotal for microrobots to access hard-to-reach sites for medical applications. To achieve this, a microrobot should exhibit reversible and dynamic shape changing. In this regard, to examine how shear and viscosity affect both movement and deformation of our microrobots, we challenged mEbot across Newtonian fluids and non-Newtonian fluids spanning a wide rheological range (Fig. S3C). Ficoll solutions provided graded increases in viscosity and hydrodynamic load, while an aqueous two-phase system (ATPS) created complex droplet microenvironments with a shear viscosity of nearly 10 mPa.s. We observed a gradual decrease in flagellar motion speed as the viscosity of the medium increased. This viscosity-dependent switching highlights mEbot’s multimodal locomotion strategy, wherein flagellar swimming and pellicle-mediated shape change (metaboly) are selectively deployed as complementary modes to sustain mobility under complex and high shear stress conditions (Fig. 3h). For instance, in the 13% Ficoll solution, the viscosity reached ~70 mPa s. At this point, the movement of mEbot was predominantly slow crawling with an average net displacement speed of around 12 μm/s. Although the flagella were still swinging, the metaboly behavior of the body contributed to a stronger propulsion in this high-viscosity environment (movie S3). Therefore, we calculated the movement speed by using the overall displacement (Fig. 3i). Importantly, the MNPs network did not impede pellicle sliding or wrinkling, preserving this adaptive motion repertoire (Fig. S2C). In summary, this E. gracilis-based biohybrids preserves a protozoan-like capacity for deformation and can generate propulsion in high-viscosity environments, where metaboly becomes particularly advantageous. When encountering physical constraints such as droplets, this deformability enables the mEbot to traverse narrow spaces or actively create room for its own movement through shape changes and pushing the nearby droplets (movie S3).

Metaboly of mEbots inside 3D collagen matrices under magnetic guidance

To further evaluate the deformability of mEbots in more complex environments mimicking the native tissues, we conducted experiments in transwell assays, followed by studies within fibrous collagen-I gels possessing defined elastic properties and porosities. These experimental setups were designed to mimic the physical properties of extracellular matrices (ECM) and the corresponding cellular behaviors observed in vivo31–34.

We first evaluated the penetration capabilities in a trans-well setting by introducing the mEbots to the upper chamber and filling the lower compartment with the ATPS to serve as a marker for observation. Notably, mEbots were observed trapped within individual pores, with some showing deformation and occasional membrane penetration (Fig. 4a). When a magnetic field was applied, most mEBots migrated to the lower chamber as observed in the 3D-reconstructed images. We also observed occasional aggregation due to magnetic attraction (Fig. 4a). Next, we examined the spatial distribution and penetration of mEbots in 3D collagen gels formed at varying concentrations. We employed two-photon microscopy combined with second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging and confocal reflection microscopy to visualize mEbot distribution relative to the collagen network. We successfully captured their spatial distribution within the 3D matrix after magnetic attraction, as well as their real-time deformation dynamics in complex collagen-I microenvironments. First, we quantified porosity and projected pore diameter from the SHG images (Fig. S5A, B). Top-view projections and 3D reconstructions revealed the fibrous, collagen network and the spatial distribution of mEbots within the collagen gels (Fig. 4b). We observed that at a collagen concentration of 1 mg/ml, mEbots responded to magnetic fields and penetrated the fibrous network, with most of them distributed near the bottom surface. On the contrary, higher-density networks impeded the movement of mEbots, as evidenced by fluorescence intensity profiles along the Z-axis extracted from 3D stacks, which also demonstrated the vertical penetration depth of mEbots (Fig. 4b, c). At 1.5 mg/ml, mEbots were detected throughout the depth, whereas at 2 mg/ml, penetration was limited. We also observed that, without any external guidance, E. gracilis in the upper supernatant rarely penetrates the collagen network (Fig. S5C). This indicates that magnetic control is critical to guiding the penetration of the mEbot.

Following initial qualitative verification using a 3D two-photon microscope, we performed quantitative penetration experiments. We established a transwell model where a 1 mm-thick collagen gel, varying concentrations (1 mg/ml and 1.5 mg/ml, Fig. S5D) is formed on the upper chamber, and we measured the fluorescence in the lower chamber to estimate the penetrated mEbots that successfully traversed the barrier (Fig. S5E, F). To get the penetration rate, we established a standard curve with the known number of mEbots and their corresponding autofluorescence (Fig. S5G). Based on our qualitative observations, we applied a magnetic field for around 10 h in the transwell system, using a permanent magnet positioned below to generate a pulling force on the mEbot toward the gel. The results demonstrate that mEbots can penetrate matrices with higher porosity and relatively larger pore sizes under long-time magnetic actuation with a penetration rate of 29.23 ± 9.11%, whereas with 10 h of magnetic attraction, mEbots in 1.5 mg/ml collagen gels only showed a penetration rate of 1.79 ± 0.66 (Fig. 4d). Notably, the quantitative assay can only measure the mEbots that fully traversed a 1 mm-thick collagen matrix, whereas 3D two-photon imaging reveals their distribution within the matrix. Due to the limited acquisition speed of 3D two-photon imaging of the collagen gel, real-time tracking of mEbots during the motion process remains challenging.

Here, collagen concentration directly determines the system’s pore size and stiffness. As the concentration increases, the pore size decreases while stiffness increases, collectively restricting mEbot locomotion (Fig. 4e). Although the positional statistics can exhibit variability due to intrinsic porosity and heterogeneity of the collagen network, and batch-to-batch differences in cross-linking31, we can still extract the dominant trend from these initial observations that the mEbot is capable of traversing a 3D matrix with a given porosity and stiffness under magnetic attraction. To further elucidate the deformation-driven locomotion of mEbots during penetration of a 3D collagen network, a high-resolution confocal reflection microscopy was used to simultaneously image the collagen matrix and track mEbot positions and morphology over time. This approach enabled preliminary observation of mEbot-matrix interactions, visualizing the deformation states of the mEbot while dynamically interacting with the surrounding collagen matrix (Fig. S5I). This dynamic interaction resulted in creating a larger space for the movement of mEbots, which is consistent with the behavior of E. gracilis reported in previous studies inside confined spaces21. Although crawling was observed to be slow, mEbots consistently exhibited characteristic deformation cycles that likely generate propulsive forces in viscoelastic matrices, in line with our earlier observations (Fig. 4f). These processes also resemble how some immune cells migrate in dense ECM with perinuclear cytoskeleton-mediated amoeboid migration or squeezing through confined spaces18,32,34.

These results illustrate that mEbot supports both fast flagellar swimming in aqueous media and can switch to large-amplitude, periodic cell deformation and crawling in viscous or confined environments. These capabilities make mEbot more advantageous than rigid synthetic or C. reinhardti-based microrobots, which typically show rapid swimming in low-viscosity fluids but tend to stall once confinement increases and pore sizes approach their body dimensions. By contrast, the metaboly-enabled deformation of E. gracilis provides a natural mechanism for generating thrust under confinement21, as we observed in a high viscosity environment and collagen gels. To summarize, we attempted to validate the deformability and magnetically guided performance of mEbot in a simulated ECM-like complex environment, specifically within collagen gels of varying concentrations. Our results indicate that in complex, fibrous collagen networks, pore size and porosity are key limiting factors determining whether mEbot can traverse a certain distance. Under the same duration, even with an applied magnetic attraction, mEbot struggled to fully penetrate high-concentration collagen gels characterized by low porosity within the tested time frame. Although imaging within the collagen demonstrated mEbot’s capacity for deformation and partial penetration, this ability appears to be limited and may face challenges in more intricate environments.

Targeted photodynamic therapy

Microalgae-based microrobots offer inherent advantages, particularly enabling in situ photosynthesis and chlorophyll-mediated therapies35,36. To harness these properties for medical purposes, we conducted an attempt at photodynamic therapy (PDT) targeting tumors, taking advantage of the E. gracilis as a larger natural reservoir of chlorophyll (Fig. 5a). To determine the effective irradiation wavelength, we first measured the absorbance spectrum of mEbot, and it displayed a pronounced band at 650–670 nm, indicating suitable excitation range for photodynamic activation (Fig. 5b-i). Then, we use CCK-8 assays to demonstrate that neither mEbots nor the MNPs inside the mEbots exhibited significant cytotoxicity toward cells, thereby supporting the biosafety of mEbots for subsequent therapeutic investigations (Fig. S6B). Then, to evaluate the mEbot-driven PDT, we first tested the effect of laser irradiation in the co-culture of E. gracilis and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and compared it to a group of laser-treated HUVEC cells in the absence of mEbots. We detected the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) using the DCFH-DA (2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) as a fluorogenic marker of ROS by fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry analysis. DCFH-DA diffuses into live cells and is oxidized in the presence of ROS, resulting in bright green fluorescence. We irradiated the mEbot-HUVEC co-culture group and the only cell and laser control groups with 660-nm laser. The oxidatively stressed cells exhibited significantly higher green fluorescence intensity, with 71% of cells showing positive signals, while control and laser-only groups displayed negligible fluorescence, with 1.4% and 5.5% cells showing positive signals, respectively (Fig. S6C, E). It’s worth mentioning that in photodynamic therapy, laser wavelengths commonly fall within the 660–680 nm range. And the magnetic nanoparticles typically do not generate ROS in this wavelength band. Nonetheless, we also tested whether MNPs induce ROS generation in cells and compared them to mEbot. As shown in Fig. S6C, particles alone induced negligible ROS generation under the same conditions, as the presence of mEbot led to detectable ROS generation. To verify the presence of particles, both bright-field and merged images are provided. After confirming the ROS generation by the mEbots upon laser irradiation and quantifying its killing effect in a two-dimensional culture environment, we further tested the mEbot-driven PDT with tumor spheroids in the presence and absence of magnetic guidance. In this experiment, we carried out the corresponding treatments for all groups on the 5th day of the tumor spheroid growth cycle (Fig. S7A). We fabricated poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) microchambers to confine CT-26 tumor spheroids in a larger reservoir and utilized magnetic guidance to steer mEbots from another reservoir towards the spheroids (Fig. 5a). In this regard, we first demonstrated the capability of precise control of the single mEbot to deposit on the surface of the tumor spheroid within a certain field of view (movie S4). Later, we performed a 30-minute magnetic attraction of mEbots towards the tumor spheroid, which resulted in the accumulation of a high number of mEbots around the spheroid surface compared to the simple co-culture of tumor spheroid and the mEbots. The bright-field microscopy images demonstrated the aggregation of the mEbots on the surface of the tumor spheroids due to the magnetic attraction (Fig. 5b-ii-iv). This phenomenon results from dipole-dipole interactions between cells bearing surface-attached magnetic nanoparticles. For penetration efficiency, cell aggregates can enhance collective migration through tissues via reduced drag and synchronized swimming trajectories, analogous to how dense bacterial suspensions exhibit improved motility through viscoelastic media compared to isolated swimmers37. In this situation for PDT efficacy, aggregation concentrates photosensitizers locally to boost ROS generation, though uneven light distribution within clusters could limit deeper light penetration.

Then, we irradiated the mEbots and performed both bright-field imaging and two-photon fluorescence microscopy to visualize the PDT effect in a greater depth (Fig. 5b-v, c, d). Live/Dead staining of the cells revealed that significant cytotoxicity was achieved in the magnetically guided mEbot-driven PDT group compared to control groups (Fig. 5c–e). Three-dimensional reconstructions and orthogonal views indicated that while mEbots exhibit some deformability, they predominantly remain embedded within the outer layers of the spheroid periphery, suggesting surface-focused killing (Fig. 5c, iv). Quantification of the confocal images further demonstrated significant cytotoxicity with a killing ratio of ~51% on the outer layer tumor cells (Figs. 5e and S7B–E). It is worth noting that the 660-nm laser also enhanced phototaxis after the magnetic attraction, driving more mEbots or E. gracilis to accumulate at the spheroid’s surface and thereby creating a locally enriched, oxygen-supportive microenvironment for downstream photodynamic reactions35,38,39.

Notably, in the long-term coculture group within the chamber, where neither magnetic nor laser treatment was applied, bare E. gracilis have increased surface contact spontaneously with the tumor spheroid and this still resulted in some degree of cell damage, leading to the death of nearly 20% of the outer-layer cells (Fig. 5e). These findings demonstrate that given enough time, spontaneous motility of E. gracilis allows them to traverse within the chamber and subsequently accumulate around the tumor spheroid.

Tumor-tropic behavior of E. gracilis and mEbots towards tumor spheroids

The chemotaxis of E. gracilis has been extensively studied previously, particularly concerning its responses to environmental cues such as carbon dioxide, pH, and nutrients40–42. However, no study has reported this phenomenon in a co-culture environment with tumor cells. During PDT studies with tumor spheroids, we noticed that both E. gracilis and mEbots exhibited directional motion towards the tumor spheroids even in the absence of any external guidance when the interaction was maintained over an extended period (movie S5). To gain further insights into mEbot-tumor spheroid interactions, we performed time-lapse imaging with multiple spheroids in a closed, no-flow environment to avoid any passive drift and advective trapping. Fourteen hours of visualization clearly demonstrated tumor-directed movement and progressive accumulation of microalgae around the tumor spheroids (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, heatmaps indicate the dynamic changes in microalgae density around the spheroids distinctly over this period. Further evaluations using fluorescent live imaging of mEbot-tumor spheroid co-cultures over an extended 18-hour period demonstrated sustained localization of the mEbots and persistent tumor-tropic behavior over time (Fig. 6c). The persistence of this phenomenon further confirms the observations (Fig. 6d). We also observed that a substantial number of E. gracilis not only migrated towards but also adhered to the surface of the tumor spheroids, with some even showing potential for surface penetration (Fig. 6e). Moreover, to test the active tropism of mEbot toward tumor spheroids, we have included fixed mEbot, bare MNP and light control groups, as shown in Movie S6 and Fig. S8. Firstly, the mEbot fixed with glutaraldehyde failed to exhibit the same chemotactic behavior under the same observation conditions. The control group that only contained magnetic particles also did not show any tendency towards the tumor; it merely demonstrated aggregation due to dipole-dipole interaction over time (Fig. S8B). This proves that the tumor chemotaxis of mEbot is largely due to the active sensing ability of mEbot itself rather than any passive enrichment or local flows. To rule out phototaxis, which is common in microalgae such as E. gracilis, we also evaluated the influence of illumination from the imaging system, specifically the exposure (around 10 ms) during image acquisition every 5 min. Based on the measured light intensity and the known light sensitivity threshold of E. gracilis reported in the literature24, we confirmed that the illumination during observation was insufficient to induce phototactic responses. At the same time, to present the results more directly, we conducted a control observation in a dark incubator (Fig. S8C). Additionally, to eliminate the influence of gravity, we used low-adhesion flat-bottomed plates for long-term observation, taking photos only at the beginning and end for verification. Immunomodulation

The majority of the medical microrobot designs have drug delivery as a central function; however, comparatively few studies exploit biological units that may also exhibit intrinsic therapeutic functions. E. gracilis possesses a rich repertoire of bioactive metabolites with documented medical, nutritional, and functional-food applications28,43–46. Studies have shown that components of E. gracilis, such as β-1,3-glucan (paramylon, Fig. S9A–C), activate macrophages by engaging with pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), dectin-1, and promote macrophage polarization and effector responses28,44,47. Given that E. gracilis, and its product paramylon have been shown to activate innate immunity and modulate macrophage function, we wanted to elucidate this potential together with PDT for reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages toward an anti-tumorigenic phenotype and enable drug-free therapeutic microrobotic operations44,46.

Firstly, we examined the interplay between mEbot and RAW264.7 macrophages in a co-culture system (Fig. 7a), where macrophages were observed to form clusters around mEbots (Fig. S9D). Bright-field imaging further revealed active phagocytic uptake and intracellular degradation of mEbot by the macrophages. Upon degradation, distinct paramylon granules, visible as rod-like or discoid structures consistent with the morphology observed by SEM, were observed within cells (Figs. 7b and S9D). We then investigated macrophage phenotypic changes after interacting with mEbots for 24 and 48 h using flow cytometry. We analyzed the surface expression level of CD80, and CD86 as markers of pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages and CD206 as a marker of anti-inflammatory (M2) macrophages, comparing these levels to macrophages stimulated with bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as a positive control for M1 polarization. Relative to controls, mEbot-treated macrophages showed a time-dependent increase in M1 polarization markers. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant increase in CD80 and CD86 expression in mEbot-treated cells compared to unstimulated cells. Specifically, CD80 expression increased by ca. 50% at 24 h and 80% at 48 h, close to the levels induced by the positive control, LPS, at the latter time point. However, at 24 h, CD86 expression remained substantially lower than that caused by LPS, indicating that mEbot stimulation did not elicit excessive activation of the cells, typical of LPS. In contrast, CD80 expression was significantly enhanced by mEbot treatment compared to unstimulated controls, surpassing the effect observed with LPS. At the same time, the enhancement of CD206 compared to LPS, was not significant, indicating that this treatment did not significantly stimulate the formation of the M2 phenotype (Fig. S10). These findings demonstrate that mEbot induces a moderate but robust activation of macrophages toward M1 phenotype without any exogenous payload, and they have the potential to modulate the immune response without triggering an overwhelming inflammatory state. To determine whether the observed effect is due to paramylon content and whether the MNP-chitosan network has any influence, we treated the macrophages with bare MNP-chitosan particles and isolated paramylon granules. The extracted granules were visualized by SEM (Fig. S9C). Then, we established paramylon gradient concentrations (10, 50, and 200 µg/mL) and compared their effects with those of magnetic nanoparticles, heat-killed mEbot, and LPS controls after 24 h to avoid the auto-polarization of macrophages in extended durations. We found that paramylon induced dose-dependent upregulation of CD86, with activation levels approaching those of live mEbot but remaining below LPS stimulation, suggesting moderate M1 polarization without excessive inflammatory response (Fig. 7d, e). In contrast, both mEbot and paramylon elicited stronger CD80 expression than LPS, revealing a distinct immunomodulatory pattern. Heat-killed mEbot retained immunostimulatory capacity comparable to live mEbot, confirming that motility is not essential for activation. Although macrophages readily phagocytosed chitosan-coated magnetic nanoparticles (Fig. 7c), these particles exerted negligible effects on polarization. The dose-dependent CD86 upregulation coupled with robust CD80 expression distinguishes paramylon from classical inflammatory stimuli such as LPS, which typically induces stronger CD86 but weaker CD80 responses48,49. Together, these findings position paramylon as the main immunomodulator that contributes to mEbot’s functional versatility while maintaining biocompatibility”.

Discussion

In this study, we developed magnetically guided E. gracilis-based multifunctional biohybrid microrobots with multimodal locomotion abilities for barrier penetration and execution of drug-free biomedical functions, including photodynamic therapy and immunomodulation for tumor treatment. We utilized a soft-ferromagnetic network structure as a relatively soft modification that does not hinder pellicle sliding and deformation. This strategy couples magnetically functionalized components with E. gracilis’s deformable mechanics and inherent therapeutic functions. Most existing microrobots lack deformability and environmental adaptivity at the single-cell level. In contrast, our mEbot demonstrates controllable navigation and steering in swimming mode and enhanced penetration into viscous and complex 3D environments in metaboly mode, with dynamic shape changes and deformation. With this, we provide long-range navigation with magnetic guidance in the swimming mode with local penetration into dense, complex, and heterogeneous tissue in the metaboly mode. Furthermore, to achieve more uniform modification, we employed a droplet microfluidic strategy, which improves the precision and uniformity of the magnetic modification process.

When designing biohybrid microrobots, each biological unit has its own intrinsic advantages and physical constraints. For instance, although it provides a concentrated reservoir of various natural products, E. gracilis is still substantially larger than C. reinhardtii and bacteria, which can restrict its access to more confined regions50. This highlights an inherent trade-off between the benefits of a larger, multifunctional organism and the need for deep tissue penetration. At comparable length scales, however, the deformation capabilities of E. gracilis are comparable to those of cell-based systems. As a future study, we envision mEbot for intraluminal delivery in bladder cancer therapy, where tumors often form superficial papillary lesions. Once mEbot is delivered through a catheter via standard cystoscopy, magnetic guidance enables targeted surface accumulation close to the papillary fronds. Then, deformation, and ECM invasion enable initial penetration through stromal barriers to access tumor regions. Then, laser-triggered ROS generation for killing the proliferative outer layers as the primary therapeutic target can be performed. In addition, this prototypical species also offers practical advantages such as low cost, easy availability, and handling. Thus, while its physical size inevitably limits certain applications, carefully matching E. gracilis-based microrobots to appropriate medical scenarios may allow us to further leverage its unique value.

In many synthetic and biohybrid microrobots, therapeutic efficacy is conceived with the assessment of exogenous drug loading, transporting, and releasing51. This leads to several issues, including compromising the viability of the biological component itself6; drug leakage and off-target distribution, and the requirement of complex triggering methods, which increases the difficulty of system construction51. In this context, our platform therapeutically differs from the conventional conception of microrobots as drug carriers. E. gracilis itself is pharmacologically active, carrying natural products with biomedical functions. In addition to chlorophyll, which serves as an endogenous photosensitizer for photodynamic therapy, E. gracilis contains the β-1,3-glucan paramylon, capable of modulating innate immune responses27,28,52. Without any exogenous drug loading, magnetically guided mEbots can accumulate at the surface of tumor spheroids in vitro, thereby concentrating chlorophyll and, upon laser irradiation, generating localized ROS that induce potent tumor cell killing. In parallel, macrophages that internalize mEbots acquire paramylon in situ and consequently upregulate M1-associated markers and polarize macrophages to an anti-tumorigenic phenotype. This “drug-free” dual therapy highlights a design paradigm in which the microrobot functions not merely as a delivery vehicle, but as a living therapeutic agent. Future designs need to carefully control the dosage and exposure time to maximize medical benefit without triggering extensive inflammatory responses. Additionally, occasional magnetic aggregation of mEbots was observed, resulting from dipole-dipole interactions between cells bearing surface-attached magnetic nanoparticles. For penetration efficiency, cell aggregates can enhance collective migration through tissues via reduced drag and synchronized swimming trajectories, analogous to how dense bacterial suspensions exhibit improved motility through viscoelastic media compared to isolated swimmers37. For PDT efficacy, aggregation concentrates photosensitizers locally to boost ROS generation, though uneven light distribution within clusters could limit deeper light penetration. Immune responses could benefit from slower clearance of larger cell clusters versus individual cells by altering macrophage uptake kinetics, though excessive aggregation risks inflammatory signaling. Despite the promising potential of this approach, future in vivo studies are essential to determine how E. gracilis interacts with tumors in a more complex biological environment. Moreover, the long-term safety, biodistribution, and clearance of mEbot require further investigation in vivo.

Another key aspect of the mEbot platform is its tumor tropism. This work provides, to the best of our knowledge, an initial observation of E. gracilis exhibiting directed migration toward tumor spheroids under our experimental conditions. Microalgal taxis have primarily been leveraged for phototactic guidance or environmental sensing, whereas no study has reported a taxis behavior in the context of tumor targeting. Our results indicate that when placed in proximity, E. gracilis-based microrobots can spontaneously accumulate around tumor spheroids over extended periods and actively reshape the spheroid periphery. While the precise cues driving this behavior remain to be identified, potential candidates include gradients of CO₂, pH, or tumor-derived metabolites41,53. Based on previous quantitative studies utilizing microfluidic platforms, E. gracilis exhibits distinct positive chemotaxis toward low concentrations of carbon dioxide (<15% partial pressure), with chemotactic distribution patterns that strikingly resemble the tumor-tropic behavior observed in our mEbot41. The microfluidic isolated micro-aquarium approach employed in a previously reported paper enabled precise quantification of CO₂-induced chemotaxis down to 64 ppm, revealing that metabolic CO₂ gradients serve as a potent chemoattractant for Euglena. Given that tumor spheroids actively generate localized CO₂ gradients through aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, a hallmark of the Warburg effect54, we hypothesize that CO₂-mediated chemotaxis constitutes a primary mechanism underlying mEbot’s tumor-targeting capability. Meanwhile, given that previous studies have not demonstrated that pH has a chemotactic effect on E. gracilis, and considering that it is a photosynthetic organism, the chemotaxis towards the photosynthetic substrate carbon dioxide is more convincing. In such a case, this behavior could be specific to tumor spheroids rather than any generic 3D cell aggregates, which typically lack the hypermetabolic CO₂ production profile, as they rely on oxidative phosphorylation without significant lactate/CO₂ accumulation. Therefore, non-tumor 3D aggregates would not produce such a concentrated, directional CO₂ gradient, making them unlikely to trigger the same chemotactic response.

Collectively, these findings revealed sustained enrichment of mEbots around tumor spheroids, demonstrating tumor-tropic behavior and highlighting their potential for enhanced precision and control through the combined use of magnetic guidance and tropism in therapeutic applications.

From an application standpoint, these inherent taxis toward tumor-like microenvironments are appealing because they can complement external control. Magnetic guidance is well-suited for long-range steering, but microscale positioning is difficult to achieve by magnetic guidance alone once microrobots enter a complex environment26. Therefore, a hybrid strategy combining phototactic or magnetic guidance with chemotactic positioning could improve the adaptability and effectiveness of practical applications in the future.

Materials and methods

Euglena gracilis culture

E. gracilis and culture medium was obtained from the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Science (Freshwater Algae Culture Collection, FACHB, Wuhan, China) and was maintained in 500-mL Erlenmeyer flasks and subcultured every 7 days. At each passage, half of the culture volume was replaced with fresh AF-6 medium. Unless otherwise indicated, downstream experiments used actively growing cultures collected on days 3–5 of the growth cycle, with an optical density (OD) of ~0.15 at a wavelength of 680 nm, measured with a plate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cultures were kept at 25 °C without shaking in a light incubator operating on a 10-h light/14-h dark cycle illuminated with light-emitting diodes (2000 lx). When harvesting or washing the microalgae, the centrifugation speed was kept 400 g for 5 min to protect the flagella. The AF-6 medium was prepared as follows. First, a trace element stock solution was prepared containing 0.75 g Na₂EDTA, 0.041 g MnCl₂·4H₂O, 0.005 g ZnCl₂·7H₂O, 0.004 g Na₂MoO₄·2H₂O, 0.097 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, and 0.002 g CoCl₂·6H₂O per liter and stored at 4 °C after preparation. The base medium was then formulated by sequentially adding 0.14 g NaNO₃, 0.22 g NH₄NO₃, 0.005 g K₂HPO₄, 0.01 g KH₂PO₄, 0.01 g CaCO₃, 0.002 g citric acid, 0.002 g ferric citrate, 0.03 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, and 0.01 g CaCl₂·2H₂O into 1 L of ultrapure water. Subsequently, 1 mL of biotin stock solution (2 μg/100 mL), 0.5 mL of thiamine HCl stock solution (1 μg/100 mL), 1 μg of vitamin B₆, and 1 μg of vitamin B₁₂ were added, followed by 5 mL of the trace element stock solution. After complete dissolution of all components, the volume was adjusted to 1 L and mixed thoroughly. The pH was adjusted to 7.0, and the medium was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, aliquoted, and stored at 4 °C until use.

Fabrication of magnetically controlled mEbots

Amine-functionalized magnetic nanoparticles (monodispersed magnetite nanospheres; 200–300 nm; 5 mg/mL stock; M814128) were prepared in network form by adding 100 μL of the particle suspension to 900 μL chitosan solution (0.3 μg/mL) and incubating for 1 h to promote formation of networks suitable for E. gracilis binding. Particles were collected by magnetic separation to remove excess chitosan and resuspended in PBS. The size and zeta potentials of the resulting chitosan coated magnetic nanoparticles were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS90). Following the chitosan modification, surfaces of nanoparticle networking were biotinylated. Briefly, NHS–PEG4–biotin was added to a final concentration of 10 mM, and the suspension was gently mixed by inversion for 4 h, with intermittent sonication to prevent settling. After reaction, particles were magnetically separated and resuspended in 1 mL deionized (DI) water.

To conjugate the modified magnetic nanoparticles with the microalgae, the streptavidin-conjugated microalgae was prepared. E. gracilis cells (1 mL at 106 cells/mL) were first incubated with 10 mM Sulfo-NHS-Biotin (1 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, A39256) for 1 h at room temperature. The biotinylated cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in 500 μL DI water, followed by dropwise addition of 0.5 mg/ml streptavidin-Cy3 (APExBIO, K1079) and incubation for 1 h at 25 °C to allow streptavidin conjugation to the cell surface.

Droplet encapsulator

We employed a droplet microfluidic system (DPBIO, OMNI 4500) to generate uniform mEbots. Specifically, the biotin-MNPs phase (5 mg/mL) and the streptavidin-modified E. gracilis phase (106 cells/mL) were loaded at 300 µL, while the oil phase volume was 900 µL. Experiments were performed on a custom-designed droplet generation chip suitable for OMNI equipment (DPBIO, CRPY6A1). To promote single encapsulation, the three inlet pressures were set to 4 (water phase) and 10 psi (oil phase), respectively. Droplets were produced at ~104 droplets per min, yielding predominantly single-cell emulsions. The droplets were incubated for 30 min to enable biotin–streptavidin coupling. To release the microalgal cells, the microdroplets were demulsified by adding 100 μL 1H,1H,2H,2H-Perfluoro-1-octanol (370533, Sigma-Aldrich) and the resulting mEbots were dispersed in AF-6 medium.

Characterization of mEbots

The autofluorescence signal of E. gracilis emitted at red and far-red spectra was utilized for fluorescence imaging. Absorption fluorescence spectra were first acquired on a multimode microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Unless otherwise noted, measurements were performed at room temperature in black, clear-bottom 96-well plates using 200 μL per well; culture medium served as the blank for background subtraction. Following the detection of an autofluorescence signal coming from E. gracilis, biotinylation and subsequent streptavidin binding to the surface of the cells were characterized by fluorescence microscopy to quantify binding efficiency. Images were acquired using an inverted Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope system. Adhesion of the particles to E. gracilis was further confirmed by using chitosan conjugated to FITC or Cy3, and the 3D z-stacks were acquired using a spinning disk high-speed confocal imaging system (Dragonfly 202, OXFORD).

The chitosan-particle network on the surface pellicle of the microalgae was further characterized using SEM imaging. The cells were fixed with a solution of 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in DI water for 30 min at 4 °C. After fixation, the cells were rinsed with distilled water and dehydrated through increasing concentrations of ethanol (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%) before being chemically dried using a series of hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) solutions in ethanol at varying HMDS: ethanol (v/v) ratios (1:1, 2:1, and 1:0). The samples were then left to dry overnight at room temperature. The samples were then coated with a thin layer of gold using an ion sputter coater (Hitachi, Ion Sputter MC1000). And the SEM images were taken using a Hitachi SEM (SU3900) at an accelerating voltage between 2–5 keV. The TEM sample preparation method involves cutting a 0.5 mm thin slice, and then mechanically grinding it to 50–70 nm, and taking images using a JEM-2100 Plus (JEOL) transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

For ICP-MS elemental quantification, mEbot samples from a single batch were first counted to determine the average cell/ml. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of aqua regia (3:1 HCl:HNO₃) was used to digest the samples, followed by dilution to a final volume of 10 mL with 2% HNO₃. Elemental analysis was performed using an Agilent 7800 ICP-MS under the following optimized parameters: RF power (1.50 kW), plasma flow (12.0 L/min), auxiliary flow (1.00 L/min), nebulizer flow (0.70 L/min), sample uptake delay (60 s), and instrument stabilization delay (60 s). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate (replicates = 3) with a 15-s replicate read time. Calibration was validated using a five-point standard curve (0–1000 μg/L Fe), yielding a linear regression equation y = 0.0114x + 0.0063 (R² = 1.0000), with a detection limit (DL) of 0.04872 μg/L and a blank equivalent concentration (BEC) of 0.5554 μg/L.

Motion of mEbots under magnetic field

Microchannels for motion test were prepared by cutting poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) and double-sided adhesive films (3 M) to the appropriate shape with a CO2 laser cutter (HM-1310) and assembling the components onto a cover glass. The movement characteristics of free E. gracilis and mEbots inside the microchannels were recorded using an inverted optical microscope (CKX53, Olympus) equipped with a ×10 objective lens, and the videos were analyzed using a custom MATLAB code and ImageJ (Fiji) software.

Magnetic control was conducted using a custom-made, 2D magnetic guidance setup consisting of two permanent magnets separated by around 37.5 mm, generating an approximate magnetic field (15 mT) along one axis inside the small confocal dish (25 mm confocal dish with a 10 mm glass bottom area) to direct the mEbots’ swimming along the same axis. We utilized the Hall magnetic field camera (HallinSightCo.) to capture the field, which can map dynamic magnetic fields in real-time. 2D swimming speed analyses of mEbots were performed by tracking microalgal cells using the open-source physics (OSP) tracker software, and the trajectories were analyzed using custom MATLAB code.

Deformation characterization

To investigate microalgal propulsion, Newtonian fluids of varying viscosities were created by mixing DI water with Ficoll (YEASEN, 40502ES25) at different concentrations with shear viscosities of approximately 5, 12, and 70 mPa.s, achieved through 3%, 7%, and 13% (w/v) concentrations of the mixture. For deformation assays, an aqueous two-phase system (ATPS) was prepared from Ficoll and polyethylene glycol (PEG 8000, Macklin, P815615) to produce a densely packed soft droplet microenvironment. The ATPS was gently mixed to avoid coalescence. To restrict out-of-plane motion, the sample was confined by sandwiching a small aliquot between two glass coverslips. For the trans-well penetration assay (24-mm trans-well insert, 8.0-µm pores; Corning), 500 µL of culture medium was added to the receiver well (lower chamber), and 100 µL of the mEbot suspension was loaded into the corresponding trans-well insert (upper chamber).

Collagen gel penetration and rheology

Collagen gels were prepared with collagen stock solution (Cultrex 3-D Culture Matrix Rat Collagen I, R&D SYSTEMS, 3447-020-01), sterile ddH2O, PBS (10×), and 1 M NaOH to adjust pH for polymerization. Final gel concentration was 1, 1.5 and 2 mg/ml with a pH of 7.5. Collagen solutions were prepared and pipetted on a 35 mm confocal dishes with a volume of 200 μl and then incubated in a temperature-controlled incubator at room temperature for 60-min to induce polymerization. After polymerization, gels were thoroughly washed with fresh PBS (pH 7.4) several times to remove unreacted species. Next, mEbot (10 μL, in PBS) were pipetted on top of the gel. Detail penetration, deformation and quantification of mEbot within the different collagen gels were done first by confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, LSM980) equipped with a 60×, oil objective to check the metaboly. A laser with 488 nm was used to illuminate the sample, and the reflected light was detected with a photomultiplier tube (PMT). Then the 3D penetration was checked by a two-photon microscopy imaging system (FVMPE-RS, Olympus) and second harmonic generation (SHG). For the different groups, the setup was incubated for 2 h with the permanent magnet setup aligning the mEbot toward the collagen gel’s bottom. A minimum of three different images was used for the 3D quantification of the 1.5 mg/ml collagen gel invasion (n = 3). To calculate collagen porosity, a total of 100 SHG frames of Z-stacks were captured and merged into 5 single images by ImageJ software, and calculated based on the previous reported method32.

Fluid viscosity was measured on a rotational rheometer (ARES G2 TA Instruments) under steady-shear conditions. Measurements were performed at 37 °C with standard 25 mm cone–plate geometry following instrument guidelines. Briefly, 0.5 ml of collagen solution was applied to the measuring stage set at 4 °C. After descending the top geometry and placing the evaporation trap, the temperature was ramped up to 37 °C for 30 min. After polymerization, a frequency sweep (0.2–2 hz) at a low amplitude of 1% was performed, and the stress-strain relationship was measured (0–100% strain) with a constant strain rate of 1%/s.

In vitro photodynamic therapy

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells, HUVECs (Procell in Wuhan, China), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Before the experiments, all cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and grown until they reached 80% confluency. Cells between passages 4 and 7 were used for the subsequent experiments. Cells were seeded on a 35-mm glass-bottom dish in 2 mL medium and cultured for 24 h until they reached 80% confluence. Then the cells were divided into three groups: (i) Control; (ii) Laser; (iii) mEbots + Laser. The cells in group (iii) were treated with 100 μL medium containing 5 × 105 mEbots, while the cells in the other group were treated with only 100 μL medium. After incubation for 12 h together, a 660 nm laser (0.5 W cm−2, 10 min) was applied. Then the production of intracellular ROS was detected by DCFH-DA. Laser-treated cells were stained with a DCFH-DA assay kit (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) and imaged under an inverted confocal microscope system (Zeiss LSM980). The evaluation of generated ROS was further quantified by a flow cytometer (BD LSRFortessa). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was obtained by using the derived function on FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC). To test whether MNPs induce ROS generation, under identical conditions, 100 μL of medium containing 5 × 105 mEbots, corresponding to a 100-fold excess of MNPs at a concentration of approximately 12 μg/mL was added to cells, followed by the laser treatment. For Live/dead cells staining, after being seeded in 6-well plates (glass bottom) at a density of 1 × 107 cells per well and treated with or without 100 μg/mL mEbots, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (0.5 W cm−2, 10 min). Then, the cells were stained using a Live/Dead cell staining kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using the same inverted Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope system as mentioned previously.

Preparation for tumor spheroids

Colon Tumor 26, CT-26 (Procell, Wuhan, China) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin and streptomycin, in a humidified, 37 °C, and 5% CO2 environment. Cells were used at passage numbers lower than 10, and surface detachment during splitting was performed using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) when they reached 80% confluence. Cells between passages 4 and 7 were used for the subsequent experiments. To prepare tumor spheroids, CT-26 cells were seeded into 96-well ultralow attachment spheroid microplates (Beyotime, FULA967) at a density of 2000 cells per well and incubated in a humidified, 37 °C, and 5% CO2 environment.

Interaction between mEbot with tumor spheroids

The interactions of mEbot and CT-26 tumor spheroids were assayed in PMMA microchambers fabricated as described above. Each chamber was affixed to a glass coverslip using a fast-curing adhesive to enable direct high-resolution imaging. Then the cells were divided into five groups: (i) Control; (ii) Laser; (iii) mEbots; (iv) mEbots + Laser. For group (iv), a permanent magnet was positioned externally to make the mEbot accumulate toward the spheroid with a homogeneous magnetic field (15 mT) along one axis. The group (iii) received the mEbot added at random locations within the chamber without magnetic guidance and were not irradiated. After 30 min, the group (ii) and (iv) received irradiation with a 660-nm laser (~0.5 W cm−2, 10 min). Then the mEbot–spheroid contact in separate groups was assessed. All tumor spheroids were then subjected to a Live/Dead viability staining (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged on a two-photon microscopy imaging system (FVMPE-RS). Z-stack images were constructed by combining sliced tumor sphere images taken at different focal planes with a step size of 2 µm, and the total range of ~200 µm depth.

To examine direct interactions between mEbot and tumor spheroids in the absence of additional cues, tumor spheroids and mEbot were co-cultured in low-attachment, flat-bottom 96-well plates (Beyotime). Time-lapse imaging was performed on a live-cell Imaging system (Celldiscoverer 7, Zeiss), acquiring one frame every 5 min for 15 h. Images were subsequently compiled into time-lapse sequences for qualitative assessment of mEbot–spheroid contact dynamics. The density maps of mEbot toward spheroids were analyzed using custom MATLAB code.

Immunomodulation

To extract paramylon granules, E. gracilis was treated in an ultrasonic machine for 1 h, then centrifuged to wash and freeze-dried. During extraction, the algal powder was washed with acetone after weighing and centrifuged at 2500 rcf for 5 min to remove lipophilic compounds. After removing the supernatant, 1% SDS was added to the precipitate, and then the centrifuge tube was placed in an 80 °C temperature for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 2500 rcf for 5 min to obtain the crude paramylon extract. Next, residual proteins were removed using ultrapure water and a 1 mg/L urea solution and then dried and weighed. The heat-killed mEbot is produced through normal procedures and then undergoes a 15-minute heat treatment at 80 degrees Celsius to complete the process. We estimated the amount of paramylon to be used by considering the ratio of mEbot to cells (1:200) and tested three different concentrations. The yield of paramylon in our study was 0.35 mg/mL, accounting for 59% of the dry weight of the algal culture. RAW 264.7 cells (ATCC: Catalog # TIB-71), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). For M1 polarization, macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, L5293, Merck) for 24 and 48 h. For co-culture treatments, mEbots were added to the cell culture supernatant at a ratio of 200:1 (macrophage: mEbot) and maintained for 24 and 48 h. For flow cytometry, at least 1 × 106 cells per condition were collected, washed twice with PBS (300 × g, 5 min), and resuspended in 100 µL PBS. Cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD86, APC-anti-CD80, and PE-anti-CD206 antibodies (105011, 104705, 141721, Rat Anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies, Biolegend) according to the manufacturer’s recommended concentrations for 20 min at 4 °C in the dark. Samples were then washed twice with 1 mL PBS, fixed in 1% methanol-free formaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was obtained by using the FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC).

Statistical analysis

MATLAB 2024b and GraphPad Prism 10.0.2 were used to perform statistical analyses. Data was presented as means ± SD of the mean, unless otherwise mentioned in the figure captions. For paired comparisons between two conditions, differences were analyzed using a two-tailed paired Student’s t test. The significance between more than two groups was analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test. Organisms were cultured under consistent environmental conditions, and randomization was used in group assignments. Sample numbers (n) and experimental repeats are indicated in figure legends.
