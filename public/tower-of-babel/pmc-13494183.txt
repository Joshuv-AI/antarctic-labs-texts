Structural context of NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase mutations that alter cytochrome P450 1A2 substrate regioselectivity
Authors: Sarah D. Burris-Hiday, Francisco Esteves, Michel Kranendonk, Emily E. Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
NADPH-cytochrome P450 (P450) reductase serves as the obligate redox partner for the majority of human P450 enzymes, those responsible for both drug metabolism and homeostasis. NADPH-derived electrons are accepted by the reductase FAD-containing domain and then transferred to its flavin mononucleotide–containing domain (FMND). This domain directly binds P450 enzymes, providing electrons essential for P450 catalysis. Reductase mutations, and specifically those of its FMND, have been widely studied and variously affect P450 function. Most intriguing, previous studies identified 5 specific FMND single amino acid mutations that alter the regioselectivity of CYP1A2 caffeine metabolism. The studies herein further show that these same mutations also alter CYP1A2 methoxyresorufin O-demethylation, mostly in the same direction and scale. To provide experimental information about these FMND mutants, their X-ray crystal structures were determined (1.1–1.5 Å resolution). Although only subtle changes in FMND structure were observed, they were not confined to the local vicinity of the mutation. Changes were instead primarily in the loops binding the flavin cofactor, shifting the backbone of these loops farther from or closer to the flavin cofactor. The extent of loop shifting varies depending on the mutation but indicates that only subtle changes in these loops are required to affect the P450 interaction enough to transmit structural changes to the P450 active site.Significance StatementExperimental structural information is lacking for the interaction between human cytochrome P450 (P450) enzymes and their reductase, but some reductase mutations alter P450 catalytic regioselectivity, suggesting that the protein/protein interaction alters substrate orientation in the distant P450 active site. The current work establishes that such mutations yield only small changes in the structure of the reductase flavin mononucleotide–containing domain, suggesting that small changes in the interaction nonetheless significantly influence P450 active site catalysis.

Introduction

NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR), encoded by the POR gene, reduces a wide variety of enzymes, including 48 human P450 enzymes. The direct reductase/P450 interaction transfers electrons to support the metabolism of ∼80% of therapeutics1 and the production of many endogenous molecules critical for human health. This system is complex in its interactions and dynamics. Initially, NADPH binds to the reductase FAD-containing domain, which accepts 2 electrons in the form of a hydride, and then passes one electron at a time to the reductase flavin mononucleotide–containing domain (FMND). This FMND then binds P450 to transfer electrons, reducing the P450 heme, enabling activation of molecular oxygen to oxidize substrates.2 Large-scale conformational dynamics are involved. The same FMND surface must alternately interact with the reductase FAD domain to accept electrons and with the proximal surface of P450 enzymes to deliver them. Structures for the closed conformation reveal the 2 adjacent flavin-containing domains, oriented for effective interdomain electron transfer.3, 4, 5 Conformational changes in the hinge region connecting the 2 flavin-containing domains are thought to control the movement of the domains, and indeed hinge modifications revealed structures of open conformations where the flavins are as far as 60 Å apart,6 with the FMND available for P450 interaction. Open conformations are additionally supported by NMR and X-ray scattering.7 Alternations between the closed and open forms must occur twice to pass 2 electrons to P450 heme for each substrate oxidation,2 and restricting such dynamics decreases P450 function.4

The interaction between the reductase FMND and mammalian P450 enzymes is much less understood structurally and functionally. There are no experimental structures of this interaction, but complementary electrostatics6 and cross-linking studies8 strongly support FMND binding on the proximal face of P450 enzymes—the surface corresponding to the Cys-ligated heme face. However, the single reductase FMND must bind and deliver electrons to 48 different human P450 enzymes, whose proximal surface is not highly conserved. Functionally, the importance of this interface is underscored by clinical reports of reductase mutations that differentially alter P450 function leading to disease.9 One of the most well documented is Antley-Bixler syndrome or POR deficiency, a rare genetic disorder characterized by skeletal and developmental malformations.10,11 Patients have abnormal steroid profiles,12 but although the responsible steroidogenic P450s had decreased activity, no mutations were observed in the P450 enzymes.13, 14, 15, 16 Instead, mutations were observed in the reductase.13, 14, 15, 16 Unexpectedly, although these mutations decreased the activity of these steroidogenic P450 enzymes, they had varied effects on other P450 enzymes.9 For example, patient mutation Q153R in the FMND decreased CYP17A1 activity by as much as 70%,15 and it increased activity for CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP1A2, and CYP2C19.9,17, 18, 19 In vitro studies have further underscored that interactions with the FMND can differentially affect the catalysis of different P450 enzymes.20,21 Recent reports indicated that the FMND differentially affects ligand binding and substrate metabolism, either increasing, decreasing, or maintaining activity, depending on both the P450 isoform and the ligand bound in the active site.20,22

Thus, increasing evidence suggests that the reductase FMND not only delivers electrons but also allosterically modulates P450 function. Although effects on catalysis could potentially be executed by controlling either altering electron delivery or allosteric alterations of the P450, substrate binding does not require reduction and supports the latter. Hydrogen deuterium exchange experiments23,24 and irreversible labeling techniques24 reveal that P450s are modified throughout their structure in the presence of the reductase FMND. Recently, FMND mutations were identified that influence the regioselectivity of substrate metabolism.25 This effect is likely due to allosteric modulation because it is the conformation of the P450 active site that controls substrate orientation and thus product formation. CYP1A2 normally metabolizes caffeine by N-demethylation at C3, C1, and C7 to produce paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline, respectively, in a ratio of 84:12:4.26 However, some single-site mutations of the reductase FMND resulted in CYP1A2 production of substantially altered metabolite profiles. Thus, either substrate orientation in the P450 active site modulates FMND binding at the CYP1A2 proximal surface 15–20 Å away,25 or FMND binding modulates substrate orientation, but the structural basis for this is unknown.

Herein, 5 FMND mutations altering CYP1A2 regioselectivity were examined functionally and structurally. Functional effects were assessed using methoxyresorufin (MthR) O-demethylation (MROD), a single, well-defined human CYP1A2-catalyzed reaction that provides a sensitive readout of catalytic activity. Although our previous study demonstrated that POR FMND mutations alter the regioselectivity of CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism, the present work extends those findings by evaluating their effects on MROD activity. Unlike caffeine metabolism, which involves multiple competing pathways, MROD allows clearer distinction between effects on electron transfer efficiency and allosteric modulation of substrate orientation. To elucidate the structural basis of these functional changes, X-ray crystallography was used. High-resolution structures reveal how single-site mutations perturb FMND conformation, loop flexibility, and surface features, which can influence both electron delivery and long-range allosteric communication to CYP1A2. Comparing mutant and wild-type FMND structures enables direct correlation of conformational changes with altered substrate positioning and product formation, linking the molecular structure to the functional outcome.

Materials and methods

CYP1A2 activity with mutations in the reductase FMND

Reductase-CYP1A2 membrane fractions were generated using the Escherichia coli cell strain BTC for heterologous coexpression of human CYP1A2 together with cDNA of human POR (expressing either full-length wild-type, mutant, or no CPR), using a biplasmid coexpression system, as previously reported.25,27 Membrane fractions were isolated and characterized for total protein (Bradford assay), cytochrome P450 content (CO difference spectrophotometry), and CPR (immune-detection) contents. The catalytic CYP1A2 activity (MROD), sustained by CPR-FMND mutants, was evaluated as previously described,20,21,25,28 using MthR as a substrate (0–2 μM), combined with 8 nM CYP1A2. Reactions were carried out with constant final solvent concentrations (acetonitrile: 0.1% [v/v]). Real-time fluorescence measurements were performed in 96-well format for 15 minutes at 37 °C (excitation 530 nm; emission 580 nm), using a SpectraMax i3× microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Initial reaction velocities were measured in triplicate, and the resulting plots of velocity versus substrate concentration were fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation (r2 ≥ 0.95) to determine steady-state kinetic parameters, using GraphPad Prism version 5.01 (GraphPad Software). Because the ratios of CYP1A2 to reductase vary among the membrane preparations for the different FMND mutants, different reductase:CYP1A2 ratios were used to normalize activities.

FMND expression

A pET15b-based expression plasmid encoding the wild-type human reductase FMND (residues 6–241) plus a histidine tag has been previously described.29 Nucleotide modifications coding for the single-point mutations D211N, T142A, V164M, A229T, and H183Y were designed and commercially generated (Genscript). From the resulting sequence-verified plasmids, each mutated FMND protein was expressed similarly to what has been previously described.29 This plasmid was transformed into chemically competent C41(DE3) cells and selected in all cultures using carbenicillin. Single colonies were grown overnight at 37 °C on lysogeny broth (LB) plates and were subsequently inoculated into 5 mL of liquid LB and grown at 37 °C with 250 rpm shaking overnight. Next, 200 μL of the overnight cultures were inoculated into 200 mL of LB in 500-mL flasks, and these were grown overnight at 37 °C with 250 rpm shaking. Subsequently, 1 liter of terrific broth in a 2.8-liter Fernbach flask was inoculated with 10 mL of overnight culture and grown at 37 °C with 250 rpm shaking until the absorbance at 600 nm reached ∼0.8 (ranging between 0.6 and 0.9). Expression was then induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside, and 1 mM riboflavin was added to facilitate flavin cofactor production. At this time, temperature and shaking were reduced to 25 °C and 180 rpm, respectively. Proteins were expressed overnight, and harvested cell pellets were either frozen at –80 °C and stored or immediately purified.

FMND purification

Both wild-type and mutant FMND proteins were purified from cell pellets as previously described,29 with some additional purification steps to ensure formation of the holoprotein. Pellets were resuspended in 20 mm Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, pH 7.9, supplemented with Sigmafast protease inhibitors (MilliporeSigma), 0.3 mg/mL lysozyme, and 0.3 mg/mL DNAase using Dounce homogenizers with pestles A and B. Cells were then lysed via sonication on ice with 30 seconds bursts alternating with 30 seconds recovery for a total of 3 minutes. The lysate was then centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 minutes at 104,000g. The supernatant containing the FMND was loaded onto a pre-equilibrated 30-mL nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid column. The column was then washed with 60 mL of resuspension buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, and pH 7.9), followed by 150 mL of wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 25 mM imidazole, and pH 7.9), and then a linear gradient to a second wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 45 mM imidazole, pH 7.9). The FMND was finally eluted with 20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 200 mM imidazole, pH 7.9, and 5 μM FMN. The eluted protein was pooled and concentrated to ∼1.5 mL in a 10 kDa Amicon centrifuge filter (MilliporeSigma). To promote flavin incorporation in the protein, samples were then incubated with 4 mM FMN for 20 minutes or longer at 4 °C, and excess flavin was removed using a 5-mL desalting column in 20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.3 M NaCl, 0.02 mM EDTA, pH 7.9. Flavin incorporation was monitored spectroscopically, and while flavin incubation promoted the incorporation of flavin into the protein, ion exchange chromatography further aided in separating out the apoprotein. To accomplish this, the FMND protein was diluted 5-fold with a low salt buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9) and loaded onto two or three 5-mL diethylaminoethyl (Cytiva) columns connected in series. After a wash with 10 mL of low salt buffer, elution occurred over a 150 mL gradient to 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 0.3 M NaCl. The reductase FMND eluted late in the gradient, and corresponding fractions were collected, concentrated, and run isocratically on a 120-mL Superdex 200 (Cytiva) size exclusion column using the same buffer. Each FMND mutant protein in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.9, 0.3 M NaCl was concentrated to 15 mg/mL.

FMND crystallization

Crystallization screening was informed by previously published conditions,30 but those conditions were optimized to generate crystals with thin plate morphology. Larger crystals, while visually homogeneous, yielded anisotropic diffraction. Crystals were grown by mixing 1 μL of concentrated FMND protein with 1 μL of 0.1 M HEPES, pH 7.0, containing the concentrations of calcium chloride, polyethylene glycol 400, and glycerol specified in Table 1. These sitting drops were then equilibrated against 200 μL of the same solution at 22 °C. Crystals formed within 24 hours and were harvested without additional cryoprotectant and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected from single crystals at Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource Beamline 12-2 and processed using XDS31 (Table 2). Structures were solved by molecular replacement using Phaser32 and the previously published wild-type structure (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 1B1C)33 as the search model to solve the structure of the FMND mutant D211N. The refined D211N structure was subsequently used as the search model for molecular replacement for the remaining mutants (T142A, V164M, A229T, and H183Y). Model building and refinement were performed using Coot34 and Phenix.35 The structures ranged between 1.1 and 1.5 Å resolution with 1 molecule in the asymmetric unit. Overall data collection and refinement information for all structures are provided in Table 2. Structures were aligned using secondary-structure matching superpose36 as implemented in Coot34 to the wild-type structure in the PDB as 1B1C. Results

CYP1A2 methoxyresorufin O-demethylase activity supported by reductase mutants

Five mutations in the FMND of human NADPH P450 reductase were examined further because they cause P450 isoform-specific effects on xenobiotic metabolism and alter the regiospecificity of caffeine N-demethylation by CYP1A2.25 The H183Y and A229T mutations were identified by random mutagenesis, whereas T142A, V164M, and D211N are naturally occurring variants. To facilitate comparison, the same reductase preparations were used herein to evaluate their effects on CYP1A2-mediated MthR metabolism (MROD assay—widely used as the canonical probe substrate for human CYP1A2 activity). Although caffeine is a fused bicyclic molecule with 3 potential sites of N-demethylation, the resorufin core is a tricyclic molecule with 1 site for O-demethylation. However, both are highly planar, consistent with the size and shape of the CYP1A2 active site.38 The MROD activity assay reveals that although A229T has an activity profile very similar to the wild-type protein, all of the other mutants affected overall activity (Fig. 1). Structures of mutations of the reductase FMND

Structures of all 5 human reductase FMND mutations were determined by X-ray crystallography. First, unsurprisingly, all adopted the same overall fold as the wild-type enzyme—an alternating α−β−α architecture arranged into a central 5-stranded parallel β sheet flanked by α helices characteristic of flavodoxin/Rossmann-type folds (Fig. 3A). Second, at the C-terminal end of the β strands and flanked by 2 loops composed of residues called the “140s loop” and the “180s loop” for the residue numbers constituting them (Fig. 3A), each mutant structure showed clear evidence of complete occupancy of the planar FMN prosthetic group (eg, Fig. 3B). The isoalloxazine dimethylbenzene ring is exposed at the protein surface and forms part of the surface that would bind to the proximal face of a P450. There are no overall differences in the interactions between the FMN prosthetic group and the protein compared with those observed previously. Finally, determined at resolutions between 1.1 and 1.5 Å, each mutant structure had clear electron density validating the desired mutation (Supplemental Fig. 1). Such high resolutions, along with the isomorphous nature of the crystals, permit confident and detailed analysis of the structural effects of the mutations by comparison with a previously determined 1.93 Å wild-type structure.33 Of the 5 residues altering CYP1A2 regioselectivity, V164 and A229 are too far away to be part of the P450-binding surface, and T142 is buried. The wild-type residue D211 is located between the C-terminal end of the β 5 strand and α7, with its side chain directed toward the protein surface and bordering 1 side of the FMN (Fig. 3A). The closest approaches are 2.8 and 3.4 Å hydrogen bonds between 1 of the Asp side chain oxygens and 2 of the ribitol hydroxyls (Fig. 4A). Overlaying the wild-type and D211N structures reveals essentially no shift of the position of this Cα or the adjacent ones. However, even though Asp and Asn are the same size and shape, the mutated Asn side chain atoms move away from the FMN and toward the solvent relative to the wild-type structure. Although it is difficult to surmise whether the Asn side chain nitrogen or oxygen is oriented toward the FMN, the distances to the same ribitol hydroxyls are too far to hydrogen bond in the mutant. Although there are slight differences in the positions of FMN ribitol atoms for the 2 structures, the variation may not be significant given the lower resolution of the wild-type structure. Thus, the largest change may be the disruption of the negative charge by the substitution of a neutral surface side chain in the middle of the 3 sequential anionic Asp residues in this loop. One other aspect to compare with the wild-type structure is the position of the G144 carbonyl (Fig. 4B). In the wild-type structure, this carbonyl is oriented toward the FMN C4 carbonyl oxygen, but in the D211N mutant, the peptide bond between G144 and E145 is clearly flipped. This is correlated with the differences in the adjacent E145 and nearby E182 side chains on the protein surface. In stark contrast to the neighboring atoms, neither the E145 nor the E182 side chains are well-defined in the electron density for the wild-type structure, suggesting that these side chains are very mobile. The residue V164 is between α5 and β4 on the opposite side of the FMND from the prosthetic group, ∼27 Å from the nearest FMN atoms (Fig. 6B). Discussion

Mutations affecting human NADPH-P450 reductase function have been discovered in patients with clinical disease and in vitro investigations. Mutations can affect the FMND function by altering available conformations for FMND relative to the FAD-containing domain,6 ablation or reduction of the FMN prosthetic group,39 redox potential modifications,39 and altering P450 binding and electron transfer.20,33 The latter 2 effects have been difficult to capture structurally. Therefore, FMND mutations that specifically affect the regioselectivity of CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism25 presented an opportunity to experimentally determine underlying structural changes in the FMND and probe communication between the protein/protein interface and the CYP1A2 active site.

In addition to the abilities of these FMND mutants to affect CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism,25 we evaluate their capacity in sustaining CYP1A2-mediated MROD activity. Compared with wild-type enzyme, D211N and H183Y increase metabolism of both substrates 1.1- to 1.4-fold, whereas A229T has turnover equivalent to the wild-type enzyme (although slightly higher for ethoxyresorufin O-deethylation21), and T142A reduces metabolism to 60%–70%. Because T142A may have not had 100% flavin occupancy,25,40 the respective MROD activity reduction observed may be misleading, but the altered regioselectivity seems to be consistent with previous data. V164M is the only mutant supporting caffeine metabolism equivalent to the wild-type enzyme but demonstrating a 1.4-fold increase with MthR. None of these mutations alter cytochrome c reduction,25 suggesting an unchanged redox potential and no residue-specific effects on that protein/protein interaction. However, while wild-type reductase supports CYP1A2-mediated caffeine N-demethylation at N3, N1, and N7 to produce paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline, respectively, in a ratio of 84:12:4,26 FMND mutants can shift this ratio. T142A significantly increases N7-demethylation to yield theophylline, whereas V164M, A229T, and D211N increase N1-demethylation to form theobromine, and H183Y decreases formation of theophylline and theobromine. The overall inference is that such mutations may change the FMND surface involved in P450 binding so as to allosterically modulate the distant CYP1A2 active site, influencing substrate orientation therein and thus product regioselectivity.

The structural effects of these mutations on the FMND are poorly understood. Computational efforts docking the human reductase wild-type FMND with CYP1A2 allowing induced fit suggested FMN residues with interdomain contacts might include Q90-T91 and E95 (β1/ α2 loop), E118-E119 (α3), 140s loop residues Y143-G144 and D147, D150-N151 and Q153 (α5), 180s loop residues N178 and T180-Y181, and D211 (β5/α7 loop), most of which are located on the surface surrounding the FMN prosthetic group but also extend along α3 toward the N-terminus. This region includes many negatively charged residues (D116, E118, E119, E145, D147, and D150) implicated in P450 interaction.3,6,33,41, 42, 43 Most predicted Cα changes in the wild-type FMND were 0.1–0.6 Å. Several of these loops have also been implicated by NMR.44 A229T has also been modeled computationally, with little predicted structural change.21

Thus, structures of the 5 regioselectivity-modifying FMND mutants were determined experimentally by X-ray crystallography. All incorporated FMN with 100% occupancy. Prior reports had suggested T142A might not be fully flavinated,25,40 but this did not seem to be the case for our preparations. Unsurprisingly for single mutations and consistent with modeling of A229T, no mutations yielded large changes in overall structure. Overall Cα root mean squared deviations are 0.19–0.28 Å between the wild-type and mutant structures, with larger changes for individual residues (root mean squared deviation 0.82–1.2 Å). High resolutions (Table 1) and well-defined electron density for mutated side chains (Supplemental Fig. 1) strongly support even small changes. The mutated residues were generally oriented similarly to the wild-type residue, often accommodated by small, energetically accessible repositioning of surrounding residues, along with corresponding changes in the backbone and/or side chains for 2 loops flanking the FMN.

Three residues examined are in the vicinity of the FMN, and structural observations may be informative. T142A results in the loss of hydrogen bonds between the wild-type side chain and 2 backbone amides in the 180s loop (Fig. 6A). This occurs alongside changes in both the 140s and 180s loops backbone and side chains, with the side chains including at least 3 anionic residues implicated in binding P450 enzymes. H183Y appears to disrupt a hydrogen bond to the E145 carbonyl correlated with similar alterations in the backbone and anionic side chains (Fig. 5A), whereas D211 directly interacts with the FMN ribitol but mutation also causes changes in the 140s and 180s loops (Fig. 4).

H183Y and D211N demonstrate a clear peptide flip between G144 and E145 (Fig. 4B; Fig. 5A), absent in wild-type structure and the other mutant structures, though V164M has a small amount of Fo-Fc residual density suggesting a partial flip. Peptide flips are important because they reverse hydrogen bond donors/acceptors and are often associated with redox tuning. In wild-type the backbone carbonyl points toward the FMN C4 carbonyl, so the carbonyl-carbonyl dipolar interaction could influence the redox potential and stabilize FMN-binding. In H183Y and D211N, the flipped peptide removes this carbonyl from the FMN vicinity. The amide NH now faces FMN but is too distant for hydrogen bonding, instead providing a weak opposite dipole. This classic backbone gating regulatory motif in redox proteins (“electrostatic switching”), typically shifts redox potential more negative, altering electron transfer rates and potentially partner interactions. In rat reductase, the corresponding carbonyl (between G141 and E142) points toward FMN N5 (the site of flavin protonation) in the reduced state and flips in the oxidized state.45 Although synchrotron X-ray exposure can oxidize heme- and flavin-containing proteins,46, 47, 48 these structures still retain the oxidized orientation at the same or higher exposures as the other mutant structures. This suggests that this peptide flip in human reductase is less tied to oxidation state. This is consistent with this carbonyl directed toward the FMN C4 oxygen in human instead of the FMN N5 (the atom where protonation occurs) in rat.45 In addition, the isoalloxazine ring is planar in the oxidized state but adopts a “butterfly bend” 15°–25° in the reduced state.46 All of the structures herein retain FMN planarity consistent with the oxidized state. Because none of these mutants change redox potential,25 the peptide flip observed for H183Y and D211N is more likely linked to structural changes than reduction.

The 2 other mutations far from the FMN prosthetic group (V164M and A229T), nonetheless result in small differences in the backbone placement and some of the side chains in both the 140s and 180s loops. Communication between the mutations and the 140s loop and 180s loop is much less obvious, although V164M occurs at the opposite end of α5 from the 140s loop and a slight overall shift of that helix could be relevant.

An overall comparison of both FMN-flanking loops between structures is informative (Fig. 7). For T142A, the 140s loop shifts away from FMN, whereas the 180s loop shifts toward it (Fig. 7, green). In the 180s loop, T142A is the most shifted, followed by V164M, and then D211N and A229T, with H183Y the least shifted. In addition to direct effects conformational changes in the 140s and 180s loops likely have on CYP1A2 binding, electrostatic changes are another significant consideration. The importance of electrostatic interactions in the FMND binding to partner proteins is well known. The negative patch around the FMN-exposed surface is thought to guide formation of the encounter complex. Chemical modification or mutation of carboxylate groups mitigate the reduction of cytochrome c and reduce P450 catalysis.49, 50, 51 The P450 proximal face thought to bind here is positively charged, with key Lys and Arg residues.52 The largest change in FMND electrostatics for these mutants is D211N, removing a negative charge in the middle of 3 sequential aspartates on the P450-binding surface. The G144-E145 peptide flip and other changes that shift the backbone of 140s and 180s loops also result in repositioning of multiple-surface negatively charged side chains, and the combination must influence CYP1A2 binding in a way that is communicated to the CYP1A2 active site. Corresponding evidence demonstrates that although D211N reduces cytochrome c reductase normally, it decreases CYP2D6 activity by 70%.33 Mutations of D147 or D150 in or near the 140s loop results also have unaltered cytochrome c reductase activity, while decreasing CYP2D6 activity by 25%–30%. Clearly, although FMND interacts differently with cytochrome c reductase, subtle conformational and/or electrostatic changes are sufficient to alter P450 function and/or regioselectivity.

Conclusions

Overall, these structures of FMND mutations that specifically alter the regioselectivity of CYP1A2 caffeine metabolism25 also modulate CYP1A2-mediated MROD activity. Structures reveal that the most substantial changes occur in the 140s and 180s loops flanking the FMN prosthetic group. The degree to which these loops were altered varies, with all T142A shifting both loops toward the isoalloxazine ring. These loops are strongly implicated in the P450/reductase protein-protein interaction. While some mutations occur near the prosthetic group and the mechanisms by which these loops are modified are clear, others are distant from both loops but appear to propagate structural changes specifically to this region involved in interacting with the P450. Structural changes at both the backbone and side chain levels in these loops may alter the CYP1A2/FMND interaction either (1) due to small conformational changes or (2) because anionic groups are shifted. Although these studies cannot rule out that there are other structural changes not captured by X-ray crystallography, these studies suggest that the P450 active site is highly sensitive to the orientation of these loops at the protein-protein interface. It is possible that substrate orientation in the P450 active site could modulate its FMND binding site or that the FMND binding modulates substrate orientation in the P450. The fact that even small structural differences are observed in the isolated FMND might support the latter form of allostery, but this remains an active area of inquiry.
