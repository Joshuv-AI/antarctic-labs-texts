Tobacco control without cessation? Policy gaps in cessation services in Bangladesh
Authors: Md Golam Kibria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tobacco use remains one of the leading preventable causes of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cancers, cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes. [1,2] Each year, it accounts for more than 7 million deaths and approximately 200 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) worldwide. [3] In Bangladesh, the prevalence of tobacco use is high, with 35.3% (37.8 million) of adults aged 15 years or above using tobacco in smoked, smokeless, or dual forms. [4] This contributes substantially to the national NCD burden, where 67% of all deaths are attributable to NCDs. [5] In addition to its health impacts, tobacco use imposes a considerable economic burden, with direct healthcare costs estimated at BDT 83.9 billion annually. [6]

Over the past two decades, the Government of Bangladesh has implemented a series of tobacco control measures through the Smoking and Tobacco Products Usage (Control) Act 2005, and its 2013 and 2026 amendments, [[7], [8], [9]] in line with obligations under the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC). [10] These measures include smoke-free public places and transport, restrictions on tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (TAPS), and pictorial health warnings on tobacco packaging. Smoke-free legislation has prohibited smoking in designated public places, including healthcare facilities, educational institutions, government offices, and public transport, thereby reducing exposure to secondhand smoke and protecting non-smokers in shared environments. [11] Restrictions on TAPS have reduced both direct and indirect tobacco marketing across traditional and digital media platforms. [12] Pictorial health warnings cover a substantial proportion of tobacco packaging and aim to increase risk perception and discourage tobacco consumption. [13] Tobacco taxation has also been progressively increased, which is widely recognised as one of the most effective demand-reduction measures by reducing affordability. [14] Together, these interventions have strengthened the country's tobacco control framework and contributed to a gradual decline from 43.3% in 2009 to 35.3% in 2017. [4]

Despite these achievements, the evolution of tobacco control policy in Bangladesh has been largely driven by population-level regulatory interventions, while comparatively little progress has been made in integrating tobacco dependence treatment into the public healthcare system. Although Bangladesh ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) in 2004. [15] and Article 14 recommends that Parties establish comprehensive, evidence-based tobacco dependence treatment services within national health systems, [10] national policy development has continued to prioritise legislative and fiscal measure over cessation service delivery. Consequently, national policies acknowledge tobacco use as a major NCD risk factor but provide limited operational guidance for integrating behavioural counselling, pharmacotherapy, structured follow-up, and referral services into routine public healthcare.

This policy gap is reflected in quitting behaviour among tobacco users. Nearly half (44.9%) of Bangladeshi tobacco users report attempting to quit each year; however, almost seven in ten (69.7%) make quit attempts without professional assistance. [4] The absence of accessible cessation services reduces the likelihood of successful quitting and represents a missed opportunity to reduce tobacco-related morbidity and mortality, particularly among individuals already living with or at high risk of NCDs. Therefore, this short communication aimed to examine policy and health system gaps that affect the provision of tobacco cessation services in public healthcare facilities in Bangladesh and identify priorities for strengthening cessation support.

Methods

Policy review approach

To examine how tobacco cessation has been addressed over time, this short communication reviewed the principal national health documents that guide NCD prevention and health service delivery in Bangladesh, including the National Health Policy 2011 [16]; the three most recent health sector programmes: the 3rd Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Development Programme (3rd HPNSDP), 2011-2016; the 4th Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Programme (4th HNPSP), 2017-2022; and the 5th Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Programme (5th HNPSP), 2024-2029 [[17], [18], [19]]; the NCD-related Operational Plans of the 3rd HPNSDP and 4th HPNSP [20,21]; and the Multisectoral Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Non-communicable Diseases (2018–2025). [22] The review assessed the extent to which these policies incorporated tobacco dependence treatment into routine public healthcare and how policy attention to tobacco cessation has evolved over time.

Results

Policy gaps

The policy review included the National Health Policy 2011, the 3rd HPNSDP, the 4th HPNSP, the 5th HPNSP, the NCD-related Operational Plans of the 3rd HPNSDP and the 4th HPNSP, and the Multisectoral Action Plan for Prevention and Control of NCDs (2018-2025) (Table 1). The National Health Policy 2011 emphasises health promotion, disease prevention, and healthy lifestyle practices as central strategies for improving population health. Although the policy recognises tobacco use as an unhealthy lifestyle behaviour, it does not explicitly identify tobacco cessation as an essential component of routine healthcare delivery. Furthermore, the policy does not provide a structured framework for integrating tobacco cessation into routine healthcare services.

The health sector programmes were first launched in Bangladesh under the sector-wide approach (SWAp) in 1998 to improve the availability, accessibility, equity, and quality of healthcare services across public health facilities. [23] The three most recent sector programmes recognise the growing burden of NCDs, acknowledge tobacco use as a risk factor for NCDs and promote tobacco control measures, including legislation, taxation, and public awareness campaigns. However, tobacco cessation receives limited operational attention. Specifically, the NCD-related Operational Plan of the HPNSDP, 2011-2016 outlines a tobacco cessation clinic initiative through the establishment of a counselling hotline centre, while the Operational Plan of the 4th HPNSP, 2017-2022 includes community counselling on healthy lifestyles and smoking cessation as part of the Essential Service Package (ESP) for NCDs. Nevertheless, neither the core sector programmes nor their accompanying Operational Plans explicitly provide a framework for integrating tobacco cessation into routine healthcare services or specify operational arrangements for delivering tobacco dependence treatment within the health system.

The Multisectoral Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Non-communicable Diseases (2018–2025) identifies tobacco control as a strategic priority for preventing NCDs and adopts a national target of a 30% relative reduction in tobacco use by 2025. The plan promotes population-level tobacco control through legislative enforcement, tobacco taxation, public awareness campaigns, measures to eliminate illicit tobacco trade, and support for alternative livelihoods to tobacco cultivation. It also proposes strengthening tobacco cessation services through primary healthcare by establishing a national quitline, integrating healthy lifestyle education, including tobacco use reduction, into health facilities, and adapting the WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable (PEN) Disease Interventions. Furthermore, it does not explicitly describe a comprehensive implementation framework for integrating tobacco cessation into routine healthcare services, including standardised clinical protocols, referral pathways, provider responsibilities, or cessation-specific monitoring indicators.

Discussion

Health system challenges

Despite Bangladesh's progress in tobacco control, the health system remains inadequately equipped to deliver comprehensive tobacco cessation services. Available evidence suggests that, beyond brief cessation advice, structured cessation services are largely absent from routine healthcare delivery in Bangladesh. [24,25] Although behavioural counselling is an effective and cost-efficient intervention that can increase the likelihood of successful tobacco cessation by 40-80% compared to minimal support, [26] it has not been systematically integrated into primary healthcare services. Consequently, opportunities to provide evidence-based cessation support to tobacco users during routine healthcare encounters remain largely underutilised.

Evidence-based pharmacotherapy, including nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and non-nicotine medications (e.g., varenicline and bupropion), is not available in public health facilities in Bangladesh. and, where available in the private sector, is very expensive. [27] As a result, only 0.2% of Bangladeshi smokers use pharmacotherapy when attempting to quit. [4] A Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis of 133 randomised controlled trials involving 64,640 participants found that NRT increases the likelihood of successful smoking cessation by 55% compared to placebo or no NRT. [28] The limited availability and affordability of pharmacotherapy therefore represent a major barrier to successful tobacco cessation in Bangladesh and may contribute to the continued burden of tobacco-related disease. Although the Multisectoral Action Plan for Prevention and Control of NCDs (2018-2025) commits to establishing a dedicated national tobacco quitline, it has not been established in Bangladesh to date. [29] The absence of such a service limits access to behavioural support, particularly for the country's rural and underserved populations. Telephone-based counselling has been shown to increase quit rates by 43% compared to minimal support [30] and provides a cost-effective means of extending cessation services beyond healthcare facilities.

Midstream actors play a critical role in translating national tobacco control policies into routine cessation support. Healthcare organisations, physicians, nurses, pharmacists, Community Health Workers (CHWs), educational institutions, and community-based organisations can facilitate policy implementation by systematically identifying tobacco users, delivering brief cessation interventions, providing follow-up support, and facilitating referrals to specialised cessation services. Conversely, commercial actors, including tobacco manufacturers, tobacco retailers, and sales representatives, may undermine these efforts by promoting tobacco products, maintaining widespread product availability, and encouraging tobacco use through point-of-sale marketing and other commercial incentives. [[31], [32], [33]]

Policy priorities and recommendations

Bridging the gap between tobacco control policy and practice in Bangladesh requires a comprehensive health system approach to tobacco dependence treatment. First, tobacco cessation should be explicitly incorporated into national health policies and future health sector programmes through dedicated implementation plans, sustainable financing mechanisms, and measurable monitoring indicators. National clinical guidelines and standard operating procedures should also be developed to support the delivery of evidence-based tobacco dependence treatment across all levels of care.

Second, tobacco cessation services should be embedded into routine primary healthcare and NCD services so that every healthcare encounter becomes an opportunity to promote quitting. Standardised protocols for screening tobacco use, delivering cessation counselling based on the WHO 5A's/5R's framework, [34] prescribing pharmacotherapy where appropriate, and providing follow-up support should be incorporated into routine clinical practice. Strengthening the capacity of physicians, nurses, and Community Health Workers (CHWs) through pre-service and in-service training is essential to ensure the consistent delivery of evidence-based cessation interventions. CHWs can further extend brief behavioural counselling, follow-up support, and referral services to rural and underserved populations, thereby improving the accessibility and continuity of tobacco cessation services. [35]

Third, access to evidence-based cessation treatment should be expanded by ensuring the availability and affordability of nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and non-nicotine cessation medications, e.g., varenicline and bupropion within the country's public healthcare facilities. Sustainable financing mechanisms, including earmarking a proportion of tobacco tax revenues, could support procurement, workforce training, and service delivery. [36]

Fourth, a national toll-free quitline should be established to provide accessible behavioural counselling and follow-up support for tobacco users. Given the widespread use of mobile phones in Bangladesh, such a service would provide a cost-effective means of extending evidence-based cessation support to rural and underserved populations who have limited access to facility-based services.

Finally, tobacco cessation strategies should be tailored to the Bangladeshi context. Public awareness campaigns should promote evidence-based quitting methods and encourage greater utilisation of cessation services. Interventions should also address the needs of smokeless tobacco users and women who use tobacco with betel leaf, [37] groups that are often overlooked in conventional tobacco control programmes. As access to digital and telephone-based services remains uneven, community-based delivery models involving CHWs, primary healthcare facilities, maternal and child health services, and community outreach programmes should complement facility-based and digital cessation services to improve equitable access among rural populations, women, and individuals with limited health and digital literacy.

Strengths and limitations

This short communication provides a comprehensive review of the major national health policy documents in Bangladesh to identify key policy and health system gaps affecting the integration of tobacco cessation services into routine public healthcare. It also presents evidence-informed recommendations to strengthen tobacco dependence treatment in line with Article 14 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control. However, the short communication is based on a narrative review of publicly available policy documents and does not include primary data or stakeholder perspectives. Future research involving empirical evidence and stakeholder engagement is needed to better understand implementation barriers and inform context-specific strategies for strengthening tobacco cessation services.

Conclusions

Bangladesh has made notable progress in tobacco control through the implementation of legislative, regulatory, and fiscal measures. However, tobacco cessation services remain inadequately integrated into the public healthcare system. Although national policies acknowledge the importance of tobacco control, important gaps persist in translating these commitments into comprehensive cessation services. Strengthening tobacco dependence treatment will require integrating tobacco cessation into routine primary healthcare and NCD services, developing national clinical guidelines and implementation frameworks, expanding access to behavioural and pharmacological interventions, building the capacity of healthcare providers, and establishing sustainable financing and monitoring mechanisms. The Government of Bangladesh should prioritise these actions to ensure that every tobacco user who wishes to quit has access to effective cessation support, thereby accelerating progress towards national NCD targets and Bangladesh's vision of a tobacco-free society.
