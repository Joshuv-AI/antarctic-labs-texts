The effect of home-based virtual exercise rehabilitation on myocardial infarction patients using the health action process approach: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zahra Fallah, Awat Feizi, Masoumeh Sadeghi, Mohammad Mahdi Hadavi, Hossein Shahnazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading cause of death globally, yet patient participation in traditional cardiac rehabilitation programs is low. Home-based rehabilitation, especially when supported by technology, offers a promising alternative. This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of a home-based virtual exercise rehabilitation program, based on the Health Action Process Approach, for improving physical activity and psychological constructs in patients with myocardial infarction.MethodsThis Randomized Controlled Trial involved a control and an intervention group, with data collected at baseline, post-intervention, and a 6-month follow-up. The intervention group received an 8-week program via a mobile app that included motivational content (e.g., perceived threat) and volitional strategies (e.g., self-efficacy). Physical activity was measured using the IPAQ, and psychological constructs were assessed with a HAPA-based questionnaire.ResultsAt baseline, there were no significant demographic differences between groups. Post-intervention, the intervention group showed significant increases in perceived threat (P = 0.000), outcome expectations (P = 0.04), and self-efficacy (P = 0.02) compared to the control group. The significant time x group interaction for perceived threat (P = 0.000) indicated a greater rate of change in the intervention group. While there were no significant group differences for perceived barriers, both groups showed significant changes over time. At the 6-month follow-up, the intervention group had a significantly greater increase in physical activity (P = 0.02) than the control group.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that a home-based virtual exercise program based on the HAPA is an effective and sustainable approach to improving physical activity and key psychological factors in patients with myocardial infarction. These results support the use of targeted digital interventions to enhance patient outcomes in cardiac rehabilitation.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials. Background

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading cause of death and long-term disability globally, placing a significant burden on both healthcare systems and the economy [1]. These diseases are a major contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide [2]. Annually, CVDs are responsible for approximately 17.9 million deaths [3]. In Iran, CVDs are also the leading cause of mortality, accounting for a high percentage of all deaths according to the Ministry of Health [4].

Exercise rehabilitation is a key intervention for improving the condition of cardiac patients, playing an important role in reducing the burden of these diseases. By reducing symptoms, improving physical function, and enhancing quality of life, these programs offer significant benefits. They often include supervised exercise, patient education, and psychological counseling [1, 4]. Such comprehensive programs can reduce mortality rates by 15–25% and significantly improve a patient’s physical and mental health [5].

Despite the proven benefits of cardiac rehabilitation in rehabilitation centers, the rate of patient participation in these programs is very low. Even in developed countries, an average of only 30% of patients access these services [6]. In Iran, the rate of patient participation in cardiac rehabilitation programs has been reported to be even lower, at only 28%. Additionally, about 33% of patients who enroll in these programs drop out before completion for various reasons, including high costs, lack of social support, physical problems, and transportation challenges [7].

Given the numerous challenges that hinder patient participation in center-based cardiac rehabilitation programs, alternative approaches like home-based exercise rehabilitation have been proposed [7–9]. These programs are designed to allow patients to continue their rehabilitation at home using non-face-to-face support and available resources [10].

Home-based exercise rehabilitation not only reduces common barriers like transportation challenges, high costs, and time constraints but also improves patient participation by offering greater flexibility [11]. Studies have shown that these programs, in addition to reducing mortality and modifying cardiac risk factors, also have positive effects on a patient’s quality of life, helping to reduce anxiety and depression, and improve their overall lifestyle [11–13].

Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) is a suitable motivational framework for promoting self-care and physical activity in the rehabilitation of post-myocardial infarction patients [14, 15]. This model is designed to account for individual characteristics at different stages of behavior change. Some patients may not have yet started physical activity or lack the motivation to do so. In contrast, those who have started may struggle with maintaining their exercise program. Additionally, patients who have stopped exercising due to physical or psychosocial limitations face significant challenges in resuming their activity. Therefore, consistent follow-up and maintenance of physical activity are always recommended to reduce the risk of heart disease complications (Fig. 1).Studies have shown that patients face barriers to accessing in-person clinic education, which effectively limits the effectiveness of these programs. Providing home-based self-care education and reducing the need for clinic visits for chronic diseases makes patient follow-up and the guidance of appropriate self-care behaviors easier for both healthcare providers and patients. Ultimately, this has a positive impact on health outcomes [13–16].

HAPA framework

Recent studies have shown that using modern communication and information technologies is effective at improving the self-care process for chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases [15, 16]. These technologies make it easier for patients to participate in self-care programs like cardiac rehabilitation.

Using modern telecommunication technologies also improves care for elderly individuals who have difficulty leaving their homes, as it removes time and location restrictions. This approach also increases the quality of service provided in underserved and remote areas [17]. Accordingly, with the rapid and widespread growth of communication and information technology, the use of telecare technologies, especially e-health, has increased globally [16, 17].

Given that insufficient physical activity remains one of the major modifiable risk factors for recurrent cardiac events and long-term morbidity in post-MI patients, this study was designed and implemented to determine the effectiveness of a home-based virtual exercise rehabilitation program based on the HAPA for increasing physical activity in patients with a myocardial infarction.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a randomized controlled trial design with parallel groups. This study has been registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials. Registration number: IRCT20210509051235N1.The target population consisted of patients diagnosed with ischemic heart disease who had suffered a myocardial infarction and were referred to Chamran Heart Hospital, a major cardiac center at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Isfahan, Iran, between March 2022 and March 2023.

Inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of ischemic heart disease, (2) consent from their attending physician to participate in the home-based cardiac rehabilitation program, and (3) access to an Android-based mobile phone or tablet to receive the digital program (either the patient themselves or a cohabiting family member).

Exclusion criteria were: (1) any musculoskeletal condition that would prevent participation in the physical activity components of the program, (2) significant mental or psychological dysfunction that could affect adherence or program comprehension, and (3) failure to view the program materials in the application or non-adherence to the educational process.

Sampling and sample size calculation

Sample size determination and participant flow

The sample size was calculated for the primary outcome of physical activity in a repeated-measures design. We aimed to detect a standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of Δ = 0.5 with a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-tailed) and a statistical power of 80%. Accounting for one baseline measurement (and one post-intervention measurements (, and assuming a conservative correlation of ρ = 0.5 between repeated measures, the initial calculation determined that 56 participants per group were required.

To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, this number was increased, resulting in a planned sample size of 67 participants per group (Total target N = 134).

Participant recruitment and randomization

A total of 150 eligible heart failure patients were recruited through convenience sampling. This figure represents a conservative oversampling beyond our target of 134 to safeguard against potential unforeseen challenges in recruitment or a higher-than-expected dropout rate.

The randomization process was conducted as follows:

Random Sequence Generation

The allocation sequence was computer-generated using the random number function in Microsoft Excel by a research assistant who was not involved in participant recruitment, enrollment, or the delivery of the intervention.

Allocation Concealment

The generated sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each envelope was opened only after the enrolled participant had completed all baseline assessments, thus ensuring the allocation was hidden from both the participants and the researcher enrolling them until the moment of assignment.

Randomization Method

A permuted block randomization method with a block size of two was used to ensure a balanced distribution of participants between the two groups (1:1 allocation) throughout the recruitment period. While larger block sizes can reduce predictability, our choice of block size 2 was intentional to ensure maximum balance throughout the recruitment period, particularly important in our study with a relatively small sample size.

Implementation

Eligible participants were enrolled by the principal investigator. After providing informed consent and completing baseline measurements, the investigator opened the next sequentially numbered envelope to reveal the participant's group assignment (intervention or control).

Intervention

This study utilized a home-based cardiac rehabilitation program delivered via a mobile application. The intervention had a total duration of eight weeks and included educational content in the form of text, audio, video, and multimedia files. The program’s content was structured according to the Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) model, progressing from motivational to volitional phases [14].

The intervention followed a phased, adaptive approach:

Week 1 (Motivational Phase): The initial content focused on establishing an intention for physical activity. It included information on awareness, perceived threat, positive outcome expectations, and action self-efficacy, along with an introduction to the sports rehabilitation program.

Week 1 (Motivational Phase): The initial content focused on establishing an intention for physical activity. It included information on awareness, perceived threat, positive outcome expectations, and action self-efficacy, along with an introduction to the sports rehabilitation program.

Weeks 2–3 (Transition to Volitional Phase): Participants were asked to confirm their intention to engage in physical activity via an in-app question. Those who had formed an intention received content focused on self-regulation and self-efficacy strategies. Patients who had not yet formed an intention received the motivational content again. In Week 3, the intention question was repeated, and patients began receiving personalized sports rehabilitation video files, along with support for personal planning and direct advice from the researcher.

To enhance social support, a separate component of the program was provided to patients’ families, encouraging them to support the patient’s rehabilitation journey.

Measures

The primary outcome for this trial was the change in physical activity level, measured by the total MET-minutes/week from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), at the 6-month follow-up. All other analyses, including between-group differences at the post-intervention time point and changes in all Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) constructs (such as self-efficacy, perceived threat, and outcome expectations), were pre-specified as secondary outcomes.

The data were collected using two main tools: a demographic questionnaire and a researcher-developed questionnaire based on the HAPA. The HAPA questionnaire included several constructs with a specific number of items for each: perceived threat (4 items), outcome expectations (14 items), task self-efficacy (6 items), coping self-efficacy (7 items), recovery self-efficacy (6 items), and perceived barriers (5 items). All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 (worse) to 5 (better). The psychometric properties of this questionnaire were previously established and confirmed in a separate study titled “Psychometric Properties of the Persian Version Rehabilitation Self-Efficacy Questionnaire in Heart Failure Patients: A Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) Perspective,” [12].

To assess physical activity behavior, we used the Persian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Intraclass coefficient (ICC) of IPAQ domains showed acceptable correlation coefficients (a > 0.7) in Iranian samples [17]. The questionnaire categorizes total physical activity into three intensity levels based on energy consumption: light, moderate, and vigorous. Activities lasting less than 10 min were not included in the calculation.

The metabolic equivalent (MET) values used for scoring were:

Walking: 3.3 METs.

Walking: 3.3 METs.

Moderate physical activity: 4.0 METs.

Moderate physical activity: 4.0 METs.

Vigorous physical activity: 8.0 METs.

Vigorous physical activity: 8.0 METs.

The total amount of physical activity was calculated as the sum of MET-minutes per week for each intensity level. The score was computed by multiplying the MET value of the activity by the minutes performed per day and the number of days per week for each activity, using the following formula:

Total Physical Activity (MET-minutes/week) = [Walking METs x minutes/day x days/week] + [Moderate METs x minutes/day x days/week] + [Vigorous METs x minutes/day x days/week].

A MET is a unit that represents the energy expended per minute during an activity relative to the energy consumed at rest.

Blinding and bias mitigation procedures

Due to the nature of the behavioral intervention, participants and the intervention team could not be blinded to the group assignment. However, to minimize the risk of bias in outcome assessment, specific blinding procedures were implemented. While complete blinding of participants was not feasible due to the nature of the behavioral intervention, the outcome assessors responsible for administering the follow-up questionnaires (both the HAPA-based and IPAQ instruments) were blinded to the participants’ group allocations throughout the data collection period. Furthermore, the data analyst was kept blinded to group assignments until all primary analyses were completed. The IPAQ was administered as part of a broader questionnaire battery, with items presented in a neutral manner without emphasizing the intervention content. We incorporated attention checks and validated the IPAQ data for internal consistency to identify potentially unreliable responses.

To mitigate the risk of detection and reporting bias associated with the self-reported nature of the IPAQ, all instructions for completing the questionnaire were delivered using standardized scripts during follow-up assessments. This approach ensured that all participants received identical, neutral guidance, thereby reducing potential influence from the research team on their responses.

Data analysis

Data were collected at three distinct time points: pre-intervention, immediately post-intervention, and at a 6-month follow-up. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of α = 0.05 was predetermined for all statistical tests.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between groups using independent samples t-test for continuous variables and Chi-square test for categorical variables.

The primary outcome, physical activity measured by IPAQ (MET-minutes/week), was analyzed using paired t-tests to assess within-group changes from baseline to 6-month follow-up. Between-group differences in physical activity were evaluated using independent samples t-tests at both baseline and 6-month follow-up.

Secondary outcomes comprising HAPA constructs (perceived threat, outcome expectations, task self-efficacy, coping self-efficacy, recovery self-efficacy, and perceived barriers) were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA to examine main effects of time, group, and time×group interaction across the three assessment points. Between-group comparisons at each time point were performed using independent samples t-tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed and conducted at the 5% significance level.

Discussion

This research indicates that a home-based virtual exercise intervention can significantly improve physical activity levels and essential psychological constructs—such as self-efficacy and coping abilities—among individuals recovering from myocardial infarction.

According to the findings, perceived threat increased significantly in both groups; however, the increase was markedly greater in the intervention group compared to the control group. This suggests that the home-based exercise rehabilitation program was effective in enhancing patients’ awareness of the consequences of their condition and the risks associated with not adhering to treatment recommendations.

A higher sense of perceived threat helps motivate patients to take part in rehabilitation programs and follow healthy habits. When people better understand the risks of their condition, they are more likely to take preventive steps and stick to their treatment plans [7]. Many studies have shown that increasing patients’ awareness of heart disease risks can improve their participation in cardiac rehabilitation and encourage healthier lifestyle behaviors [16, 19, 20].

The training delivered through the app led to a significant increase in outcome expectations in the intervention group compared with the control group. This improvement was evident not only immediately after the intervention but also remained stable at the six-month follow-up. Since no similar change occurred in the control group, it can be concluded that the app-based training had a substantial positive impact on enhancing patients’ outcome expectations.

One of the key features of the app is the continuous access to content, allowing patients to revisit the material as needed. Additionally, the app’s interactive design actively engages patients in the learning process. These aspects can enhance patients’ knowledge and foster positive beliefs about exercise-based rehabilitation. Repetition of content and reminders helped sustain these positive behavioral changes over time [12, 13]. A systematic review has shown that mobile health apps can lead to significant improvements in both health behaviors and clinical outcomes [21].

To enhance task self-efficacy, we developed exercise programs tailored to each patient’s physical condition and individual needs. This approach aimed to help patients perceive themselves as capable of maintaining long-term engagement in exercise activities. The programs provided specific guidance on gradually increasing activity levels to achieve fitness and cardiac rehabilitation goals. While some studies have indicated that digital interventions can boost patients’ self-efficacy, the magnitude of this effect depends on individual and environmental factors [22, 23]. Our findings align with those of Heidari et al. [24], who reported a 13-point increase in self-efficacy in the intervention group (P < 0.001), compared to only a 0.18-point change in the control group.

We employed targeted coping strategies to enhance self-efficacy. Barriers to exercise were assessed, and each participant was asked to identify personal, psychological, environmental, and physical obstacles that might hinder their continued engagement in exercise. Following this assessment, individualized counseling was provided to help patients overcome these challenges. This counseling included practical strategies to reduce anxiety, boost self-confidence, and foster self-efficacy. The findings of our study are consistent with those of Zanaboni et al., who specifically investigated teletherapy and home-based exercise in patients with COPD [23].

Recovery self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully perform a specific behavior. In patients with myocardial infarction, enhancing this construct can have a significant impact on their return to daily activities, adherence to treatment, overall quality of life, and cardiac function [13].

In the study by Duan et al. [22], a digital intervention enhanced patients’ self-efficacy in returning to daily activities and engaging in exercise. Their findings demonstrated that digital support can effectively boost self-efficacy and promote long-term health maintenance. The significant increase in self-efficacy observed in the intervention group highlights the positive impact of the home-based intervention, suggesting that the web-based application and the guidance provided helped patients feel more in control and empowered throughout their rehabilitation.

Particularly in the context of myocardial infarction, enhancing recovery self-efficacy can have long-term positive effects on treatment adherence and health-related behaviors, including physical activity and dietary habits [12, 13]. These findings highlight the lasting benefits of targeted digital interventions on patient health and provide scientific evidence supporting the effectiveness of digital strategies in cardiac rehabilitation.

In the intervention group, perceived barriers showed a significant change after six months. This suggests that, over time, patients became more aware of the actual obstacles they faced. However, the concurrent increase in coping self-efficacy enabled these patients to better overcome these barriers.

This finding is consistent with studies indicating that educational interventions may initially heighten perceived barriers but can strengthen coping strategies over the long term [25, 26]. Notably, the reduction in perceived barriers was positively associated with increases in both coping self-efficacy and coping planning. In other words, as patients became more aware of their barriers, they also gained greater confidence and ability to plan for and manage them.

Therefore, our intervention, which combined ongoing counseling with targeted coping programs, helped patients identify barriers and develop appropriate strategies to overcome them. Comparisons with similar studies suggest that digital interventions in cardiac rehabilitation not only assist patients in recognizing barriers but also equip them with effective strategies to manage and cope with these challenges [24–26].

The results of our study indicated that the home-based intervention positively influenced patients’ physical activity behavior. This suggests that the web-based application and associated interventions effectively increased physical activity among cardiac patients in the intervention group. This effect may be attributed to strategies such as action planning, counseling, and the continuous support provided through the application. Although some increases were also observed in the control group, these changes were not significant compared to the intervention group, likely due to their lack of access to the tailored intervention and ongoing support that the intervention group received.

The findings of our study are consistent with those of Kizilkilic et al., who demonstrated that a mobile digital health intervention, when combined with center-based cardiac rehabilitation, significantly improved physical activity and enhanced functional capacity [27].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a home-based virtual exercise program effectively enhances physical activity and key psychological factors, including self-efficacy and coping skills, in patients with myocardial infarction. Although perceived barriers were not immediately reduced, patients developed the skills to manage them over the long term. Sustained improvements at six months highlight the program’s capacity to promote lasting behavioral change, supporting the integration of digital interventions into cardiac rehabilitation.

Study strengths

A strength of this study is its use of a home-based virtual exercise rehabilitation program, which offers a flexible and accessible solution for cardiac patients. The long-term follow-up at six months is another key strength, as it demonstrates the sustained positive effects of the intervention on physical activity and psychological constructs, such as self-efficacy and outcome expectations. The use of a randomized controlled trial design with an intervention and control group allowed for a robust comparison, and the lack of significant demographic differences between the groups strengthens the validity of the findings.

Study limitations

However, this study also has several limitations. The reliance on self-reported data for physical activity and other psychological constructs may introduce a degree of reporting bias, as patients might overstate their activity levels or positive behaviors. Additionally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited, as the study was conducted on a specific population of myocardial infarction patients and the results may not be applicable to other cardiac conditions or patient demographics. Finally, the study does not account for potential external factors or support systems that may have influenced the patients’ behavior outside of the virtual program.
