Association of endotheliopathy with coagulofibrinolytic reactions and disseminated intravascular coagulation after trauma: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Hironori Matsumoto, Suguru Annen, Naoki Mukai, Muneaki Ohshita, Shirou Ogawa, Mitsuo Okita, Tsunenori Tanabe, Mitsuaki Takezawa, Yuki Nakabayashi, Satoshi Kikuchi, Jun Takeba, Norio Sato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We carried out a retrospective observational investigation to explore the association of endotheliopathy with coagulofibrinolytic reactions and the progression of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adult trauma patients. We measured syndecan-1 (SDC-1), an indicator of endotheliopathy, and biomarkers of coagulofibrinolysis in 100 trauma patients immediately transferred to Ehime University Hospital. We evaluated the correlations between the coagulofibrinolytic parameters and SDC-1. We also investigated the association between SDC-1 elevations and the development of DIC, and determined the discriminators of DIC development. The median SDC-1 concentration was 82.7 (43.5–178.1) ng/mL. DIC developed in 16 patients (16.0%), and SDC-1 concentrations were significantly higher in DIC patients than in non-DIC patients (218.8 [134.5–798.2] ng/mL vs. 67.2 [39.6–114.5] ng/mL, p < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis revealed that the circulating SDC-1 level effectively predicted the progression of DIC, with an area under the curve of 0.862 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.789–0.936). The optimal cut-off value was determined to be 92.5 ng/mL, yielding a sensitivity of 100.0% and a specificity of 67.8% (p < 0.001). A simple logistic regression analysis showed that a circulating SDC-1 concentration of > 92.5 ng/mL was significantly correlated with DIC progression (odds ratio [OR], 31.67; 95%CI: 3.97–252.31, p = 0.001). Many coagulofibrinolytic parameters were significantly correlated with SDC-1. Estimating the discriminators of DIC development by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and elastic-net regression analysis identified markers of coagulofibrinolytic activation, such as thrombin-antithrombin complex (TAT) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). A multivariate logistic regression model using TAT, tPA, and SDC-1 demonstrated that TAT and tPA, but not SDC-1, were independent factors predicting the development of DIC (TAT per 10 µg/L: OR, 1.14, 95%CI: 1.05–1.24, p = 0.003; tPA per 100pg/mL: OR, 1.03, 95%CI: 1.01–1.05, p = 0.003; SDC-1 per 10ng/mL: OR, 1.00, 95%CI: 0.99–1.01, p = 0.973). Mediation analysis showed that SDC-1 elevation was predominantly associated with the development of DIC indirectly through the increase in TAT (proportion mediated = 96.1%, p < 0.001), while there was no significant indirect effect of SDC-1 elevation on the role of TAT elevation in DIC development was observed (p = 0.340). The primary pathogenesis of DIC in the acute phase of trauma is likely driven by coagulofibrinolytic activation. Endotheliopathy, as reflected by elevated circulating levels of SDC-1, is strongly associated with coagulofibrinolytic responses. Although endotheliopathy may contribute to the early development of DIC through coagulation activation, its role appears to be limited.

Introduction

Trauma constitutes a significant cause of mortality on a global scale1. Uncontrolled hemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death in the initial phase of trauma2–4. Trauma itself induces coagulofibrinolytic responses while maintaining hemostasis, which evokes trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) when hemostasis breaks down2,3. TIC presents heterogeneous complications ranging from a bleeding tendency to a thrombotic tendency and can be aggravated by multiple factors, including acidosis, hypothermia, and dilution, which occasionally progress into disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)3,5,6. The primary pathophysiology of TIC in the early phase of trauma consists of coagulofibrinolytic activation, impaired anticoagulation, and consumption coagulopathy, which meet the pathophysiological characteristics of DIC exhibiting an excessive fibrinolysis6–8.

The endothelium is essential for preserving hemostasis. Trauma also causes endotheliopathy, leading to endothelial dysfunction with increased permeability, aberrant coagulopathy, and inflammation9–11. Previous studies have shown that endotheliopathy provokes TIC12–15, but the detailed role or extent to which endotheliopathy contributes to DIC development remains unclear. In the current study, we measured a comprehensive panel of coagulofibrinolytic markers to assess the primary pathophysiology of DIC during the early phase of trauma. We also measured syndecan-1 (SDC-1) as a biomarker of endotheliopathy.

The endothelium is a cell layer of endothelial cells coated with the glycocalyx16,17. SDC-1 is one of the core proteins in the endothelial glycocalyx; therefore, circulating SDC-1 levels increase following endothelium damage18,19. In this research, we explored the correlation between endotheliopathy and coagulofibrinolytic responses in trauma patients upon admission. We assessed the correlations between circulating SDC-1 concentrations and the values of comprehensive coagulofibrinolytic markers. In addition, we examined the association between endotheliopathy and the progression of DIC during the early phase of trauma. We contrasted the levels of SDC-1 in patients with and without DIC and performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to assess the connection between elevated SDC-1 and the diagnosis of DIC. Then, we performed the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and the elastic-net regularized regressions to estimate the discriminators of DIC development, followed by a multivariate logistic regression analysis using the selected relevant variables. We also conducted a mediation analysis to examine the relationships between the predictor, mediator, and the development of DIC. Clarifying the association between elevated SDC-1 levels and coagulofibrinolytic reactions will demonstrate that SDC-1 can serve as an indicator of endothelial cell damage associated with TIC. Additionally, by examining the association between endotheliopathy and progression to DIC, SDC-1 can contribute to the evaluation of TIC and the development of novel therapeutic approaches for the management of coagulopathy.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-institution, retrospective, observational study of adult trauma patients transferred to Ehime University Hospital. The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the Institutional Review Board of Ehime University Hospital approved this study (Approval number: 1902014). All patients or a legally authorized representative provided informed consent for the preservation and research use of blood components remaining from clinical tests.

Study population, definition, and diagnosis

Adult trauma patients (≥ 18 years) transferred to our hospital were considered eligible. We reviewed cases between October 2017 and January 2022 in which consent was obtained and blood samples could be stored. We excluded patients who had already undergone therapeutic interventions or received medications before being transferred to our hospital, those with a history of bleeding or clotting diathesis, such as advanced malignancy and liver cirrhosis, and those who received anticoagulant therapy. During the study period, serum samples for analyzing SCD-1 levels were obtained from 100 patients with complete data on coagulofibrinolytic markers.

Clinical date, including characteristics, examinations, treatments, and mortality, were analyzed. Systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg and lactate levels > 18 mg/dL during treatment at the emergency department were defined as shock. The diagnosis of DIC was established within 24 h of the emergency department presentation, utilizing the overt-DIC diagnostic criteria from the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH)20,21. The decision for transfusions was made at the discretion of the attending clinicians, considering factors such as hemodynamics and hemostasis.

Data collection and measurement

Blood sampling was conducted promptly upon arrival at the emergency department. Routine laboratory tests were measured in the clinical laboratory of the hospital as follows: (1) blood counts and biochemistries using the TBA-c16000 (Toshiba Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) and XE-5000 (Sysmex, Hyogo, Japan) analyzers, and coagulofibrinolytic markers including activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), antithrombin (AT), D-dimer, fibrin/fibrinogen degradation product (FDP), fibrinogen (Fbg), protein C (PC), plasmin-α2-plasmin inhibitor complex (PIC), plasminogen (PLG), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin-antithrombin complex (TAT), and α2-plasmin inhibitor (α2PI) using CP-2000 (Sekisui Medical, Tokyo, Japan) and STACIA (LSI Medience, Tokyo, Japan) analyzers.

After routine blood sampling, the leftover serum and plasma samples were stored at − 80 °C. Commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to determine the concentrations of SDC-1 (Human CD138 ELISA kit, Diaclone, Besancon, Cedex, France) in the serum samples according to manufacturer’s instruction. tPA (tissue plasminogen activator) was also measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Human t-Plasminogen Activator/tPA Immunoassay, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Blood samples for the assessment of IL-6 and total PAI-1(tPAI-1: total plasminogen activator inhibitor-1), representing the sum of active PAI-1, t-PA/PAI-1 and latent PAI-1, were sent to the central laboratory of the BML Inc. in Tokyo, Japan and were measured utilizing ELISA (Human IL-6 Quantikine ELISA kit, R&D Systems) and the STACIA analyzer (LSI Medience Corp).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range. The Mann-Whitney U test and Fisher exact test were used to compare the two groups. Relationships between the dependent variables were analyzed using linear regression with t-tests. However, given the multifactorial nature of TIC pathogenesis and the heterogeneity of coagulofibrinolytic responses, it was possible that a linear correlation might not be present. To address this, locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) nonparametric models were used to calculate the relationships between SDC-1 concentrations and other values of coagulofibrinolytic markers to fit equations and splines. LOESS smoothing fits a smooth curve to a set of data points using weighted linear regression without being affected by outliers or noise22. The receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves and areas under the ROC curves (AUROC) were analyzed to evaluate the predictive capacity of SDC-1 levels for progression to DIC. The optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, and sensitivity and specificity were calculated on the basis of these optimal cut-off values. The limited sample size precluded the possibility of performing multivariate logistic regression analysis encompassing all measured markers to identify predictors of DIC development. Thus, we performed the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and the elastic-net regularized regressions, which are regularization methods that shrink the regression coefficient estimates toward zero to obtain reliable estimates23,24. Variables have been standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. After purposeful selection of covariates, we selected the two most relevant variables, TAT and tPA, based on the results of LASSO and elastic-net regularized regression analyses, which were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model for factors associated with the development of DIC. Additionally, we accounted for SDC-1 to assess its correlation with the development of DIC, regardless of the results of the LASSO and elastic-net regularized regression analyses. We also used causal mediation analysis to explore whether the independent variable affected the dependent variable directly or indirectly through the mediating variable, using the ‘mediation’ package in R software. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios with 95% CIs and p-values for all covariates in the final model. Differences were considered statistically significant at p values of < 0.05. R version 4.2.0 (https://cran.r-project.org/) and IBM SPSS statistics 22 (IBM, Tokyo, Japan) were used for statistical analysis.

Results

Characteristics of the study patients

The demographic data of the 100 patients are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 73.0% were men, with a median age was of 65.5 (47.3–74.8) years. The cause of trauma in all patients was blunt force injury, and the median injury severity score (ISS) was 16 (9–26). The prevalence of shock was observed in 24 patients (24.0%) at the emergency department, and the development of DIC was diagnosed in 16 patients (16.0%) within 24 h after admission. The median time of blood sampling was 58 (43–98) minutes after injury. The median serum SDC-1 concentration was 82.7 (43.5–178.1) ng/mL. Parameters of coagulofibrinolytic responses and systemic inflammation were markedly elevated on admission compared to their reference values (TAT, 72.1 [29.6–178.7] µg/L; PIC, 6.8 [2.6–16.0] µg/mL; tPA, 6063 [3406–9728] pg/mL; FDP, 48.6 [17.9–127.8] µg/mL; D-dimer, 24.3 [8.9–70.0] µg/mL, and IL-6, 89 [25–225] pg/mL). There were 6 deaths within 7 days of hospitalization.

Correlations between serum SDC-1 levels and coagulofibrinolytic responses

Although many parameters showed significant correlations in the linear regression analysis, several parameters, such as TAT and FDP, did not exhibit a linear relationship (Fig. 1; Blue lines). Therefore, we conducted a LOESS analysis (Fig. 1: Red lines).

Correlations between serum syndecan-1 (SDC-1) concentrations and the values of coagulofibrinolytic markers. The dotted lines depict the regression lines, while the shaded regions represent 95% confidence intervals.

Serum SDC-1 levels were significantly correlated with coagulation activity evaluated by TAT on admission (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Regarding impairment in anticoagulation, AT activity was significantly correlated with SDC-1 (p = 0.001), although PC did not show a significant correlation (p = 0.083). SDC-1 was also significantly correlated with fibrinolytic activity represented by PIC (p < 0.001), as well as consumption of PLG (p = 0.029) and α2PI (p < 0.001), an instantaneous inhibitor of fibrinolysis. Both tPA and tPAI-1, a main initiator and a primary inhibitor of fibrinolysis, exhibited significant correlations with SDC-1 (tPA, p < 0.001; tPAI-1, p = 0.009). As a consequence of coagulofibrinolytic reaction, FDP and D-dimer were correlated with SDC-1 (FDP, p = 0.029; D-dimer, p = 0.013). Concerning consumption coagulopathy, Fbg and PT-INR were significantly correlated with SDC-1, whereas PLT did not show a significant correlation (Fbg, p = 0.011; PT-INR, p = 0.004; PLT, p = 0.204). Parameters of inflammation and severity were also correlated with SDC-1 concentrations (IL-6, p < 0.001; lactate, p < 0.001; ISS, p < 0.001).

Association between SDC-1 elevation and DIC development

Serum SDC-1 concentrations in patients with DIC were significantly higher than those without DIC (218.8 [134.5–798.2] ng/mL vs. 67.2 [39.6–114.5] ng/mL, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). Systemic inflammatory and coagulofibrinolytic responses in patients with DIC were significantly greater than those in patients without DIC, as shown in Table 1. Median ISS and the prevalence of shock were significantly higher in patients with DIC than in those without DIC (ISS, 29 [25–35] vs. 17 [9–22], p < 0.001; shock, 75.0% vs. 14.3%, p < 0.001). Patients diagnosed with DIC exhibited notably higher transfusion needs in comparison to those without DIC (packed red blood cells: PRBC, 10 [7–30] U vs. 0 [0–2] U, p < 0.001; fresh-frozen plasma: FFP, 13 [9–36] U vs. 0 [0–0] U, p < 0.001; platelets, 0 [0–28] vs. 0 [0–0], p < 0.001). The patients with DIC exhibited a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate compared to those without DIC (31.3% vs. 1.2%, p < 0.001). The ROC curve analysis demonstrated that serum SDC-1 concentration served as a representative factor of the DIC progression (AUROC, 0.862; 95% CI: 0.789–0.936; cut-off value, 92.5 ng/mL; sensitivity, 100.0%, specificity, 67.8%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b).

Selection of discriminators of DIC development and a mediation analysis

The values of the tuning parameters α and λ were optimized by 10-fold cross-validation to minimize the partial likelihood deviance error (LASSO: λmin [the optimal values determined by the minimum criteria] = 0.035, λ1.se [the optimal values within 1 standard deviation of the minimum] = 0.099; elastic-net: αoptimal = 0.27, λmin = 0.099, λ1.se = 0.333) (Fig. 3). Out of the 20 variables listed in Table 2, including parameters of coagulofibrinolysis and systemic inflammation and SDC-1, the LASSO and elastic-net regression analyses did not identify SDC-1 as a discriminator of the development of DIC (Table 3). The selected discriminators included TAT and tPA, which are parameters of coagulofibrinolytic activation, whose standardized coefficients were relatively higher than those of the other variables. In a multivariate logistic regression model incorporating TAT, tPA, and SDC-1 to identify factors associated with DIC development, the results indicated that TAT and tPA were independent contributing factors, whereas SDC-1 was not (Table 2). A mediation analysis revealed that the elevation of SDC-1 was primarily associated with the development of DIC indirectly via TAT elevation (total effect = 8.32 × 10− 4, p = 0.020; average direct effect = 3.28 × 10− 5, p = 0.020; average causal mediation effect = 7.99 × 10− 4, p = 0.020; proportion mediated = 96.1%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). On the other hand, no apparent indirect effect of SDC-1 elevation on the role of TAT elevation in DIC development was observed (total effect = 2.48 × 10− 4, p < 0.001; average direct effect = 2.44 × 10− 4, p < 0.001; average causal mediation effect = 4.23 × 10− 6, p = 0.340; proportion mediated = 1.7%, p = 0.340) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In the present study, a notable correlation was observed between endotheliopathy, indicated by elevated serum SDC-1 levels, and the occurrence of DIC following trauma. Circulating SDC-1 concentration was associated with coagulofibrinolytic activation, impaired anticoagulation, and consumption coagulopathy. These responses suggest progression to DIC during the early phase of trauma, particularly when they occur excessively. However, the best discriminators of DIC development were markers of coagulofibrinolytic activity, such as TAT and tPA, rather than SDC-1. Furthermore, there was no significant indirect effect of SDC-1 elevation on the role of TAT elevation in DIC development, although SDC-1 elevation contributed to the early development of DIC through TAT elevation. These findings suggest a limited association between endotheliopathy and DIC progression in trauma.

Endothelial function plays a crucial role in vascular tone, blood flow, immune responses, and hemostasis25. Accordingly, endotheliopathy leads to endothelial dysfunction, which is characterized by increased permeability, aberrant coagulopathy, and inflammation9–11. The endothelium is a single layer of endothelial cells coated with glycocalyx, which also maintains vascular homeostasis. The glycocalyx is composed of membrane-attached proteoglycans, glycoproteins, glycosaminoglycans as well as adherent plasma proteins16,17. SDC-1 is one of the core proteins in the endothelial glycocalyx; thus, SDC-1 is released into the bloodstream when endotheliopathy and degradation of the glycocalyx occur. Consequently, the serum SDC-1 concentration may serve as a parameter for endotheliopathy. Elevated circulating SDC-1 levels have been documented in critically ill patients, including those with conditions such as trauma, sepsis, cardiovascular and kidney diseases, malignancy, and others16,18,26. Previous studies on trauma have indicated a correlation between elevated SDC-1 levels and the injury severity, as well as unfavorable outcomes9,10,12,14,15,19. In the current study, circulating SDC-1 concentrations increased with severity, as represented by ISS and inflammation, as shown by IL-6, which is consistent with previous reports. These findings indicate that endotheliopathy manifests promptly following injury, and SDC-1 is likely a measurable indicator of trauma-induced endotheliopathy.

Dynamic coagulofibrinolytic responses are induced by the trauma itself, which leads to develop “trauma-induces coagulopathy (TIC)” when these responses are excessive beyond the physiological range8,27,28. TIC is worsened synergistically with acidosis, hypothermia, dilution, endotheliopathy, and many other factors, eventually leading to the development of DIC5,6,8,27,28. Furthermore, TIC presents various complications, such as hemorrhage and blood clot formation27,28. Hence, coagulopathy due to trauma is multifactorial and heterogeneous. Although the phenotypes of coagulation disorders may evolve over time, the initial coagulofibrinolytic responses after trauma are defined by the initiation of coagulation, compromised anticoagulation, consumption coagulopathy, and the activation of fibrinolysis, which is subsequently followed by the later inhibition of fibrinolysis6–8. Johansson et al.. reported significant associations between SDC-1 and parameters of fibrinolysis, which is part of the coagulofibrinolytic responses against trauma12. However, the association between endotheliopathy, as indicated by SDC-1 elevation, and the overall coagulofibrinolytic responses elicited by trauma has not been extensively examined. The present study showed that the circulating concentration of SDC-1 was correlated with coagulofibrinolytic activation, impaired anticoagulation, and consumption coagulopathy in trauma patients. These findings indicate a strong correlation between endotheliopathy and heightened coagulopathy.

Trauma-induced coagulopathy can depend on many factors, including endothelialopathy, hypoperfusion, hypothermia, dilution, hypoxia, and acidosis8,27. When this coagulopathy progresses to DIC, its mortality drastically increases29,30. We sorted out the factors that dictated the development of DIC from the markers of coagulofibrinolysis, inflammation, disease severity, and endotheliopathy in this study. We found that SDC-1, which is a marker of endotheliopathy, was not selected, whereas markers of coagulofibrinolytic activation were selected as factors determining the development of DIC. Furthermore, the causal mediation analysis showed no significant indirect effect of SDC-1 elevation on the role of TAT elevation in DIC development. These findings suggest a limited association between endotheliopathy and DIC progression in trauma. Iba et al. noted that coagulopathy in trauma is primarily triggered by thrombin generation due to tissue injury, and the role of endotheliopathy is less significant in trauma-induced DIC, supporting our results31. Nonetheless, endotheliopathy demonstrated a correlation with coagulofibrinolytic activation, impairment in anticoagulation, and consumption coagulopathy. Additionally, causal mediation analysis suggested that endotheliopathy might contribute to the early development of DIC through coagulation activation in trauma, although its association appears limited. These findings support the potential existence of cross-talk between endotheliopathy and coagulofibrinolytic activation, which may lead to the development of DIC. The glycocalyx is thought to prevent microvascular thrombosis through some mechanisms including the anticoagulant effect of antithrombin and thrombomodulin and inhibition of thrombin and some coagulation factors by heparan sulfate16,26. Richter RP, et al. clearly demonstrated a complex interplay between endothelopathy and fibrinolysis dysregulation that may promote the propagation of organ dysfunction32. Even if not directly, endotheliopathy may be involved in DIC through various mechanisms. Further investigation is required to more definitively link endotheliopathy to DIC development following trauma and to determine optimal interventions.

The current study had several potential limitations. First, this retrospective observational study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, thereby limiting the independent assessment of cause and effect relationships. In addition, TIC is multifactorial and heterogeneous, potentially contributing to the variability in this study. Our findings necessitate validation through a more extensive investigation, accounting for factors linked to TIC and trauma-related endothelial cell injury. Second, this study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in a rural area of Japan, and included some cases with relatively elderly patients, and where patient transport took longer due to the characteristics of the area. However, the patient population in this study does not differ significantly from the general trauma patient population in Japan33,34. Although there have been reports of age-related changes in glycocalyx35, compared to the SDC-1 fluctuations associated with this trauma, the effect is very small and distortion is not considered problematic enough to affect the study results. With regard to blood sampling, the timing of blood collection is extremely important in the evaluation of coagulofibrinolytic responses and inflammation associated with trauma. In particular, evaluation within the first hour immediately following trauma is appropriate36. In the present study, the median blood sampling time was 58 min, which is comparable to previous reports of early coagulation and fibrinolysis responses in trauma7,36, and is considered to be sufficiently reliable. However, it is necessary to externally validate our results in other settings using blood samples collected as early as possible. Finally, the degree to which endotheliopathy affected coagulofibrinolytic activation was unclear. Moreover, we evaluated SDC-1 and coagulofibrinolytic markers at only a single time point; thus, the influence of the duration of endotheliopathy could not be determined. Future studies should evaluate the chronological changes in SDC-1 and coagulofibrinolytic markers and their associations.

Conclusion

The primary pathogenesis of DIC in the acute phase of trauma is likely driven by coagulofibrinolytic activation. Endotheliopathy, as reflected by elevated circulating levels of SDC-1, is strongly associated with coagulofibrinolytic responses. Although endotheliopathy may contribute to the early development of DIC through coagulation activation, its role appears to be limited.
