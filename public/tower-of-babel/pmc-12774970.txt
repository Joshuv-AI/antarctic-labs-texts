Microstructural, mechanical and statistical evaluation of concrete incorporating waste glass wool exposed to elevated temperatures
Authors: Yasin Onuralp Özkılıç, Memduh Karalar, Ali İhsan Çelik, Muhannad Riyadh Alasiri, Mohamed Abdikarin Mohamud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this research was to determine the effects of using waste glass wool in concrete’s on the concrete’s mechanical properties. Samples of concrete containing varying percentages of waste glass wool by total concrete volume (0%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5%) were tested after exposure to a range of temperatures: 24 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. Several tests, including compressive strength (CS), splitting tensile strength (STS), and flexural strength (FS), were then performed on the samples in the laboratory following the completion of elevated temperature. The results showed that glass wool waste material reduces the concrete CS. Conversely, experiments have shown that samples STS and FS, increase proportionally with the amount of waste glass wool. Accordingly, the concrete compressive strength decreased by 4.26%, 7.84%, 11.87%, 14.24%, and 17.27% when the mixing ratio was increased from 1% to 5%. On the other hand, by increasing the mixing ratio from 1% to 5%, concrete STS and FS were enhanced by 9.61%; 15.19%; 27.70%; 36.58%; and 60.41% and 16.91%; 29.99%; 35.73%; 45.45%; and 59.45%, respectively. FE-SEM and EDX analysis was also included. Furthermore, equations were developed to estimate the capacities of concrete, with waste glass wool, after exposure to elevated temperatures. Taguchi analysis revealed that temperature is the most critical factor affecting mechanical performance, while the addition of waste glass wool contributed to strength retention at elevated temperatures. ANOVA confirmed the statistical significance of both temperature and waste glass wool’s contribution, reinforcing their combined role in producing sustainable concrete with improved high-temperature resistance. The addition of waste glass wool to concrete recycles waste glass wool and minimizes the need for new raw materials and waste disposal requirements. According to the ratio and distribution above, adding modest quantities of glass wool may lighten concrete without affecting its strength.Keywords: Waste glass wool, Elevated temperature, Compressive strength, Splitting tensile strength, Flexural strengthSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete, with its remarkable versatility, durability, and adaptability, stands as the most widely used construction material in modern infrastructure and serves as a cornerstone of global development through its extensive use in buildings, roads, bridges, and various other structures1,2. As a consequence of growing environmental consciousness, the concrete industry is under pressure to reduce the use of natural resources. However, the production of traditional concrete creates serious environmental impacts largely due to the intensive use of natural resources and energy3. The growing need to protect natural resources, effectively manage industrial and construction waste, and reduce environmental pollution has encouraged the use of industrial and construction waste as a sustainable building material4,5. Mineral wools, which include glass and glass wools, are often used as insulation materials in structures. One kind of mineral wool is called glass wool, and it has an amorphous structure, a high SiO2 content, and up to 16 weight% of Na2O6,7, . Typically, these materials are disposed of in landfills after the buildings are demolished8. Stone wool is a material used in the inorganic insulation process. It is also used in a broad range of industrial applications, including autos, ships, and appliances, owing to its exceptional soundproofing and sound absorption capabilities9. Approximately 800,000 tons of glass wool trash are produced each year in Europe7. This waste mostly comes from the glass wool industry and development and demolition projects. When waste glass wool is substituted for aggregate, waste glass wool can be acquired at a low cost or for free, which results in a reduction in production costs. These expenses, as well as the usage of potentially dangerous storage and disposal techniques, may be mitigated by their incorporation into concrete. Furthermore, although cement manufacture results in substantial CO₂ emissions, the use of glass wool waste as a replacement material lowers overall emissions. There are many positive aspects of incorporating waste glass wool into concrete, including its enhanced mechanical and physical characteristics, reduced environmental impact, and financial benefits. The use of this material in concrete has been the subject of several recent studies for the reasons stated above.

According to the published studies, waste glass wool has been extensively researched. An investigation was carried out by Gowri and Mary10. This research aimed to enhance the strength of concrete at a lower cost by including glass wool fiber, as compared with ordinary concrete. The compressive and tensile strengths of the material, were investigated by changing the proportion of fiber from 0.025% to 0.075% of the weight of the concrete. Increasing the glass wool content in concrete from 0.025% to 0.075% by total weight leads to an increase in compressive strength from 5.15% to 15.68% after 28 days. Ahmed et al.11 performed another investigation. In this research, glass wool fiber was added to the concrete, while copper slag was used as a replacement for the fine aggregate. The study investigated the impact of different percentages of glass wool fiber (ranging from 0.2% to 0.8% of the total weight of concrete) on the strength characteristics of concrete. This was achieved by replacing 40% of the fine aggregate with copper slag. The results revealed that the inclusion of glass wool fibers at a concentration of 0.2%, led to superior performance when compared with the control concrete. Specifically, the compressive strength increased by 6%, while the split tensile strength increased by 6% for the first mixture and 10% for the second mixtures, respectively. However, increasing the specific percentage decreased the strength parameter. Olutoge and Oluwaseun12 experimentally studied the influence of glass wool fiber (GWF) on the residual flexural strength (RFS) of conventionally reinforced concrete elements. Further experiments were performed on cube and cylindrical specimens to investigate the impact of the fibers on concrete including compressive and tensile strength tests. The results demonstrated a substantial 20.83% increase in the tensile strength when the fiber content was increased to 0.50%. Additionally, a 0.26% increase in compressive strength was observed when the fiber dose was increased to 0.25%. The effectiveness of self-compacting mortar under ambient conditions and at higher temperatures of 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C was examined by Bala et al.13 in terms of using heat-resistant paint and glass wool fiber reinforcements,. A total of sixteen mix designs were created for the use of self-compacting mortar, with 25% fly ash. These mix designs included the incorporation of glass wool fiber reinforcement at different percentages, ranging from 0% to 1.5% of the weight of cement. Additionally, the effective water-to-cement (w/c) ratio was varied throughout the mix designs, with values of 0.43, 0.49, 0.55, and 0.70. Upon concluding the investigation, it was observed that the addition of glass wool fiber and the reduction of the effective water-to-cement ratio resulted in a drop in the compressive strength of the self-compacting mortar. However, the addition of glass-wood fibers had a favorable influence on the flexural strength. Machado et al.14 performed another investigation. In this investigation, it was determined whether the incorporation of fibers made from sugarcane bagasse and/or glass wool into concrete affected the mechanical behavior of the concrete. Characterizations of the morphology, chemistry, and physical states were performed. With the addition of only 10% glass wool to concrete, it was demonstrated that the compressive strength, flexural tensile strength, and diametrical compression tensile strength of the material increased to 34.22, 6.65, and 16.44 MPa, respectively. Olutoge15 conducted another investigation to examine the impact of glass wool fiber on the strength characteristics of hollow concrete blocks. The mix was supplemented with glass wool fiber at varying proportions of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. Subsequently, a compressive strength test was conducted. The study determined that the compressive strength of the hollow concrete block progressively increased and peaked with a 15% addition of glass-wool fiber, resulting in an overall 33% increase. Additionally, the weight of the block decreased by 1.6%. Pamu and SVSNDL16 empirically investigated the production of clay bricks and explored the effects of using glass wool as a substitute. Bricks injected with various percentages of glass wool were examined for mechanical, physical, and durability properties. According to these tests, the bricks containing 4% glass wool exhibited the highest compressive strength and the lowest heat conductivity compared to control bricks. Jaber17 investigated the potential integration of glass wool (fiberglass insulation) with cement mortar as a construction material for insulation. A mixture of conventional Portland cement and sand was prepared at a ratio of 1:3. Glass wool was then added to the mixture at different weight proportions ranging from 0 to 0.24 wt%. Results indicated that although adding glass wool reduced the density, heat conductivity, and compressive strength of the cement mortar, it increased its flexural strength. With the aim of improving the mechanical properties of concrete, Kim et al.18 proposed the use of waste glass wool as an additive. In accordance with the experimental findings, glass wool was found to have a low level of pozzolanic activity a positive impact on both compressive and flexural strength. This experimental study determined that a volumetric addition of 0.5% in the concrete mix was optimal. To investigate the effectiveness of glass-wool residue in cement-based composites, Defáveri et al.19 conducted a performance study. Experiments were carried out on mortars and pastes, in which 25% of Portland cement was substituted with ground glass wool residue. The results of the compressive strength tests indicated that the residue and reference mortars performed similarly,, with only a 2% decrease in compressive strength. Additionally, the use of ground glass wool residue increased the efficiency of Portland cement production, lowering the amount of natural resources used, and the level of CO2 emissions associated with clinker production. The other investigation was performed by Pamu et al.20. Using sand cat swarm optimization and progressive graph convolutional networks, this studyintroduces a unique hybrid approach to predict the mechanical characteristics of glass-wool-fiber-reinforced concrete. The main objective was to optimize the composition of glass wool fiber reinforced concrete by precisely predicting its compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths. Experiments were conducted on concrete compositions of the M20 and M30 classes, that contained glass wool fibers (0.5%–3%) and 30% ground granulated blast furnace slag replacement for this purpose. This research found that glass wool and crushed granulated blast furnace slag increase the material’s strength and insulation, making it a feasible option for ecologically benign and energy-efficient construction material. Machado et al.21 sought to assess the potential of ground glass wool waste as an additional cementitious component in cement mortar applications. To achieve this objective, cylindrical and prismatic specimens were employed to generate cement samples that replace 0.0%, 10.0%, 17.5%, and 25.0% of the cement with pulverized glass fiber. It was observed that the utilization of this supplementary cementitious material led to a decrease in the heat of hydration of the cement mortar, as well as a reduction in CO2 emissions by approximately 15.5% in comparison to the reference experiment.

There are not many studies on the strength of glass wool in concrete. In addition to these studies, the following are available in the literature22–40. A survey of previous research indicates that there aren’t many studies that specifically address the proportion of glass wool fibers in concrete. Moreover, hardly any research exists examining the impact of temperature. This research is motivated by this gap in the literature. Comprehensive information is provided in the following sections. This research focused on investigating the increasing use of glass wool waste during construction, specifically in relation to the amount of aggregate in the concrete mixture. To achieve this goal, a number of laboratory investigations were conducted to investigate the factors that influence the compressive, tensile, and flexural strength of concrete. Concrete samples were produced in the laboratory by adding various percentages of waste glass wool; they were then exposed to various temperatures. Six distinct groups of waste glass-wool-added test concretes with varying amounts of 0%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% were designed and manufactured.

Experimental program

In this investigation, glass wool waste at six different ratios was used in place of coarse aggregate across various temperatures. The studies were conducted at the laboratory of Necmettin Erbakan University in the Department of Construction. The ideal range for employing glass wool, as studied in the literature9,41, is from 0% to 5% when replacing cement and from 5% to 15% when replacing aggregate. Decreases in density and strength of approximately 10% were observed in aggregate in the literature. For these reasons in this study, glass wool was added to concrete samples at proportions ranging from 1% to 5% of the volume of coarse aggregate. A total of 144 sample molds were filled with 48 cylindrical, 48 cubic, and 48 prismatic samples. Two of each set of samples were collected and placed individually in an oven at 400, 600, or 800 °C.

Test methods

The researchers conducted a series of laboratory experiments to investigate the elements that influence the compressive and tensile strength of concrete and assess the impact. To achieve this objective, laboratory-produced concrete samples were prepared using different proportions of waste glass wool and were subjected to various temperatures. The 28-day crushing outcomes of the samples were then analyzed. In total, six distinct groups of test concrete with added waste glass wool were created. A total of 144 sample molds were filled in each of the sets, ranging from 0% to 5% waste glass wool. These molds included eight cylindrical, eight cubic, and eight prismatic samples. A total of six samples (two from each group) were individually placed in ovens set at temperatures of 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. It was observed that the increase in the mixing percentage from 1% to 5% led to a decrease in the concrete compressive strength. The findings from the investigations published in the literature appear to be comparable. In practice, the packing and alignment of shorter fibers are more challenging than those of longer fibers17,43. Therefore, the mixture filled with short fibers leads to an increase in the number of faults, which in turn leads to a decrease in both the density and compressive strength of the finished product17,43. There was a general need for a greater quantity of water in mixes that included fibers than those without fibers17. The increase in the amount of glass wool causes a decrease in the hydration action of cement mixtures17,43,44.

The primary reason for the reduction in the compressive strength of concrete of glass wool waste is that it is a low-density and highly porous material. Consequently, the glass wool fibers themselves are unable to contribute to load carrying, but they establish a “weak zone” in the concrete. Furthermore, glass wool often has a hydrophobic surface that makes it difficult to form a strong chemical connection with cement paste. As a result, cement hydration products (C-S-H gels) have difficulty sticking to the surface of glass fibers. Concrete’s compressive strength is reduced as a consequence of the micro-voids and debonding caused by this poor adhesion at the fiber-matrix contact17,45. This might be considered the detrimental impact of glass fiber addition on CS.

Raising the proportion of the mixture from 1% to 5% resulted in an increase in the FS of the concrete. The impact of temperature is contrary to expectations with respect to the FS strength of concrete. When the weight% of glass wool increased, the flexural strength increased slightly, as shown in Fig. 9. Similar findings were observed in previous research17. One possible explanation for this behavior is that the specimens tested in the flexural test are exposed to tension more than compression17. As a result, the fibers of glass wool can withstand tensile strength greater than that of the cement mortar matrix, which, in turn, leads to an increase in effective crack energy10,17. The addition of glass wool to the cement mortar increased plastic deformation, partially absorbed energy, and lengthened the fracture propagation route17. Consequently, the increase in the percentage of glass wool in the cement mortar improved both the fracture energy and the flexural strength.

Glass wool fibers function as microfibers that are dispersed within the matrix phase of concrete, bridging fracture surfaces as the concrete begins to crack under load. In this way, the concrete may absorb energy and undergo greater deformation before breaking by delaying or rerouting the spread of cracks. This leads to enhanced ductility and load-bearing capability, thus augmenting flexural strength. Furthermore, the mechanical development of microcracks within the concrete is inhibited by glass wool fibers, which distribute stresses at the crack ends. This is especially useful for tensile regions subjected to flexural stresses, enhancing the FS value9,25. This can be called the positive effect of glass fiber addition on FS.

The relationship between compressive strength and flexural strength is given in Fig. 11. The compressive strength clearly decreases, as the sample’s flexural strength rises. The main reason for this is that glass wool waste reduces the compressive strength of concrete due to its low density and highly porous material, On the other hand, in FS behavior, glass wool fibers function as microfibers dispersed within the concrete’s matrix phase, bridging fracture surfaces when the concrete begins to crack under load. This allows concrete to absorb energy and undergo greater deformation before fracture by delaying and/or redirecting crack propagation. This, in turn, increases ductility and load-carrying capacity, thereby increasing flexural strength.

FE-SEM analysis

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis results hold critical importance in determining the morphological characteristics of materials48. These analyses provide a detailed examination of the sample’s surface structure, particle size, and distribution, thereby revealing the material’s microstructural properties. In this way, they offer valuable insights into the material’s durability, bonding capacity, and overall performance. The FE-SEM images in Fig. 12 reveal that the addition of glass wool creates various effects on the concrete matrix structure. It can be observed that the glass wool fibers are well distributed within the concrete and achieve good bonding. Glass wool fibers appearing in irregular clusters suggest that ensuring fiber homogeneity within the concrete matrix may not be challenging49. Additionally, microstructures concentrated around the fibers may contribute to enhancing the material’s mechanical strength. However, pores and irregular particles could form weak points affecting concrete strength. This analysis may be useful for understanding the potential effects of waste glass wool additives on the mechanical properties of concrete. In the image shown in Fig. 12a, the glass wool fibers appear to be oriented longitudinally and arranged in a specific order. The parallel alignment of the fibers indicates that they are likely distributed evenly within the material, contributing to crack control. While the fiber distribution within the structure reduces compressive strength, it appears to increase flexural strength. In Fig. 12b, the image obtained at moderate magnification allows for a more detailed examination of the matrix surrounding the fibers. Fine particles or microstructures are observed around the fibers, and a dense bonding is noted, which may enhance the material’s durability. In Fig. 12c, the image at a higher magnification provides a detailed view of the surface structure of particles within the matrix. Irregular particles and pores are observed in the microstructure of the concrete. This irregularity may reflect the non-uniform structure of the concrete matrix or the voids formed during the hydration process. Such voids or irregularities could negatively impact the mechanical strength of the concrete and reduce its durability. Figure 12d shows an image captured at the highest magnification, revealing much finer structures within the matrix and structural details at the nanometer scale. In this image, gel-like structures, hydration products, and very fine, branched structures are observed. Such structures indicate the presence of binding hydration products such as calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H). Glass wool fibers interact with aggregates and the binder, increasing strength50. These products could contribute to the durability of the concrete forming a binding layer between the glass wool fibers and the matrix.

EDX analysis

The Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) method identifies the elements that constitute a substance by measuring the energy distribution generated from the X-ray emissions of the material51. The analysis of the samples was conducted using EDX in conjunction with FE-SEM. According to the analysis results in Fig. 13, oxygen (O) is the most abundant element, with a weight% of 50.86% and an atomic percentage of 69.58%. This high oxygen content indicates the presence of oxide compounds in the sample, particularly silicates and calcium oxide. Calcium (Ca) is the second most common element, with a weight% of 29.8% and an atomic percentage of 16.28%. This significant amount of calcium suggests that the sample contains calcium-based compounds, especially calcium-containing phases such as C–S–H, which are common in cementitious structures. Statically analysis

Taguchi analysis

The Taguchi analysis results presented in the graph in Fig. 15 reveal the effects of varying waste glass wool (WGW) replacement at different temperatures (24 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C) on the mechanical performance of concrete. Signal-to-Noise Ratio (S/N) is a criterion that considers the desired (useful) variability in experimental results as “signal” and the undesirable (random, uncontrollable) variability as “noise.” The Taguchi method aims to make the system more stable and robust by reducing the effects of “noise” in the process. The evaluation of SN ratios (“larger is better”) shows which parameters are more advantageous in terms of concrete strength. According to the results, SN ratios decrease in all series as the temperature increases. This indicates that high temperatures negatively affect the mechanical properties of concrete and that strength loss reaches its most pronounced level at 800 °C. On the other hand, increasing the WGW substitution provided a partial improvement in strength. Specifically, a 5% WGW addition showed the highest SN ratios at all temperature levels and yielded optimal results in terms of strength. This effect can be explained by WGW acting as a micro-reinforcement, limiting crack propagation and partially preserving structural integrity under high temperatures. Furthermore, while the effect of WGW addition is limited at low temperatures (24 °C), the difference becomes more pronounced with a 3–5% WGW addition at high temperatures. This indicates that WGW contributes particularly to high-temperature resistance.

Conclusion

The primary objective of this research was to examine the extent to which the use of glass wool waste during construction is related to the quantity of aggregate used in the concrete mixture. Concrete samples were manufactured in a controlled environment by adding different percentages of glass wool waste to the mix and then subjecting the samples to different temperatures. Different proportions of waste glass wool from 1% to 5% relative to the volume of coarse aggregate were used to make concrete samples. Additionally, samples were separated and placed in the furnaces to 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. As a result of the experimental studies, the following findings emerged.

The workability of freshly mixed concrete decreased significantly as the quantity of glass wool increased. This reduction was observed in new concrete combinations of 4% and 5%.

Furthermore, when placed in an oven, it was observed to diminish the strength of the concrete. Increasing the mixing ratio from 1% to 5% reduced the concrete compressive strength by 4.26%, 7.84%, 11.87%, 14.24%, and 17.27% at different points.
