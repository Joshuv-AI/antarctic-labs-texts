Effects of stiffness-altered compression garments on lower-limb angular kinematics and force-time characteristics in countermovement jumps
Authors: Cheuk Yin Ho, Raymond Kim Wai Sum, Yijian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompression garments (CGs) with localized stiffness alterations are marketed to enhance athletic performance, yet evidence for benefits in jumping remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of stiffness-altered CGs on lower-limb angular kinematics and force-time characteristics in countermovement jumps (CMJs).Methods62 physically active adults (31 female, 31 male) performed CMJs under four conditions: control (shorts only), knee sleeves with stiffness components, compression leggings without stiffness alterations, and stiffness-altered compression leggings. Sagittal-plane angles were recorded via motion capture, and vertical ground reaction forces via force platforms. Two-way (sex*condition) ANOVA were performed for derived variables, employing both discrete and one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping (SPM) analyses.ResultsBoth leggings conditions significantly restricted hip range of motion (81-82° vs. non-leggings 85°, p = 0.001) and peak extension velocity (455-461°/s vs. non-leggings 512°/s, p < 0.001) relative to non-leggings conditions. SPM further revealed phase-specific effects, such as slower hip flexion during early unweighing phase (p < 0.001) in stiffness-altered leggings. Knee and ankle kinematics were not affected. In addition, stiffness-altered leggings reduced countermovement speed (1.37 m/s vs. control 1.43 m/s, p = 0.018) and braking impulse (1.37 N s/kg vs. control 1.43 N s/kg, p = 0.017) but did not impair jumping height (∼0.3 m) and propulsive impulse (∼2.5 N s/kg). No sex-by-condition interactions were observed. No significant differences were found between the two non-leggings conditions, nor between the two leggings conditions.ConclusionDespite no detectable performance decline, compression leggings primarily restricted hip motion and altered eccentric phase characteristics in CMJs, likely diminishing stretch-shortening cycle efficiency. To promote beneficial effects of stiffness-altered CGs, future study designs shall control for garment fitting via compression and stiffness measurements, as well as individual variations in skill levels via more specific and representative sampling.Keywords: Eccentric contraction, Plyometric exercise, Range of motion, Sports apparel, Stretch-shortening cycle

Introduction

Compression garments (CGs) apply mechanical pressure on their underlying tissues1 and are receiving increasing attention in both sports and research settings. Over half non-endurance athletes indicated sports performance enhancement as a major reason for using CGs, with over 70% of them perceiving actual performance benefits.2 In contrast, minimal-to-none benefits of CGs on non-endurance athletic tasks, including jumps, sprints and changes-of-direction were recently reported.3 It is therefore tempting for both researchers and CG manufacturers to narrow down such expectation gap.

With claimed effects of enhanced muscle support,4 some manufacturers attempted to make localized stiffness changes on CGs by adopting different knitting and weaving methods.5 Specifically, binding pattern and loop density of the body yarn will determine the textile stiffness in specific regions.6,7 Some other studies incorporated stiffness components by attaching tension bands on designated regions, such as behind the thigh8 or around the knee.4 These stiffness-altered CGs consistently restricted the hip range of motion (ROM) across different movement tasks, such as walking,5 jumping4,8 and rapid change-of-direction.9 Wannop and colleagues measured passive hip moment and reported significant increases with stiffness alterations,8 yet such differences were not detected in dynamic tasks.4,9 Even in the absence of regional designs, the buttock and thigh regions, but not the knee region, experienced the greatest pressure change when both hip and knee are flexed.10 This may support the notion that hip motion is more susceptible to localized stiffness alterations than the knee. In short, it is evident that stiffness-altered CGs affected different movement patterns, especially at the hip, but their specific contributions towards performance enhancement are not fully understood.

Countermovement jump (CMJ) tests are getting increasingly popular in both applied and research settings,11 providing the most reliable and valid assessment for lower limb explosive strength.12 Unlike cyclical, locomotive tasks (e.g., walking and running) performed at submaximal intensities, CMJ is a discrete task that demands effective utilization of the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) of both legs to maximize vertical rate of force development.13 With the aid of force platforms, CMJs offer additional insights into lower limb coordination,14 asymmetry,15 neuromuscular fatigue16 and training adaptation monitoring.17 A recent review synthesized 41 studies on the effect of CGs on jumping performance and reported inconsistent findings, with mostly none or few beneficial effects.18 Many of these studies focused on jump heights; few adopted force platform-measured force-time characteristics, such as countermovement depth,19 power20 and forces.21 For stiffness-altered CGs, only Wannop and colleagues reported improved jump height, measured with Vertec device, under the “best” stiffness and compression conditions,8 hence necessitating further research on how CMJ force-time characteristics may explain such performance enhancement. In terms of joint kinematics, localized stiffness alterations may increase the pressure changes with body positions,6 hence the overall resistance to the movement of specific covered joints.22 This study focuses on sagittal plane kinematics only, because: (i) sagittal range of motion (ROM) in CMJs is noticeably larger than those on non-sagittal planes; and (ii) sagittal-plane kinematics explain much larger proportion of jump height variations23 while non-sagittal plane ones do not.24 Clearly, despite the growing trend of localized alterations in the mechanical properties (i.e., stiffness) of sports CGs, the current biomechanics literature lacks comprehensive documentation of how this emerging type of CGs affects the CMJ patterns.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of stiffness-altered CGs on lower-limb angular kinematics and force-time characteristics in CMJs, using both the traditional 0-D (discrete) and 1-D SPM approaches. Traditional 0-D approach may simplify statistical analyses and provide clear, practically meaningful outcome measures,25 though relying solely on discrete points might omit nuanced variations between peak values.26,27 To complement these metrics, statistical parametric mapping (SPM), an alternative 1-D approach, accounts for differences in time-series data throughout the whole jumping movement. Used in tandem with traditional 0-D variables, integrating 1-D continuous analyses offers greater sensitivity to phase-by phase alterations28 and hence enriches the overall interpretation of movement pattern changes.25,29 Miyashita and colleagues studied joint kinetics at different key gait phases and observed phase-specific reduction,5 reinforcing the advantages of supplementing comparisons of discrete peak values with phase-by-phase analyses.

It was hypothesized that stiffness-altered CGs would: (i) restrict hip movements at end-ranges; (ii) no difference in knee and ankle kinematics; and (iii) improve performance-related force-time characteristics around the lowest squat position. Hypotheses (i) and (ii) grounded on consistent observations on many other movement patterns,4,5,8,9 and greater pressure changes around the hip joint as it flexes10; hypothesis (iii) grounded on the CMJ performance enhancement reported by Wannop and colleagues,8 and very strong (up to 90%) associations between force-time characteristics and CMJ performance.30

Sex differences in jumping biomechanics have been widely documented.31,32 To ensure the applicability of our findings to female garment users and to mitigate sex biases in cohorts included in sports-related CG research,18,33 this study adopted a 1:1 mixed cohort design. A secondary aim was to identify potential sex-condition interaction. Considering the lack of similar designs and our previous findings,9 we conservatively made a no-interaction hypothesis, indicating that stiffness alterations should operationally exert similar physical constraints regardless of the biological sex of the participant.

Materials & methods

Participants

Sixty-two physically active adults (31 male and 31 female) participated in this study (age = 21.9 ± 2.2 years; height = 170.1 ± 7.1 cm; mass = 62.2 ± 9.2 kg). Inclusion criteria included: (i) aged 18-30; (ii) body mass index (BMI) 18.5-24.9 kg/m2; and (iii) at least 120-min weekly exercise participation; while exclusion criteria included (i) history of lower-limb surgery or injuries that prevented sports participation for more than three weeks in the past six months; and (ii) any neuromuscular or musculoskeletal disorder that affected task performance. The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (Ref. #: SBRE-21-0681). All participants provided written informed consent.

Previous studies consistently reported hip motion restrictions in stiffness-altered CGs,4,5,8,9 while peak hip extension velocity is moderately predictive of CMJ performance.34 The effect size for peak hip extension velocity, 0.26, was adopted from the only previous study on CMJs in stiffness-altered CGs so far.8 Assuming a conservative effect size of 0.25, α = 0.05 and power = 0.95, a sample size of n = 36 for ANOVA was calculated using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.6). A previous retrospective study on biomechanical waveforms demonstrated that it may require up to 20 more participants to achieve the same statistical power for 1-D SPM analysis, when compared to 0-D discrete analysis.35 Thus, retaining the full cohort of 62 participants in our previous study9 sustained an appropriate sample size for both discrete and continuous analyses.

Experimental procedure

Body height and mass were measured upon arrival. The researcher then randomized the trial order with shuffled cards. Conditions are as follow: (i) shorts only as control (CON); (ii) knee sleeves (KS) with stiffness components (Gym Aesthetics, #20FWA048ACC); (iii) compression leggings without stiffness alterations as placebo (PB) (Gym Aesthetics, #19SSM036LGG for male; #19FWF046LGG for female); and (4) compression leggings (CL) with stiffness alterations (Gym Aesthetics, #19FWM063LGG for male; #19FWF064LGG for female) (Fig. 1). Linear stiffness of each garment was tested in accordance with the ASTM Standard Test Method for Tension and Elongation of Elastic Fabrics (ASTM D4964-96(2020)),36 in transverse direction at the patella level (point E),6 and reported in the caption of Fig. 1. Participants get accustomed to each condition with a 5-min standardized warm-up.9 16 reflective markers were then attached according to the Plug-in Gait Lower Body Model. Flexion/extension angles for lower-limb joints were captured using an 11-camera optoelectronic system (Vicon Bonita 10) at 100 Hz, and vertical ground reaction forces (GRF) were collected using two AMTI force platforms (BMS400600) at 1000 Hz.

Countermovement jump (CMJ)

A standard CMJ instruction, “lower as quickly as possible, jump as high as possible, and return to standing after landing”,37 was provided to each participant along with demonstration by the researcher. Arms were crossed in front of the chest. Successful trials required: (i) at least 1-s quiet standing before the jump; (ii) landing with one foot completely on each force platform; and (iii) returning to standing after landing. At most three familiarization trials for each condition were allowed. All participants completed three successful trials with at least 30-s between-trial rests.

Data processing

Marker trajectories and vertical GRF data were filtered with low-pass, fourth-order Butterworth filter at cut-off frequencies of 10 Hz and 50 Hz, respectively.37,38 Lower-limb joint flexion/extension angles were calculated in the Vicon Nexus software based on the Plug-in Gait Lower Body Model. Angular velocity-time curves were derived as the first derivative of the corresponding angle-time curves.

Jump onset was defined when vertical GRF drops below 97.5% of the bodyweight; while takeoff and landing were respectively defined when vertical GRF drops below and exceeds 20 N.37 The centre-of-mass (COM) acceleration-time curve was derived from the vertical GRF-time curve using the formula: acceleration = (GRF – bodyweight)/body mass, and then integrated over time to obtain the velocity-time curve. Time-to-takeoff was the duration between jump onset and takeoff, while flight time was the duration between takeoff and landing. Braking phase was defined from peak downward velocity to zero velocity at the bottom of the countermovement, while propulsive phase began when COM velocity became positive and ended at takeoff.11 Jump height was estimated using the impulse-momentum method: jumping height = (takeoff velocity)2/(2 × 9.81), while the countermovement depth was estimated using the double-integration method.11 Forces and impulses were normalized to bodyweight and body mass, respectively.

Time series were trimmed from jump onset to takeoff and normalized to the longest trial (130 and 1302 data points for joint kinematics and GRF data, respectively) using simple cubic spline interpolation.25 These data were processed with customized MATLAB scripts.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for discrete variables were performed using SPSS (Version 26.0). Mixed two-way ANOVA were performed to evaluate the effects of condition (within-subject factor) and sex (between-subject factor) on these variables and their possible interactions. Analyses for time series data were performed with the open-source package “spm-1d” (www.spm1d.org) using MATLAB (Version R2023a).39 Similarly, 1-D SPM mixed two-way ANOVA were performed to evaluate the effects of condition and sex on continuous data and possible interactions. Significance level was set at α = 0.05. Post-hoc comparisons were performed with Bonferroni corrections.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of stiffness-altered CGs on CMJ force-time characteristics and lower limb kinematics. Our results showed that the both stiffness-altered and placebo leggings restricted hip ROM and extension speed, with phase-specific effects (e.g., less hip flexion during mid-propulsive phase) revealed via SPM. While this study intended to understand the combined effects of compression and stiffness alterations, our findings alone did not detect changes beyond the effects of compression alone, which is consistent with our previous study.9 Discrete force variables reduced under leggings conditions despite no overall impact on the jumping height. No sex interaction was detected.

Consistent with Wannop and colleagues,8 stiffness-altered compression leggings restricted hip sagittal ROM and reduced peak hip extension speeds. Our restrictions were less pronounced, as stiffness alterations were not achieved by fixing additional stiffness bands. SPM analysis further revealed consistent ROM restrictions by the stiffness-altered compression leggings during early unweighing phase (0.0%-3.85%), potentially indicative of suboptimal fitting on pelvis orientation during stance and partially supporting hypothesis (i). ROM restrictions at the other end (maximal flexion) were less consistent, as reflected by the greater variability in our results (Fig. 2C).

Intuitively, complete joint coverage resisted relevant joint movements, especially for movements requiring large ROM.22 Both knee and hip joints were covered under the leggings conditions; however, knee ROM displayed no significant difference among conditions, despite greater ROM. It is not surprising that ankle kinematics did not change, as in hypothesis (ii), as it is not covered in any conditions.22 Clearly, several factors underpinned the effects of garment coverage on joint movements. Wang and colleagues10 mapped lower-limb skin deformation under yoga postures and reported the greatest skin stretches around the lower buttock and kneecap. Large curvatures around the kneecap may cause unfit between the garment and the skin, and hence limited the effects of CGs on knee kinematics in our study. In contrast, the round buttock region provided large contact area, and hence was more susceptible to compression and stiffness alterations as the garment stretches.10

A recent study revealed reduced hip flexion angles in compressive shorts during mid-propulsive phase, too.40 Slower hip flexion and extension under our leggings conditions could possibly result from reduced muscle contraction, in terms of rate and magnitude, during the eccentric phase. Consequently, SSC mechanisms, such as elastic energy use and stretch reflexes, were hampered.41 While compression could stimulate mechanoreceptors and boost performance through enhanced sensory feedback,40,42 faster extension could unlikely explain the reduced hip flexion during propulsion in our study. Apparently, effects of pressure applied on hip extension remained inconsistent8,43 and could present an interplay between enhanced sensorimotor feedback and hampered SSCs.

Indicated by reduced peak countermovement speeds and other force variables during the braking phase, the studied leggings obstructed the eccentric components of CMJs, followed by smaller peak propulsive forces. Hence, hypothesis (iii), improved performance-related force-time characteristics, was not supported. Consistent with the impulse-momentum method, jump heights, propulsive impulses and takeoff speeds consistently demonstrated no significant difference. Peak propulsive force reductions could be confounded by reduced countermovement speed, as such peaks often develop early in the propulsive phase.44 These observations aligned with previous explanations regarding the hip motion, provided the significant contributions of hip joint torques to CMJ performance.34

Nonetheless, slower hip extensions did not translate to performance declines. This could be attributed to different coordination strategies requiring different joint outputs, despite unchanged jumping heights 44, 45, 46. Our participants’ jumping skill levels and training loads were not controlled. They underperformed a previous cohort32 by more than 10 cm. Relatively low skill levels and training loads could present high coordination variability,47,48 masking potential performance decrement by slow hip extensions. Consistent with Wang and colleagues,40 our study revealed no significant difference in GRF-derived time series. This can be explained by: (i) misalignment of peaks due to individual timing variations and time normalization that prevented direct comparisons25; and (ii) smaller statistical power of SPM for the same sample size, compared to discrete analyses.35 Hence, only changes in peak hip extension speed with large effect size (η2p = 0.338) were detected.

As hypothesized, sex-condition interactions were not observed, indicating that the condition effects do not depend on sex differences, and vice versa. Consistent with previous studies,31,32 female participants presented smaller values of force variables. Our SPM analyses identified additional sex differences in COM displacement around its lowest position (59.2%-85.1%) and velocity during early braking phase (40.6%-52.4%), compared to previous SPM studies that only reported differences at the propulsive phase.49,50 These findings must be interpreted with caution as the compression garments and more conservative jumping approaches among our participants may confound the results. Further studies towards sex-specific adaptations to different sports CG designs are warranted.

This study has several limitations: First, physically active participants had diverse sports training backgrounds, introducing heterogeneity in jumping performance. Second, despite adequate warm-ups, some findings (e.g., altered pelvis orientation during stance) may reflect acute effects of our CGs, with the long-term effects remain unclear. Third, ordinal garment sizing introduced varying degrees of compression and stiffness among participants. The absence of in vivo compression and stiffness measurements limited the interpretability of our results and prevented us from elucidating any additional effects of stiffness alterations on the participants’ movement patterns, if present.

Conclusion

Our discrete analyses demonstrated that compression leggings, irrespective of stiffness alterations, restricted hip sagittal plane motion and reduced both speeds and forces during the eccentric phase. In addition to slower hip extensions, early unweighing phase-specific alterations (e.g., slower hip flexions) were identified via SPM, suggesting that compression leggings may influence movement strategies through less effective stretch-shortening cycles. Knee and ankle kinematics were not affected, which may be attributed to large curvatures around the kneecap and the uncovered ankle that limit stiffness changes with joint movements. The absence of detectable differences between compression leggings with and without stiffness-alterations suggested that compression itself, rather than localized stiffness alterations, drove the observed alterations in kinematics and force-time characteristics. Variations in garment fit and individual jump coordination may also confound our results. No sex-by-condition interactions were found, implying similar biomechanical responses to CGs across sexes. Future research should incorporate direct compression and stiffness measurements, explore long-term adaptations, and examine more specific athletic populations to better elucidate potential benefits of stiffness-altered designs for athletic performance.
