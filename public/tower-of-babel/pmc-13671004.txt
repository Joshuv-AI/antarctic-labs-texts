Disseminated Mixed Lytic‐Sclerotic Skeletal Metastases as Initial Presentation of Gallbladder Carcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Shivali Rao, Binaya Adhikari, Abhikanta Khatiwada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGallbladder carcinoma is an aggressive malignancy often diagnosed at an advanced stage. Skeletal metastasis, especially mixed lytic‐sclerotic lesions, is rare and typically occurs late. This report highlights an unusual presentation where widespread bony metastases were the initial manifestation, leading to the diagnosis of gallbladder carcinoma.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most common type of biliary tract cancer and the sixth most common form of digestive tract malignancy [1]. GBC ranks as the 23rd most common cancer in males and the 20th in females worldwide (both sexes combined: 22nd). In 2022 (updated from 2020 estimates), there were approximately 122,491 new cases globally. Incidence shows marked geographical and ethnic variation, with the highest age‐standardized rates (per 100,000) in countries like Bolivia, Chile, Bangladesh, Nepal, South Korea, and others in Latin America and parts of Asia. Asia accounts for ~72% of global GBC cases, but presentation tends to be earlier than the global average, especially in high‐incidence South Asian countries. In India and adjoining countries (including Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh—the highest‐burden areas in Asia), the mean age is ~51 ± 11 years (roughly a decade younger than in Western countries, where it is ~71.2 ± 12.5 years) [2, 3, 4].

GBC arises from the epithelial lining of the gallbladder and cystic duct. Adenocarcinomas are the most common histological type, with squamous, adenosquamous, and neuroendocrine tumors being less common. GBC is more common in women [5]. The etiopathogenesis of GBC is a multistep process involving the combination of genetic predisposition, chronic inflammatory insults (from stones, infection, and toxins), and environmental carcinogen exposure, with regional variations (e.g., higher pollution‐linked risks in South Asia) explaining geographic hotspots. Gallstones remain the strongest and most consistently identified risk factor for GBC, present in 70%–90% of cases and associated with a 4–5‐fold (or higher) increased relative risk, primarily through induction of chronic inflammation of the gallbladder mucosa [6, 7]. Other risk factors for GBC include female gender, obesity, anatomical anomalies (abnormal pancreaticobiliary duct junction and biliary cysts), Porcelain gallbladder, Mirizzi's syndrome, gallbladder polyps, pancreatic bile reflux, smoking, use of recycled oil, and multiparity [6, 8].

The symptoms of GBC are quite vague and can overlap with other benign conditions. Clinical presentations include pain in the upper right side of the abdomen, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, weight loss, loss of appetite, ascites, and the presence of an abdominal mass [9]. It is often discovered incidentally when the GB is removed due to benign issues like chronic cholecystitis or gallstones [10].

GBC is an aggressive malignancy characterized by early invasion of adjacent organs, regional lymphatic spread, and involvement of major blood vessels and the common bile duct [1]. Bone metastasis is the least common manifestation of GBC; however, the incidence can be as high as 10%, as evidenced in an autopsy series [11]. The presence of bone metastasis is an independent and adverse prognostic factor in patients with GBC [11]. Preoperative staging plays a pivotal role in optimizing resectability rates [12].

This article aims to highlight the rare presentation of disseminated mixed lytic‐sclerotic metastasis as the initial manifestation of underlying GBC.

Case History/Examination

A 57‐year‐old female with no known comorbidities presented to the orthopedic outpatient department with a one‐year history of low back pain. The pain was continuous, mild to moderate in intensity, and non‐radiating. There was no history of trauma or fever. She also denied abdominal pain, bloating, constipation, hematuria, yellowish discoloration of the eyes or skin, vaginal discharge or bleeding, or the presence of an abdominal lump. Her family history was unremarkable. Vital parameters were within normal limits. Physical examination revealed diffuse spinal tenderness and mild icterus. Neurological examination was unremarkable, and abdominal examination did not reveal any palpable masses.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Routine blood investigations showed a low hemoglobin level of 10.5 g/dL, with white blood cell and platelet counts within normal limits. Other blood tests were unremarkable, except for significantly elevated blood alkaline phosphatase levels (ALP) of 1769 U/L and gamma‐glutamyl transferase levels of 513 U/L. Serum calcium and phosphate levels were within normal limits. An X‐ray of the lumbosacral spine, performed at another facility, showed predominantly sclerotic lesions involving multiple vertebrae. In a middle‐aged female presenting with chronic low back pain and radiological evidence of multiple vertebral lesions, several differential diagnoses were considered, including metastatic bone disease (from primary malignancies such as breast, lung, thyroid, etc.), multiple myeloma, Paget's disease, and lymphoma.

Further imaging with contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the neck, chest, and abdomen (Figures 1 and 2) revealed chronic cholecystitis with GB calculi, and an enhancing polypoidal soft tissue mass within the GB lumen. The mass showed suspicious loss of fat planes with the pylorus of the stomach and the D1 segment of the duodenum, along with indistinct fat planes with segment IVb of the liver. Contiguous extension into the common hepatic duct was noted, with enhancing wall thickening, causing dilation of the common hepatic duct and bilateral intrahepatic biliary radicals. Heterogeneous lymphadenopathy was observed in the porta hepatis, porto‐caval, aorto‐caval, and peripancreatic regions. Additionally, multiple mixed lytic‐sclerotic lesions were identified in the body and posterior elements of almost all vertebrae, as well as in the pelvis, bilateral hips, scapulae, ribs, shoulder joints, sternum, clavicles, skull, mandible, and visualized femur and humerus. No obvious pathological fractures were noted. No evidence of distant metastases to the lungs or other abdominal regions was noted. A provisional diagnosis of GBC with nodal and bony metastases was made based on imaging findings.

Serum protein electrophoresis showed decreased albumin levels with a mild increase in alpha‐1, alpha‐2, and gamma‐globulin levels. Bence Jones protein was not detected in the urine on routine microscopic examination.

Fine‐needle aspiration cytology (Figure 3) of the GB mass revealed atypical columnar cells with mild nuclear atypia, positive for malignancy. Image‐guided biopsy could not be carried out because the gallbladder wall thickening was inadequate. Following this, the patient was referred to oncology for palliative chemotherapy.

Microscopic findings of fine‐needle aspiration cytology of the GB mass showing clusters of atypical columnar cells with mild nuclear atypia, suggestive of malignancy.

Since her Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status (PS) was 2 (ambulatory and capable of self‐care but unable to work or up and about > 50% of waking hours), capecitabine monotherapy was preferred over gemcitabine + cisplatin doublet as the former is less toxic and better tolerated in patients with poorer PS. She is currently receiving capecitabine monotherapy, zoledronic acid, and other supportive medications.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient tolerated the treatment well. Follow‐up was advised in 3 months with repeat CECT of the neck, chest, and abdomen, as well as renal and liver function tests and abdominal ultrasonography. The follow‐up CECT of the neck, chest, and abdomen showed no significant interval changes, and her ALP levels decreased to 969 U/L.

Discussion

Bone metastases are a frequent and striking hallmark of various tumors, often revealing the far‐reaching nature of cancer's spread. Certain cancers, such as breast and prostate carcinoma, are “osteotropic” and commonly metastasize to bone, while others, including cervical, endometrial, bladder, and gastrointestinal cancers, rarely do. This selective metastasis follows the “seed and soil” hypothesis, which suggests that bone provides a “fertile soil” for specific cancer cells. In general, bone metastases are categorized as osteolytic (bone destruction), osteoblastic (new bone formation), radio‐occult (no mineral impact), or mixed. Osteolytic lesions are seen in multiple myeloma, renal cell carcinoma, melanoma, non‐small cell lung cancer, non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, thyroid cancer, Langerhans‐cell histiocytosis, and breast cancer, while osteoblastic lesions occur in prostate cancer, neuroendocrine tumors, small cell lung cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma, and medulloblastoma. Mixed lesions are commonly observed in gastrointestinal, cervical, testicular, and squamous cell cancers. Additionally, 10%–15% of bone metastases from breast, prostate, and lung cancers are either osteoblastic or mixed [13, 14].

Metastases from GBC have been reported from almost every organ of the body, including skin, orbit, and breast, but only a few isolated cases of distant metastases to the bone have been reported in live patients [10]. Disseminated hematogenous metastases from GBC are infrequent and typically arise in the advanced stages of the disease. These metastases are thought to result from the tumor's invasion of retroperitoneal veins, facilitating the spread of cancer cells to distant sites [1]. Metastases from GBC spreading through the bloodstream are uncommon and usually happen in the later stages of the disease. Autopsy studies show that about 64.8% of patients have distant metastasis, most of which affects the liver. The GB lacks serosa on the liver‐facing side, allowing its connective tissue to merge with liver tissue. This, along with a single muscle layer, facilitates early spread of GBC, often resulting in liver invasion at diagnosis. Sites of peritoneal spread are influenced by gravity, negative pressure in the subdiaphragmatic region, and pooling of ascitic fluid in dependent areas and recesses [15]. Only around 20% of metastases are found in other organs. The lungs are the most common site for spread outside the abdomen, followed by the brain, with skeletal metastases being rare.

In a retrospective study, the incidence of skeletal metastasis was only 2% in 100 cases of primary GBC sites being the lumbar vertebra and skull [16]. In another retrospective study conducted in North India, the incidence of skeletal metastasis was about 2.1% as well [1]. A study conducted by Gera et al., which included cases from 2011 to 2020, revealed that 7.4% of cases revealed bone metastasis at diagnosis. Skeletal involvement is quite rare, with only a few reported cases of bone metastasis at the time of diagnosis [11]. Osseous metastasis is predominantly osteolytic [8]. In cases of skeletal metastasis from GBC, 90% are purely osteolytic, while 10% are a mix of lytic and blastic types [17]. Osteolytic bone metastases from GBC have been reported in the femur, lumbar vertebrae, humerus, clavicle, and scalp. In the study by Sinha et al., multiple sites of mixed lytic and sclerotic skeletal metastases were identified. These metastases were detected using PET‐CT during follow‐up and were associated with local recurrence at the post‐cholecystectomy site [18].

Our case demonstrates the presence of mixed lytic‐sclerotic bone metastases, which are rare in GBC. Furthermore, skeletal metastases were the initial presenting feature, making the case even more unusual. The case was a diagnostic challenge, as it was initially evaluated as a mixed lytic‐sclerotic bony metastasis with an unknown primary. It is atypical for GBC to present with distant metastasis—particularly skeletal metastasis—as the initial manifestation rather than as loco‐regionally advanced disease. In this case, CECT also revealed gallstones and chronic cholecystitis, which are likely underlying causes of the GBC. Imaging features of polyploid GB mass with suspicious features of invasion were also consistent with malignant etiology. Our case had certain limitations, including the inability to obtain a biopsy and perform subsequent immunohistochemical analysis.

While FNAC is minimally invasive, safe, and often highly accurate for confirming malignancy (with pooled sensitivity around 85%–98% and specificity 87%–94% in various studies for detecting GBC), it primarily provides cytological evidence of atypical/malignant cells and typically yields limited material. This often suffices to confirm adenocarcinoma but falls short for detailed subtyping (e.g., missing rare variants like neuroendocrine carcinoma, adenosquamous, or mucinous types) and crucially for modern biomarker testing such as MSI, HER2, PD‐L1, or NTRK fusions, which require adequate tissue for immunohistochemistry and next‐generation sequencing. Current guidelines recommend core biopsy (or sufficient tissue) before initiating nonsurgical therapies like chemotherapy to enable comprehensive molecular characterization and personalized regimens [19].

Bone scintigraphy or PET‐CT is not routinely included in the work‐up for GBC, as bone metastasis is rare in this condition. These tests are usually prescribed to individuals who are known cases of GB malignancy with alarmingly high levels of alkaline phosphatase, those who have musculoskeletal pain, and other relevant clinical findings [1].

GBC carries a poor prognosis because it is often diagnosed at an advanced stage and responds poorly to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. The overall 5‐year survival rate is less than 5%, with a median survival of approximately 6 months. Curative surgical resection is possible in only about 10%–30% of cases. However, when GBC is detected incidentally at an early stage, the 5‐year survival rate can reach nearly 80% following revision surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy [6]. The management of patients with osseous metastasis is usually palliative chemotherapy and supportive treatment with bisphosphonates and radiotherapy to reduce pain [1].

Skeletal metastases in GBC are uncommon, with mixed lytic‐sclerotic metastases being even rarer. This case adds to the existing literature by highlighting an unusual presentation of GBC, in which a skeletal‐related event occurred without any preceding symptoms from the primary tumor. This case highlights the need for further studies to determine the incidence of various types of skeletal metastasis in GBC, and to evaluate whether routine screening for bone metastasis should be included in the diagnostic workup.
