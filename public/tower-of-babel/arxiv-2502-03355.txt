Solutions to general elliptic equations on nearly geodesically convex domains with many critical points Thanks: 2010 Mathematics Subject classification:35B09,35B40,35Q,35J,35R01 Thanks: Keywords : Critical points, Riemannian manifold, nodegeneracy
Alberto Enciso, Francesca Gladiali, Massimo Grossi

Abstract
. Consider a complete d d -dimensional Riemannian manifold ( ℳ , g ) (\mathcal{M},g) , a point p ∈ ℳ p\in\mathcal{M} and a nonlinearity f ⁡ ( q , u ) f(q,u) with f ⁡ ( p , 0 ) > 0 f(p,0)>0 . We prove that for any odd integer N ≥ 3 N\geq 3 , there exists a sequence of smooth domains Ω k ⊂ ℳ \Omega_{k}\subset\mathcal{M} containing p p and corresponding positive solutions u k : Ω k → ℝ + u_{k}:\Omega_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{+} to the Dirichlet boundary problem { − Δ g ​ u k = f ⁡ ( ⋅ , u k ) in ​ Ω k , u k = 0 on ​ ∂ Ω k . \begin{cases}-\Delta_{g}u_{k}=f(\cdot,u_{k})\quad&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{k}\,,\\ u_{k}=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega_{k}\,.\end{cases} All these solutions have exactly N N critical points in Ω k \Omega_{k} (specifically, ( N + 1 ) / 2 (N+1)/2 nondegenerate maxima and ( N − 1 ) / 2 (N-1)/2 nondegenerate saddles), and the domains Ω k \Omega_{k} are star-shaped with respect to p p and become “nearly convex”, in a precise sense, as k → + ∞ k\to+\infty .



1.
Introduction

The calculation of the number of critical points of solutions to the equation
-\Delta_{g}u=f(u)
has long been a topic of significant interest among mathematicians. Although a substantial body of literature exists on this subject, many challenges remain unresolved, and several open problems appear to be far from a definitive solution.

In this paper we consider the problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{g}u=f(q,u)&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathcal{M}
is a bounded smooth domain,
\mathcal{M}
is a complete
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, possibly non compact, and
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
. Here
f(q,u)
is a Lipschitz function on
\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Despite its seemingly simple structure, this problem is surprisingly rich in intriguing properties. It is well known that the number of critical points of (
1.1
) is strongly influenced by both the topology and the geometry of
\Omega
(see, for instance, the classic Poincaré–Hopf Theorem).

Let us begin by providing a brief historical overview of the problem, starting with the flat case, that is,
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
, equipped with its natural metric. In this context the most general result is undoubtedly the breakthrough theorem of Gidas, Ni, and Nirenberg. In the influential paper
[
9
]
, it was proven that if
\Omega
is a domain which is convex with respect to the directions
x_{1},..,x_{d}
, symmetric with respect to the axes
x_{1},..,x_{d}
,
and if the nonlinearity is autonomous (i.e.,
f(x,u)\equiv F(u)
), then the solution has a unique critical point located at the center of symmetry of
\Omega
.

Notably, the result in
[
9
]
holds for general nonlinearities. Several researchers have attempted to extend the theorem of Gidas, Ni, and Nirenberg by removing the symmetry assumption. While it may seem natural that the uniqueness of the critical point should not depend on the symmetry of the domain, this remains a challenging open problem. In the case of the torsion problem (
f(x,u)\equiv 1
), a significant result due to Makar–Limanov
[
15
]
shows that if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a convex domain then the function
\sqrt{u}
is strictly concave, which implies the uniqueness of the critical point of the solution
u
. This result was extended to higher dimensions in
[
13
]
. We also highlight the results in
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
, which consider semi-stable solutions on convex domains of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and proved the uniqueness of the critical point.

It is important to note that the convexity condition on the domain cannot be easily relaxed. Indeed, in
[
10
]
it was proven that there exists a class of domains
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
which are close to a convex set (in a suitable sense) such that the corresponding solutions of the torsion problem exhibit multiple critical points.

When one considers analogous problems on Riemannian manifolds
(\mathcal{M},g)
, the results are rather limited (in fact, they mainly focus on the first eigenfunction).
Let us start with the classic case of the surfaces, basically with the model cases
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(round sphere) and
\mathbb{H}^{2}
(hyperbolic plane), endowed with their natural metrics.
If
\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
f\equiv 1
, the uniqueness of the critical point for solutions of (
1.1
), under the conditions that
\partial\Omega
has positive curvature and the diameter of
\Omega
is less than
\frac{\pi}{2}
, was established in the recent paper
[
11
]
.

Concerning other nonlinearities, as mentioned earlier, much attention has been given to the case of the eigenfunctions. In
[
17
]
the authors discuss some
\log
-concavity estimates for the first eigenfunction on convex Euclidean domains, providing a short proof of the result of Brascamp and Lieb
[
2
]
. This approach was later adapted in
[
14
]
to prove the
\log
-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction in geodesically convex domains of
\mathbb{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
. This last result, together with
[
18
]
, allows to prove the uniqueness and non-degeneracy of the critical point. For related results on positively curved surfaces, see also
[
12
]
.

In the case of hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, the situation is more complex. In fact, the assumption of strict convexity alone does not generally suffice to ensure the uniqueness of the critical point, as shown for the first eigenfunction in
[
1
]
. However, whenever
\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}
, stronger assumptions such as horoconvexity
[
11
]
can restore the uniqueness of the critical point for solutions to the torsion problem.

For general Riemannian manifolds, it is well known that the number of critical points of solutions strongly depend on the metric
g
. For example, in
[
7
]
it is proved that, given a compact manifold
M
of dimension
d\geq 3
and integers
N,l
, there exits a metric
g
such that the lowest
l
eigenfunctions of the Laplacian have at least
N
non-degenerate critical points. Similar results have been obtained in
[
3
,
8
,
16
]
. However, it is reasonable to expect that, under suitable assumptions on the geodesic convexity, on the curvature of
\mathcal{M}
, and on the nonlinearity
f
, the uniqueness of the critical point of the solution to (
1.1
) can be established. When the manifold has constant sectional curvature, results of this kind are of course well known.

Our objective in this paper is to show that, as soon as the convexity assumption is slightly relaxed, one cannot hope to control number of critical points of solutions. Furthermore, this phenomenon is independent of the nonlinearity and of the choice of the metric.

Specifically, our main result, which builds on
[
10
]
, ensures that, given any nonlinearity
f
, on any Riemannian manifold, and around any point on the manifold, it is possible to construct a class of domains
\Omega_{k}
that are “almost geodesically convex” as
k\to+\infty
, in the precise sense introduced in Definition
5.3
, where the boundary problem (
1.1
) admits a positive solution with arbitrarily many critical points. In the statement,
\mea_{g}(\Omega)
denotes the Riemannian measure of the set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{M}
. We recall that
q
is a
critical point
of a function
u
if
\nabla u(q)=0
, and that this critical point is
nondegenerate
if its Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}u(q)
, computed in any local chart, is invertible.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be a smooth
d
-dimensional complete Riemannian manifold, possibly non compact. Assume that
f(q,u)
and
\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(q,u)
are Lipschitz in a neighborhood of
(p,0)
, and that
f(p,0)>0
.
For any fixed integer
n\geq 2
and any point
p\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a sequence
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}\ni p
of smooth bounded domains in
\mathcal{M}
, with
k\geq 1
, such that:

a)

For every
k
, there exists a solution
\widetilde{u}_{k}
in
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
to (
1.1
), that has exactly
2n-1
critical points:
n
nondegenerate maxima and
n-1
nondegenerate saddles.

b)

The domain
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
is geodesically starshaped with respect to
p
. That is, there exists a starshaped region
U_{k}\subset T_{p}\mathcal{M}
such that the exponential map
\exp_{p}
maps
U_{k}
diffeomorphically onto
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
.

c)

The sequence of sets
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
is almost geodesically convex in the sense that

(1.2)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mea_{g}\big(\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}\setminus\Sta_{g}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{k})\big)}{\mea_{g}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{k})}=0\,.

Here
\Sta_{g}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{k})
denotes the set of points of
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
with respect to which
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
is geodesically starshaped (see Definition
2.1
).

The idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the following. We start with a domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and with the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to the torsion problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=1&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{\varepsilon}\,,\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\,.\end{cases}

By
[
10
]
, we can take a domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
which is “nearly convex” as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, and for which
u_{\varepsilon}
has
n\geq 2
nondegenerate maxima and
n-1
nondegenerate saddles (and no other critical points). Now we regard
0<{\varepsilon}\ll 1
as small but fixed. Set
\Phi_{\eta}(x):=\exp_{p}(\eta x)
,
where
\exp_{p}
is the Riemannian exponential map at
p
. The ideas is to take
\widetilde{\Omega}_{\eta}\subset\mathcal{M}
as the image of
\Omega_{{\varepsilon}}
under
\Phi_{\eta}
, for suitably small
\eta,{\varepsilon}
depending on
k
, and to construct
\widetilde{u}_{k}
as a small perturbation of
\widetilde{u}_{\eta}:=\eta^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\big(\Phi_{\eta}^{-1}(-)\big):\widetilde{\Omega}_{\eta}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

At this point, two serious problems arise:

•

To prove that
\widetilde{\Omega}_{\eta}
satisfies the properties in
a)
,
b)
and
c)
in Theorem
1.1
.

•

To find a suitably small correction of
\widetilde{u}_{\eta}
yielding a solution to (
1.1
).

To overcome these difficulties, we will need to prove several delicate additional estimates for the various objects appearing in the construction.

The paper is organized as follows: in Sections
2
and
3
we establish some additional properties on the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
which are not covered in
[
10
]
. In Section
4
we introduce a suitably small scale parameter
\eta
and analyze the pair
(\widetilde{\Omega}_{\eta},\widetilde{u}_{\eta})
. Finally in Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Results on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on some results for the torsion problem in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
, in
[
10
]
(case
d=2
) and
[
5
]
(case
d\geq 3
). Here we give a slightly different statement of these previous results, because we will need some more properties of the solution
u
of the torsion problem in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Nevertheless the main ideas of this section can be found in
[
10
]
.

For
d\geq 2
we consider the torsion problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=1&\mbox{ on }\Omega\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
n\geq 2
let us consider arbitrary real numbers

(2.2)

0<a_{1}<a_{2}<..<a_{n}\in(0,+\infty)

and the holomorphic function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

(2.3)

F(z)=-\Pi_{i=1}^{n}(z-a_{i})(z+a_{i})=-\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}z^{2i}

where of course
b_{n}=1
and the coefficients
b_{i}
depend only on the points
a_{i}
in (
2.2
).

Next, we consider the function
v_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(2.4)

v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=Re\Big(F(z)\Big)

for
z=x_{1}+ix_{2}
,
(where
Re
stands for the real part of a complex function),
which is harmonic. By construction

(2.5)

v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}P_{2j}(x_{1},x_{2})

where
b_{n}=1
and
P_{2j}
are some homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
2j
(in which, for symmetry reasons,
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
appear only with even degree).
It might be useful to write

(2.6)

v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=-x_{1}^{2n}+\tilde{b}_{0}x_{1}^{2n-2}x_{2}^{2}+\tilde{b}_{1}x_{1}^{2n-2}+Q(x_{1},x_{2})

where
Q(x_{1},x_{2})
is a polynomial of degree at most
2n-4
in
x_{1}
and
\tilde{b}_{0}=n(2n-1)
.

Finally we introduce the function
u_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(2.7)

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(1-x_{2}^{2}-\dots-x_{d}^{2})+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})

which satisfies the torsion problem

-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=1\ \ \text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Observe that, by construction,
u_{\varepsilon}
is even in all the variables.

Let us denote by
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the function

(2.8)

f(x_{1}):=v_{n}(x_{1},0).

We immediately get that
f
is even,
f(\pm a_{1})=..=f(\pm a_{n})=0
and
\pm a_{i}
are all simple zeroes for
f
. Moreover
\lim_{x_{1}\to\infty}f(x_{1})=-\infty
and
f
has
n
nondegenerate maximum points and
n-1
nondegenerate minimum points in
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. The fact that they are all nondegenerate follows since
f(x_{1})
is a polynomial of degree
2n
with
2n
simple zeroes and since
f^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
has exactly
2n-2
zeros between the zero of
f^{\prime}(x_{1})
.

Before stating our main result on the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, we need the following definition:

The case
d=3
,
n=3
and
{\varepsilon}=0.01

Definition 2.1
.

For any
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\Sta(\Omega)
the set of points
x\in\Omega
with respect to which
\Omega
is starshaped.

In the following we will denote by
B^{d-1}(0,r)
the ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
centered at
0
and by
C
various positive constants independent of
{\varepsilon}
.
The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. There exists
{\varepsilon}_{0}>0
such that for any
0<{\varepsilon}<{\varepsilon}_{0}
there exists a smooth bounded domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

i)

u_{\varepsilon}
is the unique solution to (
2.1
) in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
for all
l
.
Moreover

\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\left(-D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\right)\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
for
D=\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}
.

ii)

\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is smooth and starshaped with respect to the origin.

iii)

The set
\{(x_{1},..,x_{d})\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}:u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},..,x_{d})>\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\}
has
n
connected components. In each of these components,

(2.9)

\max u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},..,x_{d})>\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+C{\varepsilon}.

iv)

The solution
u_{\varepsilon}
has exactly
n
nondegenerate local maximum points and
n-1
nondegenerate saddle points in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

v)

\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is “almost convex” in the sense that its Lebesgue measure satisfies

\mea\big(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\Sta(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\big)\to 0\quad\hbox{as }{\varepsilon}\to 0.

vi)

If
F
is the cylinder
F:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ such that }(x_{2},..,x_{d})\in B^{d-1}(0,1)\}
and
Q
is any compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap Q\to F\cap Q
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.

Here, we prove i), ii), iii), iv) and vi), while v) will be proved in the next section.

Proof.

The proof uses some ideas of Theorem 3.1 in
[
10
]
.

Proof of
i)
.

Let us define

(2.10)

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=:\{\hbox{the connected component of}:u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})>0\hbox{ containing }0\}.

First we prove that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is non empty and contains the line
(t,0,..,0)
for
t\in(-a_{n},a_{n})
.
For
{\varepsilon}
small we have that,

\begin{split}u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,..,0)=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(t,0)\geq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}-c_{1}{\varepsilon}>0\end{split}

where
-c_{1}=\inf\limits_{t\in(-a_{n},a_{n})}v_{n}(t,0)=\inf\limits_{t\in(-a_{n},a_{n})}f(t)<0
.

Next
we prove that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\left(-D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\right)\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
when
{\varepsilon}
is small enough. Let us compute
v_{n}(\pm D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},x_{2})
for
|x_{2}|\leq\sqrt{2}
. By (
2.6
)

v_{n}(\pm D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},x_{2})=-D^{2n}{\varepsilon}^{-1}+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{-n+1}{n}})

so that, recalling that
D=\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}
and
n\geq 2

\begin{split}u_{\varepsilon}(\pm D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{3}{4n}},x_{2},..,x_{d})&\leq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}-D^{2n}+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})<0\end{split}

if
{\varepsilon}
is small enough.
Moreover, by (
2.6
), we have

\sup_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=C>0

and hence if
(x_{2},..,x_{d})\in\partial B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
and
x_{1}\in\left(-D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\right)
then

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{d})\leq-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+C{\varepsilon}<0

if
{\varepsilon}
is small enough which proves the claim.

Reasoning exactly in the same way, using that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\left(-D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\right)\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
, it can be easily proved that, for
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}

0\leq u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{d})\leq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+C{\varepsilon}

so that
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
and, in a very similar manner, also
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\leq C
for every
l
. This concludes the proof of i).

Proof of
ii)
.

To prove the star-shapedness of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the origin we will show that

x\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}<0

for every
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Note that this also proves the smoothness of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Since
u_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
we have that, for
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,

(2.11)

\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(x_{2}^{2}+...+x_{d}^{2}).

Differentiating
u_{\varepsilon}
, we have

(2.12)

\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial x_{i}}=\begin{cases}{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}&if\ i=1\\
-\frac{1}{(d-1)}x_{2}+{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}&if\ i=2\\
-\frac{1}{(d-1)}x_{i}&if\ i>2,\end{cases}

so that

x\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)={\varepsilon}\left[\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}x_{1}+\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}x_{2}\right]-\frac{1}{(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}

and using (
2.11
),

\begin{split}x\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)&=-\frac{1}{(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}x_{1}+\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}x_{2}-2v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\right].\end{split}

Since

v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}P_{2j}(x_{1},x_{2})

with
b_{n}=1
, by Euler’s theorem, for homogeneous function, we get

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}x_{1}+\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}x_{2}-2v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j-2)b_{j}P_{2j}(x_{1},x_{2}).\end{split}

Observe that

\lim_{|x_{1}|\to+\infty}-\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}P_{2j}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\infty

which implies

\sup_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times(-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})}-\sum_{j=0}^{n}(2j-2)b_{j}P_{2j}(x_{1},x_{2})=l<\infty

and finally

\begin{split}x\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)&\leq-\frac{1}{(d-1)}+l{\varepsilon}\leq-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}<0\end{split}

for
{\varepsilon}
small enough.

Proof of
iii)
.

Here we show that the set
\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}:=\{x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}:u(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})>\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\}
has exactly
n
connected components.
By the definition of
f
in (
2.8
) there exist
n
maximum points
\bar{s}_{1},..,\bar{s}_{n}
for
f
in
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
and
n-1
minimum points
s_{1},..,s_{n-1}
for
f
in
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
such that

-a_{n}<\bar{s}_{1}<s_{1}<\bar{s}_{2}<s_{2}<...<s_{n-1}<\bar{s}_{n}<a_{n}

and

\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}f(\bar{s}_{j})=d^{\prime}>0\ ,\ \max\limits_{1\leq j\leq n-1}f(s_{j})=-d^{\prime\prime}<0.

Moreover we observe that

(2.13)

\begin{split}u_{\varepsilon}(\bar{s}_{j},0,..,0)&=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(\bar{s}_{j},0)=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}f(\bar{s}_{j})\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}d^{\prime}>\frac{1}{2(d-1)},\\
\end{split}

which proves (
2.9
) with
C=d^{\prime}
.

Next we want to prove that

(2.14)

u(b,x_{2},..,x_{d})<\frac{1}{2(d-1)}

when either
b=s_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
or
b=\pm 2a_{n}
and
(x_{2},..,x_{d})\in B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
.

To prove (
2.14
) we argue by contradiction and we assume there exist a sequence
{\varepsilon}_{k}\to 0
and points
(x_{2,k},..,x_{d,k})\in B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})

such that

(2.15)

\begin{split}u(b,x_{2,k},..,x_{d,k})&=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(1-x_{2,k}^{2}-..-x_{d,k}^{2})+{\varepsilon}_{k}v_{n}(b,x_{2,k})\geq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\end{split}

for
k\to\infty
, for a fixed value of
b
. Since
{\varepsilon}_{k}v_{n}(b,x_{2,k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, formula (
2.15
) easily implies that
(x_{2,k},..,x_{d,k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Next we observe that
v_{n}(b,0)=f(b)\leq-\beta:=\max\{-d^{\prime\prime},f(\pm 2a_{n})\}<0
so that
v_{n}(b,x_{2,k})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\beta
for
k
large enough. Moreover,

(2.16)

\begin{split}u(b,x_{2,k},..,x_{d,k})&=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(1-x_{2,k}^{2}-..-x_{d,k}^{2})+{\varepsilon}_{k}v_{n}(b,x_{2,k})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}-\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon}_{k}\beta<\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\end{split}

for
{\varepsilon}_{k}
small enough. This contradicts (
2.15
) and proves (
2.14
).

Estimates (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) imply that the set
\{(x_{1},..,x_{d})\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}:u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},..,x_{d})>\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\}
has at least
n
connected components, the first one in the subset
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[-2a_{n},s_{1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
, the last one in the subset
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[s_{n-1},2a_{n}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
, and the others in the subsets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[s_{j},s_{j+1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
for
j=1,..,n-2
. In each of these components
u_{\varepsilon}
admits at least one maximum point.

Proof of
iv)
:
we have to show that
u_{\varepsilon}
has exactly
n
maximum points,
n-1
saddle points, and that they are all nondegenerate. Note
u_{\varepsilon}
cannot have any minima by the maximum principle.

The critical points of
u_{\varepsilon}
must satisfy

(2.17)

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}=0&\\
-\frac{1}{(d-1)}x_{2}+{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}=0&\\
-\frac{1}{(d-1)}x_{i}=0&\hbox{for }i\geq 3\end{cases}

so that
x_{3}=..=x_{d}=0
at a critical point.

Moreover the first equation in (
2.17
) implies that if
P_{\varepsilon}=(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon},0,..,0)
is a critical points of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, then
(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})
is a critical point of
v_{n}
contained in
(-D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}},D{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}})\times(-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})
. Assume, by contradiction, that
|x_{1}^{\varepsilon}|\to+\infty
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
. Then
\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})\to\pm\infty
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, since
\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2})\sim-2nx_{1}^{2n-1}
as
|x_{1}|\to\infty
. This is not possibile since we are assuming
\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=0
. This proves that
|P_{\varepsilon}|\leq C
for a suitable constant
C
.

Now we consider a maximum point
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}=(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon},0,..0)\in[s_{j},s_{j+1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
. The case when either
P_{\varepsilon}\in[-2a_{n},s_{1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
or
P_{\varepsilon}\in[s_{n-1},2a_{n}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
follows exactly in the same way.
The second equation in (
2.17
) gives

(2.18)

\frac{1}{(d-1)}x_{2}^{\varepsilon}={\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=O({\varepsilon})

and so
x_{2}^{\varepsilon}=O({\varepsilon})
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.

Inserting in the first equation of (
2.17
) we obtain

(2.19)

0=\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},0)+O(x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=f^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon})+O({\varepsilon})

and this implies that
x_{1}^{\varepsilon}\to\bar{x}_{j}
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
and
\bar{x}_{j}
must satisfy
f^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{j})=0
.

Since
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}
is a maximum point and
\bar{x}_{j}\in[s_{j},s_{j+1}]
then
\bar{x}_{j}=\bar{s}_{j}
and
x_{1}^{\varepsilon}\to\bar{s}_{j}
.
The Hessian matrix of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}
is

(2.20)

H_{u_{\varepsilon}}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\varepsilon}&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}

where
B=-\frac{1}{d-1}I
and
I
is the identity matrix, while

A_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})&{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})\\
{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})&-\frac{1}{(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}^{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})\end{pmatrix}

Let us observe that

\begin{split}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})&=\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},0)+O(x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(\bar{s}_{j},0)+O(|x_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{s}_{j}|)+O({\varepsilon})\\
&=f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})+O(|x_{1}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{s}_{j}|)+O({\varepsilon})=f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})+o(1)\end{split}

where
f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})<0
, and also that
\frac{\partial^{2}v_{n}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon})=O(1)
. Concluding we have

\begin{split}&{\rm det}A_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{(d-1)}{\varepsilon}f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})(1+o(1)>0\quad\hbox{and }{\rm tr}A_{\varepsilon}<0\end{split}

for
{\varepsilon}
small enough. So the Hessian matrix
H_{u_{\varepsilon}}
is negative definite at
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}
which is a nondegenerate maximum point for
u_{\varepsilon}
.
This implies also that
u_{\varepsilon}
has a unique maximum point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[s_{j},s_{j+1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
for
j=1,..,n-2
and also in

\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[-2a_{n},s_{1}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
or
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap[s_{n-1},2a_{n}]\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2})
.

Next we let
P_{\varepsilon}=(x_{1}^{\varepsilon},x_{2}^{\varepsilon},0,..0)
be another critical point for
u_{\varepsilon}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
different from the previous maximum points
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}
. Then
|P_{\varepsilon}|<C
. We can then reason exactly as above and get that
x_{2}^{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
and that
x_{1}^{\varepsilon}\to\tilde{x}_{1}\in[-C,C]
where
\tilde{x}_{1}
satisfies
f^{\prime}(\tilde{x}_{1})=0
. By the properties of the function
f
then either
\tilde{x}_{1}=\bar{s}_{j}
for
j=1,..,n
or
\tilde{x}_{1}=s_{j}
for
j=1,..,n-1
. But
\tilde{x}_{1}=\bar{s}_{j}
is not possibile since
f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})<0
and
\bar{s}_{j}
is the limit of the maximum point
P_{\varepsilon}^{j}
. Then
\tilde{x}_{1}=s_{j}
and
f^{\prime\prime}(s_{j})>0
.

Computing the Hessian matrix of
u_{\varepsilon}
at the point
P_{\varepsilon}
and reasoning as above we have that the submatrix
B
is negative definite while the submatrix
A_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

{\rm det}A_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{(d-1)}{\varepsilon}f^{\prime\prime}(s_{j})(1+o(1))<0

for
{\varepsilon}
small and this implies that for
{\varepsilon}
small the Hessian matrix
H_{u_{\varepsilon}}
in
P_{\varepsilon}
has one positive eigenvalue. This shows in turn that
P_{\varepsilon}
is a nondegenerate saddle point for
u_{\varepsilon}
.
Then
u_{\varepsilon}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
has exactly
n
nondegenerate maximum points and can have only nondegenerate saddle points. The nondegeneracy of all the critical points of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
implies that they are isolated so that we can apply the Poincaré-Hopf Theorem to the vector field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
(observe that
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu<0
on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
) and we get that

\sum_{P_{\varepsilon}}{\rm index}_{P_{\varepsilon}}\left(\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\right)=(-1)^{d}\chi(\Omega_{\varepsilon})

where
\chi(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=1
is the Euler characteristic of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. This means that the saddle points of
u_{\varepsilon}
are exactly
n-1
and concludes the proof of iv).

Proof of
vi)
.

The property vi) easily follows by the definition of
u_{\varepsilon}
since if
x\in Q
and
Q
is compact then
{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=O({\varepsilon})
and
u_{\varepsilon}\to\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(1-x_{2}^{2}-\dots-x_{d}^{2})
.
∎

3.
The “almost convexity” property of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}

Here we prove v) of Theorem
2.2
.
We want to show that the set of points
\Sta(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
with respect to which
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is starshaped (see definition
2.1
) differs from
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
by a set whose measure converges to zero as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.

For this, let us consider the set

(3.1)

D_{\varepsilon}=\left\{\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},..,\xi_{d})\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\Big|u_{\varepsilon}(\xi)>A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}

for a suitable value of
A_{0}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exits a positive constant
\bar{A}_{0}=\bar{A}_{0}(n,D,a_{i})
where
D
is the constant appearing in
i)
of Theorem
2.2
and
a_{i}
are as in (
2.2
) such that for any
A_{0}>\bar{A}_{0}
we have

(3.2)

D_{\varepsilon}\subset\Sta(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Recall that

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},..,x_{d})=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(1-x_{2}^{2}-...-x_{d}^{2})+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})

and if
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
then

(3.3)

\frac{1}{2(d-1)}+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}(x_{2}^{2}+...+x_{n}^{2})

We want to show that
(x-\xi)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)<0
for every
\xi\in D_{\varepsilon}
and for every
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)
is proportional to the unit normal at
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, this will prove that
D_{\varepsilon}\subset\Sta(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
.

From now we will often write

h(x,\xi)=O\big(g({\varepsilon})\big)

when
|h(x,{\varepsilon})|\leq Cg({\varepsilon})

for some positive constant
C
depending only
n,D,a_{i}
.
Let us compute

(3.4)

\begin{split}&(x-\xi)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\underbrace{(x_{1}-\xi_{1}){\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}}_{:=I_{1}}+\underbrace{(x_{2}-\xi_{2}){\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}}_{:=I_{2}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=2}^{d}(x_{i}-\xi_{i})x_{i}}_{:=I_{3}},\end{split}

and estimate all the terms. We have that

(3.5)

\begin{split}\frac{I_{1}}{{\varepsilon}}&=(x_{1}-\xi_{1})\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}=(x_{1}-\xi_{1})\left(-2nx_{1}^{2n-1}+O(|x_{1}|^{2n-3})\right)\\
&=-2nx_{1}^{2n}+2n\xi_{1}x_{1}^{2n-1}+O\big((|x_{1}|+|\xi_{1}|)|x_{1}|^{2n-3}\big)\\
&\quad\left(\hbox{using Young's inequality }ab\leq\frac{1}{2n}a^{2n}+\frac{2n-1}{2n}b^{\frac{2n}{2n-1}}\right)\leq\\
&\leq-2nx_{1}^{2n}+\xi_{1}^{2n}+(2n-1)x_{1}^{2n}+O\big(|x_{1}|^{2n-2}+|\xi_{1}||x_{1}|^{2n-3}\big)\\
&=v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-v_{n}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})-\underbrace{v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-x_{1}^{2n}}_{=O(x_{1}^{2n-2})}+\underbrace{v_{n}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})+\xi_{1}^{2n}}_{=O(\xi_{1}^{2n-2})}+O\big({\varepsilon}^{\frac{-n+1}{n}}\big)\\[8.53581pt]
&\Rightarrow I_{1}={\varepsilon}\Big(v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-v_{n}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\Big)+O\big({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\big).\end{split}

Moreover

(3.6)

I_{2}=(x_{2}-\xi_{2}){\varepsilon}\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x_{2}}=O\left({\varepsilon}|x_{1}|^{2n-2}\right)=O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}),

and

(3.7)

\begin{split}I_{3}&=-\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=2}^{d}(x_{i}-\xi_{i})x_{i}=-\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=2}^{d}\xi_{i}x_{i}\\
&\leq-\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}\xi_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}\xi_{i}^{2}\,.\end{split}

Hence (
3.4
) becomes

(3.8)

\begin{split}(x-&\xi)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\\
&\leq-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})+\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\sum_{i=2}^{d}\xi_{i}^{2}-{\varepsilon}v_{n}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})\\
&\qquad\left(\text{adding and subtracting }\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\right)\\
&=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\left(1-\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\right)+{\varepsilon}v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})}_{=0\text{ since }x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\left(1-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\xi_{i}^{2}\right)-{\varepsilon}v_{n}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}_{\leq-A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\text{ since }\xi\in D_{\varepsilon}}+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})\\
&\leq-A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})\leq-A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}+\bar{A}_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}.\end{split}

where
\bar{A}_{0}=\bar{A}_{0}(n,D,a_{i})
. Then we can choose in (
3.8
)
A_{0}>\bar{A}_{0}
such that
(x-\xi)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x)<0
for
{\varepsilon}
small enough. This ends the proof.
∎

For our future applications, an important observation is that
D_{\varepsilon}
is stable with respect
C^{2}
-small deformation of the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let us fix
{\varepsilon}>0
and
A_{0}>\bar{A}_{0}
as in Proposition
3.1
, and consider the set
D_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.2
). Let
S
be an open set
S\supset\overline{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
, then there exists
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}({\varepsilon})>0
, such that
D_{\varepsilon}\subset\Sta(\Phi(\Omega_{\varepsilon}))
for any map
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\|\Phi-I\|_{C^{2}(S)}<\delta_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
shows that, for any
\xi\in D_{\varepsilon}
and any
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,
(x-\xi)\cdot\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq c_{0}>0
, where
\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)
is the unit normal of
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. By continuity, this means that

(x_{*}-\xi)\cdot\nu_{*}(x_{*})\geq c_{0}/2>0

for all
x_{*}\in\partial\Omega_{*}:=\partial\Phi(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
, provided that
\Phi
is sufficiently close to the identity in the
C^{2}
-norm. Here
\nu_{*}
denotes the unit normal of
\partial\Omega_{*}
.
∎

Our final result is to show that the measure of
D_{\varepsilon}
is close to that of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\bar{A}_{0}=\bar{A}_{0}(n,D,a_{i})
be as in Proposition
3.1
and let
A_{0}>\bar{A}_{0}
in
D_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.1
).
Then

(3.9)

\frac{\mea(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{\varepsilon})}{\mea(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\to 0\quad\hbox{as }{\varepsilon}\to 0.

Proof.

By the definition of
D_{\varepsilon}
we have that

(3.10)

\begin{split}\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{\varepsilon}&=\{(x_{1},..,x_{d})\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}:0\leq u_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\}.\end{split}

With the change of variablse
y_{1}:\big(2(d-1)\big)^{\frac{1}{2n}}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2n}}x_{1},

y_{i}:=x_{i}\hbox{ for }i\geq 2
, we get

(3.11)

\mea(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{\varepsilon})=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{\varepsilon}}dx=\big(2(d-1)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2n}}{{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}}\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}dy.

Here, by i) of Theorem
2.2
, and letting
C^{\prime}:=\big(2(d-1)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2n}}D
(where
D
is the constant in Theorem
2.2
), we have set

\begin{split}K_{\varepsilon}:=&\left\{y\in(-C^{\prime},C^{\prime})\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2}):0\leq u_{\varepsilon}\left(\big(2(d-1)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2n}}{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}y_{1},y_{2},..,y_{d}\right)\leq A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}=\\
&\!\!\!\!\!=\left\{y\in(-C^{\prime},C^{\prime})\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2}):0\leq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\left(1-\sum_{i=2}^{d}y_{i}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}y_{1}^{2n}+O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})\leq A_{0}{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}\\
&\subseteq\underbrace{\left\{y\in(-C^{\prime},C^{\prime})\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2}):-B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq 1-\sum_{i=2}^{d}y_{i}^{2}-y_{1}^{2n}\leq A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}}_{=H_{\varepsilon}}\end{split}

for some suitable constants
A,B>0
. Thus we have
\mea(K_{\varepsilon})\leq\mea(H_{\varepsilon})
.

By the symmetry of
H_{\varepsilon}
we have

\int_{H_{\varepsilon}}dy=2\int_{H_{\varepsilon}^{+}}dy

where

H_{\varepsilon}^{+}=\left\{y\in[0,C^{\prime})\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2}):1-A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq\sum_{i=2}^{d}y_{i}^{2}+y_{1}^{2n}\leq 1+B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}.

With the change of variables
z_{1}:=y_{1}^{n}
,
z_{i}:=y_{i}
for
i\geq 2
, we get

\int_{H_{\varepsilon}^{+}}dy=\frac{1}{n}\int_{Z_{\varepsilon}^{+}}z_{1}^{\frac{1-n}{n}}dz

where

Z_{\varepsilon}^{+}=\{z\in[0,(C^{\prime})^{n})\times B^{d-1}(0,\sqrt{2}):1-A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\leq 1+B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\}
.

In spherical coordinates we have
z_{1}=\rho\cos\theta_{1}
and

(3.12)

\int_{Z_{\varepsilon}^{+}}z_{1}^{\frac{1-n}{n}}dz=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}(\cos\theta_{1})^{\frac{1-n}{n}}w(\theta)d\theta\int_{\sqrt{1-A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}}}^{\sqrt{1+B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}}}\rho^{d-2+\frac{1}{n}}d\rho

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}:=\{(\theta_{1},..\theta_{d-1}):\theta_{1}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}),\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)\}
and
w(\theta)
is the surface element, which satisfies
|w(\theta)|\leq C
. Since

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{+}}(\cos\theta_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}-1}w(\theta)d\theta\leq E

we have

(3.13)

\begin{split}&\int_{Z_{\varepsilon}^{+}}z_{1}^{\frac{1-n}{n}}dz\leq E\int_{\sqrt{1-A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}}}^{\sqrt{1+B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}}}\rho^{d-2+\frac{1}{n}}d\rho\\
&=E^{\prime}\left[\left(1+B{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1+\frac{1}{n}}{2}}-\left(1-A{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1+\frac{1}{n}}{2}}\right]=O({\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{n}})\end{split}

for suitable constants
E,E^{\prime}
. Together with (
3.11
), this gives

(3.14)

\mea(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{\varepsilon})\leq 2\big(2(d-1)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2n}}{{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\int_{H_{\varepsilon}^{+}}dy\leq C{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2n}}}=o(1)

as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
. The result follows easily from this.
∎

Proof of
v)
in Theorem
2.2
.

The property v) in Theorem
2.2
follows by Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
.
∎

4.
The exponential map

From now we fix
{\varepsilon}
such that Theorem
2.2
holds.
Take a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
and let
\exp_{p}:V\to U
be the exponential map at
p
, where
V
is a neighborhood of the origin in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
and
U
is a neighborhood of
p
in
\mathcal{M}
.
Let

(4.1)

\Phi_{\eta}(x):=\exp_{p}(\eta x)

for
\eta\neq 0
and
x\in\frac{V}{\eta}\subset T_{p}\mathcal{M}
.

We want to embed the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
of Theorem
2.2
into
\mathcal{M}
using the map
\Phi_{\eta}(x)
.
Corresponding to
{\varepsilon}
there exists a
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
, which depends on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and on the point
p\in\mathcal{M}
, such that for every
0<|\eta|<\delta_{\varepsilon}
we have that

\eta x\in V\quad\hbox{for any }x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

In this way, for every
0<|\eta|<\delta_{\varepsilon}
,
\Phi_{\eta}
is a diffeomorphism from
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
into its image that we will denote

(4.2)

\widetilde{\Omega}_{\eta}=\Phi_{\eta}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\exp_{p}(\eta\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

In these coordinates (“rescaled normal coordinates”), the metric can be written as

(4.3)

g_{jk}=\eta^{2}\left(\delta_{jk}+h_{jk}(\eta x)\right)

where
h_{jk}
are smooth functions, because the metric is smooth. It is standard that

(4.4)

\displaystyle|h_{jk}(\eta x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\eta^{2}|x|^{2},

(4.5)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}h_{jk}(\eta x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\eta^{2}|x|

for some positive constant
C
independent of
\eta
and
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Recall that, in local coordinates, the Laplacian reads as

(4.6)

\Delta_{g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\sqrt{|g|}g^{jk}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\right)=g^{jk}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{jk}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\,.

Since in our rescaled coordinates

(4.7)

\displaystyle|g|=|\det(g_{ij})|=\eta^{2d}\left(1+O(\eta^{2}|x|^{2})\right)\ ,\qquad\ g^{jk}=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\left(\delta_{jk}+O(\eta^{2}|x|^{2})\right)\,,
