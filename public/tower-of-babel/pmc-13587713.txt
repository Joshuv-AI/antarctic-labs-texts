Sleep-disordered breathing in patients with group 1 and group 4 pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Kemal Yüce, Selvi Aşkar, Müntecep Aşkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSleep-disordered breathing (SDB) is frequently observed in patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH) and may contribute to disease progression through recurrent nocturnal hypoxemia. However, data comparing SDB characteristics between Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and Group 4 chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) remain limited.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 51 patients with stable precapillary PH (24 Group 1 PAH and 27 Group 4 CTEPH). Demographic characteristics, clinical findings, laboratory parameters, polysomnographic data, and right heart catheterization measurements were evaluated. All patients underwent overnight polysomnography. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0.ResultsThe mean age of the study population was 55 years, and 66.7% were female. Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) was identified in 88.2% of patients. Nocturnal desaturation was observed in 60.8% of the cohort. The mean oxygen desaturation index (ODI) was 22.45 ± 20.24 events/hour among patients with OSAS and 2.82 ± 2.59 events/hour among those without OSAS. Severe OSAS was present in 55.6% of OSAS patients. Group 4 patients demonstrated significantly higher total apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) values than Group 1 patients (29.16 ± 24.37 vs. 17.65 ± 14.87 events/hour, p = 0.045). No significant differences were observed in most hemodynamic parameters according to OSAS status.ConclusionsSleep-disordered breathing is highly prevalent in patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension. OSAS and nocturnal hypoxemia were common findings, particularly among Group 4 patients. Routine polysomnographic evaluation may facilitate early identification and appropriate management of sleep-disordered breathing in this population.Keywords: Pulmonary arterial hypertension, Sleep apnea syndrome, Obstructive, Hypoxia, Apnea

Introduction

Pulmonary Hypertension (PH) is a pulmonary vascular disease characterized by increased pulmonary arterial pressure, which ultimately results in right heart failure. The majority of these patients experience sleep-related hypoxia at night, regardless of whether they have daytime hypoxemia. Therefore, the 2022 ESC guidelines recommend nocturnal oxygen therapy for these patients [1].

Numerous studies have shown the presence of other sleep-disordered breathing conditions (apnea, hypopnea, hypoventilation) accompanying sleep-related hypoxia in PH patients [2]. Depending on the type of sleep-disordered breathing identified, noninvasive mechanical ventilation devices should be prescribed in addition to oxygen therapy. If untreated, sleep-disordered breathing itself may cause recurrent intermittent hypoxic episodes, which in turn can lead to hypoxic vasoconstriction and worsen the degree of PH.

Polysomnographic (PSG) studies in these patients have shown nocturnal hypoxia at rates of 77% and 69.7% [2]. In some patients, up to 50% of hypoxic events were associated with apnea or hypopnea [2]. In such cases, oxygen therapy alone is not sufficient and may even aggravate the condition if additional respiratory events are present [3]. Therefore, PSG studies should be performed to guide appropriate treatment.

Based on this background, the objective of this study is to investigate sleep-disordered breathing in patients with precapillary PH (Group I and Group IV).

Data and sample collection

Following ethics approval, the study began with the first patient assessed on 15.11.2022 at Van YYU Dursun Odabaş Medical Center's Polysomnography Unit.

During the study, the following data were collected: age, sex, smoking status, comorbidities, clinical findings, 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), pre- and post-exercise SpO2 (%), laboratory values (PaO2 [mmHg], PaCO2 [mmHg], A-a gradient, BNP [ng/L], D-dimer [μg/mL], HGB [g/dL], HCT [%], Ferritin [ng/mL], Iron [μg/dL], TIBC [μg/dL], Uric Acid [mg/dL]), PSG parameters (BMI, ESS, TST [min], Sleep Efficiency [%], Arousal Index [/h], Maximum Heart Rate, ODI [/h], D90 [min], Total AHI [/h], REM AHI [/h], Mean Nighttime SpO2), and Right Heart Catheterization (RHC) values (RAP [mmHg], sPAP [mmHg], mPAP [mmHg], Cardiac Index [L/min/m2], PVR [Wood units], and mPWP [mmHg]).

All collected data were recorded in patient files.

Our study revealed a high prevalence of OSAS.

In a study conducted by Rafanan et al., 13 patients with Group I idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (iPAH), with a mean age of 45, underwent overnight PSG testing [4]. Among them, 10 patients (77%) were found to have nocturnal hypoxemia; however, no other respiratory events were observed apart from oxygen desaturation. While that study reported a significant correlation between nocturnal desaturation and daytime saturation, hemoglobin levels, and the alveolar-arterial gradient, our study did not detect such correlations, either in the entire cohort or in subgroup analyses. Considering the limited number of patients in Rafanan's study, we believe our findings are more robust and reliable.

In another study by Schulz et al., 20 patients with Group I PH (iPAH) underwent PSG to evaluate sleep disorders [5]. The results showed that 6 of these patients (30%) had periodic breathing (PB) characterized by central sleep apnea. In contrast, our study identified OSAS in 88% of patients, and most of these also experienced nocturnal hypoxemia, similar to the Schulz study. However, no statistically significant differences were found in terms of hemodynamic parameters RAP (mmHg), mPAP (mmHg), Cardiac Index (L/min/m2), PVR (Wood units), and mPCWP (mmHg) between OSAS and non-OSAS patients.

In another study by Minai et al., 43 patients underwent overnight home monitoring for nocturnal hypoxemia [6]. Significant nocturnal hypoxemia was found in 70% (30 patients). In those with desaturation, higher values of Hgb, BNP, RAP, mPAP, and PVR were reported, along with reduced cardiac index and clear evidence of right ventricular dysfunction on echocardiography.

In our study, 31 of the 51 patients (60.8%) who underwent overnight PSG showed nocturnal desaturation. However, no significant associations were found between desaturation status and laboratory, right heart catheterization, or echocardiographic findings.

While many studies in the literature have reported elevated pulmonary artery pressures (sPAP and mPAP) in patients with nocturnal desaturation [6], our study differed in this regard.

We believe that the larger sample size, the direct measurement of nocturnal desaturation, and the evaluation of a broader range of hemodynamic parameters potentially associated with PH strengthen our study and contribute meaningfully to the literature.

Similarly, a study by Ulrich et al. evaluated 38 patients (23 with PAH [Group I] and 15 with CTEPH [Group IV]) to assess the prevalence of sleep-disordered breathing in PH patients with right ventricular dysfunction [7]. Among these patients, apnea-hypopnea events were detected in 17, with 13 classified as CSA and 4 as OSAS.

When comparing the 13 CSA patients with the 21 who had normal respiratory patterns, no significant differences in hemodynamic parameters or ESS scores were found—consistent with our findings. Furthermore, 68% of patients in that study experienced nocturnal hypoxemia.

Despite some limitations due to data insufficiency, that study's conclusions are largely consistent with our results, suggesting that sleep-disordered breathing may not be directly associated with hemodynamic disturbances or daytime sleepiness [7].

In a study by Prisco et al., 28 patients (22 women and 6 men, mean age 55) were evaluated [8]. OSAS was detected in 50% of the patients. Among those diagnosed with OSAS, 66% reported snoring, 60% had daytime sleepiness, but only 29% had an ESS ≥ 10.

In our study, the mean ESS score of the 45 OSAS patients was 11, and 20 of them (44%) had ESS ≥ 10. The mean ESS score in non-OSAS patients was 12, but this difference was not statistically significant.

In contrast, the finding that both OSAS and non-OSAS patients had elevated mean ESS scores (>10) was clinically notable.

In a study by Hildenbrand et al., nocturnal oximetry was performed on 63 PH patients (44 Group I - PAH, and 19 Group IV - CTEPH) to investigate the relationship between daytime SpO2 and nocturnal hypoxemia [9]. Nocturnal hypoxemia was found in 49 patients (77%), and although those with lower mean nocturnal SpO2 had higher mPAP values, there was no significant relationship with other clinical or functional indicators such as WHO functional class, 6MWT, or pro-BNP.

In our study, 24 patients were classified as Group I (PAH) and 27 as Group IV (CTEPH). Nocturnal desaturation was found in 11 patients (45%) in Group I and 20 (74%) in Group IV, totaling 31 patients. The majority of desaturated patients had OSAS, but no significant differences in hemodynamic parameters were found between these groups.

In another study, Dumitrascu et al. evaluated 169 patients with precapillary PH using polygraphy to assess the prevalence and clinical features of sleep-disordered breathing [10]. With a mean age of 61, mean BMI of 27, and mean mPAP of 43 mmHg, respiratory events were detected in 45 patients (26.6%) 27 with OSAS (16%) and 18 with CSA (10.6%). The mean AHI was 20/h, and OSAS was more common in male patients with higher BMI. CSA patients were generally older and hypokapnic.

Similarly, in our study, OSAS patients had higher BMI, consistent with Dumitrascu et al. However, no CSA cases were observed. The absence of hypokapnia and the relatively higher mean cardiac index (CI) in our cohort may help explain the lack of central apneas.

A key limitation of Dumitrascu's study is that it used polygraphy instead of full PSG, whereas our study used laboratory-based PSG and incorporated a more comprehensive analysis of hemodynamic, laboratory, and RHC parameters, making our methodology more robust.

In a study by Jilwan et al., 29 iPAH and 17 CTEPH patients underwent overnight PSG with transcutaneous capnography to investigate nocturnal hypoxemia and its mechanisms [2]. 38 out of 46 patients (82.6%) had nocturnal hypoxemia, and 41 (89%) had sleep apnea.

Hypoxemic patients had higher mean mPAP values. As in Hildenbrand's study, a strong association between mPAP and nocturnal hypoxemia was observed, and clinical symptoms alone were deemed insufficient predictors of desaturation.

Our findings are generally consistent with those two studies, except that we found no significant association between mPAP and OSAS presence.

Minic, Granton, and Ryan conducted a retrospective study of 52 WHO Group I PAH patients to determine the prevalence, clinical predictors, and outcomes of SDB [11]. 71% (32 patients) had SDB: 56% with OSAS and 44% with CSA, with OSAS being more common in both genders, which aligns with our findings. 60% had nocturnal hypoxemia, but arousal index differences were not significant across SDB types. ODI was significantly lower in those without SDB a finding that is consistent with our results.

Minic's study found no significant differences in cardiopulmonary and hemodynamic parameters between OSAS, CSA, and non-SDB patients. ESS >10, female sex, and older age were associated with SDB. In contrast, our study found no association between age, gender, and sleep disorders.

In another study by Nagaoka et al., the impact of nocturnal hypoxemia and apnea-hypopnea on prognosis in PAH patients was examined through PSG [12]. Among the cohort, 58 patients had SDB (29 with OSAS and 29 with CSA), and 31 had nocturnal hypoxemia. Compared to patients without SDB (n = 93), those with SDB had higher PVR and lower PaCO2. There were no significant differences between the groups in age, gender, Hgb, BMI, BNP, or mean mPAP.

Unlike that study, we found higher BMI in OSAS patients, but no differences in age, gender, Hgb, BNP, PaCO2, PVR, or mPAP between the groups. Due to the lack of long-term follow-up, we were unable to evaluate prognostic indicators or mortality.

In the study conducted by Li et al., a total of 80 CTEPH and 40 CTED (Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Disease) patients underwent overnight PSG. OSAS was detected in 50% (40 patients) of those with CTEPH, and 62% (25 patients) of those with CTED. These findings are consistent with and supportive of the results of our study.

In our study, CTEPH (Group IV) and Group I PAH patients were compared. Both the prevalence of OSAS and rate of nocturnal desaturation were found to be higher in the CTEPH group. Additionally, among these patients, 6MWT distances were lower in those with OSAS.

Functional status and survival in OSAS patients are closely linked to exercise capacity [13]. The 6-min walk test (6MWT), although not a primary outcome, has become a standardized endpoint in many recent clinical studies on OSAS and PAH [13]. Moreover, one study has shown that 6MWT may also be used to evaluate exercise-induced oxygen desaturation, which is commonly seen in patients with pulmonary vascular disease [14]. However, studies exploring the relationship between pulmonary function and exertional desaturation in PH patients remain limited [15].

In our study, the arousal index was found to be lower in OSAS patients in both groups compared to those without OSAS. This may be attributed to impaired respiratory regulation secondary to chronic hypoxia commonly present in OSAS patients. The arousal index is a parameter measured by PSG that reflects the frequency of respiratory-related arousal events during sleep [16,17]. It refers to the number of arousals per hour and is generally considered normal if less than 10.

The finding that non-OSAS patients had higher arousal indices suggests better preserved respiratory control mechanisms. However, this may also reflect poor sleep quality and frequent arousals triggered by hypoxia or apnea, highlighting the importance of routine sleep testing in these patients. We believe that systematic application of PSG in this population would provide significant clinical benefits.

In our study, ODI values in OSAS patients were significantly higher than those without OSAS, which indicates that these patients experienced greater oxygen desaturation. However, no significant association was found between elevated ODI/h values and hemodynamic parameters or RHC data.

Furthermore, oxygen saturation was observed to fall below 90% in all patients, indicating the need for evaluation regarding supplemental oxygen therapy.

Limitations

The main limitations of this study are that it was conducted in a single center, had a limited number of participants, and included only Group I and Group IV PH patients, excluding other PH subtypes.

An important limitation of our study is the relatively small number of patients without OSAS, with only 6 of the 51 patients classified as non-OSAS. This imbalance between the OSAS and non-OSAS groups may have reduced the statistical power of comparisons, particularly for clinical and hemodynamic parameters, and may partly explain the absence of statistically significant differences in some analyses. However, this distribution was not predetermined by the study design and could not have been anticipated before polysomnographic evaluation, as patients were classified according to the results of overnight PSG. Indeed, the small number of non-OSAS patients itself reflects the unexpectedly high prevalence of OSAS observed in this population of patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension. Furthermore, OSAS represents a spectrum of disease severity rather than a simple binary condition, and our analyses were not limited to comparisons based solely on the presence or absence of OSAS. Objective polysomnographic measures reflecting the severity and physiological consequences of sleep-disordered breathing, including AHI, ODI, D90, and arousal index, were also evaluated and compared between the pulmonary hypertension groups. Therefore, although the small and imbalanced sample sizes of the OSAS and non-OSAS groups should be considered when interpreting the nonsignificant findings, we believe that the comprehensive evaluation of objective PSG parameters provides clinically meaningful information regarding the presence, severity, and characteristics of sleep-disordered breathing in this specific patient population.

Larger-scale studies are needed to better understand the clinical importance of sleep-disordered breathing in patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension.

Conclusions

Sleep-disordered breathing was highly prevalent among patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension. Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome was detected in the majority of patients, and Group 4 pulmonary hypertension patients exhibited higher apnea-hypopnea index values than Group 1 patients. Although no significant associations were observed between OSAS and most hemodynamic parameters, nocturnal hypoxemia was common across the cohort. These findings support routine polysomnographic evaluation in patients with pulmonary hypertension to enable early diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Larger multicenter studies are required to confirm these findings and determine their long-term clinical implications.
