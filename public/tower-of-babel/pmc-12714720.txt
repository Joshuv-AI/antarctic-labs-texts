Multi-omics analysis of host airway responses in pediatric Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia reveals potential mechanisms of disease exacerbation caused by co-infection
Authors: Zijie Li, Chunbo Hao, Gaijing Jia, Qiujing Liang, Qingmei Wang, Yang Wu, Yu Tang, Wangquan Ji, Yuanfang Shen, Fang Wang, Yaodong Zhang, Zhipeng Jin, Adong Shen, Yuefei Jin, Guangcai Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Respiratory viral co-infections in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) are common and cause severe clinical manifestations. However, the exact pathogenic mechanisms of MPP co-infections are still unclear. In this study, we conducted a large-scale clinical analysis of 3106 MPP patients to characterize co-infection patterns. Subsequent metabolomics, microbiomics, and cytokines analyses of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from 73 MPP cases were performed. Patients were divided into groups with single Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) infection and co-infection (including co-adenovirus [co-ADV] and co-influenza A virus [co-IAV]), while bronchial foreign body patients served as controls. Metabolomic profiling identified 616 differential metabolites between the single MP group and the co-infection group. Collectively, these metabolites contributed to the formation of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment in patients with co-infections. Notably, ADV co-infection induced profound pulmonary microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by selective depletion of Lactobacillus. Next, we observed a robust upregulation of CCL-family chemokines in patients co-infected with ADV, which showed a significant correlation with peripheral monocyte counts, suggesting that monocyte-driven inflammation may serve as a key mechanism for disease exacerbation. These findings demonstrate that co-infection (co-ADV and co-IAV) triggers a pro-inflammatory metabolic shift. Additionally, ADV co-infection specifically disrupts lung microbiota structure and increases CCL family chemokines.

Introduction

With the widespread use of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine1, Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) has become one of the most prevalent pathogens in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), accounting for approximately 10–40% of cases in pediatric populations2–4. After infecting the human body, MP can induce interstitial lung disease and lead to Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), which is a highly contagious disease. Moreover, MP infection can result in detrimental effects on human airway epithelial cells and cilia, thereby impairing the functionality of the mucociliary clearance system5 as well as compromising host immune function6, and consequently increasing susceptibility to co-infection caused by other pathogens. The co-infection rate of MPP ranges from 27.0 to 65.6%4,7, with adenovirus (ADV) and influenza A virus (IAV) being common pathogens that frequently co-infect with MP8–10. It has been proposed that co-infection with MP and various viruses is significantly associated with the severe progression of MPP as well as the development of refractory Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (RMPP). RMPP is characterized by patients who, despite receiving 7 days of standard macrolide antibiotic treatment, continue to experience fever, worsening symptoms, deteriorating lung imaging findings, and the emergence of extrapulmonary complications11. However, it remains uncertain how co-infection impacts the underlying mechanisms that are responsible for the development of severe MPP and RMPP. Currently, our comprehension of the threat posed by MP co-infection to children’s health remains relatively limited.

Recent studies have demonstrated that alterations occur in the lung microbiota during the course of infection, and these changes exhibit a strong correlation with the progression and prognosis of pneumonia12,13. However, existing research has yet to comprehensively clarify the relationship between co-infections and changes in the lung microbiota of patients with MPP. Moreover, the exploration of disease mechanisms has evolved from an emphasis on isolated molecular targets to a comprehensive and multi-faceted systematic analysis. The cytokine profile, which elucidates the dynamic imbalance of the immune regulatory network, in conjunction with the comprehensive metabolic pathway mapping provided by metabolomics, provides a valuable multidimensional framework for analyzing the pathogenesis of disease severity in infectious diseases. In the present study, we collected bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples from pediatric patients and performed cytokine profiling, microbiome analysis, and untargeted metabolomics analysis. These analyses are crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying the exacerbation of MPP in patients with co-infections.

Results

Demographic characteristics of study participants

Considering the prevalence and clinical significance of co-infections with ADV (26.6%) and IAV (14.5%) among MPP patients (Supplementary Fig. 1), this study further collected the case information and BALF samples of the relevant patients for further analysis. A total of 73 MPP patients (35 males), aged between 2.25 and 12.25 years (mean age: 7.01 ± 2.44), were included in the study. Among them, 31 were categorized as belonging to the single MP group, while the remaining 42 cases were classified within the co-infection group. The co-infection group was further stratified into two subgroups: the co-ADV subgroup (n = 21), and the co-IAV subgroup (n = 21). Ten bronchial foreign bodies (BFB) patients without pulmonary inflammatory responses were enrolled in the control group.

There were no significant differences in age or gender distribution between the control group and the other groups. Compared to patients in the single MP group, those in the co-infection group exhibited significant differences in several clinical parameters, including hospital stays (p < 0.001), duration of fever (p = 0.008), white blood cell count (p = 0.002), neutrophil count (p = 0.001), macrophage count (p = 0.044), and procalcitonin (PCT) concentration (p = 0.016). Additionally, patients with co-infection exhibited a significantly higher rate of tachypnea (64.3%, p = 0.001), pulmonary necrosis (16.7%, p = 0.017), and extrapulmonary complications (21.4%, p = 0.037). Metabolomic analysis identified pro-inflammatory metabolic patterns in BALF of MPP patients with co-infection

Principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis identified significant differences in metabolite profiles within BALF samples between the MPP group and the control group (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). A total of 296 differential metabolites (DMEs) were identified between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The statistical significance of the observed fold changes in these DMEs is illustrated in a volcano plot, while the metabolic pathways enriched by these DMEs are elucidated through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E).

The results of the partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model demonstrated that, compared to the single MP group, the metabolic profiles of both the co-ADV and co-IAV groups exhibited significant differences. However, the metabolic patterns between the co-ADV and co-IAV groups were largely indistinguishable. Consequently, in subsequent metabolomic analyses, we directly compared the single MP group with the co-infection group (Fig. 2A, B). The clustering heatmap further validated this result (Fig. 2C). The results of the orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model showed that R²Y = 0.972 and Q² = 0.855, indicating that the model had no risk of overfitting. In these two groups, a total of 2435 metabolites were identified, with 616 metabolites exhibiting significant differences in expression levels between the groups (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Data). Further classification of these DMEs revealed that the majority were identified as lipids, including glycerophospholipids, prenol lipids, fatty acyls, steroids, and steroid derivatives. Additionally, carboxylic acids and their derivatives were also prominently represented (Fig. 2F). We focused our attention on DMEs that exhibit well-defined pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory biological functions. Several well-recognized potent pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators, along with their precursor molecules, exhibited significant differences between the groups (Fig. 2G). By integrating biological functions with fold change values, we identified the top 5 key pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory DMEs, as follows: the pro-inflammatory DMEs include L-Proline (p < 0.001, FC = 3.25), Serotonin (p < 0.001, FC = 2.94), L-Serine (p < 0.001, FC = 2.78), LTB4_20-hydroxy (p = 0.001, FC = 2.73), and Uric acid (p < 0.001, FC = 2.59); the anti-inflammatory DMEs include Estrone (p < 0.001, FC = 0.33), Vitamin K (p < 0.001, FC = 0.39), Glycoursodeoxycholic acid (p < 0.001, FC = 0.50), Carnosic acid (p = 0.001, FC = 0.55), and beta-Carotene (p < 0.001, FC = 0.57). The cloud and rain map clearly illustrated the expression patterns of key DMEs between the two groups. Specifically, in the co-infection group, the levels of pro-inflammatory metabolites were significantly elevated, while the levels of anti-inflammatory metabolites were markedly reduced. This observation elucidates the underlying reason for the more severe clinical manifestations observed in patients with co-infection (Fig. 2H).

The rarefaction curves indicate that the sequencing depth of the samples satisfies the criteria necessary for microbiota analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2F). We further focused on the differences between the single MP group and the co-infection group. The findings indicated that although no significant difference was observed in the α-diversity index across these groups, a significant difference was detected in the β-diversity. This distinction was particularly pronounced between the single MP group and the co-ADV group (Fig. 3E). The lung exhibited comparable microbial compositions among three groups at the phylum level, but at the genus level, there were significant differences in the composition of lung microbiota among the three groups of patients (Fig. 3F). Specifically, the relative abundance of Streptococcus in the microbiota of BALF samples from patients in the co-ADV group was markedly higher compared to the other two groups. Compared to single MP infection, which accounts for 32.8%, co-infection—particularly with ADV, observed at a rate of 9.91%—led to a reduction in the relative abundance of MP in the BALF of patients with MPP. This phenomenon may be attributed to competitive interactions between the pathogens involved. Therefore, in the subsequent microbiota analysis, we further focused on the co-ADV group and the single MP group. All samples within these two groups were subjected to cluster analysis and divided into four clusters. The results revealed that a higher proportion of co-infection patients were classified into Cluster 1, which was predominantly characterized by a relatively high prevalence of other bacterial species (14/23). In contrast, patients with single MP infection were primarily categorized into Cluster 2, which was dominated by MP (17/21), further substantiating the aforementioned conclusion (Fig. 3G). Additionally, Cluster 3 and Cluster 4 were characterized by microbial profiles predominantly associated with Streptococcus and Haemophilus_D, respectively. However, the distribution of patients across different groups within these two clusters did not exhibit any statistically significant differences.

We subsequently investigated which microorganisms’ differential distributions predominantly contributed to these variations. Firstly, a Venn diagram was employed to elucidate the distinct distribution patterns of microbiota across diverse groups (Fig. 4A). Secondly, LEfSe and random forests (RF) models were used to analyze the differences in microbiota between single MP groups and co-ADV group, as well as to identify potential differential microorganisms (DMIs) (Fig. 4B–D). In comparison to the single MP group, the lung microbiota of patients in the co-ADV group experienced a reduction in beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus) and an increase in anaerobic and opportunistic pathogenic microorganisms (such as Actinomyces and Leptotrichia_A). The distribution of these important DMIs in the samples from both groups was further clarified (Fig. 4E), and their classification ability was validated through receiver operating characteristic curves (ROCs) analysis (Fig. 4F). The findings indicated significant differences in the abundance of four microorganisms between the two groups. Co-infection with ADV can specifically enhance the expression of CCL family chemokines in MPP patients

Through the application of protein array technology, this study first analyzed the characteristics of the cytokine profiles in the BALF of BFB patients as well as all MPP patients. The results showed that the concentration of interleukin (IL)-17A in most patients was below the detection limit, and we were unable to conduct an effective analysis of this indicator. In addition, except for IL-5, IL-8, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB), the distributions of the remaining 20 cytokines in the two groups were significantly different (Supplementary Fig. 3A). This finding suggests that patients with MPP may undergo a significant “cytokine storm.” This phenomenon could be closely associated with the case selection criteria employed in this study, as all cases necessitated alveolar lavage.

Further research results demonstrated that, compared with the single MP group, all CCL family chemokines and PDGF-BB were significantly upregulated in the co-ADV group (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we utilized the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to perform an interaction analysis of the differentially expressed cytokines and conducted a Gene Ontology Biological Process enrichment analysis (Fig. 5B, C). Our findings indicated that these differential proteins were significantly associated with monocyte chemotaxis. We further conducted a correlation analysis between the concentrations of differential cytokines (DCKs) and peripheral blood monocyte counts in patients. The results showed that the concentrations of monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1) (r = 0.26, p = 0.024), macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α) (r = 0.27, p = 0.019), and MIP-1β (r = 0.24, p = 0.044) were positively correlated with the number of peripheral blood monocytes (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Therefore, co-infection with ADV can specifically enhance the expression of CCL family chemokines in the lungs of MPP patients, and this phenomenon is associated with an increase in peripheral blood monocyte counts. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the epidemiological characteristics of MPP co-infection and systematically evaluated the impact of the two most prevalent co-infections, including IAV and ADV, on the clinical outcomes of pediatric patients with MPP. This multi-faceted assessment provides novel insights into the potential mechanisms by which co-infection exacerbates MPP.

ADV and IAV are common pathogens in pediatric CAP14. Among them, ADV is one of the most prevalent pathogens that cause co-infections in MP, often leading to poor anti-infective responses and more severe clinical consequences15,16. In the autumn of 2022, an increasing trend in respiratory pathogens such as ADV, IAV, and MP was observed across multiple countries in the Northern hemisphere. Several countries, including the United States, Canada, and China, have reported a significant increase in the incidence of pneumonia cases associated with MP 10,17,18. To further investigate this phenomenon, we conducted a retrospective analysis of cases in which MPP was the primary discharge diagnosis at Henan Children’s Hospital from 2022 to 2024. The findings revealed that in the Henan region, ADV and IAV are the predominant pathogens co-infecting MPP patients.

Consistent with previous studies, our findings demonstrate that the co-infection groups exhibited more severe clinical manifestations, laboratory indicators, and poorer prognosis compared to the single MP group10,15,19. Although studies have reported no significant difference in MP and ADV positivity rates between the pulmonary consolidation and non-consolidation groups15, our research indicates that co-infection, whether with ADV or IAV, can expand the scope of pulmonary consolidation in MPP patients. An increased extent of pulmonary consolidation is generally associated with prolonged hospitalization and worse clinical outcomes20.

Metabolomics enables the characterization of the dynamic patterns of numerous small-molecule metabolites generated by both host and microbiota life systems in response to external stimuli, thereby facilitating the elucidation of potential disease mechanisms21. The metabolomics analysis revealed a distinct pulmonary metabolic profile in patients in the co-infection group, marked by a concomitant increase in pro-inflammatory mediators and a reduction in essential anti-inflammatory and protective metabolites.

Notably, elevated levels of uric acid, a representative damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), may serve as a potential mechanistic contributor to exacerbated cellular damage, microbial dysbiosis, and the formation of DCKs induced by co-infection. Elevated uric acid levels typically indicate more severe cellular damage and necrosis22. Furthermore, alterations in the composition of the microbiota may influence the purine metabolism pathway within the pulmonary microenvironment, thereby contributing to increased uric acid production23. Critically, uric acid is a well-established activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome in immune cells such as macrophages24, which is consistent with the results showing increased levels of M1 macrophage-related cytokines (such as CCL2, CCL3, and CCL4) observed in our cytokine profile analysis. Additionally, we found that co-infection can result in a substantial reduction in the concentrations of β-carotene and vitamin K. As essential nutritional components, the critical roles of these two metabolites in immune regulation and epithelial barrier protection have been extensively acknowledged25–27. Consequently, locally administering nutritional supplements such as β-carotene and vitamin K through nebulization or other methods during the early stages of the disease may potentially contribute to preventing disease progression and improving the prognosis in children.

Perturbations in the respiratory microbiota are frequently associated with the severity and prognosis of pulmonary diseases 28,29. Compared to BFB patients, MPP patients exhibit significant alterations in both α-diversity and β-diversity of the lung microbiota, indicating that MP exerts a substantial influence on the composition and structure of the lung microbiota. The study further investigated the situation of co-infection with ADV. Specifically, the observed imbalance of microbiota is characterized by an overgrowth of pathogenic and opportunistic pathogenic microorganisms, an increased abundance of anaerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria, and a depletion of beneficial microorganisms. The increase in anaerobic and partially anaerobic microorganisms may be attributed to the more severe inflammatory response and the direct damaging effects of ADV on the epithelium, which cause destruction of the mucosal epithelium and an increase in mucus30. This exudate creates an anaerobic environment in the lungs, while damaged tissues and hemorrhage release large amounts of nutrients that promote the growth and colonization of these microorganisms31.

Regarding the microorganism exhibiting the most significant difference between the single MP group and the co-ADV group, Lactobacillus, extensive studies have been conducted on its role as a probiotic in ameliorating virus-induced pneumonia via the lung-gut axis32–34. The benefits of microbial administration have also been demonstrated in the treatment of lung infections35. Intranasal inoculation of Lactobacillus has been demonstrated to effectively activate the Nod2 receptor, promote the production of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and provide protection against respiratory tract infections in mice36. A recent study has demonstrated that the inhalation of lactobacilli-based preparations reduces neutrophilic inflammation in the lungs and improves lung function and structure37. Furthermore, in the network clustering analysis conducted on all MPP patients, Limosilactobacillus was identified as a keystone taxon with the highest centrality score. As an emerging lactic acid bacteria genus, Limosilactobacillus has many overlaps with Lactobacillus in terms of correlated DMEs, suggesting that the two may play similar biological functions during the disease progression. Moreover, the depletion of beneficial metabolites such as beta-carotene and vitamin K is likely a direct functional consequence of the observed drastic reduction in commensals like Lactobacillus, which are known to be involved in the metabolism and stabilization of such compounds38,39. The results of the correlation analysis further substantiate this perspective. The above research results collectively indicate that for MPP patients with co-infections, early inhalation of Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus preparations may potentially help prevent the development of severe pneumonia and reduce the risk of adverse outcomes.

The imbalance between elevated pro-inflammatory metabolic levels and reduced anti-inflammatory metabolic activity in the patient’s lungs is ultimately manifested in their immune response, as evidenced by a distinct cytokine profile. Co-infections, particularly those involving ADV, result in a substantial elevation in the concentrations of chemokines and PDGF-BB in the lungs of these patients. Chemokines are a family of secreted proteins that play an important role in leukocyte activation and chemotaxis40. The production of chemokines is closely associated with mononuclear phagocyte system. M1 and M2 macrophages exhibit distinct chemokine profiles. Notably, M1 macrophages possess a robust cytotoxic function against infected cells, and their polarization serves as a critical effector mechanism in the host’s defense against intracellular pathogens41.

The significantly elevated chemokines CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, and CCL11 were identified as part of the M1-type chemokine spectrum. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that the concentrations of CCL2, CCL3, and CCL4 exhibited significant positive correlations with the number of peripheral blood monocytes in these patients. These findings indicate that co-infection may result in more severe lung injury by further promoting the polarization of monocytes toward the M1 phenotype. Consequently, inhibiting the CCL/CCR axis could potentially serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for managing co-infections. In addition, given that MPP may lead to the severe sequela of pulmonary fibrosis42–44, and that existing research has demonstrated the significant pro-fibrotic effects of chemokines (e.g., CCL2, CCL3, CCL4) and PDGF-BB45–47, it is imperative to prevent pulmonary fibrosis in MPP patients resulting from mixed infections in order to enhance their prognosis.

Clustering network and correlation analyses revealed complex interactions and associations among the microbiome, metabolome, cytokine profiles, and host immune responses. Therefore, we hypothesize that co-infection may drive MPP patients into a detrimental cycle: Co-infection markedly modifies the pulmonary microbiota and metabolic patterns in MPP patients, which in turn exert a more profound influence on the patients’ immune responses and clinical outcomes. Perhaps the critical factor in treating severe and critically co-infected MPP patients lies in effectively breaking this vicious cycle.

It should be noted that, given the limited availability of BALF samples, this study was conducted at a single center and involved a relatively small sample size. Due to technical limitations, we did not detect the viral loads of ADV and IAV. In addition, this study adopted a relatively lenient fold change threshold to screen for DMEs. As an exploratory study, its aim was to improve detection sensitivity in order to capture a wider range of metabolic changes and conduct a systematic analysis of the diseases from an overall functional level. Nevertheless, for some metabolites identified based on this criterion, particularly those exhibiting change amplitudes only marginally above the threshold, their biological relevance requires further confirmation through subsequent targeted validation experiments. Moreover, it is necessary to use metagenomics and large-scale multi-center studies in the future to verify the results of this experiment.

In conclusion, these findings demonstrate a two-tiered pathogenic mechanism: First, in comparison with single MP infections, co-infections, including co-ADV and co-IAV, can induce a shift toward a pro-inflammatory metabolic profile. Second, co-infection with ADV can specifically disrupt the structure of the lung microbiota and trigger an increase in CCL family chemokines. These insights reveal potential therapeutic targets for alleviating and improving the clinical symptoms and prognosis of MPP patients complicated by co-infection.

Methods

Study participants

This study was performed between February 2022 and February 2024 at Henan Children’s Hospital. During the study period, a total of 3106 patients were identified with a primary diagnosis of MPP. To elucidate the current status of co-infections involving MP, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the co-infection situations among these patients.

A total of 73 patients diagnosed with MPP underwent flexible bronchoscopy and were included in the study following simultaneous testing for MP antibodies and nucleic acids. The diagnosis was conducted in accordance with the “Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2023 Edition)”48. A control group was established, comprising ten patients diagnosed with BFB, who exhibited neither signs of infection nor inflammatory symptoms.

Children were included in the single MP group if they presented with: (1) cough, fever, tachypnoea, and abnormal breath sounds; (2) typical MPP imaging changes (interstitial infiltrates, segmental and lobar consolidations, hilar lymph node enlargement); (3) positive nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) results for MP RNA or positive serum MP antibody results which were defined as (i) MP antibody titer ≥1:160 on admission or (ii) MP antibody titer seroconversion from negative (<1:80) to positive (≥1:160) within 2 weeks; (4) negative NAAT results for other pathogens, such as rhinovirus and respiratory syncytial virus. MPP patients who were simultaneously diagnosed with ADV or IAV infection by NAAT were included in the co-infection group, which can be further divided into the co-ADV subgroup and the co-IAV subgroup based on the pathogens. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients with infections caused by other pathogens or underlying conditions; (2) patients with immune deficiency or immunosuppressive conditions; (3) patients who did not undergo NAAT and antibody testing upon admission; (4) samples whose retention does not meet the required standards.

The clinical data, including age, gender, physical signs, comorbidities, laboratory indicators and imaging indicators, were retrieved from patients’ medical records. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University (No. ZZUIRB2024-204), and written informed consent was obtained from both the participants and their parents/legal guardian(s).

Specimen collection

The collection and storage standards for BALF are in accordance with the “Clinical Practice Guidelines for Bronchoalveolar Lavage in Chinese Children”49. The bronchoscopy examination was performed on conscious pediatric patients under intravenous sedation. A total of 5–10 mL of BALF was collected and immediately transferred into a 50 mL sterile plastic tube, which was promptly stored in a −80 °C freezer for subsequent use.

Metabolomics analysis

The metabolites in the BALF of the patients were analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). A total of 400 μL of BALF was collected from each sample, and the specific experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the methodology described by Sun et al.50.

After preprocessing the data, PCA was performed on the metabolomics data for further analysis. Next, to visualize group separation, PLS-DA was carried out. To find DMEs between group comparisons, the OPLS-DA model was used to determine the maximum separation between different types of samples based on the sample classification information. Variable importance in projection (VIP) values were used to illustrate the contribution of metabolites to the OPLS-DA model. Variables with VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.20 or ≤0.83 between two groups were considered as DMEs. MetaboAnalyst platform combined with the online KEGG classifications was used for pathway analysis of DMEs. The DMEs were mapped into their biochemical pathway. Pathway significance was based on the total number of metabolites that map to a pathway and their respective significances; a pathway was considered perturbed if the number of significant metabolites was ≥ 2 and the impact value was > 0.10 and p < 0.10. Additionally, the distribution of the relative expression values of the key DMEs between the two groups was further analyzed.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

Total genomic DNA samples were extracted using the Omega Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), and 16S rRNA genes (V3–V4 region) were amplified using the primers (F: 5’–ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’, R: 5’–GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (PANOMIX Biomedical, Suzhou) and the data and taxonomic analysis methods have been described in detail in previously published articles51.

Both α-diversity and β-diversity were analyzed by QIIME2. α-diversity metrics were presented as median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney rank-sum test. β-diversity was measured using the weighted UniFrac distance test. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used to visualize similar distances between samples. Additionally, relative abundance differences of the microorganisms ranked in the top 20 between the groups at the phylum and genus level were displayed. The LEfSe and RF model was used to characterize taxa with statistical significance and biological relevance. The DMIs were determined by an LDA score > 4.0, and a p < 0.05 in LEfSe method, or importance scores >0.03 in random forests model. ROCs and the AUC with CI were used to assess the accuracy of the DMI to identify co-infection.

Analysis of the cytokine profile in BALF

In this study, the concentrations of cytokines in the BALF from patients were quantified using the RayPlex Human Cytokine Storm Bead Array kit (Catalog number FAH-STRM-1, Ray Biotech, USA), which employs protein antibody array technology. The measurement range of the kit includes the following contents: G-CSF, GM-CSF, MCP-1, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), MIP-1α, MIP-1β, regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), eotaxin, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), PDGF-BB, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-15 and IL-17A.

Network clustering analysis

We included all MPP patients and selected the DMIs and DMEs with a Spearman rank correlation coefficient exceeding 0.3 between the single MP group and the co-infection group. We then used the greedy algorithm implemented in igraph to cluster these DMI and DME into different modules, thereby facilitating network-based clustering analysis. To evaluate the topological role of each node in the network, we computed two essential topological metrics: within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi). Based on predefined threshold values, nodes were categorized into four distinct topological classes: (1) Module hubs: Zi >2.5 and Pi <0.62; (2) Connectors: Zi <2.5 and Pi >0.62; (3) Network hubs: Zi >2.5 and Pi >0.62; (4) Peripheral nodes: Zi <2.5 and Pi <0.62.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). For quantitative data that conforms to a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation was used as the measure of central tendency and dispersion. For quantitative data that does not conform to a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were employed as the preferred descriptive statistics. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. The differences between groups were analyzed with the t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and analysis of variance. The study further clarified the potential interactions among various omics, cytokine profiles, and clinical indicators by utilizing Spearman’s Rank Correlation. These interactions were subsequently visualized through network heatmaps. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s Rank Correlation coefficient, with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05. The analysis was conducted utilizing the corrplot package in R (version 3.4.1). GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and R were used for various analyses and chart preparation. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
