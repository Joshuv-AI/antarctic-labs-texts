Relationships between ChatGPT use with self-directed learning and critical thinking among school and university nurses in Taiwan
Authors: Li-Chun Chang, Li-Ling Hung, Ting-Wei Liu, Chun-Hsia Huang, Hui-Ling Lin, Li-Ling Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence such as ChatGPT play a key role in enhancing self-directed learning and critical thinking, yet few studies have explored their use among nurses. This study aims to examine the interrelationships among ChatGPT usage, self-directed learning, and critical thinking in Taiwanese school and university nurses.MethodsA cross-sectional survey used a nationwide stratified random sample of 600 nurses from 4,304 schools and universities. Instruments included the Critical Thinking Inventory, ChatGPT Adoption, and Self-Directed Learning Scale.ResultsAmong 413 respondents, younger and more educated nurses reported higher ChatGPT usage. self-directed learning, frequent ChatGPT usage, and employment at the junior high school level were significantly associated with higher critical thinking, with self-directed learning showing the strongest association.ConclusionsThis study of nurses serving schools and higher education institutions reported the associations of ChatGPT usage, with critical thinking and self-directed learning. Given the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data, the findings highlight associations that may inform future research on AI integration in education.ImplicationsThe findings suggest that promoting self-directed learning and the purposeful use of ChatGPT is significantly related to critical thinking among school nurses. Educational policies and professional development programs should prioritize these strategies, particularly for those in junior high schools or larger student populations, to support informed and autonomous clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04069-7.

Introduction

The cultivation of nurses’ critical thinking has been acknowledged as a cornerstone of professional nursing competencies and the provision of high-quality, evidence-informed care within a safe clinical environment [1]. This imperative has gained renewed significance in light of the rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, such as ChatGPT [2], which have precipitated a paradigmatic shift in the ways healthcare professionals retrieve information [3], formulate clinical judgments [4], and enhance physician–patient communication [5]. Previous studies have reported a positive association between ChatGPT usage and self-directed learning (SDL) [6, 7], including among students and nurses [8]. By providing direct and immediate feedback, additional reading resources, and conversational suggestions, generative AI has been used as a tool for SDL and as a potential facilitator of critical thinking [9, 10].

ChatGPT usage among nurses has been reported to range from 20% to 60% [8, 11], underscoring its growing relevance in nursing practice, particularly in academic writing and professional learning [12]. The frequency of use and intensity of interaction with ChatGPT are associated with critical thinking and problem-solving [13]. Generative AI has greatly impacted education, particularly student learning and engagement [14]. As students represent one of the groups most influenced by generative AI [15], school nurses serving these populations face a unique context where integrating generative AI into practice requires closer examination than in clinical settings. The “one-school-one-nurse” policy [16] ensures the presence of professional nursing personnel on school campuses to provide healthcare services, a regulation that was legally established in Taiwan as early as 2002 [17]. Moreover, school nurses’ work practices and engagement in continuing education are influenced not only by the characteristics of the schools they serve but also by their own professional commitment [18]. As they play an important role in safeguarding the health of the entire school community, critical thinking is essential for school and university nurses to sustain professional competence through continuous learning [19], while also linking it to improved cost-efficiency in school-based health service delivery [20].

The selection of school-based nurses as the target population was informed by two key considerations. First, critical thinking constitutes a foundational competency in their practice, underpinning essential functions such as emergency decision-making, chronic disease management, and the delivery of health education [16, 19]. Second, previous empirical studies revealed a comparatively higher prevalence of ChatGPT usage among nurses in non-hospital settings, particularly in schools [8]. Moreover, school and university nurses in educational settings are particularly expected to employ ChatGPT to enhance their work efficiency [21]. Accordingly, administrators are advised to assess employees’ critical thinking abilities prior to formulating AI policies for responsible clinical application [22].

Critical thinking is defined as the process of analyzing, evaluating, and improving thought based on intellectual standards. Within this framework, dispositions are viewed as central to learning and cognitive development and these are embodied in intellectual traits such as integrity, courage, humility, perseverance, empathy, and fair-mindedness. Such dispositions provide the foundation for sustained critical thinking and lifelong learning [23]. Disposition is regarded as a prerequisite for critical thinking skills, influencing whether the learned skills are actually employed in real-world situations [24]. Given that higher-order skills typically require structured instruction and are not readily measurable in a cross-sectional design [25], this study emphasizes dispositions as a more appropriate means of examining nurses’ underlying orientation toward critical thinking. The conceptual framework of self-directed learning (SDL) and critical thinking highlights that SDL fosters reflective inquiry and autonomous engagement through self-monitoring and self-management and these behaviors enhance learners’ willingness to analyze, evaluate, and reflect. Therefore, self-directed learning provides both the structural and motivational foundation for the development of critical thinking disposition [26].

Although generative artificial intelligence is increasingly influencing the roles of nursing professionals, most existing research has focused on hospital-based nurses. To date, few studies have examined ChatGPT usage among school nurses, and none have analyzed the relationship between generative AI use and critical thinking in this population. Therefore, this study investigates the associations of ChatGPT usage and SDL with critical thinking in a nationally representative sample of school and university nurses.

Methods

Aim of the study

This study aims to explore the interrelationships among ChatGPT usage, SDL, and critical thinking among school and university nurses. Specifically, it compares users and non-users, examines differences by frequency of usage, and identifies factors associated with critical thinking in both groups.

Research questions

RQ1

Are there significant differences in demographic characteristics, critical thinking, and self-directed learning between school and university nurses who use ChatGPT and those who do not?

RQ2

Among ChatGPT users, do different frequencies of usage (high vs. low-to-mid) correspond to significant differences in demographic characteristics, critical thinking, and self-directed learning?

RQ3

What are the significant factors associated with critical thinking?

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional online survey design using Google Forms, with all questions set as mandatory to prevent missing data, and it is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [27].

Study setting and sampling

The minimum sample size was determined to be 129 using G-Power 3.1.9.2 (linear multiple regression: fixed model, with an effect size of 0.15, power of 0.95, and alpha of 0.05) [28]. To account for a potential response rate of 40–50% based on a previous survey of school nurses [29], a sample of 600 school nurses was selected.

According to statistical data from Taiwan’s Ministry of Education, the country had 4,304 schools as of August 2024 [30]. The required sample size for each county and school level was determined based on their proportional distribution across the 22 counties and cities. A random number selection method was then applied to the school list to identify the sampled schools within each county.

Inclusion criteria were nurses employed in public or private schools at all levels in Taiwan who performed nursing-related duties in accordance with the Ministry of Education’s School Health Guidelines. Exclusion criteria were (1) substitute nurses, (2) nurses working in special education schools, who cared for students with special needs or had responsibilities distinct from those of nurses in general education settings, (3) nurses registered under the Occupational Health and Safety Department, and (4) nurses who planned to retire within the next year.

Data collection and procedure

The research team collected school addresses from websites for sampling purposes. A notification document containing a QR code linked to the online survey was distributed. The document explicitly invited school nurses to participate. For schools that had two nurses, the one with fewer years of experience was invited to respond to reduce the likelihood of including school nurses preparing for retirement. To improve response rates, a follow-up notice was mailed one month after the initial invitation. The survey was open from October 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024.

The web-based platform Google Forms required mandatory responses with real-time validation to prevent missing answers, ensuring complete data without the need for imputation or exclusions. Account-based login restricted each participant to a single submission, while no email addresses or IP were recorded, thereby preserving anonymity and preventing duplicate entries. A gift voucher worth $3 USD was mailed in paper form to randomly selected schools as a token of appreciation for the time spent completing the survey. This amount complies with the compensation limits set by the institutional review board in Taiwan. Importantly, the online questionnaire did not record IP addresses or school identifiers; therefore, neither the schools nor individual nurses could be identified, ensuring participants’ anonymity and data confidentiality.

Instruments

A total of four instruments were employed in this study to assess the key variables of interest. Detailed descriptions of each instrument are provided below, and the full item sets are available in Appendix A.

California critical thinking disposition inventory

Participants completed a critical thinking questionnaire based on the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory for Medical Students (CTDI-CM) [31] and the Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV) [32], which has been adapted and validated for use with nurses in Taiwan. The CTDI includes 18 items across three subscales: open-mindedness, analyticity/systematicity, and truth-seeking, with cumulative variances of 41.37%, 46.00%, and 49.59%, respectively. It demonstrates high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.924) and strong construct validity, with factor analysis confirming its three-factor structure. Convergent validity was supported through correlations with the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory-Chinese Version. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting a greater disposition toward critical thinking. The total score ranges from 18 to 90, with higher scores indicating stronger overall critical thinking disposition.

ChatGPT Use

Given the current absence of validated instruments specifically designed to measure ChatGPT-usage, this study employed the Technology Acceptance Model Edited for ChatGPT Adoption (TAME-ChatGPT), developed by Sallam et al. in 2023 [33]. This instrument has since been adapted and applied in our previous study [8]. In the scale, participants first respond to a question regarding prior use of ChatGPT for learning. Subsequently, we utilized the behavioral subscale of the TAME-ChatGPT instrument. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale, with responses scored as 1 = never, 2 = less than once a week, 3 = 1–2 times per week, 4 = 3–5 times per week, and 5 = daily or almost daily. Total scores range from 3 to 15, with higher scores indicating more frequent use of ChatGPT. The behavior subscale within the usage subscales demonstrated good reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.78.

Self-Directed Learning Scale (SDL)

The SDL scale was adapted from the SDL subscale of the Learner Readiness for Online Learning scale [34], which comprised five items. Factor loadings for SDL ranged from 0.69 to 0.83, with an internal consistency of 0.78. This scale has been applied in nursing-related clinical education and training [35]. Responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score, calculated as the sum of all five items, ranged from 5 to 25, with higher scores reflecting greater SDL ability.

Demographic Information

Demographic information was collected, including sex, age, marital status, work setting, educational level, years of school experience, and number of students served.

Validity and reliability for the instruments

To assess the validity and reliability of the instruments, we evaluated expert content validity and internal consistency for measures of ChatGPT-usage behavior, SDL, and critical thinking. Five experts in school nursing, nursing education, and artificial intelligence in nursing reviewed the content, yielding a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.85. According to Polit et al. [36] an item-level CVI ≥ 0.78 indicates adequate content validity, suggesting that the instrument demonstrated acceptable content validity in this study. Additionally, internal consistency was assessed with 20 nurses in a pilot test, yielding Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.82 for ChatGPT-usage behavior, 0.87 for SDL, and 0.91 for critical thinking. In the full study sample, Cronbach’s alpha values were further confirmed at 0.89, 0.91, and 0.92, respectively.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (No. 202301878B0) on January 28, 2024, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The online survey was conducted voluntarily and anonymously. Upon receiving the invitation, school and university nurses accessed the online questionnaire, reviewed the eligibility criteria, and read the study description. Participants who consented proceeded with the survey, while those who declined could withdraw at any time. No personal or institutional identifiers were collected.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 for Windows. Continuous variables following normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage (n (%)). Chi-square tests were used to examine differences in proportions, while independent-sample t-tests were conducted to compare mean differences between the two groups.

To account for potential clustering effects related to the participants’ county of service, a linear mixed-effects model was employed, specifying county of service as a random intercept. This modeling approach adjusted for non-independence of responses nested within geographic units. The model was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. However, the estimated variance for the county-level random effect was zero (Var = 0.000), indicating no meaningful between-county variability in critical thinking scores. Thus, no clustering effect was observed.

Moreover, to examine the robustness of the findings, we performed three sensitivity analyses excluding college- or university-affiliated schools (3.87%), restricting to single-nurse schools, and testing an age and ChatGPT-usage interaction.

The same set of covariates as in the primary analyses was retained to ensure comparability of effect estimates. The association between ChatGPT usage and critical thinking remained statistically significant (B = 2.67, β = 0.151, p = .002), with a similar effect size and direction as in the full sample (B = 2.97, β = 0.177, p = .002). This suggests that the observed relationship was not driven by this small subgroup and is robust across school types. Sensitivity analysis testing the interaction between age and ChatGPT usage was not statistically significant (B = − 0.188, β = –0.038, p = .070; Adj. R² = 0.104), indicating that no association between ChatGPT usage and critical thinking varied by age. (Detailed results are provided in Appendix B).

Based on the above testing, multiple regression analysis was employed. Bivariate correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations among age, years of school service, ChatGPT-usage behavior, critical thinking, and SDL among the 167 school nurses who had experience using ChatGPT. To identify factors influencing critical thinking, multiple linear regression analysis was applied. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values, and no concerns were identified (all VIFs < 2.0). Based on the previous study [37] indicating that a high frequency of ChatGPT use has significant implications, participants in this study were categorized according to their ChatGPT-usage behavior scores. Those scoring at or above the 75th percentile (Q3) were classified as the “high-score group,” while those below the 75th percentile were classified as the “low-to-mid score group” for regression analysis.

Results

A total of 413 questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 68.83%. The chi-square test (χ² = 0.66, p >.05) indicated no significant differences between the population distribution and the sample distribution (see Table 1), suggesting that the sample was representative in these dimensions and that post-stratification weighting was not warranted [38]. Senior high schools and colleges exhibited the highest response rates (80%), followed by elementary schools (69.73%). Additionally, ten elementary schools returned the mail, indicating the absence of a full-time school nurse at these institutions. Of all respondents, 62.47% (n = 258) were employed in elementary schools, whereas only 3.87% worked in college or university settings. No significant association was found between work settings and ChatGPT usage.

The descriptive analysis of demographic characteristics is presented in Table 2. The average age was 45.73 years (SD = 8.21, range = 23–64 years), with 70.0% younger than 50. Differences in ChatGPT usage by demographics, SDL, and critical thinking

Chi-square tests were conducted to examine differences in demographic variables between ChatGPT users and non-users, as shown in Table 2. Significant differences in ChatGPT usage behavior were observed across age and educational level. Younger participants were more likely to report using ChatGPT compared to older participants (81.4% vs. 18.6%; χ² = 18.24, p < .001). Similarly, those with higher educational attainment were more likely to use ChatGPT than those with lower levels of education (85.0% vs. 15.0%; χ² = 6.81, p = .009). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the mean scores of SDL and critical thinking between users and non-users, as indicated by independent t-tests.

Differences between high and low ChatGPT usage across demographic variables, self-directed learning, and critical thinking

Participants were categorized into high-use (n = 43) and low-use (n = 124) groups based on their mean scores on the ChatGPT-usage behavior scale. Group differences were examined across demographic characteristics, SDL, and critical thinking (see Table 3).

A significantly greater proportion of participants in the high-use group held a university degree or higher (95.4%) compared to the low-use group (82.3%) (χ² = 6.48, p = .011), suggesting that higher educational attainment may be associated with increased ChatGPT usage among school and university nurses. SDL scores were also significantly higher in the high-use group (M = 19.21, SD = 3.05) than in the low-use group (M = 17.75, SD = 2.72) (t = − 2.89, p = .004; Cohen’s d = 0.10), indicating that individuals with stronger SDL tendencies may engage more actively or effectively with ChatGPT. The most pronounced difference was observed in critical thinking scores, with high-use participants scoring significantly higher (M = 72.23, SD = 7.71) than those in the low-use group (M = 67.36, SD = 7.56) (t = -3.57, p < .001 Cohen’s d = 0.19). This finding shows that higher ChatGPT-use frequency was associated with stronger critical thinking disposition.

Although no significant differences were found between the two groups in age, marital status, years of service, teaching setting, or school size, three sensitivity analyses revealed that excluding university-affiliated nurses and adding an interaction term between age and ChatGPT usage, the association with critical thinking remained statistically significant. The interaction was significant (p = .020), suggesting a stronger association among younger nurses. These results confirm the robustness of the findings.

Table 5 summarizes the results of hierarchical regression analyses conducted to examine factors associated with critical thinking. In Model 1, which included only demographic variables, employment at the junior high school level was significantly associated with higher critical thinking scores compared to employment at the elementary level (B = 2.04, p < .05, 95% CI [0.04 to 4.03]). Conversely, working in schools with ≥ 751 students was significantly associated with lower critical thinking compared to schools with ≤ 750 students (B = -2.82, p < .01, 95% CI [-4.50 to -1.14[). Model 2 introduced ChatGPT-usage behavior, which was significantly and positively associated with critical thinking (B = 4.12, p < .001, 95% CI [2.11 to 6.14]), contributing to a significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 0.037, p < .001). This suggests that higher levels of ChatGPT usage are associated with better critical thinking.

Discussion

This study is the first nationally sampled investigation of the interrelationships between ChatGPT-usage behavior, critical thinking, and SDL among nurses across various educational levels. Given the lack of research on generative AI use among school nurses, studies involving hospital-based nurses are cited to provide relevant context. The participants’ age and years of work experience in this study were comparable to those reported in other study with a national sample of school nurses [39].

The adoption rate of ChatGPT in this study aligns with the findings of Chang et al. (2025) [8] but is higher than reports from other countries [11]. Given ChatGPT’s ability to generate text, its application in educational settings has garnered increasing attention, particularly in supporting the development of health education materials and content writing [12]. Developing health promotion plans is primarily the responsibility of nurses working at universities in Taiwan. Additionally, these nurses frequently participate in interdisciplinary collaborative teaching activities, which may make them more susceptible to peer influence within the educational system, thus increasing their tendency to use ChatGPT.

Educational level and age significantly influence ChatGPT adoption, with younger individuals and those with higher educational attainment being more likely to use the technology. This finding is consistent with a study by Kacperski et al. [40], which found that individuals with higher education levels and younger age groups demonstrated greater awareness and acceptance of high-tech products and were more likely to use ChatGPT.

Systematic literature reviews have indicated a positive relationship between ChatGPT usage, SDL, and critical thinking, primarily among student populations [10]. In contrast, the present study focused on school and university nurses and found no significant differences in SDL or critical thinking between ChatGPT users and non-users. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in learning tasks between workplace practitioners and students, which likely influence the development and manifestation of SDL and critical thinking disposition. However, differences in ChatGPT-usage frequency were associated with critical thinking. Previous studies on integrating generative AI into educational curricula have shown that higher frequency of usage during the intervention is associated with enhanced critical thinking among users [41, 42]. An eight-month longitudinal observation of students’ interactions with ChatGPT indicated that sustained usage was more likely once users had verified the tool’s accuracy. Trust in its outputs further reinforced continued engagement. This aligns with the findings of Yu et al. [43], who observed that users intending to continue using ChatGPT often engage in prompt refinement and revision. Such practices may foster iterative and critical interaction with the tool and encourage both prompt modification and reflective thinking [43]. Among all influencing factors, SDL was most strongly associated with critical thinking, aligning with the study of Song et al. [7], which emphasized that SDL involves critically analyzing one’s learning process and adjusting, a process closely linked to critical thinking.

Nurses serving in junior high schools and those working with fewer than 750 students tend to have higher critical thinking skills. Adolescents in junior high schools frequently encounter diverse and complex health problems, including psychological distress such as anxiety, sleep disturbances, and academic or future-related stress [44]. In the post-pandemic era, school nurses are required to adapt their care models to meet these growing challenges, which not only exceed the scope of younger children’s needs but also encompass mental health and substance-use issues [20]. Addressing such problems requires a stronger disposition toward critical thinking. Moreover, barriers to critical thinking such as workload exist [45]. Dolatowski et al. [46] noted that a 1:750 nurse-to-student ratio ensures safe health services, but exceeding this ratio impairs workload management, hindering critical thinking.

Limitations and future research

This study employed a nationwide sample of school and university nurses in Taiwan to examine the interrelationships among ChatGPT-usage behavior, critical thinking, and SDL. The results provide a basis for informing future educational curriculum interventions. Several limitations warrant caution. First, the cross-sectional, self-report design precludes causal inference; observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence that ChatGPT enhances critical thinking. Educational attainment and self-directed learning (SDL) may simultaneously influence both ChatGPT use and critical thinking disposition, potentially inflating the observed associations between them. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to establish causal relationships. Second, although a nationwide sampling frame was used, potential selection bias cannot be ruled out. For instance, the rule prioritizing less experienced nurses in schools with two nurses may have skewed responses toward early-career participants, and nonrespondents may differ systematically from respondents. Third, the measurement instruments (CTDI-CM, SDL, and ChatGPT-use subscales), though demonstrating acceptable internal consistency, were originally developed for other populations. Their construct validity for Taiwanese school nurses requires further confirmation through confirmatory factor analysis and measurement invariance testing. Fourth, clustering of participants within schools and counties was not fully modeled, which may affect standard error estimation. Fifth, ChatGPT-usage measures relied solely on self-reported frequency and intensity, without behavioral tracking or longitudinal assessment. This reliance on self-report introduces potential recall and social desirability bias and may overestimate associations. Finally, the sample may overrepresent nurses who are more interested in or comfortable with technology, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader school nurse population.

Conclusions

This study examined the demographic and institutional characteristics of nurses working in school and university settings, as well as the interrelationships between ChatGPT usage, frequency of usage, SDL, and critical thinking. Although the study was limited by its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, the findings indicate that simply using ChatGPT was not directly associated with SDL or critical thinking disposition. However, school nurses who reported higher levels of ChatGPT usage and stronger tendencies toward SDL were more likely to exhibit higher levels of critical thinking. Among the variables examined, SDL emerged as the most salient factor. Educational level was also associated with ChatGPT usage; among users, those with higher levels of usage demonstrated stronger critical thinking performance. Future studies are recommended to employ longitudinal designs supplemented by training programs to elucidate the dynamic relationships among ChatGPT usage, critical thinking, and SDL.

Given the observed associations, in-service training programs could consider integrating ChatGPT-related instructional content with strategies that foster critical thinking, such as case analysis and applied exercises. In addition, these programs should provide extended opportunities for self-directed practice to enhance the effective integration of generative AI into nursing practice. For nurses whose education level is below the university level, tailored training is essential to strengthen their SDL skills and analytical thinking. Furthermore, in schools with high student-to-nurse ratios, reducing workload through temporary staffing, administrative support, or the implementation of digital tools may provide nurses with greater capacity for reflective thinking.

Beyond individual-level educational interventions, school nurses must be supported in continuously learning how to effectively apply AI in school health services. This includes developing ethical guidelines for AI use in nursing practice and systematically integrating AI literacy and critical thinking competencies into both pre-service and in-service nursing curricula. Future research should further explore how such system-level strategies can promote the sustainable, ethical, and equitable use of generative AI in educational and healthcare contexts.
