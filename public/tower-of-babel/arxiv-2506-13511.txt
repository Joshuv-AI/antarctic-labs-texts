Many-body Localization and Poisson statistics in the Quantum Sun model
Wojciech De Roeck, Amirali Hannani

Abstract
The Quantum Sun model is a many-body Hamiltonian model of interacting spins arranged on the half-line. Spins at distance n n from the origin are coupled to the rest of the system via a term of strength α n \alpha^{n} , with α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . From theoretical and numerical considerations, it is believed that this model undergoes a localization-delocalization transition at the critical value α = 1 2 \alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} . We prove that, for α ≪ 1 2 \alpha\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} , the model is localized and that its spectral statistics is Poissonian. The main interest of this result is that the model is a genuine many-body model. In particular, the number of independent disorder variables grows only logarithmically with the Hilbert space dimension.

Model and results

1.1
Introduction

The localization/delocalization phenomenon has been a central theme in mathematical physics even since the pioneering work of P. Anderson
[
4
]
. The question is whether certain random linear operators have eigenvectors that are largely supported on a few basis vectors, or equally distributed over essentially all eigenvectors, with respect to some natural basis. Initially, this question was mainly studied in the context of Schrödinger operators with random potenials, see
[
39
,
28
,
17
,
2
]
, but in the last decades it was also considered in many other models of random operators or matrices.
Besides the eigenvector properties, the localization/delocalization dichotomy is often discussed on the level of spectral statistics, with localization corresponding to Poissonian statistics, whereas delocalization corresponds to GOE or GUE statistics, depending on the symmetry class.

In the past 20 years, the focus in the physics community has been on localization/delocalization in
many-body systems

[
8
,
23
,
36
]
: In the language of quantum mechanics, the original work on the Schrödinger operator was concerned with a single quantum particle (or a wave), i.e. a single degree of freedom. In many-body models, the number of degrees of freedom is proportional to spatial volume, and hence it tends to infinity. The localization/delocalization phenomenon needs a bit more setup and subtlety to be discussed in this framework. However, the biggest challenge is that the mathematics seems considerably harder and rigorous results are scarce. Indeed, the results fall short of clearly establishing a localized or delocalized phase, though Some preliminary steps were taken in
[
12
,
3
]
and in
[
25
]
. There are also results near spectral edges
[
18
,
37
,
33
,
16
,
9
,
44
]
or for mean-field models
[
29
,
5
,
32
]
, but, on a technical level, those results seem to be unrelated to the problem of many-body localization.

1.3
Fock-space Localization

By the term ”localization”, we refer to the situation where most eigenstates are predominantly supported on one or a few eigenstates of the uncoupled system, i.e. with
\alpha=0
. Our first theorem states that we have localization in this sense. The uncoupled model is denoted by
H_{n}^{(\mathrm{free}^{\prime})}
and it is displayed in (
10
).
To describe its eigenvectors, we introduce some notation.
Let
\Sigma_{n}=\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be the set of
configurations
. To each configuration
\sigma=(\sigma(i))_{i=1,\ldots,n}\in\Sigma_{n}
, we can associate a one-dimensional projector
P_{\sigma}
given by

P_{\sigma}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\chi(Z_{i}=\sigma(i))

Similarly, we let

P_{\sigma_{\geq\ell}}=\prod_{i\geq\ell}\chi(Z_{i}=\sigma(i))

Obviously the spectral projectors of
H_{n}^{(\mathrm{free}^{\prime})}
are of the form
P^{(B)}_{j}\otimes P_{\sigma_{\geq n_{B}+1}}
for some spectral projector
P^{(B)}_{j}
of
H_{B}
and some
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
of which the first
n_{B}
components are irrelevant.

Let
\psi_{j}
be the unit eigenvectors of
H_{n}
, ordered according to increasing eigenvalue. The constants
C,c,c^{\prime}
in what follows can depend on
n_{B}
, but not on
n
or
\alpha
.

Theorem 1
.

There is
0<\alpha_{0}<1/2
such that the following holds for all
0\leq\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
. There is a random variable
\ell_{*}
with values in
\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}(\ell_{*}\geq\ell)\leq\alpha^{c\ell}

and such that the following holds: There are bijections
\{1,\ldots,2^{n}\}\to\Omega_{n}:j\to\sigma(j)
for

, such that, for all

and for all
\sigma\in\Omega_{L}
,

\langle\psi_{j(\sigma)},P_{\sigma_{\geq\ell}}\psi_{j(\sigma)}\rangle\geq 1-\alpha^{c^{\prime}\ell},\qquad

This theorem hence states that we can associate to any eigenvector
\psi_{j}
a configuration
\sigma(j)
and that this assignment is in particular meaningful from spin
\ell_{*}
onwards. To make the connection with more traditional measures of localization explicit, we introduce the inverse participation ratio
\mathrm{IPR}(\psi_{j})
w.r.t. the basis defined by
\sigma\in\Omega_{L}

\mathrm{IPR}(\psi_{j})=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}|\langle\psi_{j}|\sigma\rangle|^{4}\right)^{-1}

(2)

Here we slightly abused notation by writing
\sigma
for the unit vector in the range of the 1d projector
P_{\sigma}
.
Our result above implies that

\mathrm{IPR}(\psi_{j})\leq C{2^{\ell_{*}}}

(3)

uniformly in the choice of eigenvector
\psi_{j}
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is short and intuitive, see Section
3.1.3
, and the bound (
3
) follows in a straightforward way. In fact, Theorem
1
and the bound (
3
) do not need the variables

to be random. In particular, if
g_{i}=g
for some fixed

, then these statements would hold just as well, as one can easily verify by inspection of the short proof. In contrast, the random variables

are crucial for our proof.

In contrast, in the delocalized phase, one expects that, for some

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}\log\mathrm{IPR}(\psi_{j})\approx c^{\prime\prime}n

(4)

with high probability.

Phrased in this way, the localization-delocalization dichotomy is reminiscent of the picture that is expected for random Schrodinger operators or that is proven for some types of random matrices.
It is however a bit different from what is expected for many-body models, where (
4

1.4
Level statistics

First, we state the assumption on the bath Hamiltonian
H_{B}
.

Assumption 1

(Non-degeneracy assumptions on
H_{B}
)
.

H_{B}
is non-degenrate and non-anti-degenerate: Let us denote the eigenvalues of
H_{n_{B}}
by
\{E^{(B)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2^{n_{B}}}
. Then our assumption is that

\min_{i\neq j}|E^{(B)}_{i}-E^{(B)}_{j}|>0,\qquad\min_{i,j}|E^{(B)}_{i}+E^{(B)}_{j}|>0.

(5)

We now turn to the eigenvalue process of
H_{n}
, locally around the origin.
Let us denote the eigenvalues of
H_{n}
by
(E_{i})_{i=1}^{2^{n}}
, ordered such that
E_{i}<E_{i+1}
. We define the following point process:

\xi_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}\delta_{(1/s_{n})E_{j}}(dx),

(6)

where

s_{n}:=\mathsf{s}\times 2^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n},

(7)

is the mean eigenvalue spacing around energy
0
, where
\mathsf{s}^{2}=\frac{1}{12}
is the variance of
h_{i}
and
g_{i}
.

Let
\bar{\xi}
be the standard Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity one and denote the corresponding expectation by
\bar{\mathbb{E}}
. The expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}
.
Our main result states that
\xi_{n}
converges to
\bar{\xi}
in an appropriate way. To state this more precisely, let
C_{K}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of compactly supported non-negative continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
. For any point process
\xi
and
\varphi\in C_{K}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
, we abbreviate

\xi(\varphi):=\int\varphi(x)\xi(dx).

(8)

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. There is
0<\alpha_{0}<1/2
such that the following holds for all
0\leq\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
: For all
\varphi\in C_{K}^{+}(\mathbb{R})

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\xi_n(\varphi))\right]=\bar{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp(-
\bar{\xi}(\varphi))\right].

(9)

This implies that
\xi_{n}
converges to
\bar{\xi}
in distribution with respect to the vague topology on Radon measures.

The convergence of point processes follows in a standard way from (
9
), see e.g.
[
34
]
Section 1, and we will not discuss this further.

Let us explain the role of Assumption
1
. The non-degeneracy of the bath
H_{B}
is necessary because we do not have any assumptions that ensure that the bath is sufficiently well-coupled to the rest of the chain. In particular, let us take
H_{B}=Z_{1}
with
n_{B}>1
, such that spins indexed by
i=2,\ldots,n_{B}
are not coupled to the rest of the chain. Then, every eigenvalue of
H_{n}
with
n\geq n_{B}
, has a degeneracy that is at least
2^{n_{B}-1}
-fold and hence there cannot be Poissonian statistics.

The assumption on anti-degeneracy, i.e. the second condition in (
5
), is more subtle. To understand it, let us take
n_{B}=0
and
\alpha=0
, that is, we have the trival Hamiltonian

H^{(\mathrm{free})}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}Z_{i}.

Somehow surprisingly, for this Hamiltonian, Theorem
2
is not valid.
To see this, note that the spectrum of
H^{(\mathrm{free})}_{n}
is invariant under reflections through the origin, hence the statistics cannot be Poissonian at the origin. It would be Poissonian at the origin if we would shift the eigenvalues, i.e. if we replace
E_{j}
in (
6
) by
E_{j}-E
, with
E
any non-zero and
n-
independent value.

Alternatively, if we consider the Hamiltonian (still without coupling between bath and the rest of the chain)

H^{(\mathrm{free}^{\prime})}_{n}=H_{B}+\sum_{i=n_{B+1}}^{n}h_{i}Z_{i},

(10)

with
H_{B}
satisfying Assumption
1
, then Theorem
2
does hold. The symmetry of the spectrum has been shifted from the origin to the values
E^{(B)}_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,2^{n_{B}}
. We do not rule out that the coupled model
H_{n}
contains such shifted symmetries in its spectrum.
Finally, we note that Assumption
1
could be avoided by choosing
H_{B}
randomly from some appropriate random matrix ensemble, but we find that this just obscures the situation and therefore we keep the assumption explicit.

Dissolution of resonances

We state a third theorem, which needs more setup. Let us fix a parameter
\theta\in(2/3,1)

Given the eigenvalues

\{1,\ldots,2^{n}\}
into so-called resonant patches:
Two consecutive levels
E_{i+1},E_{i}
belong to the same resonant patch if and only if
E_{i+1}-E_{i}\leq\alpha^{\theta n}

The rationale for such a definition goes as follows. The perturbation that couples the spin at site

We have not been able to establish that

We write
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}

\sum_{k=1,2,\ldots}k|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|=2^{n}

Our result reads

Theorem 3

Assuming the same assumptions as for theorem

\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{1}|}{2^{n}}\to 1,\qquad\text{almost surely}

This theorem is a crucial ingredient in the proof of Theorem

1.6
Some elements of the proof

Let us first discuss
Theorem
1
.
We relate the model at length
n
and
n+1
through an intermediate model, by writing

H_{n+1/2}=H_{n}+h_{n+1}Z_{n+1},\qquad H_{n+1}=H_{n+1/2}+\alpha^{n+1}g_{n+1}X_{1}X_{n+1}.

The spectrum of
H_{n+1/2}
is easily related to that of
H_{n}
. Indeed, let us write
\sigma(\cdot)
for the spectrum, then

\sigma(H_{n+1/2})=(\sigma(H_{n})+h_{n+1})\cup(\sigma(H_{n})-h_{n+1})

(11)

The resonant patches of
H_{n+1}
are in one-to-one correspondance with the resonant patches of
H_{n+1/2}
, by a judicious choice of resonance threshold. The operator
H_{n+1/2}
(and consequently
H_{n+1}
) has two types of resonant patches:

1.

Old patches
: These are inherited from the previous scale”, i.e. they belong to only one of the sets on the right-hand side of (
11
), and are therefore just a shifted and deformed copy of a resonant patch of
H_{n}
.

2.

New patches
: These are created or modified in the
fusion
process above, i.e. they contain eigenvalues from both sets on the right-hand side of (
11
).

We observe that the probability of having new patches decays exponentially with
n
for
\alpha^{\theta}<1/4
. This is a straightforward computation, see Lemma
5
. Combining this statement with basic tools from spectral perturbation theory, we deduce Theorem
1
.

The Proof of
Theorem
3
is considerably more challenging. To prove this theorem, we need to show that, assuming no new resonant patches are created from a certain scale,
most
old non-singleton resonance patches will eventually dissolve, i.e. they break up into a collection of singletons. Notice that each patch gives rise to two patches at the next scale: one by the shift
+h_{n+1}
, and the other by the shift
-h_{n-1}
. In the absence of the interaction, the process of dissolution is straightforward: the inter-level distances remain constant (because of the absence of interaction), whereas the resonance threshold shrinks exponentially. The non-degeneracy of the Hamiltonian implies then the dissolution for large
n
.
We hence need to exclude that the interaction would systematically pull nearby eigenvalues closer together.
To exclude such an effect, we exploit the random variables
g_{n+1}
. In fact, we do a detailed analysis of the perturbation series at each scale using the Cartan and Remez inequalities. To this end, we exploit a ”proxy” quantity, namely the spectral width
s(p)
of the resonant patch. Using perturbation theory and the mentioned inequalities, we show that the probability (in
g_{n+1}
) of the spectral width shrinking at the same rate as the resonance threshold, is small. This means the patch will eventually break into smaller patches. Repeating this analysis proves our theorem.

The proof of
Theorem
2
uses Theorem
3
as a crucial input, but it still makes up the bulk of our paper.
We prove it essentially by moment methods.
First, we recall that the eigenvalues of
H_{n}^{(\mathrm{free})}
are labeled by configurations, i.e. elements of
\Sigma_{n}=\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. By Weyl’s inequality (see Lemma
4
), we can use this labeling also for the interacting model, although this of course does not imply anything about the eigenvectors.
We denote hence the eigenvalues of
H_{n}
by
E_{\mu}
with
\mu\in\Sigma_{n}
. Given a
k
-tuple
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\in\Sigma_{n}
, the main building block of the proof is the ”
factorization property
” of
(E_{\mu_{1}},\dots,E_{\mu_{k}})
:
The joint probability distribution function (pdf) of
(E_{\mu_{1}},\dots,E_{\mu_{k}})
is a product of pdf’s of
E_{\mu_{i}}
, up to some error that is small when
n
is large and
k
is
O(1)
, and only for
most
of such
k-
tuples. Once such a factorization property is established, the Poisson property for
H_{n}^{(\mathrm{free})}
follows in a natural way.
The proof of factorization can be split into four steps.

1.

We first prove the factorization property for the free model
H_{n}^{\text{(free)}}
. This is done by the local central limit theorem and by the Brascamp-Lieb inequality: The joint pdf and product pdf are sufficiently close to a certain Gaussian.

2.

Then we define the so called
semi-perturbed
process. To this end, we decompose
n=n_{0}+n_{1}
such that
n_{0}=\varrho n
, with a judicious choice of
0<\varrho<1
. The semi-pertubed process consists of the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{H}_{n}:=H_{n_{0}}+H_{n_{1}}^{\text{(free)}}
(cf. (
67
) ) and these eigenvalues are denoted by
\widetilde{E}_{\eta}
,
\eta\in\Sigma_{n}
. We choose
\varrho
such that
\alpha^{n_{0}}\ll s_{n}\ll\alpha^{\theta n_{0}}
. The first inequality
\alpha^{n_{0}}\ll s_{n}
ensures that
\widetilde{E}_{\eta}
is a good approximation to
E_{\eta}
while the second inequality ensures that non-resonant levels of
H_{n_{0}}
remain non-resonant.
The second step of our strategy, is then to prove the factorization property for most
k-
tuples of the semiperturbed process. This is mostly achieved by appealing to properties of Gaussians and to results from the previous step. The key point here is that pdf of eignevalues of
H_{n_{0}}^{\text{(free)}}
varies only on scales of order
\sqrt{n_{0}}
. This allows us to control perturbations of order
\alpha
.

3.

In the third step, we control contributions of
non-typical

k
-tuples: the ones for which the factorization does not hold. In this step, we make use of the fact that non-singleton resonant patches are rare (Theorem
3
).

4.

Having the previous three steps, we deduce Poisson statistics by certain standard approximation arguments.

H_{n}

\left|E_{(\sigma,\mu_{n})}-(E_{\sigma}+h_{n}\mu_{n})\right|\leq\alpha^{n},

with
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n-1}
and
\mu\in\{\pm 1\}=\Sigma_{1}
. More generally, eigenvalues of
H_{n-1+m}

satisfy

\left|E_{(\sigma,{\mu})}-(E_{\sigma}+h.{\mu})\right|\leq 2\alpha^{n},

where
{\mu}\in\sigma_{m}
and
h.{\mu}=\sum_{i=n}^{n+m-1}h_{i}\mu(i)
.

Proof.

The first bound follows by Weyl’s inequality since
||H_{n-1/2}-H_{n}||\leq\alpha^{n}
. To get the second bound, we apply the first bound inductively and estimate
\alpha^{n}+\alpha^{n+1}+\ldots
by
2\alpha^{n}
, using that
\alpha<1/2
.
∎

3

3.1
Terminology and preliminaries

3.1.1
The filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}

We define also the subalgebras for
n\geq n_{B}

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{g_{\leq n},h_{\leq n}\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n+1/2}=\{g_{\leq n},h_{\leq n+1}\}

Recall
H_{n}
.
We introduce now also

H_{n+{1}/{2}}:=H_{n}+h_{n+1}Z_{n+1}.

So that in particular

H_{n}=H_{n-1/2}+g_{n}\alpha^{n}X_{1}X_{n}.

We note that
H_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable and
H_{n+1/2}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n+1/2}
-measurable.

3.1.2
The events
\mathcal{A}_{n_{0},n_{1}}

We recall from Subsection
1.5
that the spectrum of
H_{n}
is partitioned into resonant patches. The partition is called
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and we write
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
for the set of resonant patches with
k
levels.
For
n\geq n_{B}
, the spectrum of
H_{n+1/2}
is determined in a simple way from the spectrum of
H_{n}
, namely

\mathrm{spec}(H_{n+1/2})=\bigcup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}((p+h_{n+1})\cup(p-h_{n+1}))

where
p+x=\{E+x\,|\,E\in p\}
.
This means that the spectrum of
H_{n+1/2}
can be viewed as the union of
p+\mu h_{n+1}
with
(p,\mu)\in\mathcal{P}\times\{\pm 1\}
.
We let
\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}
be the event that all these sets at larger distance than
\alpha^{\theta n}
from each other, i.e.

\mathrm{dist}(p+\mu h_{n+1},p^{\prime}+\mu^{\prime}h_{n+1})>\alpha^{\theta n},\qquad\forall(p,\mu)\neq(p^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})

For later use, we note that this implies also that

\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Conv}(p+\mu h_{n+1}),\mathrm{Conv}({p^{\prime}+\mu^{\prime}h_{n+1}}))>\alpha^{\theta n}

where, for any set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
, the interval
\mathrm{Conv}(S)=[\min S,\max S]
is the convex hull of the set
S
. This implication follows from the fact that none of the sets
p+\mu h_{n+1}
can be partitioned in two subsets such that these subsets are at distance larger than
\alpha^{\theta n}
from each other. Recall that this was the defining property of the collection
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
We will also write, for
n_{1}>n_{0}\geq n_{B}
;

\mathcal{A}_{n_{0},n_{1}}=\mathcal{A}_{n_{0},n_{0}+1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{n_{0}+1,n_{0}+2}\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{A}_{n_{1}-1,n_{1}}

and we note that this remains also meaningful for
n_{1}=\infty
.
We note that

\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}\in\mathcal{F}_{n+1/2}.

The event
\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}
has high probability, uniformly in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, namely:

Lemma 5
.

For any
n,m
, including
m=\infty
;

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n,n+m}|\mathcal{F}_{n})\geq 1-C\alpha^{c_{\theta}n},\qquad\mathbb{P}-\text{almost surely}

Proof.

For a finite set
X\subset\mathbb{R}
, the set

\{h\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,\mathrm{dist}(X-h,X+h)\leq\epsilon\}

has Lesbegue measure bounded by
\epsilon|X|^{2}
, where
|X|
denotes the number of elements of
X
. Taking
X=\mathrm{spec}(H_{n})
, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}^{c}|\mathcal{F}_{n})
is bounded by
4^{n}\times\alpha^{\theta n}
almost surely, since for this choice of
X
, above event coincide with
\mathcal{A}_{n,n+1}^{c}
given that

is finite. This yields the claim for
m=1
. Notice that the same bound holds for
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}^{c}_{n,n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n^{\prime}})
for all

, thanks to the tower property of the conditional expectation. A union bound and proper use of the mentioned tower property yields the claim for any

, including
m=\infty
.
∎

3.1.3
Spectral patches and resonant patches

Any resonant patch
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
is in particular a set of eigenvalues of
H_{n-1}
and so we can consider the spectral projector
P_{p}
associated to that patch. Let
\delta_{x}
be the Kronecker delta, i.e.
\delta_{0}=1
and
\delta_{x}=0
if

Then, for

does not contain any interaction of the spin at site

with the other spins.

Let us now assume that the event
\mathcal{A}_{n-1,n}
is satisfied.
We will study the spectrum of
H_{n}
as a perturbation of
H_{n-1/2}
.
Since
||H_{n}-H_{n-1/2}||\leq\alpha^{n}
is smaller than half of the smallest distance between sets

with
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1},\mu\in\{\pm 1\}

Lemma 6

for any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
and
\mu\in\{\pm 1\}
, there is a

of

(i.e. a set of eigenvalues), emanating from the spectral patch

Its cardinality is unchanged:

2.

3.

The endpoints of

4.

The corresponding spectral projector

(12)

Proof.

These claims follow by basic analytic perturbation theory (cf.

26

Section 1 and 4, and cf. Appendix

Given such a spectral patch

Which of these alternatives occurs, is the central question studied in the upcoming subsection

If the spectral patch does not dissolve, i.e. if

Let us introduce some notation to systematize these considerations:
Let us assume that the event

We write now

\underline{\mu}=(\mu_{n},\ldots,\mu_{n+m-1})\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}

\underline{\mu}
to its first

was simply called

Let

is well-defined because the event

Let us now assume that the event

.
Therefore, for any

is a spectral patch of

, whose convex hull is sufficiently far away from the convex hull of other such spectral patches. Just as above, we can then consider the perturbed spectral patch

that we also denote by

, referring back to the original patch

at scale

, and recalling that
\underline{\mu}_{2}=(\mu_{n},\mu_{n+1})

We can now again ask whether

.
If the answer is ”yes”, then we continue the procedure. If, for a given

the answer was ”yes”,
\ell-1

for all

and we can ask meaningfully ask whether
S_{p,\underline{\mu}_{\ell}}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1+\ell}

The event

The whole point will be that this sequence of consecutive ”yes” is highly unlikely as

,

4
Anti-resonances

This section is very analogous to Section
3
. Just as in that section, we proved that the relative number of resonances decreases with scale, we will now prove the analogous statement for anti-resonances. The analogy is not perfect; whereas the discussion of resonances proceeded via a partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
of
\Sigma_{n}
, we discuss anti-resonances by considering the set of anti-resonant 2-tuples:

\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\{(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in\Sigma^{2}_{n}\,\big|\,\left|{E}_{\sigma}+{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\right|<\alpha^{\theta n}/4\},

(48)

for
n>n_{B}
. Let us note that

1.

We allow for the case where
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
.

2.

If
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}_{n}
, then
E_{\sigma^{\prime}},E_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}
are resonant. In particular, for any
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
, there is at most one resonant patch in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
that contains levels that are anti-resonant with
\sigma
.

Let us now state the main result of this section;

Theorem 16
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}\subset\Sigma_{n}
be the set of
\sigma
such that, for some
\sigma^{\prime}
,
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}_{n}
.
Then, almost surely,

\frac{|\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}|}{2^{n}}\rightarrow 0.

4.1
Preliminaries

One of the first steps in Section
3
was the definition of events
\mathcal{A}_{n_{0},n_{1}}
. On theses events, it was guaranteed that all resonances at scale
n_{1}
could be related to resonances at scale
n_{0}
.
The analogous event for anti-resonances for

is

\mathcal{G}_{n,n+1}=\{\forall\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{n}:\min_{\nu=\pm 1}|{E}_{\sigma}+{E}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+2\nu h_{n+1}|>\alpha^{\theta(n+1)}\}

We will also write, for

;

\mathcal{G}_{n_{0},n_{1}}=\mathcal{G}_{n_{0},n_{0}+1}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n_{0}+1,n_{0}+2}\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{G}_{n_{1}-1,n_{1}}

.
We note that, just as it was the case for the event

, we have

Given

\sigma_{F}
for the collection
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in F}
and
\sigma_{D}
for the collection
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in D}
. Then,
\mathcal{C}_{n}(D,F,v,\varsigma)
is defined as the set of
\underline{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}
satisfying

1.

\sigma_{F}
is linearly independent (when viewed as a
|F|
-tuple of elements in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). If
\varsigma=1
,
\sigma_{F}
is
(|F|,n)
-typical, as defined in (
59
). If
\varsigma=0
, it is non-typical.

2.

For all
i\in D
:
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j\in F}v_{i,j}\sigma_{j}
. In case
|D|=0
, this condition is absent.

We note that the set of classes
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is a covering of
\Sigma_{n}^{k}
but the classes are not disjoint.
At first sight, one might think that the number of classes is uncountable, because any choice of a real matrix
v
corresponds to a different class. However, it turns out that all but a finite number of matrices
v
yield empty classes.

Lemma 38
.

There is a constant
C(k)
such that the following holds:
If a class
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n}(D,F,v,\varsigma)
is non-empty, then all entries of
v
are of the form
q_{1}/q_{2}
with
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|q_{1}|,|q_{2}|\leq C(k)
.

Proof of Lemma
38
.

Choose an
F
-tuple
\sigma_{j},j\in F
in the non-empty class. It is linearly independent by definition. Since the column rank of a matrix equals its row rank, one can find a
|F|
-tuple of sites
x_{i},i=1,\ldots,|F|
such that the square
|F|\times|F|
matrix

M_{i,j}=\sigma_{j}(x_{i})

is non-singular.
This means that, for any
z\in D
, the vector
(v_{z,j})_{j\in F}
is the unique solution of

\sum_{j\in F}M_{i,j}v_{z,j}=\sigma_{z}(x_{i})\quad\forall i.

By basic algebra, we can construct this solution. Since the determinant of the matrix
M_{i,j}
and of its minors is an integer whose absolute value is between
1
and
|F|!\leq k!
, the solution is of the claimed form.
∎

Naively, the number of multiconfigurations
\underline{\sigma}
in a class
\mathcal{C}
, is at most
|\Sigma_{n}^{F}|=2^{n|F|}
. However, for most classes, the number of multiconfigurations is exponentially smaller, as we show now:

Lemma 39
.

Consider an abstract class
\mathcal{C}(D,F,v,\varsigma)
with
|D|>0
, such that there is at least one
i\in D
such that
v_{i,j}
is nonzero for at least two values of
j\in F
, then

|\mathcal{C}_{n}(D,F,v,\varsigma)|\leq(2^{|F|}-1)^{n}=(1-2^{-|F|})^{n}|\Sigma_{n}^{F}|.

(125)

Proof.

Let us abbreviate
v_{j}=v_{i,j}
for (one of) the
i
as in the statement, and all
j\in F
.

For any
x\in[n]
, introduce
\tau_{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{F}
by

\tau_{x}(j)=\sigma_{j}(x),\qquad j\in F.

We argue that there is at least one element in
\{\pm 1\}^{F}
that does not occur, i.e. there is no
x
such that
\tau_{x}
equals that element.
First, note that for any site
x
,

(\sum_{j\in F}v_{j}\tau_{x}(j))^{2}=(\sum_{j\in A}v_{j}-\sum_{j\in A^{c}}v_{j})^{2}={(\sigma_{i}(x))^{2}}=1,

(126)

where
A=A_{x}=\{j\in F:\tau_{x}(j)=1\}
and
A^{c}=F\setminus A
.
Assume now that for any subset
A\subset F
, there is a
x_{A}\in[n]
such that

\tau_{x_{A}}(j)=\chi(j\in A)-\chi(j\in A^{c})
. We will show that this leads to a contradiction.
Applying (
126
) once at
x_{A}
and once at
x_{F}
, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\sum_{j\in A}v_{j}-\sum_{j\in A^{c}}v_{j})^{2}-(\sum_{j\in A}v_{j}+\sum_{j\in A^{c}}v_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=4(\sum_{j\in A}v_{j})(\sum_{j\in A^{c}}v_{j})

(127)

Since this has to hold for any choice of

, we conclude that there is at most one
j\in F
such that
v_{j}\neq 0
. We have thus arrived at a contradiction given the initial assumption on
\mathcal{C}
.
We conclude hence that there is at least one element in
\{\pm 1\}^{F}
that is not realized at any
x
. This means that the number of multiconfigurations
\tau
(which is equal to the number of multiconfigurations
\sigma
) in the class is bounded by
(2^{|F|}-1)^{n}
.

∎

7.5
Computation of moments

In this section, we put the machinery of the previous sections to work to actually compute some moments. For an interval
\mathcal{I}
, recall is rescaled version as
I=s_{n}\mathcal{I}
. Then we recall
\widetilde{N}_{n}(\mathcal{I})
(
122
) be the number of ”approximate levels” inside that interval, i.e.

\widetilde{N}_{n}(\mathcal{I})=\sum_{\eta\in\Sigma_{n}}\chi(\widetilde{E}_{\eta}\in s_{n}\mathcal{I}).

Previously, we had a collection of interals
\{\mathcal{I}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Let us first take all these intervals to be equal, i.e.
\mathcal{I}_{i}=\mathcal{I}
, and we simply write
I
for
s_{n}\mathcal{I}
. We will compute the
k
’th moment

\mathbb{E}_{A}(\widetilde{N}^{k}_{n}(\mathcal{I}))
, using corollary
41
and the terminology introduced before that corollary. This computation will not be needed in what follows, but we find it useful to present it first because the later computation (ending with (
159
)) will rely on the same ideas, but with much more notational overhead.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}(\widetilde{N}^{k}_{n}(\mathcal{I}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[k]}\right)

(152)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\sum_{\eta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[k]}\right)+o_{n}(1)+\epsilon C(K,k)

(153)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}|\mathcal{I}|^{|\mathcal{P}_{k}|}+o_{n}(1)+o_{K}(1)+\epsilon C(K,k),

(154)

where we made the dependence on
k
in the partitions explicit by writing
\mathcal{P}_{k}
, and where

is the number of partitioning sets in the partition
\mathcal{P}_{k}
. The second equality is Corollary
41
, and the last equality follows from
Lemma

.
We recognize the expression

as the
k
’th moment of a Poisson distribution with rate
|\mathcal{I}|
. Indeed, all cumulants of a Poisson distribution are equal to its rate.

Next, we choose
p
intervals
\mathcal{J}_{j},j=1,\ldots,p
such that the intervals are
pairwise disjoint
. These intervals are just like the intervals
\mathcal{I}_{i}
considered earlier, but we need to denote them by a different symbol to avoid a clash of notation in what follows.
Our goal is to compute

(155)

for naturals

.

First, we define the index sets

K_{1}=[k_{1}],\qquad K_{j}=[k_{1}+\ldots+k_{j}]\setminus K_{j-1}\quad\text{for $j=2,\ldots,p$}.

We can now cast the expectation (
155
) in a sum of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{[k]})
with

and with the rescaled intervals

determined by

Furthermore, we note that any partition of

can be interpreted as a partition of

by shifting by

, i.e. the partition

We are now ready to compute (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}((\widetilde{N}_{n}(\mathcal{J}_{1}))^{k_{1}}\ldots(\widetilde{N}_{n}(\mathcal{J}_{p}))^{k_{p}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[k]}\right)

(156)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\sum_{\eta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[k]}\right)+o_{n}(1)+\epsilon C(K,k)

(157)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{k_{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\\
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{k_{p}})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\mathcal{E}_{[k]}\right)+o_{n}(1)+\epsilon C(K,k)

(158)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}}|\mathcal{J}_{j}|^{|\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}|}+o_{n}(1)+o_{K}(1)+\epsilon C(K,k).

(159)

The first equality was explained above and the second equality is
consequence of Corollary

that any partition

that is not of the form
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{k_{p}})
leads to an event

are pairwise disjoint. Finally, the last inequality follows from Lemma

This result will be the starting point of the next section.

Appendix B

Lemma 50
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables, with an absolutely continuous (w.r.t Lebesgue measure) density
\varphi(x)dx
. Furthermore, assume that
\varphi
is bounded and compactly supported, and
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=0
, and
\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2})=\mathsf{s}^{2}
.
Denote pdf of
X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
by
\varphi^{*n}
, meaning
n
times convolution of
\varphi
. Then we have

\|\varphi^{*n}-\mathfrak{g}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}=o_{n}(1/\sqrt{n}),

(182)

where we recall
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
as the pdf of the centered Gaussian with variance
n\mathsf{s}^{2}
.

Proof.

Abberiviate
\varphi_{n}:=\varphi^{*n}
, by definition we have:

\sup_{x}\sqrt{n}|\varphi_{n}(x)-\mathfrak{g}_{n}(x)|=\sup_{x}\left|\sqrt{n}\varphi_{n}(\mathsf{s}\sqrt{n}x)-\sqrt{n}\mathfrak{g}_{n}(\mathsf{s}\sqrt{n}x)\right|=:\Delta_{n}.

Thanks to the Gnedenko version of the Local central limit theorem for densities
\mathsf{s}\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(cf. Theorem 1., Section 46, Chapter 8, Part III of
[
20
]
). We also refer to this link of
Terry Tao’s blog
for the more pedagogical version of this theorem. Notice that the assumptions of the mentioned theorem (existence of the second moment of
X_{i}
, and the fact that
\varphi_{n}\in L^{r}
,
1<r\leq 2
for some
n
) are obviously satisfied by our more restrictive assumptions on
\varphi
. This finishes the proof.

Appendix C

Lemma 51

(Counting lemma)
.

For
\underline{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k})\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}
, recall the definition of
\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}(\underline{\sigma})
for

\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{k-1}
(
58
). Then, given for any
\tau
,
\ell_{\tau}\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}

such that
\sum_{\tau}\ell_{\tau}=n
, we have:

\left\{\underline{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}\big|\>|\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}(\underline{\sigma})|=\ell_{\tau},\>\forall\tau\right\}=2^{n}{n\choose\underline{\ell}}=2^{n}\frac{n!}{\prod_{\tau}\ell_{\tau}!},

(183)

where above product is for
\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{k-1}
; moreover by
\underline{\ell}
, we mean a
2^{k-1}
-tuple constructed by
\{\ell_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
via a fixed but arbitrary ordering.

Proof.

Thanks to the construction of
\{\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
, given
\sigma_{1}
, and
\{\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
, the configuration
(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k})
is determined uniquely; moreover, given the configuration
(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k})
,
\sigma_{1}
and
\{\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}

can be constructed uniquely (a bijection exists here). Therefore, the above counting problem is equivalent to counting all sets
\{\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
, with
\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
|\mathfrak{J}_{\tau}|=\ell_{\tau}
. This is a rather well-known counting problem, and the solution is given by the so-called multinomial coefficients (cf.
[
40
]
Section 1.2) as written in (
183
).
