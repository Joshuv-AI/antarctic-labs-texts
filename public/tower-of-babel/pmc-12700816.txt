A comprehensive review of catheter dislodgement: prevalence, clinical impact, and innovations in securement devices
Authors: John T. Moon, Hanzhou Li, Emily Patel, Nathan Zhang, Shreyas Naidu, Qiyue Ma, Chang Zhou, Janice Newsome, Yue Chen, Zachary Bercu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Catheter anti-dislodgement technologies seek to reduce unplanned removals using validated securement strategies such as adhesive, subcutaneous, and breakaway devices. These systems minimize complications and are shaping new procedural standards for vascular and non-vascular care. This review summarizes recent progress, clinical burdens, risk factors, and regulatory considerations. We also propose strategic directions for standardization, implementation, and future integration of sensor-enhanced systems into routine practice.

Introduction

Catheters are ubiquitous to clinical practice across all specialties and are utilized in high frequency within acute and intensive care settings. Broadly defined, a catheter is a flexible tube inserted into the body to provide access, most commonly venous access (e.g., peripherally inserted central catheters [PICCs], central venous catheters [CVCs]) or to facilitate drainage (e.g., urinary or biliary diversion, abscess drainage, or pleural drains). Their ubiquity, however, belies a significant vulnerability to dislodgement. Dislodgement of a catheter from its target site, and device mechanisms to prevent dislodgement (i.e., anti-dislodgement), remain a persistent clinical challenge. In the following review, the term “anti-dislodgement technology” denotes engineered devices, including mechanical, chemical, or mechanico-chemical, for which their primary intent is the prevention of unintended catheter removal or migration. Catheter dislodgement can provoke complications ranging from local injury and pain, treatment delays (chemotherapy, dialysis, nutritional supplementation), and persistent infection, all of which can contribute towards increased healthcare costs1. Unplanned catheter removal may interrupt treatment, provoke local or systemic complications, and necessitate emergent replacement. These events can lead to local trauma, infection, extended hospitalization, patient distress, and considerable economic burden2–4.

Over the past decade, novel anti-dislodgement technologies have emerged to address this issue. These include adhesive-based stabilization devices, subcutaneously anchored systems, and tension-activated breakaway connectors, among others. Yet, despite their promise, technological adoption has been inconsistent across institutions, in part due to the fragmented nature of evidence, cost considerations, and the absence of standardized protocols5–7. This review synthesizes the current landscape of catheter securement by addressing the prevalence and consequences of catheter dislodgement, surveying existing and emerging securement strategies, reviewing the clinical evidence supporting their use, and exploring regulatory, patient-centered, and economic perspectives. By synthesis and clarification of the literature, we aim to support clinicians, administrators, and policymakers in reducing dislodgement-related harm.

Burden and risk factors

Vascular catheters

Vascular catheters are among the most commonly placed devices in the hospital and have myriad uses in medication administration, parenteral nutrition, hemodynamic resuscitation, and use in plasma exchange, stem cell collection and most commonly hemodialysis with larger bore catheters. Peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) specifically are placed in over 60–90% of hospitalized patients8 with an estimated 300 million insertions annually in the U.S. alone. Despite their routine use, PIVCs have a strikingly high dislodgement rate up to 24.5% before therapy completion9.

Clinical studies have corroborated this burden. A randomized controlled trial in an emergency department setting reported that 14% of PIVCs became dislodged within 48 h when standard securement with a polyurethane tape was used10. Even CVCs, which are placed with greater invasiveness and secured with sutures, are not immune. In the intensive care setting, non-tunneled CVCs have a reported dislodgement rate of approximately 1.5 catheters per 1000 catheter-days, while PICCs in oncology populations report unplanned removal rates ranging from 9.6% to 15%11,12.

The consequences of dislodged vascular catheters include local extravasation, phlebitis, local tissue injury, and bloodstream infections13,14. Each failure necessitates additional cannulation attempts, further exposing the patient to trauma. From a cost perspective, these repeat dislodgements add up to significant hospital costs. For reference, a single failed PIVC may cost $35–45 in materials and nursing time, while replacing a central line may cost hundreds (at bedside) to thousands of dollars (fluoroscopically-guided) depending on the procedural setting and complication profile15,16. One study estimates that unplanned PIVC dislodgement affects more than 75 million catheters annually in the U.S., resulting in $1.8 billion in healthcare expenditure13. Vulnerable populations include pediatric patients secondary to mobility, and geriatric patients due to cognitive impairment and skin fragility. The inconsistency in securement practices across institutions further compounds the risk14,17.

Non-vascular catheters

Non-vascular catheter dislodgement presents a similar clinical challenge. Non-vascular catheters include devices such as enteral feeding tubes (naso-/oro-gastric, gastrostomy, gastrojejunostomy, jejunostomy), urinary catheters (suprapubic, foley), percutaneous drains (nephrostomy, cholecystostomy, and biliary-/abscess-/chest- drains), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drains (ventriculostomy) also share in the prevalent clinical challenge of dislodgement.

Gastrostomy and gastrojejunostomy tubes (G/GJ-tubes) have reported dislodgement rates of up to 13.4% in adult populations with the highest risk occurring within the first month after placement and before tract maturation17. Urinary catheters, such as commonly utilized indwelling Foley catheters, are unintentionally removed at a rate of 0.32 catheters per 100 catheter days of hospitalized patients, especially among those with delirium or reduced awareness18–20. Similarly, chest tubes (5.4%), CSF shunts (2.6%), and to a lesser degree tunneled peritoneal catheters (1.5%) face complications of anti-dislodgement21–23.

These events frequently trigger emergency imaging, re-intervention, and operative repair in some cases. They extend hospital stays and increase risks related to anesthesia and radiation exposure. The emotional distress and physical discomfort for patients and caregivers are also substantial.

Securement technologies

The past two decades have seen a proliferation of catheter securement strategies, moving beyond traditional methods like sutures or tape. These novel approaches can be categorized into four major mechanisms: (1) adhesive-based stabilization, (2) subcutaneous anchoring systems, (3) tension-activated breakaway connectors, and (4) experimental or hybrid innovations. Each category presents distinct benefits and trade-offs that must be balanced against patient risk, and clinical context.

Adhesive-based securement devices

Adhesive securement devices use polyurethane or silicone adhesives to stabilize catheter hubs to the skin, often incorporating mechanical frames or clips. Examples include StatLock (Fig. 2A), CathGrip (Fig. 2B), Grip-Lok (Fig. 2C), and Tegaderm CHG. Redirection block mechanisms of action redirect tension and torsion that would otherwise be exerted directly onto a catheter (Fig. 2D). These devices aim to eliminate the need for sutures and reduce dressing-related complications. The SECURED trial, a multicenter randomized study in pediatric patients requiring long-term central access, demonstrated a 4.4-fold reduction in dislodgement rates when SecurAcath was used compared to traditional adhesive devices (5.2% vs. 22.7%)24. Additionally, the anchored group had fewer dressing-related complications and reduced dressing change time by 41%, offsetting the higher initial device cost through labor savings and decreased complications.

Disadvantages include increased insertion and removal pain, which can be mitigated with local anesthesia and provider experience. Nonetheless, SASS technology is increasingly recommended for catheters anticipated to dwell ≥14 days, especially in patients at high risk for dislodgement.

Tension-activated breakaway connectors

Breakaway connectors provide a dynamic alternative to static securement (Fig. 4). These in-line devices (e.g., Orchid Safety Release Valve [SRV]) are designed to disconnect when subjected to tensile force, approximately 3.25 pounds of pull, thereby preventing the force from reaching and dislodging the catheter itself13. Upon separation, internal valves close automatically, preserving sterility and preventing fluid loss.Fig. 4Breakaway connector for tension-activated catheter release.A Mated configuration. The male (blue) and female (white) luer halves are coupled, permitting unobstructed fluid flow through the line. B Separation under excessive traction. When axial force exceeds a preset threshold, the halves disengage (blue arrows), and spring-loaded check valves within each segment close instantaneously, preventing fluid leakage and air ingress. Reconnection is achieved by simple push-fit once the hazardous tension has been relieved.

These devices are especially valuable in settings where patients may be confused or restless, such as intensive care units, emergency departments, or long-term care facilities. Early clinician feedback indicates high usability and minimal workflow disruption. However, appropriate staff training on reconnection protocols is necessary to optimize performance.

Despite promising early data, these technologies often lack large-scale RCT validation. Nevertheless, they exemplify the future of patient-specific and sensor-integrated catheter stabilization. In sum, securement technologies have evolved into a diversified landscape, each tailored to different clinical needs. Choosing the appropriate method requires consideration of catheter type, patient mobility and cognition, dwell time, and provider familiarity. The following section reviews the comparative effectiveness data supporting these interventions.

Patient-centered considerations

Catheter securement strategies must be evaluated in terms of patient experience, comfort, and usability. Patient-centered factors including pain, skin integrity, ease of mobility, anxiety, and caregiver burden play a critical role in determining both adherence and overall outcomes.

Pain and tolerability

Pain associated with securement varies by device. Adhesive-based systems (e.g., StatLock, Grip-Lok) are generally well-tolerated and non-invasive, causing minimal discomfort after initial application. In contrast, subcutaneous anchoring systems like SecurAcath are associated with increased pain at insertion and removal29. In the SECURED trial, patients receiving SecurAcath reported higher discomfort scores during these phases, although no significant differences in overall device satisfaction were found at follow-up29. Use of local anesthetic and proper removal technique mitigates much of this discomfort.

Tissue adhesives (cyanoacrylate) offer the benefit of stabilizing the catheter with minimal surface bulk and have been associated with a mild stinging sensation on application, but generally good tolerability. Nasal bridles may cause transient nasal soreness but are often preferred over repeated reinsertions by both patients and caregivers, particularly in pediatric or ICU populations27.

Skin integrity and MARSI

Medical Adhesive-Related Skin Injury (MARSI) is a growing concern, especially in oncology, pediatric, and geriatric populations. One multi-center study found that nearly 42% of patients experienced MARSI at the insertion site (central venous, nasogastric, nasoenteral, and indwelling urinary), with higher risk among those over age 50 and those requiring frequent dressing changes30. To reduce MARSI risk, newer securement systems utilize low-trauma adhesives (e.g., silicone borders) and integrated antimicrobial dressings. For patients with fragile skin, silicone-based adhesive dressings or cyanoacrylate glue may offer a gentler alternative to traditional polyurethane tapes. Subcutaneous anchors avoid surface adhesives altogether, which may be advantageous in patients with adhesive allergies or severe dermatitis.

Regulatory and guidelines landscape

Clinical practice guidelines and regulatory oversight play a pivotal role in shaping the adoption of catheter securement technologies. As the evidence base has matured, professional societies and government bodies have increasingly emphasized securement as a core component of safe catheter care.

U.S. guidelines and standards

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), in its updated 2024 guidance for prevention of intravascular catheter-related infections, endorses the use of sutureless securement devices for central venous catheters. The guidelines emphasize that minimizing catheter movement through proper stabilization reduces the risk of mechanical phlebitis and bloodstream infections by preventing micro-motion at the insertion site31.

The Infusion Nurses Society (INS), in its 2024 Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice, dedicates an entire section to catheter securement32. The standards mandate that: (1) All vascular access devices (VADs) must be stabilized using engineered stabilization devices (ESDs) or integrated dressings; (2) securement should not compromise skin integrity or circulation; (3) pediatric and neonatal securement should accommodate mobility and skin fragility; and, (4) subcutaneous anchoring systems are appropriate for long-term VADs and require user competency for safe insertion and removal. The INS also recognizes tissue adhesive and breakaway connectors as adjunctive strategies that can improve securement in high-risk populations or environments with high line disruption frequency.

With regard to non-vascular catheters, the Society of Interventional Radiology (SIR) states that, “catheters draining abscesses or fluid collections are typically anchored to the skin with the use of nonabsorbable suture or adhesive devices.”33. No specific guidance is provided on securement method or combination strategies to prevent displacement of abscess, biliary, nephrostomy, and other percutaneous catheters, perhaps deferring to operator preferences. Similarly, the American College of Radiology describes procedural technique and success criteria of nephrostomy tube placement without mention of fixation or stabilization technique, but rather cautions that, “care to prevent tube dislodgement…must be exercised”34. The Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society of Europe (CIRSE) similarly has recommendations on placement and follow-up imaging for percutaneous catheters, but does not specify how catheters should be anchored to reduce dislodgement35. The non-specificity of recommendations may represent a notable gap in guidance for the securement of nonvascular catheters.

FDA oversight and human factors engineering

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies most securement devices as Class I or II medical devices, depending on their mechanism and level of invasiveness. Devices like StatLock are generally exempt from premarket approval but must meet quality systems regulations36. Subcutaneous anchors and breakaway valves, due to their potential impact on patient safety, undergo 510(k) clearance processes requiring biocompatibility, usability, and human factors testing37. Human factors guidance issued by the FDA in 2016 specifically highlights the importance of designing medical devices that reduce the likelihood of use error. For securement systems, this means: (1) clear instructions for use; (2) safe removal methods to avoid skin or tissue trauma; (3) fail-safe design elements (e.g., breakaway devices that auto-seal). The FDA also requires manufacturers to demonstrate that new securement products are compatible with standard catheter hub geometries and do not interfere with catheter performance or medication delivery.

Future directions

The evolution of catheter securement is poised to move beyond passive mechanical resistance toward proactive and intelligent systems that anticipate and prevent failure. The next frontiers can hence be conceptualized along two axes: the development of intelligent monitoring ecosystems and the maturation of implementation to bridge the gap between innovation and routine practice.

Integration of smart sensor technologies

The next generation of anti-dislodgement systems will incorporate a suite of miniaturized sensors to provide a continuous view of both patient and device status. Micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS), particularly accelerometers and gyroscopes, will be integrated into securement devices or wearable patches to quantify patient activity38. These sensors can objectively measure parameters like acceleration, orientation, and movement patterns, which are key inputs for identifying high-risk states such as patient agitation, frequent transfers, or fall events that often precede dislodgement. For instance, a three-axis accelerometer in a pacemaker can already adjust pacing rates based on physical activity39. A similar sensor on a catheter securement device could create a dynamic risk profile, differentiating a sleeping patient from one who is restless and at high risk of accidental line removal40. The data from a single tri-axial accelerometer mounted on the trunk, for example, has been shown to accurately recognize distinct activities typical of hospitalized patients, such as lying in bed versus walking or wheelchair transport41. Additionally, securement dressings themselves can become an active sensing platform for catheter dislodgement. E-textiles, created by weaving conductive fibers (e.g., silver, carbon) and flexible electronic components into fabrics, can be designed to monitor multiple parameters, such as piezoresistance and tension, continuously42,43. A multi-modal approach provides critical context; for example, an accelerometer detecting sudden movement is ambiguous on its own, but when combined with a simultaneous signal from an e-textile pressure sensor indicating a sharp increase in line tension and a biometric sensor showing a spike in heart rate, the system can confidently identify a forceful, high-risk pull on the catheter.

Addressing implementation barriers

Implementation barriers often originate in the design of the device itself. Different device designs may not fit into clinical workflows, and may be met with resistance, workarounds, and eventual abandonment by the end users. Rigorous usability testing in simulated clinical environments can uncover design flaws before a device reaches the market44. For securement devices, this could involve testing how easily a nurse wearing gloves can apply the device, or how intuitive the removal process is under time pressure. The goal is to design “forcing functions” i.e. features that make it difficult or impossible to perform an incorrect action.

Ultimately, the adoption of new, often more expensive, securement technology depends on demonstrating a clear return on investment (ROI) to hospital administrators and procurement committees. This requires a shift from a price-based to a value-based justification. One study of a bundled device strategy for peripheral venous catheters found that while the innovative bundle was slightly more expensive per day, it led to a mean saving of €42 per patient by reducing the frequency of unscheduled catheter removal and replacement45. Future analysis should quantify the savings from avoided re-interventions, reduced length of stay, lower pharmacy costs (from fewer infections/complications), and decreased nursing time spent on replacing failed catheters. Presenting data as the “cost per dislodgement avoided” provides a powerful metric for decision-makers45.

To move beyond ad-hoc and often unsuccessful device rollouts, implementation science provides evidence-based frameworks, ensuring interventions are effectively adopted and sustained in real-world settings. The CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research) framework helps identify barriers and facilitators to implementing a new securement device, while the RE-AIM framework evaluates its real-world impact across reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance (RE-AIM)46,47. Limitations

Our narrative review is limited by the available limited and heterogenous literature on anti-dislodgement mechanisms, specifically varied in terms of randomized trials versus observational studies, study design, follow-up duration, and patient populations. Furthermore, as cost-effectiveness was variably reported and heterogeneous in terms of clinical setting and potential complications, our review did not focus on quantifying the true economic impact of anti-dislodgement technologies.

Conclusion

Catheter dislodgement is a common yet under-recognized complication that affects millions of patients annually across vascular and non-vascular domains. Its consequences may be preventable through evidence-based securement strategies. As healthcare systems seek to reduce avoidable harm and optimize resource utilization, addressing dislodgement through thoughtful device selection and standardization becomes imperative. Modern securement technologies have redefined what is possible. Adhesive stabilization devices, subcutaneous anchors, breakaway connectors, and tissue adhesives have each demonstrated clinical effectiveness in reducing dislodgement rates and related complications. The integration of these innovations, supported by strong regulatory and guideline endorsements, should be considered a standard of care rather than an optional enhancement. However, no single securement strategy fits all clinical contexts. Successful implementation requires tailoring choices to catheter type, patient-specific risk factors, anticipated dwell time, and care setting. Equally important is embedding securement protocols into institutional workflows, nursing education, and performance improvement initiatives. Looking forward, future advancements will likely involve sensor-enhanced platforms, bundled kits for consistent application, and personalization frameworks guided by patient data. Embracing this evolution will allow clinicians and institutions to proactively address dislodgement risk, enhance patient safety, and reduce the hidden costs of unplanned device loss.
