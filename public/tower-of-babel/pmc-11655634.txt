Modulating self-assembly and polymorph transitions in bisdendronized squaramides
Authors: Sergi Bujosa, Llorenç Rubert, Carmen Rotger, Bartolome Soberats
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Supramolecular self-assembly is an advanced approach for constructing ordered nanoscale architectures with broad applications. While the principles of supramolecular polymerization have been thoroughly explored in artificial small molecules, polymer transformations remain barely explored, likely due to the lack of suitable reference models presenting well-defined and reversible transitions between aggregates. In this study, we introduce a series of bisdendronized squaramides (SQs) 1-3, showcasing complex self-assembly behaviours involving four distinct aggregates, three different interaction patterns, and various thermodynamically controlled polymorph transformations. Notably, SQ 3, with ethyl spacers between the SQ cores and the dendrons, exhibits a concentration and temperature-dependent equilibrium among three polymorphs: the particle-like Agg-A and fibrillar Agg-C, formed by slipped hydrogen bonds, and the fibrillar Agg-B, formed by head-to-tail hydrogen bonds. Additional solid-state experiments revealed that these SQs also form columnar liquid crystals, assembled by π–π interactions in SQ 1 and hydrogen bonding in SQ 2 and SQ 3. This work positions SQ units as valuable models for understanding polymorph equilibrium in solution and solid-state, which is crucial for developing stimuli-responsive supramolecular polymers.

Introduction

Over the past decades, significant strides have been made in understanding the underlying principles governing supramolecular polymerization1–5. In this context, benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxamides (BTAs)6–9 and bifunctional ureidopyrimidinones (UPys)10–13 have emerged as reference models in the construction of artificial supramolecular polymers providing multitopic hydrogen-bonding (H-bonding) patterns and yielding robust and stable self-assembled structures. Owing to their properties, the BTA motif has become ideal for studying the impact of structural mutations and the influence of solvents on the polymerization and copolymerization, while UPys have revealed as relevant moieties to develop materials with a great potential in chemical biology14,15. While early studies in the field focussed on molecular design of monomers and the thermodynamics of supramolecular polymerization processes, recent research has shifted towards exploring the kinetic aspects, unveiling the crucial role of pathway complexity16–19. However, there are no binding units inherently introducing pathway complexity to supramolecular systems, and many studies relied on the use of monomers with flexible designs combining amide groups linked to other functional molecular scaffolds20–22.

For instance, the introduction of amide units linked by flexible spacers to organic dyes like porphyrins23,24 and perylene bisimides (PBIs)25,26, among others27–32, resulted in a successful strategy to develop systems showing pathway complexity. Several studies have explored the obtention of kinetically trapped monomeric or aggregated species that evolve with time to more stable species. However, one of the most intriguing concepts unveiled through these studies, is the discovery of supramolecular polymorphism33–38. For example, it has been reported a PBI functionalized by two 3,4,5-trisdodecyloxybenzamide (dendron) groups via alkyl spacers, capable to form up to three different aggregated species with completely different self-assembly modes and optical properties33. Remarkably, the three different polymorphs were attained through different self-assembly pathways by applying different sample processing techniques, and are stable under similar conditions. Thus, controlling supramolecular polymorphism as well as polymorph transformations has become key to generate structural diversity without de novo molecular design, and therefore becoming a valuable tool for the development of precision materials and stimuli-responsive systems. In this context, understanding the correlation between supramolecular polymorphism in solution and in the solid state is also a crucial area of interest. For instance, it has been shown that functional systems can exhibit distinct assembly modes in solution versus the liquid crystalline state, a factor that is key for the development of advanced solid-state materials38–41.

We have recently focused our studies on the self-assembly of squaramide (SQ) units42,43, which have emerged as versatile binding units for supramolecular polymerization capable of interacting through multiple H-bonds and π−π interactions. Early examples of SQ supramolecular polymers utilized strong head-to-tail interactions to form fibrillar aggregates44–46. This interaction pattern, involving quadruple H-bonds, provides an additional aromatic gain in the polymer reinforcing its overall stability44. In contrast, thiosquaramide derivatives seem to prefer polymerizing via π−π interactions, which demonstrate that slight structural modifications in the SQ structure alter their self-assembly behaviour47. While the polymerization of SQ by π−π has not been reported, a recent work described that they can generate pathway complexity by the modulation of their H-bonding patterns48,49. For instance, it has been reported a bis-SQ macrocycle that can form two distinct aggregates depending on the conformation of the SQ units, which can adopt the Z,E or Z,Z forms depending on the solvent composition48. In the same line, an asymmetric SQ was shown to exhibit an uncommon thermoreversible polymorph transition promoted by a change in the H-bonding pattern from ill-defined H-bonds to the more common head-to-tail interactions49. These works highlight the versatility and potential of SQ units as simple yet powerful H-bonding entities in generating supramolecular diversity and stimuli-responsive materials. However, more research is needed to fully understand their self-assembly characteristics in both solid state and solution, and gain control over self-assembly processes and, more importantly, over the intriguing polymorph transitions49,50.

In this study, we present a series of bis-dendronized SQs (1-3) with varying spacers between the dendrons (3,4,5-trisdodecyloxybenzene groups) and the central SQ core (Fig. 1a), that all together provide a comprehensive self-assembly behaviour of SQs involving up to four distinct supramolecular assemblies in methylcyclohexane (MCH) (Fig. 1). SQ 1 formed unprecedented J-aggregates (Agg-J) through π-π interactions (Fig. 1b), while SQs 2 and 3 produced particle-like aggregates (Agg-A) and a fibrillar assembly (Agg-C), both via slipped H-bonding (Fig. 1c, d). Additionally, SQ 3 also formed a lower temperature fibrillar assembly based on head-to-tail H-bonds (Agg-B). Remarkably, SQ 3 exhibited an intriguing behaviour, presenting up to three polymorphs in equilibrium (Fig. 1d); a concentration-dependent 3-Agg-A/Agg-B and the temperature-dependent 3-Agg-A/Agg-C equilibria. Furthermore, we unveil for the first time the liquid-crystalline (LC) properties of this series of SQs forming columnar phases, stabilized by π−π interactions (SQ 1) or head-to-tail H-bonds (SQs 2 and 3). Results

Design principles

To unveil the principles governing the assembly mode of SQs we designed the compounds 1-3. All these compounds are based on a disecondary SQ core symmetrically functionalized with two 3,4,5-trisdodecyloxybenzene groups (dendrons) to provide solubility in organic solvents as well as LC behaviour. While the dendrons are directly attached to the SQ core in the case of 1, methylene and ethylene spacers were introduced in the cases of 2 and 3, respectively (Fig. 1a)49. We anticipated that the nature/length of these spacers may have a strong influence on the self-assembly mode of the three compounds, providing different degrees of steric effects and conjugation in the systems. For example, in the case of 1, the conjugation between the squaramide unit and the dendronic part may favour the assembly by π−π interactions while in squaramide 3, with less congestion near the SQ core, stronger H-bonding interactions may be favoured.

SQs 1-3 were obtained by direct reaction between diethyl squarate and the corresponding dendronic amine/aniline. All the compounds were obtained in moderate to good yields and were isolated as white-brownish powders (Supplementary Information).

Self-assembly UV/vis investigations

Initial supramolecular polymerization experiments were carried out using UV/vis spectroscopy, as SQ units absorb in the UV region and undergo significant spectroscopic changes upon aggregation44–49. We initially conducted variable temperature (VT) experiments using MCH, which is known to be a suitable solvent for self-assembly studies of SQs with bulky dendrons48,49. Pleasingly, SQ 1 showed two distinct UV/vis profiles at 363 and 278 K (Fig. 2a), which was considered an indication of aggregation. However, the VT experiments (1 K min-1) revealed that SQ 1 undergoes a gradual decomposition when subjected to high temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 4). Alternatively, the supramolecular polymerization of 1 was studied through solvent-dependent (denaturation) experiments, which were performed at 278 K using CHCl3 as a denaturing solvent51. In CHCl3, SQ 1 exhibits a broad band peaking at 320 nm, which was assigned to the monomeric species of the SQ unit conjugated with the aniline rings (Fig. 2b). The UV/vis denaturation experiment showed a gradual bathochromic shift of the UV band of SQ 1 from 320 nm to 360 nm by increasing the ratio of MCH (Fig. 2b). This behaviour was associated with the formation of an aggregate where the conjugated SQ units undergo J-type coupling52,53, and therefore this species was labelled as Agg-J. Plotting the degree of aggregation (αagg) vs. the volume fraction of CHCl3 provided the aggregation curve that fit well with an anti-cooperative self-assembly process (Fig. 2c)54,55, according to the denaturation model reported by Meijer and coworkers51. The ΔG0 for the nucleation-elongation process at 1.0 × 10–5 M was determined to be −53.6 kJ mol–1, with a σ value of 9.5 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 1). The supramolecular polymerization of compounds 2 and 3 could be studied directly by VT UV/vis experiments in MCH between 365 and 285 K since they showed good thermal stability. Both compounds 2 and 3 exhibited the characteristic (non-conjugated) SQ broad monomeric band peaking at 285–290 nm at high temperatures (365 K), ascribed to HOMO − 1, HOMO → LUMO, LUMO + 1, and HOMO − 1, HOMO → LUMO + 2, LUMO + 3 bright electronic transitions (Fig. 2d, g)48. Interestingly, compounds 2 and 3 showed two regimes in the evolution of their UV patterns during the cooling processes; first a supramolecular polymerization process by cooling to 300 K, followed by aggregate transformation by further decreasing the temperature (Fig. 2d, e, g, h). Interestingly, these aggregate conversions were reversible and were different between 2 and 3, as explained below. First, we studied the polymerization processes from 365 to 300 K, where the monomeric UV pattern of SQs 2 and 3 experiences a general broadening of the absorption pattern (Fig. 2e, h), which was accompanied by a blueshift of the absorption maxima towards 275 nm and the appearance of a shoulder around 320 nm. This behaviour was consistent with the formation of an aggregated species named as Agg-A49. For both compounds, the fraction of aggregated species (αagg) was plotted against the temperature (at λ = 320 nm for SQ2 and at 310 nm for SQ3) at different concentrations, resulting in sigmoidal aggregation curves (Fig. 2f, i, and Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). Thermodynamic analysis of the plots confirmed the isodesmic mechanism for the polymerization of SQs 2 and 3, with ΔG0 of −25.6 and −27.8 kJ mol−1, respectively (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3)58. Nevertheless, it should be noted that for SQ 2, the polymerization process (365–300 K) overlaps with a subsequent transformation of Agg-A at lower temperatures (vide infra), which may affect the accuracy of the fitting results.The broad nature of the absorption pattern and the formation mechanism of Agg-A, indicate that this aggregate probably consists of an ill-defined morphology based on relatively weak slipped H-bonded interactions (Fig. 1c)49.

As mentioned previously, further cooling of 2 and 3 solutions, after the formation of Agg-A, led to new aggregated species formed via thermodynamic processes, with no significant kinetic effects (Supplementary Figs. 7,8). The cooling of SQ 3 in MCH from 297 to 278 K promoted the appearance of a completely new UV pattern consisting of two intense and well-defined bands with maxima at 274 and 329 nm (Fig. 3a). This pattern was assigned to a new aggregated species named Agg-B, which was formed by head-to-tail H-bonds, according to various reports on SQ supramolecular polymers44,48,49. This two-band pattern is explained by the excitonic coupling and the effect of the permanent (static) dipole moment generated in the 1D head-to-tail squaramide assemblies, that distinctly affect the HOMO − 1, HOMO → LUMO, LUMO + 1, and HOMO − 1, HOMO → LUMO + 2, LUMO + 3 transition dipole moments, which are perpendicular to each other45. Thus, these changes in the spectral features of 3 in MCH upon cooling to 278 K were not part of a supramolecular polymerization process and correspond to a thermally induced aggregate transformation (Agg-A to Agg-B)49. On the other hand, by further cooling the SQ 2 sample in MCH from 308 to 278 K, we observed a significant broadening of the UV band with a prominent increase of the shoulder at 320 nm (Fig. 3c). This new UV pattern was significantly different from that of 3-Agg-B (Fig. 3a), discarding the evolution to a head-to-tail aggregate for SQ 2. This aggregate was named Agg-C, and it exhibited a broader UV pattern than Agg-A, suggesting that it is also quite disordered. Figure 3d shows the complete cooling curves for SQ 2 at different concentrations where two regimes can be observed by decreasing temperature: the polymerization process, and the Agg-A-Agg-C conversion. Remarkably, the thermal transformation from Agg-A to Agg-C for compound 2 occurred in a larger temperature range (Fig. 3d) than the Agg-A-Agg-B transition for compound 3 (Fig. 3b). It is anticipated that Agg-C is a morphological evolution of Agg-A while both assemblies present a similar interaction mode as it is described below.

According to the UV/vis aggregation studies, all three compounds underwent supramolecular polymerization processes in MCH. While compounds 2 and 3 aggregated to form Agg-A via isodesmic processes, compound 1 followed an anti-cooperative mechanism to form Agg-J. This difference is likely related to the interaction patterns and packing features involved in the supramolecular polymerization processes (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Moreover, while SQ 1 forms only one aggregated species (Agg-J), SQs 2 and 3 undergo further transformation of Agg-A upon cooling the samples (Fig. 3), which is an uncommon phenomenon.

Morphology studies

To figure out the morphologies of the different aggregated species formed by SQs 1-3, AFM experiments were carried out. For these experiments, the MCH solutions of the corresponding SQs 1-3 aggregates were spin-coated onto mica or HOPG substrates at the appropriate temperature and concentration and were measured by tapping mode at room temperature (see Methods section). AFM images of aggregate 1-Agg-J showed the formation of particle-like morphologies with a height of ca. 2.4 nm (Fig. 4a). Particles with larger dimensions (2–19 nm) were also observed in the Agg-A species formed by compounds 2 (2-Agg-A) (Figs. 4b and 3) (3-Agg-A) (Supplementary Fig. 12a). In contrast, when compound 2 was coated at lower temperatures, fibrillar structures were observed (Supplementary Fig. 11c), corresponding to Agg-C. The fibrillar 2-Agg-C structures are likely formed by the assembly of smaller particles, as seen in the AFM images (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Surprisingly, AFM images obtained for SQ 3 at 318 K and higher concentrations (1 × 10⁻⁴ M) also showed fibrillar structures with a height of 1.6 nm, composed of aggregated small particles (Fig. 4c). This suggests that SQ 3 also formed Agg-C at higher concentrations, as it will be discussed below. Finally, elongated and well-defined fibres were observed for 3-Agg-B, with a height of approximately 1.0 nm and lengths exceeding 100 nm (Fig. 4d). In parallel to the morphologies, the interaction modes for all the 1-3 aggregated species were further assessed by FT-IR experiments in MCH carried out at 5 × 10−3 M and different temperatures. The FT-IR spectrum of 1-Agg-J (5 × 10−3 M, 298 K) (Supplementary Fig. 15) showed a broad NH stretching band between 3350 and 3100 cm−1 indicating a complex assembly pattern. The main peak appeared at 3300 cm−1 which suggest that part of the NH groups is free, and the main interaction mode should be by π−π interactions57, as assumed from the UV studies. On the other hand, the FT-IR patterns of 2-Agg-C and 3-Agg-B (Agg-A could not be studied at these concentrations) showed a simpler pattern consistent with the formation of H-bonds (Supplementary Figs. 16,17). For instance, the FT-IR spectrum of SQ 2 at 5 × 10−3 M and 298 K (Agg-C) (see Supplementary Fig. 16) exhibited a broad N−H stretching band around 3184 cm−1 with an additional shoulder at 3296 cm−1 and a broad C=O stretching band at 1657 cm−1. This shoulder likely results from an asymmetric interaction pattern between the SQs, which can be attributed to slipped H-bonding interactions present in both Agg-A and Agg-C49. Similar broad pattern in the C=O stretching band was also observed for the 3-Agg-C (5 × 10−3 M, 298 K) (Supplementary Fig. 17 red line). By cooling the sample, the FT-IR spectrum showed a symmetrization and sharpening of the N−H and C=O stretching bands, accompanied by a shift towards lower wavenumbers (Supplementary Fig. 17 blue line). This behaviour was explained by the formation of symmetric head-to-tail H-bonding interactions in 3-Agg-B (Fig. 1d) at lower temperatures49.

Pathway complexity and polymorph transitions

Thermoreversible polymorph transitions are a rare phenomenon that has been observed in SQs and urea aggregates49,50. To study the polymorph transitions, we relied on a combination of different techniques including UV/vis, FT-IR, SLS, and nano- differential scanning calorimetry (nano-DSC). According to the previously shown studies, it is important to mention that the Agg-A-Agg-C as well as Agg-A-Agg-B conversion are processes under thermodynamic equilibria and not under kinetic regimes16–19, as it can be deduced from the different heating-cooling UV/vis studies using different rates, that showed no hysteresis (Supplementary Figs. 7, 8).

The transformation of Agg-A into Agg-C was studied by variable concentration UV/vis and SLS experiments. Figure 5 shows the concentration-dependent UV/vis study for compound 2 in MCH at 293 K, which confirmed the transformation of 2-Agg-A into 2-Agg-C with increasing concentration. As anticipated, this experiment confirmed that the 2-Agg-A to 2-Agg-C conversion was promoted by either increasing concentration or decreasing temperature (Fig. 3c). Similarly, 3-Agg-A to 3-Agg-C conversion occurred upon increasing concentration at temperatures above 304 K (Supplementary Fig. 6), but the corresponding variable concentration UV/vis study could not be performed at these temperatures. Instead, concentration-dependent SLS experiments were recorded for SQ 2 and SQ 3 at 298 and 313 K, respectively. At lower concentrations (<1.5 × 10−3 M), the variable concentration SLS experiment for SQ 2 (Fig. 5b) showed a slight, gradual increase in the counts (intensity/kcps) with increasing concentration, which was attributed to slight increases in the size of Agg-A. In contrast, above a concentration threshold of 1.5 × 10−3 M, a more pronounced increase in the counts with concentration was observed. This behaviour was attributed to the transformation from 2-Agg-A, with particle-like morphology, into 2-Agg-C, with fibrillar morphology. Similar results were obtained for SQ 3, which also showed the concentration-dependent Agg-A-Agg-C conversion (Supplementary Fig. 14).

To assess the thermodynamic features of these conversions, we carried out nano-DSC studies. Unfortunately, the temperature-driven conversion of 2-Agg-A into 2-Agg-C could not be observed by nano-DSC, as the corresponding experiment showed no transition peaks (Supplementary Fig. 18). This behaviour suggests that the transformation occurred in a stepwise manner, probably by the hierarchical assembly of the Agg-A particles into larger agglomerates, giving rise to fibrillar structures (see Fig. 5e, top part). Pleasingly, the thermal 3-Agg-A to Agg-B conversion could be readily followed by nano-DSC. The heating and cooling traces of SQ 3 in MCH (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 19) displayed no transitions between 363 and 310 K during the polymerization process. However, transition peaks were observed around 300 K in both the heating and cooling cycles, corresponding to the Agg-A-Agg-B transitions. These peaks are characteristic of first-order transitions typically detected in liquid crystal phase transitions61–63. Integration of the DSC peaks showed that the temperature-induced Agg-A to Agg-B transition during heating is endothermic, while the reverse process during cooling is exothermic.

Nano-DSC revealed that the thermally induced Agg-A/Agg-B transition in SQ 3 was reversible, exhibiting practically no hysteresis in the heating and cooling cycles. However, the transition temperature (TA-B) was found to be highly dependent on concentration, with the conversion occurring at higher temperatures as concentration increases, as illustrated in the phase diagram in Fig. 5e. Along with the transition temperature, the enthalpy (ΔH0) and entropy (ΔS0) also changed with increasing concentration (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 5). This phenomenon was attributed to the highly dynamic nature of the Agg-A, Agg-B and Agg-C species, which are in equilibrium and their interconversion entails not only molecular rearrangements but also morphological changes (Fig. 3e). Such effects are not relevant in other calorimetric studies of proteins or helical assemblies since the folding and unfolding processes conserve the mass balance during heating-cooling processes64,65. However, in more dynamic systems, like supramolecular polymers, it seems that the size and morphology of the aggregates strongly affect the thermodynamics of the process.

Liquid-crystalline behaviour of SQs 1-3

To get a complete vision of the self-assembly behaviour of SQs 1-3, additional solid-state experiments were conducted. Interestingly, the three SQs were found to exhibit thermotropic LC behaviour61–63, as it was previously reported for 1, which permitted to realize new studies to understand the self-assembly modes of the assembled systems57. The phase transition behaviour was analysed by polarized optical microscopy (POM) (Supplementary Figs. 20–22), DSC (Supplementary Figs. 23–25) and X-ray experiments (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Figs. 26–29) and the overview is depicted in Fig. 6. For instance, SQs 1-3 formed LC columnar phases melting at 74, 94 and 90 °C, respectively. Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) experiments conducted on the aligned fibres of SQs 1-3 allowed the assignment of all phases previously identified by POM and DSC (Supplementary Figs. 20–25). Figure 7a displays the anisotropic WAXS pattern of SQ 1 at 70 °C, revealing a set of signals along the equator assigned to the 100, 110, 200, 210, and 420 reflections of a Colh phase (Supplementary Fig. 27)63,66. Additionally, various meridional and off-meridional peaks are present in the 2D pattern, with a relevant signal cutting the meridian at 3.5 Å attributed to a π-π stacking distance. Based on the columnar parameters and an estimated density of 1 g/ml, the number of molecules per columnar slice (c = 3.5 Å) in the Colh phase of SQ 1 was calculated to be around two. In contrast, compound 2 exhibited a more complex pattern at 75 °C (Fig. 7b), that was assigned to columnar rectangular (Colr) phase (Supplementary Fig. 28)63. Notably, a significant meridional reflection at 6 Å was observed, while no π-π stacking signal was detected. This distance was initially attributed to the H-bonding distance between SQs, suggesting that the SQ units lie parallel to the columnar axis67–69. This Colr phase was found to contain three molecules of 2 in each 6 Å columnar slice. Finally, SQ 3 showed a lower temperature Colr phase and a higher temperature columnar tetragonal (Coltet) phase, as deduced from the anisotropic WAXS patterns (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Figs. 26,29). For example, Fig. 7c illustrates the WAXS pattern of SQ 3 at 65 °C, displaying the 100, 110, 200, 210, 220, 310, 320, and 400 signals corresponding to a Coltet lattice. Additionally, a meridional signal at 6.1 Å was observed, suggesting the presence of H-bonded SQs along the columns. Based on the WAXS experiments (Fig. 7a–c) and the solid-state FT-IR and UV/vis experiments (Supplementary Figs. 30–35), the self-assembly packing of LC 1-3 can be suggested. For the Colh phase of compound 1, it is proposed that the molecules form H-bonded dimers, which then stack via slipped π-π interactions (Fig. 7d)57. This mode of assembly is consistent with the formation of J-aggregates, as indicated by the UV/vis patterns (Supplementary Fig. 30), and is also consistent with the complex H-bonding pattern observed in the FT-IR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 33). Given the similar spectroscopic patterns observed both in MCH and in the solid state, it is likely that in solution 1-Agg-J is based on a similar packing mode (Fig. 1b).

In contrast, the columnar phases of compounds 2 and 3 exhibit the characteristic UV pattern of H-bonded SQs via head-to-tail interactions, characterized by two intense bands at 280 and 320 nm (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32). The FT-IR spectra of these samples also support this interaction mode, showing well-defined N-H and C = O stretching bands (Supplementary Figs. 34, 35). Therefore, the LC columnar phases of 2 and 3 likely consist of multiple H-bonded ribbons of SQs concentrated in the centre of the columns, surrounded by the alkyl chains (Fig. 7e, f)70–72. The Colr phase of compound 2 is based on three strands (Fig. 7e), while both the Colr and Coltet phases of compound 3 consist of two strands (Fig. 7f), as estimated from X-ray studies67–69. This assembly mode is likely analogous to the Agg-B structure observed in solution for SQ 3.

Discussion

Thus, through the design of three different bis-dendronized SQ derivatives it is reported a comprehensive exploration of the self-assembly behaviour of SQ units in solution and in solid state. Remarkably this study not only focuses on supramolecular polymerisation processes, but also provided new insights into the uncommon aspect of the equilibria between polymorphs. In the case of SQ 1, based on a diarylsquaramide core, a self-assembly was observed in MCH via slipped π–π interactions, leading to the formation of J-aggregates (Agg-J). This interaction mode is favoured due to the conjugation of the SQs with benzene units, which creates a planar structure that promotes the π–π stacking over H-bonding. This mode of interaction has not been reported for SQ supramolecular polymers, although it has been observed in the crystalline structures of aryl-extended SQs54. This interaction mode has also been observed in the LC phase of SQ 1 that forms a columnar phase.

Conversely, introducing methylene or ethylene spacers between the dendrons and the SQ core (as in SQ 2 and SQ 3) the H-bonding becomes pivotal in the self-assembly, while pathway complexity is also generated. For instance, SQ 2 forms two distinct aggregates based on slipped H-bonding interactions, the particle-like Agg-A and the fibrillar Agg-C, with the latter being favoured at lower temperatures and higher concentrations. In addition to Agg-A and Agg-C, SQ 3 exhibits an additional third aggregate type, Agg-B, based on head-to-tail H-bonds. Notably, these three aggregates (Agg-A, Agg-B, and Agg-C) are in thermodynamic equilibrium and were studied without significant kinetic interferences. The equilibrium between Agg-A and Agg-C is concentration-dependent at temperatures above a certain threshold (31 °C), while Agg-B formation is induced by cooling, likely due to reduced alkyl chain dynamics that facilitate head-to-tail interactions. This intriguing equilibrium between aggregates seems to arise from the steric effects of the dendrons, which influence the formation of either slipped or head-to-tail H-bonded aggregates depending on the temperature and concentration of the system. Consistently, SQs 2 and 3 exhibit columnar LC phases based on head-to-tail H-bonds. Remarkably, they adopt an uncommon assembly mode in liquid crystals, with the SQ units aligning along the columnar axis in H-bonded strands, as reported in LC dyes67–69. This unique assembly mode holds significant potential for the development of functional materials, such as ferroelectrics, as previously demonstrated in LC ureas71,72. The ability of SQ units to engage in diverse interaction patterns enables intriguing polymorph transitions, combined with their capacity to form columnar liquid crystals with polar order, makes them a promising platform for the design of stimuli-responsive materials.

Methods

Synthesis

All chemicals and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Scharlau and abcr, and were used without further purification. All reactions were carried out under argon atmosphere.

General synthetic procedure for SQs 1-3

A mixture of the corresponding dendron amine and diethyl squarate was heated (95 °C, Toluene:DMF (19:1) for SQ 1; 90 °C, ethanol for SQ 2; 50 °C, CHCl3:MeOH (1:2.5) for SQ 3) and allowed to react for 8–24 h. Then the solvent was evaporated, and the crude product was purified by chromatographic column (for SQ 1) or by precipitation (for SQs 2 and 3). The detailed description of the procedures can be found in the Supplementary Information.

NMR spectroscopy

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded at 298 K on a Bruker AVANCE (300 MHz). The residual protic solvent is used as the internal standard and chemical shifts are reported in ppm (δ). Multiplicities for proton signals are abbreviated as s, d, i and m for singlet, doublet, triplet and multiplet respectively.

Mass spectrometry

High resolution mass spectrometry (ESI-HRMS) mass spectra were measured on a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Q Exactive mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray modules.

UV/vis spectroscopy

Ultraviolet/visible spectra were recorded in solution on a JASCO V-750 UV/vis spectrophotometer equipped with a Peltier device temperature-control unit. The measurements were carried out in rectangular quartz glass cuvettes 10 mm (Hellma) using spectroscopic grade solvents (ACROS organics). Extinction coefficients were calculated from Lambert-Beer’s law. The solid-state experiments were recorded on an Agilent Cary 5000 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer equipped with a Diffuse Reflectance accessory (DRA-2500) using 1.25 mm quartz round plates (Hellma). The solvents used were ≥ 99% chromatography rich.

Atomic force microscopy

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed in tapping mode under ambient conditions on a MultiMode Veeco AFM microscope with a NanoScope IV controller. An HQ:NSC35/AlBS silicon tip with a cantilever length of 90 µm was employed with the resonance frequency region of 150–300 kHz and a scan rate of 1.0 Hz. Cross- section profiles were performed with WSxM software. Samples were spin-coated onto highly oriented pyrolytic graphite or mica using a volume of 100 µL.

Static light scattering

The light scattering experiments were recorded on a Malvern Zetasizer nano-ZS90. The measurements were carried out in glass cuvettes using spectroscopic grade solvents and toluene as a dispersion standard. The solvents used were ≥ 99% chromatography rich.

Small- and wide-angle X-ray scattering

Small-angle and wide-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS and WAXS) experiments were performed on a XENOCS XEUSS 3.0. The instrument is equipped with a GeniX 3D Cu micro focus X-ray source (λ = 1.54 Å; flux = 2 × 108 ph s‒1) and a DECTRIS Pilatus3 R 300 K silicon pixel detector with 487 × 619 pixels of 172 × 172 μm in size. The samples were inserted in 2 mm capillaries using a sample holder integrated in a Peltier.

Polarizing optical microscope

Polarizing optical microscopy (POM) images were obtained with a Flexacam C1 attached to a Leica DM2700P microscope equipped with a Linkam LTS420 hot stage.

Differential scanning calorimetry

Nano-differential scanning calorimetry (nano-DSC) measurements in solution were performed on a TA instruments nano-DSC at a pressure of 3 atm in a 300 µL platinum capillary cell, for both sample and reference, at a heating-cooling rate of 2 °C min‒1. The solvents used were ≥ 99% chromatography rich. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements in solid state were performed on a Mettler Toledo DSC 3+ Star System at a scanning rate of 10 °C/min. Transition temperatures indicated in the DSC curves correspond to the onset values.

FT-IR experiments

Fourier transform infra-red (FT-IR) spectroscopic analysis were carried out in solution on a Bruker Vertex 80/80v spectrophotometer using a CaF2 cell with a path length of 2 mm. Solid-state measurements were recorded on a Bruker Tensor 27 equipped with a PLATINUM Diamond ATR accessory. The solvents used were ≥ 99% chromatography rich.

Temperature-dependent UV/vis studies (heating/cooling)

A concentrated stock solution of SQs 1‒3 was prepared in MCH. The appropriate volume of the stock solution was diluted until reaching the desired concentrations.

Solvent-dependent UV/vis studies (denaturation)

Concentrated stock solutions of SQ 1 were prepared in MCH and CHCl3. The appropriate volume of the stock solution was diluted until reaching the desired concentration. Different volumes of the solution were mixed to achieve the desired ratio. The solutions were allowed to rest for 5 min at the desired temperature before recording their absorbance.

Sample preparation

UV/vis, SLS and nano-DSC experiments

A concentrated stock solution of SQs 1‒3 (2 and 3 for nano-DSc and SLS) was prepared in MCH. The appropriate volume of the stock solution was diluted until reaching the desired concentrations. For the SLS the most concentrated sample was diluted with MCH by a factor of 0.7.

AFM, FT-IR and SAXS experiments in solution

Compounds SQ 1‒3 were weighted and the appropriate amount of MCH (MCH-d14 for FT-IR) was added to reach the desired concentration.

UV/vis studies in solid state

A concentrated stock solution of SQs 1‒3 was prepared in MCH. The solution was then drop-casted on a quartz support and allowed to dry.

WAXS experiments

Compounds SQ 1‒3 were inserted in an extruder with a 1 mm aperture, the extruder was heated to 60 °C and pressure was applied with a piston to form a fibre.
