Association of surgical risk prediction models with days alive out of hospital at 30 days in colorectal surgeries: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Müşerref Beril Dinçer, Mert Canbaz, Esra Saka, Özlem Turhan, Demet Altun, Ahmet Kemalettin Koltka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical risk prediction models are widely used in perioperative settings to estimate postoperative risk, but their relationship with patient-centered outcomes remains unclear. Days alive out of hospital (DAOH) integrates postoperative complications, readmissions, and mortality into a single outcome. This study evaluated the associations between established surgical risk prediction models and DAOH following colorectal surgery.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients undergoing colorectal surgery in 2023. Preoperative risk was assessed using the American College of Surgeons (ACS) Surgical Risk Calculator, POSSUM-morbidity, and P-POSSUM mortality scores. Patient characteristics, surgical variables, postoperative complications, readmissions, and mortality were recorded. DAOH at 30 days (DAOH30) and DAOH at 90 days (DAOH90) were calculated; low DAOH30 was defined as below the cohort median. The primary outcome was to evaluate the associations between risk estimates and DAOH30. Secondary outcome analyses included associations between risk estimates and DAOH90, multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with low DAOH30, and receiver operating characteristic analysis with area under the curve (AUC) to assess discriminative performance of risk models for low DAOH30.ResultsA total of 145 patients were analyzed, with a median DAOH30 of 23 (18–24) days. All risk models were negatively correlated with DAOH30 (ACS-any complication: rho = − 0.603; ACS-serious complication: rho = − 0.515; ACS-death: rho = − 0.447; ACS-readmission: rho = − 0.286; POSSUM-morbidity: rho = − 0.419; P-POSSUM-mortality: rho = − 0.396; all p < 0.001), with similar findings for DAOH90. In multivariable regression analysis, higher ACS-serious complication risk (OR: 1.177; 95% CI: 1.057–1.310; p = 0.003), higher ACS-any complication risk (OR: 1.172; 95% CI: 1.062–1.293; p = 0.002), and longer operative duration were independently associated with low DAOH30. ACS-serious and ACS-any complication estimates demonstrated good discriminative ability for low DAOH30 (AUC = 0.808 [95% CI: 0.735–0.880] and AUC = 0.828 [95% CI: 0.759–0.897], respectively).ConclusionsSurgical risk prediction models were significantly associated with postoperative recovery measured by DAOH following colorectal surgery. The ACS Surgical Risk Calculator estimates for serious and any complication demonstrated good discriminative performance for reduced DAOH30, suggesting their potential utility in identifying patients at risk of impaired postoperative recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04121-2.Keywords: Days alive out of hospital, Days alive out of hospital at 30 days, ACS NSQIP surgical risk calculator, POSSUM, P-POSSUM

Introduction

Identifying perioperative risk prior to surgical intervention is fundamental to optimizing perioperative management. Numerous perioperative risk assessment models have been developed to evaluate this risk [1, 2]. Among these, the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) Surgical Risk Calculator is a widely used, web-based tool that estimates postoperative risks of major complications, readmission, mortality, and the projected length of hospital stay based on patient and procedure-related variables [3]. Similarly, the Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the Enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity (POSSUM) and its modification, Portsmouth POSSUM (P-POSSUM), are established models that predict postoperative morbidity and mortality using physiological and operative parameters [4, 5]. These models have been widely applied in colorectal surgery to evaluate perioperative outcomes [6–8].

In parallel, there has been increasing emphasis on patient-centered outcomes in perioperative research, aiming to capture postoperative recovery from the patient’s perspective. Their integration into clinical studies is encouraged to enable more meaningful benchmarking and evaluation of the quality of care [9, 10]. The Standardized Endpoints in Perioperative Medicine (StEP) initiative has identified days alive out of hospital (DAOH) as a robust patient-centered metric reflecting postoperative recovery and life impact after surgery [10].

DAOH at 30 days (DAOH30) represents the number of days a patient is alive and outside the hospital within 30 days postoperatively, integrating length of stay, readmissions, discharge disposition, and early mortality into a single measure [10, 11]. This metric has been validated as a feasible and meaningful endpoint in perioperative research, and lower DAOH30 has been associated with increased comorbidity burden, surgical complexity, and postoperative complications, reflecting its sensitivity to both short- and intermediate-term outcomes [11–13].

While surgical risk prediction models estimate postoperative morbidity and mortality based on patient and procedural factors, DAOH captures a broader dimension of postoperative recovery and life impact. Given that both approaches are influenced by similar determinants, an association between traditional risk scores and DAOH is plausible but remains incompletely characterized. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between established risk prediction models (ACS NSQIP, POSSUM, and P-POSSUM) and DAOH in patients undergoing colorectal surgery. We primarily hypothesized that predicted risk estimates would be negatively correlated with DAOH. Additionally, we sought to explore perioperative factors, including risk estimates, associated with reduced DAOH30.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective, single-tertiary-center cohort study was conducted at Istanbul University, Istanbul Faculty of Medicine. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Istanbul Faculty of Medicine, Istanbul, Turkey (protocol number: 2024/506; March 08, 2024), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent elective or emergent major colorectal surgery under general anesthesia between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, were screened. Eligible procedures included left-sided colon surgeries (low anterior resection, high anterior resection, sigmoid colectomy, left hemicolectomy, abdominoperineal resection), right-sided colon surgeries (right and extended right hemicolectomy), segmental partial colectomy, subtotal colectomy, Hartmann’s procedure, loop colostomy, colostomy closure, reversal of Hartmann’s procedure (closure of Hartmann’s colostomy), and colostomy revision.

Patients undergoing minor perianal procedures (e.g., perianal abscess drainage, fistula surgery, hemorrhoidectomy, or polypectomy) were excluded from screening. For patients with multiple procedures during the study period, only the first operation was included. Patients who underwent colorectal surgery following abdominal trauma or who lacked perioperative data were excluded. Only patients with complete datasets were included in the final analysis.

Data collection

Data were obtained from both paper-based records and electronic medical records. Intraoperative anesthesia records were first reviewed, followed by a detailed evaluation of preoperative and postoperative data from hospital databases. Surgical risk prediction models were calculated using online tools.

Collected variables included demographic characteristics, smoking status, malignancy, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status. Preoperative laboratory parameters (hemoglobin level, leukocyte count, and C-reactive protein) were recorded. Frailty was assessed using the Modified Frailty Index-11 [14, 15], with a cutoff value ≥ 0.27 defining frailty [16, 17].

Surgical variables included procedure type, emergency (elective vs. emergent), and surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. laparotomy). Intraoperative data comprised operative duration, intraoperative hypotension requiring vasopressor infusion, blood product transfusion, total fluid administration, and arterial blood gas parameters (lactate, base excess, and pH).

Risk prediction models

The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator was used to estimate patient-specific risks based on demographic, clinical, and procedural variables [18]. Calculated patient-specific risk parameters included serious complication, any complication, pneumonia, cardiac complication, venous thromboembolism, sepsis, surgical site infections, urinary tract infections, renal failure, readmission, return to the operating room, death, discharge to a nursing or rehab facility, ileus, and anastomotic leak risk for colectomy procedures. Recorded outputs included four risk estimates: serious complication, any complication, readmission, and death.

POSSUM and P-POSSUM risk prediction model scores were calculated using physiological and operative variables [19, 20]. The POSSUM model provides both morbidity and mortality estimates, whereas P-POSSUM estimates only mortality risk. P-POSSUM is more recent than the POSSUM system, which has been shown to overestimate mortality risk [5]. Given its improved calibration, the P-POSSUM-mortality risk score was used to estimate mortality risk, while the POSSUM-morbidity risk score was used to estimate morbidity risk.

Postoperative outcomes and complications

Postoperative data within 30 days included Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission and readmission, ICU length of stay, the duration of hospital stay following index surgery, readmissions, total hospital length of stay, and discharge destination (home or a nursing facility). Complications recorded within 30 days included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [21], postoperative pulmonary complications (PPC) [22], acute kidney injury (AKI) [23], need for continuous renal replacement therapy, and surgical-specific complications (surgical site infections, evisceration, paralytic ileus, and anastomotic leakage) [24]. Sepsis and septic shock [25], multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [26], reoperation, and mortality were also documented. Complications were graded using the Clavien–Dindo (CLD) classification [27], with grades ≥ II considered moderate-to-severe and grades ≥ III considered severe [28, 29].

Readmissions occurring between the 31st and 90th days, the total length of stay up to the 90th day, and mortality at 90 days were also documented. Only readmissions to our institution could be identified through the institutional electronic hospital information system; readmissions to other healthcare institutions could not be captured. Mortality during follow-up was ascertained through this system, which is linked to the national healthcare system.

DAOH30 and DAOH90 calculation

Days alive out of hospital at 30 days (DAOH30) was calculated as previously described [11], integrating mortality, length of hospital stay, and readmissions within 30 days after surgery. DAOH30 was defined as 30 minus the total number of days spent in hospital; multiple admissions were summed. Patients who died within 30 days or remained hospitalized for the entire period were assigned a DAOH30 value of 0. Time spent in nursing facilities was included in the total duration of institutional care.

DAOH90 was calculated using the same methodology over a 90-day postoperative period, incorporating mortality, total hospital stay, and readmissions within 90 days.

Blinding

To minimize potential bias, investigators assessing preoperative and intraoperative variables (M.B.D.) were separate from those evaluating postoperative outcomes, including DAOH30 and DAOH90 (E.S. and Ö.T.). Investigators were blinded to variables outside their respective domains.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was to evaluate the association between DAOH30 and perioperative risk estimates, including the ACS NSQIP estimates (serious complication, any complication, readmission, and death) and the POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM mortality scores.

Secondary outcomes included: (i) assessment of associations between risk model parameters and DAOH90; (ii) comparison of perioperative variables between low and high DAOH30 groups; and (iii) evaluation of factors associated with low DAOH30 and the discriminative performance of risk models for low DAOH30.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%), whereas continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or median (minimum–maximum [min–max]), as appropriate. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

All risk scores derived from surgical risk prediction models, as well as DAOH30 and DAOH90, were treated as continuous variables. For the primary outcome, correlations between DAOH30 and six perioperative risk estimates were evaluated: four ACS NSQIP risk estimates (serious complication, any complication, readmission, and death) and two POSSUM-derived risk estimates (POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality). Given the non-normal distribution of DAOH reported in previous studies [11, 13], Spearman’s rank correlation test was used. The strength of correlation coefficients was interpreted as follows: 0.00–0.10, negligible; 0.10–0.39, weak; 0.40–0.69, moderate; 0.70–0.89, strong; and 0.90–1.00, very strong [30]. The same approach was applied to evaluate associations between risk scores and DAOH90.

The DAOH30 was evaluated across various surgical procedures, including colorectal surgeries, exhibiting numerical variation [11, 13, 31]. Previous reports identified low and high DAOH values (poor and good) using the median as a cutoff [32–34]. Consistent with this approach, we used the median DAOH30 in our population to categorize patients into two groups: Group High DAOH30 (≥ median) and Group Low DAOH30 (< median).

Between-group comparisons were performed for patient and surgical characteristics, risk model parameters, and postoperative outcome variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Median differences and their 95% CIs were analyzed using the Hodges–Lehmann estimator. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were reported as absolute values to describe the magnitude of between-group differences.

Both univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify perioperative factors associated with low DAOH30. Candidate variables included clinically relevant perioperative factors identified in prior research and in our comparative analysis of the groups, as well as risk prediction model estimates. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of multicollinearity. Particular attention was paid to overlapping risk score components. Variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariable model utilizing the Enter method. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and model performance was expressed using Nagelkerke’s R². Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of risk prediction models for low DAOH30. Discriminative performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), interpreted as follows: 0.60–0.69 poor, 0.70–0.79 fair, 0.80–0.89 good, and ≥ 0.90 excellent. An AUC ≥ 0.80 was considered clinically acceptable [35]. Optimal cutoff values were determined using Youden’s index in an exploratory manner.

All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No formal adjustment for multiple testing was performed for the primary outcome because all correlation analyses were conducted to address the same research objective.

Sample size calculation

To our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated correlations between these risk prediction models and DAOH. An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 before screening the patient records. Assuming a medium effect size (Pearson’s r = 0.30), an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 138 patients. As the primary analyses consisted of correlation analyses between DAOH30 and multiple perioperative risk estimates using the same statistical approach, a single correlation-based power analysis was considered appropriate for estimating the required sample size. To account for an anticipated 15–20% exclusion rate, 165 patient records were reviewed.

Results

Study population

A total of 165 patient records were screened, of which 145 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age was 61.00 ± 14.15 years, the mean body mass index (BMI) was 26.44 ± 4.47 kg/m², and 78 patients (53.8%) were male. Most patients underwent surgery for colorectal malignancy (117, 80.7%). Emergency surgery was performed in 42 patients (29.0%), whereas 103 patients (71.0%) underwent elective procedures. Regarding surgical approach, 81 procedures (55.9%) were performed laparoscopically and 64 (44.1%) via laparotomy; 5 laparoscopic procedures required conversion to open surgery. The median operative duration was 120 min (IQR: 80–180).

Flow chart for participant screening and inclusion

Left-sided colorectal procedures (low anterior resection, high anterior resection, sigmoid colectomy, left hemicolectomy, abdominoperineal resection) accounted for 66 operations (45.5%), whereas right-sided procedures (right and extended right hemicolectomy) accounted for 24 (16.6%). Segmental partial colectomy was performed in 7 patients (4.8%), subtotal colectomy in 4 (2.8%), Hartmann procedure in 12 (8.3%), and loop colostomy in 21 (14.5%). Colostomy closure was performed in 4 patients (2.8%), Hartmann stoma closure in 3 (2.1%), and colostomy revision in 4 (2.8%).

The median ACS-serious complication risk estimate was 9.90 (IQR: 7.00–17.50), while the median ACS-any complication estimate was 10.80 (IQR: 7.50–19.10). Median ACS-readmission and ACS-death risk estimates were 8.80 (IQR: 6.10–12.70) and 0.30 (IQR: 0.10–1.50), respectively. The median POSSUM-morbidity risk score was 37.30 (IQR: 22.10–64.30), and the median P-POSSUM-mortality risk score was 2.10 (IQR: 0.94–5.70).

The median postoperative length of initial hospital stay was 7.00 days (IQR: 6.00–10.00). Hospital readmission within 30 days occurred in 12 patients (8.3%), while 10 patients (6.9%) died within 30 days after surgery. The median total length of hospital stay was 7.00 days (IQR: 6.00–11.00). No patients were discharged to a nursing facility.

The median DAOH30 was 23.00 days (IQR: 18.00–24.00; 95% CI: 22.00–23.00), and the median DAOH90 was 83.00 days (IQR: 77.00–84.00; 95% CI: 82.00–83.00). Histograms of DAOH30 and DAOH90 demonstrated negatively skewed distributions (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Primary outcome analysis: correlations between surgical risk prediction models and DAOH30

Moderate negative correlations were observed between DAOH30 and ACS-serious complication, ACS-any complication, and ACS-death risk estimates (rho = − 0.515, p < 0.001; rho = − 0.603, p < 0.001; and rho = − 0.447, p < 0.001, respectively). A weak negative correlation was identified between ACS-readmission risk estimate and DAOH30 (rho = − 0.286, p < 0.001). Scatter plots are presented in Fig. 2.

For the POSSUM-based models, POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality risk scores showed weak-to-moderate negative correlations with DAOH30 (rho = − 0.419, p < 0.001 and rho = − 0.396, p < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 2).

Correlations between surgical risk prediction models and DAOH90

DAOH90 demonstrated moderate negative correlations with ACS-serious complication (rho = − 0.549, p < 0.001), ACS-any complication (rho = − 0.637, p < 0.001), and ACS-death risk estimates (rho = − 0.486, p < 0.001). A weak negative correlation was observed between ACS-readmission risk estimate and DAOH90 (rho = − 0.338, p < 0.001).

Similarly, POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality risk scores were moderately negatively correlated with DAOH90 (rho = − 0.460, p < 0.001 and rho = − 0.437, p < 0.001, respectively). Scatter plots are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Comparison of perioperative factors according to DAOH30 groups

Table 1 compares demographic, perioperative, and risk model characteristics between patients with low DAOH30 (< 23 days) and high DAOH30 (≥ 23 days). Patients in the low DAOH30 group had higher ASA physical status scores, longer operative durations, and were more likely to undergo emergency surgery and laparotomy. In addition, all ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator, POSSUM, and P-POSSUM risk scores were significantly higher in the low DAOH30 group.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated significant negative correlations between DAOH30 and all evaluated surgical risk prediction model parameters, including ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator estimates for serious complications, any complications, readmission, and death, as well as POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality scores. In other words, as predicted perioperative risk increases, the duration of out of hospital stay within 30 days after surgery decreases. To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has evaluated correlations or associations between these established surgical risk assessment models and the DAOH endpoint. Hence, this study provides a novel patient-centered perspective on perioperative risk assessment.

DAOH was initially introduced in patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, particularly heart failure, as a composite endpoint integrating both mortality and morbidity into a single outcome measure [36]. It was subsequently adopted in perioperative medicine and evaluated across a range of surgical populations [11–13, 31]. Previous studies consistently demonstrated associations between lower DAOH and adverse postoperative outcomes, including complications, prolonged hospitalization, readmission, and mortality. The ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator estimates individualized risks for several postoperative outcomes, including complications, readmission, reoperation, and death, based on patient- and procedure-related variables. Therefore, we hypothesized that the risk parameters generated by this calculator, including serious complication, any complication, readmission, and death, would correlate with DAOH. In addition, we evaluated POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality scores, which are widely used perioperative risk assessment tools.

Similar negative correlations were also identified between all evaluated risk scores and DAOH90, consistent with the findings observed for DAOH30. Previous studies have shown that poor short-term recovery, reflected by lower DAOH30, is often associated with persistently reduced DAOH90 [13, 37]. In line with these observations, patients with low DAOH30 in our cohort also had significantly lower DAOH90 values, even after excluding patients who died within the first 30 postoperative days. These findings suggest that early postoperative recovery may have sustained effects on longer-term patient-centered outcomes.

Higher DAOH values reflect more favorable postoperative recovery. Previous large-scale studies involving cohorts ranging from approximately 110,000 to more than 540,000 patients commonly defined poor DAOH30 using the 10th percentile threshold [13, 37], whereas another study involving approximately 78,000 patients categorized DAOH groups according to the 33rd percentile [38]. In contrast, smaller cohort studies have frequently used the median DAOH value to define low and high DAOH groups [32–34]. Consistent with these studies, we used the median DAOH30 value as the cut-off point. The reported median DAOH30 after lower gastrointestinal or colorectal surgery ranges between 23 and 24.9 days [11, 13, 39]. Similarly, the median DAOH30 in our cohort was 23 days (IQR: 18–24).

Older age has previously been associated with reduced DAOH following surgery [11–13, 38, 39]. However, age was not significantly different between low and high DAOH30 groups in our cohort and was therefore not included in the regression analysis. In contrast, higher ASA physical status, longer operative duration, emergency surgery, open surgical approach, postoperative complications, and hospital readmission were all more common among patients with low DAOH30, consistent with previous reports [11–13, 38, 39]. In multivariable analysis, prolonged operative duration remained independently associated with low DAOH30.

We evaluated several postoperative complications, MACE, PPC, AKI, surgical complications such as surgical site infection, evisceration, paralytic ileus, and anastomotic leakage, as well as sepsis, septic shock, and MODS. All of these complications occurred more frequently in patients with low DAOH30. Furthermore, postoperative complications were graded using the CLD classification, and moderate-to-severe (CLD ≥ II) and severe (CLD ≥ III) complications were predominantly observed in the low DAOH30 group. In univariate analysis, hospital readmission, CLD grade ≥ II, and grade ≥ III complications were associated with low DAOH30, reflecting their impact on postoperative recovery. However, wide confidence intervals for readmission and CLD grade ≥ III indicate limited precision of these estimates, likely due to low event frequency and imbalance between groups.

Our findings further demonstrated that all ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator parameters, as well as POSSUM-morbidity and P-POSSUM-mortality scores, were significantly higher in patients with low DAOH30. Moreover, all risk parameters were significantly associated with low DAOH30 in univariable regression analysis. We constructed separate multivariable models because of multicollinearity and conceptual overlap among the perioperative risk estimates. Models based on complication and morbidity risk estimates are presented in the main manuscript because postoperative complications occurred substantially more frequently than readmission and mortality, both in previous colorectal surgery studies [40, 41] and in our cohort. The corresponding readmission and mortality models are provided in the Supplementary Material.

After adjustment for patient- and procedure-related covariates, ACS-serious complication and ACS-any complication risk scores remained independently associated with low DAOH30, with each one-percentage-point increase in these risk estimates corresponding to approximately a 17% increase in the odds of low DAOH30. ROC curve analysis further supported these findings, as both ACS complication estimates demonstrated good discriminative performance for low DAOH30, with AUC values exceeding 0.80. In contrast, POSSUM-morbidity did not retain an independent association after adjustment.

To date, no previous study has directly evaluated the associations between ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator, POSSUM, or P-POSSUM scores and DAOH. However, a study involving patients undergoing emergency laparotomy demonstrated that higher predicted mortality according to the National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) risk model was associated with lower DAOH30 values [38]. Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating similar associations across multiple widely used surgical risk prediction models in colorectal surgery patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study population included heterogeneous colorectal surgical procedures, precluding procedure-specific analyses. Furthermore, the findings may not be generalizable to other surgical populations. Third, although mortality during follow-up, including deaths occurring outside our institution, was recorded through our institutional electronic system, readmissions to other healthcare institutions could not be captured and were therefore not included in the DAOH metric. Fourth, although significant inverse correlations were observed between perioperative risk prediction models and DAOH, correlation analysis alone cannot establish causality or fully characterize these associations. Therefore, multivariable regression and ROC analyses were performed to further explore and substantiate the primary correlation findings. Finally, the available sample size limited the complexity of statistical modeling and precluded analyses such as quantile regression and percentile-based stratification.

Conclusion

Surgical risk prediction models were significantly associated with DAOH, a patient-centered outcome reflecting postoperative recovery, in patients undergoing colorectal surgery. In particular, the ACS NSQIP Surgical Risk Calculator estimates for serious and any complication, along with operative duration, were independently associated with reduced DAOH30. Both ACS complication estimates also demonstrated good discriminative ability for identifying patients with low DAOH30. These findings suggest that established perioperative risk models may improve risk stratification and help identify patients at risk of impaired postoperative recovery, as reflected by DAOH. Prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and further clarify the relationship between surgical risk models and patient-centered perioperative outcomes.
