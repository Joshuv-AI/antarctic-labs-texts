A 3D conductive amorphous unsaturated-ligand MIL-68(Fe) MOF as room-temperature H2S chemiresistive sensor
Authors: Guoliang Lv, Linjuan Zhang, Yuanyuan Luo, Jianqiu Zhu, Jilong Wu, Li Qin, Yanlin Zhang, Zheng Zhang, Pengwei Tan, Guotao Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simultaneously achieving chemiresistive gas sensors with high selectivity, sensitivity, and stability remains a significant challenge. In this study, inspired by the H2S-specific catalytic enzymes in biological systems, we construct an amorphous unsaturated-ligand MIL-68(Fe) 3D MOF via in situ thermal induction. Without the introduction of extrinsic catalytic or conductive components, this MOF modification strategy creates unsaturated Fe atoms within the framework itself. These atoms function dually as catalytic and conductive sites, thereby overcoming the inherent low conductivity of conventional 3D MOFs and enabling the direct construction of chemiresistive sensor for room-temperature molecular olfactory sensing. The targeted water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism bypasses the limitations of traditional oxygen adsorption processes to achieve unprecedented comprehensive performance, including fingerprint-level selectivity (against 29 interfering gases), superior sensitivity coefficient (~83/ppm), ultra-low measured lower limit (1 ppb), rapid gas sensing response/recovery characteristics (~30 s/73 s), and excellent long-term stability at room temperature.

Introduction

Since the inception of the first ZnO gas sensor in 19621, heated metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors have emerged as the dominant paradigm in chemiresistive gas sensors due to their high sensitivity and robust stability (Fig. 1a). Using hydrogen sulfide (H2S) detection as a representative case, the oxygen-mediated mechanism catalyzes H2S oxidation into higher-valence species (Fig. 1c). While oxygen’s high reactivity enables broad-spectrum gas detection, it also leads to irreversible byproduct accumulation, severely restricting the sensing cycle lifetime. Although extensive material modifications (e.g., doping, nanostructuring) have been implemented, these efforts fail to address the fundamental issue. This impasse necessitates a paradigm shift. Intriguingly, biological olfaction systems (e.g., porcine/drosophila olfactory receptors) achieve exceptional specificity through protein-targeted molecular recognition7–10, inspiring our design. Unfortunately, proteins’ inherent instability in vitro and insulating properties preclude their use in chemiresistive gas sensors (CRS)11. Then, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), as hybrid organic-inorganic analogs12, overcome stability issues while mimicking enzymatic functional groups for selective catalysis. However, conventional 3D MOFs are plagued by ultralow electrical conductivity, confining them exclusively to chemicapacitive sensors (CCS)13,14, which inherently exhibit poor sensitivity. To apply 3D-MOF to CRS, it must be composited with conductive media (such as graphene/ionic liquids, etc.) to construct a conductive 3D MOF composite material15,16. As a comparison, the emergence of 2D materials (including 2D MOFs, graphene, and transition metal dichalcogenides) has addressed this conductivity dilemma. Their exceptional charge transport properties make them ideal candidates for room-temperature CRS17–20. However, their selectivity remains inferior to 3D MOFs, while their sensitivity significantly underperforms conventional MOS sensors (Fig. 1a).

The performance trade-offs in CRS fundamentally arise from their sensing mechanism, which consists of three consecutive processes (Fig. 1b). Gas molecules donate electrons (Δ nΔn) to the sensing material, thereby modulating its intrinsic carrier concentration (nn) and subsequently altering the charge transport signal. In this framework, selectivity is exclusively governed by molecular perception capability, charge transport capacity directly reflects electrical conductivity, while sensitivity (SS)—defined as the ratio of gas-induced electrons to intrinsic carriers (Δ nΔn/nn)—depends on conversion efficiency. Conventional 2D materials exhibit an intrinsic limitation where improved charge transport (through abundant conductive sites) inevitably compromises molecular perception (by reducing catalytic sites), simultaneously degrading selectivity due to insufficient specific catalytic centers. Furthermore, enhanced material conductivity results in an excessive intrinsic carrier concentration (nn), which significantly compromises the sensitivity of both 2D materials and conductive 3D MOF composites. This reveals the critical requirement for CRS active sites to simultaneously serve as both catalytic and conductive sites—a dual functionality successfully achieved in MOS sensors through oxygen vacancies (Fig. 1c), though at the cost of selectivity2–4,21. Consequently, the central challenge in CRS development lies in constructing material systems with both selective sensing mechanisms and dual-functional active sites capable of concurrent molecular perception and charge transport.

The biological catalytic process of H2S occurs in lymphatic fluid, where water molecules serve as the reaction medium with unsaturated Fe atoms acting as the core catalytic centers (Fig. 1d)22–24. Table S1 presents the comparative advantages of the water molecule catalysis sensing mechanism over the conventional oxygen adsorption mechanism. Inspired by this natural mechanism, we have successfully mimicked the structure of H2S-specific enzymatic proteins by constructing an amorphous unsaturated-ligand 3D Fe-MOF (AUL MIL-68(Fe)) through in situ thermal induction. Without relying on additional doping or assembly steps15,16, this modification method directly transforms the pristine MOF, in a single step, into an active and conductive MOF where the incorporated unsaturated Fe atoms function simultaneously as catalytic and conductive sites. This material exhibits a remarkable four-order-of-magnitude enhancement in electrical conductivity (3.18 × 10−7 S cm−1) compared to pristine MOF (1.28 × 10−11 S cm−1), enabling its direct application in CRS fabrication. Benefiting from the amorphous coordination configuration and exceptional water-mediated catalytic cycling capability of this MOF, the sensor achieves a humidity-dependent H2S coordination catalytic cycle at room temperature under ambient conditions (Fig. 1d). This unique bioinspired design achieves selective molecular olfactory sensing at the level of a single resistive sensing element, thereby granting the sensor outstanding comprehensive performance without any external excitation including high selectivity (against 29 interfering gases), superior sensitivity (~83/ppm), ultra-low measured lower limit (1 ppb), rapid gas sensing response/recovery characteristics (~30 s/73 s), and excellent long-term stability at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Design, construction and characterization of AUL MIL-68(Fe)

In nature, there exists a class of bacteria specifically capable of catalyzing H2S—the sulfate-reducing bacteria. Their catalytic enzyme, hydrogenase, features a core catalytic site known as the H-cluster, whose structure is shown in Fig. 2a. This H-cluster consists of unsaturated Fe atoms and several organic groups22,24. Substituting the H-cluster with MOFs for constructing CRS is intriguing because of their structural similarity. The resulting CRS could potentially exhibit hydrogenase-like high selectivity for H2S detection, but modifications to the MOF are necessary to achieve this biomimicry. In hydrogenase, H2S catalysis occurs in the cellular fluid, where H2S is progressively cleaved by water molecules into HS− and S2−, which then coordinate with the unsaturated Fe atoms for subsequent catalysis (Fig. 2a). Inspired by this, selecting MOFs with high affinity for H2O molecules is more promising for replicating this process in the gas phase environment. Based on multiple studies, the MIL series of MOFs emerged as our target due to their high affinity for H2O and unique proton-conductive behavior25–28. Next, to introduce catalytically active unsaturated Fe sites, post-synthetic excitation of the MOF is essential, as as-synthesized MOFs typically exhibit extremely low unsaturation29. Another interesting phenomenon provided further insight: when subjected to high-temperature deep annealing, MOFs shed their organic ligands and transform into MOS30,31, with a significant boost in electrical conductivity. This suggests the possible existence of an intermediate state where Fe atoms in MOF detach from ligands but do not fully dissociate, creating unsaturated Fe sites while establishing Fe-O bond-dependent conductive pathways that serve dual roles in catalysis and charge transport. We selected MIL-68(Fe) as the initial MOF and achieved AUL MIL-68(Fe) through moderate in situ thermal induction. Because MIL-68(Fe) is a rigid MOF (Fig. S2a)32, its organic ligands resist complete detachment during thermal induction. For comparison, we also examined the flexible MIL-88(Fe) (Fig. S2b)33, which shares identical ligands with MIL-68(Fe) and slowly converts to MOS under the same thermal treatment, making it difficult to obtain the AUL MOF intermediate.

Based on thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) results (Fig. S3a), an appropriate temperature (350 °C) below the phase transition point was selected to thermally induce the transformation of MIL-68(Fe) into an unsaturated 3D conductive MOF. In contrast to conventional one-step synthetic approaches that enhance metal center unsaturation29,34, thermal induction drives extensive BDC (1,4-benzenedicarboxylate) linker fragmentation coupled with framework amorphization, ultimately yielding the uniquely structured AUL MIL-68(Fe) material, density functional theory (DFT) simulations revealed its structural evolution (Fig. 2b).

Compared with MIL-68(Fe), AUL MIL-68(Fe) retained the BDC chains, but the Fe-O1-C bonds became prone to cleavage (Fig. 2b), as shown in Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra. The characteristic νas (COO−) and νs (COO−) vibration bands of BDC at 1557.4 cm−1 and 1401.2 cm−1 (Fig. 2c) were consistently showed35. Meanwhile, in the low-frequency Fe-O region, the vibration band at 508.4 cm−1 (corresponding to Fe-O2 bonds) remained stable, while the band at 547.2 cm−1 (corresponding to Fe-O1 bonds) exhibited significant broadening (Fig. 2d)36,37. The bond cleavage transformed the Fe atoms into highly catalytically active unsaturated Fe species (Fig. 2b), as evidenced by the clear decrease in Fe-O (1.35 Å) and Fe-C (2.01 Å) coordination numbers in the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) profile (Fig. 2e). Notably, the X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra revealed a narrower and lower-energy white line peak (Fig. 2f), suggesting that the Fe 4p orbital reverted to a degenerate state after hybridization38. The bond cleavage also disrupted the octahedral structure of the FeO6 clusters, leading to the formation of a pre-edge peak in the XANES spectra (Fig. 2f) and driving the material’s transition to an amorphous state. This was further confirmed by the weakening and shifting of two prominent XRD peaks near 8˚ (corresponding to the largest d-spacing planes) as the temperature increased to 280 °C, followed by their complete disappearance at 350 °C without the emergence of any new diffraction peaks (Fig. 2g).

In this amorphous unsaturated-ligand state, the directional nature of original chemical bonds was disrupted, leading to atomic rearrangement and reduced interatomic distances that caused structural collapse (Fig. 2h). Notably shortened Fe-O bonds induced Fe-Fe coupling, as evidenced by the increased Fe-Fe (2.51 Å) shell intensities in the extended EXAFS profile (Fig. 2e). These changes induced modifications in both the electronic band structure and atomic configuration of the material. In the projected density of states (PDOS), the original bandgap disappeared, and orbital coupling between Fe 3d and O 2p orbitals was observed near the Fermi level (Fig. 2i). Characteristic Fe-O-Fe peaks were also detected in the Fe 2p X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. 2j). These indicate the formation of a conductive pathway dependent on Fe-O bonds (Fig. 2b), which reduced the optical bandgap of the material from 2.77 eV to 1.98 eV (Fig. S4) and ultimately enhanced the electrical conductivity by four orders of magnitude (from 1.28 × 10−11 S cm−1 to 3.18 × 10−7 S cm−1) (Fig. S5b, c and Table S2). Through this controlled approach, unsaturated Fe atoms were successfully introduced that simultaneously function as both highly active catalytic sites and conductive pathways.

For comparison, we synthesized relatively flexible spindle-shaped MIL-88(Fe) (Fig. S6a). The TG-DSC curves revealed that its mass loss rate was significantly faster than that of MIL-68(Fe) (Fig. S2). Upon annealing at 350 ˚°C, the material underwent MOS transformation, as confirmed by the following evidence. Unlike MIL-68(Fe), the complete disappearance of BDC-derived νas (COO−) and νs (COO−) vibration bands in FT-IR spectra indicates thorough removal of BDC linkers (Fig. S7a), resulting from carbon atom oxidation during thermal induction process. This was corroborated by the appearance of additional subpeaks in Fe 2p and C 1s XPS spectra (Figs. S8 and S9), potentially corresponding to carbon-oxygen compounds. Concurrent with BDC linker loss, the material underwent serious molecular structural transformation as shown in FT-IR results: the Fe-O2 vibration band at 547.2 cm−1 broadened and redshifted, while the Fe-O1 band at 611.7 cm−1 disappeared completely, accompanied by the emergence of a new vibration peak at 450 cm−1 (Fig. S7b, c). Its structural collapse was more pronounced compared to MIL-68(Fe) (Figs. 2h and S6). XRD analysis ultimately revealed weak and partially aligned characteristic peaks corresponding to Fe2O3 (Fig. S10). Due to the incomplete nature of this MOS transformation, we designated the resulting material as activated (AC) MIL-88 (Fe). Notably, its bandgap showed a significant reduction compared to pristine MIL-88(Fe) (from 2.79 eV to 1.95 eV, Fig. S4), accompanied by a remarkable conductivity enhancement (from 5.77 × 10−12 S cm−1 to 2.07 × 10−8 S cm−1, Fig. S5d, e and Table S2). These comparative results demonstrate that AUL MIL-68(Fe) not only preserves the intrinsic MOF characteristics, but also achieves MOS-level conductivity improvement—a unique advantage attributable to its highly symmetric rigid framework structure.

Water adsorption behavior of AUL MIL-68(Fe)

In conventional oxygen-adsorption mechanisms, oxygen molecules must be adsorbed and activated by the MOS sensor to form reactive oxygen species (O2−, O2−, O−) for catalytic processes (Fig. 1c)39. In the current material system, a highly analogous water-adsorption-driven catalytic mechanism was identified, where ambient water molecules spontaneously undergo electrooxidation on the material surface to generate reactive hydroxyl species for subsequent H2S catalysis (Fig. 1d). This section systematically investigates and analyzes this water adsorption behavior.

Water molecules are readily adsorbed by both MIL-68(Fe) and AUL MIL-68(Fe), as evidenced by the broad hydroxyl peak (ν (OH) band around 3400 cm−1) in FT-IR results (Fig. 2c). Subsequently, sensor was fabricated by drop-casting ethanol slurries of the materials onto planar interdigitated electrodes with Al2O3 substrates for electrochemical characterization and subsequent gas-sensing tests (Figs. 3a and S11). In situ cyclic voltammetry (CV) revealed that the adsorbed H2O undergoes electrooxidation on both MIL-68(Fe) and AUL MIL-68(Fe), related to the oxygen evolution reaction (OER)34,40. Distinct oxidation peaks were observed at 0.19 V for MIL-68(Fe) and at 0.47 V and 2.08 V for AUL MIL-68(Fe) (Fig. 3b). The oxidation peaks at 0.19 V and 0.47 V both surpass the 0 V potential and exhibit symmetric reduction peaks, indicating spontaneous and reversible electrooxidation processes. DFT calculations illustrate this spontaneous electrooxidation process, generating protons and hydroxyl species (Fig. 3c). The hydroxyl species formation requires only one transition state, with AUL MIL-68(Fe) demonstrating a lower energy barrier than MIL-68(Fe) (Figs. 3c and S12), attributable to its amorphous unsaturated-ligand characteristics. Unlike MIL-68(Fe), AUL MIL-68(Fe) enables coordinated H2O molecules to undergo free conformational torsion following the principle of energy minimization, overcoming directional coordination constraints. Variable-humidity in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) fitting results confirmed the persistent presence of spontaneous water electrooxidation. The impedance data were perfectly fitted using a two-time-constant model (Fig. 3d and Table S3). Both proton (R1) and electron (R2) resistances decreased significantly with increasing humidity, indicating enhanced electrooxidation under humid conditions. These results suggest that AUL MIL-68(Fe) maintains a hydroxylated coordination state under ambient humidity. Under these conditions, since gas sensing requires operation at a constant potential, the selection of applied voltage inevitably influences the sensing performance. Therefore, we selected 0.3 V within the low oxidation potential range common to both materials as the working potential. Current-time (I–t) characteristics at 0.3 V demonstrated typical mixed conduction behavior for AUL MIL-68(Fe) and MIL-68(Fe) (Figs. 3e and S13a)41,42. The electrooxidation-generated protons rapidly migrated under the applied electric field, producing a substantial proton current. As the proton current depleted, the current stabilized with dominant electronic conduction. Given the humidity’s influence on absolute current values, we maintained stable environmental humidity during subsequent gas sensing tests and initiated measurements only after baseline current stabilization.

Notably, broad hydroxyl peaks (ν (OH) band ~3400 cm−1) were also found for MIL-88(Fe) and AC MIL-88(Fe) in FT-IR results (Fig. S7a), and identical I–t curve characteristics were observed for MIL-88(Fe) and AC MIL-88(Fe) (Fig. S13b, c), indicating their similar water adsorption behaviors.

Hydrogen sulfide gas sensing properties of AUL MIL-68(Fe)

In gradient concentration testing, AUL MIL-68(Fe) exhibited an extensive detection range from 1 ppb to 166.67 ppm H2S (Fig. 4a), the sensor’s response value to 1 ppb H2S (0.044) is greater than three times the noise level (RMS, Root Mean Square) (0.038), demonstrating stable gas sensing responses even at high concentrations while overcoming the gas sensing response delays characteristic of MOS sensors43. The material showed rapid and consistent gas sensing response times across different concentration steps (Fig. S14) with complete recovery at the end (Fig. S15), indicating highly efficient H2S catalytic cycling. Unlike the linear gas sensing response-concentration relationship typical of MOS sensors, AUL MIL-68(Fe) displayed a distinct logarithmic correlation (Fig. 4b) and progressively decreasing sensitivity during single-exposure tests (Fig. 4c), mimicking the olfactory desensitization phenomenon observed in biological systems exposed to high H2S levels44. Remarkably, the sensor achieved an exceptional sensitivity of 83/ppm at low concentrations, highlighting its trace-detection capability. Comparative studies revealed AUL MIL-68(Fe)’s superior reproducibility in both gas sensing response magnitude and response/recovery kinetics relative to MIL-68(Fe) (Fig. 4d, e). Benchmarking against H2S sensors based on alternative material systems—including MOS45–48, conventional 3D MOFs49–51, conductive 3D MOF composite materials15,16 and 2D materials52–55—demonstrated the comprehensive performance advantages of bioinspired 3D conductive AUL MIL-68(Fe) resistive sensor in measured lower limit, sensitivity coefficient (sensitivity/ppm), and gas sensing response/recovery speed (Fig. 4f, g). Notably, the sensor exhibited fingerprint-level selectivity for H2S, showing no anysensitivity to 12 different gases at 5 ppm concentrations (Fig. 4h). Extended testing against 29 interfering gases (Figs. S17 and S18) with calculated selectivity ratio (Rsp (H2S)/Rsp (Gas)) at the same gas concentration) (Table S4) further confirmed this exceptional specificity. To visualize the results more clearly, the selective ratio reciprocal (Rsp (Gas)/Rsp (H2S)) image for 30 gases is presented (Fig. 4i and Table S5). Building on the intrinsic classification capability of MIL-68(Fe) (specific to thiols, carboxylic acids, and amines), the AUL-modified variant eliminated its amine sensitivity while markedly enhancing its characteristic response to H2S (Fig. 4i). When challenged with 29 interfering gases, it achieved selectivity on par with biomass-based chemical sensors9,10. This performance not only matches that of biomimetic sensors but also obviously surpasses reported high-selectivity abiotic systems10,56. We therefore propose to define this exceptional characteristic as fingerprint-level selectivity.

Comparative analysis revealed that thermal induction under other conditions showed limited effectiveness in improving the gas-sensing performance of both MIL-68 (Fe) and MIL-88 (Fe). The sensors exhibited unsatisfactory detection limits and poor resolution (Figs. S19 and S20).

Furthermore, the humidity dependence of the sensors was evaluated (Fig. S21). As humidity increases, both MIL-68(Fe) and AUL MIL-68(Fe) exhibit a significant improvement in response signal-to-noise ratio, and the response current also rises with increasing humidity. In contrast to the recovery rate, the response rate shows a strict positive correlation with humidity. Overall, elevated humidity contributes to enhanced gas-sensing performance of the materials. In the following section, the water-adsorption-catalyzed sensing mechanism of the materials toward H2S is discussed in detail.

Hydrogen sulfide molecular olfactory sensing mechanism of AUL MIL-68(Fe)

The high selectivity of AUL MIL-68(Fe) for H2S sensing is established on a water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism. In the CV curves, the overlapping oxidation peaks of H2O, H2S, and CH2SH at the 0.3 V test potential reflect the co-oxidation behavior between the two gases and H2O (Fig. 5a), which also explains why increasing humidity can effectively enhance the response signal/current to them (Fig. S22). Under low-humidity conditions, AUL MIL-68(Fe) exhibits very low efficiency in catalyzing H2S. When the reddish-brown AUL MIL-68(Fe) powder was placed in a 100 ppm H2S atmosphere at 20% RH, no significant color change was observed, and the powder remained its original reddish-brown color. However, in a 70% RH environment with 100 ppm H2S, the powder quickly turned black (Fig. 5c). This color change originates from ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) from HS*/S* species to the Fe atom, more specifically to its 3 d orbital57. We compared MIL-68(Fe), MIL-88 (Fe), and AC MIL-88 (Fe) and did not observe the same color change as AUL MIL-68 (Fe) under high humidity conditions (Fig. S23). Additional experiments were performed to further investigate the interaction between H2S and AUL MIL-68(Fe). In situ EIS measurements revealed a significant decrease in electron transfer resistance (R2) with increasing H2S concentration, demonstrating the redox reaction between H2S and AUL MIL-68(Fe) (Fig. 5d and Table S6). The narrower white-line peak in the XANES spectrum indicates a further reduced splitting of the Fe 4p orbitals (Fig. 5e). Simultaneously, the narrower pre-edge features suggest coordination between Fe atoms and S species (Fig. 5e). This interaction induces a rightward shift in the Fe 2p binding energy for AUL MIL-68(Fe) (H2S) (Fig. 5f), implying an increased electron density within the system. Based on the amplified edge profile (Fig. S24), the Fe valence state in AUL MIL-68(Fe) is close to +358. Upon H2S exposure, the valence state of AUL MIL-68(Fe) (H2S) increases slightly (shifting ~0.3 eV toward the MIL-68(Fe) direction), accompanied by a reduction in the occupancy of Fe 3d orbitals. Integrating the XPS and XAS results, it is demonstrated that electron delocalization within the Fe–O–Fe bridges leads to the formation of electron-rich, high-valent Fe species. This electronic state enables Fe atoms to function as both Lewis acid and Lewis base sites, facilitating facile electron transfer between the ligands and Fe centers, thereby conferring superior catalytic activity to the Fe atoms. These results demonstrate that the unsaturated Fe atoms, serving as conductive sites, also provide abundant catalytic centers, thereby endowing the sensor with ultrahigh sensitivity and an ultralow measured lower limit. The key to achieving integrated catalytic conductivity lies in the Fe atoms possessing both the ability to coordinate and catalyze water molecules and H2S molecules, as well as a high degree of orbital coupling with other atoms to facilitate electron delocalization. On one hand, this electron delocalization enhances the intrinsic carrier concentration of the material, improving its electrical conductivity. On the other hand, it also facilitates the migration of valence electrons generated from the catalytic oxidation of H2S within the material. The initial MOF is in a + 3 oxidation state, and its lattice integrity is high, making it difficult for H2S to coordinate with Fe atoms, thereby suppressing the catalytic activity of Fe. Simultaneously, electrons are confined within the metal/organic ligand nodes, making it challenging for them to overcome the high energy barrier for effective migration12. After thermal induction, AUL MIL-68(Fe) exhibits a mixed-valence state slightly lower than +3, indicating that the MOF lattice integrity has been compromised. When this disruption reaches a certain extent, unsaturated metal atoms are introduced into the material system. In a weak-field environment with the presence of water molecules, Fe3+ tends to form a high-coordination octahedral structure with six coordination sites57. The unsaturated mixed-valence Fe atoms can readily associate with H2S molecules, demonstrating catalytic activity as an electron acceptor for H2S, while also exhibiting electron-donor characteristics that promote electron delocalization. Furthermore, this symmetry breaking reduces the directional covalent bonding in the MOF, enhancing orbital coupling between metal-metal and metal-organic ligands. This results in a reduced band gap and enhanced electron delocalization.

Similar to H2O molecules (Fig. 3b), since the overlapping oxidation potential region of H2S in the CV curves is close to the zero potential point and highly symmetric with the reduction potential (Fig. 5a), the catalytic oxidation of H2S in the presence of water molecules is actually a highly reversible spontaneous process. So, when the black AUL MIL-68(Fe) (H2S) powder was divided into two portions and placed in air environments with different humidity levels, it was found that the powder in the 20% low-humidity environment remained black for an extended period, while the powder in the 70% high-humidity environment quickly reverted to its reddish-brown color (Fig. 5c). This indicates that H2O molecules accelerate the catalytic cycle of H2S.

To provide a clearer explanation of this water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism, we performed DFT calculations on this process. Under anhydrous conditions, H2S molecules are gradually decomposed into HS* and S*, with two barrier transition states (TS1 and TS2) existing in between (Figs. 5g and S25). In humid environments, based on previous results, AUL MIL-68(Fe) exists in a hydroxylated coordination state, where hydroxyl groups accelerate the decomposition of H2S coordinated with Fe atoms into HS* and S* without any intermediate barrier states (Fig. 5h). Assuming that the energy barriers for the two-step decomposition of H2S into HS* and S* can be approximated as zero, the enhancement of the reaction rate due to water-molecule catalysis can be quantified. It is evident that this water-assisted process significantly accelerates the overall catalytic cycle of H2S. In this process, H2O molecules act as catalysts, similar to oxygen in the oxygen-adsorption mechanism, providing OH* as active catalytic mediators. Unlike the MOS-catalyzed generation of high-valence S species from H2S, this process exhibits a far superior cycling efficiency compared to conventional oxygen-adsorption mechanisms. This high efficiency directly accounts for its fast response/recovery characteristics in gas sensing.

The high selectivity of AUL MIL-68(Fe) is strongly correlated with this water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism. In contrast, we found that MIL-68(Fe) catalyzes H2S more dominantly via oxygen atoms, leading to a left-shift of the Fe 2p XPS peak for MIL-68(Fe) (H2S) (Fig. 5f) along with the formation of sulfur oxides (Fig. S26a). Compared with MIL-68(Fe), the Fe 2p and O 1s XPS of MIL-68(Fe) (NH3) shows highly similar evolution characteristics to that of MIL-68(Fe) (H2S) (Fig. S27), while nitrogen oxides are simultaneously generated (Fig. S26b). This explains why MIL-68(Fe) is susceptible to interference from amine substances. In contrast, no N signal was detected in AUL MIL-68(Fe) (NH3) (Fig. S26b). Furthermore, based on Fe 2p XPS results, we found that oxidation of AUL MIL-68(Fe) at the second H2O oxidation potential (actual test potential of 2 V) (Fig. 5a) generates high-valence-state Fe (AUL MIL-68(Fe) (2 V)) (Fig. 5f), under which condition the material’s selectivity relationship between H2S and NH3 is reversed (Figs. 5b and S28). After catalyzing NH3, this high-valence state completely disappears (binding energy shifts right), with Fe atoms acting as electron acceptors. Therefore, the catalysis of NH3 by AUL MIL-68(Fe) (2 V) still belongs to the water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism. Notably, this water-adsorption-coordinated catalytic mechanism of AUL MIL-68(Fe) provides a controllable selectivity regulation method, enabling electrochemical selectivity modulation of gas-sensing responses on CRS.

Methods

Sample synthesis and sensor fabrication (Fig. S1)

Materials

Iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 99.99%) and nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Terephthalic acid, N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and NaOH (95%, AR grade) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used directly without further purification.

Synthesis of MIL-68(Fe)

32.0 mg FeCl3·6H2O and 66.4 mg terephthalic acid were dissolved in 20 ml DMF ultrasonic treatment 10 min to get a clear and transparent yellow solution (solution A), stirring for another 10 min. Meanwhile, 160.0 mg NaOH was dissolved in 10 ml ultrapure water ultrasonic treatment 10 min to get an even solution. Then 1.0 ml NaOH solution was added into solution A drop by drop, the solution was kept stirring during the process and for another 30 min, and finally got a tan emulsion (solution B). Subsequently, solution B was transferred into Teflon-sealed autoclave and heated for 12 h at 100 °C. The as-prepared samples were washed and centrifugated with DMF, absolute alcohol, absolute alcohol respectively for three times. Finally, the washed samples were dried at 65 °C for 8 h and collected.

Synthesis of MIL-88(Fe)

Except that the content of FeCl3·6H2O was changed to 115.7 mg, the other parameters and procedures were consistent with the synthesis of MIL-68(Fe).

Thermal induction for MOF

Synthetic MOF samples were simulated through annealing to induce structure response. Samples were put in ceramic crucible and located in muffle furnace to keep at different temperature for a certain time with a heating rate of 3 °C/min, and then cooled in furnace.

Amorphous unsaturated-ligand (AUL) MIL-68(Fe) and activated (AC) MIL-88(Fe)

MIL-68(Fe) and MIL-88(Fe) powders were annealed at 350 °C for 12 h in air with a heating rate of 3 °C/min and then cooled in furnace.

Sensor fabrication

The resulting powder sample was dispersed in ethanol to form a suspension, which was then dropped onto screen-printed Au planar electrodes on a 100 °C hotplate to fabricate room-temperature sensors.

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were carried out on a Rigaku D/Max 2550 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) with a scanning speed of 5°/min.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained by a JEOL JSM-7500F electron microscope. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were obtained by Japan JEOL JEM-F200 electron microscope. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) images were collected on matched spectroscopy system of SEM and TEM microscopes.

Thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) test was conducted on STA449F3 TG/DSC integrated analyzer from Germany in air atmosphere with increasing temperature rate of 10 °C/min.

UV-visible absorption spectra were performed on an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (SolidSpec-3700, Japan).

Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR) was analyzed with Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50R, American).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on AXIS Supra+ X-ray photoelectron spectrometer from Shimadzu Corporation, Japan.
