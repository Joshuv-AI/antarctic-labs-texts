Single-visible-light-modulated photoswitches with aggregation-induced emission for super-resolution imaging
Authors: Xiaowei Fang, Pengwei Jin, Fanghui Li, Tongyun Zhang, Zehan Zhang, Mengqi Li, Wei-Hong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Super-resolution fluorescence imaging (SRI) overcomes the traditional optical diffraction limit, enabling the visualization of microstructures at the nanoscale. Central to this technique is the precise photo-modulation of molecular fluorescence emission, yet it remains a formidable challenge to develop visible-light-responsive photoswitching systems with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) that are biocompatible and suitable for solid-state applications. Herein, we construct a series of unique fluorescent photoswitches with single-visible-light modulation by incorporating sulfone units into a sterically hindered diarylethene. By introducing sulfone units and adjusting the substituent effect, the fluorescent photoswitches exhibit band-tail absorption, demonstrating superior single-visible-light-stimulation ability (488 or 561 nm light) and efficiently preventing damage from short-wavelength light. Notably, the weak π–π stacking and the activated restriction of intramolecular vibration (RIV) process, caused by the large, bulky ethylene bridge, also endow the fluorescent photoswitch with unique aggregation-induced emission properties. Thus, such fluorescent photoswitches exhibit fluorescence emission with a high fluorescent quantum yield and on-off ratio (>100) in the aggregated state, which successfully enables a single long-wavelength 561 nm laser, a privileged source, to perform Stochastic Optical Reconstruction Microscopy (STORM) imaging of nano-micelles with high resolution. This work offers a robust photonics-based approach to the development of remote and non-invasive fluorescence modulation platforms, fundamentally advancing the frontiers of SRI techniques.

Introduction

Fluorescent photoswitches are key to achieving sub-diffraction spatial resolution in stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM), directly influencing the quality of the final image.1–5 Nevertheless, traditional switch molecules convert between fluorescent (on) and dark (off) states induced by two light sources,6–8 typically involving biologically harmful ultraviolet radiation.9 Switching to low-energy visible light for excitation effectively mitigates phototoxic effects in living cells and allows for a greater number of photoswitching cycles.9–12 Conventional visible-light-excited fluorophores, predominantly cyanine and rhodamine derivatives,13,14 rely on exogenous additives (e.g., oxygen scavenging system and thiol) to achieve the necessary blinking properties for SRI applications.15–17 Recently, diarylethenes (DAEs) have emerged as highly promising fluorescent photoswitches,18–20 which have been widely investigated due to their unique merits of remote non-invasive operation, excellent reversibility, and reagent-free nature.21,22 However, most fluorescent systems are prone to fluorescence quenching at high concentrations,23 which potentially constrains further enhancements in imaging sensitivity (Fig. 1a).24,25 Therefore, aggregation-induced emission (AIE) materials have emerged as a powerful tool,26,27 utilizing efficient emission in the aggregated state to realize high-brightness, high-SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) imaging that is increasingly valued for visualizing biological structures and dynamic in situ monitoring.28–32 Despite the highly biocompatible and non-invasive nature of visible-light modulation, the photoswitchable systems that exhibit AIE behaviour and operate under visible light have rarely been reported, due to a lack of appropriate building blocks.33

Here, we present a series of unique single-visible-light-driven photoswitches with AIE effect for STORM imaging by incorporating the sulfone structure into the steric-hindrance DAE system (Fig. 1b).34–36 The incorporation of sulfone units enables the fluorescent photoswitches to possess turn-on fluorescence performance upon photoisomerization. Furthermore, the electron-donating substituents endow the fluorescent photoswitches with band-tail absorption phenomenon, enabling photocyclization and photocycloreversion under exceptionally long-wavelength visible light stimulation (488 or 561 nm light). The band-tail absorption originates from the thermal transition between vibrational excited states. The enhanced electron-donating effect also improves the fluorescence quantum yield and the visible-light photoresponsive performance of the photoswitch, preventing the damage caused by short-wavelength light.37 Moreover, the closed-ring compounds (c-) in powder show distinct fluorescence with AIE effect, whereas the open-ring compound (o-) exhibits negligible emission, further conferring high-contrast, photoreversible “turn-on” type fluorescent modulation.38,39 The strong aggregated-state emission of the closed-ring isomer originates from the large, bulky benzobis(thiadiazole) bridge, which efficiently impedes intermolecular π–π stacking in the aggregated state along with the activated restriction of the RIV effect.36 Finally, we prepared cylindrical micelles by doping fluorescent photoswitches into polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PSt-b-PEO) to achieve STORM imaging performed by single-visible light (561 nm) to regulate the activation, excitation, and deactivation process. This single-visible-light-modulated AIE photoswitch offers a promising application for super-resolution imaging in the visualization of nanostructures.

Results and discussion

Oxidized diarylethene featuring high contrast turn-on mode fluorescence modulation

As aforementioned, rare fluorescent photoswitches have exhibited long-wavelength light modulation and AIE effect simultaneously, due to a lack of appropriate building blocks. Here, by taking advantage of the sterically-hindered diarylethene with a unique AIE effect from the restricted intramolecular motion process and weak π–π stacking,36,40 we further oxidized the benzothiophene groups and introduced different substituents with various electronic effects, developing single-visible-light-driven fluorescent photoswitches and gaining insight into the influence of electron effects on photochromic and photoluminescent properties. The series of photoswitches was readily synthesized through sulfur oxidation and Suzuki–Miyaura coupling (synthetic methods are described in the SI). All chemical structures were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy as well as high-resolution mass spectra (HRMS).

With the oxidation of benzothiophene, four designed photoswitches still exhibited excellent photochromic behaviour upon UV light irradiation (Fig. 2a and S24). The initial colourless THF solution of OS 2 turned yellow, accompanied by a new absorption band centred at 472 nm, with a photocyclization conversion ratio up to 97.9% (Fig. 2b). The other three compounds also exhibited exceptionally high photoreaction conversion ratios (Table 1 and S25). The decay plots show no obvious decay over 100 hours, indicating that all four closed-ring isomers (c-) possess outstanding thermal stability (Fig. 2c and S26). Electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis reveals that increasing the electron-donating strength of the substituent progressively lowers the potential at the core, reflecting an augmented local electron density (Fig. 2d and S31). Compared to OS 2, OS 3 and OS 4 featured longer absorption bands in the visible spectrum with red-shifted absorption bands of up to 45 and 73 nm, respectively, which results from the electron-donating effect of the thiophene and carbazole groups (Fig. 2e). In contrast, OS 1 exhibited an 8 nm blue-shift compared to OS 2, with a strong electron-withdrawing trifluoromethylphenyl group. This trend is similarly observable in the fluorescence emission spectra under visible light excitation, providing a clear illustration that increased electron cloud density contributes to both the visible light absorption and fluorescence emission extending towards longer wavelengths (Fig. 2f).41 Frontier molecular orbital analysis demonstrates narrower HOMO–LUMO energy gaps for OS 3 and OS 4 relative to OS 1 and OS 2, which is in excellent agreement with the preceding experimental observations (Fig. S32).

The fluorescence modulation of most diarylethene systems exhibits light-induced quenching behaviour, wherein the open-ring isomer (o-) possesses marked fluorescence that converts to a non-fluorescent or weakly fluorescent closed-ring isomer under ultraviolet irradiation.42,43 This mode yields relatively low fluorescence contrast and signal-to-noise ratios, rendering it susceptible to background noise interference. Unlike other fluorescent photoswitches, the open-ring isomer of OSs exhibited negligible emission upon visible-light excitation due to its very weak absorption in the visible-light region. Then, upon UV (λ = 313 ± 10 nm) exposure, the fluorescence emission showed a pronounced enhancement, with a fluorescence ON-to-OFF contrast ratio up to 101.6, which can be ascribed to the conversion from the open to the closed isomer (Fig. 2g, S35 and S36). Thus, OSs are classical turn-on mode fluorescent molecules: the closed form is fluorescent whilst the open form remains in a dark state, and remarkably high fluorescence contrast is achievable under visible light excitation (Fig. 2h). Overall, the rational design of oxidized DAE featuring a sterically hindered ethene bridge realizes outstanding photoresponse performance and turn-on mode fluorescence modulation. Through donor molecular engineering, OSs exhibit a significant redshift in their absorption–emission spectra and a higher fluorescence ON-to-OFF contrast ratio under visible-light excitation, making them promising for fluorescent imaging. The “turn-on” mode of fluorescence modulation improves signal-to-noise ratio in practical imaging applications.44,45

Single-visible-light-driven photocyclization via enhanced electron-donating effect

While investigating the oxidized diarylethenes, we observed a quite different photocycloreversion reaction in OS 3, which bears electron-donating thiophene groups. After prolonged visible light irradiation (λ > 470 nm) of the closed-ring isomer c-OS 3, its characteristic absorption and emission bands clearly remained (Fig. 2i and S27). This aligns with the “band tail absorption” effect specific to sulfone systems, which is absent in OS 1 and OS 2. OS 4, substituted with strongly electron-donating carbazole groups, behaves similarly. Given that the absorption edge of the open-ring isomer o-OS 4 lies below 470 nm, 510 nm light irradiation should theoretically drive a complete cycloreversion of the closed form to the open form. Surprisingly, extended irradiation at λ > 510 nm resulted in the retention of the closed-form absorption band, with 25.7% of c-OS 4 remaining unreacted (Fig. 3a). This indicates a dynamic equilibrium where visible light simultaneously drives both the cycloreversion of closed-ring isomers and the photocyclization of open-ring isomers, despite the latter lacking apparent absorption at this wavelength. This far-off resonance photocyclization is ascribed to electronic transitions enabled by weak hot bands or Urbach tail absorption.46 To visualize this visible-light responsiveness, the cyclization processes were tracked via absorption spectroscopy (Fig. 3b). 1H NMR titration was employed to quantify the structural evolution of o-OS 4 under 488 nm irradiation (Fig. 3c, d and S29). Interestingly, o-OS 4 showed significant photoresponsiveness at this wavelength, affording a high conversion ratio of 81.4% as determined by 1H NMR, whereas the corresponding result for o-OS 3 is 72.1% (Fig. S28).

Benefiting from the enhanced electron-donating effect that extends the absorption to 600 nm, OS 4 was observed to exhibit visible-light-induced cyclization at 560 nm. Under illumination at 560 nm, the corresponding conversion ratio is 15.6%, which has not been reported previously (Fig. 3e and S30). Upon irradiation with 560 nm visible light to the photostable state (PSS), absorption spectra of o-OS 4 reveal the emergence of new absorption bands in the visible region (Fig. 3f). In conventional absorption spectra of open-ring isomers, the band tail region manifested as a gradually declining curve. Notably, the absorption α of o-OS 4 varies exponentially with photon energy E in the 450–600 nm range, a phenomenon consistent with the Urbach empirical rule (Fig. 3g).47 This confirms that OS 3 and OS 4 undergo visible-light-driven cyclization via band tail absorption. Overall, through donor molecular engineering, the fluorescent photoswitch OS has been endowed with visible-light excitation properties, which are promising for biological applications.12,48

AIE behaviour enhancing fluorescent contrast by steric-hindrance diarylethene

Traditional fluorescent photoswitches always face the problem of aggregation-caused quenching, which greatly limits their practical applications.49,50 To address this, the sterically bulky central benzobis(thiadiazole) bridge employed in our system induces a highly twisted conformation to weaken intermolecular π–π stacking, thereby endowing the molecule with intrinsic AIE properties (Fig. S33 and S34).36 The AIE effect can also further enhance the fluorescent contrast, facilitating the application in fluorescence imaging. Thus, we further examined the fluorescent properties of the open and closed forms of OS 2, OS 3, and OS 4 in the aggregate state (Fig. S37 and S38). The emission spectra of closed-ring isomers in THF/H2O mixtures with different water fractions were measured to investigate their AIE behaviour. In solution, the closed form of OS 4 exhibits bright yellow fluorescence, with a fluorescence quantum yield (ΦF) of 1.81%. With the water fraction increasing to 50%, the fluorescence exhibited a red shift in wavelength, which can be ascribed to the ICT effect from the D–π–A structure of a strong electron acceptor (central benzobisthiadiazole bridge) and electron donor (carbazole group). Upon further increase in water fraction, fluorescence enhances markedly and reaches a maximum at 80% with a fluorescent quantum yield of 24.6% (Fig. 4b and e). Compared to OS 4, OS 3 exhibits similar aggregation-induced fluorescence performance with a relatively weak quantum yield of 13.3%, ascribed to different electron-donating ability between thiophene and carbazole groups (Fig. 4a and d). Compared to c-OS 4 with outstanding AIE properties, o-OS 4 shows negligible fluorescence emission upon excitation at 506 nm, with a fluorescence contrast ratio reaching 124 (Fig. 4e and S38). This is attributed to the absence of absorption in the visible light region. Moreover, the enhanced fluorescence contrast in the aggregated state relative to the solution state is highly desirable for super-resolution imaging.51

Comprehensively comparing OS 2–4, the emission of all three photoswitches shows significant enhancement in the aggregated state, with c-OS 4 demonstrating a 13.6-fold increase, exceeding that of c-OS 2 (9.9-fold) and c-OS 3 (10.2-fold) (Fig. 4f). Additionally, it was discovered that the powder displayed reversible photoswitching under alternative UV and visible light irradiation (Fig. 4c).21,52,53 Before UV exposure, the colour of OS 2 powder was yellow with negligible fluorescence. Then, under irradiation by an ultraviolet lamp (λ = 313 ± 10 nm), the colour gradually changed to dark green, accompanied by yellow fluorescence emission, corroborating the photo-induced conversion from open-ring to closed-ring isomer. OS 4 exhibits the highest fluorescence quantum yield in aggregation and the solid state (Fig. 4f and Table S4). Given the outstanding AIE behaviour and inherent high fluorescence contrast, it holds great promise for STORM imaging.

Single-visible-light modulating AIE photoswitches for STORM imaging

Long-wavelength excitation light sources hold appeal for bioimaging applications due to their enhanced tissue penetration, reduced photodamage to biological tissues, and diminished interference from background autofluorescence.9,54,55 Within our photoswitching system, the electron-donating effect of the substituent can significantly extend the visible-light absorption towards longer wavelengths, which has inspired us to investigate photoswitches activated by longer-wavelength light to modulate fluorescence emission. To demonstrate the excellent turn-on fluorescence switching of OS, we prepared OS-loaded cylindrical nano-micelles and evaluated their performance in SRI (Fig. 5a).56,57 In solid micelles, the AIE property of OSs results in sufficient fluorescence intensity and high fluorescent contrast (Fig. S39). Compared to conventional photoswitches, typically requiring two lasers to reversibly convert between fluorescence ON and OFF states, OS 3 and OS 4 possess the characteristic of simultaneously driving both photocyclization and photocycloreversion using a single visible light (Fig. S40). This enables highly tunable fluorescence modulation with a single laser. Given that OS 4 can undergo photocyclization and emit fluorescence upon illumination with 560 nm light, we attempted reversible photo-modulation using a single 561 nm light source, a typical wavelength of a STORM laser.

The process for performing STROM imaging using a single laser is as follows (Fig. 5b): (1) activating the photoswitch to its fluorescence ON state via cyclization, (2) exciting the probe's fluorescence, (3) deactivating the photoswitch to its fluorescence OFF state via photocycloreversion. OS 4-labeled cylindrical micelles underwent off/on fluorescence switching cycles upon continuous illumination from a single 561 nm laser, thereby reconstructing nano-scale resolution imaging (Fig. 5e).58 The image resolution of STORM imaging significantly enhances the signal with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 49 nm, an 8-fold reduction compared to conventional widefield fluorescence imaging of about 382 nm (Fig. 5e, right).59 It is noteworthy that the resolution of the reconstructing nanostructure image under long-wavelength laser excitation shows no degradation compared to that under dual-laser modulation (Fig. 5c). The AIE photoswitch OS 4 also realized a single light performed STORM imaging on micelles upon activation by 488 nm laser with FWHM in super resolution imaging of about 46 nm, and over 7-fold enhancement than that in conventional imaging of about 454 nm (Fig. 5d). These results suggest that the long-wavelength light-driven AIE photoswitch based on our steric-hindrance photochromic system undoubtedly eliminates the requirement for harmful UV-light radiation and offering a more biologically favourable alternative compared to traditional approaches for STORM imaging.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully developed a series of unique fluorescent photoswitches with distinct single-visible-light modulation performance and AIE properties, thereby circumventing reliance on phototoxic UV irradiation and the inherent limitations of aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). By oxidizing the sulfur atom to the sulfone moiety and incorporating strong electron-donating substituents, we effectively broadened the absorption profile, allowing us to harness Urbach tail absorption and enabling reversible photoisomerization under long-wavelength visible light (488 or 561 nm). Furthermore, the sterically hindered backbone effectively impedes intermolecular π–π stacking. It restricts intramolecular motion (RIM), endowing the system with exceptional AIE activity and sufficient fluorescent contrast, as evidenced by a 10-fold increase in fluorescence quantum yield in the aggregated state relative to the solution state. Specifically, the resulting photoswitches photoswitch OS exhibit robust fluorescence in their closed-ring isomer alongside a remarkable ON/OFF contrast ratio (>100), which is highly beneficial for high-resolution imaging. Thus, precise molecular engineering enables a single visible laser (488 or 561 nm) to simultaneously regulate the excitation, activation, and deactivation processes of OS, making it an outstanding agent for super-resolution imaging. Consequently, we successfully visualized polymer micelle nanostructures with a spatial resolution of 49 nm (FWHM), significantly surpassing the optical diffraction limit and delivering an 8-fold improvement over conventional wide-field imaging. This work not only establishes a robust and prospective design strategy for photoresponsive AIE platforms but also paves the way for their advanced nanoimaging applications in materials science and biology.
