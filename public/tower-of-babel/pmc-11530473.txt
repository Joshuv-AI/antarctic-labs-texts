Exploring the complexity of genome size reduction in angiosperms
Authors: Akihiro Ezoe, Motoaki Seki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The genome sizes of angiosperms decreased significantly more than the genome sizes of their ancestors (pteridophytes and gymnosperms). Decreases in genome size involve a highly complex process, with remnants of the genome size reduction scattered across the genome and not directly linked to specific genomic structures. This is because the associated mechanisms operate on a much smaller scale than the mechanisms mediating increases in genome size. This review thoroughly summarizes the available literature regarding the molecular mechanisms underlying genome size reductions and introduces Utricularia gibba and Arabidopsis thaliana as model species for the examination of the effects of these molecular mechanisms. Additionally, we propose that phosphorus deficiency and drought stress are the major external factors contributing to decreases in genome size. Considering these factors affect almost all land plants, angiosperms likely gained the mechanisms for genome size reductions. These environmental factors may affect the retention rates of deletions, while also influencing the mutation rates of deletions via the functional diversification of the proteins facilitating double-strand break repair. The biased retention and mutation rates of deletions may have synergistic effects that enhance deletions in intergenic regions, introns, transposable elements, duplicates, and repeats, leading to a rapid decrease in genome size. We suggest that these selection pressures and associated molecular mechanisms may drive key changes in angiosperms during recurrent cycles of genome size decreases and increases.

Introduction

Does the size of a genome, which is an assembly of DNA, have any function in itself? Because genome size is heritable and can rapidly change, the function of genome size itself can be advantageous during evolution. To date, the mechanisms driving genome size enlargement, including polyploidizations, segmental duplications, and retrotransposon activities, which occur on a large-scale, have been impactful and irreversible, contributing to the belief that plants have “a one-way ticket to genomic obesity” (Bennetzen and Kellogg 1997). Notably, pteridophytes and gymnosperms possess large genomes because of numerous whole-genome duplications (WGDs) and transposon expansions. Examples include Ophioglossum reticulatum (2n = 96x = 1,440 chromosomes) (Fig. 1) (Bennett 2004; Pellicer et al. 2018). However, decreases in the genome size of angiosperms, which diverged from gymnosperms, have resulted in an approximately 2,400-fold difference in genome size, ranging from 150 Gb in Paris japonica to 63 Mb in Genlisea margaretae (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013; Simonin and Roddy 2018). Interestingly, the distribution of angiosperm genome sizes is skewed toward small genomes (approximately 3-times difference between the median and mean C-values: 1,671 and 5,029 Mb, respectively), which contrasts with the normal distribution of the histograms for pteridophytes and gymnosperms (Wang et al. 2021). Genome size in angiosperms has decreased in general relative to the pteridophytes and gymnosperms and not just in a few species (Fig. 1). Moreover, according to the estimated size of the genome of the common ancestor of angiosperms, gymnosperms, and pteridophytes, genome size reduction occurred only in angiosperms (Simonin and Roddy 2018). These findings reflect the prevalence of decreases in genome size and the existence of related selection pressures. Furthermore, genome size reduction may play some functional roles during angiosperm evolution.

Of the molecular mechanisms mediating decreases in genome size, large-scale processes are rare under natural conditions, with comparative genome analyses revealing a bias toward small-scale insertions and deletions (Wang et al. 2021). Small-scale reductions appear to be intermittent and complex, making it somewhat difficult to detect these changes according to the genomic structure of plants that have undergone genome size reduction-related events. Thus, insights into the link between genome size reduction mechanisms and genome structure are limited.

Advances in sequencing technology have made it possible for researchers to comprehensively examine genome structures, including repetitive DNA, heterochromatin regions, transposons, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and structural variants (Mizuno et al. 2020). This has enabled comparisons of sequence features among closely related species or within populations, resulting in the identification of factors that determine genome size and the elucidation of the complexity in the traces left by genome size reduction mechanisms (Du et al. 2020a; Sato et al. 2023). For this review, we summarized related papers to clarify decreases in genome size in terms of the underlying mechanisms and the traces within genome structures. We herein introduce comparative studies of plant species, such as Utricularia gibba (genome size: 77 Mb) and Arabidopsis thaliana (genome size: 125 Mb), which evolved to have smaller genomes than their close relatives. These plants grow in environments affected by phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N) deficiencies, which are major selection pressures for genome size reductions (Stetter et al. 2015; Veleba et al. 2020). In this context, we further elucidated the relationship between P and N availability and decreases in genome size. This review may be useful for future comparisons of genome size and features between closely related species.

Molecular mechanisms underlying genome size reductions

In this section, we introduce the molecular mechanisms implicated in the loss of DNA sequences and their distinctive remnants, with a particular focus on insertion and deletion (indel) bias. Indels are the most abundant structural variations in plants and the second most frequently observed following point mutations (Vishwakarma et al. 2017). Because of their considerable abundance, indels vary in terms of length among individuals and occur throughout the genome (Guo et al. 2016a; Pu et al. 2023). If there is a selection pressure toward a genome size reduction, the decrease in genome size may broadly involve changes in indel proportions. Double-strand break (DSB) repair, unequal crossover, and replication slippage have been proposed as the mechanisms responsible for indels. Here, we introduce the characteristics of the target or remnant sequences associated with each indel as well as the related molecular mechanisms (Table 1).

Indels caused by DSB repair

One of the most serious forms of DNA damage is DSB due to ultraviolet rays or the generation of free radicals (West et al. 2004). Earlier research indicated DSBs are repaired by homologous recombination (HR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), and single-strand annealing (SSA) (Fig. 2a) (Mladenova et al. 2022; Morales-Ruiz et al. 2024), among which HR is highly accurate (i.e., indels are rare). However, NHEJ and SSA do not repair the regions deleted by DSB; they connect the broken sequences independently of homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids. Therefore, HR is possible only during DNA replication, but NHEJ and SSA occur during most cell cycle stages. Furthermore, NHEJ and SSA occur in all chromosomal regions, resulting in numerous genome-wide deletions.

Traces of proliferative transposable elements (TEs) inactivated by deletions associated with NHEJ and SSA have been detected in many angiosperms (Daniel et al. 2004; Devos et al. 2002). Because previous studies suggested that deletions due to NHEJ and SSA occur in most genome regions in several angiosperms with small genomes (Devos et al. 2002; Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013; Vu et al. 2017), NHEJ and SSA are considered to be mechanisms that maintain the genome size, with antagonistic effects on the mechanisms mediating increases in genome size (Hawkins et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2021).

During SSA, if there are similar sequences comprising approximately 10 bases around the sequence of the broken end caused by DSB, the sequence will likely be repaired, resulting in a large deletion (≥ 500 bp) (Vu et al. 2017). The sequences between the homologous sequences are deleted at this time. Therefore, the TE may be converted into a single long-terminal repeat (LTR) or target site duplication (TSD) by combining the LTR or TSD-like sequences present at both ends of the TE (Vu et al. 2017). This is indistinguishable from the deletions caused by unequal crossover or NHEJ. However, if there are no repeat sequences in the vicinity or if fragmentary deletions exist between species, even if there are repeat sequences, then the deletion may be due to SSA (Devos et al. 2002).

Indels caused by unequal crossover

Unequal crossover occurs when chromosomal recombination does not take place at the same locus of homologous chromosomes (or sister chromatids) (Fig. 2b) (Freeling 2009). The presence of similar sequences near the locus and variations in chromosome length result in non-correspondence at the same locus of homologous chromosomes (Penuela et al. 2024). When unequal crossover occurs with similar sequences in the vicinity, chromosomal segments with similar sequences are serially inserted or deleted and chromosome length is biased. Frequent unequal crossovers that occur when many homologous sequences are clustered (Hanada et al. 2011; Lehti-Shiu et al. 2009) accelerate changes in genome size (Clark et al. 2007). The recombination events resulting from unequal crossovers may be traced by focusing on the copy number variations via an intraspecific population analysis or the discrepancies in the number of homologous sequences of a particular length surrounding the recombination site. Using this approach, Hanada et al. (2008) proposed that the cluster structure in angiosperms, wherein many duplicate genes related to secondary metabolites are serially arranged in the genome, is formed by an unequal crossover (Hanada et al. 2008). This unequal crossover, which substantially affects the inactivation of TEs and the fluctuation of the homologous sequences of duplicated genes, occurs frequently in plants in which repeat regions and TEs account for 70% of the genome. If there are homologous sequences, an unequal crossover can occur at both ends of the chromosome, which is conducive to large-scale chromosomal deletions.

Indels caused by replication slippage

Replication slippage refers to a replication error that occurs when a DNA polymerase misses part of the template strand and copies the complementary strand (Fig. 2c) (Levinson and Gutman 1987; Rache et al. 2023). In regions with repeat sequences (e.g., microsatellites), the repeat sequences form a hairpin structure when converted into single-stranded DNA (template strand). If an approaching DNA polymerase skips a hairpin structure and replication continues (Fig. 2c), during the second replication, the hairpin structures of the template strand and the non-template strand result in a deletion and insertion, respectively (Fig. 2c). Replication slippage involves the duplication or deletion of the surrounding region in proportion to the size of the repeat region that forms the hairpin structure. Although this process is mediated by a relatively small-scale mechanism, it contributes substantially to changes in genome size. Most famously, certain sequences, including cytosine, thymine, and guanine (CTG) triplet repeats, retain specific hairpin structures that continue to grow with each generation via this mechanism (Cardoso and Marques 2018; Guo et al. 2016b). CTG triplet repeats that continue to grow with each generation have been detected in A. thaliana, in which the genome size has changed because of replication slippage (Sureshkumar et al. 2009). However, the continuous elongation of these repeat sequences has severe deleterious effects (Cardoso and Marques 2018; Guo et al. 2016b; Sureshkumar et al. 2009) and variations are expected to be lost via purifying selection. Additionally, numerous TEs are inactivated following deletions induced by replication slippage (Devos et al. 2002), thereby serving as a mechanism that deletes repeat sequences and decreases the genome size. Variations in length around the repeat region among species or populations may reflect the presence of indels due to replication slippage. Repeat sequences that are targets of replication slippage, which are common in angiosperms with large genomes, were revealed recently on the basis of long-read sequencing (Galvez-Galvan et al. 2024; Hamim et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2023). Therefore, replication slippage plays crucial roles in both genome expansion and reduction.

TEs as the target region of molecular mechanisms

TEs are genomic regions influencing genome size variability (Yu et al. 2023). Moreover, TEs are DNA segments present in almost all eukaryotic genomes, wherein they create genome-wide indels via transposition (Hanada et al. 2009). RNA-type TEs are reverse transcribed after transcription, whereas DNA-type TEs are translocated and propagated genome-wide by the excision of single or double strands mediated by transposases. RNA-type TEs (retrotransposons) include LTR types with an LTR near the boundary, long-chain retrotransposons with long TSD sequences instead of an LTR, and short-chain retrotransposons that have sequences homologous to long-chain retrotransposons; all of these have characteristic sequences, which are a pair of boundary sequences recognized by transferases, with similar sequences at both ends. Therefore, replication slippage and SSA can partially or completely remove these sequences from the genome (Devos et al. 2002; Hawkins et al. 2009). The excision or partial removal of TEs may leave only the boundary sequence LTRs or TSD sequences, which are referred to as isolated LTRs or miniature inverted-repeat TEs (Bennetzen et al. 2005; Lu et al. 2012). Such excision, removal, transfer, and proliferation are not limited to cell division. Thus, indels may occur throughout the genome relatively quickly. Moreover, the indel size may range from 100 to 20,000 bp (Kidwell 2002). Among closely related species that diverged recently, some have a bias toward RNA-type TEs, whereas others have a bias toward DNA-type TEs (Piegu et al. 2006; Willhoeft et al. 2002). In a previous study comparing the genome structures of closely related species that differ in terms of genome size [Arachis duranensis (1.25 Gb) and Arachis ipaensis (1.56 Gb)] and diverged from each other approximately 2 million years ago, the deletion of LTRs in regions where large inversions occurred was revealed to contribute to the genome size reduction (Bertioli et al. 2016; Ren et al. 2018). Moreover, this tendency was also observed in other comparative analyses between Phaseolus vulgaris and Vigna radiata and between Glycine max and V. radiata (Ren et al. 2018). Therefore, indels induced by TE truncation or proliferation are significantly associated with the diversity in genome sizes.

Reduction in the centromeric region

Sequences in which the unique histone CENH3 is replaced with normal histones are called centromeric regions (McKinley and Cheeseman 2016). Eukaryotic chromosomes have centromeric regions that maintain the chromosomal structure (Ma et al. 2023). These regions do not have strictly conserved sequences. The CENH3 region serves as a scaffold for proteins to which spindle fibers attach during cell division. Therefore, large chromosomes require strong binding forces, which then expand the CENH3 region and ensure that chromosomes can be segregated (Cherry et al. 1989). In fact, the size of the centromeric region is roughly proportional to the chromosome size (Zhang and Dawe 2012), with variations in chromosome size and number expected to influence genome size because of the region required to maintain these chromosomes. Thus, the causal relationships between the karyotype, centromeric region size, and genome size in angiosperms should be investigated.

Zhang and Dawe (2012) used fluorescence in situ hybridization to show that the centromeric region size per cell was highly correlated with the genome size of 10 monocot species. They also investigated the relationship between chromosome size and number, but no strong correlation was detected (Zhang and Dawe 2012). Furthermore, they added maize chromosomes with a small centromeric region to oat plants with a large genome to investigate whether centromeric regions are affected by variations in genome size or whether genome size varies due to variations in the centromeric region. They observed that the centromeric region size increased similarly in maize and oat (Zhang and Dawe 2012). These results suggest that the size of the centromeric region changes depending on the genome size. Accordingly, in monocots, a decrease in the centromeric region size due to a decrease in the number of chromosomes is expected to have a limited effect on genome size.

Many species in the family Brassicaceae, which includes A. thaliana, have eight chromosomes (Hu et al. 2011). Arabidopsis lyrata, which is a close relative of A. thaliana, has eight chromosomes, but A. thaliana has only five (The Arabidopsis Genome Initiative 2000). The loss of three chromosomes occurred through the formation of acrocentric chromosomes via pericentric inversions, reciprocal translocation between two chromosomes, and the elimination of a minichromosome that emerged alongside the fusion chromosome (Lysak et al. 2006). According to recent research, the total centromeric region size differs between A. thaliana (13.0 Mb) and A. lyrata (25.4 Mb) (Wlodzimierz et al. 2023). The difference (12.4 Mb) represents 48% of the A. lyrata centromeric region. Considering the 80 Mb difference in the sizes of the genomes of A. lyrata (approximately 200 Mb) and A. thaliana (approximately 120 Mb) (Hu et al. 2011), the 48% reduction in the centromeric region was not significantly different from the approximately 40% (80 Mb/200 Mb) difference in genome size (P = 6.59 × 10− 1, Chi-squared test). Although the exact size of the centromeric regions in many species remains unknown, it may be determined on the basis of the total genome size in monocots as well as in eudicots.

Unlike in the aforementioned angiosperms, the DNA content in each chromosome is highly conserved in fern species (Clark et al. 2016; Pellicer et al. 2018). Therefore, there is a strong correlation between genome size and the number of chromosomes in ferns, and the centromeric region is considered to represent a large proportion of the genome in ferns (Leitch and Leitch 2012). Polyploidization events occur in both ferns and angiosperms, but many angiosperm chromosomes are fractionated immediately after polyploidization (Clark et al. 2016; Pellicer et al. 2018). This difference indicates that a diploid-like karyotype helps stabilize angiosperm genomes, which may represent a selection pressure for genome size reduction (Dodsworth et al. 2016; Leitch and Bennett 2004). Alternatively, whereas all of the ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms may be under similar selection pressures for genome size reduction, but only angiosperms are likely to have the molecular mechanisms required to decrease the genome size (Michael 2014).

Changes in the centromeric region are likely not the primary mechanism mediating decreases in the size of angiosperm genomes. The centromeric region contains many TE sequences, such as retrotransposons (Kumekawa et al. 2001), and varies considerably in terms of size. Improved sequencing technologies have revealed the substantial abundance of repetitive sequences in the centromeric regions of numerous species. Future investigations of the centromeric region sequences of many species will likely further elucidate the diversity in centromeric region sizes, especially in angiosperms. Such investigations may provide valuable insights into the potential relationships between centromeric region dynamics and decreases in genome size.

Genome characteristics of plants with extremely small genomes

Species that have experienced large-scale genome size reductions provide evidence of the associated effects of replication slippage, unequal crossover, NHEJ, and SSA. In the following sections, we summarize the characteristics of the genomes in U. gibba and A. thaliana, which have much smaller genomes than other angiosperms.

The compressed genome of U. gibba

U. gibba (77 Mb genome) is a carnivorous plant belonging to the family Lentibulariaceae, which comprises species that differ considerably in terms of genome size, including G. margaretae (63 Mb genome; smallest among angiosperms) and plants with a genome exceeding 1.5 Gb (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Whole-genome sequencing data suggest that U. gibba underwent three independent WGD events, in which its genome doubled in size, after diverging from the genus Mimulus, which is most closely related to the genus Utricularia (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Despite three WGD events, U. gibba has an extremely small genome. The factors related to this decrease in genome size were predicted on the basis of genome information and structures.

The regulatory regions surrounding genes in the U. gibba genome are highly compressed, with a high proportion of bidirectional regulatory regions and bidirectional transcription termination sites. These characteristics differ from those in angiosperms with large genomes (Dhadi et al. 2013; Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). A bidirectional regulatory region, which can simultaneously regulate the transcriptional states of the adjacent genes, is common among various organisms (Dhadi et al. 2009). Bidirectional regulatory regions can also regulate the transcription of pairs of entirely different genes that were not derived from gene duplications, suggesting that the regions emerged independently rather than through duplication events (Piontkivska et al. 2009). However, in another study, local duplication in the promoter region formed a bidirectional regulatory region (Nourmohammad and Lassig 2011). Because of this diversity in the formation of bidirectional regulatory regions, many pairs of adjacent genes are expected to be subject to this regulation. The fact that repeated sequences are common in the bidirectional regulatory regions and a palindromic structure is essential for transcriptional termination may be related to small-scale deletions, including those resulting from replication slippage and SSA (Dhadi et al. 2013). Under selection pressures for genome size reduction, bidirectional regulatory regions are expected to be advantageous and preserved to maintain a compact genome. This is supported by earlier studies that showed some bidirectional regulatory regions in plant and placental mammalian genomes are under strong selection pressures (He et al. 2010; Piontkivska et al. 2009).

The U. gibba genome contains large gene families related to the regulation of retrotransposons and P uptake (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). All 19 gene families that control retrotransposons are conserved in U. gibba, with 75 microRNAs reportedly transcribed from genes in these families (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Consistent with this finding, retrotransposons represent only 2.5% of the genome, which contains countless single LTRs and fragmented TEs (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Thus, retrotransposon regulation-related gene families may be important for decreasing the genome size via the inactivation or suppression of retrotransposons, with TEs likely truncated by NHEJ and SSA.

The gene family responsible for P absorption expanded in U. gibba (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Notably, U. gibba is highly sensitive to P deficiency, which induces insectivore root growth (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Therefore, the efficient absorption of P from prey by insects is advantageous for U. gibba, in which the SCO1-like gene family that controls P absorption expanded (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2023). Additionally, because P is a major component of DNA, an increase in genome size is disadvantageous in cases where there is a substantial demand for P for purposes other than DNA synthesis. Previous studies indicated that carnivorous plants (e.g., U. gibba), which tend to grow in regions with limited N and P sources, have small genomes (Veleba et al. 2020). High sensitivity to P deficiency may be a selection pressure for genome size reductions.

Although U. gibba underwent three WGD events, the intergenic regions and regulatory regions surrounding genes were significantly compressed, suggestive of large-scale deletions (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Furthermore, the number of genes was similar to that in diploid angiosperms and many deletions occurred, but not in some dosage-sensitive genes (i.e., eight copies were retained) (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Multiple WGD events have been detected in angiosperms with small genomes or eukaryotes in environments where DSBs are easily induced (Vu et al. 2015). Several abiotic stresses can induce DSBs (Dmitrieva et al. 2011; Morales et al. 2016). These studies suggest that WGD events may protect important genes from large-scale deletions (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Therefore, the genome size may decrease because of frequent DSBs via NHEJ and SSA.

Genome size reduction-related changes have also been detected in WGD syntenic regions that tend to be highly conserved regions (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). A comparative analysis of independent WGD events in U. gibba and tomato showed that the syntenic regions in tomato and U. gibba were 3 Mb and 130 kb, respectively (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). The ratio of the tomato and U. gibba genome sizes (approximately 20:1) is consistent with the size of the syntenic regions (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Therefore, conserved regions, such as syntenic regions, also decreased in size, implying that genomic regions affected by genome size reduction are dispersed. Moreover, in the preceding study, to compare the number of genes among various plants, the same gene prediction method was applied, and the number of genes was discussed (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). Despite three WGD events, the U. gibba genome contains only 28,494 genes, which is fewer than the 34,727 genes in the tomato genome (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). More than half of the paralogous genes derived from WGD in U. gibba were likely lost because of their redundancy (Ezoe et al. 2021, 2024; Takeda et al. 2023). However, 28,494 U. gibba genes were classified into 18,991 gene families, with no major loss of gene families. Among angiosperms, papaya has the fewest genes (i.e., 24,782), suggestive of a lack of active selection pressure for gene deletions, but the number of genes is lower than the expected number following three WGD events (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013; Ming et al. 2008).

The high sensitivity of U. gibba to P deficiency may be a selection pressure for genome size reductions; the regulation of retrotransposons may also be important. It is possible that the intergenic and promoter regions, paralogous genes, and retrotransposons decreased in size because of replication slippage or NHEJ and SSA following frequent DSBs.

Genome reduction in A. thaliana: insights from comparative genomics

A. thaliana is a model plant with a compact genome (125 Mb). Highly accurate and thorough genome sequence analyses have been conducted using this model plant (The Arabidopsis Genome Initiative 2000). A comparative analysis of the genomes of closely related species was also conducted, which revealed details regarding the decrease in the size of the A. thaliana genome. We introduce the findings of this earlier research and discuss the associated molecular mechanisms in the following paragraphs.

A. thaliana and A. lyrata diverged approximately 10 million years ago (de la Chaux et al. 2012). In contrast to the A. lyrata genome (> 200 Mb), the A. thaliana genome is much smaller (125 Mb). Three chromosomes and approximately 115 Mb of genomic sequences were deleted in A. thaliana over the course of 10 million years, but sequences equaling approximately 30 Mb were inserted during the same time period (de la Chaux et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2011). Of the deleted regions (115 Mb), 42%, 29%, and 10% were in TE, intergenic, and centromeric regions, respectively (Hu et al. 2011). The deleted or inactivated TEs were mainly in regions surrounding genes (Hu et al. 2011). Furthermore, the time period since the inactivation of TEs has been longer for A. thaliana than for A. lyrata (Hu et al. 2011). It has been suggested that A. thaliana may be affected by TE removal-related selection pressures (Hu et al. 2011).

In A. thaliana, TEs have been deleted, but many other specific regions have also been deleted. The number of deletions shorter than approximately five bases is similar between A. thaliana and A. lyrata, but longer deletions have been more prevalent in A. thaliana than in A. lyrata (Hu et al. 2011). During an earlier intraspecific population analysis of A. thaliana, polymorphic indels were revealed to be longer (on average) than insertions (Hu et al. 2011). Furthermore, the rank-order of polymorphisms in terms of frequency was as follows (lowest to highest): insertions, SNPs, and deletions. This may reflect the presence of a selection pressure that suppresses insertions and enhances deletions. In particular, this tendency has been suggested to be more pronounced for long deletions than for short deletions (Hu et al. 2011). Accordingly, it is highly likely that the A. thaliana genome continues to decrease in size because of relatively long deletions.

Selfing in A. thaliana also influences genome size reduction. A. lyrata is an outcrossing plant. Although A. thaliana was also an outcrossing plant for a while after divergence, it is estimated to have switched to a selfing system approximately 0.41 million years ago (de la Chaux et al. 2012). Since the transition to selfing, the number of new TE insertions has decreased, suggesting that the reduction in recombination due to selfing may influence TE insertion–deletion bias. However, similar reduction rates were observed in the genome compositions of A. lyrata and A. thaliana: intergenic regions decreased from 41.6 to 34.0%, whereas TEs decreased from 29.7 to 23.7% (Hu et al. 2011). Therefore, a significant decrease in the number of TEs in A. thaliana does not appear to be caused by the difference in reproductive systems.

In A. thaliana, deletion-related decreases in genome size occurred in an unbiased manner in the syntenic, TE, and intergenic regions (Hu et al. 2011). Devos et al. (2002) compared the molecular mechanisms that removed TEs from the A. thaliana and A. lyrata genomes; they detected more traces of TE removal via NHEJ and SSA than through unequal crossovers (Devos et al. 2002). Furthermore, a comparison between A. thaliana and two closely related species (A. lyrata and Capsella rubella) showed that deletions caused by NHEJ and SSA rather than replication slippage or unequal crossover were responsible for decreasing the number of repeat sequences in intragenic regions (Vu et al. 2017). This may be associated with an unbiased genome size reduction due to NHEJ and SSA.

NHEJ and SSA frequently mediate decreases in angiosperm genome size

Deletions caused by NHEJ and SSA have been suggested to contribute substantially to the decreases in the sizes of A. thaliana and U. gibba genomes. To clarify the relationship between deletions resulting from NHEJ and SSA and decreases in genome size induced by selection pressure, we introduce studies related to NHEJ and SSA.

Studies comparing the repaired sequences of A. thaliana and tobacco following the introduction of a restriction enzyme that induces DSBs showed that deletions due to NHEJ and SSA occurred relatively frequently and that the average deletion was 1.5-times longer in A. thaliana than in tobacco (Kirik et al. 2000). This was supported by another study that determined deletions due to NHEJ and SSA tended to be longer in A. thaliana than in barley (Vu et al. 2017). Interestingly, most deletions in tobacco and maize involve ectopic DNA insertions, whereas most deletions in A. thaliana are not associated with insertions (Kirik et al. 2000; Wessler et al. 1990). This suppression of insertions accompanying deletions in A. thaliana may reflect a bias toward indel mutations via NHEJ and SSA. This bias toward indel mutations, particularly deletions, acts in a synergistic manner with the selection pressure caused by external factors that accelerate decreases in genome size. Therefore, after the selection pressure for genome size reductions was enhanced following the divergence of A. thaliana from A. lyrata, the pathway that preferentially uses NHEJ and SSA may have undergone a functional diversification, after which it was fixed within the species (Oyama et al. 2008).

The biased use of HR, NHEJ, and SSA for DSB repair is mainly determined by specific proteins belonging to KU and RAD protein families (Chiruvella et al. 2013; Gao et al. 2014). In NHEJ, DSBs are recognized by KU proteins and their DNA ends are processed by nucleases and polymerases for the introduction of a DNA ligase 4 complex (Du et al. 2020b). In SSA, RAD proteins mediate annealing according to homologous sequences around the DSB (Du et al. 2020b; Mansour et al. 2008). However, mutant analyses did not show a complete transition to NHEJ or SSA, implying there may be an unknown mechanism that promotes NHEJ and SSA (Roth et al. 2012). For example, NHEJ is preferentially activated for immunoglobulin class-switch recombination in animals; proteins that promote NHEJ have been identified (Ghezraoui et al. 2018). The exploration of such pathways in angiosperms may be useful for characterizing the factors underlying the increase in the number of deletions in the A. thaliana genome.

Is the reduction in genome size advantageous for angiosperms?

The mechanisms for rapid genome size reductions are considered to be specific to angiosperms and either absent or less used in gymnosperms (Michael 2014). It is highly likely that a genome size reduction will occur only in angiosperms even if both angiosperms and gymnosperms are under the same selection pressure. We speculate that the environmental factors influencing the selection pressure for a genome size reduction include drought stress and N and P costs. In response to genome size reductions, cell size decreases because of a reduction in nuclear size, which facilitates efficient gas exchange for photosynthesis and enhances water use efficiency, while also decreasing the N and P costs related to nucleic acid synthesis (Guignard et al. 2016; Vesely et al. 2020). The following sections provide additional details regarding the findings of these earlier studies.

Efficient gas exchange for photosynthesis and enhanced water use efficiency

From the time Zygnema emerged on land up to the era of gymnosperms, increasing photosynthetic activities was difficult in dry inland areas, making the advancement into these regions challenging (Augusto et al. 2014; Orton et al. 2020), but angiosperms were able to grow in drought-affected areas (e.g., land-locked regions) because of improvements in their water use efficiency and photosynthetic efficiency (Augusto et al. 2014). Decreases in genome size, which reportedly increase drought tolerance through increases in water use efficiency and photosynthetic efficiency, may have contributed to the expansion of angiosperms into drought areas (Kafi et al. 2015; Vesely et al. 2020). Intriguingly, a decrease in genome size is correlated with a decrease in stomatal size, which leads to increased stomatal density (Beaulieu et al. 2008). This increase in stomatal density facilitates rapid changes to stomatal movement (Beaulieu et al. 2008; Lawson and Vialet-Chabrand 2019). Consequently, genome size reductions are believed to contribute to improved drought tolerance through efficient gas exchange for photosynthesis and increased water use efficiency (Lawson and Vialet-Chabrand 2019; Wang et al. 2021). In a previous study, a prostrate-leaved species was found to have larger stomata and generally larger genomes than its upright-leaved phylogenetic relative, whose stomata are exposed to the ambient arid climate more than the stomata of prostrate-leaved species (Vesely et al. 2020). The findings of this earlier study strongly support the universal trends in the relationship between drought tolerance and genome size in angiosperms. Furthermore, decreases in genome size have been detected not only in angiosperms, but also in the desert gymnosperm Welwitschia mirabilis (Wan et al. 2021). Another study revealed a correlation between genome size and guard cell length in Welwitschia (Rudall and Rice 2019). Therefore, drought stress may be a selection pressure on gymnosperms and angiosperms. However, W. mirabilis may be a counter example because stomatal structure and development in conifers, which are different lineages in gymnosperms, are not closely resemble those in W. mirabilis (Rudall and Rice 2019). In addition to drought stress, other climate change-related selection pressures on stomatal size may result in changes to genome size (Franks and Beerling 2009; Vesely et al. 2020; Vinogradov 2003). However, there are many exceptions to these findings. For example, Satyrium bicorne has much larger stomata than Paris japonica, but it has a smaller genome (2 C = 9 Gb) than P. japonica (2 C = approximately 300 Gb) (Vesely et al. 2020). Thus, genome size influences stomatal size, but may not be the main determinant. Improved drought tolerance due to decreases in genome size might be associated with the selection pressure for genome size reduction in angiosperms.

Limited P and N availability as potential selection pressures affecting decreases in genome size

Both P and N are key nutrients for organisms with large genomes because nucleic acids require more P and N than other cell components (Hessen et al. 2010). Specifically, 50–80% of the cellular P content is used for the production of nucleic acids (Hessen et al. 2010). Therefore, P and N availability is a selection pressure affecting plants. In fact, previous research showed that plants fertilized with free P and nitrate N have increased ploidy levels (Guignard et al. 2016; Šmarda et al. 2013). Another study indicated that the N cost is significantly lower for the A. thaliana genome than for animal genomes (Acquisti et al. 2009). Notably, the demand for P is higher in tetraploids (e.g., Arabidopsis species) than in diploids (Anneberg and Segraves 2020). Therefore, environments deficient in P and N may induce decreases in genome size. Furthermore, P and N are not directly available under natural conditions, with plants tending to be in a state of chronic N and P deficiency (Cohen et al. 2018; Janes et al. 2018). It is possible that plants are under selection pressures for genome size reductions, but this possibility has not been comprehensively investigated (Wang et al. 2021). Thus, we examined the relationship between P and N use rates and genome size. We used data related to the P and N concentrations in various tissues and the genome sizes of 106 plant species (Kerkhoff et al. 2006). Information regarding genome sizes was obtained from the Kew Botanical Garden Database (https://cvalues.science.kew.org/) and the NCBI Genome Information by Organism database. These 106 plant species were classified as having large or small genomes according to the median genome size (1.433 Gb). Our analysis of the collected data revealed that free P concentrations in reproductive tissues and stems differed between organisms with large and small genomes [Fig. 3; FDR = 4.33 × 10− 2 (P in reproductive tissues) and FDR = 4.60 × 10− 2 (P in stems); two-tailed Student’s t-test]. In reproductive tissues, DNA is actively synthesized during gametogenesis, with P deficiency leading to severe DNA damages and delayed DNA repair (Hernandez and Munne-Bosch 2015). Furthermore, among plant organs, the stem has the highest biomass of free P, suggesting that plants with mechanisms for storing free P efficiently might be able to cope with the substantial P demand associated with large genome sizes (Vymazal 2016). Therefore, plants growing in P-deficient areas may be under selection pressures that lead to decreases in genome size.

Genome size is significantly correlated with P concentrations in the stem and reproductive tissues. Accordingly, drought and P availability may serve as selection pressures leading to genome size reductions. Serpentine soils are characterized by drought conditions and severe deficiencies of P, making them inhospitable to most normal land plants (He et al. 2012). Among serpentinophytes, obligate serpentinophytes have much smaller genomes than facultative serpentinophytes (Pustahija et al. 2013). Although serpentine soil conditions are affected by more than drought and P availability, these earlier findings support the possibility that these factors influence the selection pressure related to genome size reductions. It should be noted that the reduction in genome size due to P availability is not a causal relationship. Future studies will need to analyze these relationships, with P limitation as an independent variable.

No correlation was found between N concentrations and genome size, possibly because of the existence of counterexamples such as N-fixing legumes. Generally, the N concentration in N-fixing legumes is close to the upper limit for plants (Adams et al. 2016). However, there are differences in genome sizes among closely related species. For example, Vicia faba ‘Merkur’ and Pisum sativum ‘Terno’ have large genomes (13.41 and 4.36 Gb, respectively), whereas Medicago truncatula and Cicer arietinum have small genomes (460 and 740 Mb, respectively) (Cui et al. 2022; Macas et al. 2015; Varshney et al. 2014). Although differences in N allocation strategies may be the cause of genome size variation, this is inconsistent with the fact that in obligate N-fixing plants, such as M. truncatula with very high N concentrations, there was a rapid decrease in the number of TEs, resulting in a decrease in genome size (Dovrat et al. 2020; Wang and Liu 2008). N-fixing legumes may be affected by the factors influencing genome size reductions that differ from those in other plants, as they escaped from the constraints of N availability.

A morphological characteristic that may help plants escape from the constraints of P availability is the cluster root (also known as a proteoid root). These roots are specialized for the uptake of P and are found in Fabaceae, Proteaceae, Leguminosae, Betulaceae, Myricaceae, Elaeagnaceae, Casuarinaceae, and Moraceae (Skene 2000). Considering the convergence in the appearance of cluster roots in many different lineages, investigating genome size differences between species with and without cluster roots may help determine the relationship between P availability and genome size. The genus Lupinus includes species with both cluster roots and N-fixing nodules (Pueyo et al. 2021). Thus, it may be worthwhile to focus on this genus when examining the effects of P and N concentrations on genome size. However, such research has not been conducted and should be addressed in future studies.

Mechanisms for a cycle of WGD and genome size reduction may be a driving force for acquiring functional diversity

Given that the aforementioned environmental factors affect almost all land plants to some extent, the ancestor of angiosperms likely gained efficient mechanisms for genome size reductions. However, other than environmental conditions, it remains unknown what other factors induced the establishment of these mechanisms. We propose that WGD and genome size reduction cycles provide long-term benefits for acquiring new functions, but it is unclear how the molecular mechanisms for these cycles are conserved by natural selection. Because many duplicated genes acquire new functions during pseudogenization, the recurrent cycles of WGD and genome size reduction are believed to increase the opportunities to acquire new functions (Reese and Williams 2019). For example, in U. gibba, three rounds of WGDs and the accompanying genome size reductions resulted in an increase in the number of conserved genes (compared with the corresponding number in its ancestral lineage) (Ibarra-Laclette et al. 2013). This suggests the duplicated genes acquired new functions under relaxed selection pressures and were conserved. However, previous research showed that plants with large genomes are relatively uncommon in extreme environments (Knight et al. 2005). Another study showed that plants exposed to extreme drought conditions tend to have more diverse traits than plants under other environmental conditions (Gross et al. 2024). These study findings highlight the importance of the cycle involving genome size increases and decreases for the acquisition of new functions (Gross et al. 2024; Knight et al. 2005). Furthermore, gymnosperms closely related to angiosperms, such as Welwitschia mirabilis and Ephedra sinica, have undergone WGD and genome size reduction events (Wan et al. 2021; Wing and Boucher 1998; Wu et al. 2021). This suggests that the mechanisms necessary for WGDs and genome size reductions were acquired in a recent ancestor close to angiosperms, which may be associated with the substantial diversification of angiosperms (i.e., Darwin’s abominable mystery) via the cycle facilitating the acquisition of new functions (Davies et al. 2004; MacKintosh and Ferrier 2017).

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

We herein provide an overview of the molecular mechanisms underlying genome size reductions, the genome characteristics of plants that have undergone genome size reduction-related events, and the associated ecological selection pressures (Fig. 4). We propose that among the external factors that influence genome size variations, P deficiency and drought stress are major selection pressures promoting deletions in intergenic regions, introns, TEs, and duplicate or repeat sequences (Fig. 4). These selection pressures may affect indel retention rates, while also contributing to the functional diversification of proteins related to NHEJ and SSA, with certain biases toward insertions or deletions. The skewed retention and mutation rates of deletions have synergistic enhancing effects on deletions, leading to rapid changes to genome size. Considering the fact rapid genome size reductions are frequent and occur in a lineage-specific manner, whether the proteins or pathways mediating rapid decreases in genome size are the same among diverse plant species should be investigated. Moreover, the timing of the acquisition of the mechanisms mediating genome size reductions during plant evolution remains to be determined. However, there is limited evidence of the conservation of the molecular mechanisms underlying indels. The recent development of chromosome-level reference genomes and the comparison of these genomes with sequence data stored in databases (e.g., NCBI) have helped to characterize large genome sequences in angiosperms, gymnosperms, and pteridophytes. Compiling the relevant data may provide additional insights into how genomes decrease in size and how the underlying mechanisms are conserved.
