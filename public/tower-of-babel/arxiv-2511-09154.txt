A General Theorem for Non-Simultaneous Hat Guessing Puzzles
Souji Shizuma

Abstract
The prisoners and hats puzzle, or simply the hat puzzle, is a family of games in which a group of prisoners are each assigned a colored hat and are asked to guess the color of their own hat. Various versions of the puzzle arise depending on the number of prisoners, the number of possible hat colors, and the information available to them before and after the game begins. These puzzles are broadly classified according to whether the prisoners’ declarations are made simultaneously or non-simultaneously. In this paper we present a general theorem concerning the existence of a winning strategy when the declarations are non-simultaneous. We also discuss the relationship between the construction of such strategies and the Axiom of Choice, as well as their connection to the simultaneous-declaration case.



1
Introduction

“Prisoners and hat puzzles” are famous puzzles
in which a jailer puts colored hats on prisoners
and each prisoner must guess the color of his own hat which he cannot see.
In this paper we simply call them “hat puzzles”.

There are many mathematical studies
on hat puzzles generalized to infinitely many prisoners,
mainly in axiomatic set theory.

A textbook on these infinite hat puzzles was published in 2013
[
5
]
.

First we consider hat puzzles
in which prisoners declare their guesses simultaneously.
For instance,
consider the following puzzle:

Puzzle 1.1

(2-person, 2-color, simultaneous
declaration)
.

A jailer places two prisoners in a room
and puts a hat on each prisoner.
Each hat is colored either black or white.
Neither prisoner can see the color of his own hat,
but each sees the other’s hat.
Furthermore,
after they are placed in the room,
they can have no communication at all.
Under these conditions,
both prisoners must simultaneously declare their guesses,
either “black” or “white”.
If at least one prisoner guesses correctly,
both prisoners win and are released;
otherwise,
both lose and are executed.
Naturally prisoners do not know how the jailer will place the hats.
However,
before they are brought into the room,
the jailer informs them of all the rules
and prisoners can confer a strategy in advance.
How can prisoners win this game?
∎

A winning strategy
(which ensures that at least one prisoner guesses correctly)
will be found in Theorem
3.3
.

Here we characterize a hat puzzle
by the following five parameters:

(E1)

The number of prisoners

(E2)

The number of colors

(E3)

How each prisoner can see other prisoners’ hats
(a visibility graph)

(E4)

How and when the prisoners declare thieir guesses

(E5)

The winning condition for prisoners

In Puzzle
1.1
,
these parameters are instantiated as follows:

(E1)

There are 2 prisoners.

(E2)

There are 2 colors.

(E3)

Each prisoner sees all hats other than their own
(i.e., each sees the other’s hat).

(E4)

The prisoners declare simultaneously.

(E5)

At least one prisoner must guess correctly.

By varying these parameters,
we obtain different hat games,
and the main question in hat puzzle
research is to determine whether a strategy exists
ensuring the given winning condition
for all possible hat assignments by the jailer.

By considering puzzles
in which prisoners declare
non-simultaneously,
we obtain a variety of formats.
Here is a relatively simple example:

Puzzle 1.2

(5-person, 2-color, non-simultaneous declaration, first type)
.

Modify Puzzle
1.1

by changing the following parameters:

(E1)

There are now 5 prisoners.

(E4)

One prisoner declares first,
and after hearing that declaration,
the other four prisoners declare simultaneously.

(E5)

At least 4 prisoners must be correct.

How can prisoners win this game?
∎

In this case the first prisoner can serve as a “hint giver”,
enabling the other four to guess their own hats correctly,
thereby achieving the winning condition.
A more concrete version of this puzzle
is found in Lemma
4.1
.

Another non-simultaneous declaration
puzzle is:

Puzzle 1.3

(5-person, 2-color, non-simultaneous
declaration, second type)
.

Modify Puzzle
1.2
as follows:

(E3)

The jailer numbers the prisoners

0
through
4
and arranges them in a single file.
Each prisoner sees only the hats of prisoners
with a larger number.

(E4)

The prisoners declare one by one
in ascending order of their numbers.

Again,
how can prisoners win this game?
∎

In Puzzle
1.3
,
each prisoner sees fewer hats than in Puzzle
1.2
,
but they hear more of the previous guesses.
For instance,
prisoner 2 can no longer see the hat of prisoner 1,
but can hear what prisoner 1 says.
If prisoner 1 is indeed correct,
prisoner 2 can work off that guess as a clue,
continuing in the same manner as Puzzle
1.2
.
A more concrete version of this solution
is found in Lemma
4.1
.

We can change (E4) in numerous ways according to the number of prisoners.
If the number of prisoners is infinite,
the variety of speaking orders is even larger.
For instance,
with countably many prisoners,
one can set up puzzles in which they declare
in several distinct groupings,
or speak in a single file,
or speak in blocks of size
2^{n}
repeated countably many times,
and so on.

In fact,
there is related research concerning countably many prisoners
in a non-simultaneous declaration puzzle
like Puzzle
1.2
or Puzzle
1.3
.
For example, Theorem 3.3.2 of
[
5
]

deals with
a version of Puzzle
1.2

extended to countably many prisoners,

[
1
]
studies
a countably infinite extension of the scenario
in Puzzle
1.3
,
and
[
6
]
takes an even more general approach
to such countably infinite puzzles,
including the case of three or more colors.

This paper presents
a general theorem about non-simultaneous puzzles
that covers more variety of declaration orders
than in Puzzle
1.2

or Puzzle
1.3
,
along with the extension to infinitely many prisoners.
We also investigate the connection with the
simultaneous declaration case,
similarly to what has been done in previous research,
while extending the number of prisoners and colors
in a general manner.

In Section
2

we formalize these puzzles
from a mathematical perspective, primarily employing set theory.
In Section
3

we review previous results
on simultaneous declaration
hat puzzles (as featured in
[
5
]
)
for both finite and infinite cases,
including discussions on the role of the Axiom of Choice
in the infinite case.
In Section
4

we focus on
non-simultaneous declaration puzzles
with a finite number of prisoners
and proves some general theorems.
In Section
5

we discuss
non-simultaneous declaration puzzles
with infinitely many prisoners
and establish general theorems in parallel to the simultaneous case;
here, again, the Axiom of Choice will come into play.
Finally,
In Section
6

we investigate
relationship between general theorems for the simultaneous
and non-simultaneous cases,
introducing an alternative statement about a different puzzle
that can serve in place of the Axiom of Choice.

2
Puzzle formalization

In this section, we introduce
formal tools necessary to discuss hat puzzles
using sets and functions,
a style primarily inspired by
[
5
]
.
We begin with some notation for sets and functions.

For a set
X
and an element
x\in X
,

x^{\neq}
abbreviates
“some element in
X
distinct from
x
”.
Let
|X|
denote the cardinality of
X
.
We use
\langle a,b\rangle
to denote the ordered pair of
a
and
b
.
In this paper,
directed graphs and functions
are expressed as sets of ordered pairs.
We regard a directed graph
G
on the set
X

as
G\subseteq X^{2}
.

We write
f\colon X\to Y
to mean
f
is a function from
X
to
Y
.
In set-theoretic notation,

f\colon X\to Y
means
f\subseteq X\times Y
and for each
x\in X
,
there is a unique
y\in Y
such that
\langle x,y\rangle\in f
.
If
\langle x,y\rangle\in f
,
we write
f(x)=y
.
The set of all functions from
X
to
Y

is denoted by
\mbox{}^{X}Y
.
For
f\colon X\to Y
,
x\in X
and an element
a
,
let
f\lfloor x|a\rfloor
be the function from
X
to
Y\cup\{a\}
obtained by
changing the value of
f(x)
to
a
.
Formally,

f\lfloor x|a\rfloor:=(f\setminus\{\langle x,f(x)\rangle\})\cup\{\langle x,a\rangle\}.

Likewise for
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
a,a^{\prime}
,
we write

f\lfloor x,x^{\prime}|a,a^{\prime}\rfloor:=(\;f\setminus\{\langle x,f(x)\rangle,\langle x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime})\rangle\}\;)\;\cup\;\{\langle x,a\rangle,\langle x^{\prime},a^{\prime}\rangle\}.

We say
g
is an extension of
f
when
f\subseteq g
,
that is,

\dom(f)\subseteq\dom(g)
and for all
x\in\dom(f)
,
f(x)=g(x)
.
Let
f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}X^{\prime}
denote the restriction of
f

to
X^{\prime}\subseteq\dom(f)

that is,

f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}X^{\prime}:=f\cap(X^{\prime}\times\ran(f))
.

Now we introduce formal notation for hat puzzles.
Let
A
be the set of prisoners and
K
the set of colors,
with
2\leq|A|,|K|
.
An element
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
is called a coloring,
representing an assignment of
hat colors to all prisoners.

A directed graph
V\subseteq A^{2}

without loops
(that is,

\langle a,a\rangle\notin V
for all
a\in A
)
is called a visibility graph on
A
,
where
\langle a,b\rangle\in V
indicates
that prisoner
a
sees prisoner
b
’s hat.
We write
a\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}b
for
\langle a,b\rangle\in V
.

V(a)
denotes the set of prisoners that
a
sees
(that is,

V(a)=\{b\mid\langle a,b\rangle\in V\}
).
Note that, if
two colorings
f,g
satisfy
f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)=g\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)
,
prisoner
a
cannot distinguish those two.

Let
A
be a set of prisoners,
and
V
a visibility graph on
A
.
Let
\alpha
be an ordinal and
I
be
a surjection from
A
onto
\{1,\dots,\alpha\}
.
We call
I
an inning function on
A
.
For
\beta\in\ran(I)
,
define
{A}^{[\beta]}:=\{a\in A\mid I(a)=\beta\}
.
Hence
{A}^{[1]}
is the set of prisoners
who declare in the initial inning,

{A}^{[\beta]}
is the set
of prisoners who declare in the
\beta
-th inning,
and so on.
Let
H(a):=\{b\mid I(b)<I(a)\}
.

H(a)
is the set of prisoners
whose declaration
a
can hear.
Let
IN=\max(\ran(I))
.
We also use notation like
{A}^{[\beta-\gamma]}

to abbreviate
{A}^{[\beta]}\cup\dots\cup{A}^{[\gamma]}
,
and write
{A}^{[\beta-]}
for
{A}^{[\beta-IN]}
.

We write
\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

for the hat game (or simply game) determined
by these four parameters.
If
IN=1
,
we write
\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
.
In the hat game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
,
for each
a\in A
and
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
define
v_{a}^{f}=f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)
as
prisoner
a
’s view,
that is,
the part of the coloring
f
which prisoner
a
sees.
When
IN=1
,
each prisoner can only base their guess on
v_{a}^{f}
,
hence
a strategy for prisoner
a
in such a game
is a function
\overline{\sigma_{a}}\colon\mbox{}^{V(a)}K\to K
.
In the puzzle,
each prisoner declares

\sigma_{a}(f)=\overline{\sigma_{a}}(v_{a}^{f})
.
If two colorings look the same to prisoner
a
,
that is,
f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)=g\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)
,
then
\sigma_{a}(f)=\sigma_{a}(g)
.

When
IN\geq 2
,
the strategy for prisoner
a\in{A}^{[m]}
(with
m\geq 2
)
may also depend on the declarations
heard from all prisoners in
H(a)
.
If
\{\sigma_{b}\}_{b\in H(a)}
are already defined,
and for each
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
we define

h_{a}^{f}=\{\langle b,\sigma_{b}(f)\rangle\mid b\in H(a)\}
,
then the strategy
\overline{\sigma_{a}}
is a function

\overline{\sigma_{a}}\colon\mbox{}^{H(a)}K\times\mbox{}^{V(a)}K\to K,

and we set

\sigma_{a}(f)=\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a}^{f},v_{a}^{f}).

If two colorings
f
and
g

share the same view from
a
’s perspective
(
f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)=g\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}V(a)
)
and the same declarations
from
H(a)
(
\sigma_{b}(f)=\sigma_{b}(g)
for all
b\in H(a)
),
then
\sigma_{a}(f)=\sigma_{a}(g)
.

Prisoner
a
guesses correctly
in coloring
f
if
\sigma_{a}(f)=f(a)
,
and guesses incorrectly otherwise.
A predictor
P

for
\mathcal{G}

is a function
P\colon\mbox{}^{A}K\to\mbox{}^{A}K

such that there exists a set of strategies

\{\sigma_{a}\}_{a\in A}
producing
P
by

P(f)=\{\langle a,\sigma_{a}(f)\rangle\mid a\in A\}.

We call
\sigma_{a}(f)
the guess that
P(f)

makes for prisoner
a
under coloring
f
.
If
P(f)(a)=f(a)
,
then
a
guesses correctly under
f
.
If
f,g
are colorings,
we let
\operatorname{Match}(f,g)=\{a\mid f(a)=g(a)\}

be the set of prisoners on which
f
and
g
match.
Hence
\operatorname{Match}(f,P(f))
is the set of prisoners
who guess correctly under
f
for predictor
P
.
Hence

A\setminus\operatorname{Match}(f,P(f))=\{a\mid P(f)(a)\neq f(a)\}.

For a hat game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
,
we define two families of predictors:

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq n):=\left\{P\,\middle|\,\forall f\in\mbox{}^{A}K,\ |\operatorname{Match}(f,P(f))|\geq n\right\},

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq n):=\left\{P\,\middle|\,\forall f\in\mbox{}^{A}K,\ |A\setminus\operatorname{Match}(f,P(f))|\leq n\right\}.

That is,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq n)

is the set of all predictors guaranteeing
that at least
n
prisoners guess correctly
for any assignment
f
,
while
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq n)
is the set guaranteeing
that at most
n
prisoners guess incorrectly for any assignment
f
.

Thus the five parameters
from the puzzle descriptions correspond formally to:

(E1)

The cardinality of
A
,

(E2)

The cardinality of
K
,

(E3)

The visibility graph,

(E4)

The inning function,

(E5)

The required property of a predictor
that ensures
winning condition for prisoners.

3
Previous research on simultaneous puzzles

We now review existing results regarding puzzles
in which prisoners declare
simultaneously,
focusing on the case where the number of prisoners is finite.
Since these results have been discussed in prior research,
there is nothing essentially new about the arguments below;
we simply restate them in our own form,
allowing comparison with the non-simultaneous case.

In simultaneous declaration puzzles,
we often aim to construct
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)
,
that is,
a predictor that guarantees at least one prisoner guesses correctly.
For the finite-color case,
if the number of prisoners is not less than the number of colors and every prisoner sees all the other hats (like Puzzle
1.1
),
then such a predictor exists (Theorem
3.3
).
If there are only 2 colors,
even with a sparser visibility graph,
we can still construct such a predictor (Theorem
3.4
).
However,
if there are infinitely many colors,
no predictor can guarantee
at least one correct guess
in every assignment (Theorem
3.5
).
We start with two lemmas used in the proofs of these statements.

First,
we observe a known result on the average number
of correct guesses
across all colorings in a predictor
for a simultaneous puzzle.
The proof is based on Lemma 2.2.1 of
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,K
be finite sets,
and consider a game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
.
For any predictor
P
,

\frac{\text{(total number of correct guesses over all colorings)}}{\text{(number of all colorings)}}\;=\;\frac{|A|}{|K|}.

∎

Fix any
a\in A
and consider
all partial colorings
f_{a}
on
A\setminus\{a\}
.
For each such
f_{a}
,
there are
|K|
ways to extend it to a coloring
in
\mbox{}^{A}K
by assigning a color to prisoner
a
.
Out of these
|K|
possible extensions,
exactly one will be correct for prisoner
a
under
P
,
namely the one where the color of
a

is
P(f_{a}\cup\{\langle a,\cdot\rangle\})(a)
.
Hence the number of colorings
in which
a
is correct
equals
|\mbox{}^{A\setminus\{a\}}K|=|K|^{|A|-1}
.
Summing over all
a\in A
,
the total number of correct guesses
across all prisoners and all colorings is

|A|\cdot|K|^{|A|-1},

while the total number of colorings is
|K|^{|A|}
.
Thus

\frac{\text{(total correct)}}{\text{(total colorings)}}\;=\;\frac{|A|\cdot|K|^{|A|-1}}{|K|^{|A|}}\;=\;\frac{|A|}{|K|},

establishing the claimed average.
∎

It immediately follows that
if
|K|
is larger than the finite number
|A|
,
no predictor can guarantee
at least one correct guess in every coloring.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
be a game where
A
is finite and
|A|<|K|
.
Then no predictor can guarantee that
at least one prisoner guess correctly.
In other words,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)=\emptyset
.
∎

We divide in two cases:

If
K
is finite:
Since
|A|<|K|
,
we have
\tfrac{|A|}{|K|}<1
,
so by Lemma
3.1
,
every predictor has an average of less than one correct guess per coloring,
making
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)=\emptyset
.

If
K
is infinite:
Suppose
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)\neq\emptyset
,
so some
P
guarantees at least one correct guess under every coloring.
Since
A
is finite,
let
n-1=|A|
.
Pick any
n
-element subset
K_{n}\subseteq K
.
Consider
C_{n}=\{f\colon A\to K_{n}\mid\ran(f)\subseteq K_{n}\}
,
i.e. all colorings from
A
into these
n
chosen colors.
Fix an arbitrary
k\in K_{n}
and define
P_{n}
as follows:

P_{n}(f)(a)=\begin{cases}P(f)(a)&\text{if }P(f)(a)\in K_{n},\\
k&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
P_{n}
is a predictor in the game
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\langle A,K_{n},V,1\rangle
,
and it still guarantees at least one correct guess under each
f\in C_{n}
.
By Lemma
3.1
,
every predictor in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
must have
an average of
|A|/|K_{n}|=(n-1)/n<1
correct guesses.
This contradicts
P_{n}
apparently guaranteeing
at least one prisoner correct in every coloring.

∎

Next,
we present the classical result that if
|A|=|K|
is finite
and the visibility graph is complete (each prisoner sees every other hat),
then there is a predictor ensuring at least one correct guess for every coloring.
The following proof is based on
[
5
]
(Theorem 2.2.2).

Theorem 3.3
.

In the game
\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
for a finite set
K
satisfying
|A|=|K|
,
the following statements are equivalent.

(a)

V
is a complete graph.

(b)

There is a predictor guaranteeing
at least one correct guess
in every coloring,
that is,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)\neq\emptyset
. ∎

Let
n=|A|=|K|
.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)

Label the prisoners
A=\{0,\dots,n-1\}

and identify
K

with the additive group

\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}=\{\overline{0},\dots,\overline{n-1}\}

Define prisoner
m
’s strategy for each
f_{m}\in\mbox{}^{V(m)}K
by

\overline{\sigma_{m}}(f_{m})=\overline{m}-\sum_{b\in\dom(f_{m})}f_{m}(b).

For each coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
let
P(f)
be given by
\sigma_{m}(f)
for each
m\in A
.
In any coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
if
\displaystyle\sum_{b\in A}f(b)=\overline{m}
,
then prisoner
m
guesses correctly.
In fact,
for
m
,

P(f)(m)=\sigma_{m}(f)=\overline{m}-\sum_{b\in\dom(f)}f(b).

Since the visibility is complete,

\dom(f)=V(m)=A\setminus\{m\}
,
so

\displaystyle P(f)(m)

\displaystyle=\overline{m}-\sum_{b\in A\setminus\{m\}}f(b)=\sum_{b\in A}f(b)-\sum_{b\in A\setminus\{m\}}f(b)=f(m).

Hence prisoner
m

guesses correctly
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)\neq\emptyset
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a)

For the contrapositive,
assume
V
is not complete,
that is,
there exist distinct
a,b
with
\langle a,b\rangle\notin V
.
Then prisoner
a
does not see
b
’s hat.
We show any predictor
P
fails to guarantee
at least one correct guess for all colorings,
that is,
we can find a coloring
where everyone guesses incorrectly.

Let
P
be arbitrary.
There must be a coloring
f_{a}\in\mbox{}^{A}K
where
a
is correct:
set
f_{a}=f\lfloor a|P(f)(a)\rfloor
for some coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
.
Now define
f=f_{a}\lfloor b|P(f_{a})(b)\rfloor
.
Observe that from
a
’s viewpoint,

f
and
f_{a}
differ only on
b
’s hat,
which
a
cannot see;
thus
\sigma_{a}(f)=\sigma_{a}(f_{a})
,
so
a
remains correct. Similarly,

b
is correct in
f
because
P(f)(b)=P(f_{a})(b)
.
So in
f
,
at least these two prisoners are correct.
Meanwhile from
\frac{|A|}{|K|}=1
and the argument in Lemma
3.1
,
there must also be a coloring where no one is correct.
Hence
P
is not in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)
,
and so no predictor can be in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)
. ∎

Next,
again from
[
5
]
(Theorem 2.2.2),
we have the well-known 2-color result.
Even if each prisoner does not see all the other hats,
there can still be a predictor guaranteeing at least one correct guess
in every coloring if the visibility graph is “cyclic.”
(Puzzle
1.1
is a special case with a complete graph.)

Theorem 3.4
.

For a game
\langle A,K,V,1\rangle

where
A
is finite and
|K|=2
,
the following are equivalent:

(a)

V
is cyclic.

(b)

There is a predictor guaranteeing
at least one correct guess
in every coloring,
that is,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)\neq\emptyset
. ∎

Since
|K|=2
,
identify
K
with
\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)

Let
a_{0}\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}a_{1}\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}\dots\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}a_{n-1}\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}a_{n}\raisebox{-1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\overrightarrow{V}$}}a_{0}
be a cycle in
V
.
Then we have
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\}\subseteq A
.

Define strategies for these
n
prisoners as follows:
For each
f_{i}\in\mbox{}^{V(a_{i})}K(0\leq i\leq n)
, let

\overline{\sigma_{a_{0}}}(f_{0})=f_{0}(a_{1}),\quad\overline{\sigma_{a_{i}}}(f_{i})=\overline{1}-f_{i}(a_{i+1})\ (1\leq i<n),\quad\overline{\sigma_{a_{n}}}(f_{n})=\overline{1}-f_{n}(a_{0}).

Then for any coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
at least one of
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}

guesses correctly.
In fact,
if everyone guessed incorrectly,
we would derive a contradiction
by summing all declarations
and comparing with the hat colors,
an argument similar to that in
[
5
]
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a)

We prove the contrapositive.
It suffices to show that,
for any predictor
P
, there exists a coloring in which all prisoners guess incorrectly.
Then
P\notin\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)
,
and hence
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)=\emptyset
.
Let
P
be any predictor for the game
\mathcal{G}
.

Define
B_{0}=\{a\in A\mid V(a)=\emptyset\}
and, for

,

B_{m}=\{a\in A\mid B_{m-1}\subseteq V(a)\}\setminus\bigcup_{i<m}B_{i}.

Since

is acyclic,
B_{0}\neq\emptyset
, and there exists

such that

A=\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq n}B_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad B_{i}\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }i.

Each prisoner in
B_{0}
sees no hat,
so
\overline{\sigma_{a}}
is constant function for every

.
For any

, define

f_{0}=\{\langle a,\overline{1}-P(f)(a)\rangle\mid a\in B_{0}\}.

In any extension of

, all prisoners in

guess incorrectly.
Inductively, for

Then, for every

guess incorrectly under

Thus the final coloring

makes every prisoner in

∎

Finally,

[

]

shows that

is finite and

no predictor can guarantee at least one prisoner

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
with finite

and infinite

,
no predictor achieves
at least one correct guess on every coloring.
In other words,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)=\emptyset
.
∎

This follows immediately from Corollary

∎

Hence in simultaneous puzzles with a finite number of prisoners,
whether there is a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{c}\geq 1)

depends on the number of colors:

•

2 colors: a predictor exists
under certain visibility conditions (cyclic graph),

•

3 or more finite colors: a predictor exists
if and only if the visibility graph is complete and
|A|=|K|
,

•

infinitely many colors: no predictor can guarantee
at least one correct guess in every coloring.

In the infinite prisoner case,
it is possible to guarantee
not just one but infinitely many correct guesses.
In particular,
under the Axiom of Choice,
there is a predictor with only finitely many prisoners
guess incorrectly,
known as a “finite error”predictor.
Let us introduce one more notion.

Definition 3.6
.

For a game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
,
a predictor
P
is called a finite error predictor
if every coloring has only finitely many prisoners
who guess incorrectly,
that is,
P(f)=^{*}f
for all colorings
f
.
Here
=^{*}
means they differ only on finitely many coordinates.
Denote the set of such predictors by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{finite error})
.
∎

According to
[
4
]
,
the existence of a finite error predictor
for an infinite number of prisoners
(with complete visibility)
was discovered in 2004 by two graduate students.
It is often cited
as the starting point of infinite hat puzzle research:

Theorem 3.7

(Gabay–O’Connor)
.

Consider the game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle

where
A
is infinite and
V
is complete.
Under the Axiom of Choice,
there is a finite error predictor.
That is,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{finite error})\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Consider the equivalence relation
=^{*}
on
\mbox{}^{A}K

given by
f=^{*}g
if and only if they differ only on finitely many prisoners.
Let
[f]
be the equivalence class of
f
and choose a selector
\varphi
on these classes using the Axiom of Choice,
that is,
\varphi([f])\in[f]
. Given
f=^{*}\varphi([f])
,
define

\overline{\sigma_{a}}(v_{a}^{f})=\varphi\bigl([f\lfloor a|k\rfloor]\bigr)(a)

for some fixed
k\in K
. Then let
\sigma_{a}(f)=\overline{\sigma_{a}}(v_{a}^{f})
.
If
V
is complete,

a
sees all hats except its own,
so
f\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}(A\setminus\{a\})
is the same as
v_{a}^{f}
.
One checks that for any
f
,
P(f)
(generated by
\sigma_{a}
)
differs from
f
only on finitely many coordinates,
thus achieving finite error.
∎

In non-simultaneous puzzles with infinitely many prisoners,
a similar result holds
under the Axiom of Choice,
namely the existence of a predictor with at most one error
if conditions allow for a “hint”.
We will see this in Corollary
5.4
.

4
Non-simultaneous puzzle with finite prisoners

Throughout this section, assume
A
is finite.

For a game
\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
with
IN\geq 2
,
define the following three conditions:

(S1)

Exactly one prisoner declares in the initial inning,
that is, there is a unique
s\in A

with
H(s)=\emptyset
, or
|{A}^{[1]}|=1
.

(S2)

Each prisoner either sees or hears
the hat (or declaration)
of every other prisoner,
that is,

\forall a\in A,\ V(a)\cup H(a)=A\setminus\{a\}
.

(S3)

No prisoner both sees and hears the same prisoner,
that is,

\forall a\in A,\ V(a)\cap H(a)=\emptyset
.

Many well-known hat puzzles satisfy some of these conditions.
Puzzle
1.2
satisfies (S1) and (S2).
Puzzle
1.3

satisfies all three (S1), (S2), and (S3).
In the (S1) scenario,
the first speaker’s role
is generally to provide a “hint”,
so that all others can guess correctly
and yield at most one total error.
This usually requires a group-theoretic structure
on the color set
K
,
that is,
that
K
be an abelian group,
so that the first prisoner can encode the other hats
as a single “sum”.
In fact,
in the simultaneous puzzle approach
for finitely many prisoners,
the difference in cardinalities
|A|
versus
|K|

is crucial,
whereas in the non-simultaneous approach
with a single “hint giver”,
the group structure
is often more important than the size of
K
.

We begin with a general theorem
for finite non-simultaneous puzzles
(which also solves Puzzle
1.2

and Puzzle
1.3
).
The proof shows how the first prisoner,
using the group operation,
can announce the sum of all the other prisoners’
hat values,
enabling them to guess their own color via subtraction.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(K,+)
be an abelian group.
Suppose
IN\geq 2
and the game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

satisfies (S1) and (S2).
Then there is a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)
;
in other words,
one can guarantee at most one prisoner guesses incorrectly.
∎

Since (S1) holds, write
{A}^{[1]}=\{s\}
.
For a coloring
f_{s}\in\mbox{}^{V(s)}k
, define

\overline{\sigma_{s}}(f_{s})=\sum_{b\in\dom(f_{s})}f_{s}(b).

For
a\in{A}^{[m+1]}
with
m\geq 2
,
and for two colorings
f_{a}\in\mbox{}^{V(a)}K,h_{a}\in\mbox{}^{H(a)}K
,
define

\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a},f_{a})\;=\;h_{a}(s)\;-\;\sum_{b\in(\dom(h_{a})\setminus\{s\})}h_{a}(b)\;-\;\sum_{b\in\dom(f_{a})\setminus\dom(h_{a})}f_{a}(b).

We will show that,
under the resulting predictor
P
, in any coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
prisoner
s
may err,
but all other prisoners guess correctly.

Fix any
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
.
We show that every
a\in{A}^{[2-]}
guesses correctly.
We use induction on
n=2,\dots,IN
.
Suppose all prisoners in
{A}^{[2]},\dots,{A}^{[n-1]}

guess correctly.
Take
a\in{A}^{[n]}
.
Then

\displaystyle P(f)(a)=\sigma_{a}(f)=\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a}^{f},v_{a}^{f})

\displaystyle=h_{a}^{f}(s)\;-\;\sum_{b\in\dom(h_{a}^{f})\setminus\{s\}}h_{a}^{f}(b)\;-\;\sum_{b\in\dom(v_{a}^{f})\setminus\dom(h_{a}^{f})}v_{a}^{f}(b).

(
\dagger
)

Note

h_{a}^{f}(s)=\sigma_{s}(f)=\sum_{b\in V(s)}f(b)

and since (S2) implies
V(s)=A\setminus\{s\}
,

\sigma_{s}(f)=\sum_{b\in A\setminus\{s\}}f(b)=\sum_{b\in{A}^{[2-]}}f(b).

By induction,
all prisoners in
{A}^{[2]}\cup\dots\cup{A}^{[n-1]}
guess correctly,

\sum_{b\in\dom(h_{a}^{f})\setminus\{s\}}h_{a}^{f}(b)=\sum_{b\in{A}^{[2-(n-1)]}}f(b).

Also from (S2),
we get

\dom(v_{a}^{f})\setminus\dom(h_{a}^{f})={A}^{[n-]}\setminus\{a\}.

Thus plugging
(\dagger 1)
–
(\dagger 3)
into
(\dagger)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle P(f)(a)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{b\in{A}^{[2-]}}f(b)\Bigr)-\Bigl(\sum_{b\in{A}^{[2-(n-1)]}}f(b)\Bigr)-\Bigl(\sum_{b\in{A}^{[n-]}\setminus\{a\}}f(b)\Bigr)=f(a).

hence
prisoner
a
guesses correctly.
Thus
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)
.
∎

Looking at the proof of Lemma
4.1
,
it may appear that,
in order to construct a predictor with at most one incorrect guess,
there must be exactly one prisoner who speaks first — that is,
one who plays the role of giving a hint and may guess incorrectly.
However,
when there are only two colors,
it can be shown that there may be two prisoners who speak first instead.
In that case,
we need to modify the visibility of the prisoners slightly.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
|K|=2
,
IN\geq 2
,
and the game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
satisfies:

(S4)

Every prisoner in
{A}^{[1]}

sees the hats of all other prisoners
(so
\forall a\in{A}^{[1]},\ V(a)=A\setminus\{a\}
).

(S5)

Exactly two prisoners declare in the initial inning,
that is,
|{A}^{[1]}|=2
.

(S6)

Every prisoner who declares
later sees the hats of all prisoners
in
{A}^{[1]}
and of those
who declare in the same inning or later
(
\forall n\in\{2,\dots,IN\}\forall a\in{A}^{[n]},\ {A}^{[1]}\cup{A}^{[n-]}\subseteq V(a)
).

Then there is a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)
.
In other words,
the prisoners can guarantee at most one guess is incorrect.
∎

Identify
K
with
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}
.
By (S5), let
{A}^{[1]}=\{s_{0},s_{1}\}
.
Define the strategies for the first two speakers as follows,
for a coloring
f_{i}\in\mbox{}^{V(s_{i})}K
,

\overline{\sigma_{s_{i}}}(f_{i})=(\ \sum_{a\in\dom(f_{i})\setminus\{s_{1-i}\}}f_{i}(a)\ )+\overline{i}-f_{i}(s_{1-i})\quad(i=0,1).

For each
n\geq 2
and
a\in{A}^{[n]}
,
and for two colorings
f_{a}\in\mbox{}^{V(a)}K,h_{a}\in\mbox{}^{H(a)}K
,
define

\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a},f_{a})=\sum_{s_{i}\in{A}^{[1]}\land h_{a}(s_{i})=f_{a}(s_{i})}\overline{i}-\sum_{b\in\dom(h_{a})\setminus{A}^{[1]}}h_{a}(b)-\sum_{b\in\dom(f_{a})\setminus{A}^{[1]}}f_{a}(b).

Consider any coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
.
By (S4), both
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
see all other hats, and their strategies are designed so that exactly one of them guesses correctly.
Indeed,

P(f)(s_{0})=(\ \sum_{a\in{A}^{[2-]}}f(a)\ )+\overline{0}-f(s_{1}),\quad P(f)(s_{1})=(\ \sum_{a\in{A}^{[2-]}}f(a)\ )+\overline{1}-f(s_{0}).

If
\sum_{a\in A}f(a)=\overline{0}
, then
s_{0}
guesses correctly and
s_{1}
guesses incorrectly, while if
\sum_{a\in A}f(a)=\overline{1}
, the roles are reversed.
Hence, exactly one prisoner in
{A}^{[1]}
is correct.

Moreover, when
s_{i}
is correct, we have
\sum_{a\in A}f(a)=\overline{i}
.
Using this fact, the strategy for each subsequent prisoner
a\in{A}^{[n]}
(
n\geq 2
) can be shown, by induction, to yield

.
Indeed, since each such
a
hears which of
s_{0}
or
s_{1}
was correct and sees all previous prisoners in
{A}^{[2-(n-1)]}
(by (S6)), substituting into

P(f)(a)=\sum_{s_{i}\in{A}^{[1]}\land h_{a}^{f}(s_{i})=f(s_{i})}\overline{i}-\sum_{b\in\dom(h_{a}^{f})\setminus{A}^{[1]}}h_{a}^{f}(b)-\sum_{b\in\dom(v_{a}^{f})\setminus{A}^{[1]}}v_{a}^{f}(b),

one obtains
P(f)(a)=f(a)
directly.

Therefore, for any coloring
f
, exactly one prisoner in
{A}^{[1]}
and all prisoners in
{A}^{[2-]}
guess correctly.
That is, the predictor
P
produces at most one error in every play of the game,
and hence
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

For the case of three or more finite colors,
the same approach as in Lemma

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

be a finite set with

\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

In contrast to the simultaneous case,
if
K
is infinite and

the puzzle does allow for a predictor with at most one error!
This differs from
the simultaneous scenario (Theorem

),
since the non-simultaneity lets

We do not even need an abelian group structure on

nor the full strength of (S2);
a weaker version (S4) plus having a single “hint”suffices:

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
K
is infinite,

IN\geq 2
,
and
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

Then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Because
A
is finite and
K

there is a bijection
\varphi:K\to\mbox{}^{A\setminus\{s\}}K
,
letting us “encode”
any function on

by a single color in
K

Label
{A}^{[1]}=\{s\}
.
For a coloring
f_{s}\in\mbox{}^{V(s)}K

Let

\overline{\sigma_{s}}(f_{s})=\varphi^{-1}(f_{s}).

For any
a\in{A}^{[2-]}
,
and for two colorings

,
define

\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a},f_{a})=\varphi\bigl(h_{a}(s)\bigr)(a).

Then for every coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
prisoner

might fail, but everyone else is correct.
This yields
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

,

If

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)

Let
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

guaranteeing at least one correct guess
(equivalently forbids guaranteeing at most
|A|-1
errors)
among finitely many prisoners
if
K
is infinite.
Hence (S1) must hold,
that is,
|{A}^{[1]}|=1
.
Then (S4) follows
from Proposition
4.6
(
1
)
whenever (S1), (S3),
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
hold.
∎

In these proofs,
(S3) was used to establish the reverse
implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(a).
It is still open whether Theorem
4.2
similarly admits such a converse
under some condition (Question
7.1
).

5
Non-simultaneous puzzle with infinite prisoners

In this section, assume
A
is infinite.

We extend the idea of Lemma
4.1
to show that,
if a suitable “infinite analog”of summation exists,
then we can ensure at most one prisoner guesses incorrectly.
Since conventional summation does not directly apply to infinitely many hats,
we replace it with a “parity function”:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
X
be a set,

(G,+)
an abelian group,
and consider
\phi\colon\mbox{}^{X}G\to G
.
We say
\phi
is an
\mbox{}^{X}G
-parity function if for all
f\in\mbox{}^{X}G
,

x\in X
,
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
,

\phi\bigl(f\lfloor x|g_{1}\rfloor\bigr)\;-\;\phi\bigl(f\lfloor x|g_{2}\rfloor\bigr)=g_{2}-g_{1}.

In other words,
changing one coordinate of
f
from
g_{1}
to
g_{2}

shifts the value of
\phi(f)
by
g_{2}-g_{1}
in a uniform way.
∎

This notion follows
[
1
]
and
[
6
]
,
though we employ a version generalized in
[
6
]
.
The key point is that if
\phi
exists,
one can replicate the same
“Hint + Subtraction” strategy from Lemma
4.1
without a well-defined infinite sum.
In fact:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
A
be infinite and
(K,+)
be an abelian group.
Suppose there is an
\mbox{}^{A}K
-parity function
\phi
.
Then in any game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
with
IN\geq 2
satisfying
(S1) and (S2),
there is a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)

ensuring at most one error in every coloring.
∎

Let
0
be the identity element of
(K,+)

and
\phi:\mbox{}^{A}K\to K

an
\mbox{}^{A}K
-parity function.
By (S1), let
{A}^{[1]}=\{s\}
.
Define for
f_{s}\in\mbox{}^{V(s)}K
,

\overline{\sigma_{s}}(f_{s})=\phi\bigl(f_{s}\cup\{\langle s,0\rangle\}\bigr),

For
a\in{A}^{[2-]}
,
and for two colorings
f_{a}\in\mbox{}^{V(a)}K,h_{a}\in\mbox{}^{H(a)}K
,

\overline{\sigma_{a}}(h_{a},f_{a})\;=\;\phi\bigl(\;h_{a}\lfloor s|0\rfloor\;\cup\;\{\langle a,0\rangle\}\cup f_{a}\mbox{$\upharpoonright$}(\dom(f_{a})\setminus\dom(h_{a}))\;\bigr)\;-\;h_{a}(s).

In any coloring
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
prisoner
s
might fail,
but everyone else guesses correctly,
yielding
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)
.
The argument is a direct extension of the finite case
from Lemma
4.1
,
using the property of
\phi
that
changing one coordinate shifts the output by a predictable amount.
∎

The remaining question is whether such a parity function exists.
If we assume the Axiom of Choice,
it is straightforward to build one.
The construction is basically the “pick a representative in each
=^{*}
-equivalence class” approach from
[
1
]
(Lemma 6).

Lemma 5.3
.

Under the Axiom of Choice,
for any infinite set
X
and abelian group
(G,+)
,
there is an
\mbox{}^{X}G
-parity function.
∎

Consider the equivalence relation
=^{*}
on
\mbox{}^{X}G
:

f=^{*}g
iff
f
and
g
differ only at finitely many points of
X
.
Using Choice, pick a selector
\varphi

so that
\varphi([f])\in[f]
.
Define
\phi(f)

by summing up
f(x)-\varphi([f])(x)

over those
x
where
f(x)\neq\varphi([f])(x)
(only finitely many).
One verifies
\phi
is a parity function by checking that
altering one coordinate changes
\phi(f)

by exactly the difference in the group values.
∎

Hence whenever
(K,+)
is (or can be made into) an abelian group,
and
A
is infinite,
we can construct a single-error predictor for any game
with one “hint-giver”who sees or hears all others.
Actually,
by the well-known fact that under Choice,
one can define a group structure on any set
K

[
3
]
Corollary (f),
plus the existence of a parity function (Lemma
5.3
),
Theorem
5.2
immediately yields:

Corollary 5.4
.

Assume the Axiom of Choice. Let
A
be infinite,

K
any set,
and consider a game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

with
IN\geq 2
satisfying (S1) and (S2).
Then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
,
that is, there is a predictor
with at most one error in every coloring.
∎

6
Two infinite prisoner puzzles

Both in simultaneous and non-simultaneous puzzles with infinite prisoners,
the best-known results for guaranteeing
only finitely many errors use the Axiom of Choice.
Specifically,
Theorem
3.7
yields a finite error predictor
for the complete-visibility,
simultaneous case.
Corollary
5.4
yields a single-error predictor
for the non-simultaneous case under conditions
that allow a “hint”speaker.
Both proofs use Choice in two places:
to endow
K
with an abelian group structure
(if it isn’t yet)
and to construct a parity function.
In fact,
we can replace the second usage of Choice by a certain property
of a predictor from the simultaneous puzzle.
The relevant property is “robustness”,
meaning the predictor’s output is insensitive to changes
on finitely many prisoners’ hats:

Definition 6.1
.

For
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
,
say a predictor
P
is robust if
f=^{*}g
implies
P(f)=P(g)
.
That is,
finite changes to
f
do not alter the collective guesses
P(f)
.
Denote the set of robust predictors by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{robust})
.
∎

Note that the finite error predictor
which we constructed in the proof of Theorem
3.7
is, in fact, robust.

Corollary 6.2

(Gabay–O’Connor)
.

Under the Axiom of Choice,
for game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
with infinite
A
and complete
V
,
there is a finite error predictor that is also robust,
that is, in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{finite error})\cap\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{robust})
.
∎

Furthermore,
a robust finite error predictor yields a parity function:

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose
A
is infinite and
(K,+)
is an abelian group.
If there is a robust finite error predictor
in
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle
,
then there is an
\mbox{}^{A}K
-parity function
∎

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{finite error})\cap\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\text{robust})
.
For
f\in\mbox{}^{A}K
,
define

\phi(f)=\sum_{a\colon f(a)\neq P(f)(a)}\bigl[P(f)(a)-f(a)\bigr].

By the robustness and finite error property,
changing one prisoner’s hat color changes
P(f)
consistently,
letting us prove the parity condition
in exactly the same manner as Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Combining these facts,
we can prove, without Choice,
that
if the simultaneous game on
(A,K)
admits a robust finite error predictor,
then the non-simultaneous version with (S1) and (S2)
also admits a single-error predictor.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
A
be infinite and
(K,+)
an abelian group.
If the simultaneous game
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\langle A,K,V,1\rangle

has a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\text{finite error})\cap\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\text{robust})
,
then for the non-simultaneous game

\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle
with
IN\geq 2
satisfying (S1) and (S2),
we can construct a predictor in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)
.
∎

7
Questions

This paper has focused on hat puzzles
in which prisoners do not declare simultaneously.

For the finite case,
we saw that the existence of a “best possible”
predictor (either always at least one correct or at most one error)
once again depends on the number of colors relative
to the number of prisoners,
mirroring the simultaneous scenario.

When there are two colors and the puzzle is simultaneous,
it is known (for example, Theorem
3.4
)
that having a cyclic visibility graph is equivalent to guaranteeing
at least one correct guess.
For the non-simultaneous puzzle with two colors,
Theorem
4.2
established the
“(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)”direction,
but the converse “(b)
\Rightarrow
(a)”
remains open:

Question 7.1

(Continuation of Theorem
4.2
)
.

Consider a game
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

where
|K|=2
,

IN\geq 2
.

(a)

\mathcal{G}
satisfies (S4), (S5), and (S6).

(b)

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.

We have shown (a)
\Rightarrow
(b).
Does (b)
\Rightarrow
(a)  hold?
If not,
what additional conditions on the game would yield
the implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(a)?
∎

For the other finite cases
(3 or more colors,
or infinitely many colors),
we obtained statements that are essentially equivalent
to the existence of a predictor with at most one error,
provided (S3) also holds.
Specifically,
Theorems
4.3
and
4.7

handle the finite
|K|\geq 3
case;
Theorems
4.4
and
4.8

handle the infinite color case.
However, in these proofs,
(S3) was assumed to demonstrate the converse direction.
We ask whether (S3) can be eliminated:

Question 7.2

(Continuation of Theorem
4.7

and Theorem
4.8
)
.

In Theorems
4.7
and
4.8
,
is (S3) essential for proving (b)
\Rightarrow
(a)?
Or can we remove (S3) and still prove the same equivalences?
∎

Finally,
for a non-simultaneous puzzle with infinite prisoners,
we relied on the existence of a parity function for the color group
to guarantee that all but possibly one prisoner guess correctly.
It is still open whether the existence of such a parity function is also necessary,
or whether one might need an analogue of (S3) or similar constraints.

Question 7.3

(Analogue of Theorem
4.7
in the infinite setting)
.

Let
A
be infinite and
(K,+)
an abelian group. We know that:

(a)

There is an
\mbox{}^{A}K
-parity function.

(b)

If
\mathcal{G}=\langle A,K,V,I\rangle

with
IN\geq 2

(S1) and (S2),
then
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{e}\leq 1)\neq\emptyset
.

We have shown (a)
\Rightarrow
(b).
Is (b)
\Rightarrow
(a)  true?
Perhaps an additional condition akin to (S3) is required,
or some other structural assumption?
∎

In the special case
A=\omega
and

,
Lemma 5 of
[

]
shows (a) and (b) are equivalent.
We suspect that for more general
