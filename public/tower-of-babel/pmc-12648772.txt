Socio-cultural factors shaping complementary feeding initiation: a contextual analysis of maternal education, employment, and family support
Authors: Kaan Celebier, Metin Yigit, Furkan Kalayci, Enes Kaan Kilic, Orkun Tolunay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplementary feeding represents a critical milestone in infant nutrition, where appropriate timing and informed decision-making are of great importance. Maternal factors such as education level, socioeconomic status, cultural values, and access to information sources play a significant role in shaping complementary feeding practices.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with 504 mothers at the Pediatric Outpatient Clinic of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between February and April 2025. Data were collected through a structured questionnaire that included mothers’ sociodemographic characteristics, complementary feeding practices, and sources of information. Statistical analyses were performed to examine the associations between maternal characteristics and feeding behaviors.ResultsA total of 40.2% of the mothers reported initiating complementary feeding at the 5th month, while 39.0% started at the 6th month. Complementary feeding behaviors were significantly influenced by various sociodemographic factors such as maternal education level, employment status, and family support (p < 0.001). Mothers with lower levels of education and income were more likely to start earlier, whereas employed mothers tended to follow the recommended guidelines more closely. Differences in the timing of complementary feeding were also observed between mothers with and without family support.ConclusionsMaternal sociodemographic characteristics, cultural factors, and perceptions of workload significantly influence complementary feeding behaviors. Developing public health interventions that are sensitive to the sociocultural context—particularly for at-risk groups—is essential to promote appropriate infant feeding practices.Keywords: Complementary feeding, Motherhood, Infant nutrition, Sociodemographic factors, Health education, Formula use

Introduction

The complementary feeding period refers to the gradual introduction of solid foods into an infant’s diet following a period of exclusive breastfeeding or formula feeding [1]. Initiating complementary feeding in a timely and appropriate manner is crucial for meeting the increasing nutritional demands during the first year of life [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) identify complementary feeding, together with breastfeeding, as one of the most fundamental practices for promoting child health during the first two years of life [3].

Recommendations regarding the initiation of complementary feeding are largely consistent across the world [1]. However, research indicates that many parents do not fully comply with international guidelines on complementary feeding. Various parental factors—such as educational attainment, socioeconomic status, age, cultural background, and ethnicity—have been associated with notable differences in complementary feeding habits and practices [1]. Furthermore, prior studies have reported significant disparities in complementary feeding practices between mothers of exclusively breastfed infants and those who feed their infants with formula [4, 5].

Since 1991, the “Baby-Friendly Health Facilities Program” has been implemented by the Ministry of Health in Türkiye. This program aims to promote breastfeeding in accordance with current guidelines and to support the timely initiation of appropriate complementary foods starting from the sixth month of life [6]. As part of the program, both implementing and training healthcare professionals receive targeted education. Pediatricians, general practitioners, nurses, and midwives working in healthcare institutions are trained to provide mothers with guidance on breastfeeding and complementary feeding during routine check-ups and consultations. These support services are entirely free of charge and accessible to all mothers. Additionally, the program includes the distribution of written and digital educational materials, such as brochures and online resources.

The early introduction of solid foods before the recommended age has been associated with a range of health problems that may manifest later in life [7, 8]. Proper adherence to complementary feeding guidelines has been reported to have the potential to prevent up to 6% of deaths among children under the age of five, particularly in developing countries [9]. In this context, identifying and understanding the factors that adversely affect complementary feeding practices is of critical importance. However, given the breadth and complexity of these factors, it remains challenging to clearly determine their direct impact on feeding behaviors [10].

Materials and methods

Study design and populations

The study was conducted with 504 voluntary participants at the Pediatric Department of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital. The sample included only mothers of children aged 2 to 5 years without any chronic health conditions. Participants were selected from mothers who visited the pediatric outpatient clinic between February and April 2025, and eligibility was confirmed through the hospital’s records system by verifying each child’s age and health status. During routine check-ups or outpatient visits, researchers informed eligible mothers about the study and obtained written informed consent. The questionnaire was administered via face-to-face interviews. Mothers were excluded if they declined participation, had language barriers preventing comprehension, had children with chronic illnesses, or if their children were outside the specified age range. For mothers with more than one child, only one eligible child was included in the study, and responses were limited to experiences related to that specific child. No incentives were provided for participation.

Sample size calculation

In this study, the sample size was determined to assess complementary feeding practices and the factors affecting them, using a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 50% assumed prevalence — the most conservative estimate. Assuming an infinite population, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 384. To compensate for potential data loss or incomplete responses, a larger number of participants was targeted. As a result, 14 questionnaires with missing data were excluded, and 490 valid responses were included in the final analysis.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of 18 items, including 8 questions assessing the sociodemographic characteristics of participants and their families—such as maternal age, education level, employment status, and income. The remaining 10 questions focused on breastfeeding duration, complementary feeding practices, and related maternal behaviors. No reverse-coded or misleading questions were included. Since the items did not form a thematically homogeneous scale, internal consistency analysis (Cronbach’s alpha) was not conducted. Questions related to complementary feeding behaviors were evaluated based on the time elapsed since birth and the child’s chronological age. The questionnaire comprised both yes/no and multiple-choice questions. A pilot study was conducted with 20 mothers not included in the main study to evaluate clarity and feasibility. Based on the feedback, some questions were linguistically simplified, and instructions were made more understandable. The final version of the questionnaire was developed accordingly.

Statistical analysis and ethics

Statistical analyses of the study were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of numerical data distribution within the study group was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric numerical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-parametric data are expressed as median (Interquartile Range, IQR). Categorical variables are presented as percentages (%). The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. For numerical variables, the Independent Samples t-test was applied when parametric assumptions were met; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In this study, four different dependent variables—Formula Use, Bottle Use, Pacifier Use, and Receiving Support from Healthcare Professionals During the Introduction of Complementary Feeding—were analyzed using separate logistic regression models. In all models, the independent variables included Maternal Employment Status, Maternal Education Level, Monthly Household Income Level, Receiving Support from Extended Family in Childcare, and Presence of Chronic Illness in the Mother. These variables were first evaluated by univariable logistic regression analysis, and those with p < 0.20 were entered into the multivariable models. The “Backward Stepwise (Likelihood Ratio)” method was applied, and the results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Türkiye and the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participating mothers

The median age of the 490 mothers who completed the questionnaire was 33.00 years (IQR: 31.00–37.00), while the median age of the children assessed was 3.5 years (IQR: 2.5–4.5). Among the participants, 299 (61.0%) were employed, and 341 (69.6%) had attained a university degree or higher. Regarding monthly household income, 75 mothers (15.3%) reported insufficient income, 240 (50.0%) reported that income and expenses were balanced, and 141 (36.0%) reported an income exceeding their expenses. A total of 92 mothers (18.8%) stated that they had a chronic illness requiring regular monitoring and treatment. Only 116 mothers (23.7%) reported continuing breastfeeding for 24 months or longer. Additionally, 287 mothers (58.6%) reported receiving regular support from extended family members in child care.

Complementary feeding behaviors and practices

An analysis of the complementary feeding behaviors of the participating mothers revealed that 197 (40.2%) initiated complementary feeding at 5 months, while 191 (39.0%) began at 6 months. Yogurt was the most commonly introduced initial food, preferred by 283 mothers (57.8%). A total of 96 mothers (19.6%) reported that they began offering water to their children before the fifth month. Additionally, 314 mothers (64.1%) stated that they received informational support during the complementary feeding period, with 179 (36.5%) obtaining this support from healthcare professionals. Furthermore, 303 mothers (61.8%) reported using infant formula in their child’s diet, and 320 (65.3%) indicated that they fed their infants using a bottle.

Relationship between maternal employment status and complementary feeding behaviors

Employed mothers predominantly initiated complementary feeding at 6 months, whereas unemployed mothers were more likely to begin at or before 5 months (p < 0.001). At the start of complementary feeding, yogurt was the most commonly introduced food in both groups; however, employed mothers more frequently introduced vegetables thereafter, while unemployed mothers tended to prefer eggs as the initial food (p < 0.001). Employed mothers reported receiving more informational support during the complementary feeding period compared with unemployed mothers (p < 0.001). In terms of information sources, employed mothers primarily relied on healthcare professionals, whereas unemployed mothers were more inclined to consult family elders and peers (p < 0.001). Regarding water introduction, a notable proportion of unemployed mothers reported starting before the 5th month, while most employed mothers began after the 5th month (p < 0.001). No statistically significant association was found between maternal employment status and the use of bottles or pacifiers (p > 0.05 for both).

Relationship between maternal education level and complementary feeding behaviors

An analysis of maternal education level revealed that mothers with only primary school education predominantly began complementary feeding before the 5th month, while those with a university degree tended to initiate it after the 6th month (p < 0.001). Yogurt emerged as the most commonly selected first complementary food across all educational groups (p < 0.001). High school graduates showed a notable preference for yogurt as the initial food, followed by vegetables (p < 0.001, each). In contrast, primary school graduates more frequently chose eggs as the second option, whereas mothers with higher education levels favored vegetables (p < 0.001). Mothers with only primary school education received significantly less guidance from healthcare professionals during the complementary feeding period compared to other groups (p < 0.001). Furthermore, these mothers, along with high school graduates, were more likely to introduce water before the 6th month, unlike university-educated mothers who typically delayed water introduction until after the 6th month (p < 0.001). Bottle-feeding was most prevalent among primary school educated mothers (p < 0.001), while pacifier use was comparatively higher in both primary and high school education groups (p = 0.028).

Relationship between household income level and complementary feeding behaviors

Mothers from lower-income households received less guidance from health professionals during the complementary feeding process compared to those with higher incomes (p < 0.001). However, income level was not significantly associated with pacifier or bottle use, the timing of complementary feeding initiation, or the introduction of water (p > 0.05 for each). Yogurt was the most frequently selected first complementary food across all income groups (p < 0.001).

Factors influencing formula use

Maternal employment status and caregiving support from extended family were not significantly associated with either the use or timing of formula feeding (p > 0.05 for each). In contrast, formula use was more common among mothers with only a primary school education (p < 0.001), while those with a university degree were more likely to initiate formula feeding before the third month (p < 0.023). Similarly, lower-income mothers reported higher rates of formula use compared to their higher-income counterparts (p = 0.016), although no significant difference was observed in the timing of initiation across income groups (p > 0.05). A history of postpartum hospitalization for the mother or infant was linked to both earlier initiation and increased frequency of formula use (p < 0.001 for each). Additionally, mothers with chronic illnesses were more likely to begin formula feeding earlier (p < 0.001).

Mothers who used formula tended to initiate complementary feeding at an earlier age compared to those who did not (p = 0.001). The majority of formula-using mothers introduced complementary foods by the fifth month, whereas most non-formula users began after the fifth or by the sixth month.

The influence of support from extended family members on complementary feeding practices

Mothers without caregiving support from extended family members were more likely to seek information from healthcare professionals during the complementary feeding process than those who received such support (p < 0.001). Additionally, a higher proportion of unsupported mothers initiated complementary feeding before six months of age (p = 0.010). No significant associations were found between extended family support and the timing of water introduction, or the use of bottles and pacifiers (p > 0.05 for each).

The impact of maternal chronic illness on complementary feeding practices

Maternal chronic illness was not significantly associated with the timing of complementary feeding initiation, receiving informational support, consulting healthcare professionals, the first complementary food choice, or the timing of initial water introduction (p > 0.05 for each). However, mothers with chronic illnesses were more likely to use formula (p = 0.022). Pacifier use was also more common in this group (p = 0.016), whereas bottle use did not differ significantly between mothers with and without chronic conditions (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Although complementary feeding is a universal practice, maternal behaviors related to this process vary considerably depending on several factors. Previous studies have shown that these practices are influenced by maternal age and education level, socioeconomic status, the influence of friends and relatives, traditional beliefs, and the absence of professional guidance [10, 11]. For example, studies in the United States and Ireland reported that approximately 20% of mothers initiate complementary feeding before the fourth month [12, 13]. In Australia, a national survey found the median age for introducing complementary feeding to be 5 months [14]. Likewise, much of the available data from Europe indicates that complementary feeding commonly begins before the sixth month of life [15]. A multi-country study across six European nations revealed that 1% to 18% of parents introduced solids before their infants reached four months of age [16]. A similar trend has been observed in Indonesia and China, whereas in India, complementary feeding tends to begin significantly later than WHO recommendations [17]. The choice of initial solid foods also varies by country, shaped largely by cultural practices: fruits are typically preferred in Italy, vegetables in Sweden, and rice or cereal-based products in China, India, the United Kingdom, and Indonesia [17–20]. These variations likely reflect the diverse sociocultural and economic contexts of each population.

The WHO recommends introducing solid foods alongside breast milk or infant formula at around six months of age, upon recognition of developmental readiness cues in the infant [21]. Despite these recommendations, the actual timing of complementary feeding initiation in routine care is largely shaped by individual maternal and familial characteristics [1]. Families with lower educational attainment have been found to introduce complementary foods earlier than recommended [22–24]. Conversely, studies involving mothers with higher education levels have demonstrated greater adherence to WHO guidelines regarding the timing of complementary feeding [25, 26]. In line with the existing literature, the present study also identified a significant association between maternal education level and the timing of complementary feeding initiation.

However, our study revealed statistically significant differences in the timing of complementary feeding initiation between employed and non-employed mothers. In the Jiaxing Birth Cohort study, the impact of maternal employment on complementary feeding practices was assessed, showing that mothers working as agricultural laborers in rural areas tended to initiate complementary feeding earlier [27]. Differences between our findings and those reported in the literature may be attributed to variations in the demographic composition and other sociodemographic characteristics of employed mothers. Moreover, our study found that working mothers were more likely to receive informational support from healthcare professionals during the complementary feeding period compared to their non-working counterparts. This finding underscores the influential role of professional healthcare guidance in shaping feeding behaviors. However, an important concern raised by our results is the limited access to such support among non-employed mothers. This highlights the need for targeted communication strategies to ensure that all mothers—especially those outside the workforce—receive adequate information during this critical window for child health.

In our study, mothers who did not receive caregiving support from extended family members initiated complementary feeding earlier than those who did. This finding indicates that the timing of introducing complementary foods may be influenced by cultural and ethnic factors. In a study conducted by Yu et al. in China, even highly educated mothers were found to begin complementary feeding earlier, likely due to increased workload and responsibilities [28]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that, in addition to informational support from healthcare professionals, caregiving assistance from family members who share similar cultural values contributes to the adoption of optimal infant feeding practices [29]. Similarly, a study from Australia emphasized that traditional lifestyles and family support positively influence infant nutrition behaviors. Conversely, low adherence to recommended feeding practices in some families has been associated not only with low sociocultural status but also with conflicting advice from elder relatives and community leaders [30]. Although caregiving support from extended family members is expected to alleviate the maternal burden and positively impact complementary feeding practices, our study did not assess the quality or nature of this support, which limits our ability to draw firm conclusions on the matter.

One of the factors that has been associated with the timing of complementary feeding initiation is the use of formula in infant nutrition. Studies have shown that mothers who use formula exclusively or alongside breastfeeding are more likely to introduce complementary foods earlier than those who exclusively breastfeed [31–33]. Our findings are consistent with the existing literature. In this context, identifying the factors influencing formula feeding habits may offer valuable insights into the underlying determinants of complementary feeding practices.

Formula feeding habits are influenced by a range of complex factors. The literature suggests that lower socioeconomic status and lower maternal education levels are associated with higher rates of formula use [34, 35]. Recent studies emphasize that these behaviors are shaped not directly by employment status, but rather by insufficient maternity leave policies [36–38]. In recent years, improvements in working conditions for employed mothers, both in our country and globally, may have contributed to changes in this pattern, despite not being ideal. However, in order to propose more targeted interventions for the population in our study and for working mothers elsewhere, more detailed data on employment conditions are needed. Additionally, the perception that the baby remains hungry after breastfeeding often leads to concerns about inadequate milk production, which is a key factor associated with increased formula use [39, 40]. Postnatal hospitalization of the mother and/or infant, or the presence of maternal chronic illness, may delay mother-infant bonding. This delay may result in reduced milk production and thus increase the likelihood of formula feeding [41, 42]. In this context, our study findings align with the literature, indicating that formula feeding practices are influenced by similar factors.

Although our findings indicate that lower maternal education was associated with higher overall rates of formula use, mothers with higher education levels were more likely to initiate formula feeding at an earlier stage. This trend may reflect the increased professional and personal demands that typically accompany higher levels of education.

One of the limitations of this study is that it is based on self-reported data collected via a questionnaire. The research was conducted with mothers of children aged 2 to 5 years, and information regarding complementary feeding was obtained retrospectively. This introduces the risk of recall bias, which is a common concern in similarly designed studies, and its impact cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, although the questionnaire included items addressing the factors influencing complementary feeding practices, these factors’ subcomponents were not examined in depth in this study. Although children with a history of chronic illness were excluded from the study, whether they had been hospitalized for acute reasons during the transition to complementary feeding was not investigated. This may constitute a limitation, as it could lead to delays in the initiation of complementary feeding. Nevertheless, the large sample size may mitigate the potential impact of this factor on the study findings. Additionally, only mothers were included in the sample, and the potential influence of fathers on complementary feeding practices was not investigated. This constitutes an additional limitation, as it restricts the ability to assess the contributions of other family members. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a major center in Ankara, the capital of Türkiye. Therefore, the sociodemographic characteristics of the sample may not fully represent the general population. Moreover, because access to education and healthcare in Ankara is easier than in rural areas, the characteristics of mothers presenting to outpatient clinics may have limited representativeness for the broader population.

The characteristics of mothers, who serve as the primary caregivers for their infants, play a crucial role in shaping complementary feeding practices. Although current guidelines outline ideal complementary feeding behaviors, there continues to be a need for educational and empowering interventions that support mothers in adopting these practices. A clear identification of the factors influencing complementary feeding behaviors is essential for developing targeted policies and programs. Mothers at greater risk of engaging in suboptimal practices could benefit from enhanced social support systems that assist them in making informed decisions. The findings of our study emphasize the critical nature of the complementary feeding period and highlight the urgent need for public health interventions tailored to existing sociocultural and economic contexts to improve parental behaviors. Future research will be essential for evaluating the effectiveness of such interventions and further examining the impact of parental characteristics on this process.
