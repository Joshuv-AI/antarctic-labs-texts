Efficient dissociation of ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers via deep eutectic solvent pretreatment for sustainable composite reinforcement
Authors: Ziliang Dai, Lili Zhang, Juan Yu, Enqing Zhu, Rui Wang, Jiao Meng, Muhammad Wajid Ullah, Khalid Ali Khan, Wen He, Yimin Fan, Zhiguo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustainable structural fibers are highly desirable because many conventional high-performance fibers are neither renewable nor biodegradable. Bamboo original fibers, which possess a natural hollow vascular bundle architecture, represent a promising biomass-derived reinforcement but are difficult to isolate without damaging their structure. Here, we develop a mild “top-down” strategy to efficiently dissociate intact bamboo original fibers (BpOFs) from pretreated natural bamboo using a deep eutectic solvent (ChCl/OA-DES) treatment at 110 °C for 1 h. The treatment selectively weakens parenchyma cell cohesion (elastic modulus reduced from 6.24 to 3.14 GPa) while preserving fiber cell stiffness (15.04 to 15.92 GPa), enabling rapid separation of vascular bundles through subsequent CH₃COOH/H₂O₂ delignification and simple roller compaction. Controlled hydrogen-bond reconstruction during drying enhances mechanical performance, yielding fibers with an apparent tensile strength of 1947 ± 217 MPa while retaining the native hollow structure. Analysis of the load-bearing cell wall further reveals an intrinsic cell-wall stress of 2636 ± 197 MPa, indicating highly efficient stress transfer within the cellulose framework. The preserved tubular architecture provides low density, flexibility, and favorable interfacial interactions with polymer matrices. When incorporated into epoxy resin, BpOFs enhance bending strength and impact resistance, demonstrating their potential as lightweight, renewable reinforcement for high-performance composites.

Introduction

High-strength, high-aspect-ratio fibers are widely used in composite materials across various industries, including construction1, sports2, and transportation3. There is a growing demand for fast production and superior properties in fiber materials. However, most traditional fibers are synthetic and derived from petrochemical resources, making them non-biodegradable and contributing to significant, irreversible environmental pollution4. As a result, natural fibers are increasingly favored as a sustainable, biodegradable alternative to synthetic fibers. Previous studies have produced cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) with excellent properties using physical or chemical methods. Cellulose fibers have been fabricated via “bottom-up” approaches, including dry spinning5, wet spinning6, electrostatic spinning7, wet extrusion8, and hydrodynamic orientation9. For example, Zhou et al.10 prepared cellulose fibers with a tensile strength of 641 MPa and a tensile strain of 30% through wet spinning cellulose nanofiber suspension. Mittal et al.11 obtained cellulose fibers by fluid-assisted unidirectional alignment of CNF, achieving a tensile strength of 1.57 GPa due to enhanced crosslinking and high structural order. However, these “bottom-up” methods are limited by their complexity, which results in significant time and energy consumption. Consequently, the “top-down” approach, which involves directly decomposing biomass feedstock to the required scale through physical or chemical processes, is viewed as a simpler and more economical method for producing cellulose fibers12.

As the most abundant natural source of cellulose on Earth, wood is widely used in structural materials13, nano paper14, supercapacitors15, sensors16 and other fields. Jia et al.17 prepared dense, aligned wood textile fibers with a tensile strength of only 106.5 MPa by removing microchannels in natural wood through chemical delignification, cutting, and twisting processes. However, wood fibers are not an inherent feature of wood itself, and the slow growth rate of wood, combined with a long acquisition cycle, limits its use. In contrast, grass and hemp possess natural fibrous structures. Li et al.18 used a mixed Na2SO4 and NaOH solution for one-step degumming of gladiolus grass, producing cellulose fibers with a tensile strength of 0.9 GPa, which was utilized in a humidity-responsive smart actuator. Guo et al.19 obtained kenaf fibers with a tensile strength of 503.02 MPa through alkali and hydrogen peroxide treatment. However, the tensile strength of grass and hemp fibers remains relatively low, and their limited availability hinders widespread use. Bamboo, with its rapid growth rate, high cellulose content, excellent mechanical properties, and natural fibrous structure, has emerged as an ideal alternative to wood, grass, and hemp20. Known as “natural glass fiber”, bamboo fibers have a unique vascular bundle structure with a natural hollow core. The dissociation of bamboo fibers from the plant can overcome the challenges associated with conventional methods—such as coaxial wet spinning21, electrostatic spinning22, and interfacial polyelectrolyte complexation23, which often involve cumbersome processes, high energy consumption, and low yields. In addition, the large surface area and hollow structure of bamboo fibers24 make them well-suited for further composite applications.

Bamboo fibers are traditionally dissociated using either physical or chemical methods. Physical methods such as steam blasting25, carding26, and cracking27 typically result in fibers with poor tensile strength and performance stability. For example, Zhao et al.25 obtained bamboo fibers via steam blasting with a tensile strength of only 531.79 MPa. The low mechanical properties of these fibers are attributed to the high external mechanical forces applied during processing, which damage the fiber structure. In contrast, Wu et al.28 achieved a tensile strength of 395.45 MPa by cutting and twisting bamboo fibers after delignification using NaClO2/CH3COOH. More recently, Li et al.29 obtained bamboo fibers with a tensile strength of 1.90 GPa by treating them with peroxyformic acid for 12 h. During the drying process, hydrogen bonds formed between cellulose molecules, leading to self-densification of the fibers. These results clearly demonstrate that chemically processed bamboo fibers exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to those produced by physical methods. However, current chemical methods still face challenges, such as long processing times, the need for large quantities of reagents, and difficulty in preserving the natural integrity of the hollow vascular bundle structure. In natural bamboo, the small size of cellular structures—such as sieve cells and fibers—combined with their radially non-uniform arrangement and the complex multi-layered nature of the fiber cell walls, results in low overall porosity and poor permeability to chemical agents. In addition, the tight connections and heterogeneity between parenchyma cells and fiber cells make efficient dissociation of bamboo fibers challenging30. Therefore, pretreatment of natural bamboo is necessary to weaken the bonding between parenchyma cells and vascular bundles, loosen the dense internal structure, enhance the accessibility of the bamboo, and reduce the amount of subsequent chemical reagents required. Deep eutectic solvents (DES) offer excellent penetration of plant cell walls and high lignin solubilization compared to traditional pretreatment methods, such as hydrothermal31, acid32, and alkali33 treatments. Currently, DES is widely used in the clean separation of lignocellulosic components and the preparation of CNFs. However, there are still relatively few reports on the use of DES for the dissociation of bamboo fibers.

In this study, we present a “top-down” method for dissociating ultra-high-strength bamboo fibers. Mild ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment was used to weaken the bond between parenchyma cells and vascular bundles, reducing both the delignification time and the amount of CH3COOH/H2O2 required, while preserving the natural hollow structure of the bamboo fibers. The mechanism underlying the efficient dissociation and strengthening of these ultra-high-strength materials was also explored. This “top-down” approach offers mild processing conditions, a short processing time, high dissociation efficiency, and strong bamboo fibers, making it highly promising for applications in composite material reinforcement and other fields.

Results

Efficient dissociation of ultra-high-strength bamboo fibers

Na-Bamboo has a hollow fibrous bundle structure, which is tightly surrounded by parenchyma cells. However, the cell walls of the parenchyma cells are thin, leading to a relatively loose arrangement that allows for easier penetration and treatment by chemical agents compared to fiber cells34. Based on this difference, this study proposes a strategy for rapidly dissociating ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers through wetting, swelling, and subsequent delignification, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, bamboo was pretreated with ChCl/OA-DES to weaken the bond between fiber cells and parenchyma cells, remove part of the lignin and hemicellulose, and preserve the integrity of the vascular bundle structure35. This treatment enhances porosity and accessibility, facilitating better penetration and lignin removal during the subsequent treatment with CH3COOH/H2O2 solvent. It also reduces the amount of CH3COOH/H2O2 required and shortens treatment time. In addition, during the drying process, inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding is reorganized, increasing the bonding energy. The tightly aligned nanocellulose structure further enhances the mechanical properties of the bamboo original fiber. This approach minimizes the mechanical force needed for dissociation while effectively isolating ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers (BpOFs) with their natural vascular bundle structure intact.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the dissociation process for ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers (BpOFs).First, natural bamboo (Na-Bamboo) is pretreated with DES (ChCl/OA) to partially remove lignin and hemicellulose, weakening cell-to-cell bonding. Effects of DES pretreatment on the morphology, structure, and composition of bamboo cell walls

The effects of pretreatment time and temperature on DES pretreatment efficiency were systematically investigated. Insufficient pretreatment, such as a short duration (15 min, Supplementary Fig. 1a) or a low temperature (90 °C, Supplementary Fig. 2a), resulted in poor delignification, with parenchyma cell thickness remaining at 5.52 μm or 5.81 μm. Extending the treatment to 1 h or 110 °C (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2b) reduced the thickness to 1.75 μm and effectively opened the pore structure. However, excessive pretreatment (2 h, Supplementary Fig. 1c; 130 °C, Supplementary Fig. 2c) caused over-expansion of parenchyma cell gaps (7.42–7.78 μm), leading to severe structural damage. And as shown in Supplementary Figs. 1d, 2d, the removal rates of lignin and hemicellulose increased progressively with longer pretreatment time and higher temperature. Furthermore, comparative pretreatment studies using different DES systems highlighted the superiority of ChCl/OA-DES (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Compared to the neutral ChCl/EG-DES (Supplementary Fig. 3b) and alkaline ChCl/Urea-DES (Supplementary Fig. 3c), which only reduced parenchyma cell thickness to 5.75 and 5.35 μm, respectively, and exhibited relatively low delignification efficiency, the acidic ChCl/OA-DES achieved a significantly stronger selective removal of lignin and hemicellulose (Supplementary Fig. 3d) and more effectively opened the bamboo pore structure. These results confirm that ChCl/OA-DES provides the most effective and structurally favorable pretreatment pathway among the tested DES systems in this study.

The micromorphology of Na-Bamboo (Fig. 2a–d) and Pr-Bamboo (Fig. 2e–h) was compared. The cross-section view of Na-Bamboo parenchyma cells revealed regular morphology (Fig. 2b) and tightly structured cell corners (Fig. 2c). This compact structure limits solvent penetration. To further investigate the impact of DES pretreatment on bamboo fibers, SEM imaging was used to examine the radial section of bamboo before and after pretreatment. In Na-Bamboo, bamboo fibers are tightly surrounded by parenchyma cells, and the interface between the two is unclear (Fig. 2d), making fiber dissociation difficult. In contrast, after ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment, the parenchyma cells of Pr-Bamboo showed significant shrinkage (Fig. 2f) and the cell corners were notably opened (Fig. 2g). The thickness of the parenchyma cells decreased by 69.78%, from 5.79 μm to 1.75 μm, indicating loosening of the cells. This change increases the porosity between the parenchyma cells, facilitating enhanced solvent infiltration. And, after pretreatment, the bamboo displayed a clear and distinct vascular bundle structure with a smooth surface and no visible cracks (Fig. 2h). This suggests that the parenchyma cells on the surface of the vascular bundle were effectively removed, preserving the integrity of the vascular bundle, which is important for maintaining the properties of the bamboo fibers. Raman spectra confirmed the presence of lignin characteristic peaks at 1186 cm−1, 1284 cm−1, 1618 cm−1, 1680 cm−1, 2910 cm−1, and 2957 cm−137, but their intensities were notably reduced (Fig. 2i), indicating partial lignin removal. Raman imaging, which visualizes changes in cell wall components37, was used to monitor the lignin distribution before and after pretreatment. Lignin was effectively removed from the cell walls and cell corners of the parenchyma cells following pretreatment (Fig. 2j), enhancing the structural integrity of bamboo and facilitating efficient dissociation and stabilization of the bamboo fibers. The mechanism of action of ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment on lignin was clarified by performing two-dimensional heteronuclear single quantum coherence nuclear magnetic resonance (2D-HSQC-NMR) on the lignin extracted from the DES pretreatment solution. The spectra showed the disappearance of characteristic signals associated with β-O-4 linkages (71–72/4.7–4.8 ppm)38, directly confirming that ChCl/OA-DES cleaves these dominant covalent bonds, thereby promoting the depolymerization and dissolution of lignin. Moreover, the loss of proton signals in the aliphatic region (Supplementary Fig. 4a) highlights the destructive effect of DES on key aliphatic linkages, while the changes observed in the aromatic region (Supplementary Fig. 4b) reflect the progressive dissolution of G- and S-type aromatic units. Together, these results suggest a sequential process in which DES first disrupts the lignin backbone by breaking β-O-4 linkages and then facilitates the solvation and hydrogen-bond-assisted removal of aromatic structural units.

FT-IR spectra were also used to monitor the chemical changes in bamboo before and after pretreatment. The FT-IR spectra before and after pretreatment were similar (Fig. 2k), indicating that the structure of bamboo was preserved during DES pretreatment. However, the characteristic peaks at 1602 cm−1, 1515 cm−1, 1464 cm−1, 1425 cm−1, 1329 cm−1, 1243 cm−1, and 835 cm−1—associated with C-O, C-H, and benzene ring vibrations in lignin—were significantly weakened39, confirming the removal of some lignin from the bamboo. This was further supported by changes in component content, with a 30.96% reduction in lignin (from 26.16% to 22.87%, Formula. S4) and a 23.34% reduction in hemicellulose (from 16.38% to 15.90%, Formula. S5) following DES pretreatment (Supplementary Fig. 5). XRD analysis was used to examine the effect of DES pretreatment on bamboo crystallinity. Diffraction peaks at 2θ = 16.8°, 22.8°, and 34.8° (Fig. 2l), corresponding to the (110), (200), and (004) crystal planes of cellulose, remained unchanged, indicating that the cellulose I crystal was unaffected by pretreatment. The crystallinity of bamboo increased from 55.1% to 63.5% as a result of the removal of amorphous hemicellulose and lignin. This process not only reduced the amorphous background but also exposed additional cellulose hydroxyl groups, thereby enhancing the hydrophilicity of the cell wall and promoting solvent accessibility. The increased availability of hydroxyl groups facilitated the reconstruction of hydrogen-bonding networks among cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs). During drying, this hydrogen-bond-driven reconstruction further promoted the alignment of CNFs. The solvent penetration ability of bamboo was further investigated by monitoring its mass change before and after pretreatment during water immersion. After 8 h in water, the water absorption weight gain ratio of Na-Bamboo was 136.68 ± 3.1%, due to its dense structure and hydrophobic lignin encapsulation. In contrast, the water absorption weight gain ratio of Pr-Bamboo was 160.48 ± 4.6%, due to increased porosity and hydrophilicity (Fig. 2m). In conclusion, DES pretreatment effectively removed a portion of hydrophobic lignin and hemicellulose, while opening the pore structure of bamboo. This treatment does not excessively damage the vascular bundle, which is essential for the efficient dissociation of ultra-high-strength bamboo fibers with a natural hollow structure.

Mechanism of DES pretreatment in reducing interface binding between parenchyma cells and bamboo fibers

As shown in Fig. 3a, ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment can effectively open the parenchyma cell structure and maintain the integrity of the vascular bundle structure. Further investigation into the effects of ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment on the penetration and swelling of bamboo parenchyma cells was conducted by analyzing the distribution of nitrogen (N) in the cells using SEM-EDX. Since choline chloride contains nitrogen, the detection of N provides direct evidence of DES distribution. Supplementary Fig. 6a shows that ChCl/OA-DES treatment opened the parenchyma cells, creating a porous cell wall structure that allowed for deeper DES penetration. The nitrogen mapping (Supplementary Fig. 6b) indicates an even distribution of N in the cells and cell corners, indicating that DES fully penetrated the bamboo structure through intercellular spaces, thereby effectively facilitating lignin/hemicellulose removal40. SEM images of the interface between vascular bundles and parenchyma cells before and after DES pretreatment were also observed. Figure 3b shows the tight connection between the vascular bundles and parenchyma cells in Na-Bamboo, which complicates fiber dissociation. After DES pretreatment, Fig. 3c shows visible cracks at the interface, primarily due to the partial dissolution of lignin and hemicellulose within the parenchyma cells and the removal of lignin from the intercellular layer41. This weakened the chemical bond between parenchyma cells and vascular bundles, thereby facilitating efficient bamboo fiber dissociation. Due to its dense structure, NiSO4 solution has relatively weak penetration ability in Na-Bamboo (Fig. 3d). However, after ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment, the distribution of Ni element was observed in both the cross-sectional and longitudinal sections of Pr-Bamboo (Fig. 3e), indicating the complete penetration of the solution. This enhanced penetration is attributed to the effect of ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment on the pore structure of Na-Bamboo. The number of mesoporous pores (10-50 nm) decreased, while the number of macropores (0.1-1 μm and 5–100 μm) increased substantially (Fig. 3f). The porosity increased from 53.03% to 61.44% (Supplementary Fig. 7), and the specific surface area rose from 15.58 cm2/g to 28.54 cm2/g (Supplementary Fig. 8). These changes are attributed to the removal of non-structural substances and the opening of the internal pore structure of bamboo by DES pretreatment42. In addition, DES pretreatment significantly improved the pore structure of bamboo, thereby accelerating delignification, reducing reagent consumption, and enhancing fiber dissociation efficiency. Native bamboo, with its dense structure and high content of lignin and hemicellulose, typically required ~ 4 h of delignification to achieve satisfactory results. In contrast, Pr-Bamboo exhibited a significantly more open pore structure with increased macropores, allowing delignification to be completed within only 2.5 h (Fig. 3g). This ~ 37.5% reduction in delignification time is due to the enlarged pore structure and enhanced solvent accessibility induced by DES pretreatment, which enables delignification agents to more efficiently access lignin-rich regions. Although DES pretreatment introduces an additional step, it ultimately shortens delignification duration, reduces chemical consumption, and minimizes fiber damage during subsequent processing. This DES pretreatment causes the parenchyma cells to shrink and become more easily detached from the vascular bundle surface. Nanomechanical imaging analysis of the parenchyma cells and vascular bundles revealed that the cell hardness remained largely unchanged after pretreatment (Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting that DES pretreatment did not compromise the structural integrity of the bamboo. This preservation of the structure of bamboo is beneficial for maintaining the stability of bamboo fibers after delignification. Both the vascular bundle and parenchyma cells of Na-Bamboo exhibited high elastic modulus (Fig. 3h), with the vascular bundle modulus remaining similar after pretreatment (Fig. 3i), increasing from 15.04 GPa to 15.92 GPa (Fig. 3j). This indicates that the vascular bundle structure remained intact, preserving the natural strength of the bamboo fibers. In contrast, the elastic modulus of the parenchyma cell region significantly decreased after pretreatment (Fig. 3i), from 6.24 GPa to 3.14 GPa (Fig. 3k). This reduction may be attributed to the removal of lignin, which acts as a filler material in the parenchyma cells, thereby weakening the bond between parenchyma cells and vascular bundles and facilitating the removal of parenchyma cells during fiber dissociation43. Moreover, with the removal of part of lignin, the pore structure is opened up, effectively shortening the delignification time and avoiding damage to the bamboo original fibers caused by long-term chemical treatment. Efficient dissociation and mechanical properties of ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers

The dense structure of Na-Bamboo can hinder the dissociation of bamboo fibers; however, DES pretreatment weakens the bonding between fiber cells and parenchyma cells. This study explores the preparation of ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers. Na-Bamboo was subjected to DES pretreatment to obtain Pr-Bamboo with an open cell wall structure, while DL-Bamboo, obtained through delignification with CH3COOH/H2O2 and drying, was dissociated into fibers via a simple roller press method. A direct comparison of the impact of DES pretreatment on bamboo fiber dissociation was made by preparing BnOFs and BpOFs from Na-Bamboo and Pr-Bamboo samples, respectively, using the traditional roller press method. Figure 4a shows that delignified Na-Bamboo retained its strong connection between parenchyma cells and vascular bundles, with its structure largely intact after 400 roller press cycles at 10 kg pressure (Supplementary Movie S1). In contrast, the DES-pretreated Pr-Bamboo exhibited weakened bonding between the parenchyma cells and vascular bundles, leading to substantial dissociation after only 10 roller press cycles. Efficient dissociation of BpOFs occurred after 20 roller press cycles (Supplementary Movie S2), highlighting the effectiveness of DES pretreatment (Fig. 4b). Moreover, while DES pretreatment introduced an additional step, it significantly reduced the delignification duration (from 4 h to 2.5 h), resulting in a slightly shorter total processing time (from 4 h to 3.5 h) and greatly improving dissociation efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 10a). These results demonstrate that DES pretreatment not only preserved structural integrity but also enhanced energy-efficient fiber dissociation. Notably, BpOFs maintained a complete vascular bundle structure with an average diameter of approximately 330 μm (Fig. 4c, d), and the surface was smooth (Fig. 4e), indicating effective removal of parenchyma cells. BnOFs and BpOFs exhibited similar chemical compositions (Supplementary Fig. 10b). The crystallinity of BpOFs was 78.6% (Supplementary Fig. 11), slightly higher than that of BnOFs (75.6%). This higher crystallinity provides BpOFs with a more regular cellulose molecular arrangement and stronger intermolecular forces, resulting in superior mechanical properties. Further visualization analysis of the microscopic morphology of BpOFs through micro-CT shows that its overall structure is complete (Supplementary Fig. 12a), and the cross-sectional morphology (Supplementary Fig. 12b) is highly consistent with Supplementary Table 2. Furthermore, a visual analysis of its pore structure was conducted. This non-destructive dissociation characteristic can make the stress conduction of BpOFs more uniform, thereby contributing to higher tensile strength. The mechanical properties of BpOFs (Fig. 4f, g) demonstrate exceptional tensile strength, with a tensile stress of 2636 ± 197 MPa—21.3 times higher than that of Na-Bamboo (123.80 ± 29.62 MPa) and 3.2 times higher than that of BnOFs (824 ± 73 MPa) (Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Movie. S3). In addition, BpOFs exhibited a Young’s modulus of 54.9 ± 6.96 GPa and a work of fracture of 68.0 ± 7.17 MJ/m3, which are 9.6 and 48.6 times higher than Na-Bamboo (5.7 ± 1.54 GPa and 1.4 ± 1.06 MJ/m3), and 3.2 and 3.2 times higher than BnOFs (17.2 ± 2.03 GPa and 21.3 ± 2.68 MJ/m3). These high-modulus, high-strength BpOFs offer a promising foundation for the application of glass fiber and carbon fiber textiles, as well as alternative structural materials. Tensile tests were conducted on multiple BpOFs, and the similar stress-strain curves and displacement-load curves observed indicate the stability of the material (Supplementary Fig. 14). And in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Movie. S4, the load (N), cross-sectional morphology and area (mm²) and tensile strength (MPa) of the BpOFs samples during the testing process are presented.

Notably, despite retaining the natural hollow vascular bundle structure, the apparent tensile strength of BpOFs reaches 1947 ± 217 MPa, which is comparable to the highest tensile strength reported for bamboo macrofibers to date (1900 ± 320 MPa). A key difference lies in the fiber architecture: the high-performance bamboo fibers reported by Li et al. exhibit a solid structure with the natural cavities removed during processing, leading to a density of approximately 1.45 g/cm³. In contrast, BpOFs preserve the native hollow structure and therefore exhibit a lower apparent density of 1.22 ± 0.07 g/cm³ (Supplementary Fig. 15). As a result, the present fibers maintain comparable tensile performance while reducing weight. When normalized by density, the specific strength of BpOFs (~ 1600 MPa·cm³/g) is correspondingly higher than that of solid bamboo fibers (~ 1310 MPa·cm³/g). This observation highlights that the mild DES pretreatment enables the preservation of the natural tubular architecture without sacrificing mechanical performance, providing an alternative structural design strategy rather than a direct performance competition with previously reported solid bamboo fibers. Moreover, the retained hollow architecture offers a larger accessible surface and potential mechanical interlocking sites for polymer infiltration. This structural feature is beneficial for interfacial load transfer in fiber-reinforced composites, as demonstrated in the composite results presented later.

For broader context, the apparent tensile strength of BpOFs falls within the upper range of natural structural fibers (including coconut44, hemp19,45, cotton46, bacterial cellulose47, grass18 and other high-performance bamboo fibers29), exceeds that of synthetic fibers (polystyrene48, polypropylene49, polyester50, polyamide51) and approaches that of certain mineral52,53 and carbon fibers54–56, while maintaining the intrinsic advantages of a renewable biomass-derived material (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Table 3). Rather than outperforming all high-performance fibers, the significance of the present work lies in achieving high engineering strength together with structural integrity, lightweight character, and flexibility in a minimally processed natural fiber. BpOFs can achieve lengths of 5–30 cm (Supplementary Fig. 16) and exhibit superior mechanical properties. For example, 30 cm BpOFs (Supplementary Fig. 17a) showed diameters of 339 μm, 328 μm, and 334 μm at different locations (Supplementary Fig. 17b–d), with diameter distribution tightly clustered around 330 μm (Fig. 4i), indicating excellent diameter homogeneity. Furthermore, the cross-section also maintains a complete and consistent microscopic morphology of the vascular bundles (Supplementary Fig. 17e–g). Furthermore, BpOFs exhibit excellent flexibility: the fibers can be twisted, knotted, and folded without fracture (Fig. 4j), and a single filament is capable of lifting a 10 kg load (Fig. 4k). Overall, this work demonstrates the efficient dissociation of bamboo original fibers while preserving the vascular bundle architecture, yielding fibers with high engineering strength, good uniformity, and remarkable flexibility.

Enhanced mechanical properties of BpOFs

Hydrogen bonding, a key intermolecular directional interaction in cellulose, is often used to explain the relationship between cellulose structure and properties. The hydrogen bond characteristics of DES can provide additional hydrogen bonding sites, facilitating the reconstruction of the hydrogen bond network in BpOFs57. DES pretreatment removes some lignin and hemicellulose while opening the pore structure of bamboo, which makes lignin more easily degraded into small molecules during the delignification process. This, in turn, allows for the gradual exposure of cellulose hydroxyl groups and better reconstruction of the hydrogen bonding network. During the drying process, the interaction mechanism of cellulose molecules in BpOFs is illustrated in Fig. 5a. As water molecules bound to the cellulose evaporate, the cellulose fibers draw closer and form strong intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. To further explore hydrogen bonding changes, two-dimensional correlation FT-IR spectra (3800-3000 cm−1) were analyzed using 2D-shige software58. In the 2D and 3D correlation spectra (Fig. 5b–d), the peak values for both intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds are positive (Ф > 0), suggesting that hydrogen bonding reconstruction occurs to varying degrees during drying. The absence of cross peaks indicates independent reconstruction of the hydrogen bonds59. Figures 5e, f show 2D asynchronous correlation spectra in 3D and 2D, while Fig. 5g displays the 3D asynchronous correlation spectra at the wavelength where the intermolecular hydrogen bond O(6)H…O(3’) is located. In the 2D correlation spectra, the changes in hydrogen bonding order during drying are reflected by the positive and negative values of Ф and Ψ. According to Noda’s law60, if the positive and negative values of Ф (x1, x2) and Ψ (x1, x2) are the same, x1 changes faster than x2; if they differ, x1 changes more slowly than x2. In Fig. 5b3 and 5c3, Ф (3214, 3480) > 0 and Ψ (3214, 3480) > 0, indicating that the intermolecular hydrogen bond O(6)H…O(3’) reconstructs faster than the intramolecular hydrogen bond O(2)H…O(6). Conversely, Ф (3352, 3480) > 0 and Ψ (3352, 3480) < 0 indicate that the reconstruction of intramolecular hydrogen bond O(3)H…O(5) is slower than that of the intermolecular hydrogen bond O(2)H…O(6). Thus, the order of hydrogen bonding reconstruction during drying is: intermolecular hydrogen bonding O(6)H…O(3’) > intramolecular hydrogen bonding O(2)H…O(6) > intramolecular hydrogen bonding O(3)H…O(5). As water molecules evaporate, capillary forces cause CNFs to contract, initially forming intermolecular hydrogen bonds61. Among intramolecular hydrogen bonds, O(2)H…O(6), with lower bond energy, forms more rapidly than O(3)H…O(5). The characteristic peaks at 3427 cm−1, 2901 cm−1, 1170 cm−1, 1113 cm−1, 1039 cm−1, and 895 cm−1 in BnOFs and BpOFs, corresponding to -OH, C-H, and C-O vibrations in cellulose, indicate that cellulose is retained in the bamboo original fibers (Fig. 5h). The reconstruction of hydrogen bonds in bamboo fibers occurs in a specific order. To further investigate this, the absorption peaks in the 3800-3000 cm−1 range of the FT-IR spectra were analyzed using Peak-fit software to assess the changes in different types of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 5i, j). In BnOFs, the content of intermolecular hydrogen bonding O(6)H…O(3’) was 16.9%, and intramolecular hydrogen bonding was dominated by O(3)H…O(5) (64.3%). In BpOFs, the content of intermolecular hydrogen bonding O(6)H…O(3’) increased to 20.7%, while intramolecular hydrogen bonding shifted to a synergistic interaction between O(3)H…O(5) (31.5%) and O(2)H…O(6) (34.5%) (Supplementary Table 4). The peak positions of hydrogen-bonded OH groups shifted to lower wavenumbers, reflecting an increase in hydrogen bond strength. Quantitative analysis showed that the bond energies of the intramolecular hydrogen bonds O(2)H…O(6) and O(3)H…O(5), as well as the intermolecular hydrogen bond O(6)H…O(3′), increased by 0.19, 0.35, and 0.10 kJ/mol, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). These changes suggest a shortening of hydrogen bond lengths and a strengthening of the hydrogen-bonding network. Along with the improved alignment of CNFs, this reconstructed hydrogen-bond architecture provides a robust supramolecular basis for the superior tensile properties of BpOFs. This recognition of hydrogen bonding and enhanced bond energy contributes to the excellent mechanical properties of BpOFs60. The nanofibril orientation index (φ), calculated from the (200) plane, was further analyzed through azimuthal integration of the (200) scattering plane61. BpOFs exhibited a higher and narrower (200) crystal plane diffraction peak, with a φ value of 0.91, compared to 0.88 for BnOFs (Fig. 5m). This indicates a more ordered arrangement of CNFs in BpOFs. This structural regularity arises from the preferential reconstruction of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which effectively regulate CNF assembly and promote their directional alignment. Such alignment forms a more regular microstructure that significantly enhances interfacial interactions, increases the effective contact area, and provides a structural basis for uniform stress transfer, thereby reducing stress concentration. Importantly, when combined with the removal of amorphous component, as evidenced by the XRD results (Fig. 2l), this enhanced CNF alignment synergistically contributes to the observed improvements in tensile strength and modulus. Furthermore, the measured intrinsic density of BpOFs reached 1.55 ± 0.04 g/cm³ (Supplementary Fig. 15), which closely approaches the theoretical density of cellulose nanocrystals (approximately 1.5–1.6 g/cm³)62. This high density reflects the compact packing of CNFs achieved through the removal of amorphous components and the reconstruction of strong hydrogen bonding networks, indicating that BpOFs possess a near-theoretical dense structure capable of supporting exceptional mechanical performance. To further understand the mechanical properties of BpOFs, the fracture morphology of the bamboo fibers was observed (Fig. 5n). The fracture site exhibited a serrated, multi-stage layered structure with continuous separation of the cell walls (Fig. 5o), suggesting a ductile fracture behavior63. CNFs at the fracture site were well-aligned with good orientation (Fig. 5p), indicating that the strong hydrogen bonding interactions between the dense, ordered CNFs in BpOFs enhanced inter-fiber forces64. Bamboo fiber-reinforced epoxy resin composites

High-strength fiber-reinforced epoxy resin (EP) composites are easy to form, and exhibit high strength, making them widely used in various applications. BnOFs and BpOFs are considered an ideal choice for composite reinforcement65. When BpOFs are woven into EP, they interlace within the EP matrix (Fig. 6a). This integration enables effective stress transfer between the fibers and the matrix, thereby enhancing the overall mechanical performance of the composites. It is the unique hollow structure of BpOFs that allows EP to wrap the fibers both internally and externally (Fig. 6b), enhancing their interaction within the EP matrix. Furthermore, the interfacial shear strength of BpOFs@EP reached 16.57 ± 0.74 MPa, 1.69 times greater than that of BnOFs@EP (Fig. 6c), as BnOFs are only externally wrapped by EP (Supplementary Fig. 18). On the other hand, the interfiber binding in BpOFs is denser, with fewer parenchyma cells on the surface, resulting in stronger bonding with EP. The addition of BnOFs significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of epoxy composites. The tensile strength and tensile modulus of the composite increased from 76.04 ± 8.75 MPa and 3.31 ± 0.23 GPa to 332.29 ± 15.97 MPa and 4.06 ± 0.27 GPa, respectively—an increase of 4.40 and 1.23 times. With the addition of BpOFs, the tensile strength and Young’s modulus were further increased to 458.09 ± 15.63 MPa and 5.75 ± 0.28 GPa, representing improvements of 37.86% and 41.63%, respectively, compared to BnOFs@EP (Fig. 6d, e). These results indicate that while both untreated and DES-pretreated fibers reinforce the epoxy matrix, BpOFs provide a markedly greater strengthening effect. This improvement is due to the excellent tensile strength of both BnOFs and BpOFs, which transfer the load from the epoxy matrix to the fibers, reinforcing the composite66. Consistent with this mechanism, the composites predominantly fractured along the fiber axis (Supplementary Fig. 19), further confirming the reinforcing role of fibers. The bending strength and modulus also increased substantially, from 111.04 ± 7.98 MPa and 3.78 ± 0.19 GPa to 305.62 ± 18.25 MPa and 24.89 ± 1.09 GPa, and then to 480.26 ± 20.24 MPa and 31.43 ± 0.82 GPa, with the addition of BnOFs and BpOFs, respectively (Fig. 6f, g). Notably, although the addition of BnOFs and BpOFs improved bending strength, it reduced bending strain, leading to higher bending stiffness67. This effect is particularly pronounced in BpOFs-reinforced composites, where the rigid nature of DES-pretreated fibers more effectively restricts matrix deformation, highlighting their superior reinforcing capability. The significant difference in mechanical properties between BnOFs and BpOFs at the fiber level is somewhat reduced at the composite level due to the dominant contribution of the epoxy matrix and the critical role of fiber–matrix interfacial bonding. However, compared with other natural-fiber composites reported in the literature (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Table 6), these values of tensile strength (458.09 MPa) and bending strength (480.26 MPa) place BpOFs@EP at the forefront, highlighting the unique advantages of DES pretreatment and the strong application potential of bamboo original fiber composites. Digital images of the bending fracture show that the addition of BnOFs and BpOFs prevented the collapse of the EP composite during bending and helped maintain the relative integrity of the BnOFs@EP and BpOFs@EP composite after fracture (Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, BnOFs and BpOFs also enhanced the impact resistance of EP composites. At an impact energy of 30 J, EP reduced the velocity of a drop hammer from an incident velocity of 4.12 m/s to a residual velocity of 4.06 m/s68. In contrast, the BnOFs@EP and BpOFs@EP composites reduced the residual velocity to 1.88 m/s and 0.95 m/s, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 21). When the impact energy was 10 J, EP showed visible damage (Supplementary Fig. 22a). At an impact energy of 20 J, the BpOFs@EP composite maintained structural integrity (Supplementary Fig. 22b), and at 30 J, the addition of BpOFs effectively reduced the damage to the composite (Supplementary Fig. 22c). The impact buffering time of BnOFs@EP and BpOFs@EP composites was significantly longer than that of EP (Fig. 6i), and the total energy increased from 0.73 J to 23.85 J and 28.40 J, respectively, demonstrating stronger impact resistance. Its impact strength and specific impact strength also increased from 2.32 ± 0.67 MJ/m2 and 68.25 ± 18.63 MJ/m3 to 75.92 ± 4.84 MJ/m2 and 1986.42 ± 120.49 MJ/m3, 90.42 ± 5.14 MJ/m2 and 2252.64 ± 109.96 MJ/m3 (Fig. 6j). The displacement-force curve (Supplementary Fig. 23a) shows a high yield point and a delayed curve descent, while the time-force curve (Supplementary Fig. 23b) indicates an increase in the yield point and an extension of the impact time69. This further confirms that BnOFs and BpOFs enhance the impact resistance of EP composites. Due to the superior mechanical properties of BpOFs compared to BnOFs, the reinforcing effect of BpOFs on EP composites is also greater. Discussion

This study presents an innovative and efficient “top-down” dissociation strategy for ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers (BpOFs) by leveraging the structural variability of bamboo. By selectively weakening the chemical linkage between the parenchyma cells and bamboo fibers while enhancing mechanical properties through hydrogen bonding reconstruction, our approach preserved the vascular structure of bamboo fibers. Using a mild ChCl/OA-DES pretreatment, the modulus of elasticity of parenchyma cells was reduced while that of fiber cells slightly increased, which significantly weakened intracellular bonding, thus facilitating rapid dissociation of BpOFs with minimal mechanical force and preventing damage to their hollow vascular structure. DES pretreatment improved pore structure, increasing accessibility and reducing delignification time and reagent consumption in the subsequent CH3COOH/H2O2 treatment. During drying, hydrogen bonds were reconstructed in the following order: intermolecular hydrogen bonds O(6)H…O(3’) > intramolecular hydrogen bonds O(2)H…O(6) > intramolecular hydrogen bonds O(3)H…O(5). This process led to an increase in the content of intermolecular hydrogen bonding O(6)H…O(3’) from 16.9% to 20.7%. The reorganization of intramolecular hydrogen bonds and enhanced bond energies, combined with a well-aligned nanostructure, contributed to the ultra-high-strength mechanical properties of BpOFs. The resulting BpOFs exhibit a high apparent tensile strength of 1947 ± 217 MPa while retaining the natural tubular morphology and good dimensional uniformity and flexibility. Analysis of the load-bearing cell wall further reveals an intrinsic cell-wall stress of 2636 ± 197 MPa, indicating efficient stress transfer within the ordered cellulose framework. Importantly, the preserved hollow vascular bundle structure not only reduces density (1.22 ± 0.07 g cm⁻³) but also offers accessible surface area and potential mechanical interlocking sites, which are advantageous for interfacial stress transfer in polymer composites. Overall, this study shows that high engineering performance can be achieved without destroying the native bamboo architecture. The proposed dissociation strategy provides a sustainable route to structurally intact, mechanically efficient biomass fibers and highlights a design paradigm in which hierarchical natural structures are preserved rather than removed for advanced composite applications.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

Bamboo was obtained from Zhejiang Menglihong Bamboo and Wood Co, Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The samples were cut into slices with dimensions of 5–25 cm × 25 mm × 2 mm (length × width × thickness). Choline chloride (ChCl, ≥ 98%), Nickel sulfate hexahydrate (NiSO4·6H2O, ≥ 98.5%) and Polyethylene Glycol 6000 (PEG-6000, ≥ 99%) were sourced from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Oxalic acid dihydrate (OA·2H2O, ≥ 99.5%) and acetic acid (CH3COOH, ≥ 99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30 wt.% in H2O) was purchased from Nanjing Chemical Reagent Co, Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥ 99.7%) was purchased from Wuxi Yasheng Chemical Co, Ltd. (Wuxi, China). Epoxy resin (EP) and curing agent were purchased from Jinling Lilians Co, Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

Preparation of ChCl/OA-DES pretreated bamboo

ChCl and OA were mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio and heated to 80 °C until the solution became transparent. This ratio was chosen because it yielded a transparent and stable DES with optimal viscosity and hydrogen-bond donor–acceptor interactions, enabling effective penetration into bamboo tissues. Natural bamboo (Na-Bamboo) was then soaked in the ChCl/OA-DES at a mass ratio of 1:10 and pretreated at 110 °C for 1 h. After the reaction, the samples were washed with ethanol and deionized water to remove the residual DES to obtain pretreated bamboo (Pr-Bamboo).

Preparation of the bamboo original fiber

The delignification solution was prepared by mixing CH3COOH and H2O2 in a 1:1 volume ratio at room temperature. (Warning: Peracetic acid is a strong oxidizing agent, which is corrosive and irritating to the skin, eyes and respiratory tract. Strictly follow the operational specifications during preparation and use; avoid contact with flammable and combustible materials to prevent fire or explosion hazards.) Pr-Bamboo was immersed in the delignification solution and treated at a mass ratio of 1:10 at 80 °C for 2.5 h. After the reaction, the samples were washed with ethanol and deionized water to remove the residual delignification solution to obtain delignified bamboo (DL-Bamboo). The DL-Bamboo was subsequently dried at 60 °C and subjected to mechanical dissociation using a roller with a 4 cm diameter under 10 kg applied (Supplementary Movie S2). The rolling process was repeated 20 cycles, which successfully separated the bundles and yielded ultra-high-strength bamboo original fibers (BpOFs). In contrast, untreated bamboo required up to 400 rolling cycles but still failed to achieve effective dissociation (Supplementary Movie S1), highlighting the critical role of DES pretreatment in facilitating fiber separation.

Development of BpOFs@EP composites

The BpOFs were placed in the mold with a solid content of 40 wt.% and subsequently impregnated with EP containing 1 wt.% curing agent. The mixture was first cured at room temperature for 24 h, followed by further curing at 80 °C for 24 h to obtain the BpOFs@EP composite.

Characterization

The developed BpOFs were characterized for their structural morphology, mechanical strength, chemical composition, and water absorption capacity using various characterization techniques such as SEM, FT-IR, XRD, and Raman mapping. In addition, the BpOFs@EP composites were evaluated for their structural morphology and mechanical strength. The strengthening mechanism of BpOFs was further investigated through porosity and pore size distribution, nanoindentation, FT-IR, and 2D-WAXS. Detailed experimental procedures for these characterization techniques are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Unless otherwise specified, all quantitative results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Mechanical tests were performed on at least five specimens (n = 5), and chemical/physical measurements were based on three replicates (n = 3). Spectroscopic and microscopic characterizations were repeated on independent samples to ensure reproducibility.
