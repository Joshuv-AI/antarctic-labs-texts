Excited State Densities from Time-Dependent Density Functional Response Theory
Anna A. Baranova, Neepa T. Maitra

Abstract
While the variational principle for excited-state energies leads to a route to obtaining excited-state densities from time-dependent density functional theory, relatively little attention has been paid to the quality of the resulting densities in real space obtained with different exchange-correlation functional approximations, nor how non-adiabatic approximations developed for energies of states of double excitation character perform for their densities. Here we derive an expression directly in real space for the excited-state density, that includes the case of non-adiabatic kernels, and consequently is able, for the first time, to yield densities of states of double-excitation character. Under some well-defined simplifications, we compare the performance of the local-density approximation and exact-exchange approximation, which are in a sense at opposite extremes of the fundamental functional approximations, on local and charge-transfer excitations in one-dimensional model systems, and show that the dressed TDDFT approach gives good densities of double-excitations.

Introduction

The advent of time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) forty years ago
Runge and Gross 1984
;
Ullrich 2012
;
Marques et al. 2012
has enabled the calculation of electronic spectra, response properties, and general non-perturbative dynamics, for large and complex systems that would otherwise not be possible. This is due to the one-to-one mapping between time-dependent potentials and densities, for a specified initial state, opening the possibility of finding a non-interacting (Kohn-Sham) system that reproduces the same density while evolving in the computationally simpler one-body Schrödinger equation. While the approach would formally yield exact observables of the interacting system, in practice approximations must be made for the exchange-correlation potential.
The majority of applications operate in the linear response regime, where one considers a time-dependent perturbation on the system, from whose response excitation energies and transition properties out of the ground-state can be extracted, and, with the approximations in use today, an unprecedented balance between accuracy and efficiency has been achieved.
When thinking about the linear response of a ground-state system, it can be understood that properties related to transition densities between the ground and excited states of the true interacting system,
\langle\Psi_{I}|\hat{n}({\bf r})|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, can be directly obtained, but it is
not immediately obvious that excited-state densities
\langle\Psi_{I}|\hat{n}({\bf r})|\Psi_{I}\rangle
can be extracted from the formalism. However, with the recognition that excited states are stationary points of the Hamiltonian, then using time-independent perturbation theory instead of time-dependent perturbation theory offers a route to extracting their densities from energies computed from linear response; equivalently, framing the problem as a variational one
Furche 2001
;
Furche and Ahlrichs 2002
. (We note that excited state densities can also be obtained from extrema of the purely the ground-state energy functional
Perdew and Levy 1985
;
Levi et al. 2020
).

In this paper, we work directly in real space instead of in the space of single-excitations that the earlier pioneering work was framed in
Furche 2001
;
Furche and Ahlrichs 2002
, and going beyond the use of an adiabatic kernel that the earlier work was limited to. The latter extension enables us to obtain densities for states of double-excitation character. We explore the small-matrix approximation limit of our expressions on a series of one-dimensional model systems that cover local excitations, charge-transfer excitations, and double excitations.
Motivated by the works
Grabarz and Ośmiałowski 2021
;
Guido et al. 2018
;
Hait and Head-Gordon 2018
;
Verma and Truhlar 2017
;
Sarkar et al. 2021
;
Jacquemin 2016
;
Hickey and Rowley 2014
;
Herbert 2024
suggesting that the hybrid exchange-correlation functionals with a large fraction of exact exchange yield accurate excited-state properties, we explore the performance of the excited-state densities in two opposite limits of approximation – in the adiabatic local density approximation (LDA), and adiabatic exact exchange (EXX).
We find that the choice of ground-state approximation is important in getting good Kohn-Sham densities in the first place, on top of which TDDFT provides corrections that bring the density close to the exact. These corrections involve a sum over all orbitals, whose accuracy depends on the choice of exchange-correlation functional, and we study the convergence of this sum. Finally, we show that the DSMA and DSPA frequency-dependent approximations provide good approximations to the densities of states of double-excitation character, while the adiabatic approximation fails.

Excited-state densities from time-independent perturbation theory

A standard result from time-independent perturbation theory of quantum mechanics is that the first-order change to an excited-state energy is the expectation value of the perturbation evaluated in that excited state. Applied to many-electron systems when this perturbation is in the externally applied one-body potential, this then yields
\delta E_{I}=\int n_{I}({\bf r})\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})d^{3}r
, where
n_{I}({\bf r})=\sum_{\sigma_{1}..\sigma_{N}}\int|\Psi_{I}({\bf r}\sigma_{1},{\bf r}_{2}\sigma_{2}...{\bf r}_{N}\sigma_{N})|^{2}d^{3}r_{2}..d^{3}r_{N}
is the density of the unperturbed excited state
I
. Thus, knowledge of the excited state energy and how it varies with the potential yields the excited-state density itself:

n_{I}({\bf r})=\frac{\delta E_{I}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\,.

(1)

Eq.
1
provides the cornerstone for obtaining excited state densities from TDDFT response: while ground-state DFT provides the ground-state energy
E_{0}
, the TDDFT linear response formalism provides excitation energies
\omega_{I}
, so taking the functional derivative with respect to the external potential of the sum of these yields the excited-state density, exact in principle and dependent on functional approximations made in practise, as we shall see shortly.
Thus, we write the excited state energy in two parts:

E_{I}=E_{0}+\omega_{I}

(2)

where
E_{0}=\left\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{T}+\hat{W}|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle+\int drn_{0}(r)v_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)
(with
\hat{T}
and
\hat{W}
as kinetic and electron-interaction operators), and
\omega_{I}=E_{I}-E_{0}
is the excitation frequency of the excited state
I
. The latter are eigenvalues of the TDDFT matrix equation
Casida 1995
;
Casida 1996
;
Petersilka et al. 1996
;
Grabo et al. 2000
:

\Omega(\omega_{I})\mathbf{G}_{I}=\omega_{I}^{2}\mathbf{G}_{I}

(3)

where the matrix elements of
\Omega(\omega)
are expressed in the single-excitation basis,

q=i\rightarrow a,\;q^{\prime}=j\rightarrow b
, as

\Omega_{qq^{\prime}}(\omega_{I})=\nu^{2}_{q}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}+4\sqrt{\nu_{q}\nu_{q^{\prime}}}f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq^{\prime}}[n_{0}](\omega_{I})\,.

(4)

Here
\nu_{q}={\epsilon}_{a}-{\epsilon}_{i},\;\nu_{q^{\prime}}={\epsilon}_{b}-{\epsilon}_{j}
are the corresponding KS transition frequencies,
and
f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq^{\prime}}
is Hartree-exchange-correlation matrix element, defined through

f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq^{\prime}}[n_{0}](\omega_{I})=\iint d^{3}xd^{3}x^{\prime}\Phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}[n_{0}]({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},\omega_{I})\Phi_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

(5)

where

f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}[n_{0}]({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime},\omega)=\frac{1}{\left|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\right|}+\int d(t-t^{\prime})e^{{\rm i}\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\left.\frac{\delta v_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}({\bf x},t)}{\delta n({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\right|_{n=n_{0}}

(6)

and
\Phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{a}(\mathbf{x})
represents a KS single-excitation transition density.

Evaluating the derivative in Eq. (
1
) from Eq.
2
we have

n_{I}({\bf r})=n_{0}({\bf r})+\frac{\delta\omega_{I}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}

(7)

where the first term, the ground-state density, comes from the derivative of the ground-state energy. We focus here on
the excited state density difference

\Delta n_{I}(\mathbf{r})\equiv n_{I}({\bf r})-n_{0}({\bf r})=\frac{\delta\omega_{I}}{\delta v_{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf r})}=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\frac{\delta\omega^{2}_{I}}{\delta v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r})},

(8)

obtaining the derivative from Eq. (
3
) in the following way. Multiplying by
\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}
on the left, and then taking the derivative, yields the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}\left(\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\Omega(\omega_{I})\mathbf{G}_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}\Omega(\omega_{I})\mathbf{G}_{I}+\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\Omega(\omega_{I})\frac{\delta\mathbf{G}_{I}}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}+\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}\mathbf{G}_{I}

(9)

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}_{I}\frac{\delta}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\left(\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}_{I}\right)+\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\mathbf{G}_{I},

(where we have used the Hermitian property of
\Omega(\omega)
)
while the right-hand-side gives

\frac{\delta}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\left(\omega_{I}^{2}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}_{I}\right)=\omega^{2}_{I}\frac{\delta}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(r)}\left(\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}_{I}\right)+\frac{\delta\omega^{2}_{I}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(r)}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}_{I}.

(10)

Equating the two sides gives

\Delta n_{I}({\bf r})=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\left(\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{-1}\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\mathbf{G}_{I}

(11)

This expression gives us the exact density-difference, once we compute the functional derivative of the TDDFT matrix (see shortly).

While Eq. (
11
) is independent of the choice of normalization of the response eigenvectors
\mathbf{G}_{I}
(as it must be from Eq. (
8
)), it may be useful to consider the particular normalization derived in Ref.
Casida 1995
which directly relates the eigenvectors to the oscillator strengths via

f_{I}=\frac{2}{3}\omega_{I}|\langle\Psi_{0}|{\bf r}|\Psi_{I}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{2}{3}\left|\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}_{q}^{\dagger}S_{q,q^{\prime}}^{-1/2}G_{I,q^{\prime}}\right|^{2}

Casida 1996
, where
S
is the diagonal matrix
S_{qq^{\prime}}=\delta_{qq^{\prime}}\frac{1}{v_{q}}
. Ref.
Casida 1995
showed that the required normalization condition is

\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\left(\left.\mathbbm{1}-\frac{\partial\Omega(\omega)}{\partial(\omega^{2})}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}\right)\mathbf{G}_{I}=1.

(12)

With this choice, we have

\Delta n_{I}({\bf r})=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\frac{\delta\omega^{2}_{I}}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\left.\frac{\partial\Omega(\omega)}{\partial(\omega^{2})}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}\mathbf{G}_{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\text{ext}}({\bf r})}\mathbf{G}_{I}.

(13)

We note that the functional derivative
\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}
has two contributions evident from Eq. (
4
): one is from the variation of KS orbitals and eigenvalues
\{\phi_{i}({\bf r}),\epsilon_{i}\}
that explicitly appear in Eq. (
4
) and from the variation of the functional-argument
n_{0}
of the
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}
kernel under small changes of the external potential, and the other is from the possible frequency-dependence of the xc kernel
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}(\omega)
because this is evaluated at the solution point of Eq. (
3
),
\omega_{I}
, which depends on
v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})
,
\frac{\delta f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}(\omega_{I})}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}=\left.\frac{\delta f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}(\omega)}{\delta\omega}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}\frac{\delta\omega_{I}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}
.
Within the commonly-used adiabatic approximation, the TDDFT matrix is frequency-independent and so
\frac{\delta f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}(\omega)}{\delta\omega}=0
and
\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial(\omega^{2})}
in Eq.
13
is zero. In the general case, frequency-dependence of the exact kernel brings non-linearity to Eq. (
3
), resulting therefore in more solutions than the size of the KS single-excitation matrix.
This is essential to correctly get states corresponding to states of double-excitation character
Tozer and Handy 2000
;
Maitra et al. 2004
;
Maitra 2022
;
Dar and Maitra 2023
;
Dar and Maitra 2025
, which are not accessible with an adiabatic approximation. While the frequency-dependence of the
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}
kernel also affects the density-difference dependence through Eq. (
13
), this equation is, however, still linear, as the matrix is evaluated at the solution frequency
\omega_{I}
, yielding a unique and well-defined density-difference in Eq.
8
.

A simplification to Eq. (
8
) arises once we separate the two contributions to the functional derivative of the
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC}
kernel that were mentioned above:

\frac{\delta}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})}f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}[n_{0}](\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega_{I}[n_{0}])=\left.\frac{\delta f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}[n_{0}](\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}+\left.\frac{\partial f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}{\partial\omega^{2}}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}\frac{\delta\omega_{I}^{2}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})}

(14)

where the second term is only non-zero in the case of non-adiabatic functionals. Transferring this separation to the full
\Omega(\omega)
matrix, and inserting into Eq. (
8
), we find

\Delta n_{I}({\bf r})=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\frac{\delta\omega_{I}^{2}}{\delta v_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})}=\frac{1}{2\omega_{I}}\mathbf{G}_{I}^{\dagger}\left.\frac{\delta\Omega(\omega)}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}\mathbf{G}_{I}.

(15)

We note that this does not trivially follow from Eq.
11
because in general
G_{I}
need not be chosen orthonormal; it certainly is not in the case of the normalization chosen for oscillator strengths, Eqs..

Eq.
11
, or Eq.
15
define the exact excited-state densities once the terms of the derivative matrix
\frac{\delta\Omega_{qq^{\prime}}(\omega)}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}
are calculated. When KS states are considered real, one finds:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Omega_{qq^{\prime}}(\omega)}{\delta v_{\text{ext }}(r)}=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\left\{2\nu_{q}\Delta n^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm KS}_{q}(\mathbf{x})\delta_{qq^{\prime}}+2f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq^{\prime}}\left(\omega\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{q^{\prime}}}{\nu_{q}}}\Delta n^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm KS}_{q}(\mathbf{x})+\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{q}}{\nu_{q^{\prime}}}}\Delta n^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm KS}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right.

(16)

\displaystyle+4\sqrt{\nu_{q}\nu_{q^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{p\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{p}}f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,pa,\,jb}\left(\omega\right)\Phi_{ip}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{p\neq a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{p}}f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,ip,\,jb}\left(\omega\right)\Phi_{pa}(\mathbf{x})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\sum_{p\neq j}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{p}}f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,ia,\,pb}\left(\omega\right)\Phi_{jp}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{p\neq b}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{p}}f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,ia,\,jp}\left(\omega\right)\Phi_{pb}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right\}\left(\mathbbm{1}-f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}\right)^{-1}(\mathbf{x},{\bf r})

\displaystyle+4\sqrt{\nu_{q}\nu_{q^{\prime}}}\int d^{3}x\,\tilde{g}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC},qq^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{x},\omega\right)\chi(\mathbf{x},{\bf r})\,.

16

(17)

2.1
Small-Matrix Approximation

While Eqs.
15
–
16
provide the exact prescription for the excited-state density difference, we will make a diagonal approximation to the TDDFT matrix Eq. (
3
) to simplify the calculation in our examples. Neglecting the mixing between different single KS excitations is known as the Small-Matrix Approximation (SMA)
Grabo et al. 2000
, and taking the diagonal element of Eq.
3
yields

{\omega^{2}_{I}}\;\;{\overset{\rm SMA}{\approx}}\;\;\nu_{q}^{2}+4\nu_{q}{f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}}_{qq}(\omega_{I}).

(19)

Under this approximation, with normalization condition Eq. (
12
), Eq. (
15
) reduces to

\displaystyle\Delta n_{I}^{\mathrm{SMA}}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{I}^{2}}{2\omega_{I}}\left.\frac{\delta\Omega_{qq}(\omega)}{\delta v_{\rm ext}({\bf r})}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{I}}

(20)

with the eigenvector
G_{I}
reducing to the number

G_{I}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left.\frac{\partial\Omega_{qq}(\omega)}{\partial\omega^{2}}\right|_{\omega=\omega_{\text{SMA},I}}}}

(21)

Inserting the diagonal elements of Eq. (
16
) directly into Eq. (
20
) leads to the SMA density-difference:

\displaystyle\Delta n_{I}^{\mathrm{SMA}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{{G_{I}^{2}}}{\omega_{I}}\int d^{3}x\left\{\left[\left(\nu_{q}+2f_{\mathrm{Hxc}qq}(\omega_{I})\right)\Delta n_{I}^{\mathrm{KS}}(\mathbf{x})\right.+4\sum_{p\neq a}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{q}}{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{p}}{f_{\mathrm{Hxc}\,ip,ia}(\omega_{I})}{\Phi_{pa}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\right.

(22)

\displaystyle\left.-4\sum_{p\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{q}}{\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{i}}{f_{\mathrm{Hxc}\,pa,ia}(\omega_{I})}{\Phi_{ip}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)}\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}-f_{\mathrm{Hxc}}{\chi_{s}}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}\right)\left.+2\nu_{q}{\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{xc}\,ia,ia}\left[n_{0}\right]\left(\mathbf{x},\omega_{I}\right)}{\chi\left(\mathbf{x},r\right)}\right\}.

While this expression is formally derived from a single matrix element, it contains information about all KS states of the system through
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}
and the infinite sums over all KS orbitals
\phi_{p}
. We also define a “single-transition limit” (STL) approximation which keeps only KS orbitals
i
and
a

In this limit, a further simplication ensues: the functions
(\mathbbm{1}-f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S})^{-1}
and
\chi
can be exactly defined by the closed-form expressions

(\mathbbm{1}-f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S})^{-1}({\bf x},{\bf r})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-{\bf r})-\frac{4}{\nu_{q}+4f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq}}f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,q}(\mathbf{x})\Phi_{q}({\bf r})

(23)

\chi(\mathbf{x},{\bf r})=\frac{\nu_{q}}{\nu_{q}+4f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq}}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}(\mathbf{x},{\bf r}),

(24)

resulting from a series resummation described in Section 2 of Supplementary Material. Under simply this two-level reduction, one obtains the density difference expression in the following form:

\displaystyle\Delta n^{\mathrm{STL}}_{I}({\bf r})=

\displaystyle\frac{G_{I}^{2}}{\omega_{I}}\left\{\left(\nu_{q}+2f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq}(\omega_{I})\right)\Delta n^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm KS}({\bf r})\right.

(25)

\displaystyle+\frac{8}{\nu_{q}+4f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq}}\left[\left(\nu_{q}+f_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC},qq}(\omega_{I})\right)\left(f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,ii,\,ia}(\omega_{I})-f_{\mathrm{HXC},\,aa,\,ia}(\omega_{I})\right)\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left.\nu_{q}\tilde{g}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\rm XC},qqq}(\omega_{I})\right]\Phi_{q}({\bf r})\right\},

We note that no other approximations are applied in derivation of the Eq. (
25
). For an adiabatic approximation to the kernel, this result is formally equivalent to the density-difference matrix representation given by Ref.
Furche and Ahlrichs 2002
in its single-transition limit; see Supplementary Material for the details.

2.1.1
Numerical Evaluation of
\Delta n^{\rm SMA}_{I}({\bf r})

The calculation of the density difference through Eq. (
16
) or its SMA approximation through Eq. (
22
) requires addressing several issues, introducing approximations along the way.

First, we will truncate the infinite sums in Eq. (
22
) over all KS orbitals (
p
). Since we will be focusing only on the lower excitations, this is not a severe approximation, and convergence can be easily checked.

Second, concerning the
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}
matrix elements: As noted earlier, the required matrix elements are not only between the pairs of occupied-unoccupied transitions that are the usual ingredients of linear response calculations. While the larger span does not pose a problem for adiabatic
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}
approximations, computed from the second functional derivative of an energy functional, we note that these matrix elements are largely not known for the existing frequency-dependent
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}
approximations
Maitra et al. 2004
;
Cave et al. 2004
;
Dar and Maitra 2023
;
Dar and Maitra 2025
, although we note that the TDDFT kernel derived from the Bethe-Salpeter equation
Romaniello et al. 2009
;
Sangalli et al. 2011
;
Authier and Loos 2020
does contain a spatial-dependence that would provide this larger class of matrix elements.
Thus when we compute the densities of excited states of double-excitation character, we will instead work directly with the excitation energies provided by the dressed frequency-dependent kernel of Refs.
Maitra et al. 2004
;
Cave et al. 2004
;
Dar and Maitra 2023
;
Dar and Maitra 2025

2.2
Densities of States of Double-Excitation Character

With the adiabatic approximation, TDDFT is only able to predict excitations of single excitation character, composed of linear combinations of one electron promoted from an occupied KS state to a virtual one. The formulation for excited-state densities in Refs.
Furche 2001
;
Furche and Ahlrichs 2002
is restricted to this case.
For states of double-excitation character, a frequency-dependent kernel was developed
Tozer and Handy 2000
;
Maitra et al. 2004
;
Maitra 2022
;
Dar and Maitra 2023
;
Dar and Maitra 2025
that has been shown to perform well on a range of systems
Huix-Rotllant et al. 2011
, most recently capturing the curve-crossing between the 1Bu and 2Ag singlet states in trans-butadiene
Dar and Maitra 2025
, for example. The kernel of Ref.
Maitra et al. 2004
;
Cave et al. 2004
operates within a dressed single-pole approximation (DSPA), generalized to a dressed Tamm-Dancoff approximation (DTDA) when more than one single excitation couples to a double, while, in order to obtain meaningful oscillator strengths as well as energies, the kernel of Ref.
Dar and Maitra 2023
;
Dar and Maitra 2025
operates within a dressed small-matrix approximation (DSMA), generalized to the fully dressed TDDFT (DTDDFT).
While Eq. (
15
) and its SMA Eq (
22
) hold also beyond the adiabatic approximation, they require matrix elements of the kernel that in a larger space than those derived in the approximations, as discussed above. Therefore, we instead take the functional derivative of Eq.
8
in a different way, directly from the DSPA and DSMA expressions for the excitation energy.

We begin with the DSMA case where

3
Results and Analysis on Model Systems

We now apply our method to a set of one-dimensional models of two soft-Coulomb interacting electrons with different external potentials. We define the three following external potentials: (i) Helium
v_{\rm ext}^{\mathrm{1DHe}}(x)=\frac{-2}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}
to model local excitations, (ii) two double-well models, one in which the ground-state density is largely localized in a soft-Coulomb potential on the left,
v_{\rm ext}^{\mathrm{gs-soft}}(x)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{(x+R/2)^{2}+1}}-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(x-R/2)}

Fuks et al. 2013
, and the other in which the ground-state density is more tightly localized in the left well,
v_{\rm ext}^{\mathrm{gs-loc}}(x)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{(x+R/2)^{2}+1}}-\frac{2.9}{\cosh^{2}(x+R/2)}-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(x-R/2)}
, where
R=7
a.u., and in these wells we will model charge-transfer excitations, and (iii) perturbed harmonic
v_{\rm ext}^{\mathrm{Harm_{\gamma}}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\gamma|x|
for modeling the double excitations, where
\gamma
parameter tunes the degree of mixing between a single excitation and double-excitation in the second multiplet.

With just two electrons, the Kohn-Sham ground-state has both electrons occupying the lowest-energy orbital
i=1
, and the KS response function for all three cases can be written as

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm S}(x,x{{}^{\prime}})=-4\sum_{a}^{\mathrm{unocc}}\frac{\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{a}(x)\phi_{a}(x{{}^{\prime}})\phi_{i}(x{{}^{\prime}})}{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}}.

(40)

The simplicity of the models enables the computations of the exact KS orbitals, and we can compare the effect of using approximate versus exact KS orbitals in the expressions for the excited-state densities.
We will first consider the lowest energy excitations in 1DHe and the two double-wells. These are dominated by single excitations, and so we will consider two adiabatic approximations: exact-exchange (EXX) and local-density approximation (LDA)
Helbig et al. 2011
, which are first-principles approximations at two opposite extremes, the former derived from many-body perturbation theory has non-local density-dependence and the latter derived from the uniform electron gas paradigm has completely local density-dependence. We will compare the fully-converged SMA densities (Eq.
22
) with those obtained from STL (Eq.
25
).
For the Harm
γ
system, we compare the performance of ASMA using the EXX approximation with the DSMA Eq. (
31
).

For our calculations of the exact ground and excited-state densities, and the exact, Kohn-Sham, EXX, and LDA orbitals of these one-dimensional systems, we use the Octopus code
Andrade et al. 2015
. The exact Kohn-Sham potential and orbitals are obtained from first inverting the ground-state Kohn-Sham equation using the exact ground-state density of a two-electron system, and then finding its eigenstates and eigenvalues in an in-house code. The helium model was simulated in a box ranging from -40 to 40 a.u. with a grid spacing of 0.1 a.u. For both double-well models, a box from -50 to 50 a.u. with the same grid spacing was used. Finally, for the system in the harmonic potential, simulations were performed in a box from -20 to 20 a.u. with a grid spacing of 0.05 a.u.

4
Conclusions

In this work we developed a general formalism for obtaining the real-space excited-state densities from linear response TDDFT using time-independent perturbation theory. While the approach is equivalent to earlier work
Furche 2001
;
Furche and Ahlrichs 2002
presented using the stationary property of excitation energies and the variational principle, our work here goes beyond the restriction to the adiabatic approximation assumed there. We observe that even with two contrasting approximations (LDA and EXX), we obtain accurate results for the lowest local and charge-transfer excitations between closed-shell fragments. Within the small-matrix approximation, which in its essence retains a single KS transition in its calculation of the energy, the excited-state density still incorporates corrections from all possible KS transitions, leading to a significant improvement over the KS approximation. Our results show the importance of an accurate KS potential in the calculation of excited-state density, which is better provided by adiabatic EXX than the LDA. For the future calculations of excited-state densities in real systems, our results are consistent with previous works
Grabarz and Ośmiałowski 2021
;
Guido et al. 2018
;
Hait and Head-Gordon 2018
;

The practical use of the general formalism is limited to the adiabatic approximation and to proposed approximations that are available for frequency-dependent
f_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm HXC}

We show that for doubly-excited states, the densities from the DSPA and DSMA-based formulation, both previously shown to be good for excitation energies and DSMA for oscillator strengths, provides accurate double excitation densities, which is not achievable through adiabatic approximation.
