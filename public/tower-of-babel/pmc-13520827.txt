Inverse‐consistent deformable image registration to propagate biomechanical constraints for online adaptive radiotherapy
Authors: T J W Draper, C Zachiu, B W Raaymakers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate motion estimation remains a key challenge in adaptive radiotherapy (ART). Voxel‐wise accuracy is required, as registration errors can compromise the treatment quality. Locally constrained image registration is a promising technique to further improve ART treatment, but relies heavily on quick and accurate delineations of the local areas used to constrain. In online clinical scenarios, these delineations are often available on pre‐treatment image volumes, but not on the online data due to accuracy and time constraints.PurposeWe developed a deformable image registration (DIR) framework that integrates inverse consistency with local biomechanical constraints, enabling tissue‐specific motion modeling using contours from only a single image volume. This approach enforces tissue‐specific constraints on the inverse deformation field and propagates them to the forward deformation through inverse consistency optimization, yielding anatomically plausible motion estimates for ART workflows.MethodsThe proposed framework was evaluated in three scenarios relevant to ART: abdominal CT‐CT and multi‐modality CT‐MR registration with incompressibility constraints on the liver and kidneys, as well as thoracic CT‐CT registration with local rigidity constraints on the ribs and vertebrae. We also investigated the robustness of the framework to image degradation by evaluating motion estimates under added Gaussian noise and artificially induced streaking artifacts. Performance was compared to a baseline DIR model and an unconstrained inverse‐consistent model. Accuracy and anatomical consistency were assessed using landmark alignment, contour overlap, and physics‐based motion analysis.ResultsWe demonstrate that local biomechanical constraints imposed on the inverse deformation field are successfully propagated to the forward motion field through inverse consistency optimization. The proposed framework improves the biomechanical plausibility of the resulting deformation fields at a small cost of landmark‐based accuracy, and maintaining comparable results in terms of contour‐based evaluation. The method is applicable to both mono‐ and multimodality registration and is more robust to noise and artifacts that appear from image acquisition and reconstruction.ConclusionsThe proposed framework uses inverse consistency to propagate local constraints between motion fields. This work demonstrates the use of local biomechanical constraints for DIR in several use‐cases for ART. This methodology requires delineations of local tissue on only one of the image volumes. This approach can become beneficial for applications where voxel‐wise accuracy and anatomical consistency are required, but quick and accurate delineations of new anatomies are unavailable, such as online ART.Keywords: biomechanical constraints, deformable image registration, image‐guided radiotherapy, inverse consistency

INTRODUCTION

Deformable image registration (DIR) is an essential component in image‐guided adaptive radiotherapy (ART), enabling accurate alignment of images acquired at different times or using different imaging modalities. In ART workflows, the patient anatomy may change due to tumor response, organ movement, or tissue deformation. These anatomical changes correspond to different mechanics underlying tissue‐specific properties, such as compressibility and elasticity. 1 , 2 It necessitates registration methods that not only align image intensities but also explicitly model and constrain deformation behavior to ensure anatomically plausible estimates.

Recent guidelines and recommendations for DIR in ART therefore emphasize the anatomical plausibility of the estimated motion, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 particularly in applications such as contour alignment and propagation of quantitative information such as dosimetric values, CT numbers, or functional imaging data (e.g., diffusion and perfusion parameters). These applications require DIR methods with a high degree of accuracy and precision, which simultaneously need to be robust to noise and imaging artifacts. In areas with poor image contrast, constraining the deformation field in line with the underlying physical properties introduces additional prior information into the registration model, which has shown to improve registration accuracy in those areas. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

Incompressibility is frequently used in DIR to model tissue that can change in shape, but do not change in volume. In these methods, the Jacobian determinant plays a central role, as it quantifies the relative volume change per voxel as estimated from the motion field. In practice, we expect incompressible tissues to have a Jacobian closely distributed around one. Typical values of the liver and kidneys have been determined to be in the range 0.8–1.2. 7 Fu et al. 8 proposed a local variant of the iLogDemons algorithm, 9 by registration of incompressible regions (segmented in advance) with one model, while other regions were handled with an unconstrained model. Rohlfing et al. 10 and Dauguet et al. 11 introduced local incompressibility using the properties of B‐spline basis functions for an efficient numerical implementation. Bosma et al. 12 combined contour‐guided registration with a Helmholtz‐Hodge decomposition to enforce incompressibility and study the impact on dose accumulation. Zachiu et al. 13 combined local incompressibility with smoothness constraints in a spatially adaptive variational registration model.

A limitation of the previously mentioned locally constrained registration algorithms is the need for contours to specify the constrained regions. In online‐ART, the delineation and manual editing step is typically the most time‐consuming and error‐prone step in the workflow. 14 , 15 In these workflows, it is therefore typically not feasible to obtain the structures required for constrained DIR. A solution would be to rely exclusively on contours defined on the planning image, which can be delineated offline without stringent time constraints. This requires a technical change in the interpolation methods used to apply the motion field, to use a push‐forward approach instead of a pull‐back approach. However, a downside of this method is that it can cause “holes” in the propagated data, which can potentially cause important complications for mapping quantitative information such as CT numbers or dosimetric values. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 The pull‐back approach does not have these problems, although local constraints require contours defined on the daily image.

Alternatively, one could estimate the motion in the opposite direction, calculating the constrained inverse transformation, before inverting the motion field to obtain the deformation in the forward direction. Inverting the estimated deformation vector field after DIR is employed has been used in the context of deformable dose accumulation, though there the observed difficulty is that it is not always possible to derive a well‐defined inverse motion field. 19 The concept of inverse consistency, that is, estimating the forward and inverse deformation field simultaneously, has been used to obtain a well‐defined inverse map and reduce dose mapping errors. 20 , 21 , 22 However, these methods do not guarantee that the estimated deformation field adheres to any anatomically plausible motion patterns.

Instead, we propose a model that jointly optimizes both the forward and the constrained inverse transformations by integrating inverse consistency, while simultaneously imposing local biomechanical constraints, into a novel DIR model. By constraining the motion field in one direction, these constraints will be iteratively propagated through inverse consistency. 23 , 24 We demonstrate improved anatomical fidelity of the motion for CT‐to‐CT/synthetic CBCT/MR registration for the liver, kidneys, and rigid structures in the thorax (ribs and vertebrae). Finally, high computational efficiency is achieved through an optimized GPU implementation in CUDA, enabling seamless integration into online adaptive workflows.

Introducing biomechanical constraints to the DIR optimization

We show that at least two types of biomechanical constraints are compatible with our framework of constraint propagation through inverse consistency: local rigidity and local incompressibility. These constraints are enforced only on the inverse motion field during optimization, which will propagate to the forward motion field by minimization of the ICE.

Constrained image registration with local rigidity constraints

With local rigidity, the motion estimate is constrained such that the deformation field is described by some rotation matrix and translation vector in part of the domain. A full description of local rigidity constraints compatible with the update described in Equation (2) is given by Draper et al. 29 The numerical solution uses the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), which introduces two auxiliary variables, to estimate the deformation field followed by an update of the rigid transformations belonging to a list of structures passed to the algorithm. If such a list is available, we replace the update step of the inverse motion field v with the ADMM step. This method comes with an additional model parameter, set to κ=108.

Implementation details

All registration algorithms were implemented in C++/CUDA 32 and executed on GPU hardware. As the cuFFT library does not support a native DCT implementation required for Equation (2), a custom implementation was made using the Fast Fourier Transform and appropriate coefficient manipulation based on the work of Makhoul 33 and the multidimensional DCT implementation in Matlab (MIRT_DCTN). Using Matlab's MEX API, the registration models were compiled into Matlab executable MEX functions. Data pre‐ and post‐processing was done in Matlab (version 2025b). All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) W‐2133 CPU and an Nvidia Quadro RTX 5000 GPU.

EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

The proposed model was evaluated in four experiments relevant to online‐ART workflows, using three publicly available datasets. Together, these experiments demonstrate the versatility for mono‐modality and cross‐modality applications, as well as multiple types of biomechanical constraints in the abdomen and thorax. An overview of the datasets and experiments is provided in Figures 3 and 4.

Abdominal CT‐guided ART with incompressibility constraint on the liver: The first ten cases of the Liver‐DIR‐QA database were used, containing abdominal CT‐CT image pairs with annotated landmarks on vessel bifurcations in the liver. 34 These manually validated landmarks, on average 55 per image volume, were used to validate the accuracy. Manual delineations of the liver were created and used to enforce incompressibility. The local correlation coefficient (LCC) was used as the similarity metric, with Gaussian width σ=5 voxels, and regularization parameters were set to (α,τ)=(5.0,1.0).

DISCUSSION

The proposed methods demonstrate clear potential to improve DIR in image‐guided ART, particularly in scenarios where contour availability is limited but physically consistent deformation fields are required. Through simultaneous estimation of the forward and inverse deformations, the approach addresses a key limitation of conventional DIR, which typically do not enforce physically consistent motion constraints. This is particularly relevant in the presence of imaging noise and artifacts, where robustness of the estimation motion is essential, all while maintaining compatibility with the time‐constraints for online‐adaptive workflows.

A relevant application is CBCT‐guided online ART, where a major bottleneck remains the manual review and correction of contours propagated from the planning CT to the daily CBCT. 15 The proposed framework directly addresses this limitation by requiring contour delineations only on the planning image, potentially reducing the need for time‐consuming manual adjustments.

In parallel, recent advancements in AI‐based segmentation, such as TotalSegmentator and MedSAM, are rapidly increasing the availability of fast and accurate delineations on MR and CT imaging modalities. 36 , 48 A limitation of the proposed approach is that its relative advantage decreases in clinical settings where accurate segmentations are readily available for both the planning and daily images. However, although CBCT image quality has improved through advances in acquisition and reconstruction techniques, the modality remains affected by noise, artifacts and has reduced soft‐tissue contrast, which makes accurate segmentation more challenging than on conventional CT or MR imaging. Consequently, AI‐based segmentation on CBCT cannot yet be considered a solved problem, and robust contour propagation methods remain clinically relevant.

Importantly, DIR remains essential for applications that require voxel‐wise correspondence between images, including deformable dose accumulation, propagation of CT intensity values, and propagation of functional imaging parameters. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of complementing traditional registration accuracy metrics with measures of biomechanical plausibility 3 , 4 and inverse consistency 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 for these applications. The proposed framework therefore provides a method for improving the physical plausibility and consistency of deformation fields while remaining compatible with existing ART workflows.

The experimental results provide evidence that inverse consistency can transfer biomechanical constraints between the inverse and forward motion fields. By simultaneously minimizing the ICE and constraining the inverse motion field, we have shown that motion field characteristics such as incompressibility and rigidity are transferable properties. Concretely, for abdominal CT‐CT registration, we observed that the mean Einc. in the liver decreased by a factor four compared to the baseline model, while preserving comparable landmark registration performance. A similar pattern is observed for the thoracic CT‐CT registration task, where the mean EOC was reduced by a factor four compared to the baseline model, resulting in mean EOC comparable to values reported in Draper et al. 29 and Staring et al. 46 For both datasets, the mean TRE was below the maximum voxel size for all methods, which is argued as a sufficient degree of accuracy for radiotherapy applications by the AAPM Task Group 132. 4 However, a reduction in mean TRE was observed in Figures 6 and 7 when constraints were enforced, particularly in the absence of noise and artifacts. Moreover, we observed that these constraints remain consistent when noise and imaging artifacts are introduced to the imaging volumes. From Figures 6 and 7, the evaluated metrics in the abdominal (liver) experiment are more sensitive to induced noise and artifacts than those in the thoracic experiment. We expect this to be the case due to the sharp contrast of ribs, vertebrae, and lung tissue in POPI, compared to the liver bifurcations used for landmark annotation in Liver‐DIR‐QA, which lose contrast with increasing noise and artifacts.

This framework for DIR is compatible with different similarity metrics, such as the LCC for mono‐modality, and NGF for multi‐modality image registration as shown in Figure 8 and Tables 1 and 2, achieving contour overlap comparable to the current state‐of‐the‐art registration algorithms. 28 Incompressibility constraints on the liver and kidneys have been enforced to result in a narrow distribution of the Jacobian determinant. The Jacobian determinant of soft tissues with high water content such as the liver and kidneys has been determined to be within the range 0.8–1.2. 7 Using the local Leray projection, our constrained model ensures that almost all voxels are contained within this range, where the baseline and ICDIR model show significant variance in this structures. Our framework achieves this result without affecting the contour overlap of these structures, or surrounding structures (e.g., the spleen). Averaged over these seven cases, the achieved DSC is similar to the current state‐of‐the‐art registration models, 28 although here the affine pre‐alignment aided as a pre‐processing step. Finally, using a GPU‐accelerated implementation, we have shown that inverse‐consistent, constrained DIR can be achieved at the order of seconds, opening up applications within radiotherapy with strict time‐constraints, such as online ART.

Figures 6 and 7 and Table 2 show that enforcing inverse consistency (green) already reduces the compressibility of the liver and non‐rigidity of the ribs and vertebrae, respectively, compared to the baseline model (blue). Minimizing inverse consistency ensures that the motion fields have a well‐defined inverse. Since motion fields with a small or negative Jacobian determinant are not invertible, enforcing inverse consistency reduces the extreme values from the Jacobian determinant distribution, leading to a narrower distribution. Additionally, performing constrained registration and inverting the motion field afterwards can therefore lead to ill‐defined behavior as there is no guarantee, even with appropriate regularization, that the motion fields have a positive Jacobian determinant in the image domain. Currently, even state‐of‐the‐art registration algorithms can have a (small) number of negative Jacobian determinants. 49 Alternatively, the motion field could be expanded in an underlying basis of smooth functions, such as (cubic) B‐splines, although those approaches can lead to overly smooth deformation fields. This is highlighted in the context of sliding motion between the rib‐lung interface by Vishnevskiy et al., requiring linear instead of cubic B‐splines for motion estimation. 50

Future research will explore which motion constraints can be propagated using this approach. Clearly, incompressibility cannot be used in all regions, for instance to capture the motion patterns of the lungs, the gastro‐intestinal tract, and the bladder. 51 Tzitzimpasis et al. 52 demonstrated with the generalized div‐curl regularization that assuming a nearly uniform Jacobian determinant, rather than strict incompressibility, improved pulmonary motion estimation. This generalization has potential applications when the amount of compression/expansion is known beforehand, or could be used during optimization to smooth out the degree of compression from the motion field.

Finally, shearing motion at the rib‐lung interface results in a sharp transition of the motion gradient at the boundary. Development of inverse consistency methods of piecewise smooth motion fields would therefore be beneficial to incorporate within the proposed framework. The class of diffeomorphic registration algorithms inherently produce smooth motion fields with smooth inverses and could therefore be of potential interest. Examples are the ANTs, LDDMM, CLAIRE, and the class of log‐Demons models. 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 However, beyond constraints such as incompressibility, it remains unclear how constraints applied during the estimation process affect the final deformation field, since these methods regularize an intermediate variable (the velocity field) rather than the motion field itself. Future work could therefore focus on incorporating local biomechanical constraints within this class of registration models.

CONCLUSION

This work proposed a framework for variational DIR that propagates localized biomechanical constraints between the forward and inverse motion fields via inverse consistency. The framework was evaluated on abdominal and thoracic CT datasets, as well as abdominal CT‐MR data, demonstrating that local incompressibility constraints in the liver and kidneys, and rigidity constraints in the ribs and vertebrae can be effectively propagated between deformation fields. This resulted in substantially improved biomechanical plausibility, achieved at a small cost in landmark‐based accuracy and comparable results for contour alignment. Furthermore, this method retained its accuracy in the presence of noise and streaking artifacts representative of CBCT imaging. These findings suggest that the proposed approach provides a practical mechanism to include biomechanical information into ART workflows, when there is a limited availability of delineations.
