On Glaisher’s Partition Theorem
George E. Andrews, Aritram Dhar

Abstract
. Glaisher’s theorem states that the number of partitions of n n into parts which repeat at most m − 1 m-1 times is equal to the number of partitions of n n into parts which are not divisible by m m . The m = 2 m=2 case is Euler’s famous partition theorem. Recently, Andrews, Kumar, and Yee gave two new partition functions C ⁡ ( n ) C(n) and D ⁡ ( n ) D(n) related to Euler’s theorem. Lin and Zhang extended their result to Glaisher’s theorem by generalizing C ⁡ ( n ) C(n) . We generalize D ⁡ ( n ) D(n) , prove an analogous partition identity for the m = 3 m=3 case, and show that the general case is an example of an almost partition identity. We also provide a new series equal to Glaisher’s product both in the finite and infinite cases.

1.
Introduction

Euler’s celebrated partition theorem
[
1
]
can be stated as

(1.1)

\displaystyle A(n)=B(n)

where
A(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
into distinct parts and
B(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
into odd parts.

In a recent work, Andrews, Kumar, and Yee
[
4
]
have provided two more elegant companion partition functions to
A(n)
and
B(n)
. In particular, they have obtained the following partition identity.

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
4
, Theorem
1.1
]
)
For any positive integer
n
, we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle A(n)=B(n)=C(n+1)=\dfrac{1}{2}D(n+1),

where
C(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
with largest part even and parts not exceeding half of the largest part are distinct and
D(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
into non-negative parts wherein the smallest part appear exactly twice and no other parts are repeated.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer. Define
A_{m}(n)
to be the number of partitions of n into parts repeating less than
m
times and
B_{m}(n)
to be the number of partitions of
n
into parts not divisible by
m
. In 1883, Glaisher
[
5
]
obtained the following partition theorem which is a natural extension of Euler’s theorem (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.2
.

(
[
5
]
)
For any non-negative integer
n
, we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle A_{m}(n)=B_{m}(n).

For any integer
0\leq j\leq m-1
, define
B^{(j)}_{m}(n)
to be the number of partitions of
n
into parts not divisible by
m
and largest part congruent to
j
modulo
m
. Clearly,

(1.4)

\displaystyle B_{m}(n)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}B^{(j)}_{m}(n).

In a recent work, Lin and Zhang
[
6
]
generalized Andrews, Kumar, and Yee’s
C(n)
function to the case of
m
-
regular partitions
(partitions whose parts are not divisible by
m
). It is noteworthy to point out that the cardinality of the set
B^{\prime}_{m}(n)
in their paper is the same as
B^{(m-1)}_{m}(n)
.

Define
C_{m}(n)
to be the number of partitions of
n
in which the largest part is divisible by
m
(say
mj
) and all parts less than or equal to
j
repeat less than
m
times. Also, define
C_{m}(0):=1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

(
[
6
, Theorem
1.5
]
)
For any positive integer
n
, we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle B^{(m-1)}_{m}(n)=C_{m}(n+1).

Remark 1
.

Lin and Zhang
[
6
]
provided both
q
-series (through generating functions) and bijective proofs of Theorem
1.3
.

Let us now recall some notations from the theory of
q
-series which can be found in
[
1
]
. For any complex variable
A
and non-negative integer
N
, the conventional
q
-Pochhammer symbol is defined as

\displaystyle(A)_{N}=(A;q)_{N}

\displaystyle:=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}1\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\text{when }N=0,\\
\prod\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}(1-Aq^{i})\,\,\,\text{otherwise, and}\end{array}

\displaystyle(A)_{\infty}=(A;q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle:=\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}(A)_{N}\,\,\text{for}\,\,|q|<1.

Next, for non-negative integers
a
,
b
, we define the
q
-binomial coefficient as

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}a+b\\
b\end{matrix}\right]_{q}:=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\dfrac{(q)_{a+b}}{(q)_{a}(q)_{b}}\quad\text{for }a,b\geq 0,\\
0\qquad\qquad\text{otherwise}.\end{array}

Also, let
\zeta_{n}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
denote the primitive
n^{\text{th}}
root of unity and
\omega=\zeta_{3}
denote the primitive cubic root of unity here and throughout the remainder of the paper.

Now, define
D_{m}(n)
to be the number of partitions of
n
into non-negative parts where the smallest part occurs exactly
m
times and all other parts repeat less than
m
times.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any non-negative integer
n
, we have

(1.6)

\displaystyle C_{m}(n)=\dfrac{D_{m}(n)+E_{m}(n)}{m},

where
E_{m}(n)
is such that the generating function of
E_{m}(n)
is

(1.7)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(q):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{m}(n)q^{n}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{mn}(q^{n+1};q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}(\zeta^{j}_{m}q^{n+1};q)_{\infty}.

The
m=3
case of (
1.7
) corresponds to the following identity

Theorem 1.5
.

(1.8)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}(q):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{3}(n)q^{n}=2-q-2q^{2}+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-1)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1},

where
\chi_{3}(n)
is defined as

\displaystyle\chi_{3}(n):=\omega^{n}+\omega^{-n}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}2\qquad\,\,\,\text{if }3\mid n,\\
-1\quad\,\,\,\,\text{if }3\nmid n.\end{array}

The complexity of the generating functions involved seemed to mitigate against an analog of the
C(n)=\frac{1}{2}D(n)
portion of Theorem
1.1
; however, surprisingly, a similar result exists for
m=3
which clearly follows from (
1.6
) and (
1.8
).

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n
be any positive integer that is not equal to a triangular number plus
1
, then

(1.9)

\displaystyle C_{3}(n)=\frac{1}{3}D_{3}(n).

Even more strikingly, we obtain another example of an
almost partition identity
(they are true for almost all
n
but fail for an infinity of values; see
[
3
]
) for the general case which is as follows.

Theorem 1.7
.

The identity

(1.10)

\displaystyle C_{m}(n)=\frac{1}{m}D_{m}(n)

is true for almost all
n
. Moreover, if
N(x)
is the number of times that (
1.10
) is true for
n<x
, then

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N(x)}{x}=1.

Combining Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
, we then have the following partition identity which is a natural extension of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any positive integer
n
, we have

(1.11)

\displaystyle A_{m}(n)=B_{m}(n)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-2}B^{(k)}_{m}(n)+C_{m}(n+1)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-2}B^{(k)}_{m}(n)+\dfrac{D_{m}(n+1)+E_{m}(n+1)}{m}.

In view of the generating function of
B_{2}(n)
in
[
4
,
(2.1)
]
, we also obtain the following finite
q
-series identity.

Theorem 1.9
.

For any positive integer
N
, we have

(1.12)

\displaystyle 1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}\dfrac{q^{mn-j}}{\left(\prod\limits_{r=1}^{m-j}(q^{r};q^{m})_{n}\right)\cdot\left(\prod\limits_{r=m-j+1}^{m-1}(q^{r};q^{m})_{n-1}\right)}=\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{N}}{(q;q)_{mN}}.

Letting
N\rightarrow\infty
in (
1.12
) gives us the following
q
-series identity.

Corollary 1.10
.

(1.13)

\displaystyle 1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}\dfrac{q^{mn-j}}{\left(\prod\limits_{r=1}^{m-j}(q^{r};q^{m})_{n}\right)\cdot\left(\prod\limits_{r=m-j+1}^{m-1}(q^{r};q^{m})_{n-1}\right)}=\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}.

Remark 2
.

The
m=2
case of (
1.13
) above was recently proved by Andrews and Bachraoui in
[
2
, Lemma
1
, (1.10)]
. Clearly, the series on the left-hand side of (
1.13
) is the generating function of
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}B^{(j)}_{m}(n)
and the product on the right-hand side of (
1.13
) is the generating function of
B_{m}(n)
. The same observation (restricting all parts to be at most
mN
) proves Theorem
1.9
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sections
2
,
3
, and
4
, we provide
q
-series (generating function) proofs of Theorem
1.4
,
1.5
, and
1.7
respectively.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

From the definition of
C_{m}(n)
, we then have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{m}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(1+q^{j}+\ldots+q^{(m-1)j})q^{mn}}{(q^{n+1};q)_{(m-1)n}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{n}q^{mn}}{(q;q)_{mn}}

(2.1)

\displaystyle=(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mn}}{(q;q)_{mn}(q^{mn+m};q^{m})_{\infty}}.

Using Euler’s identity
[
1
, p.
19
, (
2.2.5
)]

(2.2)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{(t;q)_{\infty}}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}},

(replacing
q
by
q^{m}
and letting
t=q^{mn+m}
) in (
2.1
), we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{m}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mn}}{(q;q)_{mn}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mnj+mj}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}

\displaystyle=(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n,j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mn+mnj+mj}}{(q;q)_{mn}(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}

\displaystyle=(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n,j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{kn}_{m}\right)\dfrac{q^{n+nj+mj}}{(q;q)_{n}(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1})^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}}.

Using (
2.2
) with
t=\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1}
in (
2.3
), we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{m}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{(\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{(\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}(q^{m(j+1)};q^{m})_{\infty}}{(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\dfrac{(q^{m};q^{m})_{\infty}}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mj}(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}{(q^{m};q^{m})_{j}(q^{m(j+1)};q^{m})_{\infty}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}(\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{mj}\cdot\prod\limits_{i=m+1}^{\infty}(1+q^{i}+\ldots+q^{(m-1)i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{mj}(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}(\zeta^{k}_{m}q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{m}(n)q^{n}+\dfrac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{m}(n)q^{n},

where the generating function of
D_{m}(n)
clearly follows from its definition and the generating function of
E_{m}(n)
follows from (
1.7
). Comparing coefficients of
q^{n}
on both sides of (
2.4
) for
n>1
, we have

\displaystyle C_{m}(n)=\dfrac{D_{m}(n)+E_{m}(n)}{m}

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

From (
1.7
), it is clear that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{3j}}{(q;q)_{j}}\left((\omega q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}+(\omega^{-1}q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}\right).

Now, define
\chi_{3}(n)
as follows

\displaystyle\chi_{3}(n):=\omega^{n}+\omega^{-n}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}2\qquad\,\,\,\text{if }3\mid n,\\
-1\quad\,\,\,\,\text{if }3\nmid n.\end{array}

Using
[
1
, p.
19
, (
2.2.6
)]
with
t=-\omega q^{j+1}
and
t=-\omega^{-1}q^{j+1}
in (
3.1
), we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{3j}}{(q;q)_{j}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+nj}}{(q;q)_{n}}.

Using Euler’s identity
[
1
, p.
19
, (
2.2.5
)]
with
t=q^{n+3}
after interchanging summations in (
3.2
), we then have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}(q)

\displaystyle=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}}{(q;q)_{n}(q^{n+3};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}(1-q^{n+1})(1-q^{n+2})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}(1-q^{n+1}(1+q)+q^{(n+1)+(n+2)})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}-(1+q)\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+2}{2}}+\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+3}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}+(1+q)\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-1)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-2)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=(2+q)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}-2q(1+q)+(1+q)\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-1)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-2)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=2-q-2q^{2}+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\left(\chi_{3}(n)+\chi_{3}(n-1)+\chi_{3}(n-2)\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-1)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1}.

Now,
\chi_{3}(n)+\chi_{3}(n-1)+\chi_{3}(n-2)=0
for all
n
. Hence,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}(q)=2-q-2q^{2}+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\chi_{3}(n-1)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+1}

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

Define
\chi_{m}(n)
as follows

\displaystyle\chi_{m}(n):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m-1}\zeta^{jn}_{m}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}m-1\,\,\,\text{if }m\mid n,\\
-1\quad\,\,\,\,\,\text{if }m\nmid n.\end{array}

Using
[
1
, p.
19
, (
2.2.6
)]
with
t=-\zeta^{j}_{m}q^{n}
in (
1.7
), we get

(4.1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(q)

\displaystyle=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{q^{mn}}{(q)_{n}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}+nk}}{(q;q)_{k}}.

Using Euler’s identity
[
1
, p.
19
, (
2.2.5
)]
with
t=q^{k+m}
after interchanging summations in (
4.1
), we then have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(q)

\displaystyle=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}(q^{k+m};q)_{\infty}}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}(q^{k+1};q)_{m-1}.

Using
[
1
, p.
36
, (
3.3.6
)]
with the substitutions
N=m-1
and
z=q^{k+1}
, we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}q^{\binom{j+1}{2}+kj}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}q^{\binom{j+k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k)q^{\binom{j+k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=j}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\chi_{m}(k-j)\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\chi_{m}(k-j)\left(\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}-1\right),

where we have used the fact that
\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\chi_{m}(k-j)=0
in the second sum on the last line above. Hence, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}(q)=P_{m}(q)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\chi_{m}(k-j)\left(\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}-1\right),

where
P_{m}(q):=-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{k}\chi_{m}(k-j)q^{\binom{k+1}{2}}
.

The polynomial

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}\chi_{m}(k-j)\left(\left[\begin{matrix}m-1\\
j\end{matrix}\right]_{q}-1\right)

in (
4.3
) above has at most
2^{m-1}-m
terms. Now, there are at most
\sqrt{2x}+1
triangular numbers less than
x
. Hence, the maximum possible number of non-zero coefficients in
\varepsilon_{m}(q)
among the first
x
terms is at most
(2^{m-1}-m)\cdot(\sqrt{2x}+1)
leaving the number of coefficients that vanish as less than

x-(2^{m-1}-m)\cdot(\sqrt{2x}+1)

which is asymptotic to
x
as
x\rightarrow\infty
. This shows that
E_{m}(n)
is almost always zero which completes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

∎
