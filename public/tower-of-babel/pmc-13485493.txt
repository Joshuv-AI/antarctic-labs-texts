A rare acute presentation of simultaneous bilateral angle closure in a patient using a red light therapy mask
Authors: Margarita Labkovich, Paras Shah, Daniel Barmas-Alamdari, Alina Djougarian, Jung Min Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo describe a case of acute-onset bilateral angle-closure glaucoma secondary to ciliochoroidal effusions in a 43-year-old otherwise healthy female. An autoimmune and inflammatory work-up was negative, and the patient was not on any medications or supplements. She had been using a red-light therapy (RLT) mask over the past two weeks, including the morning of her presentation.ObservationsThe underlying mechanism of the patient's presentation was most consistent with secondary angle-closure due to ciliochoroidal effusions and anterior rotation of the ciliary body. The only recent lifestyle change was the new use of RLT mask therapy for over two weeks. With prompt diagnosis, close outpatient monitoring, cessation of the RLT mask use, and careful titration of IOP-lowering, cycloplegic, and steroid medications, the patient returned to her baseline vision of 20/20 OU and had bilateral choroidal effusions resolve.Conclusion and importanceChoroidal effusions have not been previously described in association with at-home RLT mask use; however, existing evidence suggests that RLT exposure may increase choroidal vascularity and thickness. Following cessation of RLT and appropriate medical management, the patient had complete resolution of the ciliochoroidal effusions and full recovery of vision. Further investigation is warranted to better characterize the ocular safety profile of RLT devices.

Introduction

Red light therapy (RLT) has been incorporated into multiple FDA-approved skin treatment devices and has rapidly gained popularity worldwide. The reported benefits of RLT are attributed to its photobiomodulation properties, which include production of increased mitochondrial Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), modulation of cell signaling pathways, synthesis of growth factors, and decreased oxidative stress.1 All these benefits are essential to supporting skin health, particularly when it comes to aging and acne prevention, and maintaining overall skin quality by improving texture, skin tone, and dyspigmentation.2 The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) was a pioneer in RLT, having explored its utility for wound healing in astronauts and for plant growth in outer space as early as the 1980s.2 RLT has been further implemented as an adjuvant treatment of skin cancers, psoriasis, acne, and warts.2 The use of RLT continues to expand given its encompassing benefits of stimulating collagen production, improving circulation to the tissues, and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress of the tissues.2,3

Many commercially available devices combine red and near-infrared light-emitting diodes (633, 830, and 1072 nm wavelength) and are marketed towards skin rejuvenation.1 Their healing and antioxidant benefits have been explored with both skin assessments and participant-reported satisfaction in improving wrinkles, UV spots, pores, and porphyrins. The safety profiles have also been taken into consideration, with their main side effects being primarily related to skin irritation and redness.4,5 Other side effects include headaches, particularly migraines, insomnia, and eye strain.6 Some companies recommend wearing UV-blocking glasses to protect eyes during RLT treatments.6 The long-term safety profile of glasses on eye health remains to be explored. While much of the existing literature exploring the efficacy of RLT consists of surveys, small cohort studies, or in vitro research, to date, there have been no randomized controlled studies with placebo groups to assess the direct impact of RLT.

In this case report, we describe a 43-year-old female with no significant past medical or ocular history who presented to the emergency department with acute bilateral angle closure. Her presenting symptoms included bilateral vision loss and headaches. The patient denied taking any medications or supplements. However, she did start using an RLT mask (Solawave, USA) two weeks prior to presentation, with her most recent use on the morning of her emergency department visit.

Case description

A 43-year-old Caucasian female with no previously diagnosed medical or ocular history, presenting to the emergency department with acute bilateral vision loss and a severe frontal headache. The patient also noted discomfort around her eyes and progressive periocular edema throughout the day. No trauma was reported. The patient denied taking any medications, substances, vitamins, or supplements. Of note, the patient reported receiving a frontalis botulinum toxin (Botox) injection one week prior and that she had been newly using an RLT mask for her cosmetic skin concerns over the past two weeks, which she also used on the morning of her presentation.

Her symptoms began suddenly at 4:00 a.m. Upon awakening and using the restroom, she noted an inability to see her reflection in the mirror. She also experienced a headache, which she initially believed accounted for her visual changes. Throughout the day, her symptoms progressed despite using acetaminophen. She presented to the emergency department that evening at around 9:00 p.m. with worsening periorbital pain and edema.

On presentation, the patient's visual acuity was counting fingers OD and 20/400 OS at near with mid-dilated and poorly reactive pupils OU. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was checked with iCare in the emergency department and ranged from 60 to 65 mmHg OU. Her extraocular movements were intact, and confrontational fields were full OU. Slit lamp exam of the anterior segment was remarkable for 360-degree iridocorneal (IK) touch OD and 180-degree IK touch OS (Fig. 2). She had 1+ microcystic edema OU; the anterior chamber (AC) was shallow OU, but without cell or flare. The patient did not have iris transillumination defects or neovascularization of her iris OU. The lens was clear OU. Four-mirror gonioscopy revealed closed angles OU with no visible angle structures in all views. On non-mydriatic dilated fundus exam, the optic nerves were sharp and pink OU. B-scan ultrasound in the emergency department did not demonstrate any obvious posterior effusions or masses, and the retina appeared flat OU.Fig. 2Slit lamp photograph demonstrating a shallow anterior chamber OD.

The patient was treated with 500mg of intravenous acetazolamide twice, as well as IOP-lowering drops including dorzolamide 2%, timolol 0.5%, brimonidine 0.2%, and latanoprost 0.005% administered every 10 minutes into each eye. Manual angle decompression using a three-mirror lens was also performed. Following treatment, IOP decreased to 28 mmHg OD and 21 mmHg OS, with significant improvement in headache. The patient was discharged on oral acetazolamide 500 mg, dorzolamide/timolol, brimonidine and latanoprost drops, and instructed to follow up the following day in glaucoma clinic.

Upon presentation to the clinic, the patient's best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/80 OD and 20/40-2 OS. Her IOP was 8 mmHg OU on applanation, and the slit lamp exam was remarkable for 3+ Descemet's folds OD with corneal edema OD > OS. The AC was shallow, almost flat OD with 2+ cell/flare and shallow OS with 2+ cell/flare. The pupils were mid-dilated OU. Gonioscopy continued to show no visible angle structures OU.

Following this diagnosis, the patient was started on cyclopentolate 1% eye drops twice daily OU, prednisolone acetate 1% four times daily OU, and sodium chloride 5% hypertonic eye drops four times daily OD given more severe corneal edema in the right eye. Due to a significant amount of inflammation in the AC and substantial periorbital edema, the patient was also started on a Medrol dose pack and was instructed to stop using the RLT mask immediately. A comprehensive autoimmune and infectious serologic workup was pursued which was overall unrevealing.

The patient was followed closely in our outpatient glaucoma clinic. IOP remained in the low teens, and acetazolamide was slowly titrated down in the first week. Over the next two months, the patient was slowly tapered off the IOP-lowering drops, topical IOP-lowering agents, cycloplegics, and steroid were gradually discontinued. Serial examinations demonstrated gradual resolution of the choroidal effusions and improvement in visual acuity (Fig. 3b and c). Her final visual acuity was 20/20 OU at two months after the initial presentation.

Discussion

Given this patient's bilateral presentation of acute angle closure, the underlying mechanism was most consistent with secondary angle closure due to ciliochoroidal effusions and anterior rotation of the ciliary body. An autoimmune and inflammatory work-up was negative and the patient was not on any medications or supplements. The only recent lifestyle change was the new use of RLT mask therapy over a two-week period. The RLT mask is a wearable device with rows of LED bulbs on its inner surface, shining light directly onto the face. The LEDs can be modulated to glow red (at 600-660 nm) or near-infrared (at around 830-860 nm), depending on the skin treatment needs.7 The RLT mask manufacturers do not recommend wearing eye protection, and our patient was not using any.8

As far as her Botox treatment, the injections were limited to the frontalis region and not administered periocularly. Moreover, the patient had undergone similar treatments in the past without any complications. With prompt diagnosis, close outpatient monitoring, and careful titration of IOP lowering, cycloplegic, and steroid medications, the patient was able to return to her baseline vision of 20/20 OU with resolution in bilateral choroidal effusions.

Based on our literature review, there is no well-defined association between at-home RLT mask therapy use and choroidal effusions. When considering the mechanism of RLT, there have been studies looking at repeated low-level red-light therapy (RLRLT) use in myopic children to enhance choroidal perfusion without impacting retinal thickness. Zhao et al. demonstrated a significant increase in subfoveal choroidal thickness after 4 weeks of treatment with red-light therapy (power 2 mW, wavelength 650 nm) for 3 min, twice a day.9 The measurement was obtained with en-face OCT angiography. Wang et al. arrived at a similar conclusion by evaluating choroidal vascular index with a swept source OCT angiography.10 Their group found a significant increase in choroidal vessel volume and thickness, which led to reduced axial length and a regression in spherical equivalent in children with myopia, a findings also demonstrated in another multicenter, randomized-control study on RLRLT.11

While the exact mechanism of RLT causing ciliochoroidal effusions is not known, we postulate that red and near-infrared light could lead to vasodilation of the uvea.9, 10, 11 In vivo studies have shown that 670 nm light causes the release of the vasoactive nitric oxide precursor, thus regulating vascular dilation.12,13 More research is needed to determine the exact mechanism of RLT on the choroidal vasculature.

These observations suggest that RLT can markedly influence choroidal perfusion and vascular dynamics. In the absence of any other risk factors in this patient, it is plausible that RLT contributed to the development of ciliochoroidal effusions. This case highlights a potential vision-threatening adverse effect of RLT and underscores the need for heightened awareness when evaluating patients with acute bilateral angle closure. Given our observation, we believe that eye protection should be considered during RLT treatment, with a warning to seek immediate medical attention for eye pain or vision loss. Further investigation of the ocular adverse effects of RLT via randomized controlled studies may be warranted.

Conclusion

This case report describes an acute onset of bilateral angle-closure glaucoma associated with ciliochoroidal effusions in a 43-year-old otherwise healthy female. While no prior literature has directly linked RLT to choroidal effusions, existing evidence suggests that RLT exposure can increase choroidal vascularity and thickness. Following cessation of RLT and appropriate medical management, the patient experienced complete resolution of the ciliochoroidal effusions and recovery of visual acuity. Further investigation is warranted to better characterize the ocular safety profile of RLT devices, particularly as their use becomes increasingly widespread.

Claims of priority statement

After conducting a literature review on January 9th, 2026, using PubMed and Google Scholar and the keywords “red light therapy”, “bilateral angle closure”, and “ciliochoroidal effusions”, we did not find any prior reports of bilateral acute angle closure in the setting of red light therapy mask use.
