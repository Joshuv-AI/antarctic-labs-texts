Global tilt angle in spinal alignment of asymptomatic older women with low bone mass
Authors: Ping-Chiao Tsai, Ssu-Hsien Lee, Yu-Chieh Chiu, Wen-Tien Wu, Ru-Ping Lee, Tzai-Chiu Yu, Ing-Ho Chen, Jen-Hung Wang, Kuang-Ting Yeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the relationship between sagittal spinal alignment and musculoskeletal health in older women, particularly those exhibiting diminished bone density without apparent symptoms. The study assessed the impact of global tilt (GT) and the presence of coronal malalignment on spinal health. The research involved 165 asymptomatic older women with an average age of 68.91 ± 7.25 years and average body mass index of 24.26 ± 3.66 kg/m². Comprehensive standing anteroposterior and lateral spine radiographs were used for assessment. Through multivariate linear regression analysis, the study identified significant correlations between increased GT angles and various factors, including coronal malalignment, the C7 slope, and pelvic incidence. This led to the formulation of a predictive GT model: GT = −9.79 + 0.06 × menopausal period + 0.19 × body mass index (BMI)− 0.81 × average T score – 0.11 × grip strength + 3.03 × (presence of coronal malalignment) + 0.08 × sagittal vertical axis (SVA) + 0.12 × C7 slope − 0.35 × upper lumbar lordosis (ULL) − 0.43 × lower lumbar lordosis (LLL) + 0.70 × pelvic incidence (PI), with an adjusted R² of 0.816. The study findings highlight the prevalence of coronal malalignment in this demographic and its significant associations with critical spinal parameters. The proposed GT predictive model may enable development of personalized treatment plans for older women with low bone mass.Keywords: Sagittal alignment, Coronal malalignment, Older, Asymptomatic woman with low bone mass, Global tilt angle, C7 slopeSubject terms: Bone imaging, Geriatrics

Introduction

Sagittal and coronal alignment of the spine has been a focal point of orthopedic research in the recent decade because of its clinical implications with respect to degenerative spinal diseases, posture-related complications, and overall musculoskeletal health1,2. Although numerous studies have investigated spinal alignment in various populations, data pertaining specifically to asymptomatic older women with low bone mass are lacking. This gap in knowledge is particularly concerning given the general aging of populations globally and the associated increase in age-related osteoporosis and bone fragility3,4. One clinically important parameter that has gained attention in the study of spinal alignment is global tilt (GT)5. GT is based on pelvic incidence (PI) and sacral slope and is a reliable index of sagittal balance6. Understanding GT and its associations with other spinal and clinical parameters may offer new avenues for tailoring treatment plans to individual patients7. Concomitant with the emphasis on sagittal alignment, the prevalence of coronal malalignment in aging populations remains an underexplored area in the literature8. An analysis of the prevalence of coronal malalignment and its associations with other spinal parameters can offer a more comprehensive perspective on spinal health in older women with low bone mass9. Therefore, this study bridged these knowledge gaps by investigating the prevalence of coronal malalignment in asymptomatic older women with low bone mass. The study also explored the associations between coronal malalignment, GT, and other spinal parameters and developed a predictive model for GT based on results on these associations.

Materials and methods

The protocol of this cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB 107-255-B). The study participants were recruited from the Orthopedic Department of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital for assessments of their bone health. All the included participants provided their written informed consent. The study included female patients aged ≥ 65 years who visited the outpatient department for consultations regarding their bone health status between August 2019 and July 2023. These patients underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans to measure bone mineral density. Patients were eligible for inclusion in the study if they had a normal walking status10 and were diagnosed as having low bone mass status, which was defined as a T score of less than − 2.0 at any of the following locations: the lumbar spine (average), right hip (femoral neck or total femur), or left hip (femoral neck or total femur). Patients were excluded from the study if they had (1) persistent neck pain or back pain for more than 3 months; (2) previous spine surgery; (3) acute spinal trauma within the past 6 months; (4) stroke; or (5) Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, or other mental disorders. Upon obtaining the patients’ consent to participate in the study, we collected pertinent information, including their age, the duration since menopause (referred to as the menopausal period), and their body mass index (BMI). Additionally, we conducted full-length standing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs to assess radiographic parameters, and grip strength was indicated in terms of the mean of triplicate measures of bilateral hand strength.

Coronal malalignment was defined as the absolute value in the coronal distance from the C7 plumb line to the coronal sacral vertical line exceeding 30 mm, per the Obeid-CM classification11 (Fig. 1). Global sagittal parameters, such as thoracic pelvic angle (TPA), GT, and sagittal vertical axis (SVA), as well as regional parameters, such as upper cervical lordosis (UCL), middle cervical lordosis (MCL), lower cervical lordosis (LCL), C7 slope, C7 tilt, chin bow vertical angle (CBVA), cervicothoracic junctional angle (CTJ), upper thoracic kyphosis (UTK), lower thoracic kyphosis (LTK), thoracolumbar junctional angle (TLJ), upper lumbar lordosis (ULL), lower lumbar lordosis (LLL), sacral slope (SS), pelvic tilt (PT) and PI, were measured from long standing lateral plain radiographs12–14. GT is determined by the angle between the C7 vertebra, the center of the sacrum, and the line connecting the center of the femoral heads to the center of the sacrum15. SVA is measured as the horizontal distance from the posterior superior sacral endplate to a vertical plumb line dropped from the centroid of the C7 vertebral body13 (Fig. 2A). UCL is the angle formed between the cranial line (C0) and the lower endplate of C216. MCL is the angle between the lower endplate of C2 and the lower endplate of C516. LCL is the angle between the lower endplate of C5 and the lower endplate of C716 (Fig. 2B). C7 Slope is the angle between a horizontal reference line and a line parallel to the upper endplate of C717. C7 Tilt is the angle between the center of the C7 vertebral body and the middle of the sacral endplate with respect to the vertical reference line18 (Fig. 2B). CTJ is the angle formed between the lower endplate of C6 and the lower endplate of T219. UTK is the angle between the lower endplate of T5 and the lower endplate of T916. LTK is the angle between the lower endplate of T9 and the lower endplate of T1216(Fig. 2C). ULL is the angle between the lower endplate of L1 and the lower endplate of L416. LLL is the angle between the lower endplate of L4 and the upper endplate of S116. TLJ is the angle formed between the lower endplate of T10 and the lower endplate of L220 (Fig. 2C). SS is the angle between the upper endplate of S1 and the horizontal reference line14. PT is the angle between a vertical reference line and a line from the midpoint of the sacral endplate to the femoral rotational axis14. PI is defined as the angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral plate at its midpoint and a line connecting the same point to the center of the bicoxofemoral axis14 (Fig. 2D). There were 5 people independently measuring the parameters, including 3 medical students, 1 resident doctor and 1 spinal surgeon. The intraclass association coefficient of the measurements was 0.89 and 0.83 for intraobserver and interobserver agreement, respectively.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 23.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square tests and independent t tests were used to determine associations of demographic characteristics with coronal malalignment status. A multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to estimate the associations between GT and bone health, handgrip strength, and other clinical and radiographic parameters. To avoid “over fitting” of the adjusted model, the covariates were introduced to this model if the p-value of crude analysis < 0.1. Subsequently, we developed a prediction model for GT by using multiple linear regression analysis. The covariates we put into the model included all the clinical parameters (menopausal period, BMI, grip strength and average T score) and the radiographic parameters with the p-value of adjusted analysis < 0.1. All reported p values are from 2-sided tests, and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

The study included a cohort of 165 female patients with an average age of 68.91 ± 7.25 years and a mean BMI of 24.26 ± 3.66 kg/m² (Table 1). The average duration of menopause among the participants was 19.28 ± 8.26 years. In addition, among the participants, 68 were aged > 70 years, and 40 exhibited coronal malalignment. The average grip strength was 19.79 ± 4.34 kg, with a mean T score of −2.71 ± 0.69. Notably, the patients aged > 70 years exhibited significantly higher MCL and lower LTK angles than did their younger counterparts. Radiographic analysis revealed mean values for PI of 51.06° ± 12.12°, GT of 19.56° ± 8.70°, lumbar lordosis of 45.13° ± 13.58°, thoracic kyphosis of −25.45° ± 7.80°, and cervical lordosis of 45.12° ± 7.32° (Table 1). Coronal malalignment, C7 slope, upper and lower lumbar lordosis (ULL and LLL, respectively), and PI were significantly associated with larger GT angles, with an adjusted R² of 0.809 (Table 2). The derived predictive model for GT, which incorporated these significant predictors, was formulated as GT = −9.79 + 0.06 × menopausal period + 0.19 × BMI −0.81 × average T score –0.11 × Grip strength + 3.03 × (presence of coronal malalignment) + 0.08 × SVA + 0.12 × C7 slope − 0.35 × ULL − 0.43 × LLL + 0.70 × PI, with an adjusted R² of 0.816 (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study revealed a significant correlation between bone density, grip muscle strength, regional sagittal alignment parameters, and the global sagittal parameter GT in asymptomatic older women with low bone mass. This population is particularly at risk for orthopedic complications. Our study further identified significant differences in key spinal parameters between individuals with and without coronal malalignment. This finding offers valuable insights into the intricate interplay between skeletal and postural factors within this demographic.

Comparison between the demographic data of two different age groups

The observed differences in MCL and LTK angles between patients aged > 70 years and their younger counterparts can be attributed to several age-related anatomical and physiological changes in the spine. One possible explanation is the degeneration of lumbar intervertebral discs, which is more pronounced in older adults. This degeneration may cause compensatory lordotic changes in the cervical spine to maintain an upright posture21,22. In the thoracic region, vertebral body changes such as osteophyte formation and vertebral compression fractures, which are more common in older individuals, may lead to a decrease in LTK as the vertebrae become more rigid and less capable of maintaining their natural curvature20,23. These changes may also contribute to an increase in MCL as a compensatory mechanism. Additionally, age-related weakening of the paraspinal muscles and ligaments can exacerbate these alterations in spinal alignment, further influencing the observed differences in MCL and LTK angles24.

In the other way, 24.2% of them exhibited coronal malalignment and there were no difference between the two groups. This finding is of particular significance because the participants are not only older adults but also have low bone mass, a demographic that is at a higher risk of musculoskeletal problems, including vertebral fractures and associated spinal deformities. The prevalence rate identified in this study is within the same range as those reported in other studies focusing on older populations. For example, Smith et al.25 reported that 20–30% of older adults have some degree of coronal malalignment. Their study, however, included both men and women and did not specifically isolate the variable of low bone mass. Safaee et al.26 revealed that degenerative scoliosis is typically diagnosed in adults aged > 50 years, with a mean age of 70.5 years, and the curves had an average gradient of + 3° per year of age. Our study may further confirm the epidemiological consistency of coronal malalignment in older populations, even when considering variations in sex and health characteristics. The high prevalence of coronal malalignment observed in our study, in line with prior research, suggest that the etiology of coronal misalignment lies in factors such as age-related changes in spinal alignment, muscle weakness, and degenerative disc disease27. These findings emphasize the importance of careful assessment of coronal malalignment in clinical practice, particularly in older women with low bone mass who are at an elevated risk of orthopedic problems.

Significance of GT and its correlation with other sagittal parameters

In this study, we demonstrated that a larger GT angle was correlated with the radiographic factors of coronal malalignment, larger C7 slope, smaller ULL, smaller LLL, and larger PI. The observed correlation between coronal malalignment and a larger GT aligns with previous findings on the interplay between sagittal and coronal spinal balance28. This association further highlights the complexity of spinal alignment in individuals with low bone mass, where malalignment in one plane may trigger compensatory mechanisms in another. The positive correlation between a larger C7 slope and GT is consistent with the view that C7 slope indicates global sagittal alignment25. The inclination of C7 may influence the positioning of the entire spine, thereby affecting the overall GT. This observation emphasizes the clinical utility of C7 slope as an indicator of sagittal balance. Conversely, the inverse correlation between ULL and LLL and GT underscores the importance of lumbar curvature in sagittal alignment. A reduction in lumbar lordosis may necessitate compensatory mechanisms, resulting in a larger GT. These findings are consonant with existing findings on lumbar lordosis and its critical role in maintaining sagittal balance29. The positive correlation between PI and GT is consistent with the understanding that PI plays a pivotal role in determining overall sagittal balance30. This result supports the notion that pelvic morphology significantly influences global spinal alignment. These correlations are consistent with findings on spinal biomechanics, further validating the relevance of these findings in the context of asymptomatic older women with low bone mass. The intricate interplay among these factors underscores the need for a multidimensional approach to assessment of spinal alignment and development of patient-specific treatment plans.

Factors correlated with GT and predictive model

In the present study, we identified several factors significantly correlated with larger GT angles, including coronal malalignment, C7 slope, ULL, LLL, and PI. A robust predictive model for GT was developed with an adjusted R² value of 0.816, incorporating all clinical parameters (e.g., menopausal period, BMI, grip strength, and average T-score) and radiographic parameters (e.g., C7 slope, SVA, ULL, LLL, PI) that showed a significant correlation with GT (p-value of adjusted analysis < 0.1, Table 2). Importantly, we included all clinical factors in the model, regardless of their individual correlation in the adjusted model, because each factor plays a critical role in maintaining normal spinal alignment and function in older women. This comprehensive approach aimed to demonstrate the importance of considering multifactorial influences, such as postmenopausal status, bone health, and muscle strength, when evaluating overall spinal alignment in aged females. Our study utilized the formula: GT = − 9.79 + 0.06 × menopausal period + 0.19 × BMI − 0.81 × average T score – 0.11 × Grip strength + 3.03 × (presence of coronal malalignment) + 0.08 × SVA + 0.12 × C7 slope − 0.35 × ULL − 0.43 × LLL + 0.70 × PI.

This formula integrates multiple clinical and radiographic variables, providing a tool that reflects the multifaceted nature of spinal alignment in older women. Our findings align with existing literature, demonstrating: (1) a strong correlation between GT and coronal malalignment, consistent with findings by Terran et al.30, who emphasized the interconnectedness of sagittal and coronal imbalances; (2) a significant relationship between GT and pelvic parameters, corroborating the established role of PI as a key determinant of spinal alignment30,31; (3) the utility of C7 slope as a relevant parameter for predicting GT, supporting its broader use in evaluating sagittal balance32; and (4) the use of GT, alongside other parameters such as T1 pelvic angle and pelvic tilt (PT), as reliable metrics for assessing sagittal balance33–35. As suggested by Obeid et al.35, GT represents a particularly robust parameter for evaluating sagittal alignment, as it combines spino-pelvic factors in a single measure that is minimally affected by patient positioning.

The predictive model for GT has several important clinical applications. It can be used as a screening tool to identify older women at risk for developing spinal deformities, allowing for early intervention and potentially preventing the progression of musculoskeletal deterioration. For example, clinicians could apply the model in routine clinical assessments to flag patients with higher predicted GT values, who may benefit from further diagnostic evaluation, preventive strategies, or targeted therapies. Additionally, the model can guide personalized treatment plans by predicting which patients might require more intensive physiotherapy, dietary management, or pharmacological treatments to improve bone density and muscle function. In surgical planning, the model can help anticipate disturbances in sagittal balance and inform decisions regarding the necessity and extent of corrective procedures.

Despite its potential, the model has several limitations. It was developed based on a specific cohort of asymptomatic, independent older women with low bone mass, which may limit its generalizability to other populations, such as men, younger adults, or those with symptomatic conditions. Furthermore, the model relies on clinical and radiographic parameters that may not be available or practical to measure in all settings. There is also the potential influence of confounding factors, such as lifestyle, physical activity, and nutritional status, that were not directly controlled for in this study. Although we selected a homogeneous cohort to minimize variance, unmeasured confounders could still affect the results. Future research should validate this model in diverse populations and settings, incorporating additional variables to refine its applicability across different clinical scenarios. By addressing these considerations, our study may make a significant contribution to the literature on spinal biomechanics and provides a foundation for future research to validate and expand the use of predictive models in clinical practice.

Strengths, limitations and future perspectives

One strength of this study is its comprehensive evaluation of both global and regional sagittal parameters, which offers a holistic understanding of spinal alignment in older women with low bone mass. Additionally, although its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, this study provides a snapshot of spinal alignment status in the target population, serving as a valuable resource for initial hypothesis generation. However, the study has some limitations. First, cross-sectional studies are inherently limited in their ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Second, the recruitment of patients from a single institution may introduce selection bias. Third, the absence of follow-up assessments restricts our understanding of the temporal relationship between low bone mass and spinal alignment changes. Fourth, despite our efforts to control for confounders by selecting a homogeneous study cohort of asymptomatic women who were independent and older than 65 years, residual confounding due to unmeasured or unknown variables cannot be entirely ruled out. These efforts were aimed at minimizing the influence of personal variance, such as differences in musculoskeletal health, physical activity levels, and nutritional status, which could potentially affect GT and other spinal alignment parameters. By focusing on asymptomatic older women and excluding those with chronic conditions or disabilities that could affect spinal alignment, we sought to create a more uniform study population and provide a focused analysis of the relationship between GT and spinal parameters. However, we acknowledge that other confounding factors, such as lifestyle, dietary habits, and physical activity levels, were not directly measured in this study. Future longitudinal or multicenter studies should be conducted, with samples comprising individuals from both sexes and a broader age range. These studies should incorporate comprehensive assessments of additional confounding variables, such as nutritional status and physical activity levels, to provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors influencing GT and the mechanisms through which low bone mass affects spinal alignment. By doing so, future research could more effectively isolate the direct effects of the observed correlations and strengthen the validity and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted the complex relationship between sagittal alignment and bone health in older women with low bone mass and revealed a notable prevalence of coronal malalignment and its impact on GT. By developing a predictive model for GT, we offer new insights into spinal biomechanics, highlighting the importance of developing personalized assessment and treatment strategies. This study advances the understanding of spinal health challenges in aging populations and can serve as a vital reference for clinicians and researchers for addressing the complexities of spinal alignment in this vulnerable demographic.
