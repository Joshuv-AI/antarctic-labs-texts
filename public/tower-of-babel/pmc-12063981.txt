Pheochromocytoma presenting with recurrent syncope, prolonged QT interval and macroscopic T-wave alternans
Authors: Raghav Bansal, Bhavik Dhirawani, Chetan Rathi, Yash Lokhandwala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn 11-year-old boy presented with recurrent exertional syncope for 1 month. The baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) suggested a diagnosis of long QT syndrome with macroscopic T-wave alternans. Volatility of blood pressure and left ventricular hypertrophy triggered further investigations, revealing pheochromocytoma as the primary cause. The child underwent laparoscopic resection of the tumor with subsequent resolution of ECG changes and symptoms. The genetic testing was negative for known mutations implicated with prolonged QT interval.

INTRODUCTION

Long QT syndrome is an important cause of malignant arrhythmias leading to syncope and sudden cardiac death, especially in children. This genetic disease remains incurable yet and requires lifelong palliative treatment with beta-blockers, cardiac sympathetic denervation, and rarely implantable cardioverter defibrillators. T-wave alternans in the presence of long QT syndrome is an ominous sign and warrants aggressive treatment. Pheochromocytoma has been known to mimic at least 30 medical disorders.[1] However, pheochromocytoma causing Torsades de Pointes (TdP) in association with prolonged QTc interval has been rarely reported. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case demonstrating macroscopic T-wave alternans secondary to pheochromocytoma.

CASE REPORT

An 11-year-old boy, born out of a consanguineous marriage, presented with recurrent episodes of palpitations, sweating, and exertional syncope over 1 month. The examination was unremarkable. The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed a grossly prolonged QTc (Bazzett) interval of 622 ms with T-wave alternans [Figure 1]. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death. A clinical diagnosis of long QT syndrome was made with a modified Schwartz score of 8. The child was immediately started on metoprolol and cardiac sympathetic denervation was contemplated. The ECG later settled with the absence of T-wave alternans after starting metoprolol 25 mg twice daily [Figure 2a]. However, echocardiography surprisingly revealed gross left ventricular hypertrophy (diastolic wall thickness of 16 mm) with normal systolic function, without outflow tract obstruction. During the hospital stay, the child was noted to have episodes of ill-sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (PMVT) [Figure 2b], as also significant fluctuations in heart rate and blood pressure. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [Figure 2c] revealed concentric hypertrophy without any anomalous papillary muscles or late gadolinium enhancement. A clinical suspicion of pheochromocytoma in view of left ventricular hypertrophy and episodic hypertension, triggered further investigations. A raised urinary vanillylmandelic acid to creatinine ratio of 12.77 mg/g (normal <8.0 mg/g) was found. This, along with a 3.3 cm × 3.0 cm × 3.4 cm contrast-enhancing mass arising from the left adrenal gland on an abdominal CT scan, clinched the diagnosis of a pheochromocytoma. Subsequently, prazosin was added, which improved the blood pressure control and surprisingly shortened the QT interval also, though it remained elevated. In view of the tumor occurring at a young age, a suspicion of a syndromic involvement was raised. However, the family history was negative for any tumor or endocrine abnormalities. There were no skin or mucosal lesions, skeletal abnormalities, thyroid swelling, or eye abnormalities. His thyroid profile was also normal. Further, a 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT ruled out any other simultaneous tumor origin or metastasis site.

The child underwent laparoscopic excision of tumor [Figure 3a]. During surgery, the hypertensive storm was managed using nitroprusside and labetalol infusion. Histopathologic analysis [Figure 3b] of the specimen revealed tumor cells expressing synaptophysin, chromogranin, and S-100 protein, confirming the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma. Postoperatively, the QT interval returned to normal [Figure 3c], the ventricular hypertrophy regressed, and the child remained asymptomatic without any medications at the 2-year follow-up. Later genetic testing was also done which failed to reveal any of the following mutations causing QT prolongation: AKAP9, ANK2, CACNA1C, CACNA2D1, CACNB2, CAV3, KCNE1, KCNE2, KCNH2, KCNJ2, KCNQ1, MYBPC3, SCN4B, SCN5A, SNTA1.

DISCUSSION

Pheochromocytoma is a rare chromaffin cell tumor arising from the adrenal glands. Collectively, pheochromocytoma and paragangliomas have an incidence of 0.6 cases per 100,000 patient-years.[2] The classic clinical presentation entails a triad of episodic headaches, palpitations, and profuse sweating, secondary to the pharmacological effects of catecholamines released by the tumor cells.[1] Around 50%–70% of patients report palpitations, while sinus tachycardia is the most common rhythm abnormality noted.[3] Besides these common abnormalities, there are a plethora of cardiovascular abnormalities associated with pheochromocytoma, including left ventricular hypertrophy, hypertensive crisis, acute coronary syndrome, dilated cardiomyopathy, repolarization abnormalities associated with prolonged QTc interval, ventricular arrhythmias including both monomorphic and PMVT, takotsubo cardiomyopathy and sudden cardiac death.[3] In addition, ST-T abnormalities and QT prolongation have been reported commonly in a few case series.[45] A more contemporary retrospective study noted a 45% prevalence of QT prolongation amongst 20 pheochromocytoma patients. QTc prolongation was found to be associated with male gender and left ventricular hypertrophy, as in our case.[6] Further, the shortening of the QTc interval and normalization of ST-T changes with resection of the catecholamine-secreting tumor has also been reported.[7] It is noteworthy that although QT prolongation is common secondary to a pheochromocytoma, presentation with polymorphic VT is limited to a handful of case reports. In the present case, the documented ill-sustained runs of PMVT make TdP the likely cause of exertional syncope.

Although the catecholaminergic effect has been implicated in prolonging QT interval and consequent TdP, the exact molecular mechanism is poorly understood. The role of beta-adrenergic receptors seems intuitive; however, the excess stimulation of alpha-adrenergic receptors has also been implicated with repolarization abnormalities and arrhythmias in animal models.[89] Thus, beta-blockade may not be enough in improving QT intervals and preventing arrhythmias. In the present case, further shortening of the QT interval was noted with the institution of prazosin, an alpha-blocking agent. This has important management implications pertaining to QT prolongation in a case of pheochromocytoma.

T-wave alternans has been reported as an ominous sign in patients with long QT syndrome heralding the onset of malignant arrhythmias.[10] Alternation of the action potential duration of the M-cells consequent to the beat-to-beat alternans of calcium release handling has been suggested as the cause of the T-wave alternans.[11] The macroscopic T-wave alternans associated with dangerously prolonged QT intervals secondary to pheochromocytoma as in this case has not been reported before. The negative genetic testing and complete normalization of QT interval on follow-up after tumor resection conclusively prove the pheochromocytoma as the sole cause of the QT prolongation in this case. This association of macroscopic T-wave alternans with pheochromocytoma-induced QT prolongation is a novel finding.

With a classical clinical presentation and a modified Schwartz score of 8, the diagnosis of long QT syndrome seemed secure. It was easy to anchor to this diagnosis and manage accordingly. However, a high index of suspicion raised by the clinical clues, including left ventricular hypertrophy and blood pressure fluctuations followed by appropriate tests, pointed to successful diagnosis and management. The other differential diagnosis here would be the rare possibility of cardiac-only Timothy syndrome secondary to CACNA1C mutation.[12] Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy per se has also been reported to be associated with QT prolongation >480 ms in 13% of the patients,[13] but not reported to be associated with such massive QT prolongation as in our patient, with consequent TdP. This case highlights the importance of recognizing pheochromocytoma as a reversible cause of an ominous “long QT syndrome” presentation with macroscopic T-wave alternans and TdP.
