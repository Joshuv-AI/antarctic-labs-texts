Evaluating Oxaliplatin Exposure in Adults with Advanced Colorectal Cancer: Development and Validation of Methods with Enhanced Bioanalytical Specificity and Clinical Applicability
Authors: John Ho, Catherine Han, Mark James McKeage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe clinical efficacy and adverse effects of oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)(oxalato)] (DACH = 1R,2R-cyclohexanediamine) may depend upon systemic drug exposure quantified as the area under the plasma concentration versus time curve (AUC). Most previous oxaliplatin pharmacokinetic studies measured total platinum exposure without separating intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] from its inactive biotransformation products, and estimated AUC using intensive sampling methods unsuitable for routine clinical application. In the current study, we aimed to (1) evaluate systemic exposure to intact oxaliplatin and (2) develop and validate enhanced methods for estimating oxaliplatin AUC in adults with advanced colorectal cancer.MethodsTwo oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic datasets were analyzed. The first dataset included 19 patients and 38 treatment cycles from our previous clinical trial (ATCRN12611000738921). The second dataset included ten patients from an independent published clinical study. In both datasets, oxaliplatin 85 or 130 mg/m2 was given by constant-rate intravenous infusion over 2 h. Reference AUCs for intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum were estimated by the trapezoidal rule using between 9 and 13 predefined concentration timepoints. End of infusion plasma concentrations were used to estimate AUC by equation-based methods. The accuracy of AUC estimations from end of infusion plasma concentrations was assessed in correlation plots and from their relative mean prediction error (MPE%) and relative root mean square prediction error (RMSE%).ResultsIntact oxaliplatin plasma concentration had almost reached steady state (> 95%) and most systemic exposure (70%) had already occurred by the end of infusion. Intact oxaliplatin accounted for 77% of the AUC of total unbound platinum. Intact oxaliplatin AUCs were dose-proportional, moderately variable between individuals (%CV = 18%), and linearly related to end of infusion plasma concentrations (y = 2.231x, R2 = 0.72). Validation studies showed acceptable levels of bias (MPE% < 15%) and imprecision (RMSE% < 20%) for estimating intact oxaliplatin AUC from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration.ConclusionsIntact oxaliplatin was the major pharmacologically active platinum species present in the systemic circulation of adults with advanced colorectal cancer in this study. Intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration offers a clinically practicable and potentially reliable method with enhanced bioanalytical specificity for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13318-026-01002-4.

Key Points

Introduction

Oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)(oxalato)] (DACH = 1R,2R-cyclohexanediamine) is a cytotoxic platinum compound used widely in oncology clinics for treating advanced colorectal cancer in both the adjuvant and metastatic settings [1]. Oxaliplatin is routinely given as a constant-rate intravenous infusion over 2 h at doses of 85 or 130 mg/m2 body surface area once every 2 or 3 weeks, together with fluorouracil and leucovorin or capecitabine in standard combination treatment regimens, such as mFOLFOX6 and XELOX [1]. Mechanistically, oxaliplatin undergoes biotransformation via substitution reactions involving the oxalate ligand, leading to its irreversible binding to the N7 of guanine and other sites of DNA purine bases. Formation of intra-strand platinum-DNA crosslinks then leads to inhibition of DNA replication and transcription and cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis [2]. Through this mechanism, oxaliplatin has potent in vitro pharmacological activity as an antitumor cytotoxin in cisplatin-resistant human colorectal tumor cell lines [3, 4], and significant clinical efficacy in adults with advanced colorectal cancer [5].

In plasma, oxaliplatin biotransformation results in the formation of complex mixtures of platinum species, including intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato], other pharmacologically active platinum species, and pharmacologically inactive unbound and plasma protein-bound platinum species, under standard treatment conditions [6, 7]. Most previous pharmacokinetic studies of oxaliplatin had measured total platinum concentrations of plasma or plasma ultrafiltrate, without separating intact oxaliplatin from other active or inactive platinum species that form from oxaliplatin in plasma [8, 9]. These previous pharmacokinetic studies revealed a triphasic decline of total unbound platinum concentration in plasma ultrafiltrate after the end of the oxaliplatin infusion corresponding to three main processes of platinum disposition [8]. In the first phase, intact oxaliplatin and other unbound pharmacologically active platinum species are rapidly and irreversibly bound to plasma proteins, blood cells, and tissues in association with an initial decline of total unbound platinum concentration in plasma ultrafiltrate with a half-life of 15–20 min. In the second phase, low-molecular-weight pharmacologically inactive platinum species, including Pt(DACH) conjugates of glutathione, l-methionine, and l-cysteine, are eliminated more slowly by glomerular filtration, associated with a second decline of total unbound platinum concentration in plasma ultrafiltrate with a half-life of about 16 h. In the final phase, protein-bound and tissue-sequestered pharmacologically inactive platinum species are very slowly released into the systemic circulation then removed, associated with a final phase of decline in total unbound platinum concentration in plasma ultrafiltrate with a half-life of 10 or more days. Due to this complexity of oxaliplatin disposition in plasma, direct measurement of the plasma concentration of pharmacologically active platinum species has not been routinely possible, and studies of oxaliplatin pharmacokinetics beyond determining total unbound platinum have been limited in scope [6, 10, 11]. Consequently, little is known about systemic exposure to intact oxaliplatin, its dose-proportionality, interindividual variability, and contributions to total platinum exposure in adults with advanced colorectal cancer.

Further to these bioanalytical limitations, other issues have impeded the evaluation of oxaliplatin exposure in adults with advanced colorectal cancer. The area under the plasma concentration versus time curve (AUC) is a critical determinant of the clinical efficacy and adverse effects of platinum anticancer drugs [12–16]. Platinum drug AUC estimation is required for evaluating drug–drug interactions, pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships [17], and for AUC-based dosing [18]. Most previous pharmacokinetic studies of oxaliplatin used methods for estimating oxaliplatin AUC requiring intensive blood sampling, such as the trapezoidal rule [17]. AUC estimation by the trapezoidal rule is based upon full definition of the plasma concentration versus time profile in its entirety, usually including 12 or more predefined concentration timepoints. Such intensive blood sampling is not feasible in most clinical settings where oxaliplatin is routinely given.

To support clinical studies of oxaliplatin drug–drug interactions (ACTRN12619000192189) and pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships (ACTRN12612000029897), we sought to establish clinically practicable methods for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure for use in everyday clinical settings where oxaliplatin is routinely given in outpatient oncology infusion clinics. Toward this end, we previously established procedures for processing and storing plasma samples that preserved oxaliplatin speciation, whereby plasma samples are processed instantly after collection from patients by methanol-deproteinization then storage of diluted plasma:methanol supernatants at −80 °C [19, 20]. Solvent deproteinization has been validated against ultrafiltration and shown to be equivalent for determinations of total unbound platinum plasma concentration [21]. However, solvent deproteinization has advantages over ultrafiltration of preserved platinum drug speciation, lower cost, higher sample yield and no requirement for single-use ultrafiltration devices. We also developed and validated high performance liquid chromatography-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (HPLC-ICP-MS) and ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-ICP-MS) bioanalytical methods for the quantification of intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] and its putative active biotransformation product [Pt(DACH)Cl2] [19, 20]. We then applied those enhanced sample processing procedures and bioanalytical techniques in a clinical trial that generated full time-course pharmacokinetic data for both intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum in a total of 19 patients and 38 treatment cycles [11]. In this clinical trial, oxaliplatin was given as a constant-rate intravenous infusion over 2 h at standard doses of 85 or 130 mg/m2 body surface area once every 2 or 3 weeks, together with fluorouracil and leucovorin or capecitabine in standard combination treatment regimens, such as mFOLFOX6 and XELOX. In our previous clinical studies, [Pt(DACH)Cl2] was undetectable in plasma of patients treated with oxaliplatin, with no significant platinum peaks corresponding to [Pt(DACH)Cl2] retention time seen in plasma samples from patients treated with oxaliplatin, despite bioanalytical assays having limits of detection for [Pt(DACH)Cl2] of 0.1 μM or less, and [Pt(DACH)Cl2] being stable during processing and storage of methanol-deproteinized human plasma samples [19, 20]. These findings suggest that intact oxaliplatin itself may be the major pharmacologically active platinum species present in the systemic circulation after oxaliplatin treatment. In the current study, we analyzed that oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic dataset with the aims of (1) evaluating systemic exposure to intact oxaliplatin and (2) developing and validating enhanced methods for estimating oxaliplatin AUC in adults with advanced colorectal cancer. We hypothesized that oxaliplatin systemic exposure could be conveniently and reliably estimated from the end of infusion plasma concentration of intact oxaliplatin.

Methods

Pharmacokinetic Datasets

The main oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic dataset used in this analysis originated from our clinical trial (ChAMPION study), whose primary results we published previously [11]. The clinical trial was approved by the New Zealand Government Ministry of Health Northern Y Regional Ethics Committee (approval number NTY/11/01/005) and conducted according to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before any study procedures, participants gave written informed consent. Participants were recruited to the trial from 16 June 2011 to 5 July 2012. The clinical trial was registered (ACTRN12611000738921). This dataset was composed of pharmacokinetic data from 19 adults with advanced colorectal cancer, from their first and second treatment cycles of oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy and a total of 38 treatment cycles. For each treatment cycle, oxaliplatin was administered as a constant-rate intravenous infusion over 2 h using a rate-controlled infusion pump. Blood samples were collected at a total of 13 timepoints during (at 0, 20, 40, 60, and 90 min after the start of the infusion), at the end of the infusion, and after the end of the infusion (at 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min). Blood samples were centrifuged immediately after their collection from the patient to prepare plasma [19, 20]. Then plasma was processed immediately at the bedside to preserve the speciation of oxaliplatin and remove plasma protein and protein-bound platinum. Plasma was processed by dilution in ice-cold methanol (plasma:methanol 1:2 v/v), deproteinized by centrifugation, then plasma:methanol supernatants were collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen before storage at −80 °C. As reported previously [19, 20], intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] was stable in processed plasma samples (methanol-deproteinized diluted plasma (plasma:methanol 1:2 v/v)) stored at −80 °C for at least 473 days and while in the autosampler at ambient temperature for up to 6.7 h. Plasma concentrations of intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum were determined by HPLC-ICP-MS [20] and ICP-MS [22], respectively. Bioanalytical validation parameters for these assays, including calibration curve linearity, accuracy, and precision, both within and between runs, were within the range required by drug regulatory authorities [23]. Intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum AUCs were estimated for each pharmacokinetic study using the linear trapezoidal rule up to the last concentration timepoint. In a sensitivity analysis, the log-linear trapezoidal rule gave AUC values within ± 1% of those from the linear trapezoidal rule. For linear trapezoidal estimations of AUC, concentration values lower than the limit of quantification of the assay were recorded as zero. Only 4 of 494 (0.8%) concentration values were missing for intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum. For timepoints with missing concentration data, interpolated concentration values were estimated from one-compartment constant-rate intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model equations fitted to the remaining concentration versus time data for the subject concerned using Pkweb [24]. Inclusion of interpolated concentration values made no difference (< 1%) to the reference AUC values except when the final concentration timepoint was missing when its replacement by an interpolated value increased reference AUC values by up to 4.3%. The clinical characteristics of ChAMPION study patients and their treatment were as previously described [11] and are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. As previously reported [11], the ChAMPION study was a double blind crossover study of adults with advanced colorectal cancer undergoing oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy who were randomized to receive study treatment with calcium and magnesium infusions (1 g Ca Gluconate plus 1 g MgSO4 in 100 mL of 5% dextrose) on cycle one and placebo (100 mL of 5% dextrose) on cycle two, or to receive the same treatments in the opposite sequence. The primary endpoint was intact oxaliplatin AUC. The ratio of geometric mean intact oxaliplatin AUC, from when oxaliplatin was given with calcium and magnesium infusions divided by that from when oxaliplatin was given with placebo, was close to one with 90% confidence intervals overlapping one (ratio of geometric mean intact oxaliplatin AUC (0.95; 90% CI 0.90–1.01)), and fell within a predefined no-effect boundary of 0.8–1.25. It was therefore concluded that calcium and magnesium infusions did not alter the pharmacokinetics of oxaliplatin in the ChAMPION study [11].

A second oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic dataset was used as an independent dataset for external validation studies [10]. This dataset was composed of ten patients with metastatic colorectal cancer who were treated with oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 by intravenous infusion over 2 h. Blood samples were collected at a total of nine timepoints during (at 10, 30, 90, and 110 min after the start of the infusion) and after the end of (at 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60 min) the infusion. Concentrations of intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] in plasma ultrafiltrate were determined by liquid chromatography in combination with post-column derivatization and ultraviolet radiation (UV) detection in this published independent study. AUC values were calculated using WIN NONLIN by a method that was otherwise not specified. For the current analysis, intact oxaliplatin AUC and maximum plasma concentration values were taken from Table 2 of the report [10], and intact oxaliplatin end of infusion and maximum plasma concentrations were assumed to be equal.

Estimating AUC from End of Infusion Plasma Concentration

For estimating AUC from end of infusion plasma concentration, two equation-based methods were evaluated, a steady-state equation method and a linear equation method. Population pharmacokinetic modeling was not required because sample-rich timepoint-dense concentration versus time data were available for each study patient and treatment cycle, and the study populations accurately reflected those eligible for routine treatment with oxaliplatin. In addition, initial analysis of interpatient variation of dose-adjusted intact oxaliplatin AUC values revealed insufficient pharmacokinetic variation (%CV < 20%) for meaningful population pharmacokinetic analysis of associations with patient covariates. The steady-state- and linear-equation-based methods evaluated in this study are described below.

Steady-State Equation Method

The steady-state equation method for estimating AUC from the end of infusion plasma concentration was conceived from the principles of a standard one-compartment intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model [17]. In this model (Supplementary Fig. 1), drug enters the compartment at a constant rate (infusion rate, k0,iv) for a fixed duration (infusion duration, t = 0 to T, where T is the time at the end of infusion). Drug is eliminated from the compartment at a rate (elimination rate, γelim) determined by the concentration of drug in the compartment (Cp) and the proportionality constant clearance (CL), as in Eq. 1. Accordingly, as the constant-rate infusion continues, plasma concentrations rise increasingly slowly as the rate of drug elimination increases with increasing plasma concentration. If the infusion continues, eventually a steady state is reached, when the rate of drug infusion exactly equals the rate of drug elimination and there is no net change in plasma concentration. Since at steady-state, the rate of drug elimination exactly equals the rate of drug infusion, and plasma concentrations equal steady-state levels, Eq. 1 can be rewritten as Eq. 2 by replacing elimination rate with infusion rate, which is known, and replacing plasma concentration with steady-state plasma concentration (Css) or end of infusion plasma concentration (CT), which can be measured. In the linear equation method for estimating AUC from end of infusion plasma concentration, AUC is estimated from the slope (m) of a linear association between AUC and end of infusion plasma concentration, as in Eq. 7. This linear equation method requires the slope (m) of this linear association to have been estimated precisely by regression analysis of a reference pharmacokinetic dataset. In this method, the linear association intercepts the x- and y-axes at zero, since it is implausible for a non-zero AUC to coexist with zero end of infusion plasma concentration.

Statistical Analyses

For graphical presentation of the time course of intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] and total unbound platinum plasma concentration, plasma concentrations were first normalized to the 130 mg/m2 dose level by multiplying the measured plasma concentration by the quotient of 130 divided by the actual dose expressed as mg/m2, before calculation of means and standard deviations for the concentration at each timepoint. One-compartment intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model equations were fitted by nonlinear regression to dose-normalized plasma concentration versus time data using Eq. 8 during the infusion and Eq. 9 after the end of infusion. The significance of differences in AUC values between different oxaliplatin doses and treatment cycles was assessed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Normal distribution of the data was confirmed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For the assessment of interpatient and intrapatient variability, AUC values were first normalized to the 130 mg/m2 dose level as described above. For interpatient variability, the percent coefficient of variation (%CV = SD/mean expressed as percentage) was calculated for intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum dose-normalized AUCs separately for treatment cycles 1 and 2. For intrapatient variation, %CVs for intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum dose-normalized AUCs were calculated for each patient, then the mean and range of intrapatient %CV values for the study population were estimated. Correlations between linear trapezoidal AUC and end of infusion plasma concentration were assessed by linear regression with intercepts fixed at zero. The accuracy of the steady-state and linear equation methods for estimating AUC from the end of infusion plasma concentration was assessed visually by comparing trapezoidal and estimated AUCs for each pharmacokinetic study in correlation plots with reference to the line of identity. Bias was further assessed by calculating the relative mean prediction error (MPE%) as in Eq. 10. Imprecision was further assessed by calculating the relative root mean square prediction error (RMSE%) as in Eq. 11.

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) or Microsoft Excel for Mac Version 16.92 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). p-Values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Treatment

The main oxaliplatin pharmacokinetic dataset used for this study included 19 patients whose baseline characteristics and treatment are detailed by patient in Supplementary Table 2. The dataset included 12 male and 7 female patients with ages ranging from 33 to 77 years, baseline body weight ranging from 47.2 to 114 kg, and serum creatinine ranging from 54 to 107 µmol/L. Oxaliplatin was given at standard doses of either 85 or 130 mg/m2 on two occasions (treatment cycles 1 and 2), with the exception of patient 17, whose oxaliplatin dose was reduced from 130 to 118 mg/m2 for treatment cycle 2, and patient 18, whose oxaliplatin dose was reduced to 97.5 mg/m2 for both treatment cycles 1 and 2. Pharmacokinetic data from 19 patients and 38 treatment cycles were thereby available for analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Intact Oxaliplatin Pharmacokinetics

Plasma concentrations of intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] were measured in samples of methanol-deproteinized plasma. AUC values were estimated using the linear trapezoidal rule using 13 predefined concentration timepoints for 38 treatment cycles (Supplementary Table 1). Graphical representation of the time course of dose-normalized intact oxaliplatin concentrations revealed plasma concentrations almost having reached steady-state and most systemic exposure having already occurred at the end of infusion (Fig. 1a). A one-compartment intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model (Eqs. 8 and 9) was fitted (R2 = 0.99) by nonlinear regression to the data with the following parameters (steady-state concentration (Css) = 7.7 μM; k = 2.2 h−1). At the end of the oxaliplatin infusion, measured plasma concentrations of intact oxaliplatin (CT = 7.4 ± 1.7 μM) were very close to model-estimated steady-state concentration (Css = 7.7 μM). Of the total AUC for intact oxaliplatin (AUC0–last = 17.0 μmol/L h), 70% occurred before the end of infusion (AUC0–T = 11.9 μmol/L h) and 30% occurred after the end of infusion (AUCT–last = 5.1 μmol/L h).

Intact oxaliplatin AUC values differed significantly between the two oxaliplatin dose levels but not between the two treatment cycles (Fig. 1b) (two-way ANOVA: dose level, p = 0.0005; cycle NS). Intact oxaliplatin AUC values differed in proportion (1.4-fold) to the difference between the two oxaliplatin dose levels (1.5-fold). In a sensitivity analysis, the dose-proportionality of oxaliplatin exposure was unchanged after excluding patient 18 treatment cycles 1 and 2 from the 85 mg/m2 dose level (received 97.5 mg/m2), and patient 17 treatment cycle 2 from the 130 mg/m2 dose level (received 118 mg/m2) (Supplementary Fig. 5). For dose-normalized intact oxaliplatin AUC values, %CV values for between- and within-patient variability were approximately 18% and 8%, respectively (Table 1). Intact oxaliplatin AUC correlated with the end of infusion plasma concentration (y = 2.231x; R2 = 0.72 (Fig. 1c)). The slope of the linear association between intact oxaliplatin AUC and the end of infusion plasma concentration (2.231 h; 95% CI 2.143–2.320 h) was numerically similar to the infusion duration (t = 2 h). The relationship between intact oxaliplatin AUC and end of infusion plasma concentration was the same for dose-normalized values (y = 2.254x; 95% CI 2.166–2.341 h) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Interpatient and intrapatient variability in oxaliplatin exposure

Intact oxaliplatin accounted for disproportionate amounts of total unbound platinum plasma concentration and systemic exposure with time (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Intact oxaliplatin accounted for 77% of the total unbound platinum AUC occurring between the first and last concentration timepoints (AUC0–last) (Table 1). However, intact oxaliplatin accounted for 87% of total unbound platinum systemic exposure occurring during the infusion (AUC0–T) compared with 63% occurring after the end of infusion (AUCT–last) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Intact oxaliplatin accounted for 99%, 81%, and 38% of the total unbound platinum plasma concentration at 0.33, 2, and 5 h after the commencement of the infusion, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Total unbound platinum AUC showed similar dose-proportionality and correlation with end of infusion plasma concentration as intact oxaliplatin (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Estimating AUC from End of Infusion Plasma Concentration

Intact oxaliplatin AUC estimated from end of infusion plasma concentration by the steady-state and linear equation methods correlated closely with intact oxaliplatin AUC estimated by the linear trapezoidal rule using 13 predefined concentration timepoints. For the steady-state equation method, correlation plots of estimated intact oxaliplatin AUC values versus those generated by the linear trapezoidal rule showed data values clustered close by but slightly below the line of identity (Fig. 2a). The bias (MPE%) of the steady-state equation method was −13.2% (95% CI −9.3% to −17.1%) and its imprecision (RMSE%) was 17.6% for estimating the AUC of intact oxaliplatin from the end of infusion plasma concentration. For the linear equation method, correlation plots of estimated intact oxaliplatin AUC values versus those generated by the linear trapezoidal rule showed data values clustered close by and evenly around the line of identity (Fig. 2b). The bias (MPE%) of the linear equation method was −3.2% (95% CI −7.5% to 1.2%) and its imprecision (RMSE%) was 13.4% for estimating the AUC of intact oxaliplatin from end of infusion plasma concentration. For total unbound platinum, correlation plots of AUC values estimated by the steady-state and linear equation methods showed similar trends, but with wider spread of data values compared with intact oxaliplatin (Supplementary Fig. 4). Both the steady-state and linear equation methods estimated oxaliplatin AUC by multiplying the end of infusion plasma concentration by a numerical coefficient that had units of time and whose value was numerically similar to the infusion duration.

External Validation

External validation studies were carried out using an independent oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic dataset [10]. Dose-normalized intact oxaliplatin AUC values from the independent dataset had a %CV value for interpatient variability of 15.7%. Intact oxaliplatin AUC (161 ± 23 μg min/mL) closely correlated with maximum plasma concentration (1.44 ± 0.20 μg/mL) of intact oxaliplatin (y = 112.2x; R2 = 0.93) (Fig. 3a). The slope of the linear association between intact oxaliplatin AUC and maximum plasma concentration (112.2 min; 95% CI 109–115 min) was numerically similar to the infusion duration (t = 120 min). Correlation plots of intact oxaliplatin AUC values estimated by the steady-state equation method versus the measured AUC values showed data values clustered close by but slightly above the line of identity (Fig. 3b). The bias (MPE%) of the steady-state equation method was 6.9% (95% CI 4.6–9.3%) and its imprecision (RMSE%) was 7.8%, for estimating the AUC of intact oxaliplatin from its maximum plasma concentration in this independent dataset. Correlation plots of intact oxaliplatin AUC values estimated by the linear equation method from the slope (m; estimated from the main oxaliplatin pharmacokinetic dataset) versus the measured AUC values showed data values clustered clearly above the line of identity (Fig. 3c). The bias (MPE%) of the linear equation method was 19.3% (95% CI 16.7–21.9%) and its imprecision (RMSE%) was 19.7%, indicating lower reliability for estimating the AUC of intact oxaliplatin from its maximum plasma concentration in this independent dataset compared with the steady-state equation method.

Discussion

Intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] was the major pharmacologically active platinum species present in the systemic circulation of adults receiving treatment with oxaliplatin for advanced colorectal cancer in this study. Concentrations of both intact oxaliplatin and total unbound platinum were measured in samples of methanol-deproteinized plasma collected from study participants. Intact oxaliplatin accounted for 77% of the AUC of total unbound platinum occurring between the first and last concentration timepoints. Intact oxaliplatin itself has potent in vitro pharmacological activity as an antitumor cytotoxin in cisplatin-resistant human tumor cell lines [3, 4, 7]. In contrast, other platinum species formed from oxaliplatin in plasma, such as Pt(DACH) amino acid conjugates, have lower pharmacological activity [25] and will contribute less (≤ 33%) to the total unbound platinum AUC. In our previous clinical studies, [Pt(DACH)Cl2], a putative active biotransformation product of oxaliplatin, was undetectable in plasma of patients treated with oxaliplatin and therefore is unlikely to contribute significantly to total unbound platinum AUC. Contributions of other active platinum species, such as [Pt(DACH)Cl(oxalato)], [Pt(DACH)Cl(OH)], and [Pt(DACH)Cl(OH)2], cannot be ruled out or defined because these platinum species are highly unstable and authentic standards of them are not readily available. Overall, platinum species formed from oxaliplatin in plasma will contribute less than intact oxaliplatin itself to clinical effects of oxaliplatin treatment because of their lower systemic exposures and reduced pharmacological activities.

Compared with intact oxaliplatin, total unbound platinum may be less reliable for estimating oxaliplatin systemic exposure because of time-dependent biases in determining total unbound platinum AUC and its disproportionate relationship with intact oxaliplatin. Intact oxaliplatin systemic exposure was complete within a few hours of the end of infusion in this and other studies [10]. In contrast, total unbound platinum remains measurable in plasma for weeks after oxaliplatin infusion due to the persistence of inactive species within the systemic circulation. Consequently, total unbound platinum AUC values are highly dependent upon how far the exposure estimate is extended post-infusion. As an example, in a previous pharmacokinetic study of oxaliplatin in adults with advanced cancer [8], total unbound platinum AUC values extrapolated to infinity (AUC0–∞) were about double those estimated from the same concentration versus time data but truncated at 48 h post-infusion (AUC0–48). In the current study, intact oxaliplatin accounted for disproportionate amounts of total unbound platinum plasma concentration and systemic exposure with time. Therefore, intact oxaliplatin plasma concentrations and systemic exposures do not simply reflect those of total unbound platinum, and conversely total unbound platinum plasma concentrations and systemic exposures do not simply reflect those of intact oxaliplatin. For evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure, intact oxaliplatin is less prone to time-dependent bias than total unbound platinum because intact oxaliplatin systemic exposure is complete within a few hours of the end of infusion. For evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure, estimation of intact oxaliplatin AUC offers a more specific bioanalytical method compared with total unbound platinum AUC, because all inactive platinum species are excluded from the determination.

Intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] systemic exposures were dose-proportional and moderately variable between adults with advanced colorectal cancer given standard doses of oxaliplatin in this study. Intact oxaliplatin AUC was measured in patients given either one of two different doses of oxaliplatin (85 or 130 mg/m2) on two different occasions (treatment cycles 1 and 2). Intact oxaliplatin AUC differed significantly and proportionally to the difference between the two doses of oxaliplatin but were similar between the two treatment cycles. Intact oxaliplatin AUC was moderately variable (%CV about 18%) [26] between subjects in this study. This dose-proportional but variable systemic exposure to intact oxaliplatin may contribute to determining the adverse effects and tumor responses from treatment with oxaliplatin, which are dose-related but otherwise unpredictable in adults with advanced colorectal cancer [5, 27, 28].

Intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration offers a clinically practicable method for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure. For this method, a single blood sample is drawn at the end of the oxaliplatin infusion, then processed immediately in clinic to prepare a sample of deproteinized diluted methanol–plasma supernatant that is snap frozen in liquid nitrogen in preparation for longer-term storage. For this method, the patient is required to remain in the treatment facility no longer than it would take them to receive their intended treatment. Clinical staff will be available at the predefined time of blood sampling as they prepare the patient for completing the oxaliplatin infusion and leaving the treatment clinic. Laboratory staff and facilities are required for sample processing in the clinic for up to 1 h after blood sampling for each intact oxaliplatin AUC determination, although samples from different patients could be processed concurrently. In contrast, conventional approaches to estimating oxaliplatin exposure require the collection and processing of multiple blood samples taken during and after the oxaliplatin infusion to fully define the plasma concentration versus time curve in its entirety for AUC estimation, for example, by the trapezoid rule. The additional resources, time, costs, and inconvenience involved with intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration are much less than conventional approaches requiring more intensive blood sampling.

Intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration was a potentially reliable method for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure in this study. This steady-state-equation-based method for estimating intact oxaliplatin AUC was derived conceptually from the principles of a standard one-compartment intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model, then its accuracy was assessed in two independent oxaliplatin pharmacokinetic datasets by comparison with reference AUCs calculated using the trapezoidal rule from multiple concentration timepoints. In those two validation datasets, intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration using the steady-state-equation-based method had levels of bias (MPE%) of −13.2 and 6.9% and imprecision (RMSE%) of 17.6 and 7.8%. This degree of inaccuracy may be clinically acceptable as it is similar to the levels of bias and imprecision of other equation-based methods used in routine clinical practice, such as those for estimating glomerular filtration rate [29] and carboplatin exposure [30, 31] in patients with cancer. In contrast, intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from end of infusion plasma concentration by the linear-equation-based method had higher levels of bias (MPE%) of 19.3% and imprecision (RMSE%) of 19.7% in external validation studies, suggesting this approach was less reliable and would require further prospective calibration.

The steady-state-equation-based method for estimating oxaliplatin exposure may have advantages over population pharmacokinetic modeling approaches. Estimating intact oxaliplatin exposure from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration is a simple and intuitive method that will be easy for clinicians to understand and use in clinical settings where oxaliplatin is given routinely, without any requirement for pharmacometrics technical support. This steady-state-equation-based method was conceived and developed from the principles of a standard one-compartment intravenous infusion pharmacokinetic model. In contrast, population pharmacokinetic models of intact oxaliplatin have not yet been described and are therefore currently unavailable for supporting maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation of intact oxaliplatin systemic exposure. In the current study, the steady-state equation method appeared potentially reliable for estimating intact oxaliplatin systemic exposure in validation studies undertaken in two independent oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic datasets. In contrast, none of the population pharmacokinetic models of total unbound platinum from oxaliplatin described to date [32–35] have been externally validated. Concerningly in these regards, these population pharmacokinetic models of total unbound platinum from oxaliplatin performed erratically and generated widely disparate estimates of oxaliplatin exposure in a simulation study [36]. In this context, the steady-state-equation-based method for estimating intact oxaliplatin AUC from end of infusion plasma concentrations warrants further investigation and prospective external validation studies in other independent pharmacokinetic datasets.

This study had several limitations and findings requiring further clarification in future studies. Differences between the two oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic datasets used in this study could have introduced bias. For example, the second dataset used a different bioanalytical method, expressed intact oxaliplatin concentrations and AUC values in different units, and did not include comparisons with total unbound platinum. However, intact oxaliplatin concentrations and AUC values were comparable between the two datasets after adjustment for different units of measurement. The second dataset also involved a different patient population of adults with advanced colorectal cancer but with broadly similar clinical characteristics such as age (49–77 years) and body weight (55–107 kg). Considering the low level of interpatient variability (%CV < 20%) in intact oxaliplatin AUC, differences in clinical characteristics between the study populations seem unlikely to have affected the study results. Merging the datasets via modeling was not considered because individual patient concentration timepoint data were not available from the second dataset for this analysis. No intact oxaliplatin exposure–response or exposure–toxicity relationship data were available within the oxaliplatin clinical pharmacokinetic datasets used in this analysis. Currently, limited information exists about exposure–response relationships, exposure–toxicity relationships, therapeutic ranges, or exposure thresholds for oxaliplatin in adults with advanced colorectal cancer. Progress in these areas has been impeded by the lack of clinically practicable and reliable methods with high bioanalytical specificity for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure. The enhanced method for estimating oxaliplatin exposure described in this manuscript represents a significant step forward in these regards.

Conclusions

Intact oxaliplatin [Pt(DACH)oxalato] was the major pharmacologically active platinum species present in the systemic circulation of adults undergoing treatment with oxaliplatin for advanced colorectal cancer in this study. Intact oxaliplatin AUC estimation from the end of infusion plasma concentration multiplied by the infusion duration offers a clinically practicable and potentially reliable method with enhanced bioanalytical specificity for evaluating oxaliplatin systemic exposure.
