Polymicrobial submandibular abscess by anaerobic bacteria: A rare case report
Authors: Priyanka Gupta, Ashish William, Oves Siddiqui, Sonal Saxena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeep neck space infections are infections in the potential spaces of the neck that can lead to the formation of abscesses, the most common site being the submandibular gland. These infections commonly are odontogenic in origin and are polymicrobial. Infection being polymicrobial can be caused by both aerobes and anaerobes, where anaerobes are present as normal microbiota and become infectious when there is a breach in the natural barrier. Anaerobes are commonly neglected due to their fastidious nature, stringent transportation, conventional culture methods, and prolonged incubation. Identifying anaerobic infections in deep neck infections is crucial as it can result in potential severity and complications if not treated. With the advent of antimicrobial resistance against anaerobes, it is important to perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing for anaerobes. We report a polymicrobial submandibular abscess caused by Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes, which were identified in the pus specimen of the patient, and were sensitive to metronidazole, piperacillin–tazobactam, meropenem, and clindamycin.

INTRODUCTION

Deep neck infections are a serious medical concern as they can rapidly spread to various neck regions. The submandibular area is most commonly affected, accounting for 67% of cases. This is followed by the lateral pharyngeal space (causing pharyngeal abscess-17%), floor of mouth and neck (leading to Ludwig’s angina -12%), and the retropharyngeal space (causing retropharyngeal abscess- 4%).[1] These infections often originate from odontogenic causes (53.2%) and upper airway issues (30.5%).[2] Submandibular abscesses, a prevalent manifestation, typically result from polymicrobial infections involving both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. Common anaerobic bacteria include Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, and Peptostreptococcus. Aerobic organisms commonly implicated are Group A streptococcus, viridans streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus, and Haemophilus influenzae.[3] Identifying anaerobic infections in deep neck infections is challenging due to the polymicrobial nature and the complexity of isolating anaerobic bacteria which involves anaerobic transport conditions, prolonged incubation, and conventional culture techniques. This complexity is compounded by the need to differentiate pathogenic anaerobes from commensal ones, as anaerobes are a part of the normal microbiota.

In clinical practice, it is crucial for clinicians to meticulously collect specimens in a way that avoids contamination from normal flora. This careful collection is essential for accurately identifying and treating pathogenic anaerobes. In this context, we describe a case of a submandibular abscess that developed as a sequelae of a dental abscess caused by anaerobes. These anaerobes were able to invade due to a breach in the mucosal barrier. The causative agents in this case were identified as Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes.

This case highlights the critical need to differentiate between pathogenic and commensal anaerobes. Clinicians are essential in managing these infections by understanding the role anaerobes play in the disease process and utilizing proper specimen collection methods. This careful approach facilitates accurate diagnosis and effective treatment, leading to better patient outcomes in deep neck infections.

CASE REPORT

Clinical presentation

A 64-year-old man came to the emergency department with history of swelling in right side of jaw which was associated with pain since 3 days. He also had history of high-grade fever since 3 days which was not associated with chills and rigor and relieved temporarily on taking antipyretics. The patient was diagnosed case of dental caries in right lower molar tooth since 4 days prior to swelling. However, the patient was on irregular treatment for his dental infection and the swelling in right side of jaw emerged subsequently which had further spread to face and neck region. On physical examination, the right-sided swelling in the submandibular region was around 8 × 6 cm in size. The swelling was confined from the angle of mouth to the hyoid bone inferiorly, and from the right side of the neck to the midline [Figure 1a]. On palpation, the swelling was firm, tender, fluctuant, compressible, and erythematous clinically which was not associated with bleeding, discharge, difficulty swallowing, difficulty breathing, or any voice change. The patient’s mouth opening was limited to about 3 finger breadth. The CT scan was done which revealed a soft tissue swelling in the right submandibular region [Figure 1b].

Microbiological investigation

The pus sample was aspirated from swelling aseptically with the help of a syringe and transported to a microbiological laboratory for anaerobic culture in Robertson-cooked meat broth within one hour. Gram stain from sample revealed gram-negative bacilli with a field full of pus cells. The specimen was inoculated on sheep blood agar (with vitamin K1 and 5% hemin) and McConkey agar and incubated in anaerobic jar at 37°C under anaerobic conditions for 48 hours along with along with metronidazole disc (5 µg) according to the routine laboratory protocol. The anaerobic conditions were maintained in the jar through Anoxomat (automated anaerobic gas generation system). After 48 hours, two types of colonies were observed and further isolated on sheep blood agar [Figure 2]. The first colony was round, shiny, convex, and white colonies. The second colony was small, circular, transparent, shiny, smooth and non-hemolytic in appearance. A zone of inhibition around the metronidazole disc was observed after 48 hours of anaerobic incubation. Gram-staining from both colonies were had shown gram-negative rods. The isolates were put for identification by MALDI-TOF MS (VITEK MS-Biomerieux, France) which is based on the matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight technology. The white (first) [Figure 2a] colony was identified as Prevotella buccae [Figure 3a], and the transparent (second) [Figure 2b] colony was identified as Dialister pneumosintes [Figure 3b].

(b) Growth of Dialister pneumosintes on blood agar (sheep blood agar with Vit K1 and hemin) with a zone of inhibition around metronidazole disc

The conventional biochemicals were also put for both the colonies which had pointed towards the diagnosis of Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes. The agar dilution test for antibiotic susceptibility was performed according to EUCAST 2023 guidelines which confirmed that both identified organisms were sensitive to metronidazole, piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, and clindamycin. However, penicillin was found sensitive for Dialister pneumosintes and resistant for Prevotella buccae.

Treatment

The patient received treatment with intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 hours and intravenous metronidazole 500 mg every 8 hours. Additionally, an incision and drainage procedure was performed on the submandibular abscess [Figure 4] on 5 January 2023. The incision (of about 5 cm size) was made at the most fluctuant point, followed by the drainage of pus by breaking the loculi using finger dissection. There was drainage of pus through intraoral route also but there was no frank defect in oral cavity. Subsequently, root canal treatment was carried out on 11 January 2023. The patient was then discharged in good condition with a prescription for outpatient metronidazole for one week and follow-up appointments for wound care. Follow-up with patient showed wound healing, and he had no complaints.

DISCUSSION

The areas of DNSI are mostly orodental in origin which needs to be managed at the earliest to prevent complications. In our case patient presented with submandibular abscess (DNSI) with a history of dental infection. In this case study, the organisms involved in causing submandibular abscess were Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes. P. buccae is a anaerobic bacteria which is responsible for orodental infections. These infections are mostly as a result of involvement of other anaerobic bacteria.[3] While monomicrobial infections with P. buccae are not common, there are documented cases in the literature which include its diagnosis from cases of breast abscess,[5] mediastinitis,[7] pericarditis,[6] and pleural empyema.[9] The disarrangement in the microbiome leads to a change in the immunological response and release of certain virulence factors causing the pathogenesis. The virulence factors of Prevotella spp. are adhesins, fimbriae, haemolysins, enzymes like nucleases, proteases, lipopolysaccharides, and exopolysaccharides.

Dialister pneumosintes is a non-spore-forming, non-motile, non-fermentative, gram-negative anaerobic bacilli.[12] According to the literature, this organism constitutes the normal flora of various sites such as nasopharynx, oral cavity, intestine, and vagina. Besides oral infections, this anaerobe is also responsible for several extraoral infections. Though the virulence factors by this anaerobic organism are still under research, the infection can also be seen in the extraoral region involving the sinuses, respiratory tract, etc. The diagnosis is best achieved by molecular analysis of samples, though due to small colonies, there is difficulty in isolating it by conventional methods.[12]

Dialister pneumosintes is responsible for the aetiology of infections in deep neck region when there is breach in the protective mechanism of innate immunity.[3] In this case, the patient had a prior history of dental caries, suggesting a potential link to the development of a submandibular abscess. It is plausible that the dental abscess may have caused a mucosal breach, ultimately leading to the submandibular abscess, with Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes identified as the likely causative agent.

As metronidazole is a bactericidal agent which acts against anaerobic organisms. Therefore, it was administered after the confirmation of anaerobes. Globally, there is an increase in antimicrobial resistance among anaerobic bacteria, including Prevotella spp.[9] and Dialister spp.[13] However, according to many studies, Prevotella spp. was observed to be sensitive to metronidazole.[5] It was also observed in studies by Jun Hirai et al. and Rachel Patel et al. that Dialister pneumosintes isolates were sensitive to metronidazole.[1314] In this case study also, Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes were susceptible to metronidazole.

CONCLUSION

Prevotella buccae and Dialister pneumosintes are usual causes of periodontitis and dental abscess. The complications of infections by these anaerobes can lead to morbidity and mortality. We report a case of submandibular abscess after the persistence of dental caries which was caused by mixed oral flora. The early diagnosis and management proved to be life-saving in this patient. This study highlights the diversity of anaerobic bacteria in odontogenic infections, suggesting the importance of using anaerobic cultures in patient treatment strategies for accurate diagnosis and management.
