A phase 1 study of PF-07242813, an anti-CD1a antibody, in healthy participants and in patients with atopic dermatitis
Authors: Spencer I. Danto, Ruolun Qiu, Yifan Zhou, Vivek Pradhan, Natalie Rath, Karen M. Page, Xingpeng Li, Elena Peeva, Michael S. Vincent, Jeremy D. Gale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimPF-07242813 (anti-CD1a) was designed to inhibit T-cell proinflammatory signaling. This analysis evaluated the safety and pharmacokinetics of single ascending doses and multiple ascending doses (MADs) of PF-07242813 in healthy participants and the pharmacodynamics of a single dose in patients with atopic dermatitis (AD).MethodsIn part 1, healthy participants (aged 18–55 years) received PF-07242813 intravenously (single ascending doses), subcutaneously (MADs) or intravenously (MADs), or placebo. In part 2, patients with moderate-to-severe AD (aged 18–65 years) were randomized (2:1) to receive 1000 mg PF-07242813 intravenously or placebo. Endpoints included safety and pharmacokinetic outcomes in healthy participants and efficacy endpoints in patients with AD.ResultsOf the 122 participants (single ascending doses: n = 57; MADs: n = 40; AD: n = 25), 41 reported treatment-emergent adverse events (mostly mild); headache (4.9%) and alanine transaminase increase (4.9%) were the most common. Peak serum PF-07242813 concentrations occurred at 0.6–2 hours (intravenously, single ascending doses) and 73–169 hours (subcutaneously, MADs). Overall, PF-07242813 did not demonstrate clinical benefit in patients with AD.ConclusionsCD1a blockade is not sufficient to produce a clinical effect in patients with AD. PF-07242813 was well tolerated and may be useful for CD1a pathway analyses. This study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT04668066).

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by itching and recurrent eczematous lesions (Weidinger and Novak, 2016). AD affects ∼171 million people globally, with the highest prevalence in children (Shin et al, 2023). Severe AD is reported in ≤17% of individuals with AD (Barbarot et al, 2018; Silverberg et al, 2021). AD can significantly impact QOL and is associated with depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances (Fishbein et al, 2020; Li et al, 2018; Silverberg et al, 2019, 2018). Current treatments include emollients, broad-acting immunosuppressants (eg, corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors), and selective inflammatory pathway inhibitors (eg, dupilumab and Jak inhibitors) (Fishbein et al, 2020); unmet medical needs remain. CD1a is constitutively and highly expressed in the epidermis (Langerhans cells) and on a subset of dermal myeloid dendritic cells (de Jong and Ogg, 2021). In AD, there is an increased frequency of CD1a expression in Langerhans cells (He et al, 2021). Although the precise physiologic role of CD1a–T-cell interactions is unknown (Hardman et al, 2022), their reactivity is thought to be regulated by a balance of permissive and nonpermissive endogenous lipids (Cotton et al, 2021b).

Known irritants contain or generate lipid CD1a ligands, which may drive potential pathways of atopic skin inflammation through CD1a-reactive T cells (Jarrett et al, 2016; Kim et al, 2016). For example, urushiol-induced inflammation was blocked by treatment with anti-CD1a in psoriasis mouse and human T-cell models (Kim et al, 2016). In addition, alteration of the outer surface of CD1a has been shown to block T-cell responses (Cotton et al, 2021a), suggesting that TCRs are specific for CD1a. These observations suggest that anti-CD1a antibodies could be a suitable treatment for inflammatory skin diseases.

PF-07242813 is an anti-CD1a antibody with, to our knowledge, a previously unreported mechanism of inhibiting CD1a–TCR interactions, preventing T-cell activation and, theoretically, downstream proinflammatory signaling. In vitro assessment of PF-07242813 confirmed high binding affinity to both human and cynomolgus monkey CD1a and the desired target effect of suppressing IL-17A secretion by primary CD3+ cells isolated from whole blood or healthy skin (data on file). In addition, in an in vivo model of skin inflammation in human CD1a transgenic mice, PF-07242813 neutralized CD1a-dependent immune responses and significantly reduced dermatitis score, total IgE, and other measures of AD (data on file).

This phase 1 study reports the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics (PK) in healthy participants who received single ascending doses (SADs) or multiple ascending doses (MADs) of intravenous (IV)/subcutaneous (SC) PF-07242813; clinical activity was also evaluated in patients with AD who received a single IV dose.

Results

Demographics and disposition

A total of 122 participants were enrolled across 5 centers within the United States. All healthy participants (SAD, PF-07242813 [n = 40], placebo [n = 17]; MAD, PF-07242813 [n = 30], placebo [n = 10]) were enrolled at a single center, and patients in the AD cohort (n = 25) were enrolled across all 5 centers. Across the SAD cohorts, 1 participant from the 3 mg IV cohort was lost to follow-up and discontinued from the treatment phase. Across the MAD cohorts, 2 participants each in the 150 mg PF-07242813 SC, 300 mg PF-07242813 SC, and placebo SC treatment groups discontinued the blinded treatment phase; the reasons for discontinuation were withdrawal by subject (n = 3), adverse events (AEs) (n = 2), and other reasons (n = 1). In the AD cohort, all patients in the 1000 mg PF-07242813 IV group completed the treatment phase; 3 patients in the placebo IV group discontinued the treatment phase owing to loss to follow-up, withdrawal by patient, and other reasons (each n = 1) (Figure 1).Figure 1Part 2 CONSORT diagram. Safety and tolerability

In the SAD cohorts (part 1), a total of 18 treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) (9 each in the PF-07242813 and placebo groups) were reported in 14 (24.6%) participants (Table 4). In the MAD cohorts, 30 TEAEs were reported in 18 (45.0%) participants, including 8 TEAEs in 5 (62.5%) participants receiving placebo SC and 1 TEAE in 1 (50.0%) participant receiving placebo IV. In the treatment dosage groups (all n = 6), the number of TEAEs was not dose dependent and ranged from 1 to 8 (Table 5). PK of PF-07242813

In the SAD cohorts (part 1), after a single IV infusion at doses ranging from 0.3 to 1000 mg in healthy participants, the maximum observed concentration (Cmax) increased with an increase in dose, with a median time it takes to reach the maximum concentration (Tmax) range of 0.6–‍2 hours across the doses (Table 7). Immunogenicity

In part 1, the overall incidences of treatment-emergent antidrug antibody (ADA) and neutralizing antibody (NAb) responses for the SAD cohorts were high: ≥50% in all cohorts, except the 0.3 and 1000 mg IV cohorts (overall incidence of 0 and 16.7%, respectively) (Table 7). For the MAD cohorts, the overall incidence of treatment-emergent ADA and NAb responses ranged from 16.7 to 83.3%, with higher incidence observed in lower-dose cohorts (Table 8). At the same dosing level of 1000 mg IV, a higher incidence of treatment-emergent ADA and NAb responses was observed in the AD cohort (93.3%) than with that observed in healthy participants in the SAD cohorts (16.7–50%) (Table 7).

Biomarkers

Discussion

This study presents clinical assessment data for PF-07242813, an anti-CD1a mAb, as single and multiple doses in healthy participants and a single dose in patients with AD. It was hypothesized that PF-07242813 would inhibit CD1a binding to TCRs, thereby decreasing downstream T-cell activation and proinflammatory signaling, potentially providing clinical benefit in AD. PF-07242813 was generally safe and well tolerated in both healthy participants (SAD and MAD) and patients with AD; however, PF-07242813 demonstrated no clinical benefit in patients with AD on the basis of EASI-50/90/100, Peak Pruritus NRS, Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure, or DLQI. These data suggest that the inhibition of CD1a–TCR–mediated proinflammatory signaling may not be sufficient to reverse the clinical manifestations of AD.

Prior to this clinical assessment, in vitro pharmacodynamic studies indicated that PF-07242813 had a high binding affinity to both human and cynomolgus monkey CD1a (KD [dissociation constant] 0.18 ± 0.01 nM and 0.06 ± 0.01 nM, respectively; data on file) and dose-dependent binding to CD1a endogenously expressed on primary monocyte-derived dendritic cells (half-maximal effectiveness concentration = 7.8 ± 6.8 nM) (data on file). In addition, PF-07242813 suppressed CD1a-dependent IL-17A secretion by primary CD3+ cells isolated from healthy participants and patients with AD (39–80% reduction in CD1a-induced enzyme-linked ImmunoSpot spot formation; data on file). In vivo studies further showed that prophylactic administration of PF-07242813 in a house dust mite model of inflammation in human CD1a transgenic mice led to statistically significant suppression of CD1a-dependent inflammation compared with that in control-treated mice. This included a lower mean dermatitis score (2 [SD ± 0.7] vs 8 [SD ± 1], P = .0004), reduced total IgE levels (719 ng/ml [± SD 105] vs 1.1 × 104 ng/ml [± SD 1080], P < .0001), reductions in inflammatory and IL-13+ cells, and suppression of AD-related gene expression (data on file). Overall, these preclinical data supported the clinical evaluation of PF-07242813 for the treatment of AD.

One possibility for the lack of effectiveness in the AD cohort could be PF-07242813 failing to block CD1a signaling sufficiently. On the basis of site-of-action modeling, a single 1000 mg IV dose of PF-07242813 was predicted to saturate CD1a receptor occupancy and provide >95% target suppression for more than 6 weeks. However, the absence of a validated biomarker prevented confirmation of CD1a blockade in clinical samples from this study. Another possibility is that CD1a proinflammatory pathways may not play a clinically relevant role in AD, possibly owing to redundancy with other, potentially more influential pathways. A lack of involvement of CD1a-restricted T cells in the pathophysiology of the study patient population specifically (due to the severity, extent, or duration of disease) may also underlie the lack of efficacy.

PF-07242813 was anticipated to have a high incidence of immunogenicity on the basis of the presence of CD1a on antigen-presenting cells. In this clinical assessment, the rate of ADA development for PF-07242813 was high, particularly in patients with AD, yet there was no apparent correlation between ADA/NAb positivity and PK or efficacy. However, this analysis was limited by differences in the duration of PK and immunogenicity assessments between healthy participants and patients with AD and by the low number of patients with negative ADA. Therefore, definitive conclusions on the impact of immunogenicity could not be reached from this study.

AD was considered an attractive model for studying PF-07242813 in humans owing to recent data supporting the potential significance of CD1a in skin inflammation (Cotton et al, 2021a, 2021b; de Jong and Ogg, 2021; Hardman et al, 2022; Kim et al, 2016). This study benefited from testing safety and tolerability in both healthy participants and patients with AD. The assessment of clinical effect included a robust, validated clinical efficacy endpoint for AD studies, allowing for clear conclusions to be made. However, the study did not demonstrate efficacy in AD for PF-07242813. The lack of a pharmacodynamic biomarker for CD1a–TCR reactivity prevented confirmation of effective target inhibition, and it is possible that a single dose of PF-07242813 is insufficient for CD1a blockade beyond 6 weeks. In conclusion, PF-07242813 was generally safe and well tolerated in the healthy participants and patients with AD evaluated in this study. CD1a blockade is not sufficient to produce a clinically significant impact in moderate-to-severe AD. However, inhibition of CD1a may have benefit in other diseases mediated by pathogenic lipids, such as psoriasis or certain types of contact dermatitis.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This participant- and investigator-blind, sponsor-open, placebo-controlled study (NCT04668066) was conducted in 2 parts (Figure 6). Part 1 was a within-cohort randomized investigation of SADs (0.3–1000 mg IV) and MADs (15–‍300 mg SC to 500 mg IV) of PF-07242813 every 2 weeks for a total of 3 doses in healthy participants. In part 2, patients with AD (AD cohort) were randomized 2:1 to receive an IV dose at the maximum tolerated dose identified in part 1 (ie, 1000 mg PF-07242813) or placebo.Figure 6Study design. The efficacious dose of PF-07242813 was projected using a site-of-action model, where PF-07242813 exposure was linked to CD1a receptor occupancy using the PF-07242813 KD and predicted human PK parameters, CD1a concentrations in skin, and the CD1a internalization/turnover rate (data on file). Using this approach, a single 1000 mg IV dose of PF-07242813 was predicted to saturate CD1a receptor occupancy and provide >95% target suppression for more than 6 weeks.

Study participants

All participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria to be eligible for the study: male and female adult participants (part 1: ages 18–55 years; part 2: ages 18–65 years) who were willing and able to provide informed consent and comply with all scheduled visits, the treatment plan, laboratory tests, and other study procedures. Self-reported race/ethnicity categories were collected to define the diversity of the study population.

In part 1 only, participants were overtly healthy, as determined by medical evaluation, including medical history, physical examination, vital sign assessments, temperature, 12-lead electrocardiography, and laboratory tests. Their body mass index was between 17.5 and 30.5 kg/m2, and they had a total body weight >50 kg (110 lbs). An additional Japanese cohort of healthy participants of Japanese descent, whose parents and grandparents were Japanese, was also enrolled in part 1.

In part 2 only, patients had a clinical diagnosis of chronic AD for approximately 1 year prior to day 1 and had the diagnosis of AD confirmed by Hanifin and Rajka criteria of AD. Patients must have either had an inadequate response to treatment with topical medications (≥4 consecutive weeks within 1 year of the first dose of the study drug) or had a documented reason why topical treatments were considered medically inappropriate (eg, because of significant side effects or safety risks) within the last year. Patients had moderate-to-severe AD (defined as having an affected body surface area [captured as part of the EASI] ≥10%, an IGA ≥3, and an EASI ≥12 at both the screening and baseline visits) and had an otherwise healthy medical evaluation (other than signs and symptoms of AD), including medical history, physical examination, vital sign assessments, temperature, 12-lead electrocardiography, laboratory tests, a body mass index of 17.5–40 kg/m2, and a total body weight >50 kg (110 lbs). However, controlled comorbid diseases (ie, controlled diabetes not requiring insulin, controlled hypertension) were acceptable, as long as they did not require administration of prohibited medications. Mild or moderate asthma that was well controlled (not requiring high-dose inhaled corticosteroids, systemic [oral or parenteral] corticosteroids, or biologic asthma treatments) was not exclusionary.

Participants were excluded from the study if any of the following criteria applied: evidence of active, latent, or inadequately treated infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculosis) as defined by both a positive QuantiFERON-TB Gold in-tube or equivalent test and a history of either untreated or inadequately treated latent or active tuberculosis infection or treatment for the same; a history of HIV infection, hepatitis B, or hepatitis C; or positive testing for HIV, hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, or hepatitis C antibody. Hepatitis B vaccination was allowed, and as an exception, a positive test due to hepatitis B vaccination was permissible. Testing for SARS-CoV-2 was at the discretion of the investigator, on the basis of local infection rates, patient exposure history, and reported signs or symptoms of COVID-19. Other exclusionary medical or psychiatric conditions included a known history of or evidence of current endocrine disease; a history of systemic infection requiring hospitalization, parenteral antimicrobial therapy, or as otherwise judged clinically significant by the investigator within 6 months prior to day 1; recent exposure to live or attenuated vaccines within 28 days of the screening visit; any malignancies or a history of malignancies, with the exception of adequately treated or excised nonmetastatic basal cell or squamous cell cancer of the skin or cervical carcinoma in situ; a history of any lymphoproliferative disorder, such as Epstein–Barr virus; related lymphoproliferative disorder, a history of lymphoma, leukemia, or signs and symptoms suggestive of current lymphatic or lymphoid tissue disease; evidence or a history of clinically significant hematologic, renal, endocrine, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, hepatic, psychiatric, neurological, or immunological/rheumatological disease; and recent (within the past year) or active suicidal ideation/behavior or a laboratory abnormality that may increase the risk of study participation or, in the investigator’s judgment, make the participant inappropriate for the study.

Other exclusions included history of alcohol abuse or binge drinking and/or any other illicit drug use or dependence within 6 months of screening; blood donation (excluding plasma donations) of approximately 1 pint (500 ml) or more within 60 days prior to dosing; history of sensitivity to heparin or heparin-induced thrombocytopenia; investigator site staff or Pfizer employees directly involved in the conduct of the study, site staff otherwise supervised by the investigator, and their respective family members; pregnancy or breastfeeding; participants with partners currently pregnant or male or female participants pursuing artificial reproductive technologies; and use of tobacco/nicotine-containing products equating to more than 5 cigarettes per day.

In part 1 only, exclusion criteria also included allergic disease (including drug allergies but excluding untreated, asymptomatic, seasonal allergies at the time of dosing); use of prescription or nonprescription drugs and dietary and herbal supplements within 7 days or 5 half-lives (whichever is longer) prior to the first dose of PF-07242813; and screening supine blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg (systolic) or ≥90 mm Hg (diastolic).

In part 2 only, exclusionary criteria also included, at the screening visit, whether there were “yes” answers on items 4 and 5 of the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale in the past year or on any question in the past 6 months. Additional exclusionary medical criteria for part 2 included screening supine blood pressure ≥160 mm Hg (systolic) or ≥100 mm Hg (diastolic), after at least 5 minutes of supine rest; active forms of other inflammatory skin diseases; a history of or current evidence of skin conditions (eg, psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, lupus) at the time of day 1 that would interfere with the evaluation of AD or response to treatment; active chronic or acute skin infection requiring treatment with systemic antibiotics, antivirals, antiparasitics, antiprotozoals, or antifungals within 2 weeks prior to day 1 or superficial skin infections within 1 week prior to day 1; a score >5 on the Fitzpatrick Skin Type Assessment; and a history of anaphylaxis (except patients with sensitivity and/or anaphylaxis only to a single, avoidable allergen [eg, aspirin, penicillin, sulfadrugs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, peanuts]) if, in the opinion of the investigator, the patient was aware of the hypersensitivity, avoids the problematic allergen, carries appropriate treatment for anaphylaxis, and knows how to manage anaphylactic reactions.

Safety endpoints

The primary objective was safety; assessments included the incidence of TEAEs, serious AEs (including deaths), vital signs, safety laboratory tests, and electrocardiography. In the SAD cohorts, cardiac telemetry was also performed.

PK endpoints

In part 1, the PK parameters assessed included AUC from time 0 to the time of the last quantifiable concentration (AUClast), AUCinf, Cmax, Tmax, and t½ after single doses; AUC within the dosing interval (AUCtau), Cmax, and Tmax after each repeated dose; and t½ after the last repeated dose. In part 2, exploratory PK endpoints included AUClast, AUCinf, Cmax, t½, and Tmax.

Immunogenicity, biomarkers, and gene expression

Blood samples were collected from healthy participants in the SAD and MAD cohorts and from patients in the AD cohort to assess the incidence of ADAs and NAbs against PF-07242813. Additional blood samples were collected from patients in the AD cohort to assess changes from baseline in circulating biomarkers (including eosinophils, IgE, IL-31, IL-17, and CCL17) and AD-related gene expression in skin biopsies.

Efficacy endpoints

In part 2, the secondary endpoint was the percentage CFB in the EASI score at week 6. The total EASI score ranges from 0 to 72, with higher values indicating a greater spread and severity of eczema (Hanifin et al, 2022). Percentage CFB in the EASI score was also assessed at predefined time points up to week 16.

Exploratory efficacy endpoints were assessed at week 6 and at all other prespecified time points (weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 16) using the IGA, EASI-50/EASI-75/EASI-90/EASI-100, Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure, DLQI, and pruritus severity and frequency in Pruritus NRS tools. For IGA, the proportion of patients achieving an IGA response was assessed, defined as a score of 0 (“Clear”) or 1 (“Almost Clear”) on a 5-point scale and a reduction from baseline of ≥2 points. For EASI-50/EASI-75/EASI-90/EASI-100, the proportion of patients achieving 50%, 75%, 90%, and 100% improvement from baseline, respectively, was observed. We measured CFB in Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure to assess the impact of AD over the past week (patient-reported frequency of 7 items: dryness, itching, flaking, cracking, sleep loss, bleeding, and weeping) on a scale from 0 to 28; 0–2 indicates clear to almost clear, and 25–28 indicates very severe eczema. CFB in DLQI score was observed, which includes 10 items that assess patient health-related QOL and is scored from 0 to 30; a minimally important difference for the DLQI has been estimated as a 2–5-point improvement from baseline. The severity and frequency of pruritus were assessed using horizontal NRS measures. Patients were asked to assess the “frequency of itching due to AD over the past 24 hours” on an NRS anchored by the terms “never/no itching” and “always/constant itching” on a scale of 0–10.

Statistical analyses

The safety analysis set was defined as all participants randomly assigned and who received ≥1 dose of study intervention. The modified intention-to-treat population was defined as all participants randomly assigned to study intervention and who received ≥1 dose of study intervention. The PK concentration set was defined as all enrolled participants who received ≥1 dose of PF-07242813 and for whom ≥1 concentration value was reported. The PK parameter analysis population was defined as all randomized participants who received ≥1 dose of study intervention and who had ≥1 of the PK parameters of interest calculated. The AD cohort sample size calculation assumed an ∼80% power in detecting a 50% difference in percentage CFB in EASI score at week 6 with a common SD of 46% between patients receiving treatment and those receiving placebo (alpha = 0.05).

For part 1, only descriptive statistics are reported, with no formal statistical hypothesis testing performed. For part 2, statistical inference was made on the percentage CFB in EASI score at week 6 endpoint, with a null hypothesis that there is no difference between the treatment and placebo groups. Analysis of covariance adjusted to baseline covariates was used to compare the 2 arms, with least square means, 90% confidence intervals, and corresponding 1-sided P-values presented. For continuous efficacy endpoints, missing data were handled using a control-based imputation method (PROC MI). For binary efficacy endpoints, all missing data values were reported as failures. Data for CFB in circulating biomarkers are presented descriptively with no statistical testing performed.

Gene expression in skin biopsy was analyzed through RNA sequencing with standard bioinformatics pipelines. Gene set variation analysis (with the z-score method) was used to evaluate CFB to week 2 and week 6 in previously reported key gene sets for immune genes, T helper 1, T helper 2, T helper 17, and T helper 22 (Bissonnette et al, 2019; Guttman-Yassky et al, 2019; Sanyal et al, 2019) in lesional skin from patients in the AD cohort who received 1000 mg PF-07242813 IV or placebo. Linear contrasts for CFB in the PF-07242813 and placebo treatment groups were made on the basis of a mixed model for each gene, with a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05 applied to control for multiplicity. Differences in baseline gene expression between lesional and nonlesional skin in the overall AD cohort as well as CFB in gene expression at week 2 and week 6 in lesional skin from patients in the AD cohort who received 1000 mg PF-07242813 IV or placebo were analyzed using the Bioconductor R package, version 3.2 (Limma, version 3.48.3 and edgeR, version 3.14) as described. A false discovery rate threshold of 0.05 and fold change >1.5 were used to define statistical significance in both the baseline and CFB gene expression analyses.
