Abstract
We consider a Navier–Stokes–Allen–Cahn (NSAC) system that governs the compressible motion of a viscous, immiscible two-phase fluid at constant temperature. Weak solutions of the NSAC system dissipate an appropriate energy functional. Based on an equivalent re-formulation of the NSAC system we propose a fully-discrete discontinuous Galerkin (dG) discretization that is mass-conservative, energy-stable, and provides higher-order accuracy in space and second-order accuracy in time. The approach relies on the approach in [ 21 ] and a special splitting discretization of the derivatives of the free energy function within the Crank-Nicolson time-stepping. Numerical experiments confirm the analytical statements and show the applicability of the approach.

Introduction

For the modelling of the dynamics of a compressible fluid that occurs in two phase states, say a liquid and a vapour one, there are two different approaches. In the sharp-interface (SI) ansatz the spatial domain is partitioned in two
bulk domains occupied by the respective phases. These are coupled by transmission conditions across the interface which separates the bulk domains.
see e.g.
[
30
]
.
Alternatively one can rely on diffuse-interface (DI) models. For the compressible regime we refer to the overview papers
[
3
,
36
]
. Then,
the interface has a small but finite thickness. In this interfacial region the different phases might mix to enable a smooth transition from one phase to the other, see Figure
1
for some illustration.
We are interested in the subclass of phase-field models which augment the hydromechanical equations by an evolution equation for the artificial phase-field variable. The phase-field variable acts as an indicator for the fluid’s phase.

(a)

(b)

Figure 1:
(a) SI approach with time-dependent liquid/vapour bulk domains
\Omega_{L/V}=\Omega_{L/V}(t)
and separating interface
\Gamma(t)
, (b) DI approach with
{\mathcal{O}}(\gamma)
-interfacial width.

The DI model concepts and thus in particular phase-field models have
the advantage that only one system of evolution equations on the entire domain needs to be solved, without the need for explicit tracking of the interface. On the other hand, the thermodynamically correct combination of the flow equations with the phase-field dynamics becomes intricate. Note that
thermodynamical consistency corresponds to an energy dissipation inequality in the case of isothermal models.

A common approach for phase-field modelling is the coupling of the equations of
hydrodynamics by the stress tensor to a parabolic phase-field equation of Allen–Cahn type. Such
Navier–Stokes–Allen–Cahn (NSAC) systems
have been introduced in
[
6
]
and
further developped in
[
1
,
14
,
19
,
23
,
39
]
. Concerning the well-posedness of weak and strong solutions for these mixed hyperbolic-parabolic systems we refer to e.g.
[
18
,
27
]
. The isothermal NSAC system from

[
14
]
is the basis for this work, see
(
2.8
) below.
The authors suggest a free energy density (see (
2.5
)) that renders the approach thermodynamically consistent and –notably– enables mass transfer between the two phases. In turn, the free energy density
inhibits a strong nonlinear coupling between density and phase field parameter.

Due to its mixed-type structure, the numerics for the NSAC models is challenging. We need to resolve steep gradients in the interfacial zone in a stable and efficient way. Numerical schemes with artificial dissipation for stabilization can lead to problems like increase of energy or parasitic currents
[
11
,
26
]
. Therefore
we aim to develop a
higher-order and energy-stable
scheme
to solve the NSAC system.
To achieve higher order in space we rely on the discontinuous Galerkin (dG)
method
[
5
,
24
]
. More precisely, to treat second-order spatial operators in the original NSAC system we propose a combination of the local
dG method
[
10
]
and interior-penalty approaches
[
12
]
. Due to their flexibility, dG methods are frequently used in the context of compressible DI models

[
7
,
13
,
28
,
35
,
38
]
. To ensure energy stability in the discrete level we follow an approach that has been first
developped in
[
20
,
21
]

for certain DI models. Our construction starts from a first-order re-formulation of the NSAC system in terms of variational derivatives of the energy which can then be used as test functions in the dG formulation (see (
2.17
) below). The
design
of tailored stabilizing inter-element fluxes guarentees then mass conservation and energy stability with respect to the spatial discretization.
To retain an at least second-order
time discretization we utilize a Crank–Nicolson-type time-stepping. The key technical step to ensure energy stability under time discretization is a special
implicit-explicit discretization of the stress tensor which involves the specific free energy density discussed above.
Our final main result is then a second-order in time,
higher-order in space dG method for the NSAC system that obeys an energy stability
estimate, see Definition
3.8
. Up to our knowledge, this is the first fully-discrete second-order scheme with this property.

Notably, our approach is different from the
construction of higher-order entropy-dissipative schemes in the realm of e.g. hyperbolic systems that are equipped with a convex entropy
[
29
,
37
]
.
The mixed-type structure of the NSAC system prevents the use of this elegant approach. But
there are various other related works on the numerics for Navier-Stokes–Allen–Cahn systems.
In
[
22
]
, a dG method is proposed generalizing the scalar auxiliar variable approach which is widely used in the incompressible realm. The achieved energy stability is restricted to a first-order time discretization. In turn,
higher-order in time is achieved for spatially continuous approaches in
[
9
]
. The works
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
refer to thermodynamically consistent discretizations based on finite-volume ideas. The first dG discretization for the NSAC system has been proposed in
[
28
]
but is also restricted to first-order accuracy in time.

This work is structured as follows. In Section
2
we review the isothermal NSAC system from
[
14
]
, that allows for phase transition dynamics. Further, we introduce a mixed formulation with corresponding notion of weak solutions, and show that these dissipate the corresponding energy (Theorem
2.3
).
In the subsequent Section
3
we propose the energy-stable dG method discussing first semi-discretizations in space and in time, respectively (Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.7
).The main rigorous result is Theorem
3.9
which establishes
mass conservation and energy stability for a higher-order fully-discrete dG method.
In Section
4
we present numerical experiments which demonstrate the higher-order accuracy and confirm the theoretical findings on energy stability. In several examples we
illustrate the capability of the model and our dG method.
Preliminary numerical results of this work can be found in the conference paper
[
33
]
. It relies on the PhD
thesis of the first author
[
34
]
.

2
A Phase-Field Approach to Compressible Two-Phase Flow

2.1
The Compressible Navier–Stokes–Allen–Cahn System

We consider the dynamics of a viscous fluid at constant temperature for some time interval
[0,T]
,
T>0
, that
occupies a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\ d\in\mathbb{N}
. The hydromechanical unknowns are the density of the fluid denoted by
\rho=\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)>0
and the velocity field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The fluid is assumed to exist in two phases, a liquid phase denoted by subscript
\mathrm{L}
and a vapor phase denoted by subscript
\mathrm{V}
.
In each phase the fluid is thermodynamically completely described by the corresponding Helmholtz free energy density
\rho f_{\mathrm{L/V}}(\rho)
, which is assumed to be given. Since we target at a phase-field description of the two-phase dynamics we introduce as further unknown the phase-field variable
\varphi=\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)\in[0,1]
, which is supposed to take the value
0
(
1
)
for a pure liquid (vapour) state.

Now, following
[
14
]
we will introduce as governing system the isothermal compressible Navier–Stokes–Allen–Cahn (NSAC) system. With the phase-field parameter
\gamma>0
, which relates to the interfacial width, the Helmholtz free energy density for the two-phase fluid is supposed to be given by

\displaystyle\rho f(\rho,\varphi,\nabla\varphi)

\displaystyle=h(\varphi)\rho f_{\mathrm{L}}(\rho)+(1-h(\varphi))\rho f_{\mathrm{V}}(\rho)+\frac{1}{\gamma}W(\varphi)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}.

(2.1)

It consists of the interpolated free energy densities
\rho f_{\mathrm{L/V}}
of the pure liquid and vapor phases. These are assumed to be convex functions with bounded derivatives.
For the nonlinear interpolation function
h=h(\varphi)
and the Van-der-Waals energy
[
8
]
using the double well
potential,
we make the polynomial choices

\displaystyle h(\varphi)=3\varphi^{2}-2\varphi^{3},\quad W(\varphi)=a\varphi^{2}(1-\varphi)^{2}\quad(a>0).

(2.2)

For the numerical analysis in Section
3
the specific choices in (
2.2
) are not essential but the use of (smooth) polynomials renders the notion of weak solutions below to be well-posed, see (
2.21
).
Let us define the mixture energy densities

\displaystyle\rho\psi(\varphi,\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq h(\varphi)\rho f_{\mathrm{L}}(\rho)+(1-h(\varphi))\rho f_{\mathrm{V}}(\rho)\quad\text{ and }

(2.3)

\displaystyle\rho\tilde{f}(\varphi,\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho\psi(\varphi,\rho)+\frac{1}{\gamma}W(\varphi).

(2.4)

Then we can rewrite (
2.1
) in the compact form

\displaystyle\rho f(\rho,\varphi,\nabla\varphi)

\displaystyle=\rho\psi(\varphi,\rho)+\frac{1}{\gamma}W(\varphi)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}=\rho\tilde{f}(\varphi,\rho)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}.

(2.5)

The hydrodynamic pressure
p
is defined
through the Helmholtz free energy
\rho f
using
the Gibbs-like relation

p=p(\rho,\varphi,\nabla\varphi)=-\rho f(\rho,\varphi,\nabla\varphi)+\rho\frac{\partial(\rho f)}{\partial\rho}(\rho,\varphi,\nabla\varphi).

(2.6)

Furthermore, we introduce the generalized chemical potential by

\mu=\frac{1}{\gamma}W^{\prime}(\varphi)+\frac{\partial(\rho\psi)}{\partial\varphi}-\gamma\Delta\varphi,

(2.7)

which steers the phase-field variable into equilibrium.
Additionally, we denote by
\eta>0
the (artificial) mobility.

The isothermal compressible NSAC system in
\Omega\times(0,T)
reads then as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+{p\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}})

\displaystyle=\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})-\gamma\div(\nabla\varphi\otimes\nabla\varphi),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}\varphi+\rho\nabla\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=-\eta\mu.

Here, we denote the identity matrix in

{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}
. The viscous part of the stress tensor is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}(\varphi,\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})=\nu(\varphi)(\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}-\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}),

(2.9)

with an interpolation of the given viscosities
\nu_{\mathrm{L/V}}>0
of the pure phases:

\displaystyle\nu(\varphi)=h(\varphi)\nu_{\mathrm{L}}+(1-h(\varphi))\nu_{\mathrm{V}}>0.

(2.10)

Note that we have set for the sake of simplicity in (
2.9
) the bulk and the shear viscosity to be equal to 1.

Assuming an impermeable no-slip wall and
90^{\circ}
static contact angle, we impose the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}\text{ and }\nabla\varphi(\cdot,t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}=0{\text{ on }\partial\Omega,t\in[0,T].}

(2.11)

By
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d-1}
we denote the outer normal of
\partial\Omega
.
Additionally, the system is endowed with initial conditions

\displaystyle\rho(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,0)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{0},\quad\varphi(\cdot,0)=\varphi_{0}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.12)

using suitable functions
(\rho_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{0},\varphi_{0})\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
.

The total energy of the system (
2.8
) at time
t\in[0,T]
is defined as the sum of bulk free energy and kinetic energy, i.e.,

E(t)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)f\big(\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t),\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t),\nabla\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)\big)+\frac{1}{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)|^{2}\ddd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}.

(2.13)

Introducing the bilinear form

B[\varphi;\cdot,\cdot]:(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})\mapsto\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\bf S}(\varphi;\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}):\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}

for some
\varphi
with
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
,
it can be readily shown that smooth solutions of
(
2.8
) that satisfy (
2.11
) in
(0,T)
fulfill the energy dissipation law

\frac{\dd}{{\rm d}t}E(t)=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\eta}{\rho}\mu^{2}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-B[\varphi;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}]\leq 0.

(2.14)

Remark 2.1

1.

The inequality (
2.14
) renders the
the NSAC system (
2.8
) thermodynamically consistent. We will prove the analogous energy dissipation
rate for a mixed formulation of (
2.8
)
in Theorem
2.3
below.

2.

The polynomial function
h
from (
2.2
) with the interpolation property
h(0)=0
,
h(1)=1
satisfies in particular
h^{\prime}(0)=h^{\prime}(1)\allowbreak\neq 0
. This guarantees that (
2.8
) allows for physical meaningful equilibria like a static single-phase equilibrium
\rho=const.,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{0},\varphi\equiv 0
. If
h^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
holds, then the right hand side of the phasefield equation
\eqref{eq:p2:NSAC}_{3}
would not vanish.
In
[
14
]
it has been shown that the choices (
2.2
) are compatible with the sharp-interface limit
\gamma\to 0
in the sense that the limit obeys well-known transmission conditions for phase transition.

3.

Up to our knowledge there are no
results on the well-posedness of solutions for (
2.8
). But
there are results on the existence of solutions to similar systems which mainly differ in the scaling of the second-order operators. The scalings are needed in order to ensure the boundedness of the
phase-field variable.
In
[
18
]
the existence of global-in-time weak solutions is proven, and in
[
27
]
the authors prove the existence and uniqueness of local strong solutions.

2.2
A Mixed Formulation of the NSAC System and Energy Stability

Our dG method will rely on a mixed, non-conservative formulation of the NSAC system (
2.8
)
which will be proposed in this section.
Notably, the mixed system is of first order if the viscous
stress tensor is neglected.
Further we introduce a notion of weak solutions, and it is
shown in Theorem
2.3
that weak solutions dissipate an energy functional that corresponds to
E
from (
2.13
).

First, using the free energy density
\rho f
from (
2.1
) with the homogeneous part
\rho\tilde{f}
from (
2.4
),
we introduce auxiliary variables

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\varphi,\quad\mu=\frac{\partial(\rho\tilde{f})}{\partial\varphi}-\div(\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}),\quad\tau=\frac{\partial(\rho\tilde{f})}{\partial\rho}+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}.

(2.15)

Note that
\mu
and
\tau
are the (variational) derivatives of the integrand of the total energy in (
2.13
) with respect to
\varphi
and
\rho
.
With these, we rewrite the NSAC system (
2.8
) into a mixed formulation and search for

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}\coloneqq(\rho,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\varphi,\mu,\tau,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}),

(2.16)

that satisfies

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})&=&0,\\
\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})-\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}-\frac{1}{2}\rho\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}-\mu\nabla\varphi+\rho\nabla\tau&=&\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}),\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}&=&\displaystyle-\eta\frac{\mu}{\rho},\\
\displaystyle\mu-\frac{\partial(\rho\tilde{f})}{\partial\varphi}+\gamma\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}})&=&0,\\
\displaystyle\tau-\frac{\partial(\rho\tilde{f})}{\partial\rho}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}&=&0,\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}-\nabla\varphi&=&0.\end{array}

(2.17)

In terms of the mixed-system variables the total energy
E
from (
2.13
) is expressed as

\hat{E}(t):=\int_{\Omega}\hskip-0.42677pt\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)\tilde{f}\big(\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t),\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)\big)+\hskip-0.42677pt\frac{\gamma}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)|^{2}+\hskip-0.42677pt\frac{1}{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t)|^{2}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}.

(2.18)

The initial conditions (
2.12
) and the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}(\cdot,t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}=0{\text{ on }\partial\Omega,t\in[0,T]}

(2.19)

complete the system.
Note that (
2.17
) involves as primary unknown the velocity as primitive variable. The non-conservative re-formulation of the momentum equations is needed to prove the discrete energy stability statements in Section

3
.

Let
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
H^{k}(\Omega)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, respectively. To cope with the boundary conditions (
2.19
) in a weak function space setting we define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)&\coloneqq&\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\colon\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},\\[4.73611pt]
H_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}}^{1}(\Omega)&\coloneqq&\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varphi}}\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{d}\colon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varphi}}|_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}=0\},\end{array}

(2.20)

and the spatial solution space as

{\mathcal{V}}=H^{1}(\Omega)\times(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))^{d}\times H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}}^{1}(\Omega).

We proceed to define a weak solution. In the sequel we use abbreviations like e.g.,
\rho(t)
instead of
\rho(\cdot,t)
for the density.

Definition 2.2
(Weak solution of the mixed formulation (
2.17
))

The function
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}:=(\rho,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\varphi,\mu,\tau,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}})\in C^{0}([0,T];{\mathcal{V}})
with

\begin{array}[]{c}\rho(t)>0,\,\varphi(t)\in[0,1]\text{ a.e.},\,\rho^{-1}(t),\,(\rho\tilde{f})(t),\,\rho(t){|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|(t)}^{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega),\\[4.73611pt]
\partial_{t}\rho(t),\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\in L^{2}(\Omega),\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(t),{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}(t)\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}\end{array}

(2.21)

for all
t\in[0,T]
is called a weak solution of the initial boundary value problem for (
2.17
)
if it satisfies the initial conditions in (
2.12
) and if we have in
(0,T)
the relations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}\rho+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}))\psi\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\\[7.3194pt]
0&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\vphantom{\frac{1}{2}}\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})-\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\\[6.45831pt]
&&\displaystyle\phantom{\int_{\Omega}}+\rho\nabla\tau-\mu\nabla\varphi-\frac{1}{2}\rho\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\Big)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}+B[\varphi;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}],\\[7.3194pt]

(2.22)

for all test functions

.

The conditions in (
2.21
) ensure not only the existence of all integrals in (
2.22
) but
also in the subsequent energy notions. Recall that weak solutions of the original system (

) satisfy a total energy inequality, see

. We show that weak solutions of (

Theorem 2.3
(Energy stability of the weak solution)

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}=(\rho,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\varphi,\mu,\tau,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}})
be a weak solution to the initial boundary value problem for (
2.17
). Then for all
t\in(0,T)
, the following energy inequality holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho\tilde{f}(\rho,\varphi)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\ddd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\right)

(2.23)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\eta}{\rho}\mu^{2}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-B[\varphi;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}]\leq 0.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\hat{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\tau-(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\varphi)\mu-\eta\frac{\mu^{2}}{\rho}-\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.99995pt-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\cdot(\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}))+\div(\rho\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.99995pt-\rho\nabla\tau\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\mu\nabla\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}{}-B[\varphi;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\eta\frac{\mu^{2}}{\rho}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}-B[\varphi;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}].

For the last line we used
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\in H_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}}^{1}(\Omega)
.

\square

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{4}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{5}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\varrho_{h}]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{6}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma[\mkern-3.0mu[(\partial_{t}\varphi_{h})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{2}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}]\dd s-B_{h}[\varphi_{h};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}].

We eliminate the time derivatives of
\rho_{h}
and
\varphi_{h}
in the volume integral
with the respective evolutions equations in (

). This results in

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(-\tau_{h}\div(\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h})-\mu_{h}\nabla\varphi_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}-\eta\frac{|\mu_{h}|^{2}}{\rho_{h}}

\displaystyle\phantom{\int_{\Omega}\Big(\,}-\div(\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}+\div(\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}|^{2}-\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\cdot\nabla\tau_{h}

\displaystyle\hskip 95.00014pt+\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\cdot\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}|^{2}\Big)\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{4}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{5}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\rho_{h}]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{6}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma[\mkern-3.0mu[(\partial_{t}\varphi_{h})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{2}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}]\dd s-B_{h}[\varphi_{h};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{3}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\mu_{h}]\dd s-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{1}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\tau_{h}]\dd s.

Finally, using integration by parts via Proposition

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{{\text{d}}}{\text{d}t}\hat{E}_{h}((t))&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{4}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{5}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\rho_{h}]\dd s\\[8.61108pt]
&&\displaystyle{}-\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{6}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma[\mkern-3.0mu[\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s\\[8.61108pt]
&&\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}\cup}[\mkern-3.0mu[\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\tau_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{1}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\tau_{h}]\dd s\\[8.61108pt]

&&\displaystyle-B_{h}[\varphi_{h};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}]-\int_{\Omega}\eta\frac{\mu_{h}^{2}}{\rho_{h}}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}.\\[8.61108pt]
\end{array}

(3.17)

To fulfill the discrete energy dissipation equation (

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{4}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{5}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\partial_{t}\rho_{h}]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}F_{6}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\dd s+\int_{\mathcal{E}}[\mkern-3.0mu[\partial_{t}\varphi_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}[\mkern-3.0mu[\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\tau_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\dd s-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{1}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\tau_{h}]\dd s

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{2}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}]\dd s-\int_{\mathcal{E}}F_{3}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\mu_{h})\dd s.

Since the traces of

are independent of the traces of the other quantities, the terms containing these terms and the ones without them cannot cancel each other.
This yields

and the conditions in (

\square

The generic (but non-unique) choice of consistent fluxes given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{1}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\psi_{h}]&=&-[\mkern-3.0mu[\rho_{h}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{h}]\mkern-3.0mu]\{\mkern-6.0mu\{\psi_{h}\}\mkern-6.0mu\},\\[4.30554pt]

for

) and (

).
For stabilization reasons we can add appropriate dissipative fluxes to the ones in (

(3.19)

3.2
A Time Semi-Discrete Scheme for the NSAC System

As the next step we devise an energy-stable time discretization for the mixed formulation (
2.17
) of the NSAC system.
Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
\Delta t_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
. Moreover, we introduce for some function
\Phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
the notations

\Phi^{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}):=\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},t_{n})\text{ and }\Phi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\Phi^{n+1}+\Phi^{n}}{2}\quad(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\Omega).

The temporal discretisation is of Crank–Nicholson type and chosen in a way that the discrete energy inequality holds. The resulting scheme is of second order accuracy.

Definition 3.6
(Second-order time discretization)

Consider the initial conditions from (
2.12
).
A function

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}:=(\rho^{\Delta t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{\Delta t},\varphi^{\Delta t},\mu^{\Delta t},\tau^{\Delta t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\Delta t}):\Omega\to C^{0}([0,T])

with

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta t_{n}}\big({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{n}}\big)(t-t_{n})+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{n}\quad(t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}],\,n\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\})

that satisfies
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}(t)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\cap C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
for
t\in[0,T]

is called time-discrete solution of (
2.17
) if the time iterates
\rho^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+1},\varphi^{n+1},\mu^{n+1},\tau^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{n+1}
satisfy for
n\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
the equations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0&=&\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{n+1}-\rho^{n}}{\Delta t_{n}}+\div(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}),\\[12.91663pt]
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}&=&\displaystyle\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n}}{\Delta t_{n}}\right)+\div\big(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\\[8.61108pt]
&&{}\displaystyle-\div(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\\[8.61108pt]
&&{}\displaystyle+\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\tau^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\mu^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\big(\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}},\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}))\big),\\[12.91663pt]
0&=&{}\displaystyle\frac{\varphi^{n+1}-\varphi^{n}}{\Delta t_{n}}+\nabla\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\eta\frac{\mu^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}},\\[17.22217pt]
0&=&\mu^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n+1})-\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n})+\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n+1})-\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n})}{2(\varphi^{n+1}-\varphi^{n})}\\[8.61108pt]
&&+\gamma\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}),\\[12.91663pt]
0&=&\tau^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n+1})-\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n+1})+\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n})-\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n})}{2(\rho^{n+1}-\rho^{n})}\\[8.61108pt]
&&{}-\frac{1}{4}\left(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+1}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n}|^{2}\right),\\[12.91663pt]
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}&=&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{n+1}-\nabla\varphi^{n+1}.\end{array}

(3.23)

For the sake of simplicity we have chosen here the classical function space
{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\cap C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
for the unknown
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}
. We will return to weak formulations in Section
3.3
.
The well-posedness of (
3.23
) could be assured for
\Delta t_{n}
small enough and is supposed to hold for the remainder of the section.
Different from the works
[
20
]
,
a special feature of our time discretization is the staggered implicit-explicit treatment of the discrete
\varphi
- and
\rho
-derivative of the mixture energy density
\rho\tilde{f}
in
\eqref{eq:p2:tempsemi}_{4,5}
.
With this time discretization we can provide unconditional energy stability for the dG method like it can be achieved by convex-concave splitting methods
[
15
]
.
The following theorem provides the exact statement for the time-discrete solution
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}
of (
2.17
).

Theorem 3.7
(Time-discrete energy stability)

The time-discrete solution
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\Delta t}
of (
2.17
) as defined in Definition
3.6
satisfies
for all
n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
the estimate

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mkern-2.0mu\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n})+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{n}|^{2}\ddd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[12.91663pt]
&=&{}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mkern-2.0mu\rho^{0}\tilde{f}(\rho^{0},\varphi^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{0}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{0}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{0}|^{2}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\\[12.91663pt]
&&{}\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Delta t_{j}\int_{\Omega}\mkern-2.0mu\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}(\varphi^{j+\frac{1}{2}},\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{j+\frac{1}{2}}):\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{j+\frac{1}{2}}+\eta\frac{|\mu^{j+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}}{\rho^{j+\frac{1}{2}}}\dd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}.\end{array}

(3.24)

Proof:

We define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{\mu}&:=&\displaystyle\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n+1})-\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n})\\[6.45831pt]
&&{}\displaystyle+\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n+1})-\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n})\\[8.61108pt]

&&\displaystyle{}+\rho^{n+1}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n+1},\varphi^{n})-\rho^{n}\tilde{f}(\rho^{n},\varphi^{n})\end{array}

(3.25)

By multiplying
\eqref{eq:p2:tempsemi}_{1}
with
\tau^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
,
\eqref{eq:p2:tempsemi}_{2}
with

and

with

(, which is analogous to the choice of test functions in the proof of Theorem

)
we obtain after integrating over the domain
\Omega
and using (
3.25
) the equations

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\Delta t_{n}}T^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{\mu}+\frac{\rho^{n+1}-\rho^{n}}{4\Delta t_{n}}\left(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+1}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt+\div(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\tau^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\ddd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2\Delta t_{n}}\left(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+1}|^{2}-|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n}|^{2}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\div(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\div(\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\tau^{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle-\mu^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}(\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}},\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}))\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\ddd\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},

&&\displaystyle{}+\frac{\varphi^{n+1}-\varphi^{n}}{\Delta t_{n}}\left(\frac{T^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{\tau}}{2(\varphi^{n+1}-\varphi^{n})}-\gamma\div(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right),\\[12.91663pt]

5
Conclusions

We presented a fully-discrete dG method to solve the initial boundary value problem for the compressible NSAC system under isothermal conditions. The method is shown to be of second-order in space and time, mass conservative, and to be unconditionally energy stable.
For the energy stability we exploited the energetic
structure of the evolution equations: the dG method relies on an equivalent re-formulation of the original NSAC system such that the
nonlinear variational derivatives of the free energy function can be expressed as elements of the dG ansatz space.

We conjecture that the same approach can be used to derive energy-stable schemes for related isothermal systems like Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard equations
[
32
]
or nonlocal
diffuse-interface approaches
[
25
]
. More challenging is the extension of the approach to temperature-dependent two-phase systems or multiphase and multicomponent mixtures with complex viscous stress tensors and diffusion operators. The same applies for
construction of energy-stable high-order methods in the regime of low Mach numbers which is typically given for at least the liquid phase.
