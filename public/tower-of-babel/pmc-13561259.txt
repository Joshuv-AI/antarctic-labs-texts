DAT-negative autoimmune hemolytic anemia following acute hepatitis a in a pediatric patient with elevated IgA: a rare case report and literature review

Introduction

Acute hepatitis A (AHA) is a common viral infection caused by the hepatitis A virus (HAV)[1]. HAV is a highly contagious pathogen that is common in childhood but is usually asymptomatic. The clinical course of AHA is self-limiting, with a mild course in 99.9% of cases[1]. Diagnosis is confirmed by the detection of HAV IgM, alongside elevated bilirubin and abnormal liver function tests[1,2]. Apart from vaccination, there are no specific treatments for AHA. Complications occur only in 0.1% of patients, including cholestasis, acute liver failure (ALF), aplastic anemia, and, very rarely, autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)[1]. Preexisting chronic viral hepatic infections, genetic diseases, autoimmune diseases, and older age increase the risk of complications such as ALF[3]. Consequently, underlying liver disease must be ruled out, especially in healthy children who develop AHA complications, as in the present case.

AIHA is a rare condition among children, with an incidence rate of 8/1 000 000 per year[4]. Approximately 50% of pediatric AIHA cases are secondary to viral infections, autoimmune disorders, and immunodeficiency syndromes, while the remainder are primary or idiopathic[5,6]. Corticosteroids remain first-line therapy, with overall response rates of approximately 80–83% in pediatric cohorts[5,6]. The clinical presentation of AIHA is non-specific, including jaundice, dark urine, and signs of anemia [7]. Laboratory results typically show low hemoglobin, elevated mean corpuscular volume (MCV), increased reticulocytes, raised lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), elevated unconjugated bilirubin, and decreased haptoglobin[1,7]. Although 3–10% of AIHA cases are DAT-negative – varying with test sensitivity – an extended DAT with monospecific reagents (anti-IgA, anti-IgM, and anti-C3) should be considered when routine testing is negative in unexplained hemolysis, as standard assays may miss non-IgG antibodies or low-affinity binding[8,9]. Hereditary conditions and hemoglobinopathies must be ruled out, as they are common in the Middle East and AIHA is rare[7].

While hepatitis A is typically self-limited, its association with DAT-negative AIHA is exceedingly rare, particularly in pediatric patients. A PubMed search identified only seven reported cases of AIHA following AHA – all in adults, all DAT-positive, and some complicated by pure red cell aplasia or pancytopenia[10]. The present case is the first documented pediatric DAT-negative AIHA following AHA.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old Middle Eastern female presented to our hospital with severe itching, recurrent dark urine, and worsening jaundice. Upon examination, she exhibited nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Four weeks prior to admission, she had been diagnosed with AHA, confirmed by positive hepatitis A virus immunoglobulin M (HAV IgM) at 3.6 S/CO. A summary of the clinical course is presented in Table 1. There was no significant family or social history. Laboratory investigations at that time revealed an elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level of 701 U/L; a total bilirubin of 26.5 mg/dL (direct 21.3 mg/dL and indirect 5.2 mg/dL); an international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.16; and a prothrombin time (PT) of 22.6 seconds. Extensive testing for underlying liver diseases – including viral hepatitis (hepatitis B virus [HBV], hepatitis C virus [HCV], human immunodeficiency virus, cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, herpes simplex virus 1 and 2), metabolic disorders (hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency), and autoimmune conditions [antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-mitochondrial antibody, anti-liver–kidney antibody, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies] – was negative. Despite supportive treatment with intravenous fluids and ursodeoxycholic acid, the patient’s condition did not significantly improve over the following 4 weeks. The patient had not received any medications commonly associated with drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia prior to the onset of hemolysis. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was negative on three separate occasions.

Further diagnostic workup included iron studies (serum iron 49.1 µg/dL, total iron-binding capacity 282.6 µg/dL, ferritin 612 ng/mL, and vitamin B12 1393 pg/mL), which were within normal limits. Hemoglobin electrophoresis was normal, excluding hemoglobinopathies. Peripheral blood smear revealed polychromasia, spherocytosis, and nucleated red blood cells, findings consistent with active hemolysis and compensatory erythropoiesis. No schistocytes were identified, making a microangiopathic process less likely. Testing for cold agglutinin disease was negative. To exclude hematologic malignancy and other primary bone marrow disorders in the setting of severe unexplained hemolytic anemia, a bone marrow biopsy was performed. The biopsy demonstrated hypercellularity with erythroid hyperplasia consistent with reactive changes secondary to hemolysis (Fig. 1). Peripheral blood flow cytometry demonstrated no immunophenotypic evidence of acute leukemia or paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH), while bone marrow examination showed reactive erythroid hyperplasia without evidence of hematologic malignancy. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and hereditary spherocytosis were ruled out through specific enzyme testing and osmotic fragility studies. Given the severity of hemolysis and the need to exclude occult lymphoproliferative disorders as causes of secondary AIHA, contrast-enhanced CT imaging was performed. No lymphadenopathy or radiological evidence of lymphoma was identified. Immunologic studies revealed normal cryoglobulin, anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), complement C3, D-dimer, and fibrinogen levels; ANA was positive at low titer (1/80). Notably, immunoglobulin A (IgA) was significantly elevated at 596.2 mg/dL (normal range: 70–400 mg/dL), while immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M (IgM) were within normal limits. The combination of laboratory evidence of hemolysis, repeated negative DAT results, exclusion of hereditary and alternative acquired causes of hemolysis, temporal association with AHA infection, and subsequent response to corticosteroid therapy supported a diagnosis of probable DAT-negative AIHA. Elevated IgA was considered an additional observation but was not regarded as confirmatory evidence of the underlying mechanism. The patient was started on oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg/day, consistent with standard first-line therapy for pediatric AIHA[6,11]. Clinical improvement was evident within 1 week: hemoglobin increased from 8.1 g/dL to 10.4 g/dL (a rise of 2.3 g/dL), accompanied by resolution of jaundice, malaise, and breathlessness. A gradual steroid taper over 4–6 months was planned once hemoglobin stabilization was achieved, in accordance with standard protocols[6,11]. Following treatment, the patient experienced no adverse effects from corticosteroid therapy. She reported significant relief from her presenting symptoms – particularly the resolution of jaundice, breathlessness, and fatigue – and was reassured by the absence of anticipated long-term complications. At 6 months of follow-up, corticosteroids had been successfully tapered without recurrence of hemolysis or other complications. Written informed consent for publication of this case report was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians following a comprehensive explanation of the importance of the case and the confidentiality of the information provided.

Discussion

We report a case of a female child who developed DAT-negative acute hemolytic anemia 4 weeks after a confirmed severe AHA. Treatment with oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg/day resulted in rapid clinical and laboratory improvement, with hemoglobin rising from 8.1 to 10.4 g/dL within 1 week.

A literature search was performed in PubMed using the terms (“Hepatitis A”[MeSH] OR “hepatitis A” OR HAV) AND (“Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia”[MeSH] OR “autoimmune hemolytic anemia” OR AIHA). This search identified a small number of published reports describing autoimmune or presumed immune-mediated hemolytic anemia associated with AHA infection (Table 2). Seven previously published cases were identified, although two reports were available only as abstracts and detailed clinical information could not be reviewed. Reported patients ranged in age from childhood to 65 years, with most cases occurring in adults. Five cases were DAT-positive and two were DAT-negative. Additional hematologic abnormalities included pure red cell aplasia and pancytopenia in some patients, whereas our patient demonstrated isolated hemolysis with reactive erythroid hyperplasia. Despite heterogeneity in presentation and diagnostic certainty, the overall prognosis was favorable, with recovery or remission reported in all cases following immunosuppressive treatment, most commonly with corticosteroids. Our case differs from previously reported cases in several important respects. This case represents the youngest fully characterized patient identified in our review, one of only three reported DAT-negative cases associated with AHA infection, and the first reported case with markedly elevated serum IgA levels. While the significance of this finding remains uncertain, the current literature does not establish an association between elevated serum IgA and AIHA. Therefore, the elevated IgA observed in our patient should be considered a noteworthy clinical observation that warrants further investigation rather than evidence of causality[9,10,12,13]. Compared with previously reported adult cases, our patient was younger, demonstrated isolated hemolysis without additional cytopenias, and underwent a more extensive diagnostic evaluation, including cold agglutinin testing, flow cytometry, bone marrow examination, and CT imaging to exclude alternative causes of hemolysis. Initially, nonimmune hemolytic anemia was considered because of the negative DAT, the high prevalence of hereditary hemolytic disorders in the Middle East, and the extreme rarity of AIHA following AHA[10–12].

Systematic exclusion of these hereditary conditions is essential in Middle Eastern pediatric populations. G6PD deficiency affects 2–12% of this population, while β-thalassemia and α-thalassemia carrier frequencies are approximately 1.5% and 5%, respectively[11]; hereditary spherocytosis, though less common, must also be considered. In our patient, comprehensive testing excluded these conditions: normal hemoglobin electrophoresis ruled out hemoglobinopathies; normal G6PD enzyme activity excluded G6PD deficiency; and negative osmotic fragility testing excluded hereditary spherocytosis. This thorough exclusion was essential given their high background prevalence and potential to mimic or coexist with immune-mediated hemolysis[6,14]. Alternative acquired causes of hemolysis were also considered. Testing for cold agglutinin disease was negative, and peripheral blood flow cytometry demonstrated no evidence of PNH. In addition, bone marrow examination and CT imaging showed no evidence of underlying hematologic malignancy or lymphoproliferative disease.

Given that 3–11% of AIHA cases are DAT-negative, this diagnosis was considered despite negative results on three occasions. Further immunological testing revealed only weak ANA positivity (1/80) and markedly elevated IgA (596.2 mg/dL), raising suspicion of an underlying autoimmune mechanism.

The literature describes several mechanisms underlying DAT-negative AIHA, including red blood cell (RBC)-bound IgG below the detection threshold of routine DAT, RBC-bound IgA or IgM antibodies, and low-affinity autoantibodies. More sensitive techniques, including monospecific anti-IgA, anti-IgM, and anti-C3 DAT assays, flow cytometric detection of RBC-bound immunoglobulins, and specialized immunohematologic testing performed in reference laboratories, may improve diagnostic sensitivity in suspected DAT-negative cases. In our patient, the rapid clinical and hematological response to prednisolone (hemoglobin rise of 2.3 g/dL within 1 week) was supportive of an immune-mediated process, although treatment response alone cannot establish the diagnosis[18]. It remains unclear whether IgG or IgA caused AIHA in this patient, as they account for 80% and 4% of DAT-negative hemolytic anemia cases, respectively[18]. IgG is suspected because it accounts for most cases of Coombs-negative AIHA–95% –including RBC-bound IgG and low-affinity IgG[18]. Nevertheless, the significant increase in IgA levels in this patient raises the possibility of IgA-associated AIHA. However, there are two factors complicating the IgA-associated AIHA hypothesis. First, there are no studies in the literature confirming an association between AIHA and elevated serum IgA levels, and the only confirmatory method is detecting IgA on the surface of RBCs in reference laboratories. Second, elevated IgA levels have been observed in conditions involving chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis, varying degrees of biliary duct obstruction, and prolonged cholestasis, possibly reflecting a reactive increase rather than a primary cause of hemolysis[19,20].

Diagnosing DAT-negative AIHA following an acute viral infection like hepatitis A can be challenging for healthcare providers and can create significant uncertainty for patients and their families. Unlike the more common DAT-positive type, DAT-negative AIHA is easily overlooked due to negative test results, potentially delaying diagnosis and appropriate treatment. This case underscores several critical principles. First, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for AIHA in unexplained post-viral hemolysis, even when the DAT is negative, and recognize that the clinical picture may evolve as the immune response develops. Close monitoring of hemolytic indicators – including hemoglobin, reticulocytes, and bilirubin – guides treatment decisions and the timing of intervention. Second, an extended DAT with monospecific reagents (anti-IgA, anti-IgM, and anti-C3) should be pursued when available, as standard testing detects only IgG and C3d and may miss purely IgA-mediated or IgM-mediated AIHA[8,9]. In settings where extended testing is unavailable, empiric corticosteroid therapy can serve both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, as a rapid response strongly supports immune-mediated hemolysis. Finally, access to reference laboratories for advanced immunohematologic testing, including flow cytometry for RBC-bound immunoglobulins, should be sought to avoid missed diagnoses and to guide optimal long-term management[8,9].

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, specialized immunohematologic testing, including monospecific anti-IgA, anti-IgM, and anti-C3 DAT assays, was unavailable; therefore, the diagnosis remains a probable rather than definitively confirmed case of DAT-negative AIHA. Second, although serum IgA was markedly elevated, red blood cell-bound IgA could not be assessed, precluding confirmation of an IgA-mediated mechanism. Third, despite extensive investigation, the diagnosis relied on a combination of clinical findings, exclusion of alternative causes, and treatment response rather than on direct immunologic confirmation. Finally, as a single case report, the findings may not be generalizable to other patients.

Conclusions

This case represents one of the very few reported pediatric cases of DAT-negative AIHA following AHA infection and is the first reported case with markedly elevated serum IgA levels. First, DAT-negative AIHA should be suspected in unexplained post-viral hemolysis despite negative DAT, as a rapid response to corticosteroids may support an immune-mediated process. Second, systematic exclusion of hereditary hemolytic anemias – including G6PD deficiency, thalassemias, and hereditary spherocytosis – is essential in high-prevalence populations in regions such as the Middle East before attributing hemolysis to immune causes. Third, extended DAT with monospecific reagents (anti-IgA, anti-IgM, and anti-C3) should be pursued when available, as the standard DAT detects only IgG and C3d and may miss non-IgG antibodies responsible for 3–11% of AIHA cases. Fourth, empiric corticosteroid therapy may be both therapeutically beneficial and diagnostically informative in selected patients, with rapid hematological improvement providing supportive evidence for an immune-mediated process even in the absence of confirmatory testing.

Fifth, access to reference laboratories for advanced immunohematologic testing should be sought to confirm antibody class, guide targeted therapy, and avoid missed diagnoses. Finally, AHA-associated AIHA carries a favorable prognosis with appropriate treatment, as all seven reported cases – including both DAT-positive and DAT-negative variants – achieved recovery with immunosuppressive therapy. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for considering AIHA in any patient with unexplained hemolysis following a viral illness, particularly when hereditary causes are excluded and DAT is negative, as early recognition and treatment can prevent diagnostic delay and improve patient outcomes. Although specialized immunohematologic testing was unavailable and the diagnosis therefore remains a probable rather than a definitively confirmed case of DAT-negative AIHA, the overall clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic findings strongly support an immune-mediated etiology.
