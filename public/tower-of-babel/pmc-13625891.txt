Gender diversity and cholesterol in early adolescence: Findings from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study
Authors: Jason M Nagata, Priyadharshini Balasubramanian, Audrey Lopez, Christiane K Helmer, Jennifer H Wong, Iris Yuefan Shao, Christopher D Otmar, Kyle T Ganson, Alexander Testa, Erin E Dooley, Holly C Gooding, Kelley Pettee Gabriel, Fiona C Baker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPrevious research has shown that gender minorities face health inequities, including mental and physical health disparities, but few studies have examined cardiovascular disparities, such as lipid profiles, in early adolescents. Monitoring cholesterol in adolescence is important, as early lipid levels can predict future cardiovascular risk. This study aimed to assess associations between multiple measures of gender diversity and cholesterol profiles among early adolescents.MethodsData from 1511 participants aged 10–14 years in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (2018–2021) were analyzed. Gender diversity was measured using transgender identity, felt gender (how much one feels like a boy/girl), gender expression (how gender is communicated through appearance and mannerisms), and gender non-contentedness (desire to be a sex or gender different from sex assigned at birth). Cholesterol measures included total, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and non-HDL cholesterol. Multivariable linear and modified Poisson regression models adjusted for sociodemographic factors were used to estimate associations.ResultsParticipants were 47.0% female, 37.9% racial and ethnic minority, and 1.9–19.1% were gender-diverse depending on the measure. Higher felt gender and gender non-contentedness scores were associated with lower HDL cholesterol. Gender non-contentedness was associated with higher non-HDL cholesterol.ConclusionsSome measures of gender diversity were associated with less favorable lipid profiles, including reduced HDL cholesterol. These findings build on prior research examining cardiovascular health in gender-diverse populations and highlight the need to investigate mechanisms underlying these associations.Keywords: Adolescent, Gender, Felt gender, Gender identity, Transgender, Lipid, HDL, Cholesterol

Introduction

Gender minority (including but not limited to transgender, gender non-conforming, and gender expansive) individuals have gender identities, expressions, or behaviors that differ from societal norms associated with their sex assigned at birth [1,2]. Gender minority communities are at greater risk of experiencing health inequities compared with their cis and gender-conforming counterparts [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Prior research documents elevated discrimination, bullying, and chronic stress, contributing to higher rates of mental health conditions, substance use, risky sexual behaviors, self-harm, and suicide [[3], [4], [5]]. Transgender adolescents, particularly females and racial and ethnic minorities, also experience a higher prevalence of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), sexually transmitted infections, substance use, and suicidality compared with cisgender peers [6].

Although mental and sexual health inequities are well studied, emerging evidence also indicates cardiometabolic disparities among gender minority adolescents and adults [7,8]. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis reported a 40% higher risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among transgender individuals compared with cisgender counterparts, along with increased CVD-related mortality for both transgender women and men [7]. Gender minority adolescents also have a higher prevalence of obesity than cisgender adolescents [9], a critical concern given the links between elevated body mass index (BMI), dyslipidemia, hypertension, and later cardiometabolic disease [9,10]. Cholesterol levels in early adolescence are particularly important, as they serve as early indicators of future cardiovascular risk, including atherosclerosis [11,12]. National U.S. data show sex differences in pediatric lipid profiles, with boys having a higher prevalence of low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and girls having a higher prevalence of elevated non-HDL cholesterol [13]. However, cholesterol patterns among gender minority youth remain understudied.

Stigma can influence health by interfering with cognitive, affective, interpersonal, and physiological processes [14]. Minority Stress Theory has been utilized to explain how individuals from stigmatized minority groups experience excess stressors related to their minority status that can influence their mental and physical health, resulting in health disparities among these groups [15,16]. Stressors can be proximal (internalized transphobia, hypervigilance, and gender identity concealment) or distal (lack of gender affirmation and stigma, rejection, discrimination, and mistreatment related to gender identity) [17]. Through cardiovascular and cortisol responses to stress, it has been suspected that minority stress is a contributor to CVD, hypertension, and increased BMI [15,17]. Though the influences of minority stress on metabolic dysfunction and CVD in gender non-conforming populations have been understudied, it is a possible contributor given its influence on racial, ethnic, and sexual minority groups [[16], [17], [18]]. Gender identity is increasingly recognized as multidimensional rather than binary, and gender non-conformity may occur across both cisgender and transgender populations [19]. Atypical gender expression can also add a layer of complexity to gender identity, as it does not necessarily indicate transgender or nonbinary identity and may occur in both cisgender and transgender individuals [19]. We examined associations between nuanced measures of gender diversity and cholesterol levels in a national sample of U.S. early adolescents. We hypothesized that greater gender diversity would be associated with less optimal lipid profiles, including lower HDL and higher non-HDL and total cholesterol.

Methods

Study population

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is an ongoing longitudinal cohort study examining brain development and health among U.S. adolescents. At baseline (2016–2018), participants were recruited from 21 sites nationwide; study design and procedures have been described previously [20]. The present analysis used data from Year 2 (2018–2020; ages 10–14) and Year 3 (2019–2021; ages 11–14).

Gender diversity was assessed at Year 3 because its higher prevalence compared with earlier assessment waves provided greater statistical power for the analysis. Collection of CVD risk measures in Year 2, which required in-person visits, was disrupted by COVID-19–related research suspensions. Participants without Year 2 cholesterol data were re-contacted in Year 3; however, cholesterol measurements remained unavailable for most. Consequently, Year 3 cholesterol data were used when Year 2 data were unavailable. Overall, 78.3% of cholesterol measurements were obtained in Year 2 and 21.7% in Year 3.

We analyzed data from the ABCD 5.0 release. The analytic sample included adolescents with available cholesterol biospecimens and gender diversity data (N = 1511). Sociodemographic characteristics of included versus excluded participants are presented in Table S1. Compared with included participants, excluded participants were more likely to be racial and ethnic minorities. The study was approved by the University of California, San Diego Institutional Review Board (IRB) (160,091) and the IRBs of participating sites. One of the co-authors identified as transgender and contributed directly to the writing and interpretation of the findings. In addition, we engaged with a San Francisco-based youth advisory board including gender-diverse youth who informed the conceptualization of the study. Written caregiver consent and adolescent assent were obtained.

Measures

Independent variables

Adolescent-reported gender diversity was evaluated using several measures, including transgender identity, felt gender (e.g., how much one feels like a boy or a girl), gender expression (e.g., how gender is communicated through appearance and mannerisms), and gender non-contentedness (e.g., wishing to be a sex or gender different from sex assigned at birth).

Transgender identity was assessed using the question, “Are you transgender?” and was examined as a categorical variable. Responses included “no,” “yes,” “maybe,” “don't understand the question,” and “refuse to answer.” Following prior methods, we combined “yes” and “maybe” responses because of their low prevalence, thereby increasing statistical power [21]. We combined “don't understand the question” and “refuse to answer” but do not report these results.

Felt gender was assessed as both a continuous and categorical measure using the same two sex-specific questions: “How much do you feel like a boy/girl?” and “How much do you feel like a girl/boy?” The wording of the questions corresponded to participants’ sex assigned at birth. Responses ranged from 1 (“Not at all”) to 5 (“Totally”). For the continuous measure, responses to the two questions were averaged to create a summary score for each participant. The resulting score was coded or reverse-coded such that higher scores indicated greater gender diversity.

For the categorical measure, responses to the same two questions using the same 1–5 response scale were categorized into four groups: cisgender, 1-STEP, 2-STEP, and minority group, based on the pattern and degree of alignment between participants’ felt gender and sex assigned at birth. The 1-STEP category indicates a slight divergence from assigned sex, such as feeling totally like a boy and a little like a girl, or mostly like a boy and not at all like a girl, among participants assigned male at birth (with equivalent coding for those assigned female at birth). The 2-STEP category indicates greater divergence, such as feeling mostly like a boy and a little like a girl (with equivalent coding for those assigned female at birth). All other combinations of responses were categorized as minority group.

Continuous measures included gender non-contentedness and gender expression. Gender non-contentedness was assessed using the question, “How much have you had the wish to be a girl/boy?” Gender expression was assessed using the question, “How much have you dressed or acted as a girl/boy during play?” Responses for gender non-contentedness and gender expression ranged from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”). Constructs were coded such that lower scores indicated higher conformity to the sex assigned at birth. Full measures, including item wording, definitions, response options, and coding of gender constructs, are provided in Table S2.

Dependent variables

Non-fasting total and HDL cholesterol were measured from blood samples collected via standard venipuncture and processed according to laboratory protocols. High total cholesterol was defined as ≥200 mg/dL [22], and low HDL cholesterol as <40 mg/dL. Non-HDL cholesterol was calculated as total minus HDL cholesterol, with high non-HDL cholesterol defined as >145 mg/dL. Total, HDL, and non-HDL cholesterol were analyzed as both continuous and binary variables using these cutoffs.

Covariates

Potential confounding was addressed by adjusting for age, sex assigned at birth (female [reference], male), race and ethnicity (Asian, Black, Latino/Hispanic, White [reference], other), household income (<$75,000, ≥$75,000 [reference]), and highest parental education (high school or less, college or more [reference]). Research has found that gender diversity varies by sociodemographics [23,24].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics summarized variables using means, standard deviations, and percentages. Multivariable linear regression assessed cross-sectional associations between gender diversity measures and continuous lipid outcomes (HDL, total, and non-HDL cholesterol), resulting in 21 models. Multivariable modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation similarly examined cross-sectional associations with binary outcomes (low HDL, high total, and high non-HDL cholesterol), resulting in 21 models. Modified Poisson regression was used as an alternative to logistic regression because prior research has shown smaller standard errors for modified Poisson models compared with logistic regression models [25]. We report models adjusted for sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, race and ethnicity, household income, parental education). ABCD Study sample weights based on the U.S. Census American Community Survey were applied to improve the representativeness of the analytic sample to the U.S. population [26]. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to adjust the false discovery rate given multiple statistical tests, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant [27].

Sensitivity analyses controlling for age, sex, race and ethnicity, household income, parental education, BMI, physical activity, sleep duration, dietary intake, and pubertal status were conducted to estimate the cross-sectional associations between gender diversity and cholesterol (Table S3).

Sensitivity analyses examining the cross-sectional associations between gender diversity, specifically transgender identity categorized as “no,” “yes,” “maybe,” or “don't understand the question/refuse to answer,” and cholesterol were conducted and are reported in Table S4; we did not present the results for “don't understand the question/refuse to answer.”

We assessed the cross-sectional associations between gender diversity and cholesterol after applying inverse probability of inclusion weighting based on race and ethnicity, given the significant differences in race and ethnicity between included and excluded participants (Table S1). The results are presented in Table S5. Lastly, we examined cross-sectional associations between gender diversity and cholesterol in Year 3 (Table S6). Associations in Year 2 were not examined because of the low prevalence of gender diversity, which resulted in underpowered analyses.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the 1511 early adolescents in the analytic sample. Nearly half were female (47.0%), and 37.9% identified as racial or ethnic minorities. The mean age was 12.9 years (SD = 0.7). Overall, 1.9% identified as transgender or “maybe” transgender, and 19.1% were classified as non-cisgender based on the categorical felt gender measure. High total cholesterol was observed in 7.9% of participants, 8.0% met criteria for low HDL cholesterol, and 5.4% met criteria for high non-HDL cholesterol. Mean (SD) total cholesterol was 157.1 (29.3) mg/dL, HDL cholesterol was 55.9 (13.1) mg/dL, and non-HDL cholesterol was 101.2 (26.5) mg/dL.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of 1511 U.S. early adolescents aged 10–14 years, greater gender diversity—reflected by felt gender category and higher felt gender, gender non-contentedness scores, and gender expression—was associated with lower HDL cholesterol and a higher prevalence of clinically low HDL cholesterol. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal or causal relationships between gender diversity and cholesterol levels. The associations between minority felt gender category and prevalence of clinically low HDL cholesterol, gender non-contentedness scores and HDL cholesterol levels, and gender non-contentedness scores and prevalence of clinically low HDL cholesterol were significant, whereas associations between felt gender category and HDL cholesterol levels were no longer significant after multiple test correction. Greater gender diversity, as measured by gender non-contentedness, was associated with a higher prevalence of high non-HDL cholesterol. Additionally, identifying as transgender or “maybe” transgender was associated with lower HDL cholesterol and higher non-HDL cholesterol.

These findings align with prior studies in older adolescents and adults showing lower HDL and higher non-HDL cholesterol among transgender individuals. However, it is important to emphasize that the associations observed in this study are modest and do not represent established cardiovascular disparities in early adolescence. Instead, the findings may represent early signs of health patterns that warrant further investigation. Systematic reviews have reported reduced HDL levels among transgender men [28,29], and a separate review found similarly lower HDL levels among transgender women [30]. Elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, and total cholesterol have also been documented in transgender men and women [[31], [32], [33]]. However, existing studies have focused exclusively on individuals receiving gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]], and little is known about lipid profiles among transgender or gender-diverse youth not using GAHT. Although GAHT use was not assessed in this study, several factors suggest limited exposure, including the low prevalence of transgender identification (1.9%), the early age of participants (10–14 years), and structural barriers to GAHT access. Consequently, observed lipid differences may reflect factors other than hormone therapy.

Minority stress has been studied as a contributor to CVD, hypertension, and elevated BMI in gender-minority populations [15,17]. While its harmful influences on sexual, racial, and ethnic minority groups are documented, influences on gender non-conforming populations remain understudied [17,18]. Social determinants of health, including gender identity, race and ethnicity, social class, and age, play a role in the formation of inequalities, discrimination, and marginalization, which may contribute to people's health and well-being in complex ways [34]. Cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in transgender and gender-diverse populations may be partly driven by psychosocial stressors, including distal stressors like structural violence, stigma, discrimination, and reduced healthcare access, and proximal stressors like internalized transphobia, gender identity concealment, and hypervigilance [17]. A sense of mastery, or perceived control over one's life, has also been proposed as a potential factor contributing to CVD risk by shaping or modifying the influences of minority stress [35]. Individuals who identified as transgender or “maybe” transgender may experience a decreased sense of mastery, which could exacerbate the influences of minority stress and further contribute to CVD. Stress may influencce cardiovascular health via neuroendocrine pathways, including sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activation, leading to inflammation, immune dysregulation, and elevated cholesterol [36]. Future research should examine how psychosocial stressors in gender-diverse adolescents relate to cholesterol profiles [17,35].

Our study has several notable strengths. The large, diverse, national sample of early adolescents enhances generalizability and allows detection of nuanced variations. Focusing on early adolescence captures a critical period of physical and mental development. Using multidimensional, continuous measures of gender, including felt gender, gender non-contentedness, and gender expression, rather than binary categories, enabled identification of subtle variations in gender identity, including among adolescents still exploring their gender [21]. Finally, the study's focus on physical health, specifically cholesterol, addresses a less-studied area and complements prior research on mental health and health behaviors, providing a more comprehensive view of health disparities among gender-diverse youth.

Several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and temporal ordering. We used Year 3 gender diversity data because its higher prevalence compared with earlier waves provided greater statistical power. Cholesterol measures were from either the prior assessment (Year 2, ∼78%) or the same assessment (Year 3, ∼22%), limiting the ability to establish temporal ordering. Future analyses using multi-assessment longitudinal data are needed to establish temporality and potential mediators. In addition, we did not analyze gender spectrum due to the small number of participants available for these models, which limited statistical power and the ability to detect meaningful associations. Gender diversity measures relied on adolescent self-report, which may be influenced by response bias due to misunderstanding or intentional misreporting. Additionally, early adolescence is a critical period of gender identity development due to puberty and social environments. As a result, some participants may still be in the process of exploring their gender identity and expression, and their gender identity categories may not be fully established [37]. The study did not account for GAHT, which can influence lipid profiles [29,32,33,38]. In a 2015 U.S. survey of transgender adults, it was found that the majority of adolescents begin GAHT between the ages of 14 and 18 years [39], which is above the age range of the current study (10–14 years). However, we cannot rule out that some participants, particularly those aged 13, may have received puberty blockers or hormone therapy, which could independently influence lipid profiles [40]. To further assess this possibility, we reviewed ABCD medication data for commonly used gender-affirming medications (e.g., estradiol, testosterone, histrelin/leuprolide, spironolactone, and norethindrone) and found no use of these medications among participants in the analytic sample. Although ABCD collected information on medication use, it did not capture whether these medications were specifically being used for gender-affirming purposes. Future ABCD Study waves should collect information on gender-affirming medication use to better characterize its potential influence on cholesterol profiles.

The use of non-fasting lipid levels could be a potential limitation, as triglyceride levels in particular have been found to increase in the postprandial state [41]. However, current guidelines, including those from the American Heart Association, accept non-fasting lipid levels to estimate atherosclerotic CVD risk given that the difference between non-fasting and fasting LDL cholesterol levels is negligible and the increase in triglycerides following meals is typically small [41,42].

Sociodemographics, BMI, physical activity, diet, sleep duration, and pubertal status were examined as potential confounders in sensitivity analyses; however, because of the cross-sectional design, we cannot determine whether these covariates function as a confounder, mediator, or collider in the observed associations. Pubertal status was not included in the primary models; thus, pubertal development may not have been fully accounted for in the primary analyses.

Although the study aimed to capture gender diversity, the minority group felt gender category combined diverse response patterns to increase statistical power, which may conflate distinct gender experiences and limit the interpretability of findings for this group. In addition, although “yes” and “maybe” transgender identity responses were combined to increase statistical power, the low prevalence of transgender identity may have resulted in underpowered analyses with imprecise estimates and wide confidence intervals. Thus, findings for this subgroup should be interpreted as exploratory given the limited statistical power. Future studies should consider oversampling gender minority youth to increase the number of cases in this rare population. Selection bias is another possible limitation, as excluded participants were more likely to be racial and ethnic minorities, though no significant differences were observed by age, sex, household income, or parental education. Missing cholesterol data may have also contributed to selection bias, particularly given disruptions to data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic. To assess the potential influence of these differences, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability of inclusion weighting based on race and ethnicity, which yielded results highly similar to those of the primary analysis. Nevertheless, differences in race and ethnicity between included and excluded participants may still limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to racial and ethnic minority populations.

Conclusion

This study found that some measures of gender diversity were associated with higher non-HDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol in early adolescents. These findings underscore the importance of considering gender identity in evaluating cardiovascular health. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying these lipid differences, including the role of minority stress, particularly among gender-diverse youth not using GAHT. Such work can inform more inclusive healthcare practices and public health guidelines to support cardiovascular health in gender-diverse adolescents.
