From Consensus-Based Optimization to Evolution Strategies: Proof of Global Convergence
Massimo Fornasier 1,2,3, Hui Huang 4, Jona Klemenc 1,2, Greta Malaspina 5

Abstract
. Consensus-based optimization (CBO) is a powerful and versatile zero-order multi-particle method designed to provably solve high-dimensional global optimization problems, including those that are genuinely nonconvex or nonsmooth. The method relies on a balance between stochastic exploration and contraction toward a consensus point, which is defined via the Laplace principle as a proxy for the global minimizer. In this paper, we introduce new CBO variants that address practical and theoretical limitations of the original formulation of this novel optimization methodology. First, we propose a model called δ \delta -CBO , which incorporates nonvanishing diffusion to prevent premature collapse to suboptimal states. We also develop a numerically stable implementation, the Consensus Freezing scheme , that remains robust even for arbitrarily large time steps by freezing the consensus point over time intervals. We connect these models through appropriate asymptotic limits. Furthermore, we derive from the Consensus Freezing scheme by suitable time rescaling and asymptotics a further algorithm, the Consensus Hopping scheme , which can be interpreted as a form of ( 1 , λ ) (1,\lambda) -Evolution Strategy. For all these schemes, we characterize for the first time the invariant measures and establish global convergence results, including exponential convergence rates.

Introduction

In this paper, we introduce, analyze, and connect a class of easy-to-implement, efficient, and inherently scalable (highly parallelizable) methods that can
provably
solve some of the most challenging problems in continuous optimization, including those that are highly nonconvex and nonsmooth, while offering guaranteed convergence rates.
The class of methods we analyze in a unified manner includes Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO)
[
62

, Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)
[

[

[

[

We prove that actually all these methods are globally convergent to global minimizers.

Our findings represent substantial advances by delivering definitive theoretical descriptions and provide simple and practically relevant guidance for achieving the most effective implementations.

Traditional optimization methodologies, most notably gradient-based schemes and quasi-Newton methods, depend heavily on local derivative information to steer the search toward minimizers. This intrinsic locality, however, becomes a major obstacle when facing genuine nonconvex or nonsmooth objectives
f
, which represent some of the most difficult classes of problems in modern optimization:

(1)

(\arg)\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x).

Nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization arises across a broad range of high-impact applications. In engineering —spanning control, robotics, design, and chemical processes— nonlinear physical models naturally lead to nonconvex formulations. In computer vision and imaging, nonconvex models and nonsmooth regularizers enable tasks such as image denoising (e.g. nonconvex total variation), computer-generated holography, and depth fusion.
In machine learning, nonconvex nonsmooth optimization underpins the training of deep neural networks with nonsmooth activations (e.g., ReLU), sparsity-promoting priors such as the
\ell_{1}
-norm, and the highly nonconvex minimax problems central to GANs.
Beyond these domains, such optimization problems also appear in mathematical finance (e.g., complex portfolio design), computational biology (e.g., protein folding), and mechanics involving nonsmooth interactions.

Metaheuristic approaches, such as Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimization, Ant Colony Optimization

Yet, despite their empirical strength, the majority of these techniques offer only limited theoretical insight: robust convergence guarantees and quantitative convergence rates to global minima are typically missing, especially in high-dimensional settings involving nonconvex objectives. Establishing methods that can

1.3.
Methodological note

Our results refer exclusively to evolutions of densities of the laws of particles. However, through well-established quantitative mean-field limits, it is possible to show that, for
all
dynamics we consider in this paper, we can approximate the mean-field dynamics in terms of
finite particle
ones for (sufficiently large) finite time. (Recently results of quantitative uniform-in-time mean-field limits are appearing
[

,
40
,
4
]
, but we do not focus on them here.) In order to provide a concrete statement, for instance, for the model (

δ

-CBO
) we can follow the same line of arguments as in
[

, Theorem 2.6]
to obtain the following quantitative mean-field-limit estimate. Let

satisfy Assumption
2.1
and let the initial distribution satisfy
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, for any

, the following holds:

(9)

\sup_{j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\mathfrak{X}}_{t}^{j}-X_{t}^{j}|^{2}\right]\leq CN^{-1},

where
\{X_{.}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are
N
i.i.d. copies of the solution to the mean-field dynamics (

δ

-CBO
), and
\{{\mathfrak{X}}_{.}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
denotes the solution to the particle system (

δ

-CBON
).
For a finite particle approximation of the Consensus Hopping Scheme we refer to analogous bounds as (
9
), see for instance
[
64
, Lemma D.3]
.
Hence, in this paper we are not going to insist on the translation of the results of global convergence to finite particle dynamics, which follow straightforwardly from (
9
), see similar well-established results in the literature (see
[
34
,
52
]
for example).

One more important disclaimer: in view of the isotropic nature of the stochastic perturbations implemented by the schemes (

-CBON
), (

) and, (

), the

will affect the constant

in (

) in general. Hence, without introducing anisotropic noise (as done, e.g., in

]
), the methods analyzed in this paper may not perform well in all problems in higher dimension. Yet, recent implementations of CBO with adapted noise, e.g.,
[
5
]
, proved to be able to solve very hard high-dimensional problems. (For an example of impact of CBO in real-life applications, CBO has recently enabled significant breakthroughs, e.g., in robotics, and we refer the reader to

]
for a detailed account.)

The finite time discretization of the schemes follows naturally from the Consensus Freezing scheme (

For an example of how the finite particle/finite time implementation of the algorithms can be concretely realized, we refer to Algorithm

1.4.
Technical innovations and breakthroughs

For the first time our results connect the theory of CBO to optimal transport theory
[
74
]
by making use of a suitable
\log
-Sobolev inequality and Talagrand inequality as in Lemma
2.2
and Theorem
2.5
. We also provide new versions and refinements of the quantitative Laplace principle
[
34
, Proposition 4.5]
as in Lemma
2.3
, Lemma

, and Lemma
4.3
.
We derive new global-in-time bounds on the moments of the densities, see Theorem

We finally obtain

2.
Global Convergence of the
\delta
-CBO Scheme

In this section we analyze the (

δ

-CBO
) scheme. We prove that the law
\rho_{t}
of
X_{t}
converges, in some sense, to a Gaussian distribution centered at the global minimizer
x^{*}
, with variance that depends on
\delta
and
\lambda
.
Here, we follow the blueprint of
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
, but with significant differences. The first is that in
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
one shows the exponential decay of the Wasserstein distance of the law to a Dirac delta placed at the global minimizer. (In the original CBO formulation (
CBO
) the noise is vanishing, hence the convergence to Dirac deltas.) Instead, here we show an analogous result for (

δ

-CBO
), by proving the exponential decay of the relative entropy of the law with respect to
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}=\mathcal{N}\left(x^{*},\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\lambda}I_{d}\right)
. Crucial in our analysis is the connection to optimal transport theory, in particular the use of a suitable
\log
-Sobolev inequality and Talagrand inequality
[
74
, Sections §22.1–§22.2]
.

We make the following assumptions on the function
f
.

Assumption 2.1
.

A1:

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous, and there exists a unique
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

f(x^{*})=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\eqqcolon\underline{f}.

Moreover,
there exists some constant
L_{f}>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L_{f}(1+|x|+|y|)|x-y|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

and there exist constants
c_{\ell},c_{u}>0
and
M>0
such that

f(x)-\underline{f}\leq c_{u}(|x|^{2}+1)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad c_{\ell}|x|^{2}\leq f(x)-\underline{f}\;\mbox{ for }|x|\geq M\,.

A2:

There exist
f_{\infty},R_{0},\eta>0
and
p\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle(f(x)-\underline{f})^{\nu}\geq\eta|x-x^{*}|\quad\text{for every }x\in B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})

\displaystyle f(x)\geq\underline{f}+f_{\infty}\quad\text{for every }x\notin B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})

For well-posedness of the dynamics and regularity of the law, we only need A1; A1 and A2 are both needed to prove convergence in Section 2 by using quantitative Laplace’s principle; For Section 3 again we only need A1.

Let us denote with
\rho^{\alpha}_{t}
the law of
X_{t}
at time
t
. We know that
\rho^{\alpha}_{t}
satisfies the following differential equation

(10)

\partial_{t}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)=\lambda\mathrm{div}\left(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))\right)+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\Delta\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x).

The well-posedness of the CBO PDE was first established in
[
21
, Theorem 3.1, Theorem 3.2]
, under the assumption that
0\leq\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, ensuring the existence of a measure-valued solution
\rho^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Higher regularity results have since been obtained in
[
75
,
38
,
31
]
. In particular, for our non-degenerate CBO PDE (
10
), one can apply the computations in
[
75
, Proposition 3]
to conclude that

(11)

\rho^{\alpha}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\quad\text{for any }k\geq 2,

provided the initial data satisfies
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The smoothness of solutions justifies the calculus used in the proofs below.

Given a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we denote with
\mathcal{N}(v,\Sigma)
the Gaussian distribution with mean
v
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Moreover, given two probability distributions
\rho
and
\nu
we denote with
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{I}
the relative entropy and the Fisher information, respectively. That is,

\mathcal{H}(\rho\ |\ \nu)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\nu}\right)\rho\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathcal{I}(\rho\ |\ \nu)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\nu}\right)\right|^{2}\rho\mathrm{d}x

Let us denote with
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}
the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(x^{*},\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\lambda}I_{d}\right)
. Namely,

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}e^{\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\quad\text{with }\sigma^{2}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\lambda}.

The results that follow are motivated and can be informally explained by the relatively simple observation that the equation (
10
) is
nearly
the Wasserstein gradient flow
[
1
]
of the energy

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\mathcal{H}(\rho\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}),

if we do justify the approximation
{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})\approx x^{*}
at all times
t\in[0,T_{*}]
(for a suitable
T_{*}
we will specify below).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let us assume that
\rho^{\alpha}
is a smooth solution of equation (
10
) with fixed
\alpha,\lambda,\delta>0
, for a given
T>0
. Then

(12)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})\leq-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})+\lambda|{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})-x^{*}|\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})^{1/2}.

Proof.

By definition of
\mathcal{H}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}
, we have

\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\log((2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}).

Taking the derivative with respect to
t
and applying (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\frac{\partial_{t}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{div}\left(\lambda\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\right)\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\lambda\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\right)^{\top}\nabla\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\lambda\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\right)^{\top}\left(\frac{\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}+\frac{(x-x^{*})}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigg(\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\frac{|\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)|^{2}}{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}+\lambda\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{\top}(x-x^{*})+\lambda\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{\top}(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))

\displaystyle+\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-x^{*})^{\top}(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

where in the last equality we used the definition of
\sigma
.
Adding and subtracting the terms
\lambda\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{\top}(x-x^{*})
and
\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)|x-x^{*}|^{2}
, and defining
q(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}
, we obtain

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\frac{|\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)|^{2}}{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}+2\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{\top}\nabla q(x)+\frac{2}{\delta^{2}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)|\nabla q(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}\left(\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)+\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\sigma^{2}}(x-x^{*})\right)\mathrm{d}x=:T^{1}_{t}+T^{2}_{t}.

We now estimate
T^{1}_{t}
and
T^{2}_{t}.

By definition of
q
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}
we have

(14)

\displaystyle\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{2}{\delta^{2}}q(x)=\log\left(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)e^{\frac{2q(x)}{\delta^{2}}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)-\log((2\pi\sigma^{2})^{d/2}),

which implies

\nabla\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{2}{\delta^{2}}q(x)\right)=\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right),

and therefore we get

(15)

\displaystyle T^{1}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left|\nabla\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{2}{\delta^{2}}q(x)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left|\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}).

To estimate
T^{2}_{t}
notice first that

\displaystyle\nabla\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x-x^{*})=\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\nabla\left(\log(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x))+\frac{|x-x^{*}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)=\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right).

Using this equality in the definition of
T^{2}_{t}
we get

(16)

\displaystyle T^{2}_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)(x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{1/2}(x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))|\ \left|\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{1/2}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{1/2}(x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)^{1/2}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\lambda|x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\left|\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}(x)}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\lambda|x^{*}-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})|\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty})^{1/2}.

Using (
15
) and (
16
) in (
13
) we get the thesis.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

It is important to notice that, while in
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
the rate of exponential convergence was given by

does not play any role and only the drift parameter

not only we prevent concentration, but we also avoid the occurrence of dominating and exploding diffusion.

Remark 2.7
.

The law
\nu_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
of the Langevin dynamics
dX_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}dB_{t}
fulfills the linear Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}\nu_{t}(x)=\mathrm{div}\left(\nabla f(x)\nu_{t}(x)\right)+\sigma\Delta\nu_{t}(x).

Its invariant measure is the Gibbs measure
\nu_{\infty}\propto e^{-\frac{f}{\sigma}}
, which depends on
f
. Remarkably the attractor for

of (

) is the Gaussian
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\infty}
, which depends on

, but not on the entire function

. The meaning of Theorem

, as also pointed out in
[
34
]
, is that (

δ

-CBON
) performs a canonical convexification of the original nonconvex optimization of
f
as the number of particles

tends to
\infty
.

While Theorem
2.5
shows that the dynamics (

δ

-CBO
) converges globally with exponential rate up to a given prescribed accuracy and until a suitable finite horizon time

, it says nothing about the behavior of the dynamics for
t>T_{*}
. This issue has also been noted in connection with the analogous result in
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
, which leads to (
3
) and has at times been interpreted as a form of truncated convergence (see, for example,
[
7
]
). (A statement such as Theorem
2.5
does not describe the asymptotic behavior as

. Yet it provides a characterization over a suitable time horizon

at any prescribed accuracy

and does offer a valid basis for asserting

Therefore, in this paper, we present new results that provide a precise description of the dynamics after

and, unlike
[
7
]
, offer an in-depth analysis of the density behavior. We start first with an analysis of the Consensus Freezing scheme (

) which allows us to build some useful tools and then we come back to an infinite-time horizon convergence result for (

δ

-CBO
) in Section

3.
The Consensus Freezing Scheme

Discretizing
Equation

δ

-CBON
directly using Euler-Maruyama has the drawback that for fixed
\lambda
and
\delta
, our choice of
\Delta t>0
is limited: if
\Delta t
comes close to
\frac{1}{\lambda}
or
\frac{1}{\delta}

-
δ

⁢
C
B

O

N
,

⁢
Δ
t

) ceases to be a faithful representation of the continuous dynamics, as the Lipschitz constant of the right-hand side of (

δ

-CBON
) is
\lambda
and
\delta
. To circumvent this issue, we propose a variant of (

δ

-CBON
), which we call the
Consensus Freezing scheme
:

(CF)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-s\cdot\lambda\left(X_{t}-{X^{\alpha}}(\varrho^{\alpha,s,\Delta t}_{t_{i}})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{s}\cdot\delta\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\text{for }t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}),

where
\varrho^{\alpha,s,\Delta t}_{t}\coloneqq\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
, and
t_{i}\coloneqq i\cdot\Delta t
for some step size
\Delta t>0
. Above, we introduced a
speed parameter

, which will become useful in later asymptotics. In the corresponding finite-particle system, denoting
\widehat{\varrho}^{\alpha,s,\Delta t,N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{{\mathfrak{X}}^{j}_{t}},

(CF
N
)

\mathrm{d}{\mathfrak{X}}^{j}_{t}=-s\cdot\lambda({\mathfrak{X}}^{j}_{t}-{X^{\alpha}}(\widehat{\varrho}^{\alpha,s,\Delta t,N}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{s}\cdot\delta\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{t},\quad\text{for }t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}),

each particle follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process for
t\in[i\cdot\Delta t,(i+1)\cdot\Delta t)
; we can thus hope to solve (
CFN
)
analytically
, without suffering any drawback if
\Delta t
is chosen large.

3.1.

Lemma 3.1

Lemma 3.3

The proof is almost identical to

-

(38)

\displaystyle\eta_{\nu}^{\alpha}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-x^{*}|\geq\varepsilon\})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}}{\nu(B_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x^{*}))}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-\underline{f})}}{C_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\,\;\to 0\;\mbox{ when }\;\alpha\to\infty.

6.
Conclusion and outlook

We analyzed a class of zero-order global optimization methods—CBO, CF, and CH—connected by quantitative asymptotics, for which we provided complete descriptions and guaranteed convergence rates to global minimizers for nonsmooth and nonconvex objectives
f
.

These are likely not the final words in global optimization, as we considered here exclusively isotropic noise, inherently affected by the curse of dimensionality. CBO has been formulated with various types of noise: isotropic, anisotropic, and jump diffusion
[
62
,
24
,
57
]
. However, without an adaptive covariance matrix for the noise, multiparticle algorithms are known to struggle with efficiently solving non-separable, ill-conditioned problems. This limitation is well-documented in the global optimization literature, e.g., in the comparison of Particle Swarm Optimization to Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)
[
47
]
. In the future we will address this issue by leveraging recent advancements in covariance-modulated optimal transport
[
17
,
20
]
.
We argue that with such adaptations the models and theoretical advances developed in this paper essentially solve with mathematically provable guarantees the core challenges of continuous nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization. What will remain is largely an engineering task: implementing these methods efficiently—leveraging their natural potential for large-scale parallelization— and deploying them systematically on real-world problems.

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}(x-x^{*})}{\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}}\geq-\frac{2\lambda r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)|x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})||x-x^{*}|}{\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq-2\frac{\lambda c^{1/2}(c^{1/2}r+B)}{r(1-c)^{2}}\phi_{r}(x)=-p_{1}\phi_{r}(x).

On the other hand, by definition of
T_{2}
we have

(136)

\displaystyle T_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta^{2}r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}{\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{4}}\left(d\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}+2|x-x^{*}|^{2}{\left(r^{2}-2|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\delta(1+c^{2})d}{r^{2}(1-c)^{4}}\phi_{r}(x)=-p_{2}\phi_{r}(x).

We now prove that if
x\in\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}^{c}
then
T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq 0.
This is true if and only if

(137)

\left(-\lambda(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}(x-x^{*})+\frac{d\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}\leq\delta^{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}\left(2|x-x^{*}|^{2}-r^{2}\right).

Since
x\in A_{2}^{c}
,
x\in A_{1}
and the fact that
(1-c)^{2}d\leq(2c-1)c
we have

\displaystyle\left(-\lambda(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}(x-x^{*})+\frac{d\delta^{2}}{2}\right)\left(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}(2c-1)r^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}(2c-1)cr^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}\left(2|x-x^{*}|^{2}-r^{2}\right),

which yields (
137
), and thus
T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq 0
for
x\in\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}^{c}
.
Let us now consider the case
x\in\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}
. By definition of
A_{2}
and Cauchy Schwartz inequality we have

(138)

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2\lambda r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}{(r^{2}-|x-x^{*}|^{2})^{2}}|x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})||x-x^{*}|\geq\frac{4\lambda^{2}|x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t})|}{(2c-1)\delta^{2}}(x-{X^{\alpha}}(\rho^{\alpha}_{t}))^{\top}(x-x^{*})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{4\lambda^{2}r(cr^{2}+B)}{(2c-1)\delta^{2}}\phi_{r}(x)=-p_{3}\phi_{r}(x).

By definition of
T_{2}
and
\Delta\phi_{r}(x)
we have that
T_{2}(x)\geq 0
if

(139)

2|x-x^{*}|^{2}(2|x-x^{*}|^{2}-r^{2})\geq d(r^{2}-|x-x|^{2})^{2}.

Since
x\in\Omega_{r}\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
we have

2|x-x^{*}|^{2}(2|x-x^{*}|^{2}-r^{2})\geq 2r^{4}c(2c-1)\geq dr^{4}(1-c)^{2}\geq d(r^{2}-|x-x|^{2})^{2}.

Thus (
139
) holds and
T_{2}(x)\geq 0
.
Putting everything together, we then get

(140)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\phi_{r}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}^{c}}T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)+\int_{\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}^{c}}T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}}T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-(p_{1}+p_{2})\int_{\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}^{c}}\phi_{r}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)-p_{3}\int_{\Omega_{r}\cap A_{1}\cap A_{2}}\phi_{r}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-p\int\phi_{r}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{\alpha}_{t}(x),

with
p=\max\{p_{1}+p_{2},p_{3}\}
. The thesis then follows from Gronwall’s inequality.
∎

Lemma 7.1
.

, let
\varrho_{t}^{\alpha,s,\Delta t}
be the solution of (
CF
) with
\rho_{0}=\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{0},\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\lambda}I_{d}\right).

δ

-CBO
) as
\Delta t
goes to 0.

Let

as

For every

\displaystyle,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle t\longmapsto f^{n}_{t}\coloneqq\varrho_{t}^{\alpha,s,\tau_{n}},

with
\varrho_{t}^{\alpha,s,\tau_{n}}
solution of (

) with
\Delta t=\tau_{n}
and

, for some
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

From (

)-(

) we have that for every

and every

,
f^{n}_{t}=\varrho_{t}^{\alpha,s,\tau_{n}}=\mathcal{N}\left(u^{n}(t),\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\lambda}I\right)

\displaystyle u^{n}(0)=\mu_{0}

\displaystyle u^{n}(t)=\mu_{t}^{\alpha,s,\tau_{n}}=X^{\alpha}(f^{n}_{t_{i}})+e^{-s\lambda(t-t_{i})}(u^{n}(t_{i})-X^{\alpha}(f^{n}_{t_{i}}))\quad\text{for }t\in[(i-1)\tau_{n},i\tau_{n}).

. First we note that, for
