What drives students’ green intentions? A psychological approach
Authors: Avus C Y Hou, Nam Tien Duong, Van Kien Pham, Thuy Dung Pham Thi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the psychological and motivational factors influencing green behavioral intention among university students, focusing on eco-anxiety, social anxiety, green motivation (intrinsic and extrinsic), and perceived environmental responsibility. Grounded in self-determination theory and value-based frameworks, the study proposes and tests a structural model using data from 350 Vietnamese students, analyzed via PLS-SEM. Results show that both eco-anxiety and social anxiety positively affect intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation, which in turn significantly predict green behavioral intention. Perceived environmental responsibility not only directly influences green intention but also moderates the effects of motivation. All nine hypotheses were supported, and the model accounted for a substantial variance in motivation and intention. The findings underscore the importance of emotional and ethical factors in shaping pro-environmental behavior and offer practical implications for sustainability education and behavior change strategies in higher education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03791-w.Keywords: Green, Behavior, Motivation, Responsibility, Anxiety

Introduction

The increasing severity of global environmental issues—such as climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss—has drawn urgent attention to the promotion of sustainable behaviors, especially among younger generations [55]. Students, as future leaders and influencers, play a critical role in environmental preservation through their engagement in green behaviors, including energy conservation, waste reduction, and sustainable consumption [35]. Despite widespread campaigns and rising awareness of eco-friendly practices, actual behavioral adoption among students remains inconsistent [36, 61]. This suggests that awareness alone may not be sufficient to drive behavior and that underlying psychological factors must be more deeply examined.

Among these factors, anxiety-related constructs have recently gained attention for their impact on pro-environmental behaviors. Two particularly relevant forms are eco-anxiety and social anxiety. Eco-anxiety, defined as chronic fear of environmental doom, is increasingly observed among younger populations [12, 30]. While it is often assumed to motivate action, some studies suggest that high levels of eco-anxiety can lead to disengagement or denial as a coping mechanism [73]. Similarly, social anxiety—the fear of being judged or ridiculed—may act as a barrier to green behavior, especially in social contexts where sustainable actions deviate from group norms [42]. Despite their relevance, few empirical studies have integrated both anxiety constructs into a unified framework to examine how they influence green behavioral intentions.

Previous research has largely focused on the role of cognitive variables such as attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control based on the theory of planned behavior [2]. While these models have proven robust, they tend to underemphasize emotional and motivational dynamics. For example, Bamberg and Möser [5] conducted a meta-analysis that identified intention as a strong predictor of behavior but acknowledged that emotional factors were often missing from predictive models. Moreover, studies focusing on green motivation typically assess intrinsic and extrinsic motivations without considering how they are shaped by emotional states such as anxiety.

Motivation itself—whether intrinsic (driven by internal values and satisfaction) or extrinsic (driven by social pressure or rewards)—is a well-established predictor of behavioral intention [58]. However, the interaction between anxiety and motivation has been underexplored in the environmental context. Existing studies from the education and technology fields [31, 74] have shown that anxiety can diminish motivation, yet little is known about how eco-anxiety and social anxiety specifically influence students’ green motivation. Some researchers have treated anxiety as a generalized negative affect, without distinguishing between different sources or types of anxiety [24], leading to inconsistent or oversimplified findings.

Furthermore, perceived environmental responsibility—defined as the personal sense of duty to protect the environment [10]—has been identified as an important yet underutilized construct in sustainability research. While self-efficacy has been commonly examined [41], it focuses on confidence in ability rather than moral obligation. Prior studies have frequently positioned environmental responsibility as a predictor of pro-environmental attitudes [59, 70], rather than as a motivational antecedent. As a result, its direct and moderating effects on behavioral intention remain underexplored. There is a pressing need to examine how a sense of environmental responsibility interacts with anxiety and motivation to shape behavioral outcomes.

Guided by self-determination theory (SDT) [58] and value-based frameworks such as the value–belief–norm (VBN) theory [68], this study identifies intrinsic green motivation and extrinsic green motivation as key mediating variables that transmit the effects of emotional antecedents (eco-anxiety and social anxiety) to green behavioral intention. SDT provides a foundation for explaining how anxiety regulates the quality of motivation—autonomous (intrinsic) versus controlled (extrinsic)—while VBN theory highlights how perceived environmental responsibility serves as a moral driver that directly predicts and strengthens the motivation–intention linkage.

By explicitly identifying these mediating and moderating mechanisms in the Introduction, the study clarifies how emotional, motivational, and moral dimensions jointly influence sustainable behavior. Specifically, it proposes an integrative framework including eco-anxiety, social anxiety, intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation, perceived environmental responsibility, and green behavioral intention among students. By examining these constructs simultaneously, the study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of the emotional and motivational processes underlying students’ sustainable behavior. This approach not only enriches the theoretical landscape by integrating emotional and moral dimensions into behavior models but also has practical implications for environmental education and communication strategies that aim to foster long-term, meaningful green behavior.

Theoretical background

Anxiety and green behavior

Anxiety, defined as a state of apprehension, tension, or unease resulting from the anticipation of potential threats or negative evaluation [65], has long been recognized as a significant factor influencing human behavior. In the context of environmental sustainability, two distinct forms of anxiety have emerged as particularly relevant: eco-anxiety and social anxiety [8, 42]. These forms of anxiety are increasingly acknowledged for their potential to shape individual attitudes, motivations, and intentions toward green behavior.

Eco-anxiety, often described as a chronic fear of environmental collapse, is characterized by emotional distress linked to climate change, pollution, and other ecological crises [12]. Research suggests that eco-anxiety can have dual effects: while it may motivate certain individuals to adopt pro-environmental behaviors as a coping strategy [73], it can also overwhelm others, leading to feelings of helplessness, disengagement, and psychological withdrawal [34]. For example, Hickman, Marks [30] found that youth experiencing high levels of climate-related anxiety often reported feelings of frustration, guilt, and a lack of control over the future—factors that can significantly impact their behavioral responses. These conflicting outcomes indicate that eco-anxiety is a complex and context-dependent emotion, the influence of which may be moderated by variables such as perceived efficacy or responsibility.

In contrast, social anxiety refers to the fear of being negatively evaluated by others in social settings [44]. In sustainability contexts, social anxiety may motivate individuals to engage in pro-environmental behaviors that align with social expectations, particularly when green behavior is seen as socially desirable. For instance, students who fear peer judgment may feel compelled to carry reusable containers or avoid single-use plastics to maintain a favorable social image. Despite this relevance, social anxiety has received limited attention in pro-environmental behavior research. While prior research has largely focused on social norms as external influences [2, 11, 63], less attention has been given to the internal emotional pressures (e.g., social anxiety) that may drive individuals to conform to environmentally responsible behaviors.

Only a handful of studies have begun to explore the intersection of anxiety and environmental behavior. For example, Gifford [24] categorized emotional barriers, including anxiety and helplessness, as significant “dragons of inaction” that hinder pro-environmental engagement. However, anxiety in these frameworks is often treated as a generalized emotional state, with little differentiation between sources such as ecological concerns versus social pressures. This oversimplification limits the explanatory power of such models and overlooks the unique mechanisms through which specific types of anxiety may influence environmental behavior.

Moreover, most empirical studies linking anxiety to behavior have focused on technological or academic domains. In these contexts, anxiety has been found to lower confidence, reduce motivation, and impair decision-making [7, 57]. These findings suggest that similar dynamics may operate in sustainability domains, yet direct evidence remains sparse. Few studies have simultaneously examined eco-anxiety and social anxiety in a unified framework to assess their combined and distinct effects on green behavioral intention, particularly in student populations.

Given this gap, the current study incorporates both eco-anxiety and social anxiety as antecedents in a model predicting green motivation and behavioral intention. By distinguishing between these two forms of anxiety and analyzing their specific roles, the study aims to clarify how emotional discomfort influences sustainability-related decision-making among students. This approach offers a more nuanced understanding of the psychological barriers and enablers of green behavior, contributing to both theory development and practical intervention strategies.

Motivation in sustainability contexts

Motivation plays a central role in shaping individuals’ engagement in sustainable behavior. It is widely regarded as a key psychological determinant of action, influencing not only the formation of behavioral intentions but also the consistency and strength of behavior over time [58]. In the context of sustainability, motivation determines whether individuals choose to adopt and maintain environmentally responsible behaviors, such as energy conservation, recycling, or sustainable consumption. While environmental knowledge and awareness are necessary, they are often insufficient without adequate motivational support to transform attitudes into action [41].

In the sustainability domain, two distinct yet interconnected types of motivation are particularly relevant: intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation refers to engaging in behavior out of internal values, moral obligations, or personal satisfaction [3]. Individuals who are intrinsically motivated to act sustainably often do so because they value nature, experience personal fulfillment from eco-friendly practices, or feel a moral duty to protect the environment [67]. These motivations are closely tied to environmental identity and personal norms, making them strong predictors of long-term behavioral commitment.

Extrinsic motivation, in contrast, involves engaging in green behaviors due to external pressures or incentives, such as social recognition, financial rewards, regulatory compliance, or peer approval [48]. While often viewed as less stable than intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivators can be effective in the initial adoption of sustainable practices and may serve as stepping stones toward more deeply internalized motivations over time [58]. In many real-world settings, external motivators—such as university sustainability policies, green labeling, or public eco-campaigns—play an important role in shaping students’ environmental behaviors.

Prior research has supported the impact of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations on pro-environmental behavior. For instance, De Groot and Steg [15] found that intrinsic values such as altruism and biospheric concern significantly predicted willingness to sacrifice personal convenience for the sake of the environment. Meanwhile, studies [22, 33] found that financial incentives and social norms—classic forms of extrinsic motivation—could also drive positive behavior change. These findings suggest that both motivational types are important, though they may operate through different psychological mechanisms and temporal dynamics.

However, the relationship between motivation and behavior is not always straightforward. External motivators may backfire if individuals perceive them as controlling or inauthentic [71], while strong intrinsic motivation may not result in action if psychological or situational barriers exist. Moreover, individual differences—such as environmental knowledge, perceived behavioral control, or emotional factors like anxiety—may moderate how motivation translates into intention or behavior [37]. For example, intrinsically motivated students who experience eco-anxiety or social anxiety may still hesitate to act due to emotional conflict or fear of judgment.

In this regard, the current study integrates intrinsic and extrinsic green motivations into its framework to assess their roles in predicting green behavioral intention among students. Building on self-determination theory [58] and prior environmental psychology literature, the study treats motivation not as a singular driver, but as a dynamic construct shaped by emotional, cognitive, and contextual factors. Additionally, by examining the moderating role of perceived environmental responsibility, the study aims to clarify the conditions under which motivation leads to sustainable behavioral intention. This comprehensive approach is expected to yield a deeper understanding of the motivational processes that underpin student engagement in green behaviors.

Perceived environmental responsibility and green behavioral intention

In the field of environmental psychology, green behavioral intention refers to an individual’s conscious plan or willingness to perform environmentally responsible actions in the future [36]. This construct is considered a crucial predictor of actual pro-environmental behavior and is often influenced by cognitive evaluations, emotional experiences, and motivational forces. However, translating intention into action is not always straightforward, and researchers have increasingly sought to identify psychological factors that strengthen or weaken this relationship. One such factor is perceived environmental responsibility [59]—an individual’s internalized belief that they are morally obligated to protect the environment.

Perceived environmental responsibility differs from commonly studied constructs like environmental attitude or environmental concern in that it emphasizes a sense of moral duty and accountability rather than general awareness or preference [68, 75]. It reflects the extent to which individuals feel personally responsible for the consequences of their actions on the natural world. This sense of responsibility can stem from personal norms, ethical convictions, or a deeper environmental identity, all of which influence how strongly a person commits to sustainable behavior.

While prior research has extensively examined the effects of perceived behavioral control and self-efficacy on behavioral intention [25], the role of perceived responsibility has often been underexplored or treated as a background variable. When it is considered, it is typically positioned as a predictor of environmental concern or attitude, rather than as a direct influence on intention or a moderator of motivational pathways. This represents a significant gap, particularly in light of studies suggesting that moral responsibility can be a powerful driver of consistent and long-term engagement in sustainability practices [49, 71].

Recent studies have begun to recognize the significance of moral and ethical drivers in environmental behavior. For example, Onwezen, Antonides [50] demonstrated that personal norms—closely related to perceived responsibility—were among the strongest predictors of intention to engage in sustainable food consumption. Similarly, Elahi, Khalid [19] found that individuals with a stronger sense of environmental obligation were more likely to express intentions to reduce energy usage, regardless of extrinsic incentives or convenience factors.

Despite this growing recognition, few studies have investigated the dual role of perceived environmental responsibility as both a direct antecedent of behavioral intention and a moderating variable that strengthens the influence of motivation on intention. It is plausible that students who feel a strong personal obligation to protect the environment are more likely to translate their intrinsic green motivation (e.g., moral values) and extrinsic green motivation (e.g., social pressure) into action. Conversely, those with low perceived responsibility may feel less compelled to act on their motivations, even if their environmental attitudes are positive.

To address this gap, the present study conceptualizes perceived environmental responsibility as both a direct predictor of green behavioral intention and a moderator of the relationship between motivation (intrinsic and extrinsic) and intention. This approach aligns with calls in the literature to integrate moral obligation more explicitly into models of pro-environmental behavior [5, 68] and to better understand the conditions under which motivations lead to sustained engagement in green practices. By examining the interactive effects of motivation and perceived responsibility, this study aims to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of student sustainability behavior and inform strategies for effective environmental education and policy design.

Self-determination theory and value-based frameworks (VBN/NAM)

This study integrates self-determination theory (SDT) [58] and value-based frameworks, including the value–belief–norm (VBN) theory [68], and the norm activation model (NAM) [60], to explain the psychological mechanisms that drive students’ green behavioral intention. Together, these frameworks provide a multidimensional understanding of how emotional experiences, motivational regulation, and moral responsibility interact to shape sustainability-oriented behavior.

SDT posits that human motivation exists along a continuum from extrinsic to intrinsic regulation. Intrinsic motivation reflects autonomous engagement in activities out of genuine interest or personal values, whereas extrinsic motivation arises from external pressures, rewards, or social expectations [53]. From an SDT perspective, eco-anxiety operates as a controlled regulatory mechanism that heightens external awareness of environmental norms and social accountability. When individuals experience anxiety about ecological harm, they may seek to alleviate this distress by aligning with socially valued environmental actions (e.g., recycling, public advocacy), which function as extrinsic forms of motivation aimed at restoring a sense of moral adequacy and social approval [16]. Conversely, social anxiety—though rooted in fear of negative evaluation [23]—can facilitate deeper internalization of pro-environmental values through self-reflective regulation. As individuals anticipate potential judgment for inaction, they may reorient their behavior toward authentic self-consistency and moral coherence, transforming social pressure into personally endorsed intrinsic motivation [63]. Thus, both emotions act as motivational catalysts that transform psychological discomfort into either externally regulated or internally integrated engagement in sustainable actions.

Complementing SDT, value-based frameworks such as VBN theory and NAM highlight the moral and normative foundations of pro-environmental behavior. These models emphasize that personal norms—feelings of moral obligation to act—serve as key drivers of environmentally responsible behavior. In this study, perceived environmental responsibility is conceptualized as an activated personal norm, representing the belief that one has an ethical duty to protect the environment. According to VBN and NAM, moral responsibility arises when individuals become aware of environmental consequences and assign personal accountability for them [60, 68]. This moral activation transforms general concern into a self-regulatory standard that guides behavior. Within the SDT framework, perceived responsibility functions as an internalized regulator that aligns both intrinsic (value-driven) and extrinsic (socially driven) motivations with self-endorsed action. Thus, it operates both directly—by generating moral intention—and indirectly—by strengthening the consistency between motivation and behavioral commitment.

Integrating these two perspectives allows for a more comprehensive theoretical explanation. SDT captures the motivational mechanism (emotion → motivation → intention), explaining why individuals engage in sustainable behaviors, while VBN/NAM introduces the moral dimension, explaining how personal values and responsibility strengthen the motivation–intention link. Together, these frameworks illustrate that sustainable behavior among students is not merely a product of rational cognition but the result of emotional arousal, motivational regulation, and moral activation working in tandem.

Hypotheses development and conceptual model

Social anxiety and green motivation

Social anxiety is defined as the fear of being judged, criticized, or rejected in social contexts, often resulting in avoidance behaviors or heightened sensitivity to how one is perceived by others [44]. In the context of green behavior, this form of anxiety may influence how students respond to social expectations regarding environmental responsibility. While social anxiety is often associated with withdrawal or nonparticipation in public activities [14], its role in pro-environmental motivation may be more complex and context-dependent.

On one level, social anxiety can increase extrinsic green motivation. Students who fear social disapproval may engage in environmentally responsible behaviors not because they are personally committed, but because such actions are socially endorsed or expected. This aligns with research indicating that socially anxious individuals often modify their behavior to avoid negative evaluation [39]. In eco-conscious environments—such as university campuses that promote sustainability—students may comply with visible green behaviors (e.g., recycling, bringing reusable containers, supporting green campaigns) to avoid standing out negatively. These behaviors are often driven by external social cues rather than internal values, which is consistent with the nature of extrinsic motivation [58].

On another level, social anxiety may also influence intrinsic green motivation, though through a different mechanism. For some students, the discomfort associated with social judgment may lead to deeper self-reflection and internalization of social norms [1]. In such cases, pro-environmental actions may be adopted not only to meet social expectations but also to construct or affirm a valued identity—one that is responsible, ethical, and environmentally conscious. Over time, these behaviors may become self-determined, reflecting a transition from external to internal regulation [58]. This aligns with self-determination theory, which recognizes that social influences can be internalized and integrated into one’s sense of self, thereby fostering intrinsic motivation.

Despite these theoretical pathways, the role of social anxiety in shaping environmental motivation has received limited empirical attention. Most research in the pro-environmental behavior domain has focused on subjective norms as external influences, without adequately accounting for the internal psychological tension individuals may feel when attempting to comply with or resist these norms [2, 45]. By overlooking internal social fears, previous models may miss a key emotional driver of motivation, particularly in youth and student populations where peer influence is strong.

To address this gap, the present study investigates how social anxiety may serve as an emotional antecedent to both intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation. Based on the dual pathways of social influence and internalization, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1: Social anxiety positively influences intrinsic green motivation.

H1: Social anxiety positively influences intrinsic green motivation.

H2: Social anxiety positively influences extrinsic green motivation.

H2: Social anxiety positively influences extrinsic green motivation.

Eco-anxiety and green motivation

In the domain of environmental psychology, eco-anxiety has emerged as a salient emotional response to the growing awareness of climate change, environmental degradation, and the uncertain future of the planet. It is characterized by feelings of fear, worry, helplessness, and frustration triggered by current and anticipated ecological crises [12, 30]. Among students, who are increasingly exposed to environmental issues through education and media, eco-anxiety may manifest as a chronic concern about their future well-being and the sustainability of the world they will inherit.

Research suggests that eco-anxiety can have dual effects on behavioral motivation. On the one hand, moderate levels of eco-anxiety may serve as a constructive emotional force that drives individuals toward action [73]. Students experiencing this form of anxiety may seek to regain a sense of control or reduce their psychological discomfort by engaging in behaviors that align with their environmental values. In this context, eco-anxiety may stimulate intrinsic green motivation, as students become internally driven to participate in sustainable actions that reflect their personal concerns and ethical convictions.

On the other hand, eco-anxiety may also heighten awareness of social and institutional expectations regarding environmental responsibility, thus reinforcing extrinsic green motivation. Students may feel compelled to engage in visible pro-environmental behaviors—such as joining green clubs, following school recycling rules, or adhering to sustainability campaigns—not solely out of internal values, but to fulfill perceived obligations or avoid criticism in a socially eco-conscious environment [13]. This aligns with findings from climate communication research indicating that emotionally charged messages, particularly those that elicit anxiety or urgency, can enhance message effectiveness by making external norms and consequences more salient [17].

Despite its relevance, eco-anxiety has not been extensively studied as a motivational antecedent in pro-environmental behavior models. Much of the literature has focused on rational predictors such as attitudes and perceived behavioral control [46], while emotional antecedents like eco-anxiety have been underrepresented or oversimplified. As a result, the potential of eco-anxiety to influence both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation remains insufficiently understood. Addressing this gap is particularly important in the context of students, whose behavioral patterns are still forming and may be highly sensitive to emotional and social dynamics.

In this study, eco-anxiety is conceptualized as a distal emotional antecedent that affects green behavioral intention indirectly through proximal motivational mechanisms—namely, intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation. This approach follows SDT [58], which posits that emotional experiences influence behavior primarily by shaping motivational regulation rather than directly determining intention. Likewise, value-based frameworks such as the norm activation model (NAM) and value–belief–norm (VBN) theory [68] view emotions and moral obligations as antecedents that operate through motivation and personal norms to influence behavior. Direct paths from eco-anxiety to behavioral intention were therefore not included to maintain theoretical parsimony and to focus on the mechanistic pathway through which emotion is internalized into motivation and subsequently translated into intention. This mediated approach also avoids conflating distal affective triggers with proximal motivational drivers, ensuring consistency with both SDT and VBN theoretical traditions.

In response, the current study investigates the extent to which eco-anxiety influences students’ intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation. It is proposed that:

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and behavioral intention

In the domain of green behavior among students, intrinsic motivation is reflected in individuals’ internal desire to act sustainably out of a sense of moral duty, environmental concern, or alignment with personal values [51]. Students intrinsically motivated to engage in eco-friendly behavior may do so because they feel connected to nature, derive satisfaction from acting ethically, or consider environmental responsibility as part of their identity [62]. For instance, the act of reducing plastic use or conserving energy may be personally fulfilling, even in the absence of external rewards. Past research has suggested that such internally driven motivation strongly predicts long-term and consistent engagement in sustainable behaviors [67].

In the context of student environmental action, extrinsic motivation may be shaped by factors such as social approval, institutional incentives, or compliance with campus sustainability initiatives. Students may adopt green behaviors to gain recognition from peers, avoid social criticism, or meet the expectations of environmental norms set by schools or communities [71, 77]. The relationship between extrinsic motivation and green behavioral intention can vary, depending on the source and authenticity of the external pressure. While some studies highlight the effectiveness of extrinsic motivators in triggering behavior change, others point out the risk of such motivations leading to short-term or superficial compliance, especially when the external incentives are removed [58].

Understanding the combined influence of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in shaping students’ green behavioral intentions is essential for designing effective environmental education and intervention strategies. This dual-pathway approach acknowledges the complex nature of human motivation, wherein sustainable behavior may be simultaneously driven by internal values and external expectations. As proposed by self-determination theory [58], these motivations can coexist and jointly influence intention, although their outcomes may differ in terms of depth and persistence. Given this theoretical and practical significance, it is hypothesized that:

H6: Extrinsic green motivation positively influences green behavioral intention.

H6: Extrinsic green motivation positively influences green behavioral intention.

The role of perceived environmental responsibility

Perceived environmental responsibility refers to the individual’s internalized belief that they have a moral duty or obligation to protect the environment through their actions [56, 68]. Unlike constructs such as environmental knowledge or attitude, which are largely cognitive or affective, perceived responsibility emphasizes a personal sense of accountability. It reflects not only awareness of environmental problems but also a felt commitment to take meaningful action—making it a key moral dimension in the study of sustainability behavior [38].

This sense of responsibility is particularly important in the student context, where individuals are forming value systems and behavioral patterns that may persist into adulthood [78]. Students who perceive high environmental responsibility are more likely to view green behaviors not merely as preferences, but as ethical imperatives. They are inclined to act sustainably even in the absence of external enforcement or incentives, guided by internal moral standards. Consequently, perceived environmental responsibility is expected to have a direct positive effect on students’ green behavioral intention.

In addition to its direct influence, perceived environmental responsibility may also moderate the relationship between motivation and intention. According to moral norm theory and value-belief-norm (VBN) theory, personal norms such as responsibility can amplify or weaken the effect of other psychological drivers on behavior [64, 68]. In this study, it is proposed that students with a strong sense of environmental responsibility will be more likely to translate both intrinsic motivation (e.g., personal values) and extrinsic motivation (e.g., social expectations) into concrete behavioral intention. In contrast, students with low perceived responsibility may be less inclined to act, even when motivated.

Consistent with VBN theory, the present study conceptualizes perceived environmental responsibility not as a fixed personality trait but as an activated personal norm—a context-dependent moral belief that can vary across situations [64]. This framing allows it to function as a situational factor that interacts with motivational processes rather than as a static disposition. Accordingly, perceived environmental responsibility is modeled as a second-stage moderator that strengthens the relationship between motivation and intention, reflecting the degree to which moral obligation reinforces motivated behavior.

Despite its theoretical importance, the role of perceived environmental responsibility as a moderator has been largely overlooked in pro-environmental behavior research. Most studies treat it as a background variable or a consequence of environmental concern [18, 69], rather than as an interactive force that shapes the effects of motivation. By investigating this moderating role, the present study addresses a significant gap in the literature and seeks to uncover how moral obligation reinforces or constrains motivational processes.

Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H7: Perceived environmental responsibility positively influences green behavioral intention.

H7: Perceived environmental responsibility positively influences green behavioral intention.

H8: Perceived environmental responsibility positively moderates the relationship between intrinsic green motivation and green behavioral intention, such that the relationship is stronger when perceived responsibility is high.

H8: Perceived environmental responsibility positively moderates the relationship between intrinsic green motivation and green behavioral intention, such that the relationship is stronger when perceived responsibility is high.

H9: Perceived environmental responsibility positively moderates the relationship between extrinsic green motivation and green behavioral intention, such that the relationship is stronger when perceived responsibility is high.

H9: Perceived environmental responsibility positively moderates the relationship between extrinsic green motivation and green behavioral intention, such that the relationship is stronger when perceived responsibility is high.

Method

Measures

Eco-anxiety is operationally defined as the emotional distress and psychological unease an individual experiences due to awareness of environmental degradation, climate change, and uncertainty about the planet’s future. It encompasses feelings of worry, helplessness, and fear in response to ecological threats. In this study, eco-anxiety is measured using a scale adapted from Clayton and Karazsia [13], consisting of six items.

Social anxiety is defined as the fear of negative evaluation, criticism, or embarrassment in social situations. In the context of green behavior, it reflects students’ discomfort or hesitation to engage in eco-friendly actions that may attract attention or deviate from perceived group norms. This study adopts the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS) by Mattick and Clarke [47], selecting five contextually relevant items modified to align with environmentally responsible behavior.

Intrinsic green motivation is operationally defined as the internal values, enjoyment, or personal satisfaction that drive individuals to engage in sustainable behaviors. It reflects a sense of ethical responsibility and environmental self-identity. This study employs and adapts the scale by Pelletier, Tuson [52], selecting four items that capture self-endorsed engagement in green behaviors.

Extrinsic green motivation refers to the performance of environmentally friendly behaviors in response to external incentives, such as social approval, institutional pressure, or public image. It is operationally defined as the extent to which external rewards or recognition influence an individual’s engagement in green actions. This study adopts items from Kuvaas, Buch [43], modifying them to reflect sustainability-related external drivers.

Perceived environmental responsibility is defined as the individual’s internalized belief in their duty to protect the environment. It reflects a moral norm that drives people to take ownership of their environmental impact. The scale used in this study is adapted from Hansla, Gamble [28] and comprises five items tailored to a student context.

Green behavioral intention is operationally defined as a student’s conscious willingness or plan to engage in pro-environmental behaviors in the near future. It represents the likelihood that an individual will perform environmentally responsible actions, such as recycling, conserving energy, or purchasing sustainable products. The scale for behavioral intention is adapted from Becerra, Carrete [6], consisting of four items revised to reflect general student intentions toward green practices.

All items are measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). All constructs and measurement items were subjected to a pretest to ensure clarity, contextual relevance, and reliability. The finalized questionnaire was used to collect data for structural equation modeling (Appendices).

Participants and procedures

This study employed a questionnaire-based survey method, which is commonly used in behavioral and social sciences to collect individuals’ self-reported perceptions, attitudes, emotions, and behavioral intentions. The target population for this study comprised university students aged 18 years and above enrolled in higher education institutions in Vietnam. This demographic was chosen due to students’ increasing involvement in environmental activities, their exposure to sustainability education, and their influential role in shaping future green behavior patterns.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Ho Chi Minh City University of Economics and Finance (Approval No: 720/QD-UEF). All participants provided informed consent before their participation. They were informed about the purpose of the study, their right to withdraw at any time, and the confidentiality of their responses. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines.

The data collection was conducted in February and March 2025 through both online distribution and in-person administration. The online version of the questionnaire was distributed via university-affiliated social media groups (e.g., Facebook and Zalo), student email lists, and environmental clubs. For the offline method, trained research assistants visited classrooms and university common areas with printed surveys, ensuring that participation remained voluntary and anonymous. To encourage honest and thoughtful responses, all participants were assured of confidentiality and informed that the data would be used strictly for academic research purposes. As a token of appreciation, participants who completed the survey were entered into a raffle to win one of 30 environmentally themed gift sets, such as reusable bottles and eco-bags.

A total of 374 questionnaires were collected. Following rigorous data cleaning procedures, including checks for incomplete answers, patterned responses, and outliers, 350 valid responses were retained for analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 93.6%. This robust sample size contributed to the statistical power and stability of the findings.

The data were coded and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 for descriptive statistics, while SmartPLS 4.0 was employed for measurement model assessment and structural model evaluation. Given the study’s objective to explore how eco-anxiety and social anxiety influence intrinsic and extrinsic green motivations, and how these, in turn, affect green behavioral intention—with perceived environmental responsibility acting as a direct predictor and moderator—PLS-SEM was adopted. This method is appropriate for complex models involving latent constructs, mediation, and moderation effects [26].

The combination of a mixed-mode data collection strategy, incentivized participation, and the use of validated analytical tools ensured the rigor, reliability, and depth of the study’s findings. This comprehensive approach provides meaningful insights into the psychological and motivational factors that influence sustainable behavioral intentions among students.

Results

Descriptive statistics

This study employed descriptive statistics to examine the demographic profile of the student sample, focusing on key characteristics such as gender, age, and academic major. Among the 350 respondents, females comprised 170 individuals (48.6%), while males accounted for 180 individuals (51.4%), reflecting a well-balanced gender distribution.

In terms of age, the majority of participants were between 18 and 21 years old, reflecting the typical age range of university undergraduates in Vietnam. Specifically, 135 respondents (38.6%) were aged 18–19, and 127 respondents (36.3%) were aged 20–21. Participants aged 22–23 accounted for 64 individuals (18.3%), while only 24 respondents (6.8%) were in the 24–25 age group. This age distribution aligns with the study’s focus on student populations and supports the contextual relevance of their green behavioral patterns.

Regarding academic disciplines, the sample represented a diverse mix of university majors. Students majoring in Environmental Science constituted the largest group, with 91 respondents (26.0%), followed by those in Engineering (70 respondents, 20.0%) and Business (66 respondents, 18.9%). Other fields of study included Arts and Humanities (64 respondents, 18.3%) and Other Disciplines (59 respondents, 16.9%). Coefficient of determination (R²)

The coefficient of determination (R²) was used to evaluate the explanatory power of the structural model by measuring the variance explained in each endogenous construct. As shown in Fig. 1, the R² value for green behavioral intention is 0.453, indicating that 45.3% of the variance in students’ green behavioral intentions is explained by intrinsic green motivation, extrinsic green motivation, perceived environmental responsibility, and moderation effects. According to Hair Jr, Hult [26], this reflects a moderate level of predictive power.

The R² value for intrinsic green motivation is 0.424, meaning that 42.4% of its variance is accounted for by eco-anxiety and social anxiety, indicating a moderate level of explanatory strength. Similarly, the R² for extrinsic green motivation is 0.425, suggesting that 42.5% of its variance is explained by the same two predictors, also reflecting a moderate level of prediction.

Following the guidelines of Hair Jr, Hult [26], R² values of 0.50–0.75 are considered substantial, 0.25–0.50 moderate, and values below 0.25 weak. Therefore, the current model demonstrates moderate explanatory capability for students’ green motivation and a moderate predictive ability for green behavioral intention. These findings support the robustness of the model in capturing the psychological and motivational mechanisms underlying pro-environmental behavior among students.

Discussions

This study investigated the psychological and motivational drivers of students’ green behavioral intention by integrating emotional (eco-anxiety and social anxiety), motivational (intrinsic and extrinsic motivation), and ethical (perceived environmental responsibility) constructs. All nine hypotheses were supported, providing robust empirical validation for the proposed model and offering theoretical insights into how affective and cognitive factors jointly shape sustainability behavior.

First, the finding that eco-anxiety positively influences intrinsic green motivation supports and extends past studies [13, 66], which describe eco-anxiety not merely as a distressing emotional state but as a productive form of worry that can stimulate deeper ecological awareness and value-driven engagement. This result is consistent with Verplanken and Roy [73], who argued that moderate eco-anxiety can enhance moral reflection and ecological responsibility. However, it contrasts with Kapeller and Jäger [34], who found that severe eco-anxiety may inhibit behavioral engagement due to emotional overload. The consistency of our findings with value-based interpretations suggests that in educational settings, moderate eco-anxiety can be harnessed as a motivational catalyst rather than a barrier.

Interestingly, eco-anxiety also positively predicted extrinsic green motivation, indicating that environmental concern may also lead students to engage in green behavior for externally oriented reasons, such as conforming to social expectations, gaining approval, or aligning with institutional norms. This finding diverges from previous assumptions that eco-anxiety is solely associated with internal commitment [4, 9]. It highlights the dual function of eco-anxiety: while it drives values-based behavior, it can also prompt externally motivated actions, especially in collectivist cultures like Vietnam, where social harmony and public image are influential behavioral cues [32].

The results also revealed that social anxiety significantly predicts both intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation. This dual influence reflects the complexity of social anxiety as both a self-protective mechanism and a socially driven motivator. On one hand, socially anxious students may be extrinsically motivated to “fit in” by adopting environmentally responsible behaviors. On the other hand, their heightened awareness of others’ evaluations may also lead to increased internal reflection, fostering personal identification with pro-environmental norms. This aligns with Fielding and Hornsey [20], who found that social pressures can accelerate the internalization of environmental norms. It also extends the findings of Yadav, Kumar [76] by suggesting that anxiety-driven conformity can transform into authentic commitment when moral reasoning is activated.

In addition, the study confirms that both intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation positively influence green behavioral intention, reinforcing self-determination theory (SDT), which argues that human behavior is driven by a continuum of motivation ranging from autonomous to controlled forms [58]. While intrinsic motivation is rooted in personal interest and environmental concern, extrinsic motivation can also be effective, particularly in social contexts where environmental responsibility is expected or rewarded. These findings are consistent with prior studies [63, 72], which found that both autonomous and controlled forms of motivation can lead to pro-environmental actions when aligned with social values. However, our results suggest a stronger effect of intrinsic motivation, which may reflect the role of moral and emotional engagement in sustaining long-term behavioral commitment among students.

Furthermore, the direct effect of perceived environmental responsibility on green behavioral intention aligns with the norm activation model (NAM) and value-belief-norm (VBN) theory, which posit that moral obligation and personal norms are central to pro-environmental behavior [68]. Students who believe they have a personal responsibility to care for the environment are more likely to act on their motivation, regardless of whether it stems from internal beliefs or external influences. This finding is in agreement with past studies [19, 50], who similarly observed that moral norms and perceived duty serve as strong predictors of intention across sustainability domains.

Critically, the moderating effects of perceived environmental responsibility offer an important theoretical contribution. The results show that students who feel a higher sense of environmental responsibility are more likely to amplify the influence of their motivation—both intrinsic and extrinsic—on actual behavioral intention. This interaction suggests that responsibility does not merely operate as a standalone predictor but also strengthens the motivational pathways. This corroborates Duong, Doan [18], but our findings further reveal that responsibility transforms motivational drive into moral commitment, reinforcing intention even under social or emotional pressure.

Finally, the explained variance in the key endogenous variables—green motivation and behavioral intention—was substantial, confirming the model’s strong predictive power. Compared with similar psychological frameworks in prior studies [37, 59], our integrated model demonstrates higher explanatory power, likely due to the inclusion of emotional antecedents alongside moral and motivational constructs. By integrating emotional, motivational, and ethical constructs, the model offers a comprehensive and theoretically grounded framework that reflects the multidimensional nature of student sustainability behavior.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the literature by demonstrating how eco-anxiety and social anxiety act as psychological triggers of green motivation, how intrinsic and extrinsic motivation jointly shape behavioral intentions, and how perceived environmental responsibility strengthens these relationships. These findings provide valuable insights for both theory and practice, emphasizing the importance of emotionally grounded and morally reinforced engagement strategies in sustainability education.

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study makes several important theoretical contributions to the fields of environmental psychology, behavioral science, and sustainability education by integrating emotional, motivational, and ethical dimensions into a unified framework for understanding green behavioral intention among students. By doing so, it responds to growing scholarly calls for integrative frameworks that connect emotional, motivational, and moral processes in explaining sustainability behavior [12, 73]. Grounded in (SDT) [58] and value-based frameworks such as the VBN theory [68], this study bridges motivational regulation and moral obligation within a single integrative model. SDT provides the foundation for understanding how eco-anxiety and social anxiety shape intrinsic and extrinsic green motivations, while VBN theory explains how perceived environmental responsibility operates as a moral norm that reinforces the translation of motivation into behavioral intention. Together, these perspectives establish an affect–motivation–morality linkage that broadens the theoretical boundaries of environmental behavior research.

First, by confirming the significant roles of eco-anxiety and social anxiety in shaping intrinsic and extrinsic green motivation, this study expands the traditional application of models such as the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and SDT [58]. While previous TPB-based studies have largely focused on cognitive predictors such as attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control [2], this study highlights the importance of emotional antecedents. In doing so, it responds to recent scholarly calls [12, 73] for the integration of affective states—such as anxiety—into behavioral intention models, showing that eco-anxiety and social anxiety serve as meaningful psychological inputs to sustainability-related motivations. This finding advances the broader academic discourse by positioning emotion as a fundamental entry point in motivational theories, illustrating that affective tension can be a constructive rather than disruptive force in sustainability-related decision-making.

Second, the study advances SDT by empirically demonstrating how specific types of anxiety differentially influence intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Whereas past SDT applications in sustainability contexts have emphasized autonomous and controlled motivation [52], this study shows that eco-anxiety can drive both internal and external motivational pathways, while social anxiety contributes to both as well, suggesting that emotional vulnerability can act as a dual force within the motivational spectrum. This dual-path finding extends SDT beyond its traditional cognitive framing by revealing how anxiety-based arousal may activate both self-determined and externally regulated behaviors. It underscores that emotional ambivalence—often treated as maladaptive—can serve a self-regulatory function that aligns with pro-social and pro-environmental goals. This supports SDT’s proposition that both internalized values and externally regulated motivations can coexist and jointly influence behavioral outcomes, especially in emotionally charged contexts like environmental concern.

Third, the study offers theoretical value by establishing perceived environmental responsibility not only as a direct antecedent to green behavioral intention but also as a moderator that enhances the effects of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Unlike prior research that treats responsibility as a static trait, this study conceptualizes it as an activated personal norm that can vary depending on situational cues and social context [64]. This framing explains why perceived responsibility effectively moderates the motivation–intention link: moral obligation becomes situationally salient when students engage with environmental issues. This contributes to the ongoing refinement of value-based theories by showing that personal norms are dynamic, contextually triggered moral mechanisms rather than fixed traits. It also bridges SDT and VBN by empirically validating how moral obligation reinforces motivational regulation—an intersection rarely tested in sustainability models. The moderating effect identified in this study has rarely been examined in prior environmental behavior literature and opens a new direction for understanding how moral constructs function within broader psychological models.

Fourth, the study’s structural model demonstrates strong explanatory power for green motivation and intention, supporting the integration of emotional, motivational, and ethical variables into a cohesive predictive framework. This finding contributes to theoretical model-building by demonstrating that combining affective and moral dimensions significantly improves predictive validity over traditional cognition-based models (e.g., TPB or simple SDT extensions). This contributes to theory-building efforts that aim to account for more of the variance in sustainability-related behaviors and provides a foundation for future models that seek to explain not just whether, but why and how students engage in environmentally responsible action.

Finally, by focusing on a Vietnamese student population, the study also adds cultural depth to existing sustainability behavior theories, which have predominantly been tested in Western contexts. In collectivist societies, where social conformity and moral duty are strongly emphasized, the role of social anxiety and perceived responsibility may be particularly salient—an observation that can inform future cross-cultural validations and theoretical refinements. This cross-cultural evidence expands the generalizability of SDT and VBN by demonstrating how emotional and moral processes operate differently under collectivist norms, providing a valuable reference point for global sustainability research.

In summary, by integrating SDT and VBN frameworks, this study enriches the theoretical landscape of sustainability behavior by demonstrating how emotional distress, motivational structure, and ethical commitment interact to shape green behavioral intention. It advances the broader academic discourse by uniting affective, motivational, and moral theories into a cohesive explanation of sustainable action. Moreover, it highlights perceived environmental responsibility as a flexible moral construct—capable of acting as both an antecedent and amplifier—thus offering new directions for theorizing the moral activation of pro-environmental motivation across cultural and emotional contexts.

Practical implications

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for educators, sustainability advocates, policymakers, and organizations seeking to promote green behavior among university students.

First, the significant influence of eco-anxiety and social anxiety on green motivation highlights the need to integrate emotional engagement into sustainability education, consistent with recent findings emphasizing the constructive role of environmental emotions [66]. Rather than treating anxiety as a barrier, institutions can design programs that constructively channel environmental concerns into action. For example, universities could offer workshops, reflection activities, or eco-dialogue spaces that help students explore their emotions about climate change while connecting them with tangible solutions. These emotion-centered interventions align with research showing that emotional reflection enhances climate engagement and fosters empathy-driven environmental learning [30]. Recognizing that such emotions can enhance both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, emotional engagement should be considered a strategic component of curriculum design.

Second, the strong effects of social anxiety on motivation underscore the importance of peer influence and social norms, supporting prior evidence that normative approval and social desirability significantly shape pro-environmental action [20]. This highlights the value of peer-led environmental campaigns, green ambassador programs, and visible sustainability commitments within student communities. When green actions are publicly endorsed and socially rewarded, students who are sensitive to peer perceptions are more likely to adopt and maintain those behaviors. Universities can therefore leverage social modeling and peer feedback systems—strategies proven to increase sustainable habits through collective identity and social reinforcement [11].

Third, the confirmed impact of perceived environmental responsibility—both directly and as a moderator—suggests that fostering moral engagement and accountability is critical for long-term sustainability behavior. This finding aligns with studies showing that perceived moral obligation is a powerful predictor of consistent green actions [50]. However, to avoid treating perceived responsibility as a fixed personality trait, institutions should focus on activating moral norms through experience-based and participatory learning. Service-learning projects, sustainability reflection assignments, and campus-wide eco-challenges can make moral responsibility situationally salient, encouraging students to internalize and act on their values. Educational institutions can incorporate value-based messaging, service-learning projects, and opportunities for environmental citizenship to cultivate this sense of responsibility. Interventions such as sustainability pledges, public commitments, and eco-leadership programs help translate ethical awareness into habitual pro-environmental action.

Fourth, the dual influence of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation implies that sustainability interventions should balance value-based and incentive-based approaches (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Gadenne et al., 2011). While some students respond well to internal values and ethical appeals, others may be more influenced by external incentives, recognition, or institutional expectations. Thus, a balanced approach that includes both value-oriented and reward-based strategies is more likely to resonate with a diverse student population. For instance, institutions could combine symbolic recognition (e.g., sustainability badges or certificates) with experiential learning opportunities to strengthen both intrinsic and extrinsic engagement. This combination reflects recommendations from Steg, Bolderdijk [67], who found that moral framing paired with visible reinforcement sustains long-term pro-environmental commitment.

Lastly, the model developed in this study can be used as a diagnostic tool for identifying students’ psychological readiness for green behavior. Sustainability offices and student affairs departments could use adapted versions of the survey to segment students based on emotional and motivational profiles, allowing for more personalized sustainability engagement strategies. This diagnostic approach is consistent with recent research advocating targeted interventions based on psychological segmentation [37], ensuring that sustainability education is not only informational but also emotionally and morally responsive.

Limitations and future research

While this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was limited to university students in Vietnam, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other cultural or demographic groups. Future research should replicate the study across different countries, age groups, and educational levels to enhance external validity. Second, the use of a cross-sectional survey and self-reported data may introduce common method bias and limit causal interpretations. Longitudinal research and the inclusion of actual behavioral data would help clarify the directionality and long-term effects of the observed relationships. Third, although this study focused on eco-anxiety and social anxiety as emotional predictors, other relevant emotions—such as environmental guilt, pride, or hope—could be explored in future models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of affective influences on green behavior. Fourth, perceived environmental responsibility was examined as a situational moderator rather than a stable trait. Future studies should test its dual nature by comparing its role as (1) an activated moral norm that strengthens motivational effects and (2) a long-term disposition interacting with eco- or social anxiety. Research could also assess whether interventions that activate moral responsibility—such as reflective learning or community projects—enhance sustainable behavior. Fifth, while green behavioral intention was used as the primary outcome, future research should investigate the intention–behavior gap by including follow-up assessments or observable pro-environmental actions. Lastly, we acknowledge an integrated alternative with direct anxiety → intention paths. Future work may compare both specifications (e.g., changes in R², indirect vs. direct effects) across cultures or contexts to assess boundary conditions for direct affect–intention links.
