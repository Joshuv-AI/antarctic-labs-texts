The Kinematic Age of 3I/ATLAS and its Implications for Early Planet Formation
Aster G. Taylor, Darryl Z. Seligman

Abstract
The recent discovery of the third interstellar object (3I/ATLAS) expands the known census from two to three and significantly improves statistical inferences regarding the underlying galactic population. 3I/ATLAS exhibits detectable activity that may increase as the object approaches perihelion. In this paper, we argue that the cometary activity likely significantly contributes to 3I/ATLAS’s brightness, since the nuclear size of 3I/ATLAS assuming an asteroidal reflectance implies an untenable interstellar object mass per star. 3I/ATLAS exhibits an excess velocity of v ∞ = 58 km / s v_{\infty}=$58\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$ relative to the Sun which is significantly larger than that of 1I/‘Oumuamua or 2I/Borisov. This velocity implies that 3I/ATLAS is relatively old in comparison to previous interstellar objects. Here, we calculate the posterior distribution of ages implied by the kinematics of the interstellar objects and find that 3I/ATLAS is likely ∼ 3 − 11 \sim 3-11 Gyr old, assuming that the interstellar object and stellar age-velocity relations are equivalent. We also calculate the distribution of host star metallicities and find that 3I/ATLAS likely originated in a lower-metallicity system than the previous interstellar objects. These results show that interstellar object formation is likely efficient at low metallicities and early in the history of the Galaxy. Finally, we estimate and approximate interstellar object formation rate throughout Galactic history implied by these three objects.

II
Estimated Number Density and Size

We calculate an approximate number density by assuming that the detection rate for an object like 3I/ATLAS is
\Gamma=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
. This value reflects the time that the ATLAS survey has been online, especially when combined with the Pan-STARRS1 survey
(
K. C. Chambers et al. 2016
)
and the Catalina Sky Survey, which are also sensitive to objects of this magnitude. Now, the ATLAS survey detects all objects down to an apparent magnitude of 19. Since 3I/ATLAS has an absolute magnitude of 12, an object with
H\leq 12
is detectable by ATLAS at a distance of approximately
d=$5\text{\,}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}$
, corresponding to a detection cross-section of
\sigma=\pi d^{2}
. Since 3I/ATLAS is moving at
v_{\infty}=$58\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
, the total number density is
n=\Gamma/(\sigma v_{\infty})\simeq${10}^{-4}\text{\,}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}^{-3}$
. This value is comparable to the previous estimate of
3\text{\times}{10}^{-4}\text{\,}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}^{-3}

(
D. Z. Seligman et al. 2025
)
if slightly different assumptions are made about the ATLAS detection area.

However, this value implies an exceptionally high mass of interstellar objects produced per star. If the stars in the Galaxy are separated by distances of approximately
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c}
, then each star should eject
{10}^{13}
interstellar objects the size of 3I/ATLAS or larger. If 3I/ATLAS were nearly
r_{N}\simeq$10\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}$
across, at the typical cometary density of
\rho=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
each star must produce approximately
{10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of interstellar objects larger than 3I/ATLAS. There must be some additional mass stored in smaller objects, which will only add to this (already significant) value.

It is presumably infeasible for formation processes to produce this density of interstellar objects. Since the typical star is
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, this would imply that there is
\sim 50
times the mass in stars than in interstellar objects. When accounting for the metallicity, the interstellar objects represent the metals of nearly twice as much matter as is represented in stars.

Therefore, the true size of 3I/ATLAS must be somewhat less than the estimated
10\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}
upper limit. This is expected, since 3I/ATLAS has been shown to be active even at
4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}
. In order for 3I/ATLAS to imply a mass of

of (see also

(1)

Given the activity already detected on 3I/ATLAS
(
M. R. Alarcon et al. 2025
;
D. Jewitt & J. Luu 2025
;
D. Z. Seligman et al. 2025
;
C. Opitom et al. 2025
)
, this is a somewhat reasonable value, since the coma can account for a significant fraction of its brightness. If 3I/ATLAS’s nuclear size is closer to
2\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}
, then the body itself will have an absolute magnitude of
H=15.5
. Given that the measured absolute magnitude is

III
Kinematic Age

The excess velocity of 3I/ATLAS is nearly
v_{\infty}\simeq$58\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
relative to the Sun, significantly higher than 1I/‘Oumuamua’s velocity of
26\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}
or 2I/Borisov’s
32\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}

For a given measured velocity, we can calculate the probability density function (PDF) of the expected ages. We first assume that interstellar objects have a velocity distribution close to that of their parent star. This is expected, since the stellar velocity distribution is modified over time by the effects of the Galactic potential, which will have nearly identical effects on the interstellar objects

V

IV
Parent Stellar Metallicities

Given that the Galactic metallicity increases with time, we can convert these age probability distributions into metallicity distributions. For a given age, we assume that the metallicity distribution has a uniform scatter of
\pm$0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}$
and a median value given by Fig. 2 in
V. A. Marsakov et al. 2011
. Assuming that the metallicity follows a uniform distribution provides
p(Z|\tau)
. Therefore,
p(Z)=\int p(Z|\tau)p(\tau){\rm d}\tau
. With
p(\tau)
given by Eq. (
3
) and

p(Z|\tau)=\begin{cases}5/3&|Z-Z_{0}(\tau)|\leq$0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}$;\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(8)

we calculate
p(Z)
. We show this distribution in Fig.
2
. The median parent metallicity of 1I/‘Oumuamua is
\text{[Fe/H]}=-0.08\pm 0.21
, 2I/Borisov has
\text{[Fe/H]}=-0.15^{+0.22}_{-0.21}
, and 3I/ATLAS has
\text{[Fe/H]}=-0.18^{+0.23}_{-0.21}
.

While the three interstellar objects do not exhibit significant variation in their parent stellar population metallicity distribution, the older objects likely originated from lower-metallicity systems. Specifically, it is not unreasonable for 3I/ATLAS to be formed around a star with a metallicity as low as
-0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}
, which would represent a system with fewer metals than the Sun by a factor of
0.4
. This result implies that interstellar object formation processes are operating at metallicities somewhat lower than the Sun. The detection of planets around stars of similar metallicity (
\text{[Fe/H]}=-0.68
,
W. D. Cochran et al. 2007
) is consistent with this result.

V
interstellar object Formation Rate

The fact that these interstellar objects cover a range of possible age values implies that interstellar objects must be formed over a similarly wide range of ages. Indeed, we can use the expected age PDFs to estimate the production rate of interstellar objects over time.

Given
N
observations, each of which has a PDF
p_{i}(\tau)
, the total PDF of these observations is given by

p(\tau)=\sum_{i}w_{i}p_{i}(\tau)\,,

(9)

where
w_{i}
are the weights of the observations and
\sum w_{i}=1
. The weight of each observation is determined by the probability of such an observation occurring. In this case, these weights will depend on the size and absolute magnitude of the interstellar objects as well as the detection capabilities of observational telescopes. To date, the size of 3I/ATLAS is not well-known, and so these weights cannot be approximated here. However, given the dearth of interstellar objects and the relatively consistent detection rate, it is reasonable to assume that the probability of observing each of the three known interstellar objects is equal so that
w_{i}=1/3
. Given that assumption, we can calculate the total interstellar object production rate versus time
p(\tau)
, which is shown in Fig.
3
.

We can assume that the interstellar object formation rate is proportional to the total PDF of interstellar object ages. This assumption is predicated on the idea that if interstellar objects are destroyed over time, that destruction rate is a constant function of the Galactic age or is negligible in comparison to interstellar object production
(
M. J. Hopkins et al. 2023
;
M. J. Hopkins et al. 2025
)
. Given these assumptions, Fig.
3
indicates that the interstellar object production rates were relatively flat for the first
10\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
of Galactic history and increased in the last
100\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
. While our approximate interstellar object formation rate then drops off to a very small production rate in the last
10\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
, this region has significant bias. Interstellar objects with ages of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
have typical velocities of only
5\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}
and have a maximum range of
50\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c}
. This sample may therefore be volume-limited, since many interstellar objects with ages
\tau\leq$10\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
could not have made it to the solar system to be detected.

VI
Discussion

In this paper, we have built off previous work on the ages of 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov (e.g.,
F. Almeida-Fernandes & H. J. Rocha-Pinto 2018b
;
T. Hallatt & P. Wiegert 2020
) to calculate the kinematic age of the new object, 3I/ATLAS. Our results indicate that 3I/ATLAS is the oldest detected interstellar object, approximately
311\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
old. 3I/ATLAS therefore provides a sample of planetary system formation in the early Galaxy, confirming that such systems exist and allowing us to determine how such systems may differ from the younger exoplanet systems currently studied. Given its age, this object also likely originated in the thick disk of the Galaxy, providing a sample of system formation in this region.

We have also calculated the metallicity distribution for the stars that may have formed each interstellar object. As expected, the older objects generally favor lower-metallicity parents, although all metallicity distributions are similar and relatively flat. Due to the large inherent scatter in the age-metallicity relation, the interstellar object metallicities are very broad and cannot be narrowed down for such a small sample. It may be possible to determine if interstellar objects preferentially formed around stars of a certain metallicity when future interstellar objects are discovered. However, this will require a larger sample than is currently available.

In addition, the planetesimal mass budget at low metallicities is relatively limited
(
G. Andama et al. 2024
)
. If the size of 3I/ATLAS is
r_{N}=$2.3\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}$
, then the interstellar object mass budget for systems with a metallicity of
\text{[Fe/H]}\simeq$-0.40.03$
is

M_{\rm ISO}=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{M}_{\oplus}$\left(\frac{r_{N}}{$2.3\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}$}\right)^{3}\,.

(10)

This predicted mass budget will vary based on the size of 3I/ATLAS. Once the size of this object is established, it will be possible to estimate the corresponding mass budget.

Regardless, we have shown that 3I/ATLAS is old and likely formed around a low-metallicity star. The detection of this object therefore indicates that interstellar object formation is efficient at low metallicities and early in the history of the Galaxy. While planets have been found around low-metallicity stars (e.g.,
W. D. Cochran et al. 2007
), the detection of 3I/ATLAS provides an independent confirmation of this result.

Lastly, we calculated the interstellar object formation rate implied by these detections. Our calculation suggests that while interstellar object production occurred early in the lifetime of the Galaxy, the interstellar object formation rate increased for the first several
\mathrm{G}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
before flattening out. The interstellar object formation rate then increased to a peak in the last
\sim
300\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
before dropping off rapidly in the last
100\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
. However, this final dropoff is complicated by a possible detection bias against such young objects, which may have difficulty reaching the solar system.

Finally, we describe caveats to these results. Our calculations are preliminary in nature and heavily rely on approximation. While many of these approximations are appropriate and will hold to an order-of-magnitude level, our results will change as more interstellar objects are detected and as the physical properties of 3I/ATLAS are better constrained with forthcoming observations. Particular caution must be taken with our calculation of the interstellar object production rate calculation, which relies on an equal-weight assumption and will require careful consideration of the detection statistics to gain true insight.
