Teleportation Fidelity of Binary Tree Quantum Repeater Networks
Soumit Roy, Md Rahil Miraj, Chittaranjan Hens, Ganesh Mylavarapu, Subrata Ghosh, Indranil Chakrabarty

Abstract
The idea of average of maximum teleportation fidelities was introduced in Mylavarapu et al. 2025 to measure the capability of the network to act as a resource for distributed teleportation between any pair of nodes. Binary tree network, being a subclass of Cayley tree network, is a significant topological structure used for information transfer in a hierarchical sense. In this article, we consider four types of binary tree repeater networks (directed and undirected, asymmetric and symmetric) and obtain the analytical expressions of the average of the maximum teleportation fidelities for each of these binary tree networks. Our work investigates the role of directionality and symmetry in this measure. Like Mylavarapu et al. 2025 , here also we have used simple Werner state-based models and are able to identify the parameter ranges for which these networks can show quantum advantage. We explore the role of maximally entangled states in the network to enhance the quantum advantage. We explore the large N N (nodes) scenario for each of these networks and find out the limiting value of the average of the maximum teleportation fidelity for each of these networks. We also include the scenarios when the Werner state parameters for each link are different and chosen from a uniform distribution. According to our analysis, the directed symmetric binary tree is the most beneficial topology in this context. Our results pave the way to identify the resourceful network in transmitting quantum information.

II.1
Entanglement Swapping and Quantum Repeater

Entanglement swapping is the process by which you can teleport a quantum entanglement from one location to another, thus by connecting two nodes which are not initially entangled with each other. The simplest scheme of entanglement swapping involves three parties, Alice, Bob and Charlie. Here Alice and Charlie shares Bell state
|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{AB_{1}}
and
|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{B_{2}C}
with Bob. Now, if Bob carries out two two-qubit Bell-state measurements on his qubits
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, Alice and Charlie will end up with two two-qubit states depending upon the measurement outcomes (See Fig.
1
) at Bob’s location,

\displaystyle|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{B_{1}B_{2}}\rightarrow|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{AC},{}

\displaystyle|\Psi^{+}\rangle_{B_{1}B_{2}}\rightarrow X_{C}|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{AC},{}

\displaystyle|\Phi^{-}\rangle_{B_{1}B_{2}}\rightarrow Z_{C}|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{AC},{}

\displaystyle|\Psi^{-}\rangle_{B_{1}B_{2}}\rightarrow Z_{C}X_{C}|\Phi^{+}\rangle_{AC}.

(1)

Here,
X
and
Z
are the Pauli operators. Alice and Charlie are able to generate a maximally entangled state between them without interacting directly. This process can be explored to create a long-distance entanglement between two parties which are not initially connected. Now, if instead of a maximally entangled state we have the same Werner state
\rho_{\text{w}er}
with input state parameter
p
,

\rho_{\text{wer}}=\frac{1-p}{4}\mathbb{I}+p\outerproduct{\Psi^{-}}{\Psi^{-}},

(2)

as a resource between Alice-Bob and Bob-Charlie, respectively. After first swapping the state between Alice and Charlie will become
Mylavarapu et al. 2023
;
Sen et al. 2005
,

\rho_{\text{wer}}^{(1)}=\frac{1-p^{2}}{4}\mathbb{I}+p^{2}\outerproduct{\Psi^{-}}{\Psi^{-}}.

(3)

If we extend this idea to a
(N+2)

1

(N+2)
th
node , then there should be
N

1

\rho_{\text{wer}}^{(N)}=\frac{1-p^{N+1}}{4}\mathbb{I}+p^{N+1}\outerproduct{\Psi^{-}}{\Psi^{-}}.

(4)

The idea of entanglement swapping can be extended to implement a quantum repeater between two nodes of a quantum network.

II.2
Teleportation Fidelity and Average of Maximum Teleportation Fidelity

Quantum teleportation is one of the celebrated methods by which one can send quantum information (qubit/qudit) (also quantum coherence) from one location to another with the help of an entangled state shared between the parties. If we consider a perfect teleportation protocol, we can send an arbitrary unknown qubit with a shared Bell state
Bennett et al. 1993
. If we do not have a maximally entangled state, we can still send a qubit with a non-maximally entangled resource
Agrawal and Pati 2002
. This can be achieved with a certain probability of success if not perfectly
Agrawal and Pati 2002
. Interestingly, it can be seen that for a two-qubit system or state
\rho
, the capacity of the state to act as a resource for teleportation can be quantified in the form of teleportation fidelity
Horodecki et al. 1996
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=(1+\mathcal{N}(\rho)/3)/2,

(5)

with
\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\Tr(\sqrt{T^{\dagger}T})
, where
T
is the correlation matrix of
\rho
. The elements of the matrix
T
are defined as
t_{mn}=\Tr{\rho(\sigma_m\otimes\sigma_n)}
. Here
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices and
(m,n)=1,2,3
. It is interesting to note that there are entangled states that are not useful for teleportation
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Chakrabarty 2010
;
Adhikari et al. 2008
;
Chakrabarty et al. 2011
and hence the teleportation witness operator
Ganguly et al. 2011
. For a Werner state
\rho_{\text{wer}}
, this fidelity comes out to be
\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2}(1+p)
. It shows quantum advantage when
\mathcal{F}>2/3

i.e.
when
p>1/3
.

For a repeater based Werner network, the achievable teleportation fidelity through a particular path (
\mathcal{P}
) connecting the source (S) and
the target (T) can be obtained as
F^{\rm tel}_{{\rm ST},\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\text{wer}})=\left(1+\prod_{i\in\mathcal{P}}p_{i}\right)/2

Mylavarapu et al. 2023
;
Mylavarapu et al. 2025
. However when all
p_{i}
are same and is equal to
p
then
F^{\rm tel}_{{\rm ST},\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\text{wer}})=\frac{1+p^{N+1}}{2}
for
N+2
nodes scenario.

The average of maximum teleportation fidelity for all paths in a network is a recent concept designed to reflect the ability of a network to act as a resource for carrying out teleportation between any source and any target
Mylavarapu et al. 2025
.
If S and T are connected via multiple paths, let
\mathcal{P}_{\rm max}
be the path with the maximum fidelity. We get the average of highest-achievable teleportation fidelity if we take the average of
F^{\rm max}_{{\rm ST},\mathcal{P}_{\rm max}}(\rho)
over all possible combinations of S and T (
i.e.
, any pair of nodes can be the source and the target):

\displaystyle F^{\rm max}_{\rm avg}(\rho)=\langle F^{\rm max}_{{\rm ST},\mathcal{P}_{\rm max}}(\rho)\rangle_{{\rm ST}}=\langle F^{\rm max}_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho)\rangle_{\mathcal{P}},

(6)

where the second step follows from the fact that in the absence of loops, the path between any S and T pair is unique. Hence, in our case, averaging over S and T pairs is equivalent to averaging over all possible paths in the network. From now on we denote this average as
\langle F^{\rm max}_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho)\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}
as
F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}
and call it as average teleportation fidelity as there is unique fidelity between source and target in absence of loops. In this article, we will calculate the
\langle F^{\rm max}_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho)\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}
for different types of binary trees.

Average teleportation fidelity for uniform distribution

In a real network, all these links between the nodes can suffer a different level of noise and can be modeled by different
p_{i}
values of the Werner states. For a fixed number of nodes
N
in each class of binary trees, we assign weights
\{p_{i}\}
to the edges, where each weight is independently drawn from a uniform distribution,
p_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
. Since a binary tree with
N
nodes contains
N-1
edges, each tree configuration is associated with
N-1
such weights. To include statistical robustness, we repeat this random weight assignment process 100 times (
T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{100}
) for each tree instance. In each time, the corresponding average teleportation fidelity,
F^{\text{tel}}
, is computed
\langle F_{\text{avg}}^{\text{tel}}\rangle_{T_{1},...,T_{100}}
. We then take the average of these values across all trials to obtain
\langle F_{\text{avg}}^{\text{tel}}\rangle_{T_{1},...,T_{100}}
. The result is shown as a histogram in Fig.
7
.

VI
Conclusion

In this article, we have investigated the average of maximum teleportation fidelity (average of teleportation fidelity as there is no loop) for four different types of hierarchical binary networks, namely DABT, UABT, DSBT and USBT, to understand the network’s ability to transfer the quantum information on a larger scale. We have obtained the analytical expressions for the average of maximum teleportation fidelities for each of these networks and their convergence for large
N
. In that process, we have modelled the edges of the network by Werner state (parametrized by
p
) to find out the range for which the networks will show quantum advantage (
i.e.

F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}>\frac{2}{3}
) in an average sense. We have also studied the role of maximally entangled states in obtaining quantum advantage in these networks by adding them to the network. Further, we consider a more general setting with different edge parameters taken from a uniform distribution to study the average of teleportation fidelity.

A.1
Directed Binary Trees

Directed Asymmetric Binary Trees (DABT):

Let
l_{d}^{(r)}
denote the number of
r
-length paths in a DABT with depth
d
. If
r>d
, then there is no path of length
r
. So,
l^{(r)}=0
for
r>d
. For
r=1
,
l_{d}^{(1)}
counts the number of edges. Hence,
l_{d}^{(1)}=2d
. For
r\geq 2
,
l_{d}^{(r)}
counts the number of
r
-length paths in the DABT of depth
d-1
rooted at the vertex
2
and the additional 2 paths that include the vertex
1
, namely,
(1,2,\cdots,2r-2,2r)
and
(1,2,\cdots,2r-2,2r+1)
. Thus, for a fixed
r
the relation we get is
l_{d}^{(r)}=l_{d-1}^{(r)}+2
, with the base case
l_{r}^{(r)}=2
, since, in the DABT with depth
r
, the
r
-length paths are
(1,2,\cdots,2r-2,2r)
and
(1,2,\cdots,2r-2,2r+1)
. Hence, the
d
-th term of this arithmetic progression we get is
l_{d}^{(r)}=2+2(d-r).
Therefore, combining the above cases, we get

l_{d}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}2+2(d-r),&\text{if }r\leq d\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

Hence, for
\mathcal{F}_{r}=\frac{1+p^{r}}{2}
, the
F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}
in the case of DABT is

\displaystyle\left.F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}(d,p)\right|_{\text{DABT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}l_{d}^{(r)}\mathcal{F}_{r}}{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}l_{d}^{(r)}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}[2+2(d-r)]\left(\frac{1+p^{r}}{2}\right)}{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}[2+2(d-r)]}=\frac{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{d}[1+(d-r)](1+p^{r})}{d(d+1)}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p[p(p^{d}-1)+d(1-p)]}{d(d+1)(1-p)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon(d).

Let the number of nodes of a DABT of depth
d
be
N=N(d)
, then for this case,
N(d)=2d+1
. Hence, we can write
F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}
in terms of
N
as

\displaystyle\left.F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}(N,p)\right|_{\text{DABT}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2p[2p(p^{\frac{N-1}{2}}-1)+(N-1)(1-p)]}{(N-1)(N+1)(1-p)^{2}}.

(19)

It is easy to see that
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
for this case.

Directed Symmetric Binary Trees (DSBT)
:

We denote the number of
r
-length paths starting from the base vertex
1
by
h_{r}
. At each level, there are two choices for selecting a vertex to include in the path up to depth
r
. Thus,
h_{r}=2^{r}
.
Now, let us denote by
l_{d}^{(r)}
the number of
r
-length paths in a DSBT with depth
d
. If
r>d
, then there is no
r
-length path, so
l_{d}^{(r)}=0
for
r>d
.

For
r\leq d
,
l_{d}^{(r)}
counts the total number of paths of length
r
in the subtrees rooted at the vertices
2
and
3
along with the paths that start with the base vertex
1
. So, for a fixed
r
we can write the recurrence for
\{l_{d}^{(r)}\}_{d}
in this form

l_{d}^{(r)}=2l_{d-1}^{(r)}+h_{r}=2l_{d-1}^{(r)}+2^{r}.

(20)

Now, let
z_{d}^{(r)}=l_{d}^{(r)}+2^{r}
. Then Eq. (
20
) becomes

z_{d}^{(r)}=2z_{d-1}^{(r)}
,
which has the general solution

z_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d}s^{(r)}

where,
s^{(r)}
is independent of
d
. Therefore, we get

l_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d}s^{(r)}-2^{r}.

(21)

If we take
d=r
, then we will have
l_{r}^{(r)}=2^{r}
, as it will only count all the paths starting with the base vertex
1
. Thus, using this in Eq. (
21
), we obtain
s^{(r)}=2
for all
r\leq d
. Therefore, for
r\leq d
, we will have

l_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d+1}-2^{r}.

(22)

Therefore, combining the above cases, we get

Hence, for

\displaystyle\left.F^{\text{tel}}_{\text{avg}}(d,p)\right|_{\text{DSBT}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2^{d+1}-d-2}{2\left[(d-1)\cdot 2^{d+1}+2\right]},&\text{if }\penalty\ p=\frac{1}{2},\\[12.0pt]

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon(d).

(25)

If the total number of nodes of a DSBT of depth

is

, then

. Hence, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p[N(1-2p)+(N+1)p^{\log_{2}(N+1)}-1]}{4(1-p)(1-2p)\left[(N+1)\log_{2}(N+1)-2N\right]},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

for this case.

Here again,
h_{r}
denotes the number of
r
-length paths starting from the base vertex
1
, and at each level, there are two choices for selecting a vertex to include in the path up to depth
r
. So,
h_{r}=2^{r}
. Now, let us denote by
l_{d}^{(r)}
the number of
r
-length paths in a USBT with depth
d
.

If
r>2d
, then there is no
r
-length path, so
l_{d}^{(r)}=0
for
r>2d
.

Now, for
1\leq r\leq 2d
,
l_{d}^{(r)}
counts the total number of paths of length
r
in the subtrees rooted at the vertices
2
and
3
along with the paths that include the base vertex
1
. So, for a fixed
r
we can write the recurrence for
\{l_{d}^{(r)}\}_{d}
in this form

l_{d}^{(r)}=2l_{d-1}^{(r)}+c_{d}^{(r)},

(31)

where,
c_{d}^{(r)}
denotes the number of paths that include the base vertex
1
, while connecting the two subtrees rooted at vertices
2
and
3
. We consider the following cases:

Case I:

d<r\leq 2d
:

In this case, no path begins with the base vertex
1
. Hence,
c_{d}^{(r)}
counts the number of paths where vertex
1
is not an endpoint. That is, it counts the total number of paths with
k
edges on the left and
r-k
edges on the right of vertex
1
, for all
r-d\leq k\leq d
. Therefore,

c_{d}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=r-d}^{d}h_{k-1}\cdot h_{r-k-1}=\sum_{k=r-d}^{d}2^{k-1}\cdot 2^{r-k-1}=(2d-r+1)\cdot 2^{r-2}.

Thus, we obtain the recurrence to be

l_{d}^{(r)}=2l_{d-1}^{(r)}+(2d-r+1)\cdot 2^{r-2}.

(32)

Let
a_{d}^{(r)}=2^{-(r-2)}l_{d}^{(r)}+(2d-r+5)
. Then Eq. (
32
) becomes

a_{d}^{(r)}=2a_{d-1}^{(r)}
,
which has the general solution

a_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d}\cdot p^{(r)}

where,
p^{(r)}
is independent of
d
. Therefore, we get

l_{d}^{(r)}=\left[2^{d}p^{(r)}-(2d-r+5)\right]\cdot 2^{r-2}.

(33)

If
r=2k
, then
l_{k-1}^{(2k)}=0
, so
l_{k}^{(2k)}=2^{2k-2}
. Using this in Eq. (
33
), we get
p^{(2k)}=3\cdot 2^{-(k-1)}
. Therefore,

{l_{d}^{(2k)}=3\cdot 2^{d+k-1}-(2d-2k+5)\cdot 2^{2k-2}}.

(34)

Now, if
r=2k-1
, then
l_{k-1}^{(2k-1)}=0
, so
l_{k}^{(2k-1)}=2^{2k-2}
. Hence, again from Eq. (
33
), we get
p^{(2k-1)}=2^{-(k-3)}
. Therefore,

l_{d}^{(2k-1)}=2^{d+k}-(d-k+3)\cdot 2^{2k-2}.

(35)

Case II:

r\leq d

In this case,
c_{d}^{(r)}
counts the number of paths where vertex
1
is indeed an endpoint and also the number of paths with
k
edges on the left and
r-k
edges on the right of vertex
1
, for all
1\leq k\leq r-1
. Thus,

c_{d}^{(r)}=h_{r}+\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}h_{k-1}\cdot h_{r-k-1}=2^{r}+\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}2^{k-1}\cdot 2^{r-k-1}=(r+3)\cdot 2^{r-2}.

Therefore, the recurrence that we obtain this time is

l_{d}^{(r)}=2l_{d-1}^{(r)}+(r+3)\cdot 2^{r-2}.

(36)

Now, let
b_{d}^{(r)}=l_{d}^{(r)}+(r+3)\cdot 2^{r-2}
. Then Eq. (
36
) becomes

b_{d}^{(r)}=2b_{d-1}^{(r)}
,
which has the general solution

b_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d}q^{(r)}

where,
q^{(r)}
is independent of
d
. Therefore, we get

l_{d}^{(r)}=2^{d}q^{(r)}-(r+3)\cdot 2^{r-2}.

(37)

If
r=2k
, then by taking
d=2k-1
in Eq. (
34
), we get

l_{2k-1}^{(2k)}=3\cdot 2^{3k-2}-(2k+3)\cdot 2^{2k-2}.

Thus, using this in Eq. (
37
), we obtain
q^{(2k)}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}
. Therefore,

l_{d}^{(2k)}=3\cdot 2^{d+k-1}-(2k+3)\cdot 2^{2k-2}.

(38)

Now, if
r=2k+1
, then again from Eq. (
35
), we get

l_{2k}^{(2k+1)}=2^{3k+1}-(k+2)\cdot 2^{2k}.

Thus, using this in Eq. (
37
), we obtain
q^{(2k+1)}=2^{k+1}
. Therefore,

l_{d}^{(2k+1)}=2^{d+k+1}-(k+2)\cdot 2^{2k}.

(39)

Therefore, combining all four cases, we get

0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(40)
