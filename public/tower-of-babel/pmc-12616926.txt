Predictive factors for dementia among older adults in South Korea: an interpretable machine learning analysis
Authors: Minsung Sohn, Jungyeon Yang, Jun Hyup Lee, Daeyoung Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSouth Korea is among the fastest-aging countries globally, with a rapidly rising prevalence of dementia. Early identification of individuals at risk is critical for effective prevention, as dementia is influenced by both non-modifiable factors, such as age, sex, and baseline cognitive status, and modifiable factors, including socioeconomic conditions, health behaviors, and psychosocial characteristics. This study aimed to identify multidimensional determinants of dementia using machine learning applied to nationally representative longitudinal data, examining how these factors interact across demographic and cognitive subgroups to inform targeted, evidence-based prevention strategies.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA; 2014–2020), including 4,958 participants aged 45 years and older without baseline dementia. Participants were stratified by baseline cognitive status (cognitively normal vs. mild cognitive impairment (MCI)), with further subgroup comparisons by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65) and sex for cognitively normal individuals. Predictors spanning sociodemographic, health, behavioral, and contextual domains were examined. Four regression algorithms—linear regression, random forests, XGBoost, and CatBoost—were applied, and model performance was evaluated via RMSE, MAE, and R². Predictor importance was assessed using a multi-method approach integrating model-based metrics and SHAP values, with top predictors identified for each subgroup.ResultsPredictive performance was comparable across algorithms, with R² ranging from 0.201 to 0.361, highest in the MCI_All dataset. Age and education were consistently the most influential non-modifiable factors. Key modifiable contributors included oral health, depression, household income, quality of life, and IADL performance. Importance patterns varied by cognitive status, age, and sex: socioeconomic and psychosocial factors were more influential in cognitively normal adults, whereas health status and IADL predominated in MCI participants. Age-stratified analyses highlighted oral health, depression change, and social contact in adults < 65, and cumulative factors including IADL decline in adults ≥ 65. Sex-stratified analyses indicated stronger effects of household income and social engagement in men, and depression and oral health in women. SHAP analyses confirmed inverse associations between changes in depression and IADL performance and predicted cognitive scores.ConclusionsAge and education were the strongest predictors of cognitive function, while modifiable factors—including oral health, depression, social engagement, and IADL performance—played significant roles across subgroups. This interpretable machine learning approach revealed nuanced patterns of predictor importance across cognitive status, age, and sex, underscoring the value of targeted interventions to reduce dementia risk in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01871-y.Keywords: Cognitive decline, Dementia, Longitudinal study, Machine learning, Older adults

Background

South Korea is one of the most rapidly aging countries globally, transitioning from an aging society to a super-aged society within just 26 years—faster than Japan (36 years) and the United States (94 years) [1]. This demographic transition has been accompanied by a marked increase in dementia prevalence: national estimates indicate that the number of Koreans living with dementia rose from approximately 610,000 in 2014 to 1 million in 2024—a 65% increase over a decade—and is projected to exceed 3 million by 2050 [2]. The prevalence rate among adults aged ≥ 65 years (10.3%) is also higher than the OECD average (approximately 7–8%), underscoring a disproportionate societal and healthcare burden [3, 4]. In response, the Korean government implemented the National Responsibility for Dementia Policy (NRDP) to expand preventive care and support services [5]. Nevertheless, stronger evidence is needed to guide preventive strategies, particularly those grounded in early identification of predictive and modifiable risk factors.

Dementia is a complex condition with multiple contributing factors, making it difficult to pinpoint a single cause. Following recent advances in dementia prevention research, including the WHO guidelines, risk factors are categorized into non-modifiable and modifiable domains to guide intervention strategies [6, 7]. Among non-modifiable factors, age is widely recognized as the most substantial risk factor, with dementia incidence rising sharply after the age of 65 [8, 9]. This age threshold also aligns with primary policy targets, including Korea’s NRDP and WHO guidelines, reinforcing the importance of analyzing cognitive decline across age-based subgroups. Sex is another crucial factor: both men and women exhibit distinct patterns in the onset and progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia [10, 11]. Specifically, biological factors (e.g., hormones, chromosomes) and early-life social conditions (e.g., education, occupation) may further contribute to these differences [12, 13]. Moreover, cognitive status—particularly distinguishing between cognitively normal individuals and those with MCI—is crucial for identifying preclinical risk and guiding timely interventions before the onset of dementia [14].

In addition, modifiable factors, offering opportunities for active intervention [15], span socioeconomic factors such as income [2, 3, 16], and health behaviors including alcohol consumption [17], smoking [18], and exercise [19, 20]. Additionally, health conditions with cardiovascular risks—such as diabetes, obesity, and hypertension—whose management has been demonstrated to reduce cognitive decline risk [21]. Psychosocial characteristics have also gained prominence: late-life depression and related symptoms such as apathy or anxiety may elevate dementia risk and often precede measurable decline, serving as early indicators of MCI or progression [22–24], and social engagement appears protective, buffering against cognitive decline [25–27]. These findings motivate prediction models that integrate both non-modifiable and modifiable domains to more precisely identify at-risk individuals and inform targeted strategies.

The key to managing dementia lies in the early identification of high-risk individuals [28]. Improving cognitive abilities years before a clinical diagnosis provides economic and public health benefits, and thus healthcare policies emphasize the optimization of dementia diagnosis timing [29–31]. Early intervention is particularly crucial for individuals with MCI, who face an increased, though not inevitable, risk of dementia [32–34]. However, prior research has often grouped all cognitively normal individuals together, without distinguishing those with MCI from those who remain cognitively normal. This limits the identification of risk profiles that could support targeted prevention strategies. Moreover, dementia risk is not uniform across populations. Differences by age and sex reflect heterogeneity in biological vulnerability, social exposures, and policy relevance, underscoring the need for stratified analyses.

To address these challenges, our study employed a layered stratification approach. Participants were first categorized into two cognitive groups: cognitively normal and those with MCI. We then further stratified the cognitively normal group by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years) and sex (women vs. men), with the final comparison groups determined in a data-driven manner after assessing model fit. This design acknowledges subgroup heterogeneity and underscores midlife as a critical yet underexamined stage that may serve as an optimal point for early intervention, thereby enhancing policy relevance in identifying those at highest risk.

Most prior studies have focused on static baseline factors, overlooking the role of longitudinal change [33]. However, dementia develops gradually, and changes in psychosocial, functional, and behavioral factors—such as worsening depression or declining social contact—may provide more sensitive signals of risk than one-time measures [33, 35]. Systematically incorporating these temporal dynamics into prediction models is essential for improving early identification of at-risk individuals.

Building on this, predictive modeling in dementia has typically relied on traditional regression approaches [36]. While valuable, such models are limited in handling high-dimensional, multi-wave data and complex, nonlinear interactions. Machine learning (ML) methods offer an alternative: they can process extensive datasets, capture subtle patterns, and provide interpretable outputs that inform prevention strategies [37, 38]. ML has already demonstrated promising predictive accuracy in dementia research [37, 38], and has been increasingly used for in-depth analysis of multidimensional risk factors [39]. However, applications using large-scale, longitudinal Korean data remain scarce.

Prior studies have often been limited by small sample sizes, cross-sectional data, and restricted geographical and cultural contexts [40], constraining the robustness and applicability of findings. More recently, a nationwide analysis using Korean health insurance claims data applied machine learning to predict dementia risk, but such studies were constrained by their reliance on medical records and static baseline indicators, without incorporating psychosocial or longitudinal factors [41]. Moreover, most studies have focused primarily on individuals aged ≥ 65 years, overlooking middle-aged populations, which limits understanding of the full spectrum of dementia risk factors [42]. In contrast, the present study leverages large-scale, nationally representative data from Korea, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the aging population and enhancing generalizability. Korea’s rapid demographic aging—projected to lead OECD nations in the proportion of older adults within the next 50 years [43]—further positions it as a crucial case study. Understanding dementia risk in this context provides valuable insights not only for national strategies but also for addressing global challenges in aging societies. In addition, Korea’s unique social structure—marked by intense educational and occupational competition, strong family obligations, and relatively limited long-term care infrastructure—creates distinct psychosocial stressors that may interact with modifiable health behaviors and social determinants. These factors underscore the need for an in-depth analysis grounded in the Korean context to inform both domestic and international dementia prevention strategies.

The present study leverages six years of data (2014–2020) from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), a nationally representative biennial survey. By incorporating both baseline and change variables across demographic, socioeconomic, health, and psychosocial domains, and by stratifying participants according to baseline cognitive status, age, and sex, we developed ML models to identify multidimensional predictors of dementia. This approach addresses gaps in prior research, provides evidence grounded in Korea’s unique demographic and sociocultural context, and contributes to improving early detection and targeted prevention strategies in rapidly aging societies. Our specific aims were to compare risk profiles between cognitively normal individuals and those with MCI at baseline, to examine heterogeneity by age and sex within the cognitively normal group, and to evaluate whether incorporating longitudinal changes in key variables improves prediction beyond static measures. By applying interpretable ML models to a large-scale, nationally representative survey, we further sought to demonstrate how community-based panel data can be leveraged for dementia risk prediction in the Korean context—yielding evidence directly relevant to national policy and potentially transferable to other rapidly aging societies.

Methods

Data and sample

We analyzed data from the KLoSA, a nationally representative biennial survey of Korean adults aged 45 years and older conducted by the Korea Labor Institute since 2006. The survey employs multi-stage stratified cluster sampling and collects demographic, biological, social, economic, and psychological information [44]. Of the 5,243 individuals who participated in the 2014 baseline survey, 285 participants with dementia at baseline—defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 0–17—were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 4,958 participants (2,815 women and 2,143 men) who formed a balanced panel across all study waves from 2014 to 2020.

Study design and analytic framework

Participants were first divided by baseline cognitive status (cognitively normal vs. mild cognitive impairment), with subgroup comparisons planned separately for sex (women vs. men) and for age group (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years) within each cognitive status group. However, preliminary analyses showed that MCI subgroups had insufficient sample sizes, resulting in model overfitting as indicated by unstable parameter estimates and inflated performance metrics during cross-validation. To ensure reliable interpretation of models and avoid spurious findings, we focused our analyses on three comparisons with adequate sample sizes: (1) cognitively normal vs. MCI (“Normal_All” vs. “MCI_All”), (2) < 65 vs. ≥ 65 years within cognitively normal (“Normal_Under65” vs. “Normal_65Plus”), and (3) women vs. men within cognitively normal (“Normal_Women” vs. “Normal_Men”).

Outcome measures and predictive factors

The primary outcome was cognitive function assessed in 2020 using MMSE scores (range 0–30, higher scores indicating better function). Baseline cognitive status was classified as dementia (0–17), MCI (18–23), or normal cognition (24–30). Although these cut-offs do not adjust for education, education level was included as a covariate in all prediction models to account for its potential influence on cognitive performance.

We examined 26 predictive factors (45 including temporal changes) across four domains: sociodemographic, health status, health behaviors, and contextual factors. Sociodemographic factors included age, sex, education, region, marital status, household size, household income, and economic activity. Health status factors comprised hypertension, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, cancer, self-rated general health, oral health, depression, Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), and quality of life. Health behavior variables included alcohol consumption, smoking, physical activity, and body mass index (BMI). Contextual factors included the frequency of social contact, coverage by National Health Insurance (NHI), and enrollment in Private Health Insurance (PHI).

Most factors were measured at baseline in 2014. Difference factors (suffixed with ‘_Diff’) were calculated as simple subtraction (2018 value − 2014 value) to capture longitudinal changes between 2014 and 2018, except for age, sex, education, region, NHI coverage, and PHI enrollment, which were assumed to be non-changeable or to have limited variability. While this simple subtraction approach provides interpretable change metrics, it assumes linear change and may not fully capture non-linear or complex cognitive decline trajectories, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Oral health was measured only in 2018 because it was not assessed in 2014. All predictors used for 2020 MMSE prediction were measured prior to the outcome assessment. No variables included information on interventions, treatments, or health services occurring after 2018, thereby preventing potential data leakage and ensuring the temporal validity of the predictive models. All factors were recorded based on participant self-report, and categorical variables were numerically coded for modeling. Data analysis

Separate datasets were created for the three final analytical strata. For each dataset, we employed four regression algorithms: linear regression, random forests, XGBoost, and CatBoost. Linear regression served as the baseline model, while random forests, XGBoost, and CatBoost were selected for their efficacy in handling tabular data regression problems [45–48]. These tree-based algorithms utilize ensemble approaches, combining bagging or boosting to improve predictive performance.

Our machine learning approach uses all factors simultaneously to generate individualized predictions, with each model naturally accounting for the complete profile of demographic, health, and behavioral characteristics when predicting cognitive outcomes for each participant. Data were split into training and test sets in an 80:20 ratio, and hyperparameters were optimized using Bayesian optimization with five-fold cross-validation, as detailed in the supplementary materials (eTable 1). Regression performance was evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R²), with lower RMSE and MAE and higher R² indicating better performance.

Factor importance was evaluated using a comprehensive, multi-criteria approach [49] to ensure robust assessment of each factor’s contribution, particularly given the marginal differences in predictive performance across algorithms. This multi-method approach reduces dependence on any single algorithmic bias and provides more reliable factor importance rankings. For each variable, we calculated seven metrics: the Pearson correlation coefficient with MMSE score, model-based feature importance values, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values from each of the three tree-based models [38]. In random forests, feature importance was based on the mean impurity reduction across trees, whereas in XGBoost and CatBoost, it was based on the frequency of a variable’s use in data partitioning. SHAP values represent the average marginal contribution of a variable across all possible coalitions. All importance values were normalized to the maximum observed value within each metric and then averaged to produce a final ranking. In interpreting the results, we focused on variables ranking among the top 15 predictors in each comparison group based on final averaged importance scores (typically ≥ 0.1) as included in the supplementary materials (eTable 2-8).

Results

Predictive performance of machine learning models

Table 2 presents the performance of four machine learning algorithms across six datasets. Performance was comparable among all algorithms, with no single algorithm demonstrating consistent superiority across all datasets. R² values ranged from 0.201 to 0.361, with MCI_All showing the highest predictive accuracy and Normal_All the lowest. Figure 1 displays the relationships between observed and predicted MMSE values for the best-performing algorithm within each group, where green and orange dashed lines demarcate MCI and dementia thresholds, respectively. Predicted values tend to be higher than observed values in the lower MMSE range, reflecting the dataset’s skew toward higher cognitive scores with the predominance of cognitively normal participants in our sample. This pattern indicates higher specificity than sensitivity, meaning that while some individuals with low cognitive scores may be missed, those predicted to have low scores are very likely to actually have cognitive impairment.

Importance of individual factors

Factor importance analysis revealed that, in the comparison between cognitively normal and MCI participants (“Normal_All” vs. “MCI_All”; Fig. 2a), age showed the highest importance in both groups, followed by education, which contributed more in the cognitively normal group. Oral health also ranked among the top contributors in both groups. The cognitively normal group demonstrated higher importance for household income, depression, depression change, social contact, and social contact change, whereas the MCI group showed higher contributions from general health, IADL change, exercise, and quality of life.

Factor importance scores across six datasets

When the cognitively normal group was divided by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years; “Normal_Under65” vs. “Normal_65Plus”; Fig. 2b), education was the most important variable in both age subgroups. Age itself ranked very highly in the ≥ 65 years group, while still showing a moderate level of importance (0.4) in the < 65 years group. Household income and quality of life contributed more in the ≥ 65 years group, while oral health, depression change, and social contact were more prominent in the < 65 years group. Notable variables that ranked highly in specific age groups included hypertension and social contact change in the < 65 years group, and IADL change and general health in the ≥ 65 years group.

In the sex-stratified analysis of the cognitively normal group (women vs. men; “Normal_Women” vs. “Normal_Men”; Fig. 2c), age and education ranked highly in both sexes, with education contributing more in women. Household income was similarly important across sexes. Women showed higher contributions from general health and depression, whereas men showed higher contributions from oral health, depression change, and quality of life.

Across analyses, several variables consistently ranked highly in all three comparisons, including age, education, household income, oral health, depression, and quality of life. Subgroup analyses (Fig. 2b and c) revealed additional variables that were less prominent in the overall cognitively normal vs. MCI comparison (Fig. 2a). These included depression change, social contact change, and hypertension in the < 65 years group; general health and IADL change in the ≥ 65 years group; baseline depression and general health in women; and depression change, oral health, and quality of life in men.

Discussion

Tracking and incorporating multiple risk factors into individual profiles is essential to understand determinants of dementia onset. Whereas most prior studies have examined overall associations among potential causes, our machine learning–based approach, combined with stratified analyses by baseline cognitive status, age, and sex, identified distinct patterns in risk factors and their temporal dynamics, offering practical insights for targeted prevention and intervention.

Our machine learning models achieved moderate predictive performance (R² = 0.201–0.361). While lower than those reported in highly clinical datasets such as Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging (ADNI) [50], direct comparison is constrained by differences in sample characteristics, measurement tools, and study design. Importantly, the community-based nature of our cohort—balanced across multiple survey waves and enriched with demographic, psychosocial, and health-related information—offers complementary evidence to clinical datasets. The relatively high specificity of our models further suggests that predictions of high risk are reliable, a feature particularly useful in public health contexts where minimizing false positives helps allocate limited resources to those most in need. Another strength is cost-effectiveness: our approach relies on routinely collected survey data rather than expensive neuroimaging or biomarker assessments, demonstrating the feasibility of scalable dementia risk prediction in resource-limited settings.

Interpreting these findings through the lens of relatively non-modifiable (e.g., age, sex, education) and relatively modifiable (e.g., socioeconomic, psychosocial, health status, and lifestyle) predictors provides a clearer understanding of potential intervention targets. Age and education consistently emerged as the most influential non-modifiable or partially modifiable predictors across all analyses, reinforcing their foundational role in shaping dementia risk. Age remains the primary factor influencing cognitive functions, showing a direct correlation with cognitive decline [51]. Education, typically determined early in life, contributes substantially to cognitive reserve and reflects broader socioeconomic pathways affecting dementia risk [52]. Educational achievement not only reflects pre-educational factors such as genetic predisposition and parental socioeconomic status, but also shapes adult socioeconomic conditions, which in turn affect dementia risk via multiple environmental pathways, including occupational exposures, lifestyle behaviors, and access to resources [53]. These factors cannot be modified directly but can identify higher-risk subgroups and reinforce the need for life-course–oriented prevention strategies. In contrast, modifiable predictors varied by subgroup, offering actionable entry points for prevention. Oral health, for example, was a significant factor in both cognitively normal and MCI groups, aligning with prior research linking oral status to cognitive outcomes [54, 55]. Despite reliance on self-reported measures, the consistency of this finding suggests that promoting oral health may contribute to cognitive well-being.

Building upon these baseline findings, our stratified analyses provide insights into the differential importance of risk factors across cognitive status, age groups, and sex, with clear implications for targeted interventions. In the cognitively normal group, socioeconomic and psychosocial factors—such as household income, baseline depression, and social contact—were relatively more influential, suggesting that early interventions targeting financial stability, mental health, and social engagement may help delay cognitive decline. In contrast, in the MCI group, health- and function-related factors such as general health, changes in IADL performance, and quality of life were more predictive, indicating that interventions prioritizing management of existing health conditions and functional support may slow progression toward severe cognitive impairment. Additionally, IADL performance emerged as a prominent indicator of cognitive status, particularly in the MCI group. In the Korean context, IADL is both clinically relevant and institutionally significant, serving as a key criterion in the long-term care insurance system. This suggests that monitoring IADL may provide useful information for identifying early functional decline or groups at higher risk of cognitive deterioration. However, given the potential for reverse causality—where declines in IADL may reflect early cognitive impairment rather than a direct causal factor—these implications should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to determine whether supporting IADL can meaningfully slow cognitive decline.

Age-stratified analyses highlighted distinct stage-specific risk profiles. In adults under 65, oral health, depression change, social contact, and hypertension emerged as particularly influential, underscoring the importance of psychosocial and lifestyle factors in midlife. These findings suggest that preventive programs in midlife should prioritize mental health support, oral health promotion, and opportunities for sustained social engagement. In contrast, adults aged 65 and older showed higher contributions from cumulative and structural factors, including age itself, education, household income, quality of life, IADL change, and general health. This pattern indicates that late-life interventions should focus on maintaining daily functional ability, stabilizing socioeconomic resources, and improving overall health-related quality of life.

Sex-stratified analyses demonstrated notable differences in the relative importance of modifiable factors. For men, oral health, changes in depression, and quality of life were particularly influential, suggesting the value of interventions that integrate oral care, mental health monitoring, and support for maintaining well-being after retirement. In contrast, for women, general health and depression emerged as more critical, underscoring the need for comprehensive health management and targeted mental health support. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring dementia prevention strategies to sex-specific risk profiles.

The temporal analysis of these risk factors reveals distinct patterns that necessitate differentiated policy approaches. Non-modifiable demographic factors such as age and sex, along with partially modifiable socioeconomic indicators, exhibited stable effects over time, underscoring the importance of long-term structural policies focused on early risk identification and support for vulnerable populations. In contrast, actively modifiable factors showed meaningful temporal changes, highlighting the need for more dynamic and responsive interventions. Specifically, changes in depression significantly influenced cognitive transitions in the normal group, particularly among men under 65. This finding is consistent with a previous study [56], which demonstrated that greater changes in depressive symptoms were significantly associated with accelerated memory decline over a 16-year follow-up. Together, these results underscore depression change as a dynamic and clinically meaningful risk factor, though its interpretation should remain cautious given potential overlap with cognitive measures. Similarly, reductions in social activity were key predictors of cognitive decline in cognitively normal adults under 65, emphasizing the preventive value of maintaining social engagement in midlife. Meanwhile, changes in IADL performance were particularly predictive in the MCI group, demonstrating that functional decline directly contributes to further cognitive deterioration. These findings suggest a dual policy approach: implementing systemic support to address stable risk factors such as age, sex, and education, while simultaneously deploying flexible intervention programs targeting dynamic factors like depression, social engagement, and functional ability. Such integrated strategies may enhance the effectiveness of dementia prevention efforts by aligning interventions with the temporal and population-specific nuances of risk factors.

The strengths of this study include the use of advanced ML techniques, stratification by cognitive status, age, and sex, and incorporation of longitudinal data, which allowed for a nuanced understanding of dementia risk factors and their temporal dynamics. However, several limitations should be noted. First, further stratification of the MCI population (e.g., by age or sex) was not feasible due to smaller sample sizes, which may have obscured subgroup-specific patterns. Second, although education consistently emerged as a key predictor, it was not prioritized for stratified analyses, limiting insights into subgroup differences. Third, some predictors, including IADL, depression, and oral health, were assessed via self-report and may be subject to recall bias. Moreover, the MMSE has limited sensitivity for detecting mild cognitive impairment, potentially underestimating cognitive decline. These factors highlight that our findings indicate associations rather than definitive causal effects. Fourth, the six-year follow-up may not capture the full trajectory of cognitive decline or late-stage dementia conversion, though prior studies suggest that meaningful deterioration can occur within this timeframe [57]. Finally, genetic risk factors, family history, and detailed medication use (e.g., antihypertensives) were unavailable, and cultural context may limit generalizability to non–East Asian populations, such as societies with different gender roles and family care structures. Future research incorporating objective measures, interventional designs, longer follow-up, and additional biological and pharmacological data is warranted.

These findings have relevance in the global context of aging populations. Korea’s rapid demographic aging makes it an important case study for understanding dementia risk factors in developed societies. The identification of different categories of risk factors, along with their sex-specific and cognitive status-specific patterns, provides valuable insights for developing targeted prevention strategies in aging populations worldwide. This comprehensive approach contributes to a deeper understanding of dementia risk, with implications for improving clinical practice and public health policies in dementia prevention and management.

Conclusion

This study identified key predictors of cognitive decline using machine learning on a nationally representative longitudinal dataset. Age and education emerged as the most influential non-modifiable factors, while oral health and IADL performance were significant modifiable factors across all groups. Stratified analyses revealed subgroup-specific patterns: in cognitively normal adults, socioeconomic and psychosocial factors such as household income, depression, and social contact were particularly important, whereas in the MCI group, health and functional measures were more predictive. Age and sex differences highlighted that adults < 65 were more affected by behavioral and psychosocial factors, women by depression and oral health, and men by socioeconomic status and social engagement. These findings support a dual prevention strategy: long-term structural support for non-modifiable factors, combined with targeted interventions addressing modifiable factors, including mental health, functional maintenance, oral health, and social engagement. Such stratified and tailored approaches can help mitigate the growing burden of dementia in aging populations.
