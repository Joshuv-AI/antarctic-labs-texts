Eroding the foundation: health and social needs of adult SNAP participants in an era of policy change
Authors: Kevin H Nguyen, Cristina M Gago, Stephanie Ettinger de Cuba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2025, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) was at the center of policy debate. SNAP-specific provisions within the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” (OBBBA) will affect about 42 million low-income participants. To ground policy proposals and national discourse in evidence, we (1) summarize the characteristics of adult SNAP participants and then (2) describe how recent legislation and proposals may affect them. Using data from 34 states and the District of Columbia, approximately 1 in 10 adults in our sample reported SNAP participation in the last 12 months. Compared to non-participants, adult SNAP participants were significantly more likely to report fair/poor health status, inability to see a doctor because of cost, and health-related social needs (eg, food insecurity, inability to pay bills, lack of reliable transportation), conflicting with some public perceptions of participant characteristics. Provisions within OBBBA will impose financial burdens on states and create barriers to eligibility (eg, excluding previously eligible populations) and enrollment/recertification (eg, adding administrative burdens) for participants. Considering adult SNAP participants face concurrent material hardship, changes to the program may exacerbate existing disparities in health and economic stability of low-income households nationwide on SNAP benefits to assist in meeting basic needs.

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) is the nation's largest anti-hunger program, providing an average of $187 per person in monthly food benefits to 42 million low-income people.1 SNAP is unique in its benefit calculation among federal programs, using household income and living expenses—like housing, utility, and health care costs—to determine eligibility. SNAP has become increasingly politicized: while studies show that SNAP is essential for ensuring that low-income households have adequate access to food, some perceive it as an unnecessary government expense. Negative public perceptions around SNAP participants are often rooted in misinformation and racial stereotyping.2 To ground national policy proposals in evidence, in this commentary we (1) use 2024-2025 data (Appendix) to summarize the characteristics of adult SNAP participants and (2) describe how recent legislation may affect them.

Among 226 289 respondents in 34 states and DC, 10.9% of adults reported SNAP participation in the last 12 months (Table 1). Notably, underreporting of SNAP participation may bias any estimates reported.3 Among adult SNAP participants: 47.7% had at least 1 child in the household; 41.5% were non-Hispanic White; 39% had a household income of 250% federal poverty level (FPL) or lower ($66 636 for a 3-person household); 33.2% were employed; 25.7% reported being unable to work; 24% had Medicare; and 48% had at least 1 chronic condition. Compared to non-participants, SNAP participants were more likely to report inability to afford to see a doctor, losing employment in the last year, insufficient food, and inability to pay utilities.

Comparison to public perceptions

Contrary to some public perceptions,2 most adult SNAP participants are non-Hispanic White. Further, most SNAP participants are employed, retired, or unable to work. There is concerningly low SNAP participation among 3 groups disproportionately affected by food insecurity: older adults and Hispanic and Black adults.4 Older adults are challenged by fixed incomes amid rising housing, food, and health care costs. Concurrently, they often face greater health care needs, which may create barriers to applying, recertification, and utilizing benefits. Hispanic and Black adults are particularly affected in the context of persistent discriminatory community disinvestment leading to fewer employment opportunities, low food availability, and the toll of experiences of discrimination. SNAP participants have substantially higher burdens of chronic disease, likely driving the 6-fold difference in reporting inability to work between participants and non-participants. The disproportionate share of SNAP participants reporting “unable to work” likely reflects the connection between disability status and SNAP eligibility. Similarly, higher prevalences of health-related social needs (HRSNs) among SNAP participants likely reflects higher susceptibility to financial precarity and actively seeking relief from food insecurity through SNAP participation, and not that SNAP participation fails to impact food insecurity. Rather, SNAP benefits provide financial relief for people who are struggling with food insecurity, unemployment, and inability to pay bills.5

Recent government shutdown and SNAP

Accessing and retaining SNAP benefits can be challenging. SNAP under-enrollment is well-documented and is primarily attributed to administrative burden.6 Most households therefore apply during times of severe financial constraint (eg, due to low or unstable income).5,7 Benefit coverage loss can have catastrophic impacts on low-income households,8 as realized nationally through the 2025 federal government shutdown, when SNAP benefit disbursement was universally paused over 2 weeks for the first time in its 60-year history. Participants reported the lapse in coverage led to anxiety, inability to purchase groceries, and weighing whether to forgo meals and other necessities (eg, utilities, medical care). Unlike other expenses, one cannot retroactively apply SNAP benefits to address household food needs; the need to eat is daily, immediate, and non-negotiable. Given the well-documented benefits of SNAP participation (eg, fewer hospitalizations, emergency room visits), enrollment gaps contribute to elevated economic hardship and health risk. Without SNAP benefits to offset costs for other necessities, gaps in HRSNs may widen.

Effects of the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act”

The crisis experienced during the federal government shutdown previews possible effects of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) if the bills' promise to reduce SNAP funding by 20% is realized over the next decade. OBBBA will (1) restrict SNAP eligibility criteria (eg, excluding lawfully present immigrants from receiving benefits), (2) increase administrative burden associated with enrollment and recertification (eg, work reporting requirements), (3) reduce benefit amounts (eg, alter how utility deductions are calculated), and (4) reduce purchasing power of benefits (eg, freeze benefit increase beyond inflation). Previous implementation of SNAP work reporting requirements in Arkansas caused confusion and did not improve levels of employment. The cumulative effect of these changes to SNAP may lead to reduced participation, increased food insecurity, increased financial instability, and negative health outcomes for low-income households that rely on the program to assist in meeting basic needs.5,7,8

Food needs do not exist in isolation from other household basic needs like health care. Thus, SNAP cuts and changes combined with health care cuts may have catastrophic impacts for low-income households who already face substantial material hardship. OBBBA is projected to cut Medicaid funding by nearly $1 billion and increase the number of uninsured people by 10 million over the next decade. OBBBA (1) introduces additional work reporting requirements, (2) reduces the recertification window from 12 to 6 months (eg, increasing paperwork burden and opportunities to be cut off), (3) allows states to impose additional cost-sharing for Medicaid patients, (4) limits state provider taxes, and (5) restricts eligibility among lawfully present immigrants. Many may lose Medicaid and SNAP benefits simultaneously—impacting the ability of millions of US households to afford basic needs.

Intersections with other safety net programs

Beyond loss of Medicaid or SNAP benefits alone, loss of either program can make it harder to identify and enroll in other federal assistance programs for which many are adjunctively (or categorically) eligible. Medicaid and SNAP enrollment can be used to directly certify children for school meals and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children; and households for utility discounts (ie, reduced rates for electricity and gas). Adjunctive eligibility reduces burdensome, duplicative, and stigmatizing application procedures across multiple programs. When households are automatically certified for multiple programs, it increases the likelihood of participation. Losing eligibility for one program can result in subsequent underutilization across multiple benefit programs, driving up multiple household expenses. To reduce disenrollment, state agencies may consider extending recertification windows and simplifying recertification procedures to the maximum allowed under federal law.

Further, the Department of Homeland Security proposed in November 2025 to rescind the 2022 Public Charge rule. This rule is applicable when immigrants are applying for permanent residency and historically has excluded SNAP benefits from consideration of whether an immigrant may unduly rely on state resources. The proposed revision expands the definition to include broader means-tested public benefits, likely causing a “chilling effect” out of fear of negative consequences and impacting health outcomes through forgone nutrition assistance and medical care, worse mental health, and increased food insecurity for immigrant households.8,9

In contrast to some characterizations of the SNAP program, our work illustrates that most SNAP participants are employed, unable to work, retired, and face concurrent material hardships. Provisions within recent policies like OBBBA (such as work requirements) may lead to confusion, administrative burden, reduced SNAP participation, economic instability, and important tradeoffs about whether to forego food and other daily necessities for low-income households that rely on the program.8
