Assessment of Enterococcus faecalis biofilm response to amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin using a dynamic in vitro pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic model of catheter-associated urinary tract infection
Authors: Desiye Tesfaye Tegegne, Paweł Jajor, Jacek Bania, Paweł Migdał, Robert Dziedzic, Błażej Poźniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) are among the most common types of healthcare-associated infections. Among uropathogens, Enterococcus faecalis is particularly notable for its strong capacity to form biofilms on urinary catheters, thereby decreasing its susceptibility to antibiotics commonly used to treat CAUTIs. However, current antibiotic dosing strategies still rely on planktonic phenotypes, limiting their applicability to biofilm-associated infections. To address this limitation, a dynamic in vitro pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic model was developed to assess E. faecalis biofilms’ response to different treatment regimens.MethodsBiofilms of model E. faecalis strains (ATCC 29212 and 54) were preformed on urinary catheters. Mature biofilms were subjected to simulated urinary dosing regimens of amoxicillin (500 mg q12h for 3 days) or ciprofloxacin (500 mg q24h for 3 days), as well as to constant antibiotic concentrations for 3 days. Viable bacterial counts and biofilm biomass were then measured at predefined timepoints, and the effects of the simulated dosing regimens were compared to those observed under constant antibiotic exposure.ResultsOur findings indicate that daily ciprofloxacin demonstrated the greatest antibacterial activity, significantly surpassing the effects of constant ciprofloxacin exposure, twice-daily dosing of amoxicillin, and constant amoxicillin exposure. Although the simulated amoxicillin regimen was more effective than constant amoxicillin exposure, both achieved only moderate reductions. Notably, constant amoxicillin exposure induced biofilm formation. Ciprofloxacin exhibited markedly greater antibiofilm activity than amoxicillin against both E. faecalis strains. Among the evaluated regimens, CIP 500 mg q24h produced the most pronounced reduction in biofilm bacterial burden, achieving decreases exceeding 3 log10 cfu/cm2 in both strains after 72 h of exposure.ConclusionsThe findings show that antibiotic efficacy against E. faecalis biofilms depends on the antibiotic and its dosing regimen, with fluctuating ciprofloxacin exposure producing the strongest bactericidal effect and biofilm reduction.

Introduction

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) are one of the most common healthcare-associated infections and are linked to increased morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients.1,2 Among the major uropathogens, Enterococcus faecalis is a predominant species associated with CAUTIs due to its strong ability to form biofilms on urinary catheters, which reduces its susceptibility to antimicrobial agents used in the treatment of CAUTIs. As a result, conventional antibiotic therapies have limitations in effectively managing biofilm-associated infections.3,4 Despite the reduced susceptibility of biofilms, these infections are still often treated with standard antibiotic regimens in clinical practice.5,6

Optimal management of CAUTIs requires the use of antibiotics with pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic (PK–PD) properties that ensure sufficiently high urinary concentrations following oral administration.7 Previous studies have shown that therapeutic efficacy in urinary tract infections (UTIs) is directly influenced by the drug concentration in the urine.8 Accordingly, antibiotics, such as amoxicillin, which exhibits time-dependent killing, and ciprofloxacin, which has concentration-dependent activity, are frequently prescribed for UTIs due to their favourable PK profiles and high urinary concentrations.7,9–13 Conversely, exposure to sub-therapeutic antibiotic levels may act as a signalling cue for bacteria potentially promoting biofilm formation.14,15 Time-dependent antibiotics exhibit maximal bacterial killing when the drug concentration exceeds the MIC for a significant fraction of the dosing interval, which can be particularly achieved with constant rate infusion. Although ciprofloxacin exhibits concentration-dependent bactericidal kinetics, where Cmax/MIC drives rapid initial bacterial eradication, the AUC/MIC remains the dominant and more widely accepted PK–PD index predictive of overall clinical efficacy and microbiological success.16,17 To the best of our knowledge, there are still limitations regarding whether these PD principles can be applied to biofilm-associated bacterial populations. In the present study, we investigated whether these two contrasting antibiotic dosing strategies, simulated urinary PK profiles reflecting common dosing (maximizing fluctuations) versus constant concentration (minimizing fluctuations), contribute to the different responses of the E. faecalis biofilms. Elucidating these differences may inform the design of improved preclinical dosing strategies, even for established and widely utilized antimicrobial agents.

Numerous in vitro models have been developed to investigate antimicrobial activity against biofilms.16,17 Although a few are sophisticated and account for numerous physiological aspects,18 the more common, simpler models often neglect the dynamic fluctuations in urinary drug concentrations and fail to incorporate variations in fluid flow or PK parameters, such as those observed in 96-well plate culture and crystal violet (CV) staining assays.19–21 As a result, such exposure does not reflect the clinical situation, where bacteria are exposed to changing antibiotic concentrations between dosing intervals.22 Moreover, in many previous models, catheters are positioned horizontally, promoting bacterial incorporation into the biofilm via gravity.23 This may contribute to the fact that some biofilm-embedded bacteria under study do not actually have a biofilm phenotype.24–26 To overcome this limitation, we employed a dynamic in vitro PK–PD model with a vertical catheter position that allows only bacteria exhibiting a true biofilm phenotype to attach to the catheter surface (the same concept as applied in the Calgary Biofilm Device).27

Therefore, the objectives of this study were (i) to simulate urinary PK profiles of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin in humans following oral administration using a dynamic in vitro PK–PD model and (ii) to compare the response of E. faecalis biofilms to simulated profiles (dynamically fluctuating concentrations) versus constant antibiotic concentrations.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, culture media and reagents

Two model E. faecalis strains were used: E. faecalis ATCC 29212 (originally isolated from UTI, obtained from Microbiologics, St. Cloud, MN, USA) and E. faecalis strain 54, obtained from the Department of Food Hygiene and Consumer Health Protection, Wrocław University of Environmental and Life Sciences, Poland (a clinical strain originally provided by Prof. A. Toledo-Arana, Instituto de Agrobiotecnología y Recursos Naturales, Universidad Pública de Navarra, Pamplona, Spain). The biofilm-forming potential of E. faecalis strain 54 has been well characterized in previous studies.28,29

Tryptone soya broth (TSB) and CAMHB (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) were used for bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), respectively. To better mimic the physiological conditions of the human urinary tract, artificial urine medium (AUM) was used. Two formulations, AUM.1 and AUM.2, were initially chosen based on previous studies [recipes are provided in Tables S1 and S2 (available as data at JAC Online)].30–32 Bacterial growth dynamics in AUMs were subsequently compared with growth in TSB (Supplementary Method S1). Preliminary experiments showed that AUM.2 better supported E. faecalis growth than AUM.1 (Figure S1). Consequently, AUM.2 was selected as the medium for all subsequent experiments. To support clinically relevant biofilm formation, 0.1% glucose (w/v)33 was added to AUM.2. Such concentration may be observed in diabetic patients, a subpopulation particularly susceptible to UTIs.34,35

Amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), formic acid (Chem-Lab NV, Zedelgem, Belgium), acetonitrile (Supelco, Darmstadt, Germany) and Milli-Q water (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) were used, and all reagents were of HPLC/MS analytical grade.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The MIC and MBC of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin against E. faecalis strains were determined using the CLSI broth microdilution method.36 Additionally, static time–kill assays were performed following a previously described method with minor modifications.37 (Supplementary Method S2).

Dynamic in vitro PK–PD model

This model was developed based on a previously published model,18,38 to investigate catheter-associated biofilms under physiologically relevant conditions (Figure 1), with detailed descriptions provided in Supplementary Method S3. It integrates a biofilm growth platform (urinary catheter) with a flow system, creating a dynamic in vitro PK–PD model that simulates urinary PK profiles of amoxicillin39 and ciprofloxacin9,40 according to first-order elimination kinetics. The simulated PK profiles were achieved via a continuous dilution–elimination process using two peristaltic pumps (Lead Fluid Technology, China).

Dynamic in vitro PK–PD model. The medium flow is driven by two programmable peristaltic pumps. The assembled model was placed in an incubator at 37°C, and the antibiotic filled reservoir is located on a magnetic stir plate to ensure adequate mixing of the medium and antibiotics before being pumped into the biofilm chamber.

To facilitate the formation of mature biofilms prior to antimicrobial exposure, a 36 h conditioning period was implemented. Following inoculation with a bacterial suspension, catheter segments were maintained vertically under static conditions for 12 h to allow initial bacterial adhesion, followed by a 24 h dynamic phase with continuous perfusion of AUM.2 at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, which is slightly below the physiological range and may be expected in many patients subjected to catheterization.18,41 Following the establishment of mature E. faecalis biofilms, the biofilms were exposed for 3 days either to fluctuating antibiotic concentrations, simulating urinary drug PK profiles, or to constant antibiotic concentrations. The constant concentrations were selected to provide equivalent total exposure (AUC0–24) to the simulated profiles, corresponding to 214 mg/L for amoxicillin and 74 mg/L for ciprofloxacin.

Simulated antibiotic regimens and targeted PK parameters

Targeted human urinary PK parameters were generated to reproduce the changes in antibiotic concentrations over time typically observed in human urine following standard dosing regimens, based on PK data reported in previous studies.9,39,40 The simulated urinary PK profiles corresponded to regimens of amoxicillin (500 mg q12h for 3 days) and ciprofloxacin (500 mg q24h for 3 days). The dosing regimens were adapted from established human PK data and implemented to generate representative ‘real-life’ PK profiles. To achieve the defined maximal urinary antibiotic exposure, the absorption phase of each drug was not explicitly modelled. Instead, urinary concentrations were simulated under the assumption of direct drug delivery to the urinary tract. Simulations assumed that the concentrations represented the protein-unbound fraction in urine, as is typically observed in patients without significant proteinuria. For amoxicillin, the targeted urinary PK parameters included a Cmax of 1000 mg/L, a t1/2 of 1.8 h, a Cmin of 9.85 mg/L and an AUC0–24 of 5144 mg × h/L,39 whereas for ciprofloxacin, the PK parameters used in the simulations included a Cmax of 360 mg/L, a t1/2 of 3.45 h, a Cmin of 2.94 mg/L and an AUC0–24 of 1785mg × h/L.9,40 The AUC was calculated by integration of the resulting simulated PK profiles.

In parallel to the experiments on simulated urinary profiles of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin, separate experiments were carried out to assess the biofilm response upon exposure to constant concentrations of these drugs. In order to achieve identical total exposure as with simulated profiles, the constant concentration was fixed (no dilution process was applied; instead, the culture medium was prepared directly with the fixed final drug concentration) at a value that provided exactly the same AUC0–24 values as with urinary PK simulations: 214 mg/L for amoxicillin and 74 mg/L for ciprofloxacin. Therefore, E. faecalis biofilms in urinary catheters were exposed to either changing or constant antibiotic concentrations to compare their effects on viability of biofilm-embedded cells and biofilm biomass.

Validation of simulated PK profiles

To verify the attainment of the target PK profiles, medium samples were collected from the injection port immediately upstream of the catheter segments at predefined timepoints. For amoxicillin, samples were obtained at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 h, whereas for ciprofloxacin, sampling occurred at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h on each day of antibiotic administration. All samples were stored at −80°C until analysis using HPLC with UV detection (Supplementary Method S4). On each day of antibiotic administration, the following PK parameters were determined: Cmax, t1/2, Cmin and AUC0–24 (calculated using the logarithmic trapezoidal method). Agreement between measured and target antibiotic concentrations was evaluated using simple linear regression and Bland–Altman analysis. In the Bland–Altman analysis, percentage bias was calculated as % difference = [(measured − targeted)/average] × 100 and plotted against the mean of the measured and targeted concentrations for each antibiotic.42

PD analysis

At 24, 48 and 72 h of antibiotic exposure, the viable bacterial counts (log10 cfu/cm2) were quantified by culture-based enumeration, and biofilm biomass was measured by CV staining (OD570 nm), as described in Supplementary Method S5. The change in viable bacterial counts over the 72 h treatment period (Δlog10 cfu/cm2) for each dosing regimen was calculated as Δlog10 cfu/cm2 = log10 cfu/cm2 at 72 h − log10 cfu/cm2 at 0 h. Negative Δlog10(cfu/cm2) values indicated a reduction in viable bacterial counts during antimicrobial exposure. An absolute reduction of ≥3 log10 cfu/cm2 was defined as bactericidal activity, whereas reductions between 0 and <3 log10 cfu/cm2 were interpreted as bacteriostatic activity. No change in cfu counts was considered indicative of the absence of antibacterial effect.43,44 In addition, bactericidal activity was evaluated by calculating the area under the bacterial kill curve (AUBC) at 24 h intervals and across the entire 0–72 h period for the different dosing regimens. Lower AUBC values correspond to greater bacterial killing, whereas higher AUBC values reflect limited antimicrobial activity.

Scanning electron microscopy imaging of biofilms

Results of quantitative microbiological tests were confirmed by visualization of biofilms using scanning electron microscopy. After 72 h of dynamic flow exposure to either constant antimicrobial concentration or simulated dosage regimens (see PK–PD conditions), the biofilm samples in catheter sections were fixed using 4 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Merck), rinsed three times with 0.1 M cacodylate solution (Merck) and subjected to a graded alcohol series. The fixed samples were coated with a layer of carbon using an EM ACE600 sputter (Leica Microsystems, Germany) and examined using a scanning electron microscope, Zeiss EVO MA25 (Oberkochen, Germany).

Resistance studies

The development of antimicrobial resistance was assessed over a 3-day antibiotic exposure. To minimize antibiotic carryover, each sample was washed and then resuspended in sterile saline. Undiluted bacterial suspensions were spread onto tryptone soya agar plates supplemented with amoxicillin or ciprofloxacin at concentrations corresponding to 1×, 2× and 4× the MIC, at 24, 48 and 72 h post-antibiotic exposure. The inoculated plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The presence of bacterial colonies was interpreted as indicative of resistance to the respective antibiotic, whereas the absence of growth was considered to reflect maintained susceptibility.45

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. To compare the effects of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin within the model, an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test was employed, with a P value of <0.05 considered statistically significant. For both E. faecalis strains, log-transformed cfu/cm2, biofilm biomass and AUBC values were compared between amoxicillin (500 mg q12h) and a constant concentration of 214 mg/L, as well as between ciprofloxacin (500 mg q24h) and a constant concentration of 74 mg/L at 24, 48 and 72 h. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Susceptibility testing

Both E. faecalis ATCC 29212 and 54 exhibited higher MIC and MBC values for amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin in AUM.2 compared with CAMHB (Table S3). Despite these differences, both strains were susceptible to both antibiotics. Static time–kill curves for each strain are shown in Figures S2 and S3.

PK parameters

The targeted PK parameters and corresponding model-derived data are summarized in Table S4. For amoxicillin, the mean measured Cmax was 950.9 ± 32.2 mg/L, while the corresponding Cmin was 16.37 ± 5.20 mg/L. For ciprofloxacin, the mean measured Cmax and Cmin were 343.5 ± 26.4 mg/L and 5.14 ± 0.77 mg/L, respectively. The concentration–time profiles of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin were accurately reproduced in the model for all dosing regimens tested, as shown in Figure 2(a and d). Figure 2(b and e) indicates a strong linear relationship between the targeted and measured antibiotic concentrations for amoxicillin (slope = 0.929; R2 = 0.99) and for ciprofloxacin (slope = 0.965; R2 = 0.98). Overall, the measured concentrations of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin were closely aligned with their respective target values.

Diagnostic plots for simulated PK profiles of amoxicillin (left, a, b, c) and ciprofloxacin (right, d, e, f). Panels a and d depict the simulated PK profiles versus measured concentrations; panels b and e illustrate the relationship between measured and targeted concentrations; and panels c and f display Bland–Altman plots evaluating the agreement between measured and targeted concentrations for amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin, respectively. Limits of agreement (LoA) define the range within which 95% of the differences between simulated and measured antibiotic concentrations are anticipated to fall.

Effects of simulated profiles and constant antibiotic concentrations on E. faecalis biofilms

The effects of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin on biofilms of both E. faecalis strains over 72 h are shown in Figure 3. Prior to antibiotic exposure, bacterial counts increased similarly across all regimens, reaching ∼7 log10 cfu/cm2 at treatment onset. During antibiotic exposure, biofilm responses varied depending on the antibiotic and dosing regimen. For amoxicillin, both the simulated 500 mg q12h regimen and a constant 214 mg/L concentration produced moderate reductions in bacterial counts over 72 h, with slightly greater reductions observed for the simulated regimen by the end of treatment. Significant differences between the simulated and constant exposures were noted at 48 and 72 h for both strains (P < 0.05), whereas at 24 h, significance was observed only for ATCC 29212 (P < 0.05) (Table S5). In the case of ciprofloxacin, the simulated 500 mg q24h regimen exhibited pronounced bactericidal activity, yielding significantly greater reductions at 48 and 72 h for both strains (P < 0.05). By contrast, the constant 74 mg/L concentration resulted in minimal bacterial reduction. At 24 h, the effects of the two ciprofloxacin regimens differed significantly against strain 54 (P < 0.05) but not against ATCC 29212 (P > 0.05) (Table S5). Moreover, AUBC was calculated to assess the bactericidal activity of both strains. The simulated CIP regimen produced the lowest AUBC values and achieved the greatest reduction in bacterial counts (>3 log10 cfu/cm2) compared with all other treatments (Figure S4; Table S6).

Effect of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin dosing regimens on biofilm-embedded viable cell counts of E. faecalis ATCC 29212 (a) and strain 54 (b) and reduction in log10 cfu/cm2 (0–72 h) (c) for both strains under different regimens.

The effects of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin on the biofilm biomass over 72 h are presented in Figure 4. Constant amoxicillin exposure promoted biofilm formation, surpassing that observed in the growth control. In contrast, the simulated amoxicillin regimen resulted in a reduction in biofilm biomass relative to the constant concentration. The simulated ciprofloxacin regimen produced the greatest effect, progressively reducing biofilm biomass throughout the experiment (Table S5). Overall, these findings indicate that the simulated ciprofloxacin regimen was significantly more effective at reducing biofilm biomass than the constant ciprofloxacin concentration and both amoxicillin regimens.

Effect of different amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin dosing regimens on biofilm biomass density of E. faecalis strains ATCC 29212 (a) and 54 (b) in the dynamic in vitro PK–PD model.

The SEM images revealed that only the simulated CIP dosage regimen resulted in a clearly visible reduction in the number of attached bacteria. The extracellular matrix was scarce in both the control and antimicrobial-exposed biofilms. However, constant exposure to CIP appeared to be associated with the presence of small amounts of thin extracellular matrix filaments in the biofilm formed by strain 54 (Figure 5).

Scanning electron microscopy images of biofilms formed on urinary catheters following incubation in a dynamic in vitro PK–PD model under drug-free conditions and after exposure to different amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin dosing regimens.

Susceptibility testing

E. faecalis ATCC 29212 exhibited no development of resistance to either amoxicillin or ciprofloxacin, whereas E. faecalis strain 54 remained susceptible to amoxicillin but showed an increase in ciprofloxacin MIC from 0.5 to 1 mg/L after 72 h exposure.

Discussion

Most previous PK–PD studies have focused on planktonic bacteria, and extrapolating these findings to biofilms is challenging due to substantial differences in physiology and antibiotic susceptibility.46,47 In planktonic bacteria, time-dependent antibiotics, such as amoxicillin, achieve maximal efficacy when their concentrations remain above the MIC for a sufficient portion of the dosing interval (T > MIC). In contrast, concentration-dependent antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin, exert optimal bactericidal activity primarily through high peak concentrations.48–51 To test whether these clinically relevant principles apply to biofilms, we employed a dynamic in vitro PK–PD model to replicate in vivo antibiotic exposure patterns in E. faecalis biofilms grown on catheter sections. The model successfully replicates urinary hydrodynamics and PK profiles, providing a clinically relevant platform to study urinary catheter-associated E. faecalis biofilms and allowing the measurement of both viable bacterial counts and total biofilm biomass.

AUM was selected for this model because its formulation has been validated for in vitro bladder models using key CAUTI pathogens.30,52–54 Both E. faecalis ATCC 29212 and 54 exhibited higher MIC and MBC values for amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin in AUM.2 compared with CAMHB. This slight increase in MIC may be attributed to experimental variability or physicochemical differences between the culture media. However, both strains remained susceptible to both antibiotics. Although MIC values increased by one doubling dilution, the magnitude of these changes was small. Therefore, the observed increase can be considered a slight shift in susceptibility, and its clinical significance remains uncertain. A one-dilution increase in MIC alone is unlikely to indicate a clinically meaningful change in antimicrobial sensitivity or to affect the treatment outcome. Also, these findings likely reflect the physicochemical differences between the culture media, such as pH, ionic composition and nutrient availability in AUM.2, factors that can modulate bacterial physiology and antimicrobial activity, thereby potentially altering susceptibility.55–58 Consequently, standard AST in CAMHB may not fully predict antibiotic efficacy in UTIs, highlighting the value of conditions that better reflect the urinary environment. However, AST methods are strictly regulated to ensure reproducibility, inter-laboratory comparability and clinical interpretability. Thus, the approach used in this study is intended for research applications as a complementary tool, providing additional pharmacologically relevant insights under condition-specific models, rather than replacing standard clinical AST.

Our results demonstrated that daily ciprofloxacin (average Cmax 343.5 mg/L) demonstrated the greatest antibacterial activity, significantly surpassing the effects of constant ciprofloxacin exposure (74 mg/L), twice-daily dosing of amoxicillin (average Cmax 950.9 mg/L) and constant amoxicillin exposure (214 mg/L). The enhanced bactericidal activity of ciprofloxacin under clinically relevant conditions reflects its concentration-dependent killing and post-antibiotic effect, whereby the attainment of high peak concentrations is critical for the eradication of biofilm-embedded bacteria.59,60 Unlike constant exposure, the simulated urinary PK profile produced repeated high peaks followed by declines, thereby maximizing the PK–PD drivers of ciprofloxacin efficacy against biofilm-associated bacteria. These findings are consistent with previous in vitro studies showing that intermittent high-dose ciprofloxacin improves biofilm penetration and bacterial killing compared with constant exposure.18,61,62 These results also emphasize that ciprofloxacin requires high peak concentrations for maximal effect63–65 and that constant ciprofloxacin levels may not meet these PK–PD targets against biofilm-associated E. faecalis. The superior performance of the simulated ciprofloxacin regimen compared with a constant concentration strategy might not have been revealed without reproducing dynamic urinary PK. In contrast, β-lactam antibiotics, such as amoxicillin, often show limited efficacy against biofilm-embedded bacteria. Their hydrophilicity, relatively large molecular size, restricted diffusion through the dense extracellular polymeric matrix and reduced activity against slow-growing bacterial populations restrict their effectiveness within biofilms. In contrast, ciprofloxacin demonstrates superior penetration and retention in biofilms owing to its amphipathic (zwitterionic) character, greater lipophilicity and capacity for intracellular accumulation.66–69 By targeting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, which remain active in non-dividing cells, ciprofloxacin retains bactericidal activity against slow-growing bacterial subpopulations.70 For example, ciprofloxacin exhibits substantial in vitro activity against Staphylococcus aureus biofilms when administered at very high concentrations. This efficacy is thought to result from its relatively low dependence on bacterial metabolic activity, effective penetration into the biofilm matrix and reduced susceptibility to the inhibitory effects of acidic pH and high bacterial inocula.71,72 Nevertheless, the clinical applicability of ciprofloxacin for biofilm eradication is limited by the exceptionally high concentrations required to achieve these effects in vitro.71 These observations highlight the need for rational antibiotic selection and optimized dosing regimens to improve the management of biofilm-associated infections, including CAUTIs. Collectively, these PK and PD advantages account for the greater efficacy of ciprofloxacin compared with amoxicillin in biofilm settings and indicate the importance of rational antibiotic selection and optimized dosing strategies during preclinical dose optimization for biofilm-associated infections.

In the second approach, E. faecalis biofilms were exposed to constant concentrations of the two antibiotics. The concentrations were selected in order to achieve the same total exposure of the biofilms (i.e. AUC) as in the simulated dosing regimens. The constant concentration of the drug does not produce a steep gradient (i.e. fluctuations with high point Cmax/MIC), but it maximizes the duration of time for which antibiotic concentration remains moderately high above the MIC.73 Despite the theoretical advantage of constant concentrations (maximized T > MIC), the simulated 12 h amoxicillin dosage regimen performed better against biofilm-embedded cells, suggesting that PK–PD targets based on planktonic bacteria may not fully apply to biofilms and showing the need for alternative dosing strategies in biofilm-associated infections. The relative superiority of the simulated regimen indicates that fluctuating concentrations may partially overcome biofilm-associated protective mechanisms by intermittently exceeding effective bactericidal levels.74 However, according to the study by Bin et al. (2025), oxacillin demonstrated excellent penetration through staphylococcal biofilms, with an effect comparable to that of ciprofloxacin.75 This could suggest that the physicochemical properties may be more important than the PD profile of an antimicrobial. We found that constant amoxicillin exposure promoted biofilm formation in both E. faecalis strains, which may reflect an adaptive response to prolonged antibiotic stress. Sustained exposure to β-lactam antibiotics can induce biofilm-associated survival mechanisms, including enhanced matrix production, extracellular DNA release and enrichment of phenotypically tolerant subpopulations.71,72 Moreover, biofilm behaviour is influenced not only by the external antibiotic concentration but also by local concentration gradients arising from diffusion limitations and heterogeneous metabolic activity within the biofilm, which may allow protected cells to persist despite constant exposure.76 The AUM environment may further contribute to this phenomenon by mimicking urinary tract conditions, including nutrient availability, osmolarity and pH, which can alter bacterial physiology, reduce growth rates and favour biofilm persistence.77 We have recently shown that amoxicillin at sub-MIC levels induces biofilm formation and affects gene expression in E. faecalis ATCC 29212 and E. faecalis 54, which is in agreement with previous reports on β-lactam-induced stress responses in Gram-positive bacteria, including E. faecalis.78,79 Interestingly, the SEM pictures did not reveal much extracellular matrix, even in the amoxicillin-exposed biofilms. However, it should be kept in mind that CV stains not only the matrix but also dead cells that may not be distinguished from the live ones using SEM but which do contribute to the biofilm-based shield that protects the viable cells below. Although these explanations remain speculative and require further investigation, we believe that our findings show the need for clinically relevant models to optimize antimicrobial treatment strategies, as therapeutic protocols aiming to maximize classic PK–PD targets (i.e. T > MIC) may fail in biofilm-associated UTIs.

Despite the improved translational relevance of the dynamic in vitro PK–PD flow model, several limitations of this study should be noted. First, only two E. faecalis strains were evaluated, which may not reflect the genetic and phenotypic diversity of clinical isolates. Second, although the model simulates urinary flow and fluctuating drug concentrations, it cannot fully reproduce host immunity, mucosal interactions or in vivo polymicrobial communities. Third, the study duration of 72 h limits conclusions about long-term biofilm persistence, potential resistance development or adaptive responses that may occur during prolonged therapy. Fourth, while maximal urinary antibiotic exposure was targeted, the absorption phase was not modelled; direct simulation of urinary concentrations assumes ideal, consistent delivery to the urinary tract. Fifth, the simulated dosage may not reflect the most recent clinical guidelines as the currently recommended interval for amoxicillin is 8 h and for ciprofloxacin 12 h; including different dosages may shed more light on possible directions in preclinical dosage optimization. Finally, while the model uses physiologically relevant AUM, differences in urinary composition between patients may influence antibiotic penetration and biofilm susceptibility.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that a dynamic in vitro PK–PD flow enables effective assessment of simulated PK profiles and constant concentrations of amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin against E. faecalis biofilms. Compared with static models, this approach introduces a PK dimension that enhances the evaluation of potential dosing strategies in a preclinical setting. Under simulated human dosing conditions, ciprofloxacin produced a substantially greater reduction in both viable biofilm bacteria and overall biomass than constant-concentration exposure, whereas amoxicillin exhibited limited antibiofilm activity and, at constant concentration, appeared to promote biofilm formation. These findings indicate that traditional PK–PD principles derived from planktonic cells, particularly for time-dependent antimicrobials, such as amoxicillin, may not reliably predict biofilm-associated efficacy. Overall, integrating dynamic PK–PD biofilm models into preclinical evaluations may support the optimization of antibiotic selection, facilitate initial testing of dosing strategies and provide a foundation for hypothesis generation in future translational studies.
