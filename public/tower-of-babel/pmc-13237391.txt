The impact of wrist flexion on task performance and compensatory movements in transradial prosthesis users
Authors: Laura A Miller, Quinn A Boser, Vikram Darbhe, Kristi L Turner, Jacqueline S Hebert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpper-limb prosthetic technology has advanced in recent years, yet most commercially available prosthetic wrists offer only a single active degree of freedom (DOF), typically rotation, with few providing powered flexion/extension. This limitation often forces users to compensate with proximal joints, leading to unnatural movements and potential overuse injuries. Despite user demand for improved wrist movement, few powered wrist options exist, and current outcome measures rarely quantify compensatory strategies directly. To address this gap, the Gaze and Movement Assessment (GaMA) metric was used to evaluate task performance and compensatory movements in five adults with unilateral transradial limb absence, fitted with a custom 2-DOF powered wrist. Participants completed the GaMA Tasks and functional outcomes under two conditions: with and without wrist flexion/extension. Results showed that, though individual variability was high, enabling wrist flexion/extension was seen to reduce some compensatory trunk and shoulder movements towards normative data. These findings suggest that powered wrist flexion/extension may support more normative movement patterns and highlight the importance of individualized prosthetic training. Future work should focus on targeted rehabilitation strategies to minimize compensatory movements as device complexity increases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47669-2.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Upper-limb prosthetic technology has advanced substantially over the past decade. However, most commercially available prosthetic wrists provide at most one active DOF (rotation), and few offer powered flexion/extension of the wrist. This limitation means that proper positioning of the hand in space must often be accomplished by moving more proximal joints like the elbow, shoulder, or trunk. When the wrist cannot flex, individuals are forced to compensate with unnatural movements of the upper body to accomplish routine tasks1,2. Over time, these compensatory movements can lead to overuse injuries and pain in the neck, shoulder, and back, affecting the intact limb of individuals with upper limb absence or difference (ULA)3,4. Reducing these compensatory movements through prosthetic design therefore has the potential to make a large clinical impact. Across multiple user surveys and reviews, individuals with upper-limb absence consistently rank wrist movement among the top priorities for prosthetic improvement5.

Despite user interest and potential benefits, few prosthetic wrists with powered flexion/extension exist. There is a clear motivation to quantify how a powered wrist impacts functional performance and movement quality for prosthetic users. Common outcome measures, however, primarily record completion time or success, and do not capture the quality or strategy of movement. Other measures, such as the Activity Measure for Upper Limb Amputees (AM-ULA) and the Assessment for Capacity of Myoelectric Control (ACMC) do include compensatory movement as a factor in the scoring, but it is not directly quantified. This is a significant limitation when comparing whether a prosthesis can reduce awkward motions rather than eliminate them entirely.

To address this assessment gap, the Gaze and Movement Assessment (GaMA) metric was developed6. GaMA combines motion capture and eye tracking to evaluate task success as well as the kinematics of upper-body movement and gaze behavior during functional tasks. Initial normative validation data collected with able-bodied individuals performing GaMA tasks have characterized the typical joint angles and hand trajectories required for these tasks6,7. A multi-site study confirmed the reliability and validity of GaMA in different lab settings, supporting its use as a consistent outcome metric8. Previous studies have shown that compensatory strategies of prosthesis users differs across tasks and between levels of amputation9, as well as according to skill level7.

Motion analysis has been used to study the impact of prosthesis wrist movement. Transradial myoelectric prosthesis have shown greater trunk motion and shoulder abduction in the absence of a functional wrist, confirming that they adopt compensatory upper-body strategies10. A study of experienced prosthesis users given flexible vs. locked passive wrist units found no significant group-level difference in functional test performance or shoulder compensatory angles between the two conditions, but large inter-individual differences, during the execution of six ADL tasks11. In that study, nearly all participants subjectively preferred having the passive wrist flexion capability and believed it improved their movement, even though objective measures did not show a uniform effect11. More recent work evaluated three individuals with transradial ULA performing a clothespin relocation task with and without a powered wrist device (the Luke Arm or the Utah Wrist). They reported that adding a powered wrist significantly improved task success and reduced compensatory trunk lean by nearly 50% on average, without adding to cognitive load12. These findings suggest that providing wrist motion can reduce the need for awkward upper-body movements and improve functional dexterity12.

The current study aims to build on this background by evaluating a powered 2-DOF wrist (flexion/extension and rotation) in transradial prosthesis users, using the GaMA functional tasks to quantify performance and movement quality. We hypothesized that enabling active wrist flexion/extension would result in movement patterns more similar to normative data (i.e. reduced proximal compensations specifically less trunk flexion or shoulder abduction) at the cost of slightly longer task completion time to control the additional DoF. By contrast, with a fixed (1-DOF) wrist, we expect to see quicker movements but greater deviations in upper-body kinematics as users compensate for the missing wrist motion.

Methods

Participants

Five adult individuals with unilateral transradial ULA were recruited for this study (Table 1). Inclusion criteria required that participants could tolerate wearing a prosthetic socket for the duration of the testing and had no orthopedic or neurological conditions (aside from amputation) that would significantly affect upper-body movement. All 5 participants were male, with transradial amputation secondary to trauma. The study protocol was approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board and the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board and all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent, including consent to publish images for scholarly presentations or publications, was obtained from all participants.

Prosthetic device and setup

Participants were fitted with a custom designed powered wrist unit capable of two degrees of freedom: flexion/extension and axial rotation, with an Ottobock Transcarpal single-DOF (open/close) prosthetic hand (Supplementary Fig. S1). The wrist provided a total flexion/extension range of approximately 100° (about 58° of flexion and 42° of extension from neutral) and 351° rotation (pronation/supination). The overall length and mass of the wrist were 94.2 mm and 342 g, respectively. The prosthetic components were mounted on a custom-fitted thermoplastic forearm socket with a flexible inner socket and frame fit and fabricated by a certified prosthetist13.

Prosthesis control was through an 8-electrode myoelectric pattern recognition system, similar to the system described by Englehart and Hudgins (2003) using a linear discriminant analysis-based classifier14. Proportional control was achieved using the motion normalized proportional control approach described previously by Scheme et al.15. Each participant underwent a calibration of the pattern recognition system to map specific muscle activation patterns to the following prosthetic movements: hand open, hand close, pronation, supination, wrist flexion, and wrist extension.

Motion capture and eye-tracking instrumentation were set up in accordance with the GaMA protocol, using a cluster-based kinematic model16, with rigid marker plates attached to the pelvis, upper back, upper arms, forearms, and hands, with modifications for the mechanics of the prosthesis (see Supplement). An eight-camera optical motion capture system (Vicon Nexus 2.10.3, Oxford, UK https://www.vicon.com/software/nexus/) recorded the 3D marker trajectories at 120 Hz.

Experimental tasks and procedure

Each participant was evaluated under two conditions in a within-subject experimental design:

Condition 1: No Wrist Flexion (noWF)—the motor for wrist flexion/extension was disabled and the wrist was locked in a neutral (0 degrees of flexion/extension) position. Wrist rotation and hand open/close were calibrated and enabled,

Condition 1: No Wrist Flexion (noWF)—the motor for wrist flexion/extension was disabled and the wrist was locked in a neutral (0 degrees of flexion/extension) position. Wrist rotation and hand open/close were calibrated and enabled,

Condition 2: With Wrist Flexion (withWF)—the wrist flexion/extension motor was enabled, allowing the participant to actively flex or extend the wrist in the full 2-DOF wrist configuration (rotation + flexion/extension). The pattern recognition controller was configured to calibrated wrist flexion/extension, wrist rotation and hand open/close.

Condition 2: With Wrist Flexion (withWF)—the wrist flexion/extension motor was enabled, allowing the participant to actively flex or extend the wrist in the full 2-DOF wrist configuration (rotation + flexion/extension). The pattern recognition controller was configured to calibrated wrist flexion/extension, wrist rotation and hand open/close.

All participants experienced Condition 1 (noWF) first, followed by Condition 2 (withWF). This fixed order was chosen to mimic clinical practice where complexity is increased gradually17,18. To mitigate learning effects, participants practiced extensively in each condition before data collection (and, where possible, completed a home trial in each configuration—see below).

Before data collection, each participant underwent a training period with an occupational therapist, including basic use of the device as well as guided practice on each functional task. The study protocol included a two-week home trial in each condition, however, scheduling and pandemic-related delays meant that not all participants were able to complete the home trial for all conditions (see Table 1)13. After the home trial period (or after training, for those without home trial), participants returned for outcome assessments and a second visit for the GaMA tasks.

Data collection

In each condition, participants performed two GaMA tasks—the Cup Transfer Task and the Pasta Box Task (see Supplement)—while instrumented with the motion capture and eye tracker6. Each trial began with the wrist (when enabled) in a neutral flexion/extension position. The hand was rotated palm down with slight supination and held open, allowing the wrist, index finger, and thumb markers to remain visible while resting on the home position. We aimed to collect 7–10 successful trials of each task per condition for each participant (trials with errors or drops were repeated). Trials were considered successful if the participant completed the entire sequence without dropping the object or deviating from the instructed order. The order of tasks was randomized to prevent order effects (e.g., some participants did Cup task first then Pasta, others vice versa in each condition). They were allowed breaks between trials to avoid fatigue.

In addition to the GaMA tasks, we administered standard upper-limb outcome measures13. These included the Box and Blocks Test (BBT), the Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function Test, the Southampton Hand Assessment Procedure (SHAP), the Assessment for Capacity of Myoelectric Control (ACMC) and the Activities Measure for Upper Limb Amputees (AM-ULA).

Data analysis

Kinematic Processing: Using the recorded motion capture data, we computed three sets of outcome metrics for each task trial: (1) Task completion time (overall and subdivided by phase), (2) Hand trajectory characteristics, and (3) Joint angular kinematics of the trunk and arm. Kinematic analyses were performed using the custom GaMA software platform6,7, developed in MATLAB with the specific analysis for this study completed using MATLAB 2023a (MathWorks Inc., https://www.mathworks.com/products/new_products/release2023a.html). Details on the marker placement, joint calibration process, and data analysis are provided in the Supplement.

Participants’ joint usage and timing were compared to normative data from 20 control participants (individuals without ULA or other upper limb involvement) performing the tasks. Because control participants naturally use their wrist, their trunk and shoulder motion can be considered an ideal baseline scenario (minimal necessary compensation).

Statistical analysis

For each task (Cups and Pasta Box), descriptive statistics were calculated for each metric within each condition. For each trial the timing and peak-to-peak range of motion (RoM) for the various angles (e.g. shoulder flexion, shoulder ab/adduction, etc.) were calculated. The median and Interquartile range (IQR) were then determined for the number of trials in each condition. Given the small sample size (n = 5) and exploratory nature of this study, statistical comparisons were not conducted. Instead, we examined individual participant trends since large inter-subject variability was observed. Given the absence of a clearly established MCID for prosthesis users, we used a 5° threshold as a reference for clinically meaningful differences, based on values reported for normative populations19.

Cups transfer task results

Wrist usage

For the Cups Transfer Task, participants utilized wrist rotation for both conditions, even with the addition of wrist flexion and extension (Fig. 1). Rotation was utilized to orient the hand for picking up the near cup by the top and the far cup from the side. Participant S5 chose to rotate the hand such that the thumb and the index finger were parallel to the table (pinky up) for portions of the task, specifically for picking up the far cup, resulting in large rotation angles. This movement also caused marker occlusion which reduced accuracy of continuous angle measures and also limited calculation of certain timing events.

With the addition of wrist flexion, participants would typically flex the wrist at the start of the task to pick up the near cup but then varied on repositioning, with some participants using wrist extension when interacting with the far cup (side grasp), while others mostly keeping the wrist in flexion throughout the task. Overall, the median range of wrist flexion/extension for the 5 participants was 64.5°–90.5°. The non-zero value for the NoWF condition reflects a measurement artifact, with opening/closing the terminal device slightly perturb the hand marker plate, producing a small apparent change in wrist angle.

Timing

In Condition 2 (with WF), three of the five participants increased the amount of time spent completing the task (Fig. 2). The remaining two participants (S4 and S5) took a similar amount of time across both conditions; notably, these two participants were slower than the other three in Condition 1. Overall, participants tended to spend more time in the Reach phases, which involved positioning the device. Interestingly, Participant S4 reduced the time spent grasping and releasing the cups compared to Condition 1. For Participant S5, more detailed timing could not be obtained due to marker occlusion related to his positioning of wrist rotation described above.

Trunk and shoulder compensation

For the Cups Transfer Task, we examined the peak-to-peak values for each of the shoulder and trunk movements. Within participant values are shown in Fig. 3 and differences in the median for each condition are presented in Table 2. All participants demonstrated at least one degree of freedom at the trunk or shoulder that appeared to benefit from the addition of wrist flexion/extension. Specifically, the range of motion in these joints became smaller and more aligned with normative values in at least one movement when wrist flexion/extension was enabled (Fig. 3). However, the specific degrees of freedom and movements showing reduced compensation varied across participants. In general, reductions in range of motion were observed in trunk flexion/extension, trunk lateral bending, and/or shoulder abduction. Some participants also showed decreased shoulder internal rotation, although one participant (S3) exhibited increased shoulder internal rotation. S5 also showed increased trunk flexion/extension, shoulder flexion/extension, and shoulder ab/adduction.

Pasta box task results

Wrist usage

For the Pasta Box Task, participants used the wrist rotator with little difference between the 2 conditions (Fig. 4). Two of the participants (S1 and S5) preferred to pronate the hand thumb down, such that the pinkie was “up” in order to pick up the pasta box from the side table. This rotation effectively extends the hand since the opening of the hand is biased towards the palmar surface. Even with the addition of wrist flexion, both participants continued to rotate the hand to this orientation.

For each participant (S1–S5), box plots compare the two conditions: noWF (gray) and withWF (purple). Each box represents the interquartile range (IQR), with the median shown as a line inside the box. All participants also initiated wrist extension at the beginning of the task, even the two who adopted the strategy of picking up the pasta box with the pinky side of their hand up. As with S5 in the Cups Transfer Task, this rotation caused marker occlusion and errors in motion tracking at some points of the task, resulting in a large standard deviation for S1 for the WF condition. Some participants also utilized the wrist flexor and extensor during the task as the box was moved to the various shelves and to the home position, but due to the variability it was difficult to identify when these movements were intentional.

Timing

Unlike for the Cups Transfer Task where the time to complete the task increased, for the pasta box, only one participant (S3) increased median time taken to complete the task (Fig. 5). The other four participants took a similar amount of time in Condition 1 vs. Condition 2. For all participants, both conditions were slower than the normative data.

Trunk and shoulder kinematics

For the Pasta Box Task the within participant peak-to-peak values for each of the shoulder and trunk movements are shown in Fig. 6 and differences in the median for each condition are presented in Table 2. Though the differences were not as large as with the Cups Transfer Task, all participants demonstrated at least one degree of freedom at the trunk or shoulder with a reduced RoM when wrist flexion/extension was enabled. Specifically, when wrist flexion/extension was enabled, joint ranges of motion became smaller and more aligned with normative values in at least one movement (Fig. 6). For example, the addition of wrist flexion and extension resulted in decreased RoM in Trunk flexion/extension (S1, S3, and S5) and Shoulder flexion/extension (S1, S2). However, it also resulted in increased Shoulder Internal/External rotation for S1, S2, S3 and S4 and the decrease in Shoulder flexion/extension for S4 was further from the normative values. The increased internal rotation mostly occurred when the participants were placing or picking up the box at the second shelf. This is a challenging location for myoelectric control as it involves cross-body movement to the highest shelf.

Discussion

In previous reporting of this work, the addition of wrist flexion/extension did not significantly alter most timed functional outcomes (e.g., Box and Blocks Test, Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function Test), which is promising given the increased complexity of control13. In the current study, participants did use the wrist flexion DoF for both tasks, with variable effect on the usage of wrist rotation, which continued to be activated. The additional degree of freedom had an effect on the total time taken for the Cups Transfer Task (longer for 3 participants) but did not seem to substantially delay the pasta task times.

Individual differences were observed in performance and kinematics. Participants S1 and S4 showed the most substantial improvements in functional testing and were also subjectively observed to complete the GaMA task with greater consistency and ease. In contrast, S5 struggled with the GaMA testing, requiring two days to complete the no-wrist-flexion condition. For S5, inadvertent wrist movements also led to bead spillage, necessitating the replacement of plastic beads with a solid weight to reduce trial repetition and allow completion. Participant behavior varied both in terms of control strategies and observed kinematics. Two participants performed the pasta task with a 180-degree wrist rotation, which allowed them to complete the task in a clinically acceptable way (e.g., achieving pronation), but this then deviated from the expected movement path with the GaMA tasks. This variation in performance is consistent with findings from previous studies, where individuals have been shown to have increased variability in kinematics with a variety of devices and tasks compared to controls10,20–23. Understanding these individual factors affecting performance, there were some noted individual differences in compensatory movements between conditions.

It has previously been shown that prosthesis users compensate with more trunk movement (increased trunk movement in all three planes)10 and particularly more trunk flexion and extension during the pasta task9. Participants using the 2DOF system also had increased trunk compensation compared to normative values for both conditions. When provided with wrist flexion, there was a reduction in excessive trunk flexion/extension in the pasta task and trunk lateral bend in the Cups Transfer Task. For body-powered prosthesis users, it has been shown that higher skilled users tend to use trunk axial rotation as a compensation more than trunk lateral bending or trunk flexion, which were strategies employed by less skilled users24. However, large changes in trunk axial rotation were not noted between conditions or participants in this study. Task-specific compensation patterns were evident in the current study and past studies: forward-reaching tasks (e.g., cups, carton pouring) elicited more lateral bending, while cross-body tasks (e.g., pasta box, tray transfer) involved greater trunk flexion/extension10,24.

Excessive shoulder abduction was seen in both tasks, with no consistent response to the wrist flexion condition. Shoulder flexion and extension compensation was less than expected compared to normative values, most notable for the Pasta Box Task. Inconsistent shoulder rotation ranges were seen across participants, similar to previous results when performing these tasks9. Other studies on shoulder compensations have shown myoelectric prosthesis users increase shoulder abduction/adduction to complete tasks such as cutting, page turning, and carton lifting10. Greater (static) wrist flexion has been associated with less shoulder abduction, with recognition that the type of terminal device and individual strategy also affect shoulder compensation25. It is unclear if static pre-positioning of the wrist as a primary strategy would be more or less effective than active wrist control throughout the task in providing a consistent task strategy that would reduce or impact the variability of shoulder compensation.

Examining individual participant performance, S1 and S4 appeared to benefit from the added degree of freedom the most, showing the greatest changes in compensatory flexion/extension movements at shoulder and trunk, though more obvious for cups vs. pasta. These individuals also tended to perform better in functional outcomes, suggesting a potential link between improved control and reduced compensation, a trend also noted in other research24,26. This highlights the need for future studies to explore therapy approaches aimed specifically at minimizing compensatory strategies when additional degrees of freedom are introduced and aligns with conclusions from prior work indicating that increased complexity in prosthetic control does not necessarily compromise function, making targeted compensation-focused interventions feasible.

Control of the additional degree of freedom was especially challenging during tasks performed outside the typically constrained workspace in front of the body, reinforcing the importance of testing across varied workspaces9. Overall, for the Pasta Box Task there was greater variability in the results, with both decreases and increases in trunk and shoulder RoM observed for participants with the addition of wrist flexion/extension. The Pasta task, which involved reaching a high shelf, was particularly challenging and led to frequent inadvertent movements. Similarly, picking up the box at the lowest shelf (first pickup) required substantial torso movement across both conditions. All participants exhibited much larger trunk flexion and extension and trunk bend compared to normative for the Pasta Box Task in both conditions. Interestingly, the tallest participants (S2 and S3) also struggled with placing the Pasta Box Task on the top shelf, even though their height should have made the top shelf more accessible. Tasks performed at counter height, such as the Cups Transfer Task, yielded slightly more consistent results and less compensation, suggesting that workspace complexity influences control demands. The benefit of testing with more difficult tasks is that it identifies the challenges that still exist which may be barriers to prosthesis usage at home.

Due to time constraints and funding limitations, home trials were restricted, which may have impacted participant performance. S1 completed home trials for both conditions and showed strong performance. Interestingly, S4 performed well despite limited experience and no home trials, whereas S2 had abandoned prosthesis use and showed fewer improvements. S3 and S5, less frequent users, also demonstrated limited gains. Though other studies have not found that compensatory movements were correlated to duration of prothesis ownership27, it was noted that for this study participants whose functional outcomes leaned toward higher scores also trended toward reduced compensatory movements. There is agreement that therapy is critical to prosthetic usage and satisfaction28–30, but little evidence to show how best to train to specifically reduce compensations.31 There is also suggestion that factors other than prosthesis type may need to be considered to explain the individual differences in compensation seen across studies27, which further underscores the need for studies of therapy approaches specific to reducing compensatory movements when additional degrees-of-freedom are added.

Other control metrics, for example counting activations or limb trajectories throughout the task, may be a potential avenue for future work. These metrics, used in other studies to quantify control32, could help clarify how well participants manage additional degrees of freedom. However, challenges with counting these metrics, particularly with noisy and missing data, did not permit this analysis for this study. In the future, improving these algorithms and incorporating control metrics could enhance understanding of user behavior and device effectiveness.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. First, the number of participants was small, and individual variability was high, which may limit generalizability. Second, due to time constraints and funding limitations, home trials were restricted and not all participants had equal exposure to training, potentially affecting participant performance. As therapy was limited to 1 to 2 sessions per condition, far fewer than would typically occur in clinical practice, the short duration of training was likely not sufficient to retrain any existing habits to reduce compensation. Finally, our primary kinematic summary (total range of motion) does not distinguish active wrist motion from prepositioning and holding.

Task design allowed for some flexibility in movement strategies, which may have led participants to adopt clinically acceptable but non-normative approaches, and contributed to the individual variability despite the relative constraints of the tasks. These strategies also resulted in marker occlusion for portions of the trials, making full data analysis difficult. To mitigate marker occlusion during large wrist rotations, future studies could increase camera coverage and/or add additional markers to the hand cluster to improve redundancy across orientations. Where feasible, directly logging wrist/hand joint angles from the device and time-synchronizing those signals with motion capture may further reduce dependence on optically tracked markers in highly occluded postures.

Conclusions

This study explored the impact of adding wrist flexion/extension to a prosthetic control system on functional performance and compensatory movement strategies. Participants who demonstrated improved functional performance also tended to show reductions in compensatory movements, suggesting that enhanced control may support more normative movement patterns. The addition of a degree of freedom did not compromise task completion, even in more complex tasks, indicating that users can adapt to increased control complexity. These findings support the feasibility of designing targeted rehabilitation strategies that focus on reducing compensatory movements without sacrificing functional performance.

The variability in participant behavior highlights the importance of individualized approaches in prosthetic training and evaluation. Differences in prior experience, home trial exposure, and control strategies all contributed to performance outcomes. Future work could consider incorporating control metrics to better quantify user adaptation and effectiveness, and explore therapy approaches that directly address compensatory movement patterns when additional degrees of freedom are introduced.
