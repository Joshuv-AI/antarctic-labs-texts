Abstract
. We give an unexpectedly simple presentation of the maximal prolongation of a first-order differential calculus in terms of the bimodule map of a torsion-free bimodule connection. We then show that in the quantum homogeneous space case this simplifies even further. More explicitly, we show that the bimodule map associated to a bimodule connection, for any relative left Hopf module endowed with its canonical right module structure, admits a concise formula, given in terms of the adjont action of a Hopf algebra on a bimodule. These results are applied to the quantum Grassmannian Heckenberger–Kolb calculi, yielding a simple uniform presentation of their degree two anti-holomorphic relations.



1.
Introduction

In the differential calculus approach to noncommutative geometry, as presented for example in the recent monograph
[
2
]
, one normally starts with a first-order differential calculus (an algebraic structure modeled on the space of one-forms of a smooth manifold) and then extends to a differential calculus (an algebraic structure modeled on the de Rham complex of a smooth manifold). In the classical setting, this extension is simply given by the exterior algebra of the one-forms. However, in the noncommutative setting this is a much more subtle question. There does exist a universal, or maximal prolongation, an extension such that every other extension arises as a quotient of it. Indeed, in many cases the maximal prolongation proves to be the most geometrically motivated extension. (Although there are many situations where it is more natural to consider non-trivial quotients, for example Woronowicz’s Yetter-Drinfeld extension of a bicovariant differential calculus over a Hopf algebra
[
67
, §3]
.)

Explicitly describing this maximal prolongation (for example determining its space of highest degree non-zero forms) is in general a challenging problem. In many ways, it is analogous to determining when the Nichols algebra of a braided vector space is finite-dimensional. In the Hopf algebra case one has the
left-invariant element approach
for calculating the degree two relations
[
34
, §14.3.3]
. For example, this can be effectively used to determine a set of relations for the maximal prolongation of Woronowicz’s
3D
-calculus on
\mathcal{O}_{q}(\mathrm{SU}_{2})
, see
[
34
, Example 14.5]
for details. In the quantum homogeneous space setting, we have a reative version of this approach
[
49
, Theorem 5.7]
, which has been used to explicitly describe the maximal prolongation of the Heckenberger–Kolb calculus of quantum projective space
\mathcal{O}_{q}(\mathbb{CP}^{n})

[
49
, Proposition 5.8]
. It has also been used to calculate the maximal prolongation of the Lusztig first-order differential calculi of the
A
-series full quantum flag manifolds
[
51
]
, and the maximal prolongation of the Lusztig calculus of the
C_{2}
full quantum flag manifold
[
13
]
. While the left-invariant element approach is a systematic procedure, in practice it can be time-consuming and tedious. An alternative approach, dualised to the level of the tangent space of the calculus, was used by Heckenberger and Kolb in
[
29
, §3.3]
. However, this also necessitates a non-trivial set of calculations in the corresponding Drinfeld–Jimbo quantised universal enveloping algebra. Thus a more effective means for determining the maximal prolongation would be very welcome.

This article explores the idea that bimodule connections have a role to play in any noncommutative geometric understanding of quantum exterior algebras. We recall that a left bimodule connection for a
B
-bimodule
\mathcal{F}
, is a left connection
\nabla:{\mathcal{F}}\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}{\mathcal{F}}
, with an additional compatibility between the connection and the right module structure, first considered in the 1990s in
[
19
,
20
,
47
]
. Explicitly, we require that the map

\displaystyle\sigma:{\mathcal{F}}\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}{\mathcal{F}},

\displaystyle f\otimes\mathrm{d}b\mapsto\nabla(fb)-\nabla(f)b,

is well-defined. We call
\sigma
the
bimodule map
associated to
\nabla
. In the commutative case,
\sigma
reduces to the flip map. Since the flip defines the exterior algebra, it is natural to ask if
\sigma
can be used to describe the maximal prolongation of a first-order differntial calculus. The first indication that this might be the case came from the work of Fiore and Madore
[
23
]
, who observed that the relations contain the image of the map
\sigma+\mathrm{id}
if and only if the torsion of the connection
\nabla
is a right module map, see also
[
1
, Proposition 2.2.2]
. In later work, Beggs and Majid
[
1
]
showed that the covariant connection
\nabla
for the Podleś calculus is a torsion-free bimodule connection whose torsion is a right module map, thus implying that the degree-two relations of the calculus contain the image of
\sigma+\mathrm{id}
. This work was later extended to the anti-holomorphic subcomplex of the quantum projective space by Matassa in
[
44
, Theorem 3.5]
, and it was shown that the inclusion is an equality. In fact, the more general quantum Grassmannians were shown in
[
36
]
to admit a Nichols algebra structure. However, in contrast to the bimodule map setting, the relations in this case are realised as the kernel of the map
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
. The calculus here is the Heckenberger–Kolb calculus and the connection
\nabla
is the unique covariant connection for the calculus introduced in
[
48
, §8]
. Moreover, in
[
45
, Theorem 8.4]

\nabla
was shown to be a noncommutative Levi-Civita in the sense of Beggs and Majid
[
2
, Definition 8.3]
.

In this paper, we show that if a first-order differential calculus admits a torsion-free bimodule connection, for example if it admits a noncommutative Levi-Civita connection, then its maximal prolongation admits an explicit description in terms of the connection and its associated bimodule map. Explicitly, if
\sigma
is the bimodule map associated to a bimodule connection
\nabla
, then we show that the maximal prolongation is equal to the quotient of the tensor algebra of
\Omega^{1}(B)
by the
B
-bimodule generated by the elements
\sigma(\omega\otimes\nu)+\omega\otimes\nu
, for
\omega,\nu\in\Omega^{1}
, and
\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)
, for
b\in B
. (Here we are very much motivated by the approach of
[
43
, Lemma 2.2]
in which Majid constructed an extension of an inner differential calculus by quotienting by the kernel of the map
\sigma-\mathrm{id}
, for
\sigma
bimodule connection bimodule map.) For the special case of a covariant differential calculus
\Omega^{1}(B)
over a quantum homogeneous space
B\subseteq A
, and a left relative Hopf module
\mathcal{F}
endowed with its canonical right module structure, then the bimodule map associated to a bimodule connection
\nabla
satisfies the simple identity

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma(f\otimes\mathrm{d}b)=\mathrm{d}(f_{(-2)}cS(f_{(-1)}))\otimes f_{(0)},

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }f\in\mathcal{F},\,b\in B.

For the case when
{\mathcal{F}}=\Omega^{1}(B)
, this then gives an explicit presentation of the degree two forms of the maximal prolongation. Moreover, for covariant almost-complex structures over quantum homogeneous spaces, we give explicit criteria for when a first-order almost-complex structure can be extended to an almost complex structure. Moreover, the almost-complex structure is factorisable if and only if the bimodule map squares to the identity when exchanging holomorphic and anti-holomorphic forms.

The Heckenberger–Kolb calculi for the quantum projective spaces are part of the much larger family of Heckenberger–Kolb calculi for the irreducible quantum flag manifolds. These differential calculi are one of the most important classes of quantum group noncommutative geometries, and have been the subject of intense investigation over the last twenty years. Moreover, the quantum exterior algebras of these calculi have been studied from an algebraic and representatation theoretic point of view. For example see the work of Berenstein and Zwicknagl on quantum exterior algebras
[
5
]
. It was then shown by Krähmer and Tucker–Simmons that these algebras are Frobenius and Koszul
[
35
, §4]
. A description of the quantum Grassmannian exterior algebra as a Nichols algebra appeared later in
[
36
]
, using a relative Hopf module version of the Woronowicz’s Yetter–Drinfeld extension, where the braided vector space was constructed from a quantum principal bundle constructed from the coquasi-triangular structure of
\mathcal{O}_{q}(\mathrm{SU}_{n})
.

One of the main results of this paper is to apply the general framework of torsion-free bimodule connections to the
Heckenberger–Kolb differential calculi

\Omega^{1}_{q}(G/L_{S})
. Each of these calculi possesses a unique covariant connection
[
17
, §4.5]
. Indeed, this connection has recently been identified as a
noncommutative Levi-Civita connection

[
6
, §6.5]
. For the quantum Grassmannina case We establish that these connections are
strongly torsion-free
, thereby implying that all relations are of the form

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\nu+\sigma(\omega\otimes\nu),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for }\penalty\ \omega,\nu\in\Omega^{1}(G/L_{S}).

This yields a novel noncommutative geometric description of the associated
quantum exterior algebras
, offering new insight into their structure.

Summary of the Paper

The paper is organised as follows: In §
2
we recall some necessary preliminaries about differential calculi, maximal prolongations of first-order calculi, connections, the torsion of a connection, and bimodule connections.

In §
3
we consider a first-order differential calculus
\Omega^{1}(B)
over an algebra
B
, endowed with a torsion-free bimodule connection
\nabla
. We give a description of the maximal prolongation of
\Omega^{1}(B)
in terms of
\nabla
and its associated bimodule map.

In §
4
we specialise to the case of a covariant differential calculus
\Omega^{1}(B)
over a quantum homogeneous space
B\subseteq A
, where we show that for a relative Hopf module
\mathcal{F}
, with canonical right module structure, that the bimodule map associated to a bimodule connection
\nabla
satisfies the simple identity. In the presence of a framing calculus for
\Omega^{1}(B)
, we establish a
local
version of this formula. Finally, under some assumptions, we produce a dual formula at the tangent space level.

In §
5
we treat our motivating family of examples, the Heckenberger–Kolb calculi over the irreducible quantum flag manfiolds, endowed with their unique covariant Levi-Civita connections
\nabla
. For the case of the anti-holomorphic quantum Grassmannian Heckenberger-Kolb calculi, this connection is shown to be strongly torsion-free, yielding a presentation of the degree-two relations in terms of the bimodule map of
\sigma
.

2.
Preliminaries

This section provides basic material on differential calculi, connections, bimodule connections, and the torsion of a connection for a differential calculus.

2.1.
Differential Calculi

A
differential calculus
, or a
dc
, is a differential graded algebra (dga)

\left(\Omega^{\bullet}\simeq\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\Omega^{k},\mathrm{d}\right)

that is generated as an algebra by the elements
a,\mathrm{d}b
, for
a,b\in\Omega^{0}
. When no confusion arises, we denote the dc by
\Omega^{\bullet}
, omitting the exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}
. We denote the degree of a homogeneous element
\omega\in\Omega^{\bullet}
by
|\omega|
. For a given algebra
B
, a differential calculus
over

B
is a differential calculus such that
B=\Omega^{0}
.
For a subalgebra
C\subset B
, we call the fodc
\Omega^{1}(C):=\mathrm{span}_{B}\{\mathrm{d}b\,|\,b\in B\}
the
restriction
of
\Omega^{1}(B)
to
C
.

A
*
-differential calculus
, or a
*
-dc
, over a
*
-algebra
B
is a differential calculus over
B
such that the
*
-map of
B
extends to a (necessarily unique) conjugate-linear involution
*:\Omega^{\bullet}(B)\to\Omega^{\bullet}(B)
satisfying the identity
\mathrm{d}(\omega^{*})=(\mathrm{d}\omega)^{*}
, and for which

\displaystyle(\omega\wedge\nu)^{*}=(-1)^{kl}\nu^{*}\wedge\omega^{*},

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }\omega\in\Omega^{k},\,\nu\in\Omega^{l}.

A
first-order differential calculus
(fodc) over an algebra
B
is a pair
(\Omega^{1}(B),\mathrm{d})
, where
\Omega^{1}(B)
is a
B
-bimodule and
\mathrm{d}:B\to\Omega^{1}(B)
is a derivation such that
\Omega^{1}
is generated as a left (or equivalently right)
B
-module by those elements of the form
\mathrm{d}b
, for
b\in B
. A
first-order
*
-differential calculus
(
*
-fodc) over a
*
-algebra
B
is defined in analogy with the
*
-dc case.

We say that a dc
(\Gamma^{\bullet},\mathrm{d}_{\Gamma})

extends
a fodc
(\Omega^{1},\mathrm{d}_{\Omega})
if there exists a bimodule isomorphism
\varphi:\Omega^{1}\to\Gamma^{1}
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\Gamma}=\varphi\circ\mathrm{d}_{\Omega}
. It can be shown
[
49
, §2.5]
that any fodc admits an extension
\Omega^{\bullet}
which is
maximal
in the sense that there exists a unique differential map from
\Omega^{\bullet}
onto any other extension of
\Omega^{1}
. We call this extension the
maximal prolongation
of
\Omega^{1}
.

By construction, the maximal prolongation is a quotient of the tensor algebra
\mathcal{T}(\Omega^{1}(B))
by an ideal generated in degree
2
. We find it convenient to denote the degree two elements by
N^{(2)}
. So explicitly, it holds that

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}(B)\simeq\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)/N^{(2)},

\displaystyle\Omega^{\bullet}(B)\simeq\mathcal{T}_{B}(\Omega^{1}(B))/\langle N^{(2)}\rangle.

For a more detailed presentation of the maximal prolongation see
[
2
, §1]
, or
[
34
, §14]

2.2.
Bimodule Connections and Torsion

In this subsection we briefly recall some basic notions about connections and their torsion operators. Let
\Omega^{\bullet}(B)
be a differential calculus over an algebra
B
and
\mathcal{F}
a left B-module, a
connection
on
\mathcal{F}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

\nabla:\mathcal{F}\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\mathcal{F}

satisfying the identity

\displaystyle\nabla(bf)=\mathrm{d}b\otimes f+b\nabla f,

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,f\in\mathcal{F}.

The
torsion
of
\nabla
is the left
B
-module map

\displaystyle T_{\nabla}:=\wedge\circ\nabla-\mathrm{d}:\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{2}(B).

If
T_{\nabla}=0
, then we say that
\nabla
is torsion free.

Let
\Omega^{1}(B)
be a fodc over an algebra
B
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a left
B
-module. A
left bimodule connection
for
{\mathcal{F}}
is a pair
(\nabla,\sigma)
, where
\nabla
is a left connection and
\sigma
is a bimodule map
\sigma:{\mathcal{F}}\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}{\mathcal{F}}
, satisfying the identity

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla(fb)=\nabla(f)b+\sigma(f\otimes db).

Note that in the commutative case
\sigma(f\otimes\mathrm{d}b)=\mathrm{d}b\otimes f
, that is to say,
\sigma
reduces to the flip map. Moreover, since
\sigma(f\otimes db)=\nabla(fb)-\nabla(f)b
, we see that
\sigma
is uniquely determined by
\nabla
, so in particular being a bimodule connection is a
property
of a connection.

3.
Torsion-Free Bimodule Connections and the Maximal Prolongation

In this section we establish the main theoretical result of the paper, that is, the explicit description of the maximal prolongation of a differential calculus in terms of a torsion-free bimodule connection. Moreover, we investigate the special cases of an invertible bimodule map, and when this inverse can be expressed in terms of a
*
-map for the calculus.

3.1.
The Description of the Maximal Prolongation

We begin with the main theorem of this section, we then introduce the notion of a strongly torsion-free connection, and finally give a generating set method for verifying this property.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
B
be an algebra and
(\Omega^{1}(B),\mathrm{d})
a fodc over
B
. Moreover, let

\nabla:\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)

be a bimodule connection, with associated bimodule map
\sigma
, that is torsion-free with respect to the maximal prolongation
\Omega^{\bullet}(B)
of
\Omega^{1}(B)
. Then it holds that
N^{(2)}
of the maximal prolongation of
\Omega^{1}(B)
is generated as a
B
-bimodule by
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2},
where

(3)

\displaystyle G_{1}:=\Big\{\omega\otimes\nu+\sigma(\omega\otimes\nu)\,|\,\penalty\ \textrm{for }\omega,\nu\in\Omega^{1}(B)\Big\},

\displaystyle G_{2}:=\Big\{\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)\,|\,\penalty\ \textrm{for }b\in B\Big\}.

Proof.

Denote by
J
the
B
-sub-bimodule of
\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)
generated by the elements in
G.
Consider the quotient bimodule

\Gamma^{2}:=\Omega^{1}\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}/J,

and denote the canonical projection by

\displaystyle\wedge_{J}:\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Gamma^{2},

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\nu\mapsto\omega\wedge_{J}\nu.

Consider next the linear map

\mathrm{d}_{J}:=\wedge_{J}\circ{\nabla}:\Omega^{1}\to\Gamma^{2}.

We observe that, for any
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B)
, and
b\in B
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J}(b\omega)=\mathrm{d}b\wedge_{J}\omega+b\mathrm{d}_{J}(\omega).

Moreover, multiplying by
b
on the right, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J}(\omega b)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{J}(\omega)b+\wedge_{J}\circ\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b)=\mathrm{d}_{J}(\omega)b-\omega\wedge_{J}\mathrm{d}b.

Moreover, we note that, since by definition
\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)\in J
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J}\circ\mathrm{d}(b)=\wedge_{J}\circ\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)=0.

Thus we have a dc

B\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{d}\ }\Omega^{1}(B)\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{d}_{J}\ }\Omega^{2}(B)\xrightarrow{\ 0\ }0

extending
\Omega^{1}(B)
. Since this must arise as a quotient of the maximal prolongation, we see that
N^{(2)}\subseteq J
.

To see the opposite inclusion, consider the defining identity of
\sigma

\displaystyle\nabla(\omega b)=\nabla(\omega)b+\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Looking at the image of this identity under the projection
\wedge:\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{2}(B)
, and recalling that
\nabla
is by assumption torsion-free, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(\omega b)=\mathrm{d}(\omega)b+\wedge\circ\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

A simple application of the Leibniz rule now implies that

\displaystyle-\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}b=\wedge\circ\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Thus we see that

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b+\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b)\in\mathrm{ker}(\wedge)=N^{(2)},

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Moreover, for any
\mathrm{d}b
, our assumption of torsion-freeness implies that

\wedge\circ\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)=\mathrm{d}^{2}b=0.

Hence we must have that
\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)\in N^{(2)}
, giving the opposite inclusion. Thus the proposed set of relations span
N^{(2)}
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

It holds that
\wedge\circ\sigma=-\wedge
.

Proof.

The first identity follows immediately from the fact that, for any
\omega,\,\nu\in\Omega^{1}(B)
, the element
\omega\otimes\nu+\sigma(\omega\otimes\nu)
is in the kernel of the wedge map, and so, that

(4)

\displaystyle-\omega\wedge\nu=\wedge(\sigma(\omega\otimes\nu))

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

.
For any
\omega\otimes\nu\in\Omega^{2}(B)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\nu-\sigma^{2}(\omega\otimes\nu)\in N^{(2)}.

Proof.

Note that the element

\displaystyle(\mathrm{id}+\sigma)(X-\sigma(X))=X-\sigma(X)+\sigma(X)-\sigma^{2}(X)=X-\sigma^{2}(X)

is contained in the image of
\mathrm{id}+\sigma
. Hence
X-\sigma^{2}(X)
is a relation as claimed.
∎

In fact, as we will see below, in many important examples, the generators of
G_{2}
will be realised as linear combinations of the elements of
G_{1}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

We say that a connection is
strongly torsion-free
if
G_{2}
is contained in the
B
-bimodule generated by the elements of
G_{1}
.

For the case where
G_{2}
is not contained in the bimodule generated by
G_{1}
, the following proposition shows that we only need to calculate terms coming from a set of generators.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be a set of generators of
B
as a unital algebra. Then it holds that
G
is spanned as a
B
-bimodule by
G_{1}
and the elements

\Big\{\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)\,|\,\textrm{ for }b\in X\Big\}.

Proof.

For
b,c
two elements of the generating set
X
, we see that

\displaystyle\nabla(\mathrm{d}(bc))

\displaystyle=\,\nabla((\mathrm{d}b)c)+\nabla(b\mathrm{d}c)

\displaystyle=\,\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)c+\sigma(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\mathrm{d}c)+\mathrm{d}b\otimes\mathrm{d}c+b\nabla(\mathrm{d}c).

Thus we see that
\nabla(\mathrm{d}(bc))
can be written as a sum of the elements
\nabla(\mathrm{d}b)c
and
b\nabla(\mathrm{d}c)
, and an element of
G_{1}
. The claimed result now follows from an elementary inductive argument.
∎

3.2.
The Case of an Invertible Bimodule Map

In this subsection we examine the case where the bimodule map
\sigma
is invertible. This gives us an alternative description of the degree two relations of the maximal prolongation using the inverse
\sigma^{-1}
. As we will see in Corollary
4.5
, for the quantum homogeneous space case, this gives us a description of degree two relations that does not involve the antipode. In practice, such a formula can be easier to calculate with.

Lemma 3.6
.

For a bimodule connection
\nabla
with invertible bimodule map
\sigma
,

\nabla^{R}:=\sigma^{-1}\circ\nabla

is a right bimodule connection and its bimodule map is given by
\sigma^{-1}
. Moreover, it holds that

\overline{T}_{\nabla}:=\wedge\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\nabla+\mathrm{d}=0,

Proof.

The fact that we have a right connection follows from the calculation

\displaystyle\nabla^{R}(\omega b)=\,\sigma^{-1}\circ\nabla(wb)=\,(\sigma^{-1}\circ\nabla(\omega))b+\sigma^{-1}\circ\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b)=\,\nabla^{R}(\omega)b+\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b.

Next we note that

\displaystyle\overline{T}_{\nabla}(\omega)=\mathrm{d}\omega-\wedge\circ\nabla^{R}(\omega)=\mathrm{d}\omega-\wedge\circ\sigma^{-1}\circ\nabla(\omega)=\mathrm{d}\omega+\wedge\circ\nabla(\omega)=T_{\nabla}(\omega)=0,

where the third identity follows from Lemma
3.6
. Thus we see that
\nabla^{R}
is anti-torsion-free.
∎

We call
\overline{T}_{\nabla}
the
anti-torsion operator
. When the anti-torsion operator
vanishes
, that is to say, when
\overline{T}_{\nabla}=0
, then we say that the connection
\nabla^{R}
is
anti-torsion free
.

We now produce a description of the maximal prolongation in terms of the inverse
\sigma^{-1}
of the bimodule map. The proof is the same as for Theorem
3.1
, but there is a subtle issue with signs, so we find it instructive to explicitly present the proof.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
B
be an algebra and
(\Omega^{\bullet}(B),\mathrm{d})
a fodc over
B
. Moreover, let

\nabla:\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)

be a torsion-free bimodule connection with an invertible associated bimodule map
\sigma
. Then it holds that
N^{(2)}
of the maximal prolongation of
\Omega^{1}(B)
is generated as a
B
-bimodule by

G_{R}=G_{1,R}\cup G_{2,R},
where

(5)

\displaystyle G_{1,R}:=\Big\{\omega\otimes\nu+\sigma^{-1}(\omega\otimes\nu)\,|\,\penalty\ \textrm{for }\omega,\nu\in\Omega^{1}(B)\Big\},

and

\displaystyle G_{2,R}:=\Big\{\nabla^{R}(\mathrm{d}b)\,|\,\penalty\ \textrm{for }b\in B\Big\}.

Proof.

Denote by
J_{R}
the
B
-sub-bimodule of
\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)
generated by the elements in
G_{R}
. Consider the quotient bimodule

\Gamma_{R}^{2}:=(\Omega^{1}\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1})/J_{R},

and denote the canonical projection by

\displaystyle\wedge_{J,R}:\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Gamma_{R}^{2},

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\nu\mapsto\omega\wedge_{J,R}\nu.

Consider next the linear map

\mathrm{d}_{J,R}:=-\wedge_{J,R}\circ{\nabla_{R}}:\Omega^{1}\to\Gamma_{R}^{2},

where we note the change in sign from the corresponding proof for the left connection
\nabla
. We observe that, for any
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B)
, and
b\in B
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J,R}(b\omega)=

\displaystyle\,-\wedge_{J,R}\circ{\nabla_{R}}(b\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\wedge_{J,R}\big(\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)+b\nabla(\omega)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}b\wedge_{J,R}\omega-b\mathrm{d}_{J,R}(\omega).

When we instead multiply
\omega
on the right by
b
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J,R}(\omega b)\,=-\wedge_{J,R}\circ{\nabla_{R}}(\omega b)=\,-\wedge_{J,R}\big({\nabla_{R}}(\omega)b+\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b\big)=\,\mathrm{d}_{R,J}(\omega)b-\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}b

Moreover, we note that, since by definition
\nabla^{R}(d(b))\in J
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{J}\circ\mathrm{d}(b)=\wedge_{J,R}\circ\nabla(db)=0.

Thus we have a dc

B\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{d}\ }\Omega^{1}(B)\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{d}_{J}\ }\Gamma^{2}(B)\xrightarrow{\ 0\ }0

extending
\Omega^{1}(B)
. Since this must arise as a quotient of the maximal prolongation, we see that
N_{R}^{(2)}\subseteq J
.

As for the left connection case examined in Theorem
3.1
, this inclusion is an equality. Indeed, take the identity

\displaystyle\nabla_{R}(b\omega)=\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)+b\nabla(\omega),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Looking at the image of this identity under the projection
\wedge:\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{2}(B)
, and recalling that, by Lemma
3.6
, the right connection
\nabla_{R}
is anti-torsion-free, we see that

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}(b\omega)=\wedge\circ\,\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)+b\mathrm{d}(\omega),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

A simple application of the Leibniz rule now implies that

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}b\wedge\omega=\wedge\circ\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b),

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Thus we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega+\sigma(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)\in N^{(2)},

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all }b\in B,\,\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B).

Moreover, for any
\mathrm{d}b
, Lemma
3.6
implies that

\wedge\circ\nabla^{R}(\mathrm{d}b)=-\mathrm{d}^{2}b=0.

Hence we must have that
\nabla^{R}(\mathrm{d}b)\in N^{(2)}
, giving the opposite inclusion. Thus the proposed set of relations span
N^{(2)}
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

It holds that
\wedge\circ\sigma^{-1}=-\wedge
.

Proof.

Since

is invertible by assumption, we see that the second identity follows by pre-composing (

\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle\sigma(N^{(2)})=\sigma^{-1}(N^{(2)})=N^{(2)}.

Note first that

Corollary 3.10

3.3.
Conjugate Connection of a
*
-Invertible Bimodule Connection

Let
B
be a
*
-algebra and let
\Omega^{\bullet}(B)
be a
*
-dc over
B
. Moreover, let
\nabla:\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)
be a bimodule connection with associated bimodule map
\sigma
. In this subsection, we investigate how
\sigma
and the
*
-map interact.

Consider the usual lift of the
*
-map of
\Omega^{2}(B)
to the map

\displaystyle\ast:\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B)\to\Omega^{1}(B)\otimes_{B}\Omega^{1}(B),

\displaystyle\omega\otimes\nu\mapsto\nu^{*}\otimes\omega^{*}.

We note that we have the identity
\ast\circ\wedge=-\wedge\circ\,\ast
. Moreover, this gives an isomorphism of
real
vector spaces since the
*
-map of
\Omega^{1}(B)
is involutive.

Definition 3.13
.

We say that
\sigma
is
*
-invertible
if it is invertible and

\sigma^{-1}=\overline{\sigma}:=\ast\circ\sigma\circ\ast.

In the following proposition, we show that the assumption of
*
-invertibility allows us to construct a new connection from
\nabla
and the
*
-map.

Proposition 3.14
.

If
\sigma
is
*
-invertible, then a left
A
-covariant bimodule connection for the
*
-fodc
\Omega^{1}(B)
is given by

\overline{\nabla}:=\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla\circ\ast.

We call
\overline{\nabla}
the
conjugate connection
of
\nabla
.

Proof.

Note first that, for
b\in B
, and
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(B)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}(b\omega)=\,\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla\circ\ast(b\omega)=

\displaystyle\,\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla(\omega^{*}b^{*}).

Since
\nabla
is a bimodule connection by assumption, this expression reduces to

\displaystyle\sigma\circ\ast\Big(\nabla(\omega^{*})b^{*}+\sigma(\omega^{*}\otimes\mathrm{d}b^{*})\Big)=b\overline{\nabla}(\omega)+\sigma\circ\ast\circ\sigma\circ\ast(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)\Big).

Since by assumption
\ast\circ\sigma\circ\ast=\sigma^{-1}
, we see that this further reduces to

\displaystyle b\overline{\nabla}(\omega)+\sigma\circ\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega)=b\overline{\nabla}(\omega)+\mathrm{d}b\otimes\omega.

Thus
\overline{\nabla}
is a connection as claimed.

We now move on to showing that
\overline{\nabla}
is a bimodule connection. Note first that

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}(\omega b)=

\displaystyle\,\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla\circ\ast(\omega b)=\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla(b^{*}\omega^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sigma\circ\ast\Big(\mathrm{d}b^{*}\otimes\omega^{*}+b^{*}\nabla(\omega^{*})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sigma\Big(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b+\nabla(\omega^{*})^{*}b\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sigma(\omega\otimes\mathrm{d}b)+\overline{\nabla}(\omega)b.

Thus we see that
\overline{\nabla}
is a bimodule connection with associated bimodule map
\sigma
.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

A connection
\nabla
is torsion-free if and only if
\overline{\nabla}
is torsion-free.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\nabla
is torsion-free, which is to say, let us assume that
\wedge\circ\nabla=\mathrm{d}
. This is true if and only if

\ast\circ\wedge\circ\nabla\circ\ast=\ast\circ\mathrm{d}\circ\ast.

Now since
\ast\circ\wedge=-\wedge\circ\,\ast
, this is true if and only if

-\wedge\circ\ast\circ\,\nabla\circ\ast=\mathrm{d}\circ\ast^{2}=\mathrm{d}.

Finally, we note that since
\wedge\circ\sigma=-\wedge
, this identity is equivalent to

\wedge\circ\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla\circ\ast=\mathrm{d},

in other words, this is equivalent to

\wedge\,\circ\overline{\nabla}=\mathrm{d},

which is to say, equivalent to the connection
\overline{\nabla}
being torsion-free.
∎

Definition 3.16
.

We say that a bimodule connection
\nabla
is
self-conjugate
if
\nabla=\overline{\nabla}
.

We see that for a self-conjugate connection
\nabla

\displaystyle\nabla\circ\ast=\sigma\circ\ast\circ\nabla.

In other words,
\nabla
does not commute with the
*
-map, but commutes up to an action of the bimodule map
\sigma
.

4.
The Quantum Homogeneous Space Case

We now treat the special case of a quantum homogeneous space, and show how the bimodule map associated to a torsion-free bimodule connection admits a particularily simple form. Remarkably, this bimodule map is independent of the choice of connection.

We use Sweedler notation for Hopf algebra coproducts and coactions. We denote by
\Delta
,
\varepsilon
and
S
the coproduct, counit and antipode of a Hopf algebra respectively. We write
A^{\circ}
for the dual coalgebra (Hopf algebra) of a (Hopf) algebra
A
, and denote the pairing between
A
and
A^{\circ}
by angular brackets. Throughout the paper, all algebras are over
\mathbb{C}
and assumed to be unital, all unadorned tensor products are over
\mathbb{C}
, and all Hopf algebras are assumed to have bijective antipodes.

4.1.
Some Preliminaries on Quantum Homogeneous Spaces

We begin by briefly recalling Takeuchi’s equivalence for relative Hopf modules, see
[
9
, Appendix A]
for more details. For
A
a Hopf algebra, we say that a left coideal subalgebra
B\subseteq A
is a
quantum homogeneous
A
-space
if
A
is faithfully flat as a right
B
-module and
B^{+}A=AB^{+}
, where
B^{+}:=\ker(\varepsilon|_{B})
. We denote by
{}^{A}_{B}\mathrm{mod}
the category of relative Hopf modules which are finitely generated as left
B
-modules, and by
{}^{\pi_{B}}\mathrm{mod}
the category of finite-dimensional left comodules over the Hopf algebra
\pi_{B}(A):=A/B^{+}A
, which we call the
quantum isotropy subgroup
. An equivalence of categories, known as Takeuchi’s equivalence, is given by the functor
\Phi:{}^{A}_{B}\mathrm{mod}\to{}^{\pi_{B}}\mathrm{mod}
, where
\Phi({\mathcal{F}})={\mathcal{F}}/B^{+}{\mathcal{F}}
, for any relative Hopf module
{\mathcal{F}}
, and the functor
\Psi:{}^{\pi_{B}}\mathrm{mod}\to{}^{A}_{B}\mathrm{mod}
is defined using the cotensor product
\square_{\pi_{B}}
over
\pi_{B}(A)
. A unit for the equivalence is given by
\mathrm{U}:{\mathcal{F}}\to(\Psi\circ\Phi)({\mathcal{F}})
, where
\mathrm{U}(f)=f_{(1)}\otimes[f_{(0)}]
, and
[f_{(0)}]
denotes the coset of
f_{(0)}
in
\Phi({\mathcal{F}})
.

Consider next the category
{}^{A}_{B}\textrm{mod}_{0}
consisting of objects

in

endowed with the right

-module structure defined by

, and

. This category is clearly equivalent to

. The category

comes equipped with an evident monoidal structure
\otimes_{B}
, moreover, the category
{}^{\pi_{B}}\mathrm{mod}
comes with the monoidal structure given by tensor products of comodules. For
{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{G}\in{}^{A}_{B}\mathrm{mod}_{0}
, the natural isomorphism

\displaystyle\mu_{{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{G}}:\Phi({\mathcal{F}})\otimes\Phi(\mathcal{G})\to\Phi({\mathcal{F}}\otimes_{B}\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle[f]\otimes[g]\mapsto[f\otimes g].

makes Takeuchi’s equivalence into a monoidal equivalence. See
[
49
, §4]
for further details.

4.2.
Some Preliminaroies on Quantum Homogeneous Tangent Spaces

In this subsection we recall the tangent space formulation of covariant differential calculi over quantum homogeneous spaces. Let
A
be a Hopf algebra, and

be a Hopf subalgebra of

, such that

For the special case where the relative Hopf module is the calcukus itself, we get the following immediate formulae.

4.4.

The question of invertibility of the bimodule map can in practice be difficult to verify. The following proposition says that if our differential calculus is a

Proposition 4.4
.

Let

be a quantum homogeneous space and let

be a left

-covariant

-fodc over

. For left

-covariant connection

is

-invertible. Moreover, we have the explicit identity

\displaystyle\Phi(\overline{\sigma})(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\mathrm{d}c)=\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}\otimes\mathrm{d}\!\left(S^{-1}(c_{(2)})bc_{(1)}\right).

Proof.

Let us first produce an explicit expression for

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\mathrm{d}c)=

\displaystyle\,\ast\circ\sigma(\mathrm{d}c^{*}\otimes\mathrm{d}b^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\ast(\mathrm{d}(c^{*}_{(1)}b^{*}S(c^{*}_{(2)}))\otimes\mathrm{d}c^{*}_{(3)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}\otimes\mathrm{d}(c^{*}_{(1)}b^{*}S(c^{*}_{(2)}))^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}\otimes\mathrm{d}((S(c^{*}_{(2)}))^{*}bc_{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}\otimes\mathrm{d}(S^{-1}(c_{(2)}))bc_{(1)}),

Using this identity, we observe that

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}\circ\sigma^{-1}(\mathrm{d}b\otimes\mathrm{d}c)=

\displaystyle\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}\otimes\mathrm{d}\!\left(S^{-1}(c_{(2)})bc_{(1)})\right)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\!\left(c_{(3)}S^{-1}(c_{(2)})bc_{(1)}S(c_{(4)})\right)\otimes\mathrm{d}c_{(5)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\!\left(bc_{(1)}S(c_{(2)})\right)\otimes\mathrm{d}c_{(3)}

4.6.

Rather than working with

\displaystyle\langle\widecheck{\sigma}(X\otimes Y),v\otimes w\rangle=\langle X\otimes Y,\sigma(v\otimes w)\rangle,

\displaystyle\textrm{ for all \penalty\ }Y\in T.

It follows from the description of

\displaystyle\langle X\otimes Y,\,\sigma([b]\otimes[c])\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\langle\rho_{B}(lE)\otimes\rho_{B}(W),\,[b_{(1)}cS(b_{(3)})]\otimes[b_{(2)}]\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\langle lE,b_{(1)}cS(b_{(3)})\rangle\langle W,b_{(2)}\rangle.

Using the properties of the dual pairing between

5.
The Irreducible Quantum Flag Manifolds

5.1.
Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum groups

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebra of rank
r
. We fix a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
with corresponding root system
\Delta\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
, where
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
denotes the
linear dual of
\mathfrak{h}
. With respect to a choice of simple roots
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{r}\}
, denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the symmetric bilinear form induced on
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
by the Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}
, normalized
so that any shortest simple root
\alpha_{i}
satisfies
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})=2
. The coroot
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
of a simple
root
\alpha_{i}
is defined by

\displaystyle\alpha^{\vee}_{i}:=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{d_{i}}=2\frac{\alpha_{i}}{(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})},\quad\text{where }d_{i}=\frac{(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})}{2}.

The Cartan matrix
\mathcal{A}=(a_{ij})_{ij}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is the
(r\times r)
-matrix defined by
a_{ij}:=(\alpha^{\vee}_{i},\alpha_{j})
. Let
\{\varpi_{1},\cdots\varpi_{r}\}
denote the corresponding set of fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}
, which is to say, the dual basis of the coroots.

Let
q\in\mathbb{R}
such that
q\notin\{-1,0,1\}
, and denote
q_{i}:=q^{d_{i}}
. The quantized universal enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
is the noncommutative associative algebra generated by the
elements
E_{i},F_{i},K_{i}
, and
K_{i}^{-1}
, for
i=1,\cdots,r
,
satisfying the relations (12) - (16) of
[
34
, Chapter 6]
.

A Hopf algebra structure is defined on
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
by

\displaystyle\Delta E_{i}=E_{i}\otimes K_{i}+1\otimes E_{i},\quad\Delta F_{i}=F_{i}\otimes 1+K^{-1}_{i}\otimes F_{i},\quad\Delta K^{\pm}_{i}=K^{\pm}_{i}\otimes K^{\pm}_{i},

\displaystyle S(E_{i})=-E_{i}K^{-1}_{i},\quad S(F_{i})=-K_{i}F_{i},\quad S(K^{\pm}_{i})=K^{\mp}_{i},

\displaystyle\epsilon(K_{i})=1,\quad\epsilon(E_{i})=\epsilon(F_{i})=0.

A Hopf
\ast
-algebra structure, called the compact real form of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, is defined by

\displaystyle K^{\ast}_{i}:=K_{i},\quad E^{\ast}_{i}:=K_{i}F_{i},\quad F^{\ast}_{i}=E_{i}K^{-1}_{i}.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset{\mathfrak{h}}^{\ast}
be the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{+}\subset\mathcal{P}
its set of dominant integral weights.
For each
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
there exists an irreducible finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-module
V_{\mu}
,
uniquely defined by the existence of a vector
v_{\mu}\in V_{\mu}
, which we call a highest weight vector, satisfying

\displaystyle E_{i}\triangleright v_{\mu}=0,\quad K_{i}\triangleright v_{\mu}=q^{(\mu,\alpha^{\vee}_{i})}v_{\mu},\quad\text{for all $i=1,\cdots,r$}.

Moreover,
v_{\mu}
is the unique such element up to scalar multiple. We call any finite direct
sum of such
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-representations a finite-dimensional type-
1
representation.
For further details on Drinfeld–Jimbo quantized enveloping algebras, we refer the reader to the standard
texts
[
34
]
.

5.2.
Quantum Coordinate Algebras and irreducible flag manifolds

In this subsection we recall some necessary material about quantized coordinate algebras.
Let
V
be a finite-dimensional left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-module,
v\in V
, and
f\in V^{*}
, the
\mathbb{C}
-linear dual of
V
, endowed with its right
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
-module
structure.

Consider the function
c^{\tiny{V}}_{f,v}:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathbb{C}
defined by
c^{\tiny{V}}_{f,v}(X):=f\big(X\triangleright v\big)
. The
space of matrix coefficients
of

is the subspace

\displaystyle C(V):=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\!\left\{c^{\tiny{V}}_{f,v}\,|\,v\in V,f\in V^{*}\right\}\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{*}.

Let
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
denote the Hopf dual of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
. It is easily checked that a Hopf subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
is given by

(8)

\mathcal{O}_{q}(G):=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{+}}C(V_{\mu}).

We call
\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)
the
quantum coordinate algebra of
G
, where
G
is the compact, connected, simply-connected, simple Lie group having
\mathfrak{g}
as its complexified Lie algebra.
We note that
\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)
is a cosemisimple Hopf algebra by construction.

Now we recall the definition of quantized flag manifolds. For
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in S}\subseteq\Pi
a subset of simple roots, consider the Hopf
*
-subalgebra

\displaystyle U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S}):=\big<K_{i},E_{j},F_{j}\,|\,i=1,\ldots,r;j\in S\big>.

The category of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-modules is known to be semisimple. The Hopf
\ast
-algebra embedding
\iota_{S}:U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})\hookrightarrow U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
induces the dual Hopf
*
-algebra
map
\iota_{S}^{\circ}:U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\circ}
. By construction
\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
, so we can consider the restriction map

\displaystyle\pi_{S}:=\iota_{S}^{\circ}|_{\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)}:\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\circ},

and the Hopf
*
-subalgebra

\mathcal{O}_{q}(L_{S}):=\pi_{S}\big(\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)\big)\subseteq U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})^{\circ}.

The
quantum flag manifold associated
to
S
is the quantum homogeneous space associated to the surjective Hopf
*
-algebra map
\pi_{S}:\mathcal{O}_{q}(G)\to\mathcal{O}_{q}(L_{S})
. We denote it by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{q}\big(G/L_{S}\big):=\mathcal{O}_{q}\big(G\big)^{\mathrm{co}\left(\mathcal{O}_{q}(L_{S})\right)}.

Since the category of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})
-modules is semisimple,
\mathcal{O}_{q}(L_{S})
must be a cosemisimple Hopf algebra.

Definition 5.1
.

A quantum flag manifold is
irreducible
if the defining subset of simple roots is of the form

S=\{1,\dots,r\}\setminus\{s\}

where
\alpha_{s}
has coefficient
1
in the expansion of the highest root of
\mathfrak{g}
.

5.4.
Some General Eigenvalue Calculations

In this subsection we produce two families of eigenvectors for the general case of the irreducible quantum flag manifolds. Moreover, we calculate their explicit eigenvalues.

The first family of eigenvectors are the lowest elements of
T^{(0,1)}\otimes T^{(0,1)}
, given by the tensor product of the lowest weight vector of
T^{(0,1)}
with itself.

Lemma 5.3
.

For a cominiscule root
\alpha_{x}
, with Heckenberger–Kolb anti-holomorphic tangent space
T^{(0,1)}=U_{q}(\mathfrak{l}_{S})E_{x}
, a lowest weight vector of
T^{(0,1)}\otimes T^{(0,1)}
is given by

E_{x}\otimes E_{x}.

Proof.

Since
F_{i}E_{x}=E_{x}F_{i}
, for all
i\neq x
, we see that
E_{x}
is a lowest weight vector of
T^{(0,1)}
. Hence if follows from the coproduct formula for
F_{i}
, that
E_{x}\otimes E_{x}
is a lowest weight vector of
T^{(0,1)}\otimes T^{(0,1)}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

The element
E_{x}\otimes E_{x}
is an eigenvector of
\widecheck{\sigma}
, explicitly

\widecheck{\sigma}(E_{x})=q^{(\alpha_{x},\alpha_{x})}E_{x}.

Proof.

The action of
\widecheck{\sigma}

E_{x}\otimes E_{x}
is given by

(9)

\displaystyle\widecheck{\sigma}(E_{x}\otimes E_{x})=S(K_{x})E_{x}\otimes E_{x}=K_{x}^{-1}E_{x}\otimes E_{x}=q^{-(\alpha_{x},\alpha_{x})}E_{x}\otimes E_{x},

giving the claimed identity.
∎

The second family of eigenvectors are again lowest weight vectors. Their form varies somewhat across the series, but they have a clearly similar form.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\alpha
be a cominiscule root, with Heckenberger–Kolb anti-holomorphic tangent space

1.

If
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
, then a lowest weight vector of

is given by

E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1}-q^{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}E_{1}\otimes E_{2}E_{!}.

2.

If
\alpha=\alpha_{l}
, then lowest weight vector of
T^{(0,1)}\otimes T^{(0,1)}
is given by

E_{l-1}E_{l}\otimes E_{l}-q^{(\alpha_{l-1},\alpha_{l})}E_{l-1}E_{l}\otimes E_{l}.

3.

If
\alpha=\alpha_{r}
, for

T^{(0,1)}\otimes T^{(0,1)}
are

(10)

\displaystyle E_{r+1}E_{r}\otimes E_{r}-q^{(\alpha_{r+1},\alpha_{r})}E_{r+1}E_{r}\otimes E_{r},

\displaystyle E_{r=1}E_{r}\otimes E_{r}-q^{(\alpha_{r-1},\alpha_{r})}E_{r-1}E_{r}\otimes E_{r}.

Proof.

We demonstrate the proof for the first case, the proof for the other two cases being completely analogous. Note first that, for any
j\notin\{1,2\}

F_{j}(E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1}-q^{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}E_{1}\otimes E_{2}E_{1})=0.

We move next to the case of

\displaystyle F_{2}(E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1}-q^{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}E_{1}\otimes E_{2}E_{1})=F_{2}E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1}-q^{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}K_{2}^{-1}E_{1}\otimes F_{2}E_{2}E_{1}.

\displaystyle\dfrac{(K_{2}^{-1}-K_{2})E_{1}}{q^{d_{2}}-q^{-d_{2}}}\otimes E_{1}-q^{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}K_{2}^{-1}E_{1}\otimes\dfrac{(K_{2}^{-1}-K_{2})E_{1}}{q^{d_{2}}-q^{-d_{2}}}

∎

All three vectors are eigenvectors of
\widecheck{\sigma}

-1

Proof.

We again prove the statement for the first eigenvector, as the proof for the other three is completely analogous.

For the first summand of

\displaystyle\widecheck{\sigma}(E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1})=E_{2}K_{1}^{-1}K_{2}^{-1}E_{1}\otimes K_{2}E_{1}+K_{1}^{-1}K_{2}^{-1}E_{1}\otimes E_{2}E_{1}-K_{1}^{-1}E_{2}K_{2}^{-1}E_{1}\otimes E_{1},

\displaystyle(q^{-(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})}-q^{-2(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})-(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})})E_{2}E_{1}\otimes E_{1}+q^{-(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})-(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})}E_{1}\otimes E_{2}E_{1}.
