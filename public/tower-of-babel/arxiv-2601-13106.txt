Product-State Approximation Algorithms for the Transverse Field Ising Model
Vincenzo Lipardi, David Mestel, Georgios Stamoulis

Abstract
We study classical polynomial-time approximation algorithms for the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) Hamiltonian, allowing a mixture of ferromagnetic and anti-ferromagnetic interactions between pairs of qbits, alongside transverse field terms with arbitrary non-negative weights. Our main results are a series of approximation algorithms (all approximation ratios with respect to the true quantum optimum): (i) a simple maximum of two product state rounding algorithm achieving an approximation ratio γ ≈ 0.71 \gamma\approx 0.71 , (ii) a strengthened rounding, inspired by the anticommutation property of the two X i , Z i ​ Z j X_{i},Z_{i}Z_{j} observables achieving ratio γ ≈ 0.7860 \gamma\approx 0.7860 , and (iii) a further improvement by interpolation achieving ratio γ ≈ 0.8156 \gamma\approx 0.8156 . We also give an explicit (purely ferromagnetic) TFIM instance on three qbits for which every product state achieves at most 169 / 180 ≈ 0.9389 169/180\approx 0.9389 of the true optimum, yielding an upper bound for all algorithms producing product state approximations, even in the purely ferromagnetic case.

Introduction

Local Hamiltonians
are operators associated with the total energy of quantum many-body systems and are represented by Hermitian matrices. Even though general Hamiltonians can have exponentially large descriptions with respect to the number of interacting particles, local Hamiltonians can be succinctly represented as the summation of only the non-trivial locally interacting terms, which are usually very small (2 or 3).

The
Local Hamiltonian problem
asks to determine whether the
ground state energy
(associated with the smallest eigenvalue of the operator) of a given local Hamiltonian is below a certain threshold
\alpha
or above a different threshold
\beta
, with a promise that one of these two conditions holds. This problem is the quantum analogue of classical constraint satisfaction problems
[
12
]
. In a seminal work
[
22
]
Kitaev showed that computing the ground state energy of such Hamiltonians is a
QMA
-hard problem
[
6
]
, even when the interactions are restricted in an one-dimensional line and the dimension of each particle is bounded by 12
[
19
,
1
,
2
]
. Such problems are believed to be intractable even for quantum computers (if and when they might be built).

In recent years, the study of Local Hamiltonians has also attracted growing interest within the computer science community. Given the fundamental importance and computational intractability of the problem, research efforts have focused on finding
approximation algorithms
: efficient algorithms designed to estimate the ground-state energy by producing low-energy states that approximate the true ground state within provable performance guarantees.

These algorithms typically output product states or states that can be efficiently represented on a classical device, such as Matrix Product States. Although the ‘ansatzes’ from which these states are drawn are not fully general, they are frequently relevant in practice, for example in the efficient simulation of material properties in quantum chemistry.

The Transverse Field Ising model (TFIM) is a well-known and well-studied quantum spin system consisting of competing
Ising
interactions and
transverse fields
. An instance consisting of
n
interacting particles (qubits) can be represented by a simple, non-directed simple graph
G=(V,E)
with vertex set
V
, one for each particle, and edge set
E
, one for each pair of interacting particles. The TFIM Hamiltonian is defined as

H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}-\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}X_{i},\qquad J_{ij}\in\{-1,+1\},w_{i,j},h_{i}\geq 0.

(1)

where
X_{i},Z_{i}
are Pauli operators on qubit
i
,
J_{ij}
are arbitrarily signed couplings and
h_{i}
are the single-qubit transverse-field strengths.

This paper studies
multiplicative
approximation algorithms, which are subtle in quantum Hamiltonians because, for instance, the ground state energy of the Hamiltonian as written in (
1
) could be negative or even zero. More generally, adding a constant multiple of the identity changes multiplicative approximability even though it does not change the ground state. This is the same classical phenomenon behind “Ising energy” versus “Max-Cut” objectives, and is explicitly discussed in the quantum setting (i.e., Gharibian-Parekh
[
14
]
for the Heisenberg model). Consequently, we fix an equivalent maximization version of the problem in which the Hamiltonian is a weighted sum of local
projections
(taking values between 0 and 1), and we approximate its maximum eigenvalue.

Given the equivalent formulation of the TFIM problem, we design classical polynomial-time algorithms outputting explicit
product states
(unentangled states) achieving a guaranteed fraction of the true quantum optimum. Product states are a natural restricted ansatz (mean-field) and are algorithmically attractive due to efficient description and evaluation. Indeed, a product state can be described with polynomially many bits (in the input instance size), as opposed to entangled states which typically require exponentially many classical bits to be described. A central question is:
how well can product states approximate the true optimum of TFIM instances?

Our Contributions.

We give three constant-factor approximation algorithms and one product state upper bound:

1.

One simple, baseline algorithm achieving
0.7154

2.

We introduce an SDP relaxation strengthened by

We again define two rounded product-state candidates and return the maximum of the two: a standard hyperplane rounding, and a second rounding that uses the SOC tradeoff to lower bound the

The third algorithm performs interpolation on the

The Model and its relaxation

On one qbit, let

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{I}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

On
n
qubits,
X_{i}
denotes
X
acting on qbit
i
tensored with identity on others; similarly for
Z_{i}
.
Pauli operators satisfy
X_{i}^{2}=Z_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, and they anticommute when acting on the same qubit
X_{i}Z_{i}=-Z_{i}X_{i}
, while they commute when acting on different qbits.

A (mixed) quantum state on
n
qbits is a density matrix
\rho\succeq 0
with
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1
.
For a Hermitian observable
A
, its expectation value is denoted
\langle A\rangle_{\rho}:=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)
.
A
product state
is
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_{n}
. Any single-qubit state can be written in the Bloch form

\rho_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}+x_{i}X+y_{i}Y+z_{i}Z\right),\quad\mbox{ such that }x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}+z_{i}^{2}\leq 1.

We note that
\langle X_{i}\rangle_{\rho_{i}}=x_{i}
and
\langle Z_{i}\rangle_{\rho_{i}}=z_{i}
.

2.1
Formulating the approximation problem

A fundamental computational task is to approximate the ground-state energy
\lambda_{\min}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}})
and to output a quantum state (in our case, a
product state
) whose energy is close to optimum (ground state). For classical approximation algorithms, however, the “usual” multiplicative approximation ratio for minimization is not well suited for quantum Hamiltonians because the ground energy can be negative, or even zero. To obtain a robust notion of approximation we transform our
H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}
to an equivalent
maximization
formulation built from positive semidefinite (PSD) “constraint” operators. This affine shift does not change the physics of the model, it only transforms the Hamiltonian to one where the approximation mechanics make sense. The idea is to replace each local term by a PSD operator that rewards the
locally energy-minimizing
eigenspace, so that the global objective becomes a sum of nonnegative constraint values. This provides a natural scale for multiplicative approximation guarantees.

For an edge
\{i,j\}\in E
, the operator
J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}
has eigenvalues
+1/-1
. The contribution of this edge to (
1
) is minimized when
J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}=-1
. This can happen in two ways: either
J_{ij}=+1
in which case the corresponding vertices would like to be
anti
-aligned (an ‘antiferromagnetic’ interaction), or
J_{ij}=-1
in which case
i
and
j
would like to be aligned in the
z
-direction (a ‘ferromagnetic’ interaction). We define the PSD projector onto this minimizing eigenspace:

\Pi_{ij}^{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}-J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}\bigr).

This operator has eigenvalues
\{0,1\}
and equals
1
exactly when the edge is “satisfied” in the energy-minimizing sense.

Similarly for the field “constraints”, for a vertex
i\in V
the single-qbit operator
-h_{i}X_{i}
is minimized when
X_{i}=+1
(the
|+\rangle
eigenstate of
X
). We define the PSD projector onto this minimizing eigenspace:

\Pi_{i}^{X}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}+X_{i}\bigr).

Again
\Pi_{i}^{X}
has eigenvalues
\{0,1\}
.

We can now define the equivalent tranfsformed Hamiltonian by summing these PSD constraints with the same nonnegative weights as in the TFIM:

H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\Pi_{ij}^{Z}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\,\Pi_{i}^{X}.

(2)

By construction we have that
H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}\succeq 0
and that
\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}|\psi\rangle
is the energy of the state
|\psi\rangle
. By expanding (
2
) we immediately see that that maximizing
H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}
is exactly equivalent to minimizing
H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}
, up to an affine shift. Indeed,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\Pi_{ij}^{Z}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\,\Pi_{i}^{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\frac{w_{ij}}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}-J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}\bigr)+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}+X_{i}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\Big)\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{i,j)\}\in E}J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\Bigr)\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}X_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\Bigr)\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{2}H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}.

Taking maximum eigenvalues gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\bigl(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\bigl(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}\bigr)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\bigl(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\Bigr)-2\lambda_{\max}\big(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}\big).

Moreover,
H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}
and
H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}
have the same eigenvectors, so any state that approximately maximizes (
2
) immediately yields an approximate ground state for (
1
).

The shift from (
1
) to (
2
) serves two purposes. First, it gives us an objective that is a nonnegative sum of PSD local terms so that we can talk about multiplicative approximation ratios in a meaningful way. Second, the constraint form (
2
) reveals the competing and incompatible local preferences of the transverse-field and the Ising (edge) terms, which would be the core of our Second Order Programming relaxations in subsequent sections. In the remainder of the paper we design classical algorithms that, given a signed TFIM instance, round a suitable semidefinite relaxation to produce product states with provable constant factor guarantees for
\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}})
, and hence for the TFIM ground-state energy.

Remark 1
.

Because
Z_{i}Z_{j}
has eigenvalues
\pm 1
, the operator
J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}
has eigenvalues
\pm J_{ij}
. Therefore
\Pi^{Z}_{ij}
has eigenvalues
(1\pm J_{ij})/2
.
Thus
\Pi^{Z}_{ij}
is a
projector
(eigenvalues
\{0,1\}
) if and only if
J_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
. If one only needs PSD constraints (not necessarily projectors), then it suffices that
|J_{ij}|\leq 1
; however the cleanest tranformation uses
\{\pm 1\}
signs and separates weights/magnitudes into
w_{ij}
.

Remark 2
.

Allowing arbitrary signs
J_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
includes frustrated “spin-glass” instances. A standard invariant is the sign product along a cycle: if for some cycle
C
,

\prod_{(i,j)\in C}J_{ij}=-1
, then the instance is frustrated (no gauge transform can make all signs
+1
).

Remark 3
.

In the case where all
h_{i}=0
and all
J_{ij}=-1
we have that
\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}})=\textsc{MaxCut}(G)
. Since the latter is known to be UGC-hard to approximate with approximation ratio better than
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\approx 0.8786

[
20
]
, we have the same inapproximability result for
\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}})
.

3
Warm-up: a
0.7154
two-candidate product state approximation algorithm

We start with a simple algorithm that already achieves
0.7154
approximation guarantee. In the next section we will refine it by explicitly accounting for the incompatibility between the two types of constraints (Ising vs. field), i.e., the fact that the corresponding observables anti-commute. The present algorithm simply defines two easy product state solutions: one that tries to gain as much as possible on the Ising terms while essentially ignoring the field terms, and a second one that does the opposite i.e., ignores the Ising terms but maximizes the field terms. We then output the better of the two. Recall that

H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\Pi^{Z}_{ij}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X},\qquad\mathrm{OPT}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}).

We also define

H=\sum_{i\in V}h_{i},\qquad H_{Z}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\Pi^{Z}_{ij},\qquad\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{Z}).

Since
H_{Z}
is diagonal in the computational (
Z
) basis,
\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}
is achieved by a
Z
-basis product state (i.e., a classical
\{\pm 1\}
assignment). Moreover, each
\Pi^{Z}_{ij}\preceq\mathbb{I}
, so
\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}\leq|E|
.

The Field Solution:

The field projector is
\Pi_{i}^{X}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+X_{i})
, which is maximized when
X_{i}
has eigenvalue
+1
, i.e., on the state
\left|+\right\rangle=\frac{\left|0\right\rangle+\left|1\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
. We therefore define the field solution
\mathrm{SOL}^{F}
to be the product state
\left|+\right\rangle^{\otimes n}=\left|\psi\right\rangle
. On this state, every field term is fully satisfied:

\langle\psi|h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}|\psi\rangle=h_{i}.

On the other hand,
\langle\psi|Z_{i}Z_{j}|\psi\rangle=0
and so

\langle\psi|\Pi^{Z}_{ij}|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for every }\{i,j\}\in E,

independent of
J_{ij}
. Therefore,

\mathrm{SOL}^{F}=\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}}{2}+H\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}}{2}+H,

where the last inequality uses
\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}\leq|E|
.

The Ising Solution:

The Ising solution
\mathrm{SOL}^{I}
complements the above by focusing on the edge clauses. Maximizing
\sum_{\{i,j\}}\Pi^{Z}_{ij}
over
ZZ
-basis product states is exactly the classical
signed MaxCut
(equivalently Max2XOR) objective: if
J_{ij}=+1
the clause prefers anti-alignment, and if
J_{ij}=-1
it prefers alignment. The Goemans–Williamson hyperplane rounding applies unchanged in this setting (it is the same SDP and the same rounding guarantee), giving the approximation ratio
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\approx 0.878567
. Thus, we can compute in polynomial time a
ZZ
-basis product state
\rho^{I}
such that

\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}H_{Z}]\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}.

For the field terms, since
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}X_{i}]=\langle X_{i}\rangle_{\rho^{I}}=0
on any
Z
-basis state, we have

\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}\,h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}]=h_{i}\cdot\frac{1+\langle X_{i}\rangle_{\rho^{I}}}{2}=\frac{h_{i}}{2},

and so we get

\mathrm{SOL}^{I}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}H_{Z}]+\sum_{i}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{I}h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}]\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}+\frac{H}{2}.

We output the better of the two product states (i.e., the larger value between
\mathrm{SOL}^{F}
and
\mathrm{SOL}^{I}
).

Theorem 1
.

Taking the maximum of the two product state solutions above gives an approximation algorithm for TFIM with approximation ratio
1-1/4\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\approx 0.7154
.

Proof.

First, by subadditivity of the maximum eigenvalue,

\mathrm{OPT}=\lambda_{\max}\Big(H_{Z}+\sum_{i}h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}\Big)\leq\lambda_{\max}(H_{Z})+\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{i}h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}\Big)=\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}+H,

since
\lambda_{\max}(\Pi_{i}^{X})=1
and the
\Pi_{i}^{X}
act on different qbits.

Define

t=\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}}{H+\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}}\leq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}}{\mathrm{OPT}},

and hence correspondingly
1-t=\frac{H}{H+\mathrm{OPT}_{Z}}\leq\frac{H}{\mathrm{OPT}}
. Then we have

\frac{\mathrm{SOL}^{I}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot t+\frac{1}{2}(1-t)=\frac{1}{2}+t\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

and

\frac{\mathrm{SOL}^{F}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\frac{t}{2}+1-t=1-\frac{t}{2}.

Hence the returned value satisfies

\frac{\max\{\mathrm{SOL}^{I},\mathrm{SOL}^{F}\}}{\mathrm{OPT}}\geq\min_{t\in[0,1]}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}+t\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-\frac{1}{2}\right),1-\frac{t}{2}\right\}.

The first argument is an increasing function of
t
and the second a decreasing function, so the minimum is at their intersection with
t=t^{*}=\frac{1}{2\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}}
and approximation ratio

1-\frac{t^{*}}{2}=1-\frac{1}{4\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}}\approx 0.7154457.

∎

4
A SOC-SDP relaxation upper-bounding the true quantum optimum

4.1
The anticommutation tradeoff inequality

The key local geometric constraint comes from anticommutation. Intuitively, a single qbit cannot simultaneously have large
X
-polarization and large
Z
-polarization. In our setting, this translates into a quantitative tradeoff between
\langle X_{i}\rangle
and any incident correlation
\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
A,B
be Hermitian operators with
A^{2}=B^{2}=\mathbb{I}
and
AB=-BA
. Then for every state
\rho
,

\langle A\rangle_{\rho}^{2}+\langle B\rangle_{\rho}^{2}\leq 1.

Proof.

Let
a=\langle A\rangle_{\rho}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho A]
and
b=\langle B\rangle_{\rho}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho B]
, and define
M=aA+bB
. Using
A^{2}=B^{2}=\mathbb{I}
and
AB=-BA
, we get

M^{2}=a^{2}A^{2}+b^{2}B^{2}+ab(AB+BA)=(a^{2}+b^{2})\mathbb{I},

hence
\|M\|=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
. On the other hand, because of the linearity of the trace

\operatorname{Tr}[\rho M]=a\operatorname{Tr}[\rho A]+b\operatorname{Tr}[\rho B]=a^{2}+b^{2}.

Since
|\operatorname{Tr}[\rho M]|\leq\|M\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max\{|\lambda_{\min}(M)|,|\lambda_{\max}(M)|\}
for Hermitian
M
, we obtain
a^{2}+b^{2}\leq\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
i.e.,
a^{2}+b^{2}\leq 1
.
∎

4.2
The relaxation

We now define a convex relaxation that upper-bounds the
true quantum optimum

\mathrm{OPT}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}})
. The relaxation uses variables for (i) single-qbit
X
-expectations
x_{i}\approx\langle X_{i}\rangle
, and (ii) pairwise
ZZ
correlations
c_{ij}\approx\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle
.
Lemma
1
yields the SOC constraints relation between
x_{i}
and
c_{ij}
on each edge incident to
i
by substituting
A=X_{i}
and
B=Z_{i}Z_{j}
and observing that they anticommute.

Recall from Section 2 that the edge constraints are

\Pi_{ij}^{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}-J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}\bigr),\qquad\Pi_{i}^{X}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbb{I}+X_{i}\bigr).

Accordingly, for each edge
\{i,j\}
we define for convenience the signed variable

t_{ij}:=-J_{ij}c_{ij},

so that the (relaxed) edge constraint value is exactly
(1+t_{ij})/2
, matching
\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]
.

Definition 1

(SOC-SDP relaxation)
.

Let
n=|V|
. The relaxation has variables
x_{i}\in[-1,1]
and a Gram matrix
C=(c_{ij})
with
c_{ii}=1
.
Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
be unit vectors such that
c_{ij}=\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle
, and define

t_{ij}:=-J_{ij}c_{ij}\in[-1,1].

Maximize

\mathrm{SDP}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\,\frac{1+t_{ij}}{2}+\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\,\frac{1+x_{i}}{2}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\mathrm{SDP}_{E}[i,j]+\sum_{i\in V}\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i]

(3)

subject to:

\displaystyle C=(C_{ij})=(c_{ij})

\displaystyle\succeq 0,

(4)

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{i},u_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1\qquad\forall i\in V,

(5)

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\in[-1,1]\qquad\forall i\in V,

(6)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{2}+c_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1,\quad x_{j}^{2}+c_{ij}^{2}\leq 1\qquad\forall\{i,j\}\in E.

(7)

The next lemma states that every quantum state induces feasible moments for the SOC-SDP with same objective function value i.e., the SOC-SDP is an upper bound on the true
\mathrm{OPT}
even for arbitrary signed/frustrated patterns
J_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Lemma 2
.

For every state
\rho
on
n
qbits, there exists a feasible solution to Definition
1
with objective value exactly
\operatorname{Tr}[H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}}\rho]
.
Consequently,

\mathrm{SDP}\geq\mathrm{OPT}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}).

This holds for
arbitrary
sign patterns
J_{ij}\in\{+1,-1\}
.

Proof.

Let us fix an arbitrary quantum state
\rho
and let us define

x_{i}:=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho X_{i}],\qquad c_{ij}:=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho Z_{i}Z_{j}].

Since Pauli operators have operator norm
1
, we have
|x_{i}|\leq 1
and
|c_{ij}|\leq 1
.

PSD constraints:

Let
C=(c_{ij})
with
c_{ii}=1
. For any
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

b^{\top}Cb=\sum_{i,j}b_{i}b_{j}\,\operatorname{Tr}[\rho Z_{i}Z_{j}]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho\left(\sum_{i}b_{i}Z_{i}\right)^{2}\right]\geq 0,

so
C\succeq 0
and admits a Gram representation
c_{ij}=\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle
with
\|u_{i}\|=1
.

SOC constraints:

Fix an edge
\{i,j\}\in E
. Let
A=X_{i}
and
B=Z_{i}Z_{j}
. Then
A^{2}=B^{2}=\mathbb{I}
and
AB=-BA
.
By Lemma
1
,

\operatorname{Tr}[\rho X_{i}]^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}[\rho Z_{i}Z_{j}]^{2}\leq 1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{i}^{2}+c_{ij}^{2}\leq 1.

The same argument with
X_{j}
gives
x_{j}^{2}+c_{ij}^{2}\leq 1
.

Objective function value:

Using
\Pi_{ij}^{Z}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}-J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j})
and
\Pi_{i}^{X}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+X_{i})
and linearity of trace,

\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]=\frac{1-J_{ij}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho Z_{i}Z_{j}]}{2}=\frac{1+(-J_{ij}c_{ij})}{2}=\frac{1+t_{ij}}{2},\qquad\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{i}^{X}\rho]=\frac{1+\operatorname{Tr}[\rho X_{i}]}{2}=\frac{1+x_{i}}{2}.

Therefore the SOC-SDP objective equals
\operatorname{Tr}[H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}}\rho]
. Taking
\rho
to be an optimal eigenstate (ground state) for
\mathrm{OPT}
yields
\mathrm{SDP}\geq\mathrm{OPT}
.
∎

In the analysis below, we will solve the SOC-SDP relaxation up to some arbitrary precision
\epsilon\geq 0
to obtain a feasible solution consisting of vectors
x_{i}
and a Gram matrix
(C)_{ij}
. We denote by SDP the objective function value obtained (over the instance we are solving) and we further decompose SDP into the edge Ising terms and the field terms:

\mathrm{SDP}=\mathrm{SDP}_{E}+\mathrm{SDP}_{X}=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\mathrm{SDP}_{E}[i,j]+\sum_{i\in V}\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i]

5
A 0.786 approximation algorithm based on SOC-SDP

Our algorithm computes two randomized product-state candidates from the
same
SOC-SDP solution and returns the better one.
The point of having two candidates is that different instances can be edge-dominated or field-dominated:

•

Candidate A (pure-
Z
solution):
ignores the SDP
x_{i}
values and uses a Goemans-Williamson style hyperplane rounding on the edge constraints
Z_{i}Z_{j}
. This gives us a strong approximation guarantee on edge constraints but only a trivial factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
on the field constraints.

•

Candidate B (
X
,
ZZ
mixed solution):
this solution matches the SDP field marginals exactly (hence achieves factor
1
on field constraints) and uses the remaining budget according to Lemma
1
in order to round the edge constraints.

We then take the better of the two values to get a
0.786
approximation factor.

5.1
A pure
Z
solution

Algorithm
A
, given some TFIM input instance for the transformed hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{TFIM^{\prime}}}
, solves the corresponding SOC-SDP to obtain a feasible solution, then ignores the
x_{i}
variables and uses pure a
ZZ
-basis rounding.

Algorithm A

Pure
ZZ
solution

Input:
Unit vectors
u_{i}
from the SOC-SDP solution.

1.

Sample
g\sim N(0,\mathbb{I})
.

2.

Set
s_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\left\langle g,u_{i}\right\rangle)
for all
i
.

3.

Output the product state
\rho=\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}
where

\rho_{i}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{I}+s_{i}Z_{i}\Big),

(so we have that
\langle X_{i}\rangle=0,\langle Z_{i}\rangle=s_{i}
).

In this algorithm, we crucially use Grothendieck’s identity (see for example Lemma 3.6.5, page 82 from
[
28
]
), which is described in the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(Grothendieck’s Identity)
.

Let
u_{i},u_{j}
be arbitrary unit vectors,
g\sim N(0,\mathbb{I})
be a random vector, and
s_{i},s_{j}
be their random signs obtained by the hyperplane rounding according to
s_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\left\langle g,u_{i}\right\rangle)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[s_{i}s_{j}]=K(\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle),\text{ where }K(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(t).

The following is also a standard inequality used in the analysis of the Goemans-Williamson
MaxCut
approximation algorithm:

Definition 2
.

For
t\in[0,1]
let

R_{A}(t)=\frac{1+K(t)}{1+t},\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}=\min_{t\in[0,1]}R_{A}(t)\approx 0.878567.

Now we analyze the value of each term for the solution produced by Algorithm
A
compared to its value in the SDP solution. We start with the field terms in Lemma
4
followed by the Ising terms in Lemma
5
.

Lemma 4
.

For each vertex
i
we have that
\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi^{X}_{i}\rho]=\frac{1}{2}
. Hence we have

\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\,\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi^{X}_{i}\rho]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\,\frac{1+x_{i}}{2}=\frac{\mathrm{SDP}_{X}}{2},

i.e. the total field contribution is at least
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}
.

Proof.

By construction
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho X_{i}]=0
, so
\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi^{X}_{i}\rho]=(1+0)/2=1/2\geq(1+x_{i})/4
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
t_{ij}:=-J_{ij}\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle
. Then for each edge
\{i,j\}\in E
,

\mathbb{E}\big[w_{ij}\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]\big]=w_{ij}\frac{1+K(t_{ij})}{2}\geq w_{ij}\cdot\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\frac{1+t_{ij}}{2}=\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}[i,j].

Hence the total Ising contribution is at least
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}
.

Proof.

Since
\rho
is a product state with
\langle Z_{i}\rangle=s_{i}
, we have
\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle=s_{i}s_{j}
and

\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]=\frac{1-J_{ij}\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle}{2}=\frac{1-J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}}{2}.

Taking expectation and applying Grothendieck’s Identity (Lemma
3
),

\mathbb{E}[-J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}]=-J_{ij}K(\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle)=K(-J_{ij}\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle)=K(t_{ij}),

since
K
is odd. This gives
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]]=(1+K(t_{ij}))/2
.
The inequality with
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
holds by definition of
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
on
t\in[0,1]
. For
t\leq 0
the ratio is at least
1
.
∎

Combining the two previous lemmas gives the following guarantee for Algorithm
A
:

Theorem 2
.

The product state
\rho^{(A)}
output by Algorithm
A
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}}\rho^{(A)})]\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X},

where
\mathrm{SDP}_{E}
and
\mathrm{SDP}_{X}
denote the edge and field parts of the SOC-SDP objective.

5.2
A mixed
X
,
ZZ

Algorithm

matches the SDP field marginals exactly and uses the remaining “budget” according to Lemma

to round the
Z
-marginals for edges. Thus, it will be easy to see that it achieves approximation factor 1 on the field terms, and in the following (Lemma

Algorithm B

X
,
ZZ
mixed solution

Input:
SOC-SDP solution
(x_{i},u_{i})
from Definition

1.

For each vertex

\alpha_{i}=\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}
.

2.

Sample
g\sim N(0,\mathbb{I})
.

3.

.

4.

\rho=\bigotimes_{i\in V}\rho_{i}

Lemma 6
.

For each vertex

Proof.

\Pi_{i}^{X}=(\mathbb{I}+X_{i})/2

Lemma 7
.

Let
t_{ij}:=-J_{ij}\left\langle u_{i},u_{j}\right\rangle
and
\alpha_{i}=\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}
as in Algorithm

. Then for every edge
\{i,j\}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]\big]=\frac{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}K(t_{ij})}{2}.

Proof.

Because

is a product state, we have that
\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle_{\rho}=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{i}Z_{i}]\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{j}Z_{j}]=(\alpha_{i}s_{i})(\alpha_{j}s_{j})
.
Therefore

\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]=\frac{1-J_{ij}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}s_{i}s_{j}}{2}.

Taking expectation and applying Grothendick’s Identity (Lemma

gives
\mathbb{E}[-J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}]=K(t_{ij})
and hence the desired bound.
∎

We also need the following Lemma that relates the SDP Ising values

with the“leftover” rounded values

Lemma 8
.

If

Proof.

The SOC anticommutation constraint (

Thus

Definition 3
.

For

achieved at

.

Finally, we can analyse the contribution of the Ising terms as compared to the SDP solution.

Lemma 9
.

For each edge
\{i,j\}

For

and
8

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{ij}^{Z}\rho]]=\frac{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}K(t_{ij})}{2}\geq\frac{1+t_{ij}^{2}K(t_{ij})}{2}=R_{B}(t_{ij})\cdot\frac{1+t_{ij}}{2}\geq\beta\cdot\frac{1+t_{ij}}{2}.

For

and

1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}K(t_{ij})\geq 1+K(t_{ij})
.
Moreover, for

, i.e. the ratio is at least

.
∎

Theorem 3
.

The product state
\rho^{(B)}

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{TFIM}^{\prime}}\rho^{(B)})]\geq\beta\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}+1\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}.

5.3
Combining Algorithms
A
&
B

Proof idea.

Algorithm
A
is strong on edges (factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
) but weak on fields (trivial factor of only
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
) while Algorithm
B
is perfect on fields (factor
1
) but weaker on edges (factor
\beta
). The SOC-SDP value decomposes into an “edge portion”
\mathrm{SDP}_{Z}
and a “field portion”
\mathrm{SDP}_{X}
. Let
p
to be the fraction of the SDP coming from edges allows us to express each candidate’s guarantee into an affine function of
p
. Taking the better candidate yields the maximum of these affine functions and the worst-case over instances is the minimum of that maximum over
p\in[0,1]
.

Theorem 4

(Two-candidate product rounding)
.

The algorithm which on an input TFIM instance executes Algortihms A and B and returns whichever of the two candidate outputs has higher energy achieves approximation ratio

\gamma=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-1/2}{\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+1/2-\beta}\approx 0.786

(where
\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}
and
\beta
are as defined in Definitions
2
and
3
respectively).

Proof.

Recall that we have

\mathrm{SDP}=\mathrm{SDP}_{E}+\mathrm{SDP}_{X}.

Let

p=\frac{\mathrm{SDP}_{E}}{\mathrm{SDP}}\in[0,1],

the fraction of the optimal solution energy represented by the Ising part, and correspondingly
1-p=\frac{\mathrm{SDP}_{X}}{\mathrm{SDP}}
is the fraction represented by the field part.

Now we can express the two candidate guarantees in terms of
p
:

Candidate A:

From Theorem
2
we have

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{CSP}}\rho^{(A)})]\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}\Rightarrow\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{CSP}}\rho^{(A)})]}{\mathrm{SDP}}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\Big(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cdot p.

Candidate B:

From Theorem
3
we have

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{CSP}}\rho^{(B)})]\geq\beta\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{E}+1\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}\Rightarrow\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{CSP}}\rho^{(B)})]}{\mathrm{SDP}}\geq 1-(1-\beta)\cdot p.

Finally, we can take the best of the two solutions and calculate the worst-case guarantee. Then we have that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}(H_{\mathrm{CSP}}\rho)]}{\mathrm{SDP}}\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot p,1-(1-\beta)\cdot p\right\}.

Note that the two terms inside the maximum are respectively increasing and decreasing functions of
p
. They intersect at
p^{\star}=\frac{1/2}{\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}}+1/2-\beta}
, giving an overall worst-case approximation factor of
\gamma=1-(1-\beta)p^{*}\approx 0.786016
.

∎

An improved
0.8156

For the algorithm in the previous section we considered the two options of either setting each qubit to the purely

.

We proceed with the analysis, in the same way as in Algorithm

. We start by analyzing the field term contribution to the objective function.

Lemma 10
.

For each field term (vertex)

\operatorname{Tr}[h_{i}\Pi_{i}^{X}\rho(q)]=h_{i}\frac{1+\langle X_{i}\rangle_{\rho(q)}}{2}=h_{i}\frac{1+qx_{i}}{2}\geq\frac{1+q}{2}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i].

The equalities are immediate since by construction

h_{i}\frac{1+qx_{i}}{2}=\frac{1+qx_{i}}{1+x_{i}}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i]\geq\frac{1+qx_{i}+q+x_{i}}{2(1+x_{i})}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i]=\frac{1+q}{2}\cdot\mathrm{SDP}_{X}[i],

since
0\leq q,x_{i}\leq 1\Rightarrow q+x_{i}\leq 1+qx_{i}
.

∎

Now we proceed, exactly as before, with the contribution of the Ising terms (edges) to the solution energy.

.

For every Ising term (edge

(8)

A product-state
169/180
optimality gap on a triangle instance

In this section, we give a concrete
3
-qbit purely ferromagnetic TFIM instance where product states provably cannot achieve the true optimum. This gives an upper bound for any method that is restricted to output product states.

Let
V=\{0,1,2\}
and
E=\{\{0,1\},\{1,2\},\{0,2\}\}
.
Set unit edge weights
w_{ij}=1
and uniform field weights
h_{i}=g
with

g=\frac{3}{5}.

We take the signs

J_{01}=J_{12}=J_{02}=+1.

Let
H_{t}
denote the corresponding
H_{\mathrm{CSP}}
Hamiltonian on this instance. This is a
3
qbit Hamiltonian and as such a
2^{3}\times 2^{3}
Hermitian matrix. A direct diagonalization
gives the spectrum

\left\{\frac{18}{5},\ \frac{16}{5}\ (\times 2),\ \frac{13}{5}\ (\times 2),\ \frac{8\pm\sqrt{19}}{5},\ \frac{4}{5}\right\},

so the maximum eigenvalue is
\frac{18}{5}
.

The interesting direction is to upper bound the value achievable by any product state.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}=\max_{\rho\ \mathrm{product}}\operatorname{Tr}[H_{t}\rho]
. For this instance

\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}=\frac{169}{50}.

Proof.

We have:

\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}=\max_{\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\otimes\rho_{3}}\operatorname{Tr}[H_{t}\rho].

We will represent each
\rho_{i}
by its Bloch vector
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
; note that trivially the optimum is reached at a pure state so we have
x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}+z_{i}^{2}=1
. Further, we may assume that
x_{i}\geq 0
and
y_{i}=0
, since otherwise replacing
(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
with
(\sqrt{x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}},0,z_{i})
improves the solution. Hence also we have
x_{i}=\sqrt{1-z_{i}^{2}}
.

Now we will write the objective as a function of
(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})
.
Since
\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle=z_{i}z_{j}
and
\langle X_{i}\rangle=x_{i}
we have

\operatorname{Tr}[H_{t}\rho]=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\frac{1-J_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}}{2}+\frac{g}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{2}(1+x_{i}).

Putting
J_{ij}=+1
for all edges,
g=\nicefrac{{3}}{{5}}
and
x_{i}=\sqrt{1-z_{i}^{2}}
we obtain

F(z)=\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{0}z_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{1}z_{2})+\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{0}z_{2})+\frac{3}{10}\sum_{i=0}^{2}(1+\sqrt{1-z_{i}^{2}}),

and so our optimization problem is transformed to a simple optimization problem in the cube
[-1,1]^{3}
:

\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}=\max_{|z_{i}|\leq 1}F(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2}).

Since
g>0
, an optimal solution cannot have
|z_{i}|=1
(equivalently
x_{i}=0
) since otherwise pertubing that qbit slightly so that
z_{i}
decreases by
\epsilon
and
x_{i}
increases by
\sqrt{\epsilon}
changes the edge terms only by
\approx\epsilon^{2}
but increases the field contribution by
\epsilon
.

Now we can differentiate
F
with respect to each
z_{i}
. The first-order stationary conditions gives us the system

x_{0}(z_{1}+z_{2})=-gz_{0},\qquad x_{1}(z_{0}+z_{2})=-gz_{1},\qquad x_{2}(z_{0}+z_{1})=-gz_{2},\qquad(g=3/5).

Solving this system over
|z_{i}|<1
gives the following critical points:

(i)

z_{0}=z_{1}=z_{2}=0
(hence
x_{0}=x_{1}=x_{2}=1
);

(ii)

up to relabeling, one coordinate is
0
and the other two are opposite:

z_{a}=0,\qquad z_{b}=-z_{c}=+/-\frac{4}{5},\qquad x_{a}=1,\qquad x_{b}=x_{c}=\frac{3}{5}.

Now we evaluate
F
on these candidates solutions. At
(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=(0,0,0)
we have
x_{i}=1
and thus

F=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3}{10}\cdot 3\cdot 2=\frac{33}{10}=3.3.

At (ii), say
(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=(0,4/5,-4/5)
with
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=(1,3/5,3/5)
, we get

\text{edges}=\frac{1}{2}(1-0)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\Bigl(-\frac{16}{25}\Bigr)\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}(1-0)=\frac{91}{50},

and

\text{fields}=\frac{3}{10}\Bigl[(1+1)+\Bigl(1+\frac{3}{5}\Bigr)+\Bigl(1+\frac{3}{5}\Bigr)\Bigr]=\frac{78}{50}.

So
F=91/50+78/50=169/50
.

Also, there are also solutions with all
z_{i}\neq 0
, but numerical optimization shows that they give worse bounds with respect to F.
∎

A product state that matches the value
169/50
is:

\left|\rho_{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|0\right\rangle+\left|1\right\rangle\right)\otimes\left(\sqrt{0.9}\,\left|0\right\rangle+\sqrt{0.1}\,\left|1\right\rangle\right)\otimes\left(\sqrt{0.1}\,\left|0\right\rangle+\sqrt{0.9}\,\left|1\right\rangle\right),

Corollary 1
.

For this instance,

\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{prod}}}{\mathrm{OPT}}=\frac{169/50}{18/5}=\frac{169}{180}\approx 0.938888.

Consequently, no algorithm restricted to output product states can guarantee a worst-case approximation ratio
exceeding
169/180
for TFIM instances.
