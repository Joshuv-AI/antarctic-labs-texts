A 30 m winter wheat distribution dataset for the North China Plain from 2000 to 2024
Authors: Fangjie Li, Inbal Becker-Reshef, Josef Wagner, Yuval Sadeh, Jean Rehbinder, Françoise Nerry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Winter wheat is a major food crop in China, with the North China Plain (NCP) serving as the primary production region and playing a critical role in national food security. However, long-term, high-resolution winter wheat maps for this region remain limited. This study presents a 30 m resolution winter wheat distribution dataset for the NCP (WheatMapNCP) from 2000 to 2024, generated by combining automated training sample generation with a random forest (RF) classifier. A stratified random sampling framework was used to estimate unbiased winter wheat area. Validation results show that the produced maps achieved an average overall accuracy (OA) of 95.98 ± 1.15%, and an average F1 score of 86.4%. The estimated planted areas show a consistent temporal trend with official statistics, and the mapped areas show strong correlation to statistics at the provincial (R² = 0.97–0.98) and municipal (R² = 0.84–0.96) levels. The dataset not only provides long-term, high-quality support for monitoring winter wheat dynamics, but also offers a reliable approach for generating timely, transparent wheat area estimates based on satellite data.

Background & Summary

Wheat is one of the most important food crops globally and plays a significant role in global food security1,2. China is the largest wheat producer and consumer globally, with a self-sufficiency rate of more than 90%3. Winter wheat dominates China’s wheat production4,5. In 2022, the production of winter wheat was about 132 million tons, accounting for about 96% of the national wheat production (National Bureau of Statistics, https://www.stats.gov.cn/). The North China Plain (NCP) is one of the most important wheat producing regions in China6, with its winter wheat planting area accounting for 88% of the national total, making it critical to national food security. Therefore, obtaining accurate information on the spatial distribution, area statistics, and temporal dynamics of winter wheat in this region is essential not only for monitoring crop production and spatial pattern changes, and ensuring national food security, but also supporting agricultural policy-making and assessing the impacts of climate change.

Remote sensing technology has become an important tool for crop mapping and informing agricultural statistics due to its wide coverage, high efficiency and powerful dynamic monitoring capabilities7–10. Supervised classification is a commonly used approach for crop mapping. It uses training samples of known classes to learn the relationships between features and classes through algorithms, and then applies the trained classification model to assign classes to unknown pixels in the imagery11. Traditionally, training samples are usually obtained through manual labelling, such as conducting field surveys or visual interpreting remote sensing imagery. While this method can provide relatively accurate reference data, the process is time-consuming and labor-intensive12, limiting its applicability to large areas or multi-year studies. In recent years, street-view imagery (e.g., Google Street View, GSV) has become an emerging approach for crop type ground-truth data collection13,14. By identifying crops in roadside fields, street-view imagery can provide ground-level visual evidence for crop type labelling13. Combined with deep learning methods, these images can provide a low-cost and scalable source of ground reference data for crop type mapping14–16. For example, Liu, et al.13 developed a deep learning-based CropSight framework by integrating GSV and PlanetScope imagery to retrieve object-based crop type ground truth. Yan and Ryu14 applied a convolutional neural network (CNN) to GSV imagery and explored its use for automatic ground truthing in crop type mapping. Although street-view imagery can effectively support crop mapping by providing ground reference information, its applicability remains constrained by factors such as road accessibility, uneven spatial coverage, and limited historical data13,17.

To reduce the reliance on labor-intensive and spatially constrained ground-truth collection while supporting large-area and multi-year crop mapping, automated training sample generation has become an effective strategy and has been widely used to produce crop land and crop type maps8,18. Existing methods for automatic sample generation can generally be divided into two broad categories. The first category mainly relies on existing maps or labeled samples. One common strategy uses historical or current land cover or crop classification maps to identify high-confidence target crop distribution areas through rigorous quality screening, from which representative training samples are extracted19–21. While this approach is simple to implement and efficient, its effectiveness highly dependent on the classification accuracy and timeliness of reference products. Furthermore, its applicability will be limited in areas where reference layers are unavailable. Another strategy is sample transfer, which aims to alleviate the issue of insufficient training samples in the target domain by reusing labeled samples from a reference year or region. A commonly used method involves spectral similarity, which measures the spectral similarity between the reference year and target year images and retains samples with high similarity as training samples for the target year22–24. However, significant interannual phenological variations, as well as intra-class and inter-class variability may compromise the quality of transferred samples25.

The second category is phenology-based approaches, which take full advantage of the unique seasonal growth patterns of crops. By analyzing multi-temporal remote sensing data (e.g., vegetation index time series), it captures the distinct spectral–temporal characteristics exhibited by the target crop during key growth stages (such as regreening, heading, flowering, and maturity), and uses these to automatically identify and extract pure target crop pixels from imagery as training samples8,26,27. These automatic sample generation methods can effectively overcome the problem of insufficient training samples in large-scale crop mapping. By combining machine learning algorithms, it provides a feasible theoretical framework for large-scale, long-term, and high-accuracy winter wheat mapping. At present, most phenology-based winter wheat sample generation methods mainly rely on normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) time series. However, NDVI shows limited capability in distinguishing winter wheat from other crops with similar phenological patterns, such as garlic and rapeseed. Previous research has demonstrated that the green chlorophyll vegetation index (GCVI) exhibits a strong linear relationship with leaf area index (LAI)28,29, enabling it to more reliably capture crop growth dynamics. Compared with NDVI, GCVI is more effective at distinguishing chlorophyll differences between winter wheat and other winter crops, particularly during the late growth stages28,30. Despite these advantages, GCVI has not yet been fully exploited in current research on automatic training sample generation.

Furthermore, despite significant progress in remote sensing methods for crop mapping, publicly available winter wheat distribution datasets for China remain limited. Existing products often represent a trade-off: some provide national coverage but at coarse resolutions (≥500 m), which limit field-scale analysis31,32; others achieve higher resolutions (10 m) but are constrained to relatively short timeframes (typically post-2015) due to sensor availability8,23. The Landsat satellite series has provided continuous, stable, and openly accessible 30 m resolution imagery since 197233. Its balance of spatial detail and long-term observation capacity makes it a critical data source for large-scale winter wheat monitoring across multiple years. Nevertheless, long-term winter wheat mapping at 30 m resolution remains limited, with only a few studies having released such products19,34. Therefore, it is necessary to develop large-scale, long-term, high-resolution winter wheat mapping to improve the understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of winter wheat cultivation.

In addition, accurately estimating regional crop planting areas is critical for food security assessments, crop statistics, and agricultural policy-making. Due to omission and commission errors, area estimates based on classified pixel counts often contain biases35,36. Currently, many studies have adopted sample-based approaches for map accuracy assessment and area estimation, aiming to produce unbiased area estimates with quantified uncertainties37–40. This approach combines a probability sampling design, precise reference labelling, and a consistent analysis protocol to generate unbiased estimates of classification accuracy and area, while effectively quantifying the uncertainty by reporting confidence intervals for both accuracy and area parameters36. Compared with direct pixel counting, which can introduce biases into area estimates as misclassifications and mixed pixels are accumulated in the total pixel counts40,41, this approach provides unbiased area estimates with associated uncertainties. Hence, this sample-based approach offers a robust framework for precise winter wheat area estimation, providing essential data support for regional crop monitoring and agricultural decision-making.

The objective of this study is to construct a long-term (2000–2024), high-resolution (30 m) winter wheat distribution dataset for the NCP (WheatMapNCP) and to estimate winter wheat planting areas. Specifically, we developed an automated training sample generation method based on Landsat GCVI time series, and integrated these phenology-based samples with samples extracted from existing map products to enhance their representativeness and spatial coverage. Subsequently, important features during key phenological periods of winter wheat were identified through feature importance and separability analyses. By combining with a random forest (RF) classifier, a 30 m resolution winter wheat distribution dataset for the NCP from 2000 to 2024 was produced, and the annual winter wheat planting areas were estimated. Finally, the generated maps were evaluated using validation samples, agricultural statistics, and existing winter wheat maps. The WheatMapNCP provides long-term, high-quality data support for monitoring the spatiotemporal dynamics of winter wheat planting, yield estimation, and regional agricultural policy-making in the NCP.

Methods

In this study, we developed a 30 m resolution winter wheat distribution dataset covering the NCP from 2000 to 2024 by integrating automated training sample generation with an RF classifier. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the dataset generation process involved the following main steps: (1) preprocessing Landsat-5/7/8/9 imagery to generate 10-day interval Landsat time-series data, and preparing reference data, official statistical data, and existing winter wheat products; (2) generating training data by combining rule-based samples derived from GCVI time-series phenological features with supplementary samples from existing winter wheat maps; (3) constructing the optimal feature set by identifying key growth periods through feature importance analysis and selecting features based on separability evaluation; and (4) producing annual winter wheat maps for the NCP from 2000 to 2024 using an RF classifier, followed by area estimation based on stratified random sampling36 and accuracy assessment using independent validation samples, existing winter wheat maps, and official agricultural statistics.Fig. 1Workflow of this study.

Study area

The study area is located in the NCP (29°–42° N, 105–123° E), including ten provinces/municipalities: Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Anhui, Jiangsu, Hubei, Shanxi, and Shaanxi (Fig. 2). The total cropland area is 42.06 million hectares (Mha). This region is the largest winter wheat producing area in China. In 2022, it produced 121.60 million tons of winter wheat, with a planting area of 19.77 Mha, accounting for 92% of national production and 88% of the national planting area (https://www.stats.gov.cn/). The study area spans the Qinling–Huaihe demarcation line42, and features a temperate continental monsoon climate in the north and subtropical monsoon characteristics in the south43. The topography is characterized by mountainous terrain in the west, hilly terrain in the south, and plains dominate the central and eastern parts. Fertile tidal soils and brown soils provide favorable conditions for crop growth44,45. A double-cropping system is commonly practiced in the region, involving the rotation of winter wheat with maize or rice7. Winter wheat is typically sown in October and harvested in May to June of the following year. Other winter crops grown during the same season include garlic, which is mainly distributed in the Huaihe River basin, and rapeseed, which is mainly cultivated in the Yangtze River basin.Fig. 2(a) Provincial average winter wheat planting area in China during 2000–2022 and (b) location of the study area.

Data acquisition and processing

Landsat data

To map winter wheat from 2000 to 2024, Landsat surface reflectance (SR) data from October 1999 to July 2024 were used, including Landsat-5 Thematic Mapper (TM) (1999–2012), Landsat-7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM +) (1999–2021), Landsat-8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) (2013–2024), and Landsat-9 Operational Land Imager 2 (OLI-2) (2021–2024). During overlapping periods, data from multiple sensors were combined to enhance observation frequency (Fig. 3). The Landsat series provides nearly 50 years of continuous Earth observations46, with imagery at a spatial resolution of 30 m and a temporal resolution of 16 days, offering a valuable database for large-scale, long-term crop type mapping43,47. Compared with Landsat top of atmosphere (TOA) data, the SR products have been atmospherically corrected using the LEDAPS (Landsat Ecosystem Disturbance Adaptive Processing System) and LaSRC (Land Surface Reflectance Code) algorithms, which effectively reduce the effects of aerosol scattering and water vapor absorption, making the reflectance data closer to the true surface values48. The spectral bands of the Landsat data are listed in Table 1, including the visible (blue, green, red), near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) bands. In addition to spectral bands, seven vegetation indices (VIs) were calculated, as listed in Table 2. These indices were selected based on their proven effectiveness in crop mapping studies. NDVI, the most widely used vegetation index, exhibits high sensitivity to green vegetation and is effective for monitoring vegetation coverage and growth dynamics49. However, it is prone to saturation in areas with dense vegetation canopies30. The enhanced vegetation index (EVI) can reduce atmospheric interference and soil background effects, and demonstrates superior performance in high-biomass environments50. GCVI exhibits a strong linear correlation with LAI, making it particularly suitable for quantifying vegetation growth status and coverage28–30. Since rapeseed and winter wheat are both winter crops with overlapping phenological stages, confusion between the two is common in remote sensing classification. The winter rapeseed index (WRI) and canola index (CI) are used to detect the degree of vegetation yellowing, can be used to distinguish rapeseed from winter wheat and are commonly applied in rapeseed mapping26,47. The chlorophyll vegetation index (CVI) is a broadband spectral index that is particularly sensitive to leaf chlorophyll content at the canopy scale51. The land surface water index (LSWI) effectively reflects canopy moisture and surface wetness, and is useful for improving the classification of agricultural land and forests52. For each spectral feature, a continuous time series with 10-day intervals was generated based on the cloud-free observations using linear interpolation53. To ensure sufficient valid observations for interpolation, we used a temporal search window of 100 days before and after each target date. Linear interpolation was performed only when valid observations were available on both sides within this window; otherwise, the pixel remained masked. Land cover data

In this study, we used the annual China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD) published by Yang and Huang55, a 30 m resolution land cover classification product spans from 1985 to 2023. This dataset was generated from Landsat imagery and provides nine land cover types, including cropland, forest, shrub, grassland, water, snow and ice, barren, impervious, and wetland. It has an accuracy of 79.31% through validation. In this study, the CLCD was mainly used to extract stable forest, water, and impervious surface areas from 2000 to 2023 to generate representative non-cropland reference data. Additionally, the cropland layer of CLCD was used to exclude winter wheat training samples located outside cropland areas.

Reference data

To obtain reference vegetation index time series for different land cover types, a high-quality reference dataset was constructed using land cover products and the visual interpretation of high-resolution remote sensing imagery. Specifically, non-cropland samples, including water, forest, and built-up land, were obtained from the CLCD product. To ensure the randomness and uniformity of the sample distribution and reduce spatial autocorrelation27,56,57, a hexagonal grid with a horizontal and vertical spacing of 60 km was generated across the study area (Fig. 4a). Compared to a square, a hexagon has a shorter average distance from any point within it to its center and a shorter perimeter for the same area, resulting in a more balanced spatial distribution and reducing sampling bias due to edge effects58. CLCD products from 2000 to 2023 were overlaid to extract the extent of water, forest, and built-up land that remained stable over multiple years. Within each hexagonal grid containing the target land cover types, two samples per class were randomly selected (Fig. 4b). Agricultural statistical data

To evaluate the area accuracy of the generated winter wheat maps, agricultural statistical data on winter wheat planting areas at both provincial and municipal levels were collected. The provincial level data were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (https://www.stats.gov.cn/), and the municipal level data were collected from statistical yearbooks published by individual provinces and cities. Considering the continuity and completeness of the available statistics, we ultimately collected winter wheat planting area data for eight provinces and 85 municipalities from 2000 to 2023, providing an important reference for the validation of the mapped area.

Training data generation

Rule-based sample generation

Due to differences in spectral characteristics, various land cover types exhibit distinct temporal patterns in vegetation index time series22. In this study, a rule-based method for automatic training sample generation was developed based on the GCVI time series derived from Landsat imagery. GCVI was selected because previous studies have demonstrated its strong linear relationship with LAI, and its effectiveness in capturing phenological differences between winter wheat and other winter crops, such as garlic and rapeseed28,30. Reference GCVI time series curves for different land cover types were obtained based on reference samples from 2022 and 2023 (Fig. 5). In addition, considering the differences in satellite data availability across years, we calculated spectral similarity between 2023 and the years 2017 and 2011 using spectral angle distance (SAD)63 to analyze the GCVI time-series patterns of different land cover types across years. Only samples with SAD values less than 0.4 were retained, the winter crop reference samples from 2023 were then transferred to 2011 and 2017, thereby generating the corresponding reference GCVI time series for those years.Fig. 5GCVI time series for different land cover types in 2023, 2022, 2017, and 2011.

GCVI time series for different land cover types in 2023, 2022, 2017, and 2011.

From the GCVI time series, non-vegetation exhibited consistently low values throughout the winter wheat growing season, while forest land showed a decreasing then increasing trend. In contrast, winter crops displayed an increasing then decreasing trend. Notably, during the heading stage (April to May) of winter wheat, its GCVI values were consistently higher than those of garlic and rapeseed. In June, the GCVI values of winter wheat dropped to relatively low levels, whereas forest land retained higher values. Feature construction

In this study, a 10-day interval time series dataset was constructed, comprising six spectral bands (blue, green, red, NIR, SWIR1, and SWIR2) and seven VIs (NDVI, EVI, GCVI, WRI, CI, CVI, and LSWI). While these multi-temporal features provide rich information for winter wheat classification, directly using all of them as model inputs may lead to feature redundancy and increased model complexity64,65. To improve classification performance and reduce computational costs, an optimal feature set for winter wheat mapping was selected through two analyses: (1) identification of key phenological periods; and (2) feature separability analysis to determine the optimal feature.

We extracted the time series values of 13 spectral features across 27 time points (a total of 351 features) for each training sample in each province. These features were input into the RF model to calculate their Gini importance. To avoid class imbalance effects, equal numbers of winter wheat and non-winter wheat samples was used during the feature importance analysis. Gini importance measures the contribution of each feature to classification by evaluating the reduction in Gini impurity across decision trees66. Figure 8a to h presents the temporal distribution of feature importance in different provinces. The horizontal axis represents the time nodes, and the vertical axis shows the different spectral features. Color density reflects the importance level of the features, with darker red indicating a greater contribution to winter wheat classification. The results indicate that the most informative periods were concentrated in the late growth stages of winter wheat, particularly from the jointing to heading stages.Fig. 8(a–h) Feature importance distribution of each spectral feature at different time points for Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Jiangsu, Anhui, Hubei, Shanxi and Shaanxi provinces, respectively, (i) trend of the average feature importance over time for each province.

(a–h) Feature importance distribution of each spectral feature at different time points for Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Jiangsu, Anhui, Hubei, Shanxi and Shaanxi provinces, respectively, (i) trend of the average feature importance over time for each province.

Figure 8i further presents the trend of average feature importance at different time points in each province. It can be observed that the feature importance of the early stages was low, but increased from mid- to late February, peaking between March and May, indicating that the spectral response at this period is crucial for winter wheat identification. As winter wheat reached maturity and harvest, feature importance declined. Based on these results, the ten most important time points, ranging from February 27 to May 28 were identified as the key phenological window for feature selection.

After identifying the key phenological period, the time series features for this period were extracted. The ability of each feature to discriminate between winter wheat and non-winter wheat was then evaluated using the spectral discrimination index (SDI), which quantifies class separability. SDI is defined as the absolute difference between the mean values of two classes divided by the sum of their standard deviations. SDI values between 1 and 3 indicate good separability and ease of classification67. Figure 9a to h shows the SDI values of spectral features across different provinces during the key phenological period. Overall, NDVI, EVI, GCVI, CVI, LSWI, and NIR exhibit higher SDI values, which indicates that a better performance in distinguishing between winter and non-winter wheat classes. In contrast, features such as the Blue, Green, Red, SWIR1, SWIR2 bands and CI, WRI have lower separability, predominantly appearing as blue areas in the heatmaps. Figure 9i further presents the average SDI values of different features in each province. It can be seen that NDVI, EVI, GCVI, CVI, LSWI, and NIR typically achieve values between 0.8 to 1.4, whereas most other features are below 0.6. Random Forest Classification

After obtaining the training samples and features, we utilized the RF algorithm on the GEE platform to map winter wheat distribution across the NCP from 2000 to 2024. The RF classifier was selected because it performs better in terms of accuracy and stability66, and has been widely used in crop mapping studies23,27,57,68. As an ensemble learning algorithm consisting of multiple decision trees, RF is robust and well-suited to large-scale, high-dimensional datasets39,69. The RF model has three main user-defined hyperparameters: the number of trees, the number of variables per split, and the maximum number of splits70. In this study, the number of trees was set to 100, with other parameters were set by default in GEE. To reduce the variability of results due to random sampling, the random seed was fixed to 42.

Due to the large spatial extent of the study area and the large amount of remote sensing data, we adopted a provincial mapping strategy to improve computational efficiency. For each province, a 20 km buffer along the administrative boundaries was generated to mitigate edge effects during classification. Winter wheat mapping was performed individually within each buffered province, and the final distribution maps were obtained by clipping and mosaicking the provincial results. Considering that Beijing and Tianjin City are relatively small, these two regions were combined with Hebei Province for winter wheat mapping to ensure spatial continuity.

Area Estimation

To produce unbiased estimates of winter wheat area and quantify the associated uncertainty across the NCP, we adopted the stratified random sampling framework proposed by Olofsson, et al.36. This approach ensures that samples within each stratum are representativeness and provides unbiased estimates with quantifiable uncertainties. It has been widely applied in the estimation of crop area and the accuracy assessment of remote sensing classifications37,38,40,71–74. The procedure involves three main steps: (1) sampling design, (2) response design, and (3) estimation of area, and confidence intervals.

Sampling design

Based on the cropland layer from the CLCD product55 and our winter wheat map, the study area was stratified into three classes: winter wheat, non-winter wheat cropland, and non-cropland. Stratified random sampling was then applied to generate sample points within each stratum. Response design

Each random sample point was visually interpreted using high-resolution Google Earth imagery, Sentinel-2 true color composites, Landsat true color composites, and GCVI time series curves. During the jointing to heading stage, winter wheat exhibits vigorous growth with increased LAI and high canopy cover, appearing as a distinct dark green in true color imagery. In June, winter wheat reaches maturity and harvest, displaying a brownish color. In addition, the GCVI time series of winter wheat shows clear phenological dynamics throughout the growing season (Fig. 5). In the NCP, other winter crops such as garlic and rapeseed, which are cultivated concurrently with winter wheat, may cause confusion during interpretation. Garlic generally appears lighter green than winter wheat (Fig. 4c), while rapeseed is in its typical flowering period from March to April and appears distinctly yellow (Fig. 4d). Moreover, the GCVI time series of garlic and rapeseed are generally lower than those of winter wheat (Fig. 5). By integrating multi-temporal imagery and vegetation index time series characteristics, winter wheat planting areas can be reliably distinguished. Non-cropland (e.g., impervious surfaces, water, and forest/plantation) exhibit stable and distinctive spectral and textural characteristics, making them easy to interpret. Finally, each sample was labeled as winter wheat, non-winter wheat cropland, or non-cropland. Technical Validation

Accuracy assessment

To evaluate the accuracy of the winter wheat distribution maps, four validation approaches were applied. (1) Unbiased estimates of user accuracy (UA), producer accuracy (PA), and overall accuracy (OA) were derived from area proportion-based confusion matrix (Table 7), along with 95% confidence intervals. The F1 score was also computed to provide a comprehensive measure of classification performance. (2) Pixel-level accuracy assessments for the 2022 and 2023 winter wheat maps were conducted using the collected validation samples (Table 3). (3) The WheatMapNCP was compared with three publicly available winter wheat products to assess spatial consistency. (4) Winter wheat planting areas derived from the maps were compared with official agricultural statistics at the provincial and municipal levels. Agreement was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and root mean square error (RMSE).Table 7Accuracy metrics. Validation of annual winter wheat maps and area estimation

Based on the stratified random sampling method, a total of 24000 samples were labeled to systematically evaluate the classification accuracy of winter wheat maps in the NCP from 2000 to 2023 (Fig. 12). The results showed that the maps exhibited high accuracy, with OA ranging from 93.79 ± 1.58% to 97.15 ± 0.87%, and a multi-year average OA of 95.98 ± 1.15%. Compared with OA, UA and PA showed more pronounced interannual fluctuation, with UA ranged from 80.4 ± 3.48% to 90.2 ± 2.61%, and PA ranged from 76.47 ± 7.58% to 93.56 ± 5.26%. The F1 score ranged from 79.03% to 90.09%, with values stabilizing above 85% in most years, and a multi-year average F1 score of 86.4%. Notably, the accuracy showed a clear decline in 2012, mainly due to the limited availability of Landsat imagery, with fewer than five good observations generally available during the key growth period of winter wheat (see Limitations for further discussion). Overall, WheatMapNCP demonstrated high accuracy and temporal stability, providing a reliable support for long-term monitoring of spatial distribution dynamics.Fig. 12Accuracy assessment results of WheatMapNCP from 2000 to 2023 (OA_CI denotes the 95% confidence interval of OA).

Accuracy assessment results of WheatMapNCP from 2000 to 2023 (OA_CI denotes the 95% confidence interval of OA).

Winter wheat planting area and its 95% confidence interval were also estimated across the NCP from 2000 to 2023. Overall, the planting area increased from 18.61 ± 1.44 Mha in 2000 to 19.41 ± 1.36 Mha in 2023 (uncertainties are expressed as 95% confidence interval). From 2000 to 2023, the standard error of the estimated area ranged from 0.59 to 1.07 Mha, corresponding to 95% confidence interval of 1.15 to 2.1 Mha. Compared with official agricultural statistics, the estimated winter wheat planting areas showed a consistent temporal trend, with most differences falling within the 95% confidence intervals (Fig. 13). Temporally, the winter wheat area exhibited a pattern of decline, followed by growth, and then a gradual decrease. From 2000 to 2004, the winter wheat area decreased by 2.09 Mha (about 11%), which may be a result of a variety of factors, such as the Grain for Green Program (GGP)76,77, the acceleration of urbanization78, and low profitability of wheat cultivation79. After 2004, the government gradually abolished the agricultural tax and implemented a series of agricultural subsidies and preferential policies, which significantly improved farmers’ income expectations and enthusiasm for agricultural production80–82, leading to an increase in winter wheat planting areas. From 2004 to 2015, the area increased from 16.53 Mha to 20.8 Mha, representing a 26% growth. Between 2015 and 2023, the winter wheat planting area exhibited a slow fluctuating downward trend, with a decrease of about 7%. This trend may be attributed to the implementation of crop rotation and fallow policies83,84. In some regions, the traditional double-cropping system (e.g., winter wheat–summer maize or winter wheat–rice) has been replaced by single-season cultivation85. These policies aim to alleviate environmental issues such as groundwater over-extraction, soil degradation, and soil erosion84,86.Fig. 13Estimated winter wheat area with 95% confidence intervals, and comparison with agricultural statistics in the NCP from 2000 to 2023.

Comparison with agricultural statistical data

We compared the winter wheat planting areas derived from WheatMapNCP with agricultural statistical data at both provincial and municipal levels (Fig. 15 and Fig. 16). The results showed a strong correlation between the mapped areas and the statistical data. At the provincial level, the R² ranged from 0.97 to 0.98, with RMSE values between 208.89 thousand hectares (kha) and 254.91 kha across different time periods. At the municipal level, R² values ranged from 0.84 to 0.96, with RMSE values between 41.5 kha and 75.68 kha. Additionally, we observed that from 2000 to 2011, the mapped winter wheat area at the provincial level was generally higher than the statistical data, and after 2011, this overestimation trend diminished, with underestimation observed in some provinces. At the municipal level, correlations between mapped winter wheat areas and statistical data were mostly in the range of 0.84 to 0.90 during 2000–2014, with an average R² of 0.88. After 2014, the correlations further improved, with R² ranging from 0.91 to 0.96, and an average R² of 0.94. These results indicate a gradual improvement in the accuracy of the winter wheat mapping, which may be attributed to the advances in the quality of remote sensing data and the increased availability of high-quality imagery.Fig. 15Comparison of winter wheat planting areas derived from WheatMapNCP and agricultural statistical data at the provincial level from 2000 to 2023.Fig. 16Comparison of winter wheat planting areas derived from WheatMapNCP and agricultural statistical data at the municipal level from 2000 to 2023.

Comparison with existing winter wheat maps

Overall, WheatMapNCP produces a spatial distribution of winter wheat that is generally consistent with other existing products, but it provides more accurate spatial details in some regions. Usage Notes

Limitations

This study successfully mapped 30 m winter wheat distribution across the NCP from 2000 to 2024 and estimated the winter wheat planting area, providing a valuable foundation for long-term crop monitoring. Nevertheless, some uncertainties remain. The availability and quality of remote sensing imagery are critical for accurate crop mapping32,47. Although linear interpolation was used to reduce data gaps, persistent cloud cover and other factors often led to missing or degraded observations9, thereby affecting classification accuracy. For example, Landsat-7 ETM + suffered a scanline corrector (SLC) failure in 2003, resulting in striping artifacts and data loss93. In 2012, only Landsat-7 imagery was available, resulting in generally fewer than five good observations during the key growth period (Fig. 24). In addition, frequent cloud cover in the southern NCP further reduced the availability of high-quality observations. These factors not only compromise the completeness of temporal features but also increase the uncertainty of winter wheat mapping.Fig. 24Number of good observations of Landsat images from March to June for each year from 2000–2024.

Number of good observations of Landsat images from March to June for each year from 2000–2024.

In addition, winter wheat classification in this study was performed using an RF classifier. Although RF has proven effective for large-scale crop mapping, deep-learning-based crop mapping methods have developed rapidly in recent years. Compared with traditional classifiers, deep learning models can automatically learn complex and informative features from remote sensing data, and have shown strong potential in crop classification and mapping94–96. In recent years, various deep learning models, such as CNNs, long short-term memory (LSTM), and semantic segmentation models such as U-Net, have been widely applied to large-scale crop mapping95,97,98. The current study did not utilize these deep learning methods, which represents a limitation compared with recent advances in crop mapping algorithms.

Moreover, this study mainly focused on winter wheat mapping in the NCP, and the applicability of the method to other regions or crop types still needs to be assessed. For the GCVI-based automated training-sample generation method, its applicability mainly depends on whether the target crop exhibits sufficiently distinct temporal spectral characteristics from other land-cover or crop types during key phenological stages. In regions where crop phenological characteristics and spectral patterns are clearly separable, the method may be transferable with appropriate threshold adjustments and local phenological calibration. However, in regions with more complex cropping structures, weaker inter-crop separability, or greater differences in crop calendars and management practices, the discriminative ability of GCVI alone may be insufficient. In such cases, the decision rules may need to be adjusted, and additional spectral indices, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data, or additional information may be required to ensure sample quality and mapping accuracy.
