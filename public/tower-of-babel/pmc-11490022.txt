A novel biomimetic nanovesicle containing caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots for the the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease via nasal administration
Authors: Yu Hu, Jingwen Cui, Junpeng Sun, Xiaobang Liu, Shuang Gao, Xifan Mei, Chao Wu, He Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a common neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive cognitive and physical impairment. Neuroinflammation is related to AD, and the misfolding and aggregation of amyloid protein in the brain creates an inflammatory microenvironment. Microglia are the predominant contributors to neuroinflammation, and abnormal activation of microglia induces the release of a large amount of inflammatory factors, promotes neuronal apoptosis, and leads to cognitive impairment. In this study, we used microglial membranes containing caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots to prepare a novel biomimetic nanocapsule (CDs-CA-MGs) for the treatment of AD. The application of CDs-CA-MGs via nasal administration can bypass the blood‒brain barrier (BBB) and directly target the site of inflammation. After treatment with CDs-CA-MGs, AD mice showed reduced inflammation in the brain, decreased neuronal apoptosis, and significantly improved learning and memory abilities. In addition, CDs-CA-MGs affect inflammation-related JAK-STAT and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways in AD mice. CDs-CA-MGs significantly downregulated interleukins (IL-1β and IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α). This finding suggested that CDs-CA-MGs may improve cognitive impairment by modulating inflammatory responses. In conclusion, the use of CDs-CA-MGs provides a possible therapeutic strategy for the treatment of AD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02912-8.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is one of the leading causes of disability and death in elderly people [1]. The prevalence of AD increases with age [2]. Currently, there are more than 15.07 million AD patients over the age of 60 in China, accounting for approximately one-fourth of the total number of AD patients in the world [3, 4].The major pathological changes in AD include extracellular β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, neuroprogenitor fibril tangles, and tau protein (p-tau) hyperphosphorylation [5]. Numerous studies have shown that the secretion of inflammatory factors is associated with the development of AD. This finding implies that inflammation may be a major contributor to the aggregation of pathological proteins in the brain and drive the development of cognitive deficits in patients with AD [6, 7]. As the disease progresses, Aβ aggregation activates microglia, leading to increased secretion of cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and a significant increase in chemokine and reactive oxygen species levels, exacerbating the inflammatory response. The interaction between the inflammatory microenvironment and cytokines accelerates neuronal death and promotes the development of AD-related cognitive impairment [8]. Therefore, inhibiting the inflammatory response and restoring neuronal function represent key strategies for the treatment of AD.

Caffeic acid (CA), a natural product, can be extracted from coffee grounds [9]. Given its di-octahydroxyaromatic structure, it has strong anti-inflammatory effects and antioxidant effects [10]. It has been reported that CA has been widely used to treat neurological disorders such as AD [11, 12]. However, due to the low bioavailability of caffeic acid and its insignificant therapeutic effect, its clinical application has been greatly restricted. Carbon quantum dots (CDs) are spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 5 nm [13]. CDs are water-soluble materials that are amenable to surface modification and exhibit good biocompatibility and stability [14]. Thus, they are widely used in bioimaging, drug delivery, and photoacoustic and photothermal therapy [15, 16]. Moreover, strong ligands on the surface of CDs can adsorb Cu2+ by chelation and inhibit Aβ aggregation to treat AD [17]. The literature reports that the pathological microenvironment of neuroinflammation in the AD brain is weakly acidic. Boronic acid ester bonds are unstable and prone to breakage in acidic environments [18]. Inspired by this, we chose 3-aminophenylboronic acid (APBA) as an intermediate and prepared a novel drug delivery system (CDs-CA) by connecting CD and CA through borate bonds. The targeted release of CA was achieved by breaking the borate bond [19–21]. We hypothesized that CDs-CA could play a greater therapeutic role than CDs or CA alone.

The use of inflammation as a therapeutic target to improve cognitive deficits has become a current research topic [22]. Microglia, astrocytes, and neurons are all involved in the inflammatory response in the brain [23]. An imbalance between Aβ production and clearance triggers neurotoxic and inflammatory responses that impair cognitive function. Microglial activation results in the phagocytosis of Aβ and promotes its clearance, which is important for the treatment of AD [24]. When exogenous, nanoparticles are easily recognized and cleared by the phagocytic system in vivo. Cell membrane-wrapped nanoparticles can model the surface properties and functions of source cells [25, 26]. These nanoparticles further improve the precise targeting ability and long duration of drug circulation to enhance the therapeutic effect. Microglia are not only the most abundant endogenous immune cells in the brain but also play a dominant role in the inflammatory response in AD [26, 27]. Therefore, we used microglial membranes (MGs) wrapped with caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots (CDs-CA-MGs) for homologous targeting [28]. This allows rapid drug accumulation at the site of brain injury and enhances efficacy [29]. In addition, the nasal injection of CDs-CA-MGs allowed them to successfully bypass the BBB and directly target the site of brain injury, improving the bioavailability of the drug and increasing the therapeutic potential of CA [30–32].

Based on the above description, we designed a novel bionic nanovesicle containing caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots (CDs-CA-MGs) to improve cognitive deficits via nasal administration. CDs-CA-MGs were able to successfully target the site of injury in the brain to ameliorate inflammation. We demonstrated in vitro experiments that CDs-CA-MGs reduce inflammatory responses, increase neuronal activity, and improve cognitive deficits in AD mice and Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated mice. This information provides a new therapeutic idea for precise drug targeting and improving cognitive impairment (Scheme 1).

Preparation of CDs, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs

CDs preparation: CDs containing carboxyl groups were prepared by hydrothermal method. 1.40 g of sodium citrate and 10.5 g of ammonium bicarbonate powder were added to 70 mL of triple-distilled water and stirred until completely dissolved [33]. The reaction was carried out in an autoclave at 180ºC for 8 h, cooled down to room temperature, and then centrifuged at 4ºC and 14,000 rpm for 15 min to remove the precipitate, and the supernatant was taken and used for the subsequent experiments.

CDs-CA preparation: 5mL of CDs solution was added with 0.01 mM EDC and NHS and stirred at room temperature until completely dissolved, then 2 mg of APBA was added and reacted at room temperature for 24 h, and then dialyzed by dialysis (MW = 3500) for 3 days. The solution was changed every 10 h to filter the free APBA. Excessive amount of CA was dissolved into the above solution, and the solution pH was adjusted to alkaline [34]. The solution was stirred for 8 h and then dialyzed with a dialysis bag (MW = 3500) for 3 days, changing the solution every 10 h to remove free CA, and then freeze-drying gave solid powder CDs-CA.

CDs-CA-MGs Preparation: Digestion was performed to collect BV2 cells in which a hypotonic solution was added to rupture the cell membrane. The cell membranes were subsequently ruptured completely using a grinder. Cell membranes were collected by gradient centrifugation. CDs-CA was sonicated with microglial cell membranes at a volume ratio of 1:2 for 30 min, and CDs-CA-MGs was purified by a dextran gel column and freeze-dried to obtain a solid powder [28].

Characterization of CDs, CDs-CA, CDs-CA-MGs

The morphology and structure of the materials were determined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Tecnai G2F30, FEI, USA). The particle size and zeta potential of the synthesized nanoparticles were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Functional groups in CDs-CA-MGs were observed using Fourier infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, TA, USA).

Brilliant blue staining

The presence of microglial cell membrane proteins in CDs-CA-MGs was qualitatively detected by SDS-PAGE. A certain amount of CDs-CA-MGs and BV2 cell membrane was taken to determine the protein content in the supernatant by BCA. Electrophoresis was performed using SDS-PAGE gels, incubated with 0.25% staining solution for 2 h, decolorized with decolorizing solution for 24 h, and developed.

CDs-CA-MGs drug loading and drug release experiment

The drug content was detected by UV spectrophotometry (UV-2000, Unico, USA) and the drug loading capacity was calculated according to the following formula: drug loading capacity (LC) = (drug mass) / (total weight of nanomaterials) × 100%. In vitro drug release studies were performed in a dissolution apparatus (RC-8D, Tianjin Guoming Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.). The dissolution medium was 1000 ml of phosphate buffer solution with neutral and acidic pH, respectively. The temperature was 37 °C and the paddle speed was 100 rpm. samples (equivalent to 10 mg CA) were placed into the dissolution medium. The dissolution medium (5 mL) was collected at predetermined time intervals for filtration and then supplemented with an equal amount of blank medium. Finally, the concentration of CA in the filtrate was measured by UV (UV-2000, Unico, USA) at 323 nm. All experiments were repeated three times.

Pharmacokinetic and biodistribution in vivo experiment

Male SD rats (200 ± 20 g) of SPF class were selected from the Animal Center of Jinzhou Medical University, Jinzhou, China. 9 rats were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 3). CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs suspension (equivalent to 20 mg/kg CA) were injected intranasally. Orbital venous blood was taken at different time points (0.08 h, 0.25 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, 24 h). Plasma was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The above blood samples were stored at -20 °C to be measured.

When the 5xFAD mice reached 6 months of age, the mice were randomly divided into 2 groups. Mice were injected with CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs suspension (equivalent to 20 mg/kg CA) by nasal drip respectively, and brain tissues were collected at different time points (1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h, 6 h). Brain tissues of the same weight were weighed and ground into tissue homogenate by adding saline. The supernatant was taken and stored at -20℃ for measurement.

We optimized the research methodology based on the existing [35]. The 200 µL plasma sample or tissue sample was mixed with 10 µL Phloretin (4 µg/mL) and 400 µL methanol. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 8 min, and the supernatant was extracted. It was determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at a wavelength of 323 nm using methanol and 0.4% phosphoric acid (15:85) as mobile phase, and the metabolic kinetic parameters were calculated by Pksolver 2.0.

CCK-8 experiment

BV2 and SH-SY5Y cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well. An equal volume of CDs-CA-MGs at different concentrations was added to each well (blank and control groups were left). The 96-well plates were incubated in a cell culture incubator with 5% CO2 air at 37 °C for 12 h. 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 0.5 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by an enzyme marker.

Hemolysis test

Hemolysis test was used to evaluate the in vivo safety of CDs-CA-MGs. Whole blood of mice was taken, centrifuged at 3000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min, and mixed with saline to configure 2% (V/V) erythrocyte solution. The solution was mixed with different concentrations of CDs-CA-MGs (100, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200, 1400 µg/mL) in equal volumes and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 576 nm using a microplate reader (Versa Max, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Luminosity. Saline was used as a negative control and deionized water as a positive control. All experiments were repeated three times. Percentage of hemolysis (%) = (sample - negative control)/(positive control - negative control). Hemolysis was considered as hemolysis if the percentage of hemolysis exceeded 5%.

Preparation of Aβ solution

According to the manufacturer’s instructions and relevant literature [1, 36, 37], a stock solution of 1 mg/mL was prepared by adding 1 mL of deionized water to Aβ (25–35) powder. Incubated at 37 °C for 7 days for aging of Aβ monomers to form Aβ oligomers, which were diluted to 10 µM for in vitro experiments. Aβ monomer and Aβ oligomers were co-cultured with certain concentrations of CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs, respectively. The structural changes of Aβ were observed using TEM.

Cell culture and model establishment

SH-SY5Y cells and BV2 cells were placed in complete medium (DMEM + 10% fetal bovine serum + 1% penicillin-streptomycin) and incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2 until the growth stabilization period for cell experiments. Aβ oligomers was diluted to 10 µM and added into the cell culture medium of SH-SY5Y and BV2 cells, to simulate the damaged neurons in Alzheimer’s disease. The cell experiments were categorized into 5 groups of Control, Aβ, CA (Aβ + 10µg/ml CA), CDs-CA (Aβ + 80µg/ml CDs-CA), and CDs-CA-MGs (Aβ + 60μg/ml CDs-CA-MGs) (equivalent to 10ug/ml CA) [38, 39]. Collect the supernatant of BV2 cells and use it as conditioned medium for experiments [40–42].

Cellular uptake experiment

The fluorescence intensity of CDs-CA-MGs was measured by confocal laser scanning microscopy to assess the uptake of CDs-CA-MGs by SH-SY5Y and BV2 cells. Briefly, SH-SY5Y and BV2 cells were co-incubated with CDs-CA-MGs solution for 12 h. After fixed staining, the cells were photographed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), and the fluorescence intensity of each group was analyzed by ImageJ software.

DCFH-DA experiment

SH-SY5Y and BV2 cells were induced by 10µM Aβ (25–35) for 24 h, then PBS, CA, CDs-CA, CDs-CA-MGs were added and the cells were incubated for 12 h. The cell culture medium was changed, loaded with 10µM DCFH-DA probe, and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were washed with PBS for three times to fully remove the DCFH-DA probe. Observe with an inverted microscope.

Flow cytometry

SH-SY5Y, BV2 cells were induced by 10µM Aβ (25–35) for 24 h, then PBS, CA, CDs-CA, CDs-CA-MGs were added and incubated with cells for 12 h. The cell culture medium was changed, loaded with 10µM DCFH-DA probe, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed with PBS three times to fully remove the DCFH-DA probe. Digest the treated cells with 0.25% trypsin EDTA, centrifuge, and resuspend the cells with PBS. The cell suspension was placed in a flow assay tube and detected by flow cytometry.

ELISA assay

Collect cell supernatant and tissue homogenate. Standard wells, blank wells, and sample wells were added sequentially to the enzyme marker plate according to the instructions. Then, 100 µL of biotinylated antibody working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Wash the plate 3 times. Add 100 µL of HRP enzyme conjugate working solution per well and incubate at 37 °C for 30 min. Wash the plate 5 times. Add 90 µL of substrate solution (TMB) to each well and incubate at 37℃ for 15 min, avoiding light. Add 50 µL of termination solution to terminate the reaction. Immediately measure the OD value of each well at 450 nm with an enzyme labeler.The expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α and Aβ were calculated in each group according to the instructions.

THT fluorescence intensity detection

Dilute the Aβ monomer solution to 20µM, add a certain concentration of CA, CDs-CA, CDs-CA-MGs solution and mix well. Thioflavin T (THT) was added and incubated at 37℃. The fluorescence signal intensity at the excitation wavelength of 450 nm and the emission wavelength of 485 nm were recorded at 4 time points, i.e., 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, respectively.

Establishment of animal models

5xFAD and C57BL/6 male mice were purchased from the Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co. Mouse DNA was extracted by alkaline lysis: 0.1 cm of mouse tail tissue was taken, 100 µL of lysate (25 mmol/L NaOH + EDTA 0.2 mmol/L) was added, and the sample was incubated for 70 min at 90 °C in a metal heated pan. After cooling, an equal amount of 40 mmol of Tris-HCl was added, and then the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant is the desired mouse DNA. The supernatant is the desired mouse DNA solution. PCR amplification was performed using BeyoFusion™ PCR Master Mix (2X). Mouse genotypes were identified by differences in agarose gel electrophoresis bands. 5xFAD mice produce Aβ protein at 1.5 months of age, which rapidly accumulates in the brain, with significant pathological changes at 6 months of age. We selected 6-month-old 5xFAD and C57BL/6 male mice for animal experiments. They were randomly divided into 5 groups (control, AD, CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs) with 27 mice in each group. Mice were given CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs suspensions (equivalent to 20 mg/kg CA) intranasally three times daily for 8 weeks [43].

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation mouse model: To further investigate the effects of CDs-CA-MGs on inflammation-based cognitive dysfunction. Referring to existing studies [26], we intraperitoneally injected LPS (0.25 mg/kg) for 7 consecutive days to establish a mouse model of LPS-induced cognitive impairment. Mice were simultaneously administered suspensions of CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs (equivalent to 20 mg/kg CA) for 11 days. 3 days later, behavioral tests were performed.

In vivo targeting and uptake experiments

To verify the targeting effect of CDs-CA-MGs, CDs-CA-MGs (20 mg/kg) was injected by nasal drip administration, and brain tissues and hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of mice were taken at set time points. The fluorescence signals in each group of tissues were detected at 518 nm and 494 nm using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer).

Brain slices were rewarmed at room temperature for 2 h, washed three times with PBS, incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min, and washed three times with PBS. Incubate with goat serum (10-fold dilution of the original solution) for 2 h, incubate with primary antibody at 4℃ for 16 h, wash with PBS for 3 times, incubate with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h, wash with PBS for 3 times. DAPI was added dropwise to seal the plate. The fluorescence intensity of each group was analyzed by ImageJ software.

Behavioral experiments

Water maze experiment: Mice were treated for 8 weeks and measured in a water maze with a diameter of 1.2 m. The temperature of the test water was maintained at 22 ± 1 °C. The mice were trained in the water maze. During the training period, a 10 cm platform was fixed at 1 cm underwater. For the first 5 days, mice were trained 4 times at fixed positions in each quadrant of the maze. On day 6, the platform was removed to test the mice’s learning and memory ability. The mice entered the water at a random location in the quadrant away from the platform and swam freely for 60 s. The time to reach the platform, the swimming speed, and the number of times the mice crossed the platform area were recorded.

Nesting experiment: Different groups of mice were moved to cages with the addition of six sheets of paper (6 × 6 cm2). These mice were allowed to move randomly and their nesting behavior was recorded daily by taking pictures and calculating scores.

Nissl staining and HE staining

The specimens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, rinsed with ultrapure water for 4 h, then subjected to gradient dehydration, transparency, wax dipping and embedding, and cut into sections of 6 μm thickness. The slices were cut into sections of 6 μm thickness.

Nissl staining: The paraffin sections were routinely deparaffinized to water, and then operated according to the steps of the kit. The sections were stained with methylene blue staining solution for 10 min, differentiated with Nissl differentiation solution for 1 min, and observed under the microscope until the Nissl bodies were clear. Ammonium molybdate solution was treated for 2 min and rinsed with deionized water. Anhydrous ethanol was used for dehydration, xylene was used for transparency, and neutral gum was used for sealing. Observed using an orthogonal microscope.

HE staining: Tissue paraffin sections were routinely deparaffinized to water, following the steps of the kit. The sections were stained with hematoxylin staining solution for 15 min, differentiated with differentiation solution for 30 s, and soaked in tap water for 3 times, each time for 5 min. the sections were put into eosin staining solution for 2 min, and the excess staining solution was thrown off and then dehydrated quickly, and then the sections were transparent in xylene and sealed with neutral gum. The slices were observed with an orthogonal microscope.

Western blotting

The expression levels of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α) and Aβ cleaving enzymes (APP, BACE) were detected in mouse brain tissues by Western blotting. Proteins were extracted from the brain tissues of mice in each group by RIPA lysate (RIPA: PMSF = 100:1). The protein concentration of each group of samples was detected by BCA quantification kit and the samples were prepared. An 8%/10% protein gel was prepared and electrophoresis was performed at a constant voltage of 150 V. Then it was transferred onto PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% blocking solution using skimmed milk powder at room temperature for 2 h. After blocking, the primary antibody (anti-IL-1β, 1:1000; anti-IL-6, 1:1000, anti-IL-10, 1:1000, anti-TNF-α, 1:1000; anti-APP, 1:1000; anti-BACE, 1:500; anti-GAPDH, 1:1000; anti-β-Tubulin, 1:20000) was incubated at 4 ℃ on a shaker for 16 h. The primary antibody was recovered and incubated at room temperature on a shaker. The primary antibody was recovered and the secondary antibody was incubated with shaking at room temperature for 2 h. The bands were visualized on a gel imaging system using ECL chemiluminescent agent (A: B = 1:1) and analyzed by ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

After cell induction treatment, 4% PFA was fixed for 30 min, washed 3 times with PBS. 0.1% Triton was incubated for 15 min. Brain tissue sections were dried at room temperature for 30 min, washed three times with PBS. 0.3% Triton was incubated for 30 min and washed three times with PBS. The sections were blocked with goat serum for 2 h, incubated with primary antibody at 4℃ for 16 h (anti-IL-1β, 1:500; anti-IL-6, 1:500, anti-β-Tubulin, 1:1000). The sections were washed three times with PBS, incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The sections were washed three times with PBS, and incubated with DAPI for 15 min. The sections were photographed by using an orthogonal microscope, and fluorescence intensities were analyzed in each group by ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data in the experiment were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and expressed as mean ± SD deviation. Comparisons between data from multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Comparisons between groups were made by LSD-t or Tambane’s analysis; if the variance was not uniform, Kruskal-Wallis test was used. p < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of CDs-CA-MGs

Referring to the literature, we synthesized CDs with carboxyl groups using a hydrothermal method [33]. The TEM results showed that the CDs were well dispersed and had regular and uniform particle structures, and the particle size was approximately 5 nm. CDs-CA-MGs exhibited an obvious vesicle structure with a particle size of approximately 50 nm (Fig. 1A). DLS results showed that the CDs was 3.22 nm, and the value for the CDs-CA-MGs was 55 nm (Fig. 1B). The zeta potential of the CDs was − 11.35 ± 3.31 mV, and that of the CDs-CA-MGs was − 22.83 ± 3.83 mV. This potential change confirmed the successful synthesis of this material (Fig. 1C). The structural information of the functional groups and chemical bonds of the CDs-CA-MGs samples was obtained using FT-IR. For the CDs, the absorption peak at 3439 cm− 1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the O-H bond. The absorption peaks at 1586 cm− 1 and 1404 cm− 1 are attributed to the vibrations of C = O and O-H, respectively. The absorption peak at 1258 cm− 1 is the C-H bending vibration peak of C = C. The results show that the synthesized CDs are graphitized carbon with hydroxyl, carboxyl and methyl groups on the surface. The absorption peak of CDs at 1289 cm− 1 is weakened due to the reaction of the amino group on the surface of APBA with -COOH on the surface of CDs, resulting in the reduction of the aniline bond there. The absorption peak of APBA at 1602 cm− 1 is shifted to 1579 cm− 1 due to the superimposed absorption of amide-bonded CO-NH at 1579 cm− 1. This finding indicates that the -COOH on the CDs reacts with the amino group on APBA to form amide bonds. The absorption peaks of the borate bond are mainly located at 1200–1400 cm− 1. In CDs-CA, the telescopic vibration peak of C-O of the borate bond is at 1373 cm− 1, and the characteristic absorption peak of B-O-C in the borate bond is at 1161 cm− 1. This finding indicates that the o-diphenol hydroxyl group in CA reacts with the borate group to form a borate bond. In summary, we synthesized CDs with carboxyl groups using a hydrothermal method. These CDs reacted with the amino group on APBA to form amide bonds, and the boronic acid group in APBA reacted with the o-diphenol hydroxyl group on CA to form boronic acid ester bonds, confirming the successful synthesis of CDs-CA (Fig. 1D). To further demonstrate the successful encapsulation of CDs-CA by microglia, we examined protein expression in the cell membrane on the surface of CDs-CA-MGs using SDS‒PAGE. The proteins in the CDs-CA-MGs group were basically the same as those in the control group (MGs), providing direct evidence for the successful synthesis of CDs-CA-MGs (Fig. S1). The drug loadings of CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs were 12.9% and 17.1%, respectively, as determined by UV spectrophotometry. CDs-CA-MGs showed a stronger drug loading capacity, which is favorable for increasing the concentration of drug aggregates at the site of injury. A severe inflammatory response has been documented in the AD brain. The pathological microenvironment of neuroinflammation is weakly acidic. Therefore, we examined the release behavior of drugs in a simulated microenvironment in vitro. As shown in Fig. 1E, 90.59 ± 1.09% of the CDs-CA-MGs were released after 12 h in a weakly acidic release medium. The amount of drug released was significantly greater than that released in neutral medium (31.49 ± 2.18%). The release rate was faster than that of the neutral release medium. This finding suggests that the CDs-CA-MGs have pH-sensitive release characteristics and high drug release efficiency in weakly acidic environments, which is conducive to improving the bioavailability of the drug for therapeutic efficacy. The results of pharmacokinetic experiments further verified this conclusion. As shown in Fig. 1F, the t1/2 value of the CA group was 1.25 ± 0.25 when administered via the nose, and the drug content was close to 0 and almost completely metabolized at 8 h. The t1/2 values of the CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs groups were 1.58 ± 0.38 and 2.50 ± 2.17, respectively. The AUC is the area under the drug-time curve, reflecting the degree of absorption and utilization of the drug in the body. The AUC0 − t of CDs-CA-MGs (2725.67 ± 462.62) was significantly 4-fold greater than that of CA (652.17 ± 155.78), indicating that CDs-CA-MGs significantly increased the bioavailability of CA. The biosafety of CDs-CA-MGs was tested using CCK-8 assays, hemolysis assays, blood biochemical analysis and HE staining. The CCK-8 assay results showed that greater than 80% cell viability was noted for the CDs-CA-MGs group with drug concentrations in the range of 0 to 150 µg/mL (Fig. 1I). CDs-CA-MGs exhibited low cytotoxicity in a certain range. A hemolysis assay further verified the biosafety of CDs-CA-MGs in vitro. As shown in Fig. 1G, H, 1400 µg/mL CDs-CA-MGs showed no significant hemolysis (1.02 ± 0.05%), whereas CDs-CA showed a slight tendency toward hemolysis at the same concentration (1.63 ± 0.29%). The hemolysis rates were all less than 5%. In addition, 8 weeks after CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs administration, blood was collected from the mice for biochemical analysis (Fig. 1J). As shown in Fig. 1K, CDs-CA-MGs did not affect the normal function of the liver, kidney or heart. HE staining revealed that the mouse heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys still exhibited normal cellular structure and morphology, and no obvious damage occurred (Figure S2).

CDs-CA-MGs attenuate ROS and inhibit inflammatory responses in BV2 cells

ROS levels in BV2 cells were detected with a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. The results showed that the ROS levels in BV2 cells increased significantly after Aβ induction. After treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs, the ROS fluorescence level was reduced to different degrees in all three groups compared with that in the Aβ group (Aβ: 209.27 ± 9.99, CA: 155.64 ± 8.41, CDs-CA: 124.82 ± 8.43, CDs-CA-MGs: 84.78 ± 9.20). The reduction in the ROS fluorescence level in the CDs-CA-MGs group was the most significant, which was about 0.41 times that of group Aβ (Fig. 3A, B). This result was further verified by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3C, D, the addition of Aβ induced a significant increase in the ROS level in BV2 cells. Compared with those in the Aβ group (22.50 ± 0.96), the ROS fluorescence levels in the CA (16.67 ± 1.25), CDs-CA (11.90 ± 0.50), and CDs-CA-MGs groups (6.51 ± 1.12) were reduced to different degrees, and the reduction in the ROS fluorescence levels of the CDs-CA-MGs group was the most significant. All the above results demonstrate that CDs-CA-MGs were able to reduce the ROS levels caused by early Aβ protein release. Many studies have demonstrated that microglia-mediated inflammatory responses accelerate the deterioration of AD. We preliminarily quantified the effect of CDs-CA-MGs on the secretion of inflammatory factors using ELISA. As shown in Fig. 3E-H, the levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β (141.68 ± 6.39 pg/mL), IL-6 (1967.98 ± 60.83 pg/mL), and TNF-α (261.63 ± 22.56 pg/mL) were significantly elevated in BV2 cells after Aβ induction compared with those in the control group. Moreover, the content of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 (15.86 ± 3.43 pg/mL) decreased. Compared with those in the Aβ group, the levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α decreased and the level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased in BV2 cells after treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs. The changes in the levels of inflammatory factors in the CDs-CA-MGs group were the most significant (IL-1β: 52.43 ± 7.87 pg/mL, IL-6: 550.94 ± 27.89 pg/mL, TNF-α: 75.06 ± 14.28 pg/mL, IL-10: 79.51 ± 11.70 pg/mL). Cellular immunofluorescence experiments further verified this result. As shown in Fig. 3I and J, after treatment with A, the fluorescence signal intensity of the pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β (132.07 ± 3.77) and IL-6 (172.03 ± 14.79) was significantly increased. CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs were able to reduce the fluorescence signal intensity of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-6. The fluorescence intensity of cytokines was most significantly reduced in the CDs-CA-MGs group (IL-1β: 76.93 ± 1.56, IL-6: 81.53 ± 6.15) (Fig. S3). In addition, we analyzed the changes in the expression levels of inflammatory factors induced by Aβ by Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 3K-N and S4, the expression levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β (1.143 ± 0.19), IL-6 (1.24 ± 0.11), and TNF-α (1.20 ± 0.18) were significantly increased in BV2 cells after Aβ induction compared with those in the normal group. Moreover, the level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 (0.30 ± 0.06) decreased. The levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly decreased after treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs. The content of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased, and the inflammatory response improved. Among them, the anti-inflammatory effect of CDs-CA-MGs was most significant (IL-1β: 0.54 ± 0.10, IL-6: 0.47 ± 0.17, TNF-α: 0.63 ± 0.14, IL-10: 0.93 ± 0.02). The above experimental results proved that CDs-CA-MGs strongly inhibited the inflammatory response.

CDs-CA-MGs reduce neuronal apoptosis by inhibiting inflammation

The effect of CDs-CA-MGs on neuronal cells after the reduction in the inflammatory response was verified. We used conditioned medium to treat SH-SY5Y cells. The medium of BV2 cells was collected and added to SH-SY5Y cell medium (Fig. 4A). ROS levels in SH-SY5Y cells were detected with a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. The results showed that the ROS fluorescence levels were significantly greater in the Aβ group (123.33 ± 15.17) and the CA (96.34 ± 3.36), CDs-CA (79.65 ± 9.21), and CDs-CA-MGs groups (63.11 ± 3.72) than in the control group. Compared with those of the Aβ group, after treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs, the ROS fluorescence levels of all three groups were reduced to different degrees, and the reduction in ROS fluorescence levels in the CDs-CA-MGs group was the most significant (Fig. 4B, S5). Then, we assessed the role of CDs-CA-MGs in protecting neurons by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 4C, the rate of apoptotic neurons was significantly greater in the Aβ-treated group than in the control group. After treatment with CDs-CA-MGs, The apoptotic cell rate decreased from 15.57 ± 1.29 to 5.63 ± 0.70, and apoptosis significantly improved. This finding indicates that after the inflammatory response weakened, the neuronal ROS level decreased, and the number of apoptotic neurons decreased.

CDs-CA-MGs effectively inhibit Aβ aggregation

The Aβ protein often exists in three aggregated forms in the brain. Oligomers produce much greater toxic effects on neurons than monomers and fibers. We investigated whether the CDs-CA-MGs inhibit Aβ aggregation after the inflammatory response was reduced. We quantitatively analyzed the changes in Aβ content in SH-SY5Y cells with an ELISA kit. As shown in Fig. 4E, compared with that in the Aβ group (173.96 ± 2.85 pg/mL), the Aβ content of SH-SY5Y cells in the CA (135.82 ± 1.70 pg/mL), CDs-CA (87.67 ± 7.22 pg/mL), and CDs-CA-MGs groups (83.04 ± 3.57) decreased significantly. Among them, the Aβ content in the CDs-CA-MGs group decreased the most. This suggests that CDs-CA-MGs may reduce Aβ aggregation by inhibiting the inflammatory response. In addition, we also treated Aβ fibers and monomers in vitro with conditioned medium. The culture medium of BV2 cells was collected and added to the Aβ fiber and monomer solutions (Fig. 4D). The effects of CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs on the structural changes of Aβ were observed by TEM. As shown in Fig. 4G and H, the Aβ monomers presented a spherical structure. The structure of a large number of long-fiber aggregates was gradually revealed after 48 h of aging incubation. After treatment with CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs for a specific period of time, the fibrous aggregates were depolymerized, and the long fibers were gradually shortened to a spherical shape. This finding demonstrated that CDs-CA-MGs could effectively inhibit Aβ aggregation. THT specifically binds to β-folded structures in the Aβ protein. The greater the number of β-folded structures is, the stronger the fluorescence signal intensity. We further explored the effect of CDs-CA-MGs on Aβ fibrillar aggregation based on THT fluorescence intensity. With increasing incubation time, the fluorescence intensity of the Aβ group gradually increased, indicating the formation of β-sheet structures and the gradual aggregation of Aβ monomers to form Aβ protofibrils. Compared with those of the Aβ group, the fluorescence intensities of the CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs groups were significantly lower, and the Aβ fiber structure was significantly inhibited (Fig. 4F). The inhibitory effect was most significant in the CDs-CA-MGs group. Among them, the CDs-CA-MGs group showed the most significant decrease in Aβ content.

Targeting capacity of CDs-CA-MGs in the brains of AD mice

The brain targeting ability of the CDs-CA-MGs was validated using small animal in vivo imaging and tissue immunofluorescence experiments. The results of small animal in vivo imaging showed that after intranasal administration of CDs-CA-MGs, a fluorescent signal first appeared in the nose. After 2 h, the signal rapidly accumulated in the brain tissue (181.86 ± 14.35), and the intensity of the fluorescent signal was the strongest in the brain at 4 h (633.63 ± 44.85). This proves that CDs-CA-MGs accumulate in large quantities in brain tissue, the drug concentration peaks at 4 h, and metabolism gradually begins after 5 h (133.67 ± 6.50) (Fig. 5A, C). The fluorescence signals of the CDs-CA-MGs in the liver and kidney were stronger than those in other organs, demonstrating that the CDs-CA-MGs were metabolized through the liver and kidney (Fig. 5B, D). Meanwhile, we investigated the biological distribution patterns of CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs at 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h and 6 h in mice (Fig. S6). The concentrations of CA in brain tissues at different time points in the CDs-CA-MGs group were higher than those in the CDs-CA group, indicating that CDs-CA-MGs could release more CA at the site of injury and had good targeting ability. The results showed that CDs-CA-MGs could significantly increase the accumulation of CA in brain tissue, and the concentration was highest at 4 h (949.72 ± 345.39 µg/g). This was attributed to the encapsulation of MGs, which enabled CDs-CA to acquire homologous targeting ability and improved the targeting ability to the site of brain injury. And the use of transnasal drug delivery, which was not hindered by the blood-brain barrier, allowed CDs-CA-MGs to accumulate rapidly in the brain and improved the therapeutic efficiency. This suggests that CDs-CA-MGs have great potential in targeting inflammation. In addition, we further determined using brain tissue immunofluorescence experiments which type of brain cells would take up CDs-CA-MGs. As shown in Fig. 5E and F, the uptake of CDs-CA-MGs by microglia was about three times higher than that of neuronal cells, and significantly higher than that of neuronal cells. This suggests that CDs-CA-MGs have the ability to target microglia, which can accumulate at the site of brain injury and play a therapeutic role.

CDs-CA-MGs improve cognitive impairment and protect neurons in AD mice

After 8 weeks of drug administration, we assessed the spatial cognition and memory ability of each group of mice using water maze and nesting experiments. The results of the water maze experiment showed that the swimming paths of the AD mice were evenly distributed across the four quadrants, with no signs of finding the platform (Fig. 6A). This observation indicates that AD mice have low spatial exploration, learning and memory abilities, as well as cognitive dysfunction. After treatment with CA, CDs-CA, or CDs-CA-MGs, the swimming paths of the mice in each group were relatively concentrated in the target area. There were crossings with the platform, indicating better spatial exploration ability. With the increase in the number and duration of training, the escape latency of mice in each group of CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs groups was shortened to different degrees compared with that of the AD group.The shortening was most obvious in the CDs-CA-MGs group(Fig. 6B). After the platform was removed on Day 6, compared with the AD group (181.86 ± 14.35), the mice in the CA (181.86 ± 14.35), CDs-CA (181.86 ± 14.35) and CDs-CA-MGs groups (181.86 ± 14.35) stayed longer in the target quadrant. The most significant change was observed in the CDs-CA-MGs group. Meanwhile, compared with AD group, the number of crossing the platform was significantly increased in the CA, CDs-CA and CDs-CA-MGs groups. The CDs-CA-MGs group was approximately 5 times that of the AD group. This indicated that the mice had good learning, memory and spatial orientation ability (Fig. 6C, D).

Nesting experiments further validated the therapeutic effect of CDs-CA-MGs. Due to cognitive deficits, compared with normal mice, AD mice (0.5 ± 0.55) were unable to complete nest building tasks and had lower nesting scores. After treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs, the nesting ability of the mice in each group was restored to different degrees. Among them, the treatment effect of the CDs-CA-MGs group (3.5 ± 0.55) was the most significant (Fig. S7). Behavioral experiments demonstrated that the nasal administration of CDs-CA-MGs could effectively improve the learning, memory and spatial exploration ability, as well as the nesting behavior in AD mice. CDs-CA-MGs treatment was significantly more effective than CA and CDs-CA. We verified the role of CDs-CA-MGs in protecting neurons using Nissl and HE staining. HE staining revealed that the cells in the control group in the cortical area and CA1 and CA3 areas of the hippocampus were neatly arranged, and the nuclei were obvious. In the AD group, eosinophilic staining and deep cell nuclei staining were observed. After treatment with CDs-CA-MGs, the nuclear staining of cells in the cortical and hippocampal regions of mouse brain tissue decreased, and the number of cell vacuoles decreased (Fig. 6E). Nissl staining showed that the control group had abundant Nissl vesicles and tightly arranged cells. In contrast, Nissl vesicles in the AD group exhibited lighter staining, and their number decreased or even disappeared. After treatment with CDs-CA-MGs, the staining of the Nissl vesicles deepened, and the number of Nissl vesicles increased (Fig. 6F). This finding indicates that CDs-CA-MGs can effectively increase the number of Nissl bodies in brain tissue and restore the function of nerve cells. These results demonstrated that CDs-CA-MGs reduce the damage to neuronal cells caused by the inflammatory response, restore neuronal function, and effectively improve cognitive impairment in AD mice.

Aβ is hydrolyzed from β-amyloid precursor protein (APP). The APP cleavage enzyme (BACE) generates Aβ by cleaving APP. APP and BACE protein levels were examined with Western blotting. After treatment, APP and BACE expression in the brain tissues of the mice was significantly reduced, most significantly in the CDs-CA-MGs group (APP: 0.58 ± 0.12, BACE: 0.64 ± 0.10) (Fig. 7D-G). We verified that CDs-CA-MGs inhibited Aβ aggregation and attenuated cerebral neurotoxicity in mice after the expression level of the Aβ cleaving enzyme was reduced. The expression levels of Aβ plaques in the cerebral cortex and hippocampal CA1 regions of brain tissue was observed using immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of Aβ in the cerebral cortex and hippocampal CA1 regions of the brains of AD mice was significantly increased (cortex: 184.16 ± 10.49, CA1: 144.92 ± 9.31). After treatment, the fluorescence intensity of Aβ in the cortex and hippocampal CA1 regions of mice in the CA (cortex: 145.95 ± 8.98, CA1: 117.99 ± 5.37), CDs-CA (cortex: 122.35 ± 5.91, CA1: 105.63 ± 5.71) and CDs-CA-MGs groups (cortex: 109.53 ± 7.352, CA1: 84.74 ± 9.76) was significantly reduced, most significantly in the CDs-CA-MGs group (Fig. 7A, B). Then, we further analyzed the Aβ protein content in brain tissue using an ELISA kit. The assay results were consistent with the immunofluorescence staining results. Compared with those in the AD group (207.11 ± 10.82 pg/mL), the Aβ content in the brain tissues of AD mice in the CA (150.52 ± 10.08 pg/mL), CDs-CA (130.12 ± 11.94 pg/mL), and CDs-CA-MGs groups (108.93 ± 16.92 pg/mL) was significantly lower. Among them, the CDs-CA-MGs group showed the most significant decrease in Aβ content (Fig. 7C). This finding indicates that CDs-CA-MGs reduce APP and BACE protein levels in the brains of AD mice, effectively inhibiting Aβ aggregation and reducing the Aβ content.

Exploring the mechanism by which CDs-CA-MGs improve cognitive impairment

To further investigate the mechanism by which CDs-CA-MGs improve cognitive impairment in AD mice. We analyzed the expression of related factors in the brain tissues of mice in the AD and CDs-CA-MGs groups using transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 9A). Analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that compared to those in the AD group, 46 genes were upregulated and 131 genes were downregulated in the CDs-CA-MGs group (p-value < 0.05, |log2FC|>1.0) (Fig. 9B). Box plots of gene expression levels (FPKM) were used to characterize the distribution of gene expression for each sample. The FPKM results showed that the gene expression of the samples was basically consistent without major fluctuations (Fig. 9C). The DEGs were used for GO database analysis. GO enrichment analysis showed that down-regulated genes could be categorized into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) [44]. The top 10 DEGs enriched at the BP, CC and MF levels in the GO analysis results. The results showed that microglia were activated to induce neuroinflammation, and proinflammatory cytokines were produced in large quantities. The inflammatory response was reduced in the CDs-CA-MGs group, tumor necrosis factor signaling was downregulated, and chemokine ligand (CCL1, CCL5) signaling was downregulated (Fig. 9D). Based on KEGG analysis, the top 20 down-regulated pathways were listed. We observed that the inflammatory microglia activation-related pathways, i.e., the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and immune response, were affected. These genes are enriched in inflammatory immune responses and neurological processes. Inflammatory signals associated with microglial activation were downregulated after CDs-CA-MGs treatment (Fig. 9E). In the WikiPathway results, it was shown that the therapeutic effects of CDs-CA-MGs is closely related to the regulation of microglia pathogen phagocytosis pathway, IL-1 signaling pathway, oxidative stress and reduction pathway, and BDNF pathway (Fig. S10). Based on the above results, combined with the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network graph. We found a high concentration of central nodes in the network, such as Serpina3i, Serpina3n, II1a, Timp1, CCL3, CCL4, Cd79b, and Pdcd1. These nodal proteins play crucial roles in inflammatory responses, immune regulation, regulation of signaling pathways, and the promotion of macrophage aggregation and activation at sites of inflammation and role. In addition, these proteins are closely associated with cell proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis [45] (Fig. S11). This finding further suggested that the improvement in cognitive deficits in AD mice by CDs-CA-MGs may be related to the mechanism of action of the inflammatory response associated with microglial activation. The results of gene enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on marker gene sets showed that oxidative stress and redox levels were decreased, inflammatory responses were downregulated, and neurological functions were upregulated in the CDs-CA-MGs group. This was consistent with our previous findings (Fig. 9F-H). The above results fully demonstrated that the CDs-CA-MGs could attenuate the neurotoxicity produced by Aβ aggregation by inhibiting the inflammatory response. This action subsequently promotes the recovery of nervous system function and improves cognitive function in AD mice.

CDs-CA-MGs improve cognitive deficits in LPS-treated mice

To further investigate whether microglia-targeted delivery of CA improves inflammation-related cognitive dysfunction, we evaluated the therapeutic effects of CDs-CA-MGs in a LPS-induced inflammation mouse model. Based on the available literature [26], LPS (0.25 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally for 7 consecutive days to establish a mouse model of LPS-induced cognitive impairment. Mice were simultaneously injected with suspensions of CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs for 11 days. After 3 days, the spatial memory capacity and cognitive ability of each group of mice were assessed using the Morris water maze test [26] (Fig. 10A). LPS-induced inflammation model mice had a swimming path similar to that of the AD mice and showed no signs of platform seeking. Compared with those of the control mice, spatial exploration ability, learning and memory ability were significantly reduced. After treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs, the swimming paths of the mice in each group were relatively concentrated within the target area, showing better spatial exploration ability (Fig. 10B). In addition, we analyzed the escape latency, number of platform crossings, and residence time in the target quadrant of mice with LPS-induced inflammation. The results revealed that the escape latency was significantly shorter in the CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs groups than in the LPS group, and the escape latency in the CDs-CA-MGs group was significantly shorter than that in the LPS group (Fig. 10C). After the platform was removed on Day 6, the mice in the CA (20.87 ± 3.11), CDs-CA (26.17 ± 3.52) and CDs-CA-MGs groups (33.60 ± 3.58) stayed in the target quadrant for a longer time and exhibited good learning, memory and spatial orientation ability compared with the LPS group (43.60 ± 4.17). Meanwhile, we observed that mice in the CA (1.83 ± 0.75), CDs-CA (2.17 ± 0.41) and CDs-CA-MGs groups (2.5 ± 0.55) crossed the platform significantly more often than mice in the LPS group (0.67 ± 0.52) (Fig. 10D, E). Behavioral experiments demonstrated that LPS-induced mice exhibited some degree of cognitive impairment. CDs-CA-MGs could effectively improve inflammation-induced cognitive dysfunction to a certain extent. The nesting experiment further validated the therapeutic effect of CDs-CA-MGs on inflammation-dependent cognitive impairment. As shown in Fig. 10F and S9, the mice in the LPS group (1.17 ± 0.41) exhibited lower nesting ability and lower nesting scores than did those in the control group (4.67 ± 0.52). After treatment with CA, CDs-CA, and CDs-CA-MGs, the nesting ability of the mice in each group recovered to different degrees. Among them, the treatment effect of the CDs-CA-MGs group (4.00 ± 0.89) was the most significant. The above experimental results demonstrated that intranasal administration of CDs-CA-MGs improved the learning, memory and spatial exploration ability of LPS-treated mice and improved the nesting ability of LPS-treated mice CDs-CA-MGs effectively improved inflammation-induced cognitive dysfunction and protected cerebral neurons to a certain extent.

Discussion

Cognitive dysfunction is the main symptom of neurodegenerative diseases and seriously affects quality of life. Neuroinflammation is closely related to cognitive dysfunction, and chronic inflammation is present in the brain tissue of patients with neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD [46–48]. Current clinical treatments are still unable to completely cure AD, making the development of effective treatment options and personalized therapeutic strategies particularly important. In this study, we designed a novel bionic nanovesicle containing caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots (CDs-CA-MGs) for the treatment of AD. CDs-CA-MGs were precisely targeted to the site of inflammation through nasal administration to achieve sustained release of the drug at the site of brain injury, effectively improving the bioavailability and therapeutic effect of the drug.

In recent years, the application of nanotechnology in medical research has become increasingly widespread, and CDs have attracted considerable attention due to their low toxicity, good hydrophilicity, easily modifiable surfaces, etc [49, 50]. CA is a natural active substance extracted from coffee grounds that has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects [51]. In this study, we report for the first time a novel drug delivery system in which CA is coupled to CDs using borate bonding(Scheme. 1). AD is a chronic neurological disease that requires long-term treatment. The sustained release of drugs in the brain is the key to improving drug efficacy and therapeutic effectiveness. Due to the presence of borate bonds, CDs-CA exhibited enhanced drug loading and sustained release in acidic environments. According to previous studies, the cell membrane-encapsulated nanoparticle approach shows great potential in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. This approach results in nanoparticles with better biosafety, low immunogenicity, high stability and better targeting ability. Microglia, the most abundant type of immunogenic cell in the brain, play a dominant role in inflammation. Therefore, we chose microglial membranes to improve the brain targeting of the drug (Figs. 2C and 5). After microglial membrane encapsulation, CDs-CA-MGs showed better slow release, targeting ability and good biosafety (Fig. 1, Fig. S1 and S2). CDs-CA-MGs encapsulated by MGs showed better slow release, targeting and biosafety. In the treatment of cerebral neurological diseases, how to maximize drug delivery to the site of brain injury is a matter of concern. Transnasal drug delivery avoids the BBB, and the drug reaches the brain directly through the trigeminal or olfactory pathways [30, 31, 52]. This method of drug delivery is more direct and convenient than intravenous drug delivery. CDs-CA-MGs accumulate rapidly in the brain in a short period of time and effectively target microglia to exert therapeutic effects (Fig. 5 and Fig. S6).

The inflammatory cascade response is a dynamic result of the interaction of various cells at the site of injury [34].In fact, neuroinflammation occurs even earlier than protein aggregation [53]. Increased neuroinflammation can exacerbate cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease [34]. Inflammation may represent the root cause of cognitive impairment in the brain. Protein misfolding and aggregation in the brains of Alzheimer’s patients activate an immune response in the brain, leading to chronic neuroinflammation. This further exacerbates cognitive dysfunction and neurodegenerative disease. Therefore, suppressing neuroinflammation is critical for slowing cognitive impairment caused by neurodegenerative diseases. We induced BV2 and SH-SY5Y cells with the Aβ peptide to mimic pathological processes. The role of CDs-CA-MGs was verified by culturing SH-SY5Y cells in conditioned medium. In BV2 cells, CDs-CA-MGs significantly reduced Aβ peptide-induced ROS levels. In addition, CDs-CA-MGs significantly inhibited the release of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, while increasing the protein level of anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 [54]. Upon stressful conditions, decreased IL-10 causes damage to microglial cells and accelerates inflammation [55]. Thus, up-regulation of IL-10 can reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors and effectively inhibit inflammation (Fig. 3). Furthermore, we verified the protective effect of CDs-CA-MGs on neurons after attenuating inflammation by intervening SH-SY5Y cells with conditioned medium (Fig. 4A, D). After treatment, the level of oxidative stress of SH-SY5Y cells was attenuated, the level of Aβ protein was reduced, and the apoptosis of neurons was improved. Overall, CDs-CA-MGs attenuated the cellular oxidative stress level, inhibited the neuroinflammatory response, decreased the Aβ protein level, and reduced neuronal apoptosis (Fig. 4). During the development of AD, due to the aggregation of Aβ in the brain, microglia are activated to release cytokine proteins that promote neuroinflammation, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which are significantly upregulated. In contrast, the expression of anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 was significantly down-regulated, which may be due to the fact that AD is in the pathological microenvironment of chronic inflammation for a long period of time, resulting in the protein level of IL-10 in AD mice would be lower than that in normal mice. The release of the pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α was significantly decreased and the protein level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 in AD mice after treatment with CDs-CA-MGs increased (Fig. 8). CDs-CA-MGs treatment significantly attenuated the inflammatory response and reduced the number of neuronal apoptosis in the brains of AD mice. It has been reported that microglia activation is associated with Aβ plaque formation, which leads to the development of symptoms of cognitive impairment such as memory loss and learning disabilities in AD [7, 56]. Based on the analysis of the results of behavioral experiments, the CDs-CA-MGs also improved the spatial exploration ability, learning and memory ability of AD mice (Fig. 6A-D, and Fig. S7). This finding further suggested that CDs-CA-MGs can prevent neuronal damage caused by neuroinflammatory responses and effectively protect neurons (Fig. 6E, F). As we all know, Aβ plaques are one of the hallmarks of AD. An imbalance between Aβ production and clearance causes Aβ to accumulate extracellularly, leading to fibrosis and the formation of large deposits or plaques [57]. We found that CDs-CA-MGs can effectively loosen the tight structure of Aβ plaques and inhibit Aβ aggregation. CDs-CA-MGs also reduced APP and BACE protein levels. A dynamic balance between Aβ production and removal was achieved to restore learning, memory and spatial exploration in mice and to improve cognitive impairment in AD mice (Fig. 7).

Microglia are myeloid innate immune cells that are present in the central nervous system [58]. Upon activation, cells internalize the pathogenic factors through cytotoxicity, phagocytosis, or receptor-mediated endocytosis and attempt to degrade them through endocytosis pathways, inducing the expression of relevant gene modules. We found that proinflammatory cytokines, such as the JAK-STAT signaling pathway associated with microglia activation, hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and immune response, interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6), tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), chemokine ligands (CCL1, CCL5), and small molecule messengers (prostaglandins, NO, reactive oxygen species), were produced in large quantities in the brain tissues of AD mice. These genes are enriched in inflammatory immune responses and neurological processes. Inflammatory signaling associated with microglia activation was downregulated after CDs-CA-MGs treatment. This further suggests that the mechanism of action of our CDs-CA-MGs is closely related to microglia pathogen phagocytosis pathway, inflammatory response, IL-1 signaling pathway, as well as oxidative stress and reductive pathways (Fig. 9, Fig. S10 and Fig. S11). This suggests us that the improvement of cognitive deficits in AD mice by CDs-CA-MGs may be related to the mechanism of action of microglia activation-related inflammatory response. Considering neuroinflammation-related disorders other than AD, such as Parkinson’s disease [59]. We therefore used an LPS-induced mouse model to continue to explore whether CDs-CA-MGs improve inflammation-dependent cognitive deficits. A series of experiments demonstrated that the CDs-CA-MGs effectively improved cognitive impairment and protected brain neurons in LPS-induced inflammation model mice to a certain extent (Fig. 10 and Fig. S9). This finding provides a new therapeutic strategy for the future treatment of cognitive impairment. Our findings suggest a possible mechanism of action by which CDs-CA-MGs inhibit inflammatory responses to improve cognitive impairment. However, using inflammation as a therapeutic target to improve cognitive dysfunction in neurological disorders remains a challenge. In future studies, we will explore the mechanism of action of the inflammatory response associated with microglial activation in more depth.

Conclusion

In this study, we designed novel biomimetic nanovesicles containing caffeic acid-coupled carbon quantum dots for the treatment of AD. First, we elucidated the two major advantages of CDs-CA-MGs, i.e., improved drug bioavailability and precise targeting of inflammatory sites in the brain. Then, we explored the efficacy and possible mechanism of action of CDs-CA-MGs in attenuating the inflammatory response for the treatment of AD and improving cognitive impairment. The results of both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed the great therapeutic potential of CDs-CA-MGs in improving cognitive impairment. This nanodelivery system provides new ideas and possibilities for the treatment of AD.
