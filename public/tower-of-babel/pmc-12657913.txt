Enhanced optical visible light positioning via RSS-based evolutionary optimization under LOS and NLOS conditions
Authors: Pankaj Pathak, S Sharavanan, M Karthikeyan, Azath Mubarakali, U Arun Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPositioning through visible light is a wireless method for positioning in optical communication This method has advantages such as low cost, safety and high accuracy compared to positioning based on RF. In this article, the Received Signal Strength algorithm (RSS) is used for locating. A hypothetical room with dimensions of 6 × 6 × 4 (m3) is considered and the receiver is located at a fixed height from the ground. The error due to the presence of shot noise, thermal noise and multipath and under the conditions of low power, in this method is about several tens of centimeters and is significant. Linear approximation itself is the cause of error. In order to reduce the error, genetic algorithm (GA) and particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm, which are an evolutionary method to minimize the error function, have been proposed instead of least squares estimation. The slope results show that the error is significantly reduced. It will also be shown that changing the distance between the LEDs leads to a difference in the positioning error. The higher the transmitted power, the lower the error. On the other hand, as the height increases, the error also increases, and the greater number of transmitters are used, the positioning error decreases. The simulation results show that the presence of reflection increases the positioning error. The value of the root mean square error (RMSE) for the Line-of-Sight (LOS) scenario is equal to 0.3493 m and for the Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS) scenario is equal to 0.4461 m. Finally, a method for choosing the most powerful LEDs is suggested, this choice reduces the positioning error.Keywords: Wireless optical communication, Positioning, Wireless optical communication, Genetic algorithm, Particle swarm algorithm, Least squares estimationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The volume of data sent and received via the Internet has increased significantly in the past decade. The majority of data traffic now comes from new or relatively new services and applications including the Internet of Things (IoT), video conversations on mobile phones, remote video conferencing, cloud computing, and device-to-device connectivity. The increase in the number of devices that have access to the mobile network (smart devices) is the main reason for the sharp increase in mobile data traffic. Besides that, the development of online social services (such as Facebook and Twitter, etc.) is also very effective in increasing data traffic1–4. The fifth generation (5G) telecommunication systems provide many new services with extremely high system capacity, huge device connectivity, very low delay, high security, very low energy consumption and very high quality of user experience. Supporting very high-volume data is a big challenge for G5 telecommunication systems and beyond. Therefore, the increasing growth of mobile data use requires efficient technical solutions to guarantee the quality of services to end users4–7. It is clear that the telecommunications based on RF radio frequency will face limitations due to limited spectrum resources, and the continuation of this growing trend will unintentionally lead to severe congestion of the RF spectrum and its saturation in the near future. Apart from spectrum scarcity issues, RF communication suffers from limitations such as interference, low data rates, expensive licensing, security issues, and high installation and access costs. Despite these limitations and the ever-increasing demand for high-speed Internet services, more innovation, research and development are needed in emerging technologies that are capable of providing extremely high data rates to end users8–12.

In 1880, before inventing the telephone, Alexander Graham Bell had tested his optical telephone, which succeeded in transmitting sound over a distance of more than 200 m using sunlight. This device was able to modulate sunlight with the vibration caused by speech and send this modulated light to a predetermined receiver13. About 20 years later, with the discovery of the LED light emitting diode by the Russian scientist Oleg Losev, real improvements were made14–16.OWC wireless optical communication was initially proposed by Bapst and Gfeller in 1979, using IR light beams. Their research work initiated a growing global research activity and has culminated in what is now known as OWC17,18.Conn and Berry did pioneer work in OWC (mostly infrared band 780–950 nm) in which systems with data rates of 50 Mbps and 70 Mbps were designed in 1996 and 2000. Visible Light Communication (VLC) was introduced by Nakagawa et al. in 2000 at the Visible Light Communication Consortium (VLCC) of ultra-bright white LEDs for data communications10. Since then, the research activities of the researchers continued with exponential growth until 2011, Harald Haasstad of the University of Edinburgh, Scotland, introduced LiFi to the world for the first time18–20. LiFi is a complete wireless optical telecommunication network that, in addition to providing ambient lighting, enables two-way wireless communication with receiver mobility and support for multiple users. In 2014, PureLifi Company exhibited the first commercial LiFi products called Li-1st at the World Mobile Phone Conference in Barcelona21. In 2018, Harald Haas’s team set up the first LiFi network in a trial way in a school in Scotland, and for this purpose, LiFi-XC dongles made by PureLifi company were used. In this method of information transfer, 8 LED lamps are installed on the ceiling of the classrooms and the information is received by receivers connected to the USB port of the computers22–24.

Waves’ frequency and energy both rise as their wavelength falls throughout the electromagnetic spectrum. Radio waves occupy the frequency band from 3 kHz to 300 MHz. Since currently, the severe shortage of RF spectrum is a serious challenge and an important issue for companies providing high-speed telecommunication services, the wireless telecommunication industry, by using radio frequencies above 10 GHz (millimeter wave communications), to this challenge has been answered. Optical wireless communication (OWC) is the continuation of the movement towards higher frequencies22. OWC systems, which use ultraviolet (300 GHz − 430 THz), visible light (430 THz- 790 THz) and infrared (7 THz − 30 PHz) frequency bands for wireless data transmission, as a reliable solution and It is proposed to be reliable to overcome the problem of lack of spectrum. OWC can be considered as an independent or complementary technology for existing RF systems to remove the pressure on the RF spectrum. OWC technology provides unique and attractive features such as the spectrum without the need for an operating license with almost unlimited bandwidth, inherent security, low cost of installation and commissioning, no electromagnetic interference and high energy efficiency26–28.

Recent research in RSS-based visible light positioning (VLP) has increasingly focused on addressing positioning inaccuracies caused by nonlinear RSS–distance relationships, multipath reflections, and low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions27–33. Several recent studies have demonstrated that data-driven and optimization-based techniques can substantially enhance positioning accuracy by mitigating the effects of signal distortion and reflection-induced bias29–31. Machine learning–based channel modeling and reflection-aware optimization frameworks have also been proposed for both indoor and vehicular VLC systems, showing notable improvements in localization robustness under noise and multipath constraints32,33. These developments collectively emphasize the importance of nonlinear and noise-resilient optimization strategies for reliable indoor positioning. In this context, the present work introduces a comparative analysis of Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) as robust alternatives to conventional Linear Least Squares (LLS) methods, aimed at minimizing RSS-based positioning errors under low-SNR and reflection-dominated environments.

Although numerous studies have investigated RSS-based positioning with intelligent algorithms, most efforts either simplify the receiver orientation or neglect multipath interference. The present work establishes a controlled baseline for evaluating the relative effectiveness of GA and PSO in error minimization, which forms the foundation for future extensions involving orientation and multipath-aware positioning. In this article, the genetic algorithm is introduced as the first suggested method for estimating the receiver position. The second proposed method for estimating the position of the receiver and selecting the strongest transmitters is particle swarm optimization.

First proposed method

Estimation of receiver position using genetic algorithm

The nonlinear nature of the received signal strength (RSS)–distance relationship often introduces multiple local minima, making classical linear methods such as LLS insufficient for accurate receiver localization. To address this, a continuous Genetic Algorithm (GA) is employed to perform global optimization of the receiver’s position parameters and minimize the nonlinear cost function.

The GA begins by generating an initial population of potential solutions and iteratively evolves them toward the global optimum through selection, crossover, and mutation operators. Roulette-wheel selection and elitism are adopted to ensure that high-fitness individuals propagate to subsequent generations while maintaining diversity. Typical parameter settings are: population = 300, crossover = 0.6, mutation = 0.01, and 50 generations. Mutation prevents premature convergence and enhances global exploration by slightly perturbing selected genes within allowable limits. The algorithm terminates when the change in the global minimum fitness becomes negligible or the maximum generation count is reached. The resulting coordinates provide the optimized estimate of the receiver’s position. The parameters of the genetic algorithm are given in Table 2. The RMSE corresponding to the configuration with nine LEDs (2.5 cm spacing) is slightly higher than that obtained with five LEDs (1.5 cm spacing). While a larger number of transmitters generally enhances positioning accuracy by providing additional measurement diversity, excessive geometric symmetry can introduce an over-constrained or ill-conditioned system in the LLS approximation, thereby amplifying the influence of measurement noise. In the evolutionary algorithms, this condition manifests as multiple shallow minima in the cost landscape, slightly degrading convergence precision. Therefore, the result reflects a geometric conditioning issue rather than a limitation of the optimization algorithm itself. Similar phenomena have been reported in dense VLP deployments where transmitter redundancy introduces correlation among distance constraints.

These results collectively verify that the proposed GA–PSO method offers superior robustness to noise and reflection compared with the classical LLS approach.

Conclusion

In this paper, the Received Power Intensity (RSS) method is used to estimate the position of the receiver. A hypothetical room with dimensions of 6 × 6 × 4 (m2) was considered, and the height of the receiver from the floor is equal to 1 m. In the RSS algorithm, to determine the location of the receiver, the response of a nonlinear equation device with two unknowns (12.3) which are the length and latitude of the room must be obtained. Therefore, it is suggested to use evolutionary algorithms to find the optimal answer. In order to reduce the error, genetic algorithm and particle swarm optimization algorithm, which are an evolutionary method to minimize the error function were proposed. The results of sloping showed that the error was significantly reduced. What made PSO superior to genetic algorithm was the much shorter execution time of the algorithm. It was also shown that changing the distance between the LEDs leads to a difference in the positioning error and the optimal arrangement is obtained through trial and error. The higher the transmitted power, the lower the error. On the other hand, as the height increases, the error also increases, and the greater number of transmitters are used, the positioning error decreases. The root mean square error (RMSE) value for the LOS scenario is equal to 0.3493 m and for the NLOS scenario is equal to 0.4461 m. The present study focuses on a simplified RSS-based indoor visible light positioning model, primarily emphasizing the effect of transmitter power, LED spacing, and reflection-induced noise on positioning accuracy. The current model assumes perfect photodetector alignment and considers only first-order reflections. In future work, the model will be extended to jointly estimate both receiver position and orientation, and to incorporate higher-order multipath effects. These extensions will require advanced hybrid optimization methods capable of handling strongly non-convex objective functions, such as combined Differential Evolution–PSO or Reinforcement Learning–assisted evolutionary optimization, to further enhance accuracy under realistic indoor conditions.
