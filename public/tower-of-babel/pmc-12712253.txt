DNA methylation-mediated ADA overexpression drives pancreatic cancer progression
Authors: Jingyi Zeng, Juying Jiao, Bo Lin, Hegen Li, Weijie Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is characterized by its aggressive nature, limited options for radiochemotherapy, and poor prognosis, highlighting the urgent need for the identification of novel therapeutic targets to broaden treatment strategies.MethodsWe employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach, utilizing genetically informed druggable genes to identify key genes associated with PC risk. To validate these candidate targets, we conducted sensitivity, colocaliztion, and summary data-based MR (SMR) analyses. Furthermore, DNA methylation mediation analysis was utilized to investigate upstream regulatory mechanisms of significant genes.ResultsAt a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05, five druggable genes were found to be significantly associated with PC. Through rigorous validation, adenosine deaminase (ADA) was identified as a potential contributor to increased PC risk. Mediation analysis indicated that higher methylation at cg20622019 was associated with lower ADA expression and reduced PC risk, with methylation mediating approximately 36% of the association between ADA and PC. Sensitivity analysis, SMR and Colocalization analysis supported our findings, demonstrating high statistical robustness.ConclusionThis study identifies ADA as a prioritized, genetically supported druggable target for PC, supported by colocalization evidence (PPH4 = 0.7949), and elucidates the regulatory role of DNA methylation at the cg20622019 locus. Notably, other significantly screened genes (e.g., PDE2A) failed colocalization validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04014-9.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains one of the most aggressive malignancies, as evidenced by a 5-year survival rate of merely 12.8% [1]. Furthermore, projections indicate that by 2040, PC will become the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [2]. Surgical resection is currently the sole potentially curative option for patients with early-stage disease [3, 4]. However, due to the insidious nature of PC, more than 80% of patients are diagnosed with unresectable disease or early metastases. Existing first-line systemic chemotherapy regimens, such as FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine in combination with albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane), have failed to extend overall survival beyond 12 months [5–7]. Although molecularly targeted therapies have achieved significant success in other cancers, their application in PC has been hindered due to the scarcity of effective druggable targets. This highlights an urgent need to identify and validate novel therapeutic targets to advance PC treatment.

Recently, the integration of Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis with druggable genome data has emerged as a promising approach for drug target discovery. By utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), MR can simulate the conditions of randomized trials, offering a novel avenue for therapeutic development. Such an approach offers robust evidence to support the development of potential therapeutic targets [8, 9]. Moreover, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) within the genome serve as pivotal instruments for the identification and validation of drug targets [10]. Previous studies have successfully utilized the combination of MR and eQTL to validate targets in diseases such as lung cancer [11], rheumatoid arthritis [12], and pulmonary fibrosis [13]. However, no such studies have yet to be applied to evaluate possible treatment targets for PC systematically.

In this study, we employed MR to identify potential drug targets for PC by utilizing eQTL of druggable genes. Furthermore, we explored upstream regulatory mechanisms of these targets through mediation analysis. The study design adheres to the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines [14].

Materials and methods

Study design

The flowchart visually describing the overall of the study is shown in Fig. 1. In the beginning, we carried out a two-sample MR analysis using cis-eQTL of druggable genes in the blood as the exposure and the genome-wide association study (GWAS) of PC as the outcomes to examine the causal connection between druggable gene expression and PC. For the druggable genes showing significant MR results, a series of sensitivity analysis, colocalization analysis, and replication MR analysis at the transcript level were conducted to further validate the relationship between druggable genes and PC at the proteomic level. In the second part, we employed mediation analysis to further investigate how significant druggable genes affect the development of PC by regulating the level of DNA methylation.

Exposure and outcome data

Drug-targeting genes were derived from Finan et al. study [15], which identified a total of 4,479 drug genes and categorized them into three groups of genes, the first containing 1427 genes encoding approved or clinical-phase drug targets, the second containing 682 genes encoding proteins that bind to known drug molecules or have similarities to approved drug targets, and the third containing 2,370 genes that are members of the members of key drug gene families or encoding proteins with distant similarity to approved drug targets (Supplementary Material: Table S2).

The GWAS summary statistics for PC were obtained from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog [16], which includes 587 cases of European ancestry and 455,761 control samples of European ancestry, and can be visualized and downloaded via this website (fast GWA | Yang Lab (westlake.edu.cn)).

Our study exclusively utilized publicly available datasets, each of which received ethical approval in their respective original studies.

Instrumental variables selection

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of our results, the three basic assumptions of MR must be strictly followed [8]: (1) The selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) must be significantly correlated with the exposure factor; (2) The SNPs are independent, and the associations with the outcome must be independent of any confounding factors; (3) There is no presence of horizontal pleiotropy, meaning that the SNPs only linked to the outcome data through the exposure factor.

In accordance with these assumptions, we selected SNPs from the eQTL data using a stringent genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10− 8) and clustered genetic variants with an r2 threshold of 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window. To minimize bias, we excluded weakly instrumented SNPs by applying an F-statistic threshold of 10, calculated as β2 divided by the squared standard error.

Potential confounders were further screened using PhenoScanner V2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to remove SNPs associated with known confounders or other phenotypes.

Mendelian randomization and sensitivity analysis

In this study, we used R4.2.2 statistical software combined with specialized R packages such as Two Sample MR and MR - PRESSO to perform systematic MR analysis. Among the five methods, including inverse variance weighting (IVW) [17], weighted median [18], MR Egger [19], simple model [20], and weighted mode [18] methods, we mainly chose the IVW method as the primary method for MR analysis because of its accuracy and stability. A p-value < 0.05 was considered a significant association between exposure and outcome. We also used the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method to correct the p-value, and associations remaining significant after FDR correction (p < 0.05) were retained.

We assessed the degree of heterogeneity using Cochrane's Q statistic based on IVW and MR Egger methods, with a p-value greater than 0.05 indicated no significant heterogeneity [21]. In addition, MR- Egger regression was used to test for the presence of horizontal pleiotropy in MR analyses, where a p-value greater than 0.05 was considered to be absent [22].

Colocalization analysis

For genes that were significant in MR results, colocalization analysis was performed using the R package coloc. There were five main hypotheses in the colocalization analysis [23]: PPH0: the selected SNPs are not associated with either the druggable genes or PC; PPH1:the selected SNPs are associated with the druggable genes but not PC; PPH2: the selected SNPs are associated with PC but not the druggable genes; and PPH3: the selected SNPs are associated with the druggable genes or PC, but with different causal variants.PPH4: the selected SNPs are associated with both druggable genes and PC with the same causal variants. The threshold of significance for colocalization was set at PPH4 >0.75, which was supportive of a potential drug target [24].

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis

We performed Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) as a complementary validation of the primary MR findings. Cis-eQTLs within a window of ± 1000 kb around the corresponding genes were using a p-value threshold of 1.0 × 10−5, based on a previous study [13]. In addition, the Heterogeneity in Dependency Instrumentation Detection (HEIDI) was used to discriminate between pleiotropic effects and chaining of genetic variants. When a p-value was less than 0.05, it implied that the genetic variant might have an effect by affecting multiple biological pathways rather than a single causal relationship. Therefore, such variants were excluded from the analysis process. SMR analysis was performed using the SMR software downloaded from the website(https://cnsgenomics.com/software/smr/) (version 1.3.1). Cis-eQTLs data in SMR format can be downloaded from the publicly available link (https://molgenis26.gcc.rug.nl/downloads/eqtlgen/cis-eqtl / SMR _ formatted / cis-e QTL-SMR _ 20191212. tar.gz) to access it. Results would be considered positive and valid when the SMR p-value was less than 0.05 and the p-value of the HEIDI test was over 0.05.

Mediation analysis

We employed two-step Mendelian randomization (TSMR) [25] to assess the potential mediating role of DNA methylation in the causal pathway linking PC and druggable genes. The TSMR approach assumes that exposure factors influence the mediator variable independently of the mediator’s own effects. We initially estimated the total effect of druggable genes on PC using two-sample MR analysis. The mediation effect was quantified by multiplying the effect of DNA methylation on drug-target genes (β₁) by its effect on PC (β₂). The direct effect was calculated as the difference between the total effect and the mediation effect. A mediation effect was considered present when all causal relationships among druggable target genes, PC, and DNA methylation demonstrated consistent directional effects.

DNA methylation data were obtained from the Genetics of DNA Methylation Consortium (GoDMC) [26]. CpG sites associated with druggable targets were selected from the GoDMC mQTL dataset based on a significance threshold of p < 1 × 10–5, and linkage disequilibrium pruning was conducted using an r2 < 0.1 within 100 kb window to ensure independence among SNP-CpG pairs.

Detailed statistical results are presented in Supplementary Tables S3–S12.

Results

Based on strict criteria for IVs, 40,356 SNPs were used as IVs for 2,525 druggable genes. Interestingly, all IVs had F-statistics of more than 10, indicating a strong instrumental effect without any signs of weak instrument bias. (Supplementary Table S3)

MR analysis of the relationship between druggable genes and PC

Using the IVW approach with a FDR below 0.05 and a p-value less than 0.001, we identified five druggable genes showing significant associations with PC risk. MARK3, NOD2 and CD52 were found to be protective factors against PC, whereas PDE2A and ADA were found to be risk factors (Supplementary Material: Table S4). The Cochrane’s Q test showed no significant heterogeneity for any genes (Supplementary Tables S5). In the test for horizontal pleiotropy(Supplementary Table S6), NOD2, CD52, PDE2A and ADA showed no pleiotropy, only MARK3 was excluded from subsequent analyses due to pleiotropy. Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis enables a method to assess the presence of shared causal variation in the region for two traits and helps to exclude confounding factors [26]. For druggable genes with significant MR results, we performed colocalization analyses to calculate the probability of shared causal variants for ci-eQTL and PC results. The results of the colocalization analysis showed that only ADA (PPH4 = 0.7949) exceeded the predefined threshold of 0.75. This finding suggests a shared genetic etiology between ADA and PC risk. (Supplementary Table S7)

SMR analysis

SMR is a MR analysis method using publicly available GWAS summary statistics, which has higher statistical efficacy compared to traditional MR analysis [27]. Using the SMR method, we identified a positive association between ADA and PC risk progression (p = 0.001, OR = 1.636), with HEIDI test results (p = 0.549) supporting this relationship. These findings align with our prior analyses (Supplementary Table S8).

Mediation analysis

Through the analysis in Part I, we determined that ADA plays a key role in the pathogenesis of PC. To deeply investigate how DNA methylation regulates the expression level of ADA, which in turn affects the development and progression of PC, mediation analysis was implemented in this study. We screened seven CpG sites associated with ADA from the GoDMC database, including cg06079428, cg0828393, cg11091439, cg06049791, cg09030830, cg18704595 and cg20622019. Based on the strict screening criteria, only 2 CpG loci were finally eligible. cg11091439 was excluded due to an insufficient number of conditionally independent instruments for both the exposure to mediator and mediator to outcome steps, precluding reliable effect estimation and HEIDI method exclusion of linkage. At last, only the cg20622019 locus was analyzed in depth.

In two-step MR, higher methylation at cg20622019 was associated with lower ADA expression (IVW p = 4.33 × 10− 8, OR = 0.436) and reduced PC risk (IVW p = 0.0086, OR = 0.678) and accounted for 36% mediation effect (p = 0.0057, 95% CI= -0.61774 — -0.1051). These findings support a methylation-mediated downregulation of ADA as a protective pathway. While cg20622019 was the only CpG meeting all analytic criteria, sensitivity analyses indicated robustness of the mediation direction. Nonetheless, broader site-level mediation remains to be tested when stronger instruments and multi-tissue mQTLs become available. (Supplementary Table S9-S12)

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive genetic evidence establishing ADA as a causal therapeutic target for PC through convergent multi-omics approaches. Our key innovation not only lies in establishing the causal relationship between ADA expression and PC risk but also in identifying the upstream epigenetic mechanism by which cg20622019 methylation modulates ADA expression, accounting for 36% of the mediated association.

While ADA canonically maintains purine homeostasis by degrading adenosine (ADO), theoretically conferring anti-tumor effects [28, 29], clinical observations [30, 31] and our causal evidence unequivocally demonstrate that elevated ADA associates with poor prognosis in PC. This paradox suggests ADA exerts dual mechanisms in tumor development and progression. First, ADA forms signaling complexes with CD26, enhancing tumor cell migration and invasion while activating the IL-6/STAT3, TNF-α/NF-κB, and IFN-γ pathways to sculpt a tumor microenvironment characterized by both inflammation and immunosuppression [32–35]. Second, its overexpressed activity becomes detrimental rather than beneficial, being pathologically co-opted by the tumor. While degrading extracellular ADO, it drives intracellular purine metabolic imbalances favorable to malignant proliferation and couples with compensatory immunosuppressive pathways mediated by T-cells and myeloid lineages, collectively sustaining the malignant state [36]. Consequently, tumors in vivo progressively develop an adaptive dependency on the "low-ADO, high-invasiveness" pathological niche remodeled by ADA, exploiting it to promote survival and proliferation. Conversely, in vitro, studies confirm that pharmacological ADA inhibition reduces PC cell viability by induing rapid ADO accumulation to toxic level, thereby triggering apoptosis [29, 37, 38]. This indicates the therapeutic potential of targeting ADA in clinical translation, although robust preclinical models and early-phase clinical trials remain essential to evaluating ADA inhibitors, particularly when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Although the precise mechanistic role of ADA in PC remains incompletely understood, our mediation analysis provides preliminary evidence for upstream epigenetic regulation. Our mediation analysis demonstrated that hypermethylation at cg20622019 was associated with reduced ADA expression and decreased pancreatic cancer risk, mediating approximately 36% of the genetic association. Genomic context analysis revealed that this CpG site is located at chr20:43,279,793 (hg19) within an intergenic region approximately 1.34 Mb upstream of the ADA gene, outside classical enhancer sequences or CpG islands [39, 40]. Such long-range regulatory mechanism may resemble the distant gene control achieved by super-enhancers and the β-globin locus control region [41–43]. We hypothesize that hypermethylation at cg20622019 disrupts the binding of structural proteins such as CTCF, reducing chromatin looping frequency with the ADA promoter and thereby decreasing ADA transcription [44, 45]. Additionally, ADA activity-regulated adenosine metabolism may influence S-adenosylmethionine availability through one-carbon metabolism pathways, establishing a metabolic feedback regulation of DNA methylation [46, 47]. Unlike genetic variants, DNA methylation is reversible and pharmacologically modifiable, providing potential opportunities for epigenetic-targeted therapy [48, 49]. However, these regulatory hypotheses require further experimental validation, such as functional validation through CRISPR-dCas9 epigenome editing.

ADA inhibitors such as pentostatin have demonstrated therapeutic potential in malignant tumor treatment [50]. However, their clinical application faces significant challenges. First, conventional ADA inhibitors like pentostatin exhibit relatively short plasma half-lives (3–9.6 h), and non-selective ADA blockades may precipitate adverse effects including immunosuppression and hepatotoxicity [51]. Moreover, the dense desmoplastic stroma and activated cancer-associated fibroblasts characteristic of PC substantially impede drug penetration, while chemoresistant cancer stem cells and the distinctive tumor microenvironment further compromise therapeutic efficacy [52, 53]. Given these challenges, future therapeutic strategies should transition from monotherapy to multifaceted combination approaches, and from universal treatment paradigms to precision-guided interventions. First, we propose utilizing cg20622019 methylation status as a companion biomarker for patient stratification, thereby identifying individuals most likely to benefit from ADA-targeted therapy. Second, combination strategies pairing ADA inhibitors with stromal modulators should be pursued to enhance drug penetration through the fibrotic barrier. Additionally, the development of tumor-activated prodrug formulations or targeted nanocarrier systems would enable tumor-specific ADA inhibition while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Beyond ADA, we observed MR signals for PDE2A, NOD2, and CD52. However, colocalization analysis only supports ADA (PPH4 = 0.7949), while these genes did not pass colocalization validation, indicating limited evidence for shared causal variants. Accordingly, these genes should be considered exploratory leads pending independent replication and mechanistic validation [54]. Their identification highlights the heterogeneity of PC and lays emphasis on the necessity for personalized therapeutic strategies. As a phosphodiesterase enzyme, PDE2A is involved in the intracellular hydrolysis of cyclic adenosine monophosphate and cyclic guanosine monophosphate. And then it regulates the concentration of these second messengers and participates in a broad range of physiological activities [55]. PDE2A has been implicated in inhibiting hepatocellular, biliary, and PCs by affecting tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [56]. NOD2, a pattern recognition receptor involved in inflammatory and cellular stress responses, influences NF-κB pathway activity, which regulates the expression of ABC transporters associated with drug resistance, thereby affecting the sensitivity of PCs to chemotherapeutic drugs [57]. CD52 is a glycoprotein widely expressed in various immune cells. It can bind to the Siglec-10 receptor on T cells [58], inhibiting T cell function, and is used as an immunosuppressant for the treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia and lymphoma [59]. It also has been shown to regulate the proliferation and apoptosis of lung cancer via the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [60], although its role in PC remains to be elucidated.

Analysis of 1,847 PC samples from TCGA, GEO, and other consortium-derived GWAS datasets provided robust support for our findings, leveraging comprehensive genetic variation data. The public availability of these datasets not only facilitates the validation process but also motivates the scientific community to conduct additional research. We followed strict screening procedures for choosing IVs to reduce bias and guarantee the accuracy of our MR analysis. By combining colocalization, SMR, and other techniques, we were able to further validate the functions of pharmacogenetic genes and determine their importance at different levels. Furthermore, we minimized potential horizontal pleiotropy by restricting our analysis to cis-eQTL variants. In contrast to previous research, we employed mediation analysis to systematically investigate associations between ADA and PC through potential mechanistic pathways. Combined methylation and expression profiling elucidated molecular mechanisms underlying ADA dysregulation in PC.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our results. First, the relatively small number of PC cases (n = 587) may result in insufficient statistical power, particularly for detecting associations with small effect sizes, potentially leading to the oversight of other therapeutic targets. Future larger-scale GWAS studies will be essential to validate the universality of the ADA association and explore additional targets that may have been missed due to power limitations. Second, while MR analysis simulates randomized trial conditions using genetic variation as instrumental variables, it remains susceptible to residual confounding factors, including unmeasured pleiotropic effects and population stratification, which may influence causal interpretations. Third, because our cohort consisted exclusively of European ancestry participants, the generalizability of our findings to other populations remains uncertain, necessitating larger multi-ethnic studies to ensure broad applicability. Finally, the clinical translation of ADA as a therapeutic target requires rigorous experimental validation through preclinical models and clinical trials to confirm its biological mechanisms and therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, leveraging MR, SMR, and co-localization, our study provides convergent genetic evidence that prioritizes ADA as a promising and potentially druggable target for PC. Colocalization analysis only supports ADA (PPH4 = 0.7949), whereas other significantly screened genes (e.g., PDE2A) did not pass colocalization validation. Mediation analysis further clarifies that methylation at cg20622019 likely confers protection by downregulating ADA expression. However, a series of further thorough evaluations, including laboratory studies and clinical trials, are necessary to determine the effectiveness and safety of this target.
