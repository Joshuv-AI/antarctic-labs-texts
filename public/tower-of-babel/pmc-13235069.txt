Plasma antioxidant capacity as a predictor of mortality in critically ill patients

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis and shock are associated with high morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. Oxidative stress contributes to endothelial dysfunction and multiorgan failure, but the prognostic value of total plasma antioxidant capacity, measured by the Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay, has not been systematically evaluated in postoperative patients.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, multicentre observational study including 464 critically ill postoperative patients admitted to intensive care units at three Spanish hospitals (2021–2026). Plasma FRAP levels were measured within the first 24 h after surgery or after clinical diagnosis of sepsis or shock, according to patient status. Clinical severity was assessed using Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores. Associations between FRAP, shock, endothelial damage and 90-day mortality were analysed using survival and regression analyses. Endothelial-associated responses were evaluated in Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells incubated with patient plasma.ResultsFRAP levels were significantly higher in patients with shock, particularly in those with septic shock and in non-survivors (p < 0.001). FRAP positively correlated with Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II scores and was independently associated with 90-day mortality (HR = 4.69; p < 0.05). Elevated FRAP was associated with endothelial damage and with increased FRAP levels in HUVEC supernatants. FRAP showed good discrimination for 90-day mortality (Area Under the Curve = 0.881; 95% CI: 0.775–0.987). Comparative analyses demonstrated slightly higher discriminative performance for FRAP compared with APACHE II within the present cohort, while combined FRAP-APACHE II models showed improved performance in precision-recall analyses.ConclusionsElevated plasma Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power levels in postoperative critically ill patients are associated with shock severity, endothelial injury and increased mortality, particularly in those with septic shock. This biomarker may serve as an early prognostic marker and a rapid, inexpensive and reproducible tool for risk stratification in critical care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06119-1.Keywords: Sepsis, Shock, Oxidative stress, FRAP, Endothelial dysfunction, Prognostic biomarker, Critical illness, Mortality

Background

Sepsis may be defined as a dysregulated host response to infection which may cause life-threatening organ dysfunction and is a major cause of mortality in critical care patients [1]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Bauer et al. [2], it is reported that sepsis affects approximately one in three patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), impacting more than 30 million individuals worldwide each year and accounting for around 6 million deaths [2]. Shock constitutes an even more severe condition, characterized by inadequate tissue perfusion, leading to cellular hypoxia, multiorgan dysfunction and high mortality rates [3, 4]. The impact of shock varies according to its etiology: mortality from septic shock is approximately 38% [5], while other types of shock, such as cardiogenic shock, reach mortality rates of about 41% [6]. In this context, endothelial dysfunction is considered an important component of the pathophysiology of sepsis and septic shock, as microcirculatory alterations and loss of vascular barrier integrity contribute to the development of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and increased mortality in critically ill patients [7].

Oxidative stress, defined as an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and endogenous antioxidant defences, is associated with lipid peroxidation, DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction [8–10]. This process has been identified as an important contributor to cellular injury and persistent organ dysfunction in the setting of shock [9–11]. Total plasma antioxidant capacity has emerged as a potential integrative marker of systemic redox-related alterations [12]. The Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay allows quantification of plasma reducing capacity and has been applied in various critical conditions, including patients with shock [12]. Several studies have suggested associations between redox-related parameters and clinical outcomes in critical illness [13, 14], supporting interest in plasma reducing-capacity biomarkers as potential prognostic indicators. Despite increasing interest in the role of redox-related alterations in shock, the prognostic utility of plasma antioxidant capacity measured by the FRAP assay has not been systematically evaluated in patients with shock, particularly in relation to mortality. Available studies are limited, heterogeneous and often fail to comprehensively analyse the association between plasma reducing capacity and mortality [15–17]. In this context, the present study aims to assess plasma antioxidant capacity using the FRAP assay and to determine its association with mortality, evaluating its potential prognostic value for risk stratification in postoperative critically ill patients.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

The study was carried out between 2021 and 2026 in the intensive care units of three Spanish hospitals: University Clinical Hospital of Valladolid, Toledo University Hospital and Bellvitge University Hospital. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committees of all participating centres (Valladolid, PI-18–972; Toledo, CEIC-466–2019; Bellvitge, PR295/22). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, EU 2016/679). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal representative prior to study inclusion and all data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

A prospective, observational, multicentre study was conducted to evaluate the association between plasma antioxidant capacity, measured by the FRAP assay, and clinical outcomes in postoperative critically ill patients, with or without shock. The study design and reporting followed the recommendations of the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines and, when applicable, the STARD (Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) guidelines for biomarker evaluation. Finally, the development and validation of the prognostic risk score adhered to the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guidelines.

Study population

A total of 464 postoperative critically ill patients were included, recruited from the intensive care units of the three participating hospitals, comprising 283 patients without shock and 181 patients with shock.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, having undergone any surgical procedure prior to ICU admission and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included lack of informed consent, pregnancy, terminal illness or limitations of therapeutic effort.

All patients were included after admission to the ICU following surgery. Blood sampling and clinical classification were performed within a predefined 24-h window, during the early postoperative ICU admission period, depending on clinical status. In postoperative ICU patients without sepsis or shock, blood samples were obtained within the first 24 h after surgery. In patients meeting criteria for sepsis or shock, blood sampling was performed within the first 24 h following the clinical diagnosis of sepsis or shock.

A priori sample size estimation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1) assuming a one-tailed test, an effect size of 0.3, α = 0.05 and power = 0.90. The analysis indicated that 402 patients (245 vs 157) were required. The final cohort included 464 patients (283 vs 181), providing an achieved statistical power of 93%.

Clinical categories and microbiological diagnosis

Sepsis and septic shock were defined according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) [1]. Sepsis was defined as infection associated with acute organ dysfunction, reflected by an increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of ≥ 2 points. Septic shock (SS) was defined as sepsis complicated by persistent circulatory failure requiring vasopressor support to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mmHg and serum lactate levels ≥ 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Non‑septic shock (NSS) was defined by the presence of acute circulatory failure requiring vasopressor support to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg and/or evidence of tissue hypoperfusion with lactate levels ≥ 2 mmol/L, in the absence of clinical or microbiological evidence of infection. Postoperative controls consisted of postoperative ICU patients who did not meet shock criteria and displayed no clinical, laboratory or microbiological signs of infection following surgery. All patients were individually evaluated by a medical team prior to assignment to each study group. To confirm infection in suspected cases, microbiological studies were performed, including Gram staining and culture on appropriate media. In cases of suspected fungal infection, specific fungal cultures were performed on Sabouraud agar supplemented with chloramphenicol. Infection was confirmed in accordance with the guidelines of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and the American Society for Microbiology (ASM) [18].

Biological samples

Clinical and biological data were collected and stored in an anonymized manner to ensure confidentiality and compliance with current regulations on personal data protection (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR, EU 2016/679).

Whole blood samples were collected into EDTA-containing tubes (3.6 mg, BD Vacutainer, Belgium) within the first 24 h after surgery in non-septic postoperative ICU patients, or within the first 24 h following the clinical diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock in septic patients. Samples were processed in the biobanks of each hospital to obtain plasma by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. Plasma was aliquoted into sterile tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Samples were stored in single-use aliquots and underwent no freeze–thaw cycles prior to FRAP determination.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

Total plasma antioxidant capacity was measured using the FRAP assay, a widely used colorimetric method for assessing plasma reducing capacity, as previously described by Benzie and Strain (1996) [19].

The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared on the day of analysis by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM 2,4,6-tri(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine dissolved in 40 mM HCl and 20 mM ferric chloride hexahydrate. TROLOX solutions ranging from 62.5 µM to 2000 µM were used to generate the standard curve.

In 96-well plates, 6 µL of each TROLOX standard were mixed with 24 µL of PBS, while plasma samples were prepared by mixing 6 µL of plasma with 24 µL of PBS. Subsequently, 225 µL of FRAP reagent were added to all wells. Standards and samples were measured in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (SPECTROstar Nano, BMG Labtech, Germany).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in endothelial growth medium (EGM-2, Lonza, Switzerland) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Once confluent, cells were treated with 7.5% individual patient plasma from the different patient groups (n = 100, 25 patients per group, including postoperative controls, sepsis, non-septic shock and septic shock) and incubated for 6 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the cell supernatants were collected and their reducing capacity was determined using the FRAP assay, following the same protocol described above.

Endothelial integrity assessed by VE-cadherin immunostaining

In parallel, following plasma exposure, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed repeatedly with cold 1X PBS. To block nonspecific binding, wells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with PBS-T containing 5% BSA and 22.52 mg/mL glycine. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C under gentle agitation with primary anti-VE-cadherin antibody [1:1000] (Abcam, UK, Ref. #ab33168) diluted in PBS-T, used as the staining solution. After multiple washes with 1X PBS, cells were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 [1:200] (donkey anti-rabbit IgG H&L, Alexa Fluor 488, preadsorbed; Abcam, UK, Ref. #ab150065) diluted in staining solution for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, nuclei were stained with Hoechst (5 µg/mL) for 20 min at room temperature. VE-cadherin immunostaining analyses were performed in HUVECs following incubation with individual patient plasma samples.

Endothelial integrity was evaluated by assessing VE‑cadherin junctional membrane localization, cytoplasmic redistribution/internalization, and the presence of intercellular gap formation. Endothelial damage (ED) was defined by loss of continuous junctional VE‑cadherin staining and cytoplasmic internalization, whereas preserved junctional staining was classified as no endothelial damage (NED). For categorical analysis, patients were classified using a predefined visual scoring system (presence vs. absence of the VE‑cadherin internalization phenotype), and scoring was performed blinded to clinical outcomes.

For each individual patient plasma sample, experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates on separate days. In each experiment, HUVECs incubated with pooled healthy donor plasma were included as a reference control and used for qualitative normalization of the endothelial phenotype (intact junctional VE‑cadherin localization and preserved monolayer integrity). Several microscopic fields were analysed per condition using predefined morphological criteria. The assessment of endothelial damage was conducted using predefined morphological criteria. Categorical assignment (ED vs. NED) was accepted only when the observed phenotype was reproducible across biological replicates.

Angiopoietin determination

Circulating angiopoietin-1 (ANGPT-1) and angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT-2) levels were determined in plasma samples from patients included in the HUVEC supernatant FRAP analyses using a Luminex Flow Luminescence Immunoassay (FLIA) xMAP magnetic bead-based assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Cloud-Clone Corp, TX, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were analysed using Student’s t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ).

Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test to compare survival distributions between groups. FRAP was analysed as a continuous variable after logarithmic transformation in all regression models. Categorization of FRAP values was performed exclusively for Kaplan-Meier visualization using ROC-derived cut-offs based on the Youden index. To evaluate variables independently associated with 90-day mortality, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted using the survival and survminer packages, including clinically relevant covariates associated with disease severity and organ dysfunction, including age, sex, APACHE II score, lactate, creatinine, pre-existing renal disease and FRAP. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Model performance and discrimination were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with the pROC package. The area under the curve (AUC) was compared using DeLong’s test and model calibration and discrimination were further assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the concordance index (C-index).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 464 critically ill postoperative patients were included and classified into two groups according to their clinical category (shock vs. non-shock). The non-shock group, comprising postoperative controls and septic patients without shock criteria (n = 283), whereas the shock group included patients with septic and non-septic shock (n = 181).

Significant differences were observed between the two groups (non-shock vs. shock) across several clinical and biochemical variables (Table 1). Regarding comorbidities, patients with shock had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease (p < 0.05), pulmonary disease (p < 0.01), chronic kidney disease (p < 0.01) and a history of cancer within the preceding three months (p < 0.05). From a biochemical perspective, patients with shock exhibited significantly elevated levels of creatinine, lactate, leukocytes, neutrophils, procalcitonin and C-reactive protein (p < 0.001). In addition, patients with shock had significantly higher SOFA and APACHE II scores (p < 0.001), reflecting greater clinical severity at ICU admission. This increased severity translated into adverse clinical outcomes: patients with shock required longer durations of intubation and ICU and hospital stays (p < 0.001) and exhibited significantly higher mortality rates at the analysed follow-up time points (28, 60 and 90 days; p < 0.001).

FRAP levels are increased in patients with greater clinical severity

Plasma antioxidant capacity, assessed by the FRAP assay and expressed as logFRAP, was significantly higher in the shock group compared with the non-shock group (Fig. 1A). Additional subgroup analyses were subsequently performed according to four clinical categories: postoperative controls, sepsis, non-septic shock and septic shock (Fig. 1B). Significant differences in plasma FRAP levels were observed among the different clinical groups, with the highest FRAP levels detected in patients with septic shock.

(A) Comparison of plasma FRAP levels, expressed as logFRAP, between patients without shock (postoperative controls and sepsis) and patients with shock (septic and non-septic shock). Moreover, FRAP levels were significantly higher in non-survivors compared with survivors (Fig. 1C). Supplementary Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics and differences between survivors and non-survivors. Given that the highest FRAP levels were observed in the septic shock subgroup, mortality analyses were further stratified according to septic shock status (Fig. 1D). No significant differences in FRAP levels were observed between survivors and non-survivors in patients without septic shock, whereas significantly higher FRAP levels were detected in non-survivors with septic shock.

FRAP as a predictor of 90-day mortality

To evaluate the discriminative capacity of FRAP as a predictor of 90-day mortality, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed (Fig. 2A). FRAP levels showed good discriminatory capacity for 90-day mortality, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.881 (95% CI: 0.775–0.987). APACHE II showed an AUC of 0.860 (95% CI: 0.768–0.952), while the combined FRAP-APACHE II model yielded an AUC of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.790–0.960). Although the ROC curves showed substantial overlap, FRAP demonstrated slightly higher discriminative performance than APACHE II within the present cohort. Comparative ROC analysis using DeLong’s test demonstrated a modest but statistically significant improvement of the combined model compared with APACHE II alone. Precision-recall curve analysis additionally showed improved performance for FRAP-based models compared with APACHE II alone in the context of class imbalance (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Association between FRAP levels and 90-day mortality

Given that patients with greater clinical severity exhibited both higher FRAP levels and increased mortality, its prognostic value was further evaluated. Patients were stratified into two subgroups according to FRAP levels (high vs. low), using an optimal cutoff value of 800.92 determined from ROC analysis using the Youden index. Patient distribution across FRAP subgroups was assessed using Fisher’s exact test. Additionally, the relationship between FRAP levels and the requirement for vasopressor support was analysed as an indicator of haemodynamic severity. Patients requiring noradrenaline at doses above 0.2 µg/kg.min had significantly higher FRAP levels compared with those receiving lower doses or no vasopressors (OR = 2.24; p < 0.001, Fisher’s exact test).

90-day survival was evaluated according to FRAP levels using Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 2B). Patients with elevated FRAP levels exhibited significantly higher mortality compared with those with lower FRAP levels, with a significant separation between survival curves (log-rank test, p = 0.017).

A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed and visualized using a forest plot (Fig. 2C). The model included logFRAP, age, sex, lactate, creatinine, renal disease and APACHE II as independent variables. In this model, logFRAP remained independently and significantly associated with 90-day mortality (HR = 4.69; p < 0.05), as were APACHE II (HR = 1.06; p < 0.001) and lactate (HR = 1.25; p < 0.001). Additional multivariable analyses were performed to evaluate the association between plasma FRAP levels and major circulating compounds known to contribute to plasma reducing capacity, including bilirubin, albumin and uric acid (Supplementary Table 2). Significant associations were observed for total bilirubin and albumin, whereas no significant association was detected for uric acid.

Causal mediation analysis was performed to explore whether the association between FRAP and 90-day mortality was mediated through shock. Results suggested that only a small proportion of the observed association was mediated by shock (ACME average: 0.02%, 95% CI 0–0.056%, p = 0.03), whereas most of the association appeared to remain independent of shock status (ADE average: 0.192%, 95% CI 0.0046–0.889%, p = 0.032), resulting in a total effect of 0.194% (95% CI 0.0052–0.916%, p = 0.032). Given the observational nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

FRAP levels are increased in patients with endothelial damage

Given the important role of the endothelium in the pathophysiology of shock and sepsis, and the observation that the high-FRAP group was mainly composed of patients with shock and higher mortality, the relationship between FRAP levels, determined in HUVEC supernatants after incubation with patient plasma and markers of endothelial damage was evaluated. Patients with evidence of endothelial damage (ED) exhibited significantly higher FRAP levels compared with those without endothelial damage (NED) (Fig. 3A).

Association between endothelial damage and FRAP levels determined in HUVEC supernatants. (A) Comparison of FRAP levels measured in HUVEC supernatants between patients without endothelial damage (NED) and patients with endothelial damage (ED). (B) Comparison of FRAP levels measured in HUVEC supernatants between patients without septic shock (No SS) and patients with septic shock (SS). (C) FRAP levels in patients without septic shock and patients with septic shock stratified according to endothelial damage status (NED vs. ED). Statistical significance is indicated in the figure

Subsequently, patients were stratified according to the presence or absence of septic shock. FRAP levels determined in HUVEC supernatants were significantly higher in patients with septic shock compared with those without septic shock (Fig. 3B). Additional stratified analyses according to endothelial damage status showed that patients with endothelial damage exhibited higher FRAP levels both in the non-septic shock and septic shock groups (Fig. 3C).

To evaluate the relationship between plasma FRAP levels and endothelial-associated in vitro responses, the association between FRAP measured directly in plasma and FRAP quantified in the supernatant of HUVECs after incubation with patient plasma was analysed. A strong positive correlation was observed between both measurements (ρ = 0.85, p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings suggest a close association between plasma reducing capacity and the endothelial associated in‑vitro readout after endothelial exposure. Patients with elevated HUVEC supernatant FRAP levels also exhibited higher plasma FRAP levels and a greater likelihood of endothelial damage (OR = 4.66; p = 0.014). To evaluate potential redundancy between plasma FRAP and HUVEC supernatant FRAP, both variables were included in a multivariable logistic regression model predicting endothelial damage. No significant multicollinearity was observed (variance inflation factor ≈ 1), indicating that the endothelial associated FRAP readout may provide complementary information beyond plasma FRAP alone.

Circulating angiopoietin levels are associated with endothelial damage

To further evaluate the relationship between plasmatic FRAP levels and endothelial dysfunction, circulating ANGPT-1 and ANGPT-2 levels, two well-established biomarkers associated with endothelial activation and vascular dysfunction, were analysed according to endothelial damage status in the HUVEC assay. No significant differences in ANGPT-1 concentrations were observed between samples with and without endothelial damage (Fig. 4A). In contrast, ANGPT-2 concentrations were significantly higher in samples exhibiting endothelial damage compared with those without endothelial damage (Fig. 4B).

Circulating angiopoietin levels according to endothelial damage status. (A) Circulating ANGPT-1 levels in patients without endothelial damage (NED) and patients with endothelial damage (ED). (B) Circulating ANGPT-2 levels in patients without endothelial damage (NED) and patients with endothelial damage (ED). Statistical significance is indicated in the figure

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were subsequently performed to evaluate the independent association between FRAP, angiopoietins and endothelial damage. In the model including FRAP, ANGPT-1 and ANGPT-2, both FRAP and ANGPT-2 remained independently associated with endothelial damage, whereas ANGPT-1 was not significant. Variance inflation factor analyses demonstrated minimal collinearity among predictors (VIF ≈ 1 for all variables), indicating that each variable provided independent statistical information within the model.

Discussion

In this study, total plasma antioxidant capacity was evaluated in patients undergoing major surgery, comparing those who presented with shock with those who did not, using the FRAP assay. Our analysis yielded three key findings: (1) FRAP values were significantly higher in patients who presented with shock compared with those who did not, with the highest FRAP levels observed specifically in patients with septic shock, suggesting an association between an altered plasma reducing capacity and greater clinical severity; (2) elevated FRAP concentrations were associated with higher 90-day mortality and (3) high plasma FRAP levels correlated with endothelial damage and with higher FRAP levels in HUVECs, supporting an association between systemic redox-related alterations and endothelial integrity. Collectively, these findings provide both clinical and experimental evidence that plasma antioxidant capacity, as assessed by FRAP, is closely associated with shock severity, endothelial injury and early mortality in postoperative critically ill patients, an aspect not previously evaluated in an integrated manner within multicentre cohorts, although the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory and not as evidence of causal mechanistic relationships.

FRAP represents the total reducing capacity of plasma, determined by the concentration of molecules with antioxidant properties such as uric acid, vitamins C and E, plasma proteins, bilirubin and other endogenous compounds [19, 20]. In our cohort, patients with worse clinical outcomes exhibited higher FRAP levels, consistent with previous observations in conditions associated with marked redox-related alterations, including severe sepsis and severe COVID-19 [21, 22]. These findings suggest that elevated FRAP in critical states may reflect a compensatory antioxidant response that may be insufficient to counterbalance the profound systemic alterations associated with postoperative shock and severe inflammation, although the contribution of metabolite accumulation and organ dysfunction cannot be excluded [23]. This phenomenon could be interpreted as an overload of the plasma antioxidant system, indicating a characteristic redox imbalance in patients with shock [24]. In addition to its association with clinical severity, elevated FRAP concentrations were consistently associated with higher 90-day mortality. The association between high FRAP and mortality may reflect the underlying pathophysiological alterations associated with shock. In these patients, excessive production of reactive oxygen species has been associated with endothelial damage, mitochondrial dysfunction and haemodynamic alterations that surpass the body’s antioxidant defence capacity [25–29]. Accordingly, high FRAP may reflect not only activation of compensatory antioxidant mechanisms but also the magnitude of systemic metabolic and redox-related alterations contributing to multiorgan dysfunction [23]. Similar patterns have been described in pediatric patients with sepsis, where early infection is associated with increased FRAP, interpreted as an indirect marker of cellular damage and severe oxidative stress [15, 30]. Our multivariate analyses, adjusted for age, sex, APACHE II, lactate, creatinine, pre-existing renal disease and clinical category, confirmed that FRAP remains an independent predictor of 90-day mortality, reinforcing its prognostic value. Our mediation analysis suggests that the association between elevated FRAP and 90-day mortality is largely independent of shock, with only a minimal portion of the effect explained by haemodynamic failure. This indicates that elevated FRAP may reflect broader systemic derangements, such as oxidative stress and cellular injury, rather than simply serving as a marker of circulatory collapse. The fact that FRAP demonstrates predictive capacity comparable to, although not necessarily exceeding, well-validated clinical scores such as APACHE II suggests that it captures pathophysiological aspects not fully reflected by traditional scoring systems. Although APACHE II is a widely used clinical scoring system and performed well in our cohort, its application has certain limitations [31, 32]. Furthermore, these scores do not directly capture specific pathophysiological processes, such as oxidative stress or endothelial damage, which are central mechanisms in the progression of shock. In this context, biomarkers such as FRAP can provide complementary information, improving risk stratification and offering additional pathophysiological information complementary to established clinical severity scores.

Furthermore, the vascular endothelium has emerged as a central component in sepsis research over recent years, as the condition has been shown to be associated with endothelial dysfunction [33]. Endothelial dysfunction increases vascular permeability, promotes tissue edema and induces a procoagulant state, disrupting leukocyte trafficking and microcirculation, leading to impaired organ perfusion and functional failure, manifesting, for example, as acute respiratory distress syndrome or worsening hypotension. In this context, endothelial dysfunction plays a critical role in progression to septic shock, where the combination of uncontrolled vasodilation, capillary leak and poor tissue perfusion amplifies multiorgan damage and worsens patient prognosis [33, 34]. Several studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress is a central mechanism in the induction and maintenance of endothelial dysfunction, by altering nitric oxide bioavailability, promoting endothelial inflammation and favouring a pro-oxidant and pro-thrombotic phenotype [35]. In this context, our results suggest an association between plasma FRAP and endothelial-associated responses, as elevated FRAP levels, both in plasma and in HUVECs, in the context of shock or severe inflammation, are associated with markers of endothelial activation and injury. In addition, circulating angiopoietin-2, a well-established biomarker of endothelial activation and vascular dysfunction [36, 37], was significantly associated with endothelial damage in the HUVEC assay. In contrast, ANGPT-1 concentrations were not significantly associated with endothelial damage in our cohort. ANGPT-1 is considered a vascular stabilising factor involved in the maintenance of endothelial quiescence and barrier integrity, whereas ANGPT-2 is more closely linked to endothelial activation and vascular leakage during sepsis and shock. Therefore, the absence of significant changes in ANGPT-1 together with the marked increase in ANGPT-2 may reflect a predominant endothelial activation phenotype rather than alterations in basal endothelial stabilisation mechanisms [38]. Moreover, both plasma FRAP and ANGPT-2 remained independently associated with endothelial damage in multivariable analyses, reinforcing the relationship between systemic redox-related alterations and endothelial injury observed in the present cohort. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory endothelial-associated observations rather than definitive mechanistic evidence or functional endothelial validation. Plasma FRAP may therefore reflect systemic redox-related alterations associated with endothelial dysfunction [17]. Thus, plasma FRAP could be interpreted as an integrative marker of the circulating oxidative environment associated with endothelial function, supporting a potential association between systemic redox imbalance, vascular dysfunction and the clinical deterioration observed in shock.

From a practical perspective, FRAP assessment could be incorporated as a complementary tool in the initial evaluation of critically ill patients, particularly in intensive care or postoperative settings. Its measurement may allow clinicians to identify patients at higher risk of mortality. If confirmed in subsequent studies, integrating FRAP with routine biomarkers could optimize clinical decision-making, guide more intensive monitoring strategies and support interventions aimed at modulating oxidative stress.

This study has several limitations. First, although its multicentre design and sample size provide robustness and statistical power, the cohort included exclusively postoperative critically ill patients. While this ensured greater clinical and analytical homogeneity, it limits the generalizability of results to other causes of sepsis or shock. Second, plasma samples were obtained at a single time point, preventing assessment of dynamic changes in redox status over the clinical course. Third, the study did not include direct measurements of reactive oxygen species or oxidative damage biomarkers, limiting mechanistic interpretation of the FRAP findings. In addition, residual confounding related to comorbidities, organ dysfunction and administered therapies cannot be completely excluded given the observational nature of the study. Therefore, causal relationships between FRAP, endothelial dysfunction and mortality cannot be definitively established. Finally, the absence of an independent external validation cohort further limits immediate clinical applicability. Future studies with more heterogeneous populations and longitudinal follow-up will be necessary to validate and expand these findings.

Conclusions

Plasma FRAP is significantly elevated in postoperative critically ill patients with shock, particularly in those with septic shock and is independently associated with increased 90-day mortality. Elevated FRAP levels may reflect systemic redox-related alterations and are associated with endothelial dysfunction, supporting its potential utility as a biomarker associated with disease severity.

These findings suggest that FRAP may serve as an early prognostic marker for risk stratification and identification of high-risk patients, providing clinically relevant information complementary to conventional severity scores. Importantly, the FRAP assay represents a rapid, inexpensive and reproducible method that requires only small plasma volumes, supporting its potential applicability in routine clinical settings. Its potential application as a tool to monitor systemic redox-related alterations and its validation in prospective cohorts may facilitate future studies evaluating the role of systemic redox-related alterations in critical illness. However, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design of the study and the absence of an external validation cohort.
