SARS-CoV-2 reinfection in interstitial lung disease patients and its association with clinical deterioration: a two-year prospective cohort study
Authors: Yuanying Wang, Dongmei Wang, Yawen Song, Na Wu, Xuqin Du, Qiao Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has raised concerns about respiratory sequelae, particularly in the patients with preexisting interstitial lung disease (ILD). This study aimed to evaluate the longitudinal impacts of SARS-CoV-2 reinfection on patients with preexisting ILD, focusing on differential outcomes between single and multiple infection episodes.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted (December 2022–March 2023), enrolling 347 adults with preexisting ILD and confirmed COVID-19 infection. Follow-up assessments were performed at baseline, 6-, 12-, and 24-month intervals. Data included demographics, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) scores, and clinical outcomes. Composite deterioration endpoint incorporating acute exacerbation (AE), decline in forced vital capacity (FVC), decline in diffusing capacity (DLCO), and radiographic progression of lung fibrosis on HRCT.ResultsAmong 347 patients (82.13% single infection, 17.87% multiple infections), the multiple infection group exhibited significantly higher disease deterioration rates (61.29% vs. 41.75%, P = 0.005). Multivariable Cox analysis identified multiple infections (HR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.03–2.19), higher baseline fibrosis scores (HR = 1.64), respiratory failure (HR = 1.84), and COVID-19 pneumonia (HR = 1.58) as independent risk factors. Patients with multiple infections showed accelerated FVC% decline (P = 0.016). All-cause mortality did not differ significantly between groups (11.29% vs. 9.12%, P = 0.598). Subgroup analyses suggested broadly consistent risks across ILD subtypes.ConclusionRepeated SARS-CoV-2 infections may be associated with disease deterioration in patients with preexisting lung fibrosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12020-z.

Introduction

The emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), responsible for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), has profoundly reshaped global public health since December 2019 [1]. It has presented a range of clinical manifestations, ranging from asymptomatic infections to severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multi-organ failure [1]. China’s strict containment measures effectively suppressed the virus until policy changes on December 7, 2022, leading to a surge in infections [2]. A sentinel surveillance report from the China CDC estimates that nearly 90% of the population was infected within 35 days after restrictions eased [3].

COVID-19 survivors, particularly those with preexisting lung conditions, often experience long-term respiratory issues [4]. This vulnerability is exacerbated in patients with interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), where viral-induced alveolar injury may interact synergistically with underlying fibrotic pathways, accelerating functional decline [5]. ILD encompasses a group of heterogeneous pulmonary disorders characterized by diffuse lung fibrosis of the lung parenchyma, which leads to compromised lung structure and impaired respiratory function [5]. Emerging evidence suggests a bidirectional pathogenic interaction between ILD and COVID-19: preexisting ILD not only increases susceptibility to severe SARS-CoV-2 infection [6] but may also be exacerbated by virus-triggered inflammatory cascades and aberrant tissue repair mechanisms. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a severe form of ILD, carries a poor prognosis even with treatment [7]. The deterioration of these conditions significantly impacts the quality of life of IPF patients and necessitates ongoing medical care [8]. Studies indicate that individuals with fibrotic ILD face a higher risk of acute and post-acute mortality following COVID-19 infection [6, 9]. Factors such as impaired lung function, advanced age, and comorbidities like obesity further elevate this risk [6, 9].

Emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, like Omicron, show increased transmissibility and reinfection rates [10]. Understanding the long-term effects of repeated infections on preexisting pulmonary fibrosis is crucial. This study aimed to investigate the long-term risks of disease deterioration in patients with preexisting ILD following SARS-CoV-2 infection. During a 2-year cohort study of patients with fibrotic ILDs, we explored the COVID-19-associated deterioration of fibrotic disease trajectories and identified clinical risk factors.

Methods

Study design, setting, and patients

This is a prospective cohort study of patients with ILD attending the ILD center at Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital, China, a tertiary referral center specializing in ILDs, from December 7, 2022 to March 31, 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) preexisting ILD before SARS-CoV-2 infection; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) provided informed consent; (4) infected with COVID-19 within 3 months of the first visit. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: (1) malignancies at baseline or during follow-up; (2) decompensated heart or renal failure; (3) pulmonary embolism; (4) small sample size in certain diagnostic subgroups; (5) coexisting respiratory infections with confirmed etiological evidence; (6) loss to follow-up. (Fig. 1). Enrolled patients were followed from the baseline visit to death or censoring date (January 31, 2025). The study was conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics committee (2020-KY-437, 2023-KY-34). The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies [11].

Definitions

SARS-CoV-2 infection: defined by a positive result from nasopharyngeal or nasal swabs, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid for SARS-CoV-2 RNA detection by polymerase chain reaction or SARS-CoV-2 antigen rapid detection test [12] based on patient-reported symptoms and clinician discretion. For patients with multiple infections, reinfection was defined as a new infection occurring at least 90 days after the initial infection, to exclude the possibility of persistent viral presence from the first infection [13]. All patients in the multiple infection group had at least one negative PCR test during the recovery period following the first infection. Patients with two or more infections were classified into the multiple infection group.

Co-infections with other respiratory pathogens were recorded based on two categories: (1) microbiological evidence (RT-PCR, culture, or antigen testing); (2) self-reported infections. However, this co-infection surveillance has inherent limitations, including potential underdetection of asymptomatic concurrent infections and variability in diagnostic testing coverage across pathogens.

COVID-19 pneumonia is characterized by: (1) A confirmed infection with SARS-CoV-2, and (2) new lung shadows developing 7 days to 3 weeks after symptom onset or positive SARS-CoV-2 test that cannot be explained by other pulmonary infection [12]. Chest radiographs or CT scans obtained at the time of infection were used to confirm the presence of COVID-19 pneumonia.

ILD was defined by hallmark manifestations on chest HRCT, compatible clinical symptoms, and, when necessary, supported by pathological findings [14]. ILD subtype classification was carried out through a central multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) involving two pulmonologists, one radiologist, one rheumatologist, and one pathologist. The MDD panel systematically reviewed clinical data, HRCT images, pathology, and other relevant information. Final ILD subtype classifications were determined by consensus among the MDD members. Acute exacerbation (AE) of ILD was defined using IPF-derived criteria: (1) diagnosis of ILD; (2) acute deterioration of dyspnea occurring within 1 month; (3) new bilateral diffuse GGO or consolidations; (4) exclusion of heart failure or fluid overload [15]. The key distinction between COVID-19 pneumonia and AE-ILD lies in the presence of SARS-CoV-2 as the etiological agent for the former, whereas AE-ILD lacks evidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection. In case of diagnostic discrepancies, consensus was reached through MDD.

For connective tissue diseases (CTDs), the diagnoses were made based on the relevant international classification criteria for each condition. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) was diagnosed according to the 2010 classification criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [16]. Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) was diagnosed using the 2016 classification criteria developed by the ACR and EULAR [17]. Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) were diagnosed following the 2017 classification criteria outlined by the ACR and EULAR [18]. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) was diagnosed based on the 2019 ACR/EULAR criteria [19]. Systemic sclerosis (SSc) was diagnosed according to the 2013 classification criteria formulated by the ACR and EULAR [20]. Idiopathic pneumonia with autoimmune features (IPAF) was diagnosed following the 2015 research statement criteria established by the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) [21]. Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis (AAV) was diagnosed according to the 2022 classification criteria published by the ACR and EULAR [22].

Data collecting and follow‑up strategy

The study employed a 24-month longitudinal design with scheduled assessments at baseline (within 3 months post–COVID-19 diagnosis), 6 ± 1, 12 ± 1, and 24 ± 1 months. Baseline evaluations included demographic data (age, sex, smoking history), comorbidities, and medication history. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs), high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest, and arterial blood gas analysis were performed, with respiratory failure defined as an arterial partial pressure of oxygen < 60 mmHg on room air. Vaccinated status was defined as having received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine. Imaging and functional assessments were tailored to the patient’s clinical condition, while annual HRCT and/or PFTs were mandated to objectively evaluate fibrosis progression. This approach balanced individualized clinical management with systematic data collection. The use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and antifibrotic agents was defined as continuous administration for ≥ 3 months during the study period. Concurrent respiratory infections with confirmed pathogen evidence were also documented throughout follow-up.

HRCT images acquisition and visual scoring

Baseline and follow-up chest HRCT scans were independently reviewed by two thoracic radiologists (> 10 years’ expertise) blinded to clinical data, with discordances resolved through consensus. The overall extent of parenchymal abnormalities and the extent of ground-glass attenuation (GGO) and fibrosis were also assessed. The 0–5 scoring for GGO at each lobe was adopted (0, no involvement; 1, ≤ 5% involvement; 2, 5 to < 25% involvement; 3, 25–49% involvement; 4, 50–75% involvement; 5, >75% involvement) [23]. Similarly, the fibrotic change in each lobe was classified into 5 grades (0, no fibrosis; 1, interlobular septal thickening without honeycombing; 2, honeycombing < 25%; 3, 25–49%; 4, 50–75%; 5, >75% of the lobe) as fibrosis score [24]. The respective total score of GGO and fibrosis was the sum of each lobe’s score and ranged from 0 (no involvement) to 25 (maximum involvement). The scores for each lobe were averaged for both readers, and the average score was summed as the HRCT score. The inter-rater reliability assessed by the Kappa coefficient, was 0.534 for GGO and 0.772 for fibrosis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the composite deterioration endpoint of ILD during the follow-up period, defined as the first occurrence of: (1) AE, (2) an absolute decline in FVC% pred. >5%, (3) an absolute decline in DLCO% pred. >10%, and (4) radiographic fibrosis progression on HRCT (fibrosis score increase ≥ 1) [9]. Deterioration-free survival was calculated from the time of the baseline visit to the first ILD deterioration event or the end of follow-up. Vital status and AE occurrence were acquired from a review of the patients’ medical records and telephone interviews.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using PASS 11.0 (NCSS, USA) based on prior data [25–27]. Assuming a 14% reinfection rate and 33% progression after a single infection, with hypothesized doubling of risk after reinfection, 327 cases (286 single-infection, 41 reinfection) were needed for 90% power (α = 0.05). After accounting for 10% attrition, 385 patients were screened, and 347 included, achieving 90% power.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (1st quartile, 3rd quartile), and categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used t-test, ANOVA, or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses assessed deterioration-free survival, with complete case analysis for FVC and DLCO. Lung function changes were analyzed via linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slope, adjusting for key covariates including age, BMI, presence of emphysema, use of antifibrotic treatment, and diagnosis of IPF. The covariance structure for the repeated measures was set to autoregressive (AR1), which provided the best model fit.

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the effect of multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections across ILD subtypes (uILD, IPF, CTD-ILD, and occupational-related ILD including silicosis and asbestosis), and vaccination status. Sensitivity analyses excluding AE-related deterioration events were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. E-values were calculated to assess the robustness of the association between multiple infections and disease deterioration in the multivariable Cox regression model (https://www.evalue-calculator.com/evalue) [28]. To address potential detection bias, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) to balance PCR testing likelihood between the multiple and single infection groups. Variables included in the model were age, gender, IPF diagnosis, GGO and fibrosis scores, use of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants, and vaccination status. PSM was conducted using nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.2 standard deviation caliper to ensure balance. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.2, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline and follow-up characteristics

A total of 347 patients with ILD were analyzed, comprising 158 (45.53%) with CTD-ILD, 57 (16.43%) with IPF, 47 (13.54%) with silicosis, 57 (16.43%) with uILD, 14 (4.03%) with asbestosis, 9 (2.59%) with sarcoidosis, and 5 (1.44%) with FHP (Fig. 2). Among the patients, 82.13% experienced a single SARS-CoV-2 infection, 17% had two infections, and only 0.86% were infected three times. No statistically significant differences in the baseline clinical characteristics between the single infection and multiple infection groups (Table 1). A higher proportion of patients in the multiple infection group received antifibrotic treatment compared to those in the single infection group (27.42% vs. 12.98%, P = 0.004; Table 2). Disease deterioration within two years was significantly more frequent in the multiple infection group (61.29%) than in the single infection group (41.75%, P = 0.005). However, the all-cause mortality rate did not differ significantly between the two groups (11.29% in the multiple infection group vs. 9.12% in the single infection group, P = 0.598). Additionally, descriptive analysis of respiratory co-infections observed during follow-up showed that a small subset (n = 13) had concurrent infections with other pathogens, confirmed by definitive microbiological evidence. No significant difference in co-infection rates was observed between groups (P = 0.216; Table S1).

Risk factors of outcome after SARS-CoV-2 infection

Over a period of up to 2 years of follow-up (median 24.33 months), patients with multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections demonstrated significantly shorter time to composite disease deterioration compared to single-infection counterparts (61.29% vs. 41.75%, P = 0.003, Fig. 3).

In the univariable Cox regression analysis, suffering from disease deterioration was significantly associated with older age, pre-existing IPF, higher baseline fibrosis score, respiratory failure, infection frequency, and COVID‐19 pneumonia (Table 3). In the Cox multivariable analysis, higher baseline fibrosis score (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.09–2.45, P = 0.017), respiratory failure (HR 1.84, 95% CI 1.19–2.83, P = 0.006), multiple infection (HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.03–2.19, P = 0.034), and COVID‐19 pneumonia (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.07–2.34, P = 0.021) remained to be risk factors for disease deterioration after infection. The E-value for the point estimate was 1.98, and for the confidence interval, it was 1.17. By multivariate Cox analysis, age ≥ 65 (HR 12.23, 95% CI 1.50-99.65, P = 0.019), FVC% pred. (HR 5.52, 95% CI 1.30-23.38, P = 0.020), and respiratory failure (HR 12.22, 95% CI 3.30-45.32, P < 0.001) were associated with an increased risk of mortality (Table 4).

Pulmonary function parameters

Using mixed linear-effects analysis, the estimated slope of FVC% predicted in the single infection group was − 0.08% per month (95% CI, − 0.21 to 0.05, P = 0.220). In the multiple infection group, the estimated slope was − 0.24% per month (95% CI, − 0.42 to − 0.05, P = 0.016; Fig. S1). For DLCO% predicted, the estimated slope was − 0.10% per month (95% CI, − 0.35 to 0.16, P = 0.453) in the single infection group and 0.20% per month (95% CI, − 0.20 to 0.61, P = 0.311) in the multiple infection group. After adjusted for covariates including age, BMI, emphysema, anti-fibrotic treatment and IPF diagnosis, in the multiple infection group, the FVC% predicted still trend to decline over time (P = 0.015; Table S2).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between multiple infections and disease deterioration was consistent across all predefined subgroups, including uILD, IPF, CTD-ILD, and occupational-related ILD, with no significant interactions observed (P for interaction > 0.05 in both univariable and multivariable Cox models; Tables S3–S5). Sarcoidosis was excluded due to the small sample size. No significant interaction effects were found between subgroups defined by vaccination status (Table S6). In the unvaccinated group, 9 cases of multiple infections occurred among 42 participants (21.4%), with an unadjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 1.99 (95% CI: 0.77–5.14, P = 0.157) for disease deterioration in multiple versus single infections. In the vaccinated group, 53 cases of multiple infections occurred among 305 participants (17.4%), with an unadjusted HR of 1.72 (95% CI: 1.15–2.55, P = 0.008) for disease deterioration in multiple versus single infections. After adjustment, all P-values were not significant. In sensitivity analyses excluding AE-related events, multiple infections remained significantly associated with disease progression (HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.12–2.54, P = 0.013; Table S7). The E-value for the point estimate was 2.23, and for the confidence interval, it was 1.38.

Propensity score matching

Following propensity score matching, a total of 61 patients with multiple infections and 61 patients with single infections were matched. The proportion of deterioration was 53.28% in the total population, with 44.26% in single-infection and 62.30% in multiple-infection group (P = 0.046; Table S8).

Discussion

This study highlights the impact of multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections on ILD patients, emphasizing the roles of infection frequency, age, and baseline fibrosis in post-infection outcomes. While previous research has explored the effect of pre-existing ILD on COVID-19 prognosis [29], long-term data on ILD patients post-infection are limited, and the effect of SARS-CoV-2 reinfection on pre-existing ILD remains unclear.

The Omicron variant, first detected in November 2021, carries over 50 mutations, including 15 in the receptor-binding domain of the spike protein, enhancing its binding affinity for ACE2 and accelerating transmission [10]. Omicron has since evolved into several sublineages, each accumulating mutations that improve immune evasion. During the study period, dominant strains in China shifted from BA.5.2/BF.7 (December 2022 to March 2023) to the XBB series (April 2023 onwards) and then to XDV/XEC by 2024 [30]. The increased transmissibility and immune evasion of Omicron have significantly raised reinfection risks [31]. Systematic reviews estimate Omicron-era reinfection rates at 3.35%, nearly triple the pre-Omicron rate of 1.16% [32–33]. Another study reported a recurrence rate of 11.5% during the Omicron wave [34]. Our cohort revealed a markedly higher reinfection rate among fibrotic ILD patients, possibly due to overexpression of pulmonary epithelial ACE2 receptors, which facilitate viral entry and replication [35]. Additionally, immune dysregulation in CTD-ILD patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy impairs viral clearance and increases reinfection-related fibrosis exacerbation risks [36]. This, compounded by Omicron’ s evasion of vaccine-induced immunity, creates an environment that promotes viral persistence and disease progression [37].

Reinfection has been associated with increased risks of death, hospitalization, and organ damage, particularly within the first six months [38]. Consistently, in our cohort, patients who experienced multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections had a significantly higher rate of fibrotic ILD deterioration over two years compared with those with a single infection (61.29% vs. 41.75%, P < 0.05), although no statistically significant difference in mortality was observed. Reinfection emerged as an independent risk factor for disease deterioration, highlighting its potential role in accelerating disease progression in this vulnerable population. Furthermore, advanced age was identified as a significant risk factor for increased mortality in fibrotic ILD patients following COVID-19 infection due to increased severity of pneumonia and comorbidities [7, 39]. Similarly, patients with IPF exhibited persistently elevated mortality rates post-COVID-19 [7]. Even after excluding patients with AE, IPF patients showed a higher mortality risk, indicating shared pathobiological mechanisms between IPF and SARS-CoV-2 infection. Genomic studies implicate interleukin (IL)-17and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)pathways in promoting fibrosis and severe disease through cytokine dysregulation [40], while both IPF and COVID-19 involve AT2 cell dysfunction, telomere damage, and cellular senescence [41], contributing to heightened mortality risk.

Numerous factors may contribute to the cause and progression of pulmonary fibrosis and AE after SARS-CoV-2 infection [42]. The virus causes lung tissue damage through immune-mediated inflammation and direct cytopathic effects [41], with mechanisms like cytokine storms [42], type I and III interferon responses [43], and neutrophil extracellular trap formation [44] fueling a cycle of injury and fibrosis. A Japanese study reported AE-ILD mortality rates of 50% at 30 days and 75% at 90 days [45]. Our study showed a higher incidence of AE after reinfection, though not statistically significant, and even after excluding acute-phase exacerbations, the deterioration rate remained significantly elevated in the reinfection cohort. Emerging evidence suggests that repeated viral insults may persistently alter the pulmonary microenvironment through epigenetic reprogramming, potentially explaining why reinfection accelerates disease progression even in the absence of AE [41–45].

Baseline pulmonary fibrosis severity is a key predictor of disease deterioration and mortality in ILD patients following COVID-19 infection. Patients with advanced fibrotic features on CT imaging, such as GGO, reticular patterns, honeycombing, or impaired pulmonary function, face significantly higher risks of acute exacerbations and death [46, 47]. Our data confirmed that baseline fibrosis severity, quantified by CT scores, is an independent predictor of post-COVID deterioration. SARS-CoV-2 infection may accelerate lung function decline, with repeated infections exacerbating this trajectory. Multiple infections were associated with a steeper decline in FVC% compared to single infections [46]. This functional impairment correlates histopathologically with diffuse alveolar epithelial injury, hyaline membrane formation, and fibroblastic proliferation [46]. Thus, prolonged viral insults may perpetuate inflammatory-fibrotic cascades, driving irreversible lung function loss and worsening the burden of progressive fibrosis.

While acute SARS-CoV-2 impacts on ILD patients are well-documented, long-term consequences, particularly long COVID, remain poorly understood. Long COVID, characterized by persistent fatigue, dyspnea, and cognitive dysfunction, may exacerbate underlying lung damage, accelerating fibrotic progression in ILD [4, 5, 48]. Notably, long COVID and reinfection form a mutually reinforcing cycle: repeated infections amplify pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis, while long COVID-associated immune dysregulation further drives disease progression [49]. For ILD patients, recurrent viral exposure, sustained immune abnormalities, and progressive lung function decline collectively elevate long-term risks, highlighting the critical need for novel technologies in enhancing diagnosis accuracy and monitoring symptoms in this vulnerable population. Some emerging technologies in recent years hold promise to redefine ILD management and precise tracking of disease trajectories [50–52].

This study also explored the potential role of vaccines in ILD patients. Although the unvaccinated group exhibited a higher frequency of multiple infections (21.4% vs. 17.4%), the hazard ratio (HR) for disease deterioration was lower in vaccinated patients (HR = 1.72, P = 0.008) compared to unvaccinated counterparts (HR = 1.99, P = 0.157). Notably, this association did not retain statistical significance after adjustment for confounding variables; however, the unadjusted results may suggest a potential protective effect of vaccination against disease deterioration in the context of multiple infections. These findings align with previous evidence demonstrating that vaccination mitigates COVID-19 severity and reduces the risk of hospitalization and mortality, even among reinfected individuals [53]. Nevertheless, due to the limited sample size, these observations should be interpreted with caution. Further large-scale longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate the long-term impact of vaccination on immune responses and disease progression in ILD patients with repeated SARS-CoV-2 infections.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort comprised ILD patients attending regular follow-up visits, which may introduce selection bias. Second, the small number of patients with rare ILD subtypes limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, while SARS-CoV-2 can affect immune homeostasis, this study did not assess longitudinal changes in autoantibodies, which could influence disease progression, particularly in CTD-ILD. Fourth, the potential impact of concurrent respiratory infections during COVID-19 was not fully accounted for, as incomplete or inaccurate documentation of these infections may have confounded the results. Fifth, SARS-CoV-2 testing was primarily symptom-triggered or based on clinician discretion, which could have introduced bias, especially in detecting asymptomatic infections and underestimating the true infection burden. Additionally, distinguishing COVID-19 pneumonia from AE-ILD is challenging due to overlapping clinical and radiological features [54, 55]. The lack of comprehensive virological data further complicates accurate diagnosis. Finally, the relatively short follow-up period may not fully capture the long-term effects of repeated infections, such as mortality or chronic functional decline.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections may be associated with the risk of disease deterioration in patients with ILD. Clinicians should be particularly vigilant in managing ILD patients with a history of multiple COVID-19 infections, as they represent a population at heightened risk for disease deterioration. Future studies with longer follow-up are needed to fully elucidate the long-term effects of recurrent SARS-CoV-2 infections on both morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable patient group.
