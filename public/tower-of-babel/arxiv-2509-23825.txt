Electric Currents for Discrete Data Generation
Alexander Kolesov, Stepan Manukhov*, Vladimir V. Palyulin, Alexander Korotin

Abstract
We propose E lectric C urrent D iscrete D ata G eneration (ECD 2 G), a pioneering method for data generation in discrete settings that is grounded in electrical engineering theory. Our approach draws an analogy between electric current flow in a circuit and the transfer of probability mass between data distributions. We interpret samples from the source distribution as current input nodes of a circuit and samples from the target distribution as current output nodes. A neural network is then used to learn the electric currents to represent the probability flow in the circuit. To map the source distribution to the target, we sample from the source and transport these samples along the circuit pathways according to the learned currents. This process provably guarantees transfer between data distributions. We present proof-of-concept experiments to illustrate our ECD 2 G method.

Introduction

Generative modeling is a challenging problem with most powerful solutions often getting a direct inspiration from concepts in
physics
. The complexity of generation is especially hard for
discrete data
(text, graphs, molecules, etc.), where the structure of the state space presents unique challenges.

Recently,
Diffusion Models
(DM) have been successfully adapted to discrete domains. While their initial remarkable success was in continuous data such as images
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song & Kingma 2021
;
Dhariwal & Nichol 2021
)
, a new wave of research has developed discrete diffusion processes
(
Austin et al. 2021
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
. Instead of corrupting data with Gaussian noise, these models define a forward process that progressively corrupts discrete structures (e.g., tokens) through random transitions to other discrete states or a masking token. Learning to reverse this process has led to strong performance in tasks ranging from text generation
(
Lou et al. 2024
)
to molecule design
(
Zhang & Zhang 2025
)
.

In parallel,
Electrostatic-based generative models
have emerged as a powerful physics-inspired framework for continuous data. These methods interpret data samples as electrostatic charges within an electrostatic field. Pioneering work
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
learned the field generated by data points on a plate inside a hemisphere, enabling data generation from the uniform distribution on the hemisphere. The further development
(
Kolesov et al. 2025
;
Manukhov et al. 2025
)
introduced a capacitor-based paradigm, learning the field between charged plates to enable data-to-data mapping.

However, a significant
gap remains
: despite their strong performance in continuous settings, electrostatic theory-based methods have not yet been formulated for generative modeling in discrete data spaces. The fundamental concepts of continuous charge and potential do not have direct analogues in discrete domains, presenting a novel and open research challenge.

2.1
Electric circuits, Kirchhoff and Ohm laws.

Figure 2:
An illustration of an electric circuit

An electric circuit can be considered as a graph
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of vertices (nodes) and
E
is the set of edges, each associated with a resistor between these nodes. An edge
e(v,v^{\prime})\in E
between vertices
v\in V
and
v^{\prime}\in V
has a resistance
R(v,v^{\prime})
. We manually select a subset of vertices called
sources
and denote them by
V^{(src)}\subset V
. A current is injected into the circuit through these source vertices. We denote the current flowing into each vertex
v
by the function
I^{(src)}(v)
, which equals zero if
v\notin V^{(src)}
.

Similarly, we define another subset of vertices
V^{(snk)}\subset V
called
sinks
, such that
V^{(src)}\cap V^{(snk)}=\emptyset
. We denote the currents flowing out of these sink vertices by the function
I^{(snk)}(v)
, which equals zero if
v\notin V^{(snk)}
. As current flows from the sources
V^{(src)}
to
V^{(snk)}
, they obey the following physical laws.

Kirchhoff’s Current Law.
Consider any vertex
v\in V
. There exists a set of edges

\{e(v,v^{\prime})\}_{v^{\prime}\in J(v)}
connecting
v
to all its neighboring vertices, where the set of these neighbors is denoted by
J(v)
. Let
J_{in}(v)
be the set of vertices from which current flows into
v
, and let
J_{out}(v)
be the set of vertices to which current flows out from
v
.

Figure 3:
An illustration for Kirchhoff’s Law

If the current between
v
and a vertex
v^{\prime}\in J(v)
is equal to zero, we assign
v^{\prime}
to
J_{in}(v)
by default. The sets
J_{in}(v)
and
J_{out}(v)
are disjoint and their union is
J(v)=J_{in}(v)\cup J_{out}(v)
.

The Kirchhoff’s law states that the algebraic sum of all currents entering any node must equal zero. Equivalently, the total current entering a node equals the total current leaving it. For any node
v\notin V^{(src)}\cup V^{(snk)}
:

\sum_{v^{\prime}\in J_{in}(v)}I(v^{\prime},v)=\sum_{v^{\prime}\in J_{out}(v)}I(v,v^{\prime}),

(1)

where
I(v,v^{\prime})
denotes the current flowing from
v
to
v^{\prime}
. If a node
v\in V^{(src)}
or
v\in V^{(snk)}
is considered, then equation (
1
) becomes:

I^{(src)}(v)=\sum_{v^{\prime}\in J_{out}(v)}I(v,v^{\prime}),\quad I^{(snk)}(v)=\sum_{v^{\prime}\in J_{in}(v)}I(v^{\prime},v).

(2)

A direct consequence of applying the Kirchhoff’s rule to every node in the circuit is that the total current entering the entire circuit must equal the total current leaving it. For our circuit with a source vertex set
V^{(src)}
and a sink vertex set
V^{(snk)}
,

\sum_{v\in V^{(src)}}I^{(src)}(v)=\sum_{v\in V^{(snk)}}I^{(snk)}(v),

(3)

where the left side of the equation represents the total injected current at the source nodes while the r.h.s. is the total extracted current at the sink nodes.

Ohm’s law.
In a circuit, there exists a function
\phi:V\to\mathbb{R}
called electric potential, defined up to a constant that fully describes the set of currents in the circuit. For arbitrary neighboring nodes
v
and
v^{\prime}
in the circuit
\phi
satisfy:

\frac{\phi(v)-\phi(v^{\prime})}{R(v,v^{\prime})}=I(v,v^{\prime}),

(4)

where
R(v,v^{\prime})
and
I(v,v^{\prime})
are the resistance and the current between the nodes, respectively

1

1

The difference in potentials
\phi(v)-\phi(v^{\prime})
for neighboring vertices is called the
voltage
and denoted as
U(v,v^{\prime})
. Eq. (
4
) can be rewritten as
\frac{U(v,v^{\prime})}{R(v,v^{\prime})}=I(v,v^{\prime})
.
.

Superposition principle.

Consider two systems of sources and sinks on
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
:
\{I^{(src)}_{1}(v)\}_{v\in V_{1}^{(src)}},\;\{I^{(snk)}_{1}(v)\}_{v\in V_{1}^{(snk)}}
and
\{I^{(src)}_{2}(v)\}_{v\in V_{2}^{(src)}},\;\{I^{(snk)}_{2}(v)\}_{v\in V_{2}^{(snk)}}
. Suppose the first system establishes potentials
\phi_{1}(v),v\in V
, and the second system establishes potentials
\phi_{2}(v)
. Then, the two systems merged together will produce a potential

\phi(v)=\phi_{1}(v)+\phi_{2}(v),

(5)

which also means that current in the merged system is additive for all
v,v^{\prime}\in V
:

I(v,v^{\prime})=I_{1}(v,v^{\prime})+I_{2}(v,v^{\prime}).

(6)

3.1
Problem formulation and an intuitive explanation of our ECD
2
G method

Problem formulation.
We assume that the state space
\mathcal{X}
is a discrete space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{S}^{D}
, where
\mathbb{S}
is a finite set. For convenience, we say that it is the space of categories, e.g.,
\mathbb{S}=\{1,2,..,S\}
. We denote a set of probability distributions on this space as
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{D})
and introduce a
source
distribution as
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{D})
and a
target
distribution as
q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{D})
. These data distributions are assumed to be accessible by random empirical samples
x_{1},...,x_{N}\sim p
and
y_{1},...,y_{M}\sim q
that are
D
-dimensional vectors of categories.
The task of distribution transfer
is to use these empirical samples to build a procedure
T
that is capable to transform any new samples
x^{new}\sim p
in to samples from
q
. Formally, this is equivalent to samples
T(x^{new})
being distributed as the target samples from
q
.

Intuitive explanation of our ECD
2
G approach.
We construct a current circuit
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
with
V^{(src)}=\mathcal{X}
and
V^{(snk)}=\mathcal{X^{\prime}}
, where
\mathcal{X^{\prime}}
is an independent copy of
\mathcal{X}
. We interpret a current
I^{(src)}(v)
flowing into a circuit at a subset of states
\mathcal{X}
as the source distribution
p(x)
, where
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Analogously, a current
I^{(snk)}(v)
flowing out of the circuit through states
\mathcal{X^{\prime}}
is associated with the target distribution
q(x^{\prime})
, where
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X^{\prime}}
. We approximate the currents using a neural-network-based approach. By associating the electric current in the circuit with the transfer of probability mass, we map samples
x^{new}\!\sim\!p
through the circuit to the target distribution
q
.

3.2
Main theoretical construction of our ECD
2
G method

For convenience, we consider a general electric circuit as a connected graph
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
. However, a particular practical realization of the circuit is given in the next section (
\wasyparagraph
3.3
). We assume that each edge
e(v,v^{\prime})\in E
has a resistance
R(v,v^{\prime})
. Recall that from Kirchhoff’s law (
3
) the total incoming current
I^{(src)}
equals the total outgoing current
I^{(snk)}
. Without a loss of generality, we suppose that the total incoming current as well as the total outgoing current are each equal to 1:

\sum_{v\in V^{(src)}}I^{(src)}(v)=\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V^{(snk)}}I^{(snk)}(v^{\prime})=1.

(7)

This allows to interpret
I^{(src)}
and
I^{(snk)}
as the source distribution
p
and the target
q
, respectively:

\forall v\in V^{(src)}:I^{(src)}(v)=p(v)\quad\text{ and }\quad\forall v^{\prime}\in V^{(snk)}:I^{(snk)}(v^{\prime})=q(v^{\prime}).

(8)

The transfer of probability mass from source to sink is realized by the stochastic walk on the circuit. We set up the following rule for the steps between neighboring vertices.

Definition 3.1

(Movement Rule)
.

The probability of transition from vertex
v\in V:v\notin V^{(snk)}
to a neighbor
v^{\prime}\in J_{out}(v)
is defined by:

\mathrm{prob}(v^{\prime}|v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{I(v,v^{\prime})}{\sum\limits_{v^{\prime\prime}\in J_{out}(v)}I(v,v^{\prime\prime})}.

(9)

If
v\in V^{(snk)}
, the flow of current either terminates with probability
\mathrm{prob}(\mathrm{term}|v)
, or, if possible, continues to vertex
v^{\prime}
with probability
\mathrm{prob}(v^{\prime}|v)
as follows:

\mathrm{prob}(term|v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{I^{(snk)}(v)}{I^{(snk)}(v)+\sum\limits_{v^{\prime\prime}\in J_{out}(v)}I(v,v^{\prime\prime})},\quad\mathrm{prob}(v^{\prime}|v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{I(v,v^{\prime})}{I^{(snk)}(v)+\sum\limits_{v^{\prime\prime}\in J_{out}(v)}I(v,v^{\prime\prime})}.

(10)

The denominator represents the total current flowing out of vertex
v
. Therefore, the probabilities (
9
) and (
10
) of movement to neighboring vertices are proportional to the current in the respective edges. Having determined one-step transition (
9
) in the circuit, we note that the following termination property holds for the multi-step transition in the circuit.

Proposition 3.2

(Finite-step termination)
.

If the movement through the circuit starts at vertices
V^{(src)}
and obeys the movement rule
3.1
, it terminates at nodes
V^{(snk)}
in a finite number of steps.

Proof.

Since the graph is finite, infinite movement is only possible if there exists a cycle of vertices
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{C}
, where
v_{C}=v_{1}
with non-zero current. However, current flows from higher to lower potential. For a cycle to sustain a current, the potentials must satisfy
\phi(v_{1})>\phi(v_{2})>...>\phi(v_{C})=\phi_{1}
, which is a contradiction. Thus, the movement consists of a finite number of steps.
∎

Proposition
3.2
allows us to define a
stochastic
mapping
T:V^{(src)}\to V^{(snk)}
that transforms sources to sinks according to movement rule
3.1
. We now formulate the proposition, which guarantees a successful transfer between the source and target distributions.:

Corollary 3.3

(ECD
2
G transport)
.

Considering the aforementioned circuit scheme, the sequential movement by
T
from

v\in V^{(src)}
terminates at
T(v)\in V^{(snk)}
with probability 1. Moreover, if
v\sim p
is a random variable, then
T(v)\sim q
, i.e. the movement through the circuit with probabilities proportional to the currents transforms distribution
p
on
V^{(src)}
into distribution
q
on
V^{(snk)}
.

This result follows directly from classical network flow theory
(
Ahuja et al. 1993
,
\wasyparagraph
6)
and properties of absorbing Markov chains
(
Grinstead & Snell 1997
,
\wasyparagraph
11)
. The conservation (
1
) and normalization (
7
) conditions establish a feasible unit flow from sources
V^{(src)}
to sinks
V^{(snk)}
. The movement rule (
9
) defines an absorbing Markov chain where sinks are absorbing states. For a rigorous proof of this absorption property for Markov chains defined by unit flows see, e.g.,
(
Kemeny & Snell 1960
,
\wasyparagraph
3)
.

3.3
Practical realization of an electric circuit

In practice, we are interested in transforming distributions on a particular discrete space
\mathcal{X}
. Thus, we consider a specific structure of an electric circuit as an
L
-partite graph
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
. This graph consists of
|V|=(L+1)|\mathcal{X}|
vertices, where each partite (layer) is composed of
|\mathcal{X}|
vertices. We denote the set of vertices on the
\ell
-th layer as
\mathcal{X}_{(\ell)}
, then
V=\mathcal{X}_{(0)}\cup..\cup\mathcal{X}_{(L)}
.
Edges
E
of this graph can only connect vertices from neighboring layers, that is,
E=\cup_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{X}_{(\ell)}\times\mathcal{X}_{(\ell+1)}
. In this configuration (Figure
1
), the distribution
p
is defined on
\mathcal{X}_{(0)}\!\equiv\!V^{(src)}
, while the distribution
q
is defined on
\mathcal{X}_{(L)}\equiv V^{(snk)}
. A vertex
x_{0}\!\in\!\mathcal{X}^{(0)}
is a source vertex in
V^{(src)}
where a current
I^{(src)}(x_{0})\!=\!p(x_{0})

x_{L}\!\in\!\mathcal{X}_{(L)}
is a sink in
V^{(snk)}
where a current
I^{(snk)}(x_{L})\!=\!q(x_{L})

We denote the potentials, currents, and resistances with an additional layer index
\ell
as
\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell}),\;R_{\ell,\ell+1}(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}),\;I_{\ell,\ell+1}(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1})
.
We set the resistances as follows:

R_{\ell,\ell+1}(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1})=\begin{cases}r,&x_{\ell}=x_{\ell+1}\\
R,&x_{\ell}\neq x_{\ell+1}\end{cases}

(11)

In other words, the ”forward” resistance is
r
, and all ”lateral” resistances are equal and denoted by
R
. Thus, using
R>r
in practice, we encourage the preservation of the state in the next layer rather than its change. By introducing the resistances
r
and
R

L

\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)

Calculation of potentials
\phi_{l}
for
\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)

x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{(0)}
which flows out at
x_{L}\in\mathcal{X}_{(L)}

\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell}|x_{0},x_{L})

in such system. Then, the superposition principle yields

\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell})=\sum_{x_{0},x_{L}}\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell}|x_{0},x_{L})\pi(x_{0},x_{L}),

(12)

where
\pi(x_{0},x_{L})
is an arbitrary joint distribution on
\mathcal{X}_{(0)}\times\mathcal{X}_{(L)}
satisfying
\sum_{x_{L}}\pi(x_{0},x_{L})=p(x_{0})
and
\sum_{x_{0}}\pi(x_{0},x_{L})=q(x_{L})

\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell})

and output

current distributions, the following observation arises.

(Transport plan independence)

Potential
\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell})

Generative Flow Networks

(

sample
discrete structures with a probability proportional to a given reward function. Although, the problem they consider differs from ours, their approach shares similarities with our work. They also look at the flow of probability mass through a graph. However, they learn edges of a graph unlike our method, where we first predefine resistance of edges and then compute the flow.

Discrete Diffusion Models

(
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Gat et al. 2024
, DDM)
are powerful classes for data generation in discrete data setups. DDM use forward (data destruction) process built on
predefined
transition matrix unlike our method. As a result, the forward process of DDM via uniform
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
or absorbing
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
transition matrix noises clean data either into a uniformly distributed sample or a special token. In contrast, our approach is theoretically able to learn transformations between any discrete data distributions.

Discrete Flow Matching

(
Gat et al. 2024
, DFM)
learns a flow field between two data distributions. In contrast to our ECD
2
G, their flow learned in their DFM depends on the data transport plan
\pi
used for training, while our flow is the uniquely determined flow of the electric current (Remark
3.4
). Note, in our method, one may estimate the current in any edge
(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1})
with arbitrary batch size of samples from
\pi
. In turn, in DFM, and, generally, FM, only one-sample estimates are available, and only for points specifically sampled from the interpolant
(
Ryzhakov et al. 2024
, §1.2)
.

Electrostatic-based methods

(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Kolesov et al. 2025
;
Manukhov et al. 2025
)
interpret data distributions
p(\cdot)
and
q(\cdot)
as positive and negative charge densities, respectively. Data transport and/or generation are performed by moving along the field lines generated by these charge distributions. The theoretical justification for these methods relies on the principle of flux conservation in the generated field. The proposed ECD
2
G model also employs electricity theory, where flux conservation corresponds to the Current Law (
1
). However, all existing electrostatic-based models operate in a continuous setting, whereas ECD
2
G performs discrete data generation.

6
Future work

Defining the optimal training volume.
Our training approach involves sampling layers
{\ell}
and states
x_{\ell}

within these layers. We use uniform discrete distributions over
L
layers and
S^{D}
categories (see
\wasyparagraph
3.4
). This allows us to approximate the current in the circuit between a state
x_{\ell}
and states
x_{\ell+1}
. However, this sampling is not unique, and there are many other possible ways to define a training volume for efficient learning. This is a promising direction for further research.

Circuit construction.
Circuit scheme and edge resistances are the key hyperparameters in our approach. Our proposed current flow model and its resistance assignment scheme encourage flow to remain at the same state in the next layer, while slightly suppressing flow to other nodes. Future work could explore resistance schemes that assign distinct resistances to each node pair. More broadly, developing more efficient circuit architectures remains a promising research direction.

Factorization.
We perform low-dimensional experiments where we can explicitly perform each sampling step using (
9
). However, the denominator in (
9
) likely becomes computationally infeasible as the dimensionality and number of categories increase. Following discrete diffusions
(
Lou et al. 2024
;
Campbell et al. 2022
)
, one could explore a factorization technique to improve the method’s scalability, which represents an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Single source-sink setting

A.1
Conductance Matrix and the Node-Voltage Equation in a Matrix Form

To solve the formulated problem, we need to define the conductance matrix and write down the node-voltage equation in a matrix form.

Definition A.1

(Conductance Matrix)
.

The conductance matrix
G
, defined for an electrical circuit represented by a graph
G(V,E)
, is a matrix of size
|V|\times|V|
, where:

\begin{split}G_{ii}&=\sum_{v_{k}\in J_{n}(v_{i})}\frac{1}{R(v_{i},v_{k})}\quad\text{for diagonal terms,}\\
G_{ik}&=-\frac{1}{R(v_{i},v_{k})}\quad\text{for non-diagonal terms }(i\neq k).\end{split}

(15)

Let us define the vector containing all node potentials:

\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(v_{1})\\
\phi(v_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\phi(v_{|V|})\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

And the vector of currents:

i=\begin{pmatrix}i(v_{1})\\
i(v_{2})\\
\vdots\\
i(v_{|V|})\end{pmatrix},

(17)

where
i(v_{k})=\sum_{v\in J_{n}(v_{k})}I(v,v_{k})
. If a current
I^{(\mathrm{src})}(v_{k})
flows
into
node
v_{k}
, then
i(v_{k})=I^{(\mathrm{src})}(v_{k})
; if a current
I^{(\mathrm{snk})}(v_{k})
flows
out of
node
v_{k}
, then
i(v_{k})=-I^{(\mathrm{snk})}(v_{k})
. In all other cases, by Kirchhoff’s Current Law,
i(v_{k})=0
, since the sum of currents flowing into a node equals the sum of currents flowing out.

Thus, the successive application of Ohm’s and Kirchhoff’s laws leads to the equation for determining the node potentials, known as the
node-voltage equation
:

G\cdot\phi=i.

(18)

A.2
Analytical Solution for
\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)

Consider an electrical circuit
\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)
with a unit current entering the system at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{(0)}
and leaving at
x_{L}\in\mathcal{X}_{(L)}
. Assume there are no other sources or sinks in the circuit besides
x_{0}
and
x_{L}
. Each layer
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}
of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)
is a copy of
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{S}^{D}
. Consequently, two distinct situations may arise:
x_{0}=x_{L}
or
x_{0}\neq x_{L}
. These cases must be analyzed separately.

A.2.1
Structure of the potential
\phi
and current
i
vectors

The graph
\mathcal{G}_{L}(|\mathcal{X}|,R,r)
consists of
L+1
layers. Each layer contains
n=|\mathcal{X}|
vertices. Therefore, we will express the potential vector
\phi
and the current vector
i
in a block structure:

\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{0}\\
\phi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{L}\end{pmatrix},\quad i=\begin{pmatrix}i_{0}\\
i_{1}\\
\vdots\\
i_{L}\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

Here, each
\phi_{\ell}
and
i_{\ell}
is a column vector of height
n
:

\forall\ell=0,\dots,L:\quad\phi_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell 1})\\
\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell 2})\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{\ell}(x_{\ell n})\end{pmatrix},\quad i_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}i_{\ell}(x_{\ell 1})\\
i_{\ell}(x_{\ell 2})\\
\vdots\\
i_{\ell}(x_{\ell n})\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

A.2.2
Symmetry of the problem

The problem possesses a high degree of symmetry. In formulas (
19
) and (
20
), we considered ”vertical” cross-sections of the graph, i.e., layers. Let us now consider a ”horizontal” cross-section, namely, the sets of values:

\left(\phi_{0}(x^{\prime}),\ \phi_{1}(x^{\prime}),\ \phi_{2}(x^{\prime}),\ \dots,\ \phi_{L}(x^{\prime})\right),\quad\text{where }x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\text{ is fixed}.

(21)

These sets are entirely equivalent for all
x^{\prime}\neq x_{0}
and
x^{\prime}\neq x_{L}
. Therefore, the problem of finding
n(L+1)
unknown potentials reduces to finding only
3(L+1)
potentials at the points:

\begin{cases}\phi_{\ell}(x_{0}),&\ell=0,\dots,L;\\
\phi_{\ell}(x_{L}),&\ell=0,\dots,L;\\
\phi_{\ell}(x^{\prime}),&\ell=0,\dots,L,\ \text{where }x^{\prime}\neq x_{0},x_{L}.\end{cases}

(22)

A.2.3
Structure of the Conductance Matrix and the Node-Voltage Equation

For the resistances defined in (
11
), the conductance matrix takes the form of a block-tridiagonal matrix:

G=\begin{pmatrix}D&B&0&0&\cdots&0\\
B^{T}&2D&B&0&\cdots&0\\
0&B^{T}&2D&B&\cdots&0\\
0&0&B^{T}&2D&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&B\\
0&0&0&\cdots&B^{T}&D\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

Here,
B
and
D
are
n\times n
matrices,
n=|\mathcal{X}|
, with the following form:

D=\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0&..&0\\
0&1&..&0\\
..&..&..&..\\
0&0&..&1\end{pmatrix};\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{1}{r}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&\cdots&-\dfrac{1}{R}\\
-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{r}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&\cdots&-\dfrac{1}{R}\\
-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{r}&\cdots&-\dfrac{1}{R}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&-\dfrac{1}{R}&\cdots&-\dfrac{1}{r}\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

Given that all current vectors
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{L-1}
are identically equal to zero (since the sum of incoming and outgoing currents is balanced at the vertices of the intermediate layers), equation (
18
) takes the following form:

\begin{cases}D\phi_{0}+B\phi_{1}&=i_{0},\\
B^{T}\phi_{\ell-1}+2D\phi_{\ell}+B\phi_{\ell+1}&=0,\quad\forall\ell=1,\dots,L-1,\\
B^{T}\phi_{L-1}+D\phi_{L}&=i_{L}.\end{cases}

(25)

A.2.4
Case
x_{0}\neq x_{L}

Now we are finally ready to consider the cases of coinciding and non-coinciding source and sink.

Equation (
25
) in this case takes the form of the following system of equations:

The first set (corresponding to
D\phi_{0}+B\phi_{1}=i_{0}
):

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{0}(x_{0})&-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{1}(x_{0})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{1}(x_{L})=1,\\
\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{0}(x^{\prime})&-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{1}(x_{0})-\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-3}{R}\right)\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{1}(x_{L})=0,\\
\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{0}(x_{L})&-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{1}(x_{L})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{1}(x_{0})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(26)

Next, for all
\ell=1,2,\dots,L-1
(corresponding to
B^{T}\phi_{\ell-1}+2D\phi_{\ell}+B\phi_{\ell+1}=0
):

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{L})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})\\
&\quad+2\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{L})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0,\\
&-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})\\
&\quad+2\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0,\\
&-\frac{1}{R}(\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})+\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{L}))-\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-3}{R}\right)\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})\\
&\quad+2\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{\ell}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{R}(\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})+\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{L}))-\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-3}{R}\right)\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(27)

Finally, the last set (corresponding to
B^{T}\phi_{L-1}+D\phi_{L}=i_{L}
):

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})+\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})=0,\\
&-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{R}\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})-\frac{n-2}{R}\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})+\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})=-1,\\
&-\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-3}{R}\right)\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{R}(\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})+\phi_{L-1}(x_{L}))+\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{n-1}{R}\right)\phi_{L}(x^{\prime})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(28)

These equations can be significantly simplified by noting that
n=|\mathcal{X}|=S^{D}\gg 1
. In this case,
1/n\to 0
, and
n\approx n-1\approx n-2\approx n-3
. Then, multiplying the equations by
R/n
and introducing
\widetilde{r}=r\cdot n/R
, we obtain:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{0}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{1}(x_{0})-\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})=\frac{r}{\widetilde{r},}\\
&\phi_{0}(x^{\prime})=\phi_{1}(x^{\prime}),\\
&\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{0}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{1}(x_{L})-\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(29)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})-\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})+2\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0,\\
&\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{L})-\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})+2\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{L})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0,\\
&-\phi_{\ell-1}(x^{\prime})+2\phi_{\ell}(x^{\prime})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x^{\prime})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(30)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})-\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})+\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{L}(x_{0})=0,\\
&-\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})-\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})+\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}+1\right)\phi_{L}(x_{L})=-1,\\
&\phi_{L-1}(x^{\prime})=\phi_{L}(x^{\prime}).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(31)

Hence:

\phi_{0}(x^{\prime})=\phi_{1}(x^{\prime})=\dots=\phi_{L}(x^{\prime}).

Recall that the potential is defined up to an additive constant. Therefore, we are free to choose the reference point from which it is measured. Let us set:

\phi_{\ell}(x^{\prime})=0\quad\forall\ell=0,1,\dots,L.

Consequently, the system of equations simplifies further, decoupling into two independent parts:

System for
x_{0}
:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\gamma\phi_{0}(x_{0})-\phi_{1}(x_{0})=r,\\
&-\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})+2\gamma\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})=0,\quad\text{for }\ell=1,\dots,L-1,\\
&-\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})+\gamma\phi_{L}(x_{0})=0.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(32)

System for
x_{L}
:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\gamma\phi_{0}(x_{L})-\phi_{1}(x_{L})=0,\\
&-\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{L})+2\gamma\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{L})=0,\quad\text{for }\ell=1,\dots,L-1,\\
&-\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})+\gamma\phi_{L}(x_{L})=-r.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(33)

Here,
\gamma=1+\widetilde{r}=1+\frac{r\cdot n}{R}
. From the solution of (
32
), the solution of (
33
) can be obtained easily:
\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})=-\phi_{L-\ell}(x_{0})
.

Suppose that
\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})
is proportional to
\phi_{0}(x_{L})
via some polynomial of
\gamma
:

\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})=P_{\ell}(\gamma)\phi_{0}(x_{L}).

(34)

Substituting this into (
33
), we find that the polynomial
P_{\ell}(\gamma)
must satisfy the conditions:

\displaystyle P_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=1,

(35)

\displaystyle P_{1}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\gamma,

\displaystyle P_{\ell+1}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=2\gamma P_{\ell}(\gamma)-P_{\ell-1}(\gamma).

These are the recurrence relations for the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind:

P_{\ell}(\gamma)\equiv T_{\ell}(\gamma).

Substituting (
34
) into the last equation of (
33
), we obtain:

-\phi_{L-1}(x_{L})+\gamma\phi_{L}(x_{L})=\left(-T_{L-1}(\gamma)+\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)\right)\phi_{0}(x_{L})=-r.

Thus:

\phi_{0}(x_{L})=-\frac{r}{-T_{L-1}(\gamma)+\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)}

and the solutions are:

\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})=-\frac{T_{\ell}(\gamma)\,r}{-T_{L-1}(\gamma)+\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)},\quad\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})=+\frac{T_{L-\ell}(\gamma)\,r}{-T_{L-1}(\gamma)+\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)}.

(36)

A.2.5
Case
x_{0}=x_{L}

In this case, similarly,
\phi_{\ell}(x^{\prime})=0
for all
\ell=0,1,\dots,L
, and the system of equations for
\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})\equiv\phi_{\ell}(x_{L})
takes the form:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\gamma\phi_{0}(x_{0})-\phi_{1}(x_{0})=r,\\
&-\phi_{\ell-1}(x_{0})+2\gamma\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})-\phi_{\ell+1}(x_{0})=0,\\
&-\phi_{L-1}(x_{0})+\gamma\phi_{L}(x_{0})=-r.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(37)

Suppose the solution has the form:

\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})=P_{\ell}(\gamma)\phi_{0}(x_{0})-rF_{\ell}(\gamma).

(38)

Then the polynomials satisfy the recurrence relations:

\displaystyle P_{\ell+1}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=2\gamma P_{\ell}(\gamma)-P_{\ell-1}(\gamma),

(39)

\displaystyle F_{\ell+1}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=2\gamma F_{\ell}(\gamma)-F_{\ell-1}(\gamma)

with the initial conditions:

P_{0}(\gamma)=1,P_{1}(\gamma)=\gamma,\quad\quad F_{0}(\gamma)=0,F_{1}(\gamma)=1,F_{2}(\gamma)=2\gamma.

(40)

This allows us to identify
P_{\ell}(\gamma)
and
F_{\ell}(\gamma)
with the Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kind, respectively:

P_{\ell}(\gamma)=T_{\ell}(\gamma),\quad F_{\ell}(\gamma)=U_{\ell-1}(\gamma).

Note that the polynomial
U_{\ell}(\gamma)
incorporates an additional condition
U_{-1}(\gamma)\equiv 0
, which is not usually implied in the standard definition.

Substituting (
38
) into the last equation of (
37
), we obtain:

\phi_{0}(x_{0})=r\frac{\gamma U_{L-1}(\gamma)-U_{L-2}(\gamma)-1}{\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)-T_{L-1}(\gamma)}.

Therefore, the final expression for the potentials is:

\phi_{\ell}(x_{0})=r\left(T_{\ell}(\gamma)\frac{\gamma U_{L-1}(\gamma)-U_{L-2}(\gamma)-1}{\gamma T_{L}(\gamma)-T_{L-1}(\gamma)}-U_{\ell-1}(\gamma)\right).

(41)

This completes the proof of the Lemma.

, we use a simple MLP model with hidden layers of size

, weight decay of
