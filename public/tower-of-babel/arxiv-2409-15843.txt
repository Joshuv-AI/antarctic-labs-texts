Abstract
. In online education, innovative tools are crucial for enhancing learning outcomes. SAM (Study with AI Mentor) is an advanced platform that integrates educational videos with a context-aware chat interface powered by large language models. SAM encourages students to ask questions and explore unclear concepts in real time, offering personalized, context-specific assistance, including explanations of formulas, slides, and images. We evaluated SAM in two studies: one with 25 university students and another with 80 crowdsourced participants, using pre- and post-knowledge tests to compare a group using SAM and a control group. The results demonstrated that SAM users achieved greater knowledge gains specifically for younger learners and individuals in flexible working environments, such as students, supported by a 97.6% accuracy rate in the chatbot’s responses. Participants also provided positive feedback on SAM’s usability and effectiveness. SAM’s proactive approach to learning not only enhances learning outcomes but also empowers students to take full ownership of their educational experience, representing a promising future direction for online learning tools.

1.
Introduction

Chatbots have increasingly become valuable tools in educational settings, offering significant enhancements to the learning experience and outcomes
(
Hobert and Meyer von Wolff, 2019
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
. Research consistently shows that these artificial intelligence (AI)-driven tools can increase student engagement, motivation, and knowledge retention by providing personalized support and immediate feedback
(
Pappagallo, 2024
;
Ghayoomi, 2023
;
Tapalova and Zhiyenbayeva, 2022
;
Benotti et al., 2017
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
;
Wu and Yu, 2024
)
. Chatbots have been effectively used to deliver course materials, assist with assignments
(
Sinha et al., 2020
)
, and foster interactive learning environments
(
Hobert and Meyer von Wolff, 2019
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
, leading to improved academic performances
(
Pappagallo, 2024
)
across various disciplines. However, these tools are not without their limitations. Common drawbacks include the potential for contextually irrelevant or inaccurate responses
(
Goodman et al., 2023
)
, limited ability to handle complex queries
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, and reliance on pre-programmed content
(
Ranoliya et al., 2017
)
, which can restrict the depth and adaptability of interactions.

Building on the growing influence of AI in education
(
Kasneci et al., 2023
)
, our tool, SAM (Study with AI Mentor), significantly enhances the learning experience. SAM is an innovative and context-aware platform designed to assist learners in grasping complex topics through interactive, AI-driven mentoring. SAM is built to function as an intelligent video-watching platform where users can watch educational videos, particularly lectures, and engage with an AI mentor to ask questions related to the content. This real-time assistance aims to deepen students’ comprehension by providing immediate, context-relevant feedback, thereby simulating an interactive learning environment. Furthermore, SAM builds on existing literature that demonstrates how asking questions while studying leads to increased engagement and, consequently, improved learning outcomes
(
Zeegers and Elliott, 2019
;
Bühler et al., 2023
;
Sedova et al., 2019
)
. By fostering this proactive behavior, SAM contributes to a more effective and engaging learning process.

The core functionality of SAM revolves around its ability to integrate multiple sources of information. Users can provide a link to the lecture video and optionally upload the associated slides, allowing SAM to rely on both the video transcript and the slides to deliver more accurate and informative responses. As the video progresses, users can interact with the AI mentor by asking questions directly related to the content they view. We designed SAM to be responsive, recognizing specific references to slides or concepts and generating detailed explanations. Additionally, SAM employs LaTeX for rendering complex mathematical formulas, ensuring that all visual elements are presented clearly and accurately, which is essential for subjects that rely heavily on precise notation. Upon completing the video, users could download a PDF summarizing their questions and the AI’s responses, providing a record of their learning process.

For the purpose of the user study, we tailored SAM to support a crowdsourced user study where participants engaged with a pre-selected lecture video. To this end, the platform featured a simplified interface, allowing participants to start the already uploaded video, ensuring uniform interaction with the content and features across the study. In this study, we focused on measuring the knowledge gain to provide insights into how a dynamic and real-time chatbot assistant like SAM can enhance learning outcomes. By examining the impact of SAM on participants’ comprehension and retention, we aimed to better understand the effectiveness of integrating AI-driven, real-time assistance into educational environments.

While chatbots have significantly advanced education, critical gaps remain, particularly in fields requiring complex, context-specific explanations that SAM specifically addresses. SAM represents a new way of learning, where learners take full ownership of their educational journey. By encouraging proactive behavior, SAM not only enhances engagement—a factor already shown to be effective in previous studies
(
Zeegers and Elliott, 2019
;
Bühler et al., 2023
;
Sedova et al., 2019
)
—but also fosters a more interactive and responsive learning environment. Through our user study with SAM, we provide valuable insights into future online learning techniques, offering guidance on how to design tools that trigger and sustain interactive learning experiences. The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Development of SAM, an AI-driven educational tool that enhances learning by integrating real-time question-answering with video content, supporting both text and image-based queries, and accurately rendering complex mathematical formulas.

•

Comprehensive evaluation of SAM’s impact on knowledge gain and user satisfaction through crowdsourcing, highlighting its effectiveness and positive effect. The version, which was used to this study is available at
https://gitlab.lrz.de/hctl/sam
.

•

Identification of key demographic subgroups, such as students and younger users, that demonstrated the highest knowledge gains when using SAM, providing valuable insights for tailoring future educational tools.

2.2.
Chatbots and Virtual Tutors in Learning

Chatbots are increasingly recognized for their transformative educational potential, leveraging AI to enhance learning experiences. They offer personalized and continuous support, which can significantly improve student engagement and motivation, particularly in e-learning environments where traditional interaction may be limited
(
Pappagallo, 2024
;
Ghayoomi, 2023
;
Tapalova and Zhiyenbayeva, 2022
;
Benotti et al., 2017
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
. They can enhance learning by providing course materials
(
Cunningham-Nelson et al., 2019
)
, assignments and practice questions
(
Sinha et al., 2020
)
, while also engaging students individually
(
Hobert and Meyer von Wolff, 2019
)
or facilitating group learning activities
(
Tegos et al., 2014
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
. Chatbots usually engage learners by utilizing text, speech, visuals, touch, and gestures to support them in different educational activities
(
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
. Previous research indicates that chatbots can foster higher-order cognitive skills and reduce dropout rates by adapting to individual student needs
(
Pappagallo, 2024
)
. A recent study
(
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
indicates that many previous works have predominantly utilized chatbots in the fields of computer science and language education. In these contexts, chatbots can assume various roles to enhance learning, most commonly serving as teaching
(
Coronado et al., 2018
;
Song et al., 2017
)
or peer agents
(
Lee et al., 2020
;
da Silva Oliveira et al., 2019
)
, but also functioning as improving
(
Law et al., 2020
)
or motivational agents
(
Schouten et al., 2017
;
Kuhail et al., 2023
;
Wu and Yu, 2024
)
. Teaching agents employ a variety of approaches to enhance learning. These methods include recommending personalized tutorials, initiating discussions based on educational videos or past learning, presenting scenario-based exercises, and providing assessments through multiple-choice questions. On the other hand, peer agent chatbots primarily support students by offering on-demand assistance, including answering specific questions, clarifying terms or concepts, providing guidance on using certain technologies, and facilitating group discussions
(
Kuhail et al., 2023
)
.

Several chatbot implementations highlight these roles, showcasing how chatbots can effectively address diverse educational needs across various subjects. For instance, the FAQ chatbot
(
Han and Lee, 2022
)
assists students in MOOC classes by answering administrative questions, leveraging a pre-trained large language model (LLM) based on information from a FAQ website. Similar to the FAQ chatbot
(
Han and Lee, 2022
)
, Cipherbot
(
Salminen et al., 2024
)
was developed to answer students’ questions based on educational materials that were uploaded by educators to minimize hallucinations. Powered by GPT-3.5 Turbo, Cipherbot achieved an 87.0% accuracy rate in its evaluation study. Both tools are designed to respond to course- or material-specific questions; however, while both utilize externally validated sources, their potential for a close integration with a video player remains to be investigated. To bridge this gap, Sara, the lecturer
(
Winkler et al., 2020
)
, offers scaffolding support during lecture viewing by prompting students with questions and exercises at predefined points within the video. Sara guides learners through these tasks, providing scaffolds until they can solve the posed problems independently. This tool prioritizes engaging learners through inquiry and active questioning rather than directly answering user queries
(
Winkler et al., 2020
)
. Unlike these tools, we designed our framework to provide real-time support during lecture viewing by responding to any student question with high-quality, accurate answers related to the lecture content, focusing specifically on seamless integration with the lecture material to enhance understanding.

On the other hand, there are notable challenges associated with using chatbots. Their integration also raises ethical concerns, including issues of privacy, data manipulation, and the potential for academic dishonesty, particularly with tools like ChatGPT
(
Achiam et al., 2023
;
Yang et al., 2024
;
Lo et al., 2024
)
. Research indicates that user privacy is a significant issue, with self-disclosure being a primary concern due to the sensitive data shared during interactions with chatbots
(
Ischen et al., 2020
;
Gumusel, 2024
)
. Furthermore, the misuse of chatbots can lead to the dissemination of incorrect or harmful safety-related information, emphasizing the need for expert verification and ethical safeguards
(
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Lo et al., 2024
)
. Additionally, as LLMs like GPT-4 evolve, challenges in accurately responding to specific user intents emphasize the need for improvements in intent recognition to enhance user satisfaction and ensure more reliable interactions
(
Bodonhelyi et al., 2024
)
. Overall, while chatbots offer substantial benefits, these concerns highlight the necessity for responsible usage and regulatory frameworks to mitigate risks associated with privacy, ethics, and information accuracy.

While chatbots can facilitate language learning and stimulate curiosity through project-driven tasks, their reliance on extensive knowledge bases poses challenges in development and implementation
(
Ghayoomi, 2023
)
, often leading to difficulties in handling complex messages effectively
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
. This dependency can lead to instances of chatbot fallibility, where the information provided may be inaccurate or misleading
(
Goodman et al., 2023
)
. Such reliability issues can undermine the learning experience, necessitating careful oversight and regular updates to ensure that chatbots remain accurate and trustworthy educational tools
(
Roldan-Vasquez et al., 2024
)
. Educators must be aware of these limitations and supplement chatbot interactions with human guidance to maintain the quality of instruction. To address these challenges, SAM focuses on increasing answer accuracy by providing context-aware responses. By integrating real-time feedback with relevant contextual information, SAM aims to minimize errors and enhance the reliability of its educational support.

Learner engagement, which refers to the investment of students’ cognitive and emotional effort in completing a learning task

, has been shown to positively influence key educational outcomes such as academic performance, persistence, satisfaction, and a sense of community

, engagement becomes crucial to ensure students remain focused, driving better learning outcomes. Effective online engagement also involves leveraging digital tools that promote interaction and personalized learning experiences. AI-driven approaches, such as automated classroom observation protocols, can provide teachers with specific feedback on their practices, streamlining the assessment process while ensuring consistent and valuable insights

Proactive behaviors, such as students actively asking questions, serve as key indicators of engagement in online learning environments
(

Bühler et al., 2023
;
Sedova et al., 2019
)
. When students take the initiative to ask about course content, it reflects a high level of cognitive engagement, pointing out that they are not passively absorbing information but critically processing it. Taking all into account, SAM aligns with and extends theoretical perspectives on active and interactive learning, particularly as articulated in the ICAP framework

By integrating real-time and context-aware interactions into video-based learning, SAM elevates the traditional passive consumption of educational content to an interactive learning experience. Sedova et al., 2019

. This interactive paradigm bridges a critical gap in the literature by demonstrating how AI-driven pedagogical tools can operationalize theoretical constricts of interactive learning in a scalable, real-world application.

3.
SAM Design and Implementation

SAM is an innovative tool designed to enhance educational experiences by integrating AI-driven assistance with video-based learning. SAM operates as an advanced video-watching platform, enabling users to view YouTube lectures while interacting with an AI mentor to pose questions related to the content (
Figure 1
). The integrated AI mentor is powered by GPT-4o
(
OpenAI, 2024
)
, a highly advanced language model that offers sophisticated natural language processing capabilities. The AI mentor not only supports the instant clarification of doubts but also promotes a deeper understanding of complex topics by delivering explanations tailored to learners’ needs. This dynamic and personalized learning experience ultimately helps students grasp the subject matter more effectively, enhancing their overall educational journey.

SAM actively fosters interactive learning, aligning with the principles highlighted in the ICAP framework
(
Chi and Wylie, 2014
)
, which identifies interactivity as the most effective level for enhancing learning compared to constructive, active, or passive approaches, thereby ensuring an optimal learning experience. The user interface design of SAM prioritizes simplicity and aesthetic appeal, keeping in mind previous findings that users tend to favor interfaces that are visually attractive and easy to navigate over overly complex designs
(
Senevirathne and Manathunga, 2021
)
. Research suggests a strong positive relationship between user satisfaction and key factors such as learnability, visibility, and interactivity
(
Senevirathne and Manathunga, 2021
)
. In SAM, the chat window is strategically placed at the bottom of the interface, differing from the conventional right-side placement commonly seen in streaming platforms. This bottom placement allows for a resizable chat window that partially overlaps with the video. However, to enhance user focus, the video automatically pauses when users begin typing, enabling them to engage more effectively with the AI tutor and view detailed responses, including formulas, without distraction.

Figure 2.
Example of SAM in action: user submission of an image and formula visualization.

The user experience with SAM begins when a lecture link from YouTube is provided. Users may also upload corresponding slides with the possibility to also upload previous slides or relevant related materials, which enriches the AI mentor’s ability to deliver accurate and context-aware responses by leveraging both the video transcript and the additional slide information. To achieve highly accurate answers and only answer lecture-related questions, the following prompt was used:

You are roleplaying as an assistant teacher helping students understand their lecture content. Answer their questions based on the video, video transcripts, and slides. If a question is about a lecture unrelated topic, respond with ‘‘Please focus on the lecture material.’’ If you don’t know the answer, just say that you don’t know. Do not attempt to fabricate an answer.

During video playback, users can engage the AI mentor by entering queries in a designated text field and the AI mentor formulates responses to the user inquiries. When a user submits a message, the lecture slides, transcript, the 30 seconds of the transcript before and after the current timestamp, and the chat history are sent to the LLM. The video itself is not forwarded. The timely relevant parts of the transcript are also provided, offering additional context to the embedded language model. Additionally, SAM allows users to upload images (e.g., screenshots from the slides), further aiding the LLM in providing precise and contextually relevant answers (
Figure 2
). SAM is designed with features to enhance the learning process, including the automatic transmission of the current slide whenever the word “slide” appears in a user’s message. For instance, if a user asks, “Explain this slide!” the slide is sent as an image along with the query to the AI mentor, significantly increasing the accuracy and relevance of the response. Prior to the studies, we specifically tested this feature and found that including the slide improved answer accuracy, as the LLM often struggled to pinpoint the exact content of the current slide without this contextual aid. Moreover, SAM supports LaTeX-type formatting for visualizing mathematical formulas, ensuring that complex expressions are displayed clearly, facilitating better comprehension (
Figure 2
). Upon completion of the session, users can download a PDF containing the questions posed and the AI mentor’s responses, serving as a valuable study resource.

To support the smooth operation of SAM, the platform’s architecture (
Figure 3
) was designed with distinct frontend and backend components, each hosted on separate servers. The frontend, deployed on a dedicated server, is responsible for the user interface and interaction. The backend, hosted on Amazon’s Web Services (AWS) server
(
Services, [n. d.]
)
, handles the core processing and data management tasks. AWS
(
Services, [n. d.]
)
was chosen for its robust infrastructure, scalability, and reliability, allowing SAM to efficiently manage multiple user sessions simultaneously. Each user’s data is stored under a specific but anonymous ID, enabling the platform to securely save their interactions and responses, which are also needed for the resulting downloadable PDF. These records are then compiled into a resulting PDF and JSON files for further analysis. The coding of SAM leverages Node Package Manager for managing dependencies, with the core files written in TSX (TypeScript and JSX), a powerful combination that enhances both the type safety and flexibility of the development process. This technical setup ensures that SAM remains a robust, scalable, and user-friendly tool for enhancing educational experiences.

4.
Methodology

Figure 4.
Representation of the structural elements in the pilot and main studies.

To explore how proactive behavior can be encouraged in online learning environments, we designed SAM to provide learners with personalized, real-time support while maintaining their full control over the learning process. This approach aims to investigate the impact of SAM in fostering active participation and deeper engagement during independent learning. We evaluated the effectiveness of SAM in two studies. In the pilot study, we tested SAM among university students with similar educational backgrounds. Our goal was to evaluate the tool within a specific demographic, namely young university students, to gather preliminary insights on its design and usefulness. We then incorporated this feedback to refine our tool before expanding the study to a broader audience. In the main study, we introduced SAM to a diverse audience recruited through an online platform, i.e., Prolific, to assess its effectiveness across a more diverse participant pool with varied learning backgrounds.
Figure 4
represents the structural elements and the differences between the pilot and main studies. We adjusted the study design for the main study to address potential knowledge gaps among participants, as detailed below.

For the purpose of our user studies, we adapted SAM to simplify the user experience. The selected YouTube video and corresponding slides were uploaded into the platform. Participants were only required to click a “Watch Lecture” button to initiate the session, streamlining the process and focusing on the interaction with SAM.

4.1.
Study Design

During both studies, we divided our participants into two groups: a test group and a control group. The test group had full access to SAM, meaning they could watch the video and interact with the AI mentor to ask questions anytime during the lecture (Figure
5b
). In contrast, the control group also used SAM’s video-watching interface, but the chat functionality was disabled (Figure
5a
). This setup (
Figure 5
) enabled us to compare the learning outcomes between participants who had access to the AI mentor and those who did not, providing insights into the impact of interactive AI support on the learning process. In the pilot study, participants were randomly assigned to groups using a backend function. For the main study, however, due to platform limitations (Prolific and Qualtrics), randomization was achieved by sequentially advertising separate study links, first the test group and then the control group. To ensure that participants could only enroll once, we restricted participation through Prolific. The advertisement included minimal information, primarily detailing exclusion criteria and specifically requesting that individuals with advanced knowledge of machine learning refrain from participating. After accepting the invitation, participants received additional information about the study.

After the pilot study, we made a few modifications to our study design. In the pilot study, all participants were enrolled in the same course and had attended the same previous 11 lectures, ensuring a similar background knowledge level across the group. However, the main study was advertised to a broader pool on Prolific, where participants varied in their prior knowledge. To account for this, we introduced pre-knowledge assessments to establish a baseline for each participant. We also included a brief theoretical overview, specifically an explanation of linear regression (refer to
subsection A.4
for the full text), prior to the lecture. Additionally, based on insights from the pilot study, we standardized the test questions by ensuring each question had the same number of answer choices and an equal distribution of correct answers to more effectively measure knowledge gain. In the main study, we also introduced an attention check after 20 minutes of video playback to assess participants’ attentiveness. A window popped up asking, “Would you like to continue to watch the video?” with a button labeled “Continue.” The time taken to click this button was recorded as an indicator of attentiveness.

When designing our user study, we drew inspiration from a study
(
Alneyadi and Wardat, 2023
)
conducted in an elementary school where eleventh graders were divided into two groups: an experimental group with access to ChatGPT and a control group without it, using pre- and post-tests to measure learning outcomes; however, while their study was conducted in a traditional classroom setting over twelve weeks, our research focuses on the context of online learning.

4.2.
Procedure

This section outlines the three phases of our user studies, which were consistent across both the pilot and main studies. Each study followed the same sequence of phases to ensure comparability and reliability in the results. The differences between the pilot and main studies are highlighted in each phase and also summarized in
Figure 4
. The questionnaires used in the studies were designed and administered through the Qualtrics platform.

4.2.1.
Phase 1: Pre-tests

In the first phase of the pilot study, participants were asked to complete a pre-test (Appendix
subsubsection A.5.1
). The pre-test contained six questions and participants were informed that one or more answers could be correct, which helped measure their initial understanding. The pre-test questions were categorized into three key areas: graph neural networks, generative adversarial networks, and reinforcement learning, with two questions per category.

In the first phase of the main study, participants were asked to complete both a pre-knowledge test (Appendix
subsubsection A.6.1
) and a pre-test (Appendix
subsubsection A.6.2
). The pre-knowledge test, which consisted of six questions with only one correct answer each, was designed to assess and categorize participants based on their existing knowledge of neural networks (NNs). The pre-knowledge test was introduced to better understand participants’ prior knowledge of NNs. This was not required in the pilot study, as it was conducted within a university course where participants had similar levels of familiarity with the topic. The pre-test also contained six questions with two correct answers each. The instructions remained consistent with the pilot study, indicating that one or more answers could be correct. Similarly, the pre-test questions were categorized into three key areas: the basics of NNs, the structure and training of NNs, and activation functions, with two questions per category. Following these assessments, participants were provided with a brief text on linear regression in the main study to ensure they had the necessary background knowledge before moving on to the video-watching phase of the study (Appendix
subsection A.4
).

4.2.2.
Phase 2: Lecture watching

In the second phase of our studies, participants engaged in a lecture-watching activity. We piloted the platform among participants of a university course named as “Introduction to Deep Learning” at the Technical University of Munich, on the topic of deep learning. This pilot study focused on the
12th lecture
of the course with a specific focus on more advanced topics, allowing us to gather preliminary feedback on SAM’s usability and measure the participants’ knowledge gain. The video had a duration of 1 hour and 56 minutes. As it encompassed the entire lecture content delivered by the instructor, it was not possible to reduce its length. We chose this lecture because NNs and machine learning are increasingly popular topics that resonate with a broad audience, making them ideal for engaging participants. The course at the university had hundreds of students enrolled, providing a substantial pool of potential participants. Participation in this pilot was entirely voluntary.

In the main study, participants watched the lecture titled
“Introduction to Neural Networks”
. For the main study, we edited the
original video
down to 28 minutes, focusing exclusively on the introduction section. This adjustment was made to accommodate the diverse participant pool by ensuring sustained attention and engagement. Unlike the full-length lecture used in the pilot study, which spanned the entire university course session, the shorter video was better suited to the objectives of this study and the varied backgrounds of the participants. This approach also allowed us to streamline the content, making it more accessible and relevant for the study’s purposes.
We chose this specific lecture because it introduces the fundamentals of NNs, a topic of growing interest in the field of AI. To tailor the content to our study’s needs, we edited the original video to focus exclusively on NNs, ensuring the material was challenging enough to test SAM’s AI-driven mentoring capabilities while still being conceivable by beginners.

4.2.3.
Phase 3: Post-tests

In the third phase of our studies, participants were asked to complete a post-test (Appendix
subsubsection A.5.2
,
subsubsection A.6.3
) and a feedback questionnaire on SAM’s usability (only for the test group), and share their demographic information. This phase followed the same structure in both studies. The post-test comprised six questions, mirroring the structure of the pre-test, with two questions per topic across the same three categories. Both the control and test groups were required to complete this test in the studies. Participants were informed that one or more answers could be correct, similar to the pre-test questionnaire. This assessment was designed to gauge any improvements in the participants’ understanding of the material after watching the lecture, with a specific focus on whether the AI-driven mentoring of SAM facilitated enhanced learning outcomes.

Key aspects of the feedback questionnaire included the response time of SAM, the quality and reliability of its answers, and whether their comprehension of the three NN topics improved after interacting with SAM. Afterward, we collected demographics from both groups, covering age, gender, and employment status.

4.3.
Participant Recruitment

In our main user study evaluating SAM, we recruited participants from the Prolific platform, splitting them into two groups—one utilizing an AI mentor during video watching (i.e., test group) and the other simply watching the video (i.e., control group). We chose Prolific due to its reputation for providing high-quality data
(
Peer et al., 2022
)
. Our sample was gender-balanced, including participants who were 18 or older, located in Germany, and fluent in English. After recruitment, participants were directed to Qualtrics for the study, and upon completion, they returned to Prolific for compensation, which was set at a rate of €12.41/hour. Given the time difference between interacting with the AI mentor and watching the video uninterrupted, the main study duration varied between 42 and 45 minutes. All data was collected anonymously, with digital informed consent obtained from each participant before the study began. Participants could withdraw at any time without further consequences to them. For the main study on Prolific, we recruited
n_{control}=51
for the control group and
n_{test}=59
for the test group. For the pilot study in the university course, we recruited
n_{control}=15
for the control group and
n_{test}=15
for the test group.

We excluded participants who completed the study in an unusually short amount of time. Given the time differences between the two groups and after analyzing outliers based on the total study duration, we set minimum time thresholds of 20 minutes for the control group and 35 minutes for the test group. These thresholds were consistent with testing in smaller groups, which identified the optimal time range for each phase. Participants who spent less time than this likely did not fully engage with the questions or the lecture, making their responses less reliable. We excluded participants who failed the attention check, which involved responding to a message that appeared during the video. They were excluded from the study if they did not click on this message within eight minutes. Additionally, participants who provided low-quality answers were excluded, as they directly entered the post-test questions into SAM, resulting in responses that did not accurately reflect their comprehension. After filtering out participants with age discrepancies greater than three years or mismatches in gender (e.g., listed as a man in one place and woman in another) between Prolific (if applicable) and Qualtrics, we ended up with the final sample sizes that are presented in
Table 1
. We also provide more detailed demographics in the Appendix
subsection A.1
. Ethical considerations were strictly adhered to, with all user data collected anonymously, and an electronic consent obtained for storing, using, and potentially sharing the conversational data for research purposes.

Analysis

We evaluated the collected results through a comprehensive analysis of multiple aspects, including knowledge gain, user satisfaction, and answer accuracy. By examining these key metrics, we aim to assess the overall effectiveness of SAM in enhancing the learning experience. We assessed participants’ overall performance by assigning points to each question based on the number of correct answers it contained. Participants earned points for correctly selecting valid answers and for refraining from selecting incorrect ones. We initially analyzed the total points achieved by participants in both the pre- and post-test phases. Additionally, we examined the number of incorrect selections made, providing insight into participants’ accuracy and comprehension before and after using SAM. After the pilot study, a pre-knowledge test was included because self-reported assessments of prior knowledge, rated on a 5-point scale from “no knowledge” to “expert knowledge,” can be subjective and inconsistent. To obtain a more accurate measure of participants’ understanding of NNs, we included six objective pre-knowledge questions in the main study. These questions provided a standardized basis for grouping participants according to their actual level of expertise, ensuring a more reliable analysis of the results. Participants who scored 1-2 points on the pre-knowledge test were categorized as having no prior knowledge, while those who scored 3-4 points were considered to have basic knowledge, and those who scored 5-6 points were classified as having intermediate knowledge.

Furthermore, we analyzed the frequently asked questions to understand user focus areas and identify key learning challenges, alongside a detailed examination of all hallucinations generated by SAM. This included evaluating every hallucination individually as well as the accuracy of the responses, their potential causes, and how users reacted to these inaccuracies. Additionally, we collected user feedback on our tool’s usability and satisfaction using a 5-point Likert scale.

5.
Results

In this section, we present the findings from our study, focusing on the evaluation of SAM’s effectiveness as an educational tool. By comparing participants’ performance in the test and control groups and in demographic subgroups, we aim to offer insights into how triggered interactions enhance learning outcomes. Additionally, we explore how these insights can inform the design of future educational applications for more effective learning experiences.

On average, participants in the main study interacted with SAM (lecture watching and/or chatting) for
31.71\pm 10.91
minutes and
31.78\pm 17.64
minutes in the control and test groups, respectively, reflecting consistent usage patterns across groups. While the average duration in the test and control groups are similar, the difference in standard deviation indicates that SAM users tended to spend more time actively engaging with the system. In both groups, participants frequently used playback controls such as speed adjustments and rewinds, which accounts for variations in interaction duration. The interaction duration in the pilot study was more diverse due to students utilizing SAM to prepare for the exam and note-taking while stopping the video.

6.
Discussion and Limitations

SAM is an AI-driven educational tool designed to enhance learning by integrating real-time, context-aware question-answering with video content. A main user study involving 80 participants evaluated SAM’s effectiveness, focusing on knowledge gain, user satisfaction, and answer accuracy. However, variability in participants’ prior knowledge, engagement levels, or motivation could have influenced the results. In our main study, we used a video lecture from a university course as the main instructional material. This video, being the third in the course series, may not have been sufficient to bring all participants to a similar baseline understanding of the topic at hand. By providing a summary of the concept to the participants before watching the lecture helped address this issue. This gap is evident in the test group, where many participants sought further explanations (e.g., on linear regression) during the study. Although we instructed participants not to use external resources during the tests, we cannot be entirely certain that this was adhered to. We attempted to mitigate this by monitoring the time participants spent on the study.

Moreover, because the pilot study was integrated into the course, we lacked clear insights into the participants’ prior knowledge. Since the question, “Do you have previous knowledge in the following areas: Graph Neural Networks, Generative Models, or Reinforcement Learning?” relies on self-reporting, we were unable to objectively measure how much they had absorbed from previous lectures or related courses in machine learning and deep learning offered by the same university. Feedback from the pilot study was valuable and informed adjustments for the main study, though some areas for improvement may have remained unaddressed. Another limitation identified was SAM’s inconsistent performance in answering questions. In rare cases, SAM responded with “I don’t know,” even when it had previously provided correct answers to differently phrased questions. There were also some instances where SAM failed to understand the specific context of a question, offering general but irrelevant answers to the current lecture content.

In designing SAM, we aligned its functionality with the ICAP framework
(
Chi and Wylie, 2014
)
, ensuring it operates at the interactive learning level, where learners actively engage through real-time interactions, such as they could discuss the given justifications, ask questions, and discuss similarities and differences if needed. In contrast, the control group was limited to the active learning level, allowing them only to manipulate the video without additional support. However, not all participants utilized the option to interact with the video, resulting in some engaging solely in passive learning. Future work could explore alternative support for the control group, such as a blog for asynchronous assistance. However, this approach was beyond the scope of this study due to practical constraints with Prolific, as varying interaction times would disrupt the real-time scenario we aimed to simulate. SAM’s primary goal is to provide immediate, real-time support, and offering instant responses through a blog would compromise the integrity of the results. Also, future work could explore how SAM’s design principles inform broader educational theories, particularly in asynchronous or hybrid learning contexts.

Furthermore, while SAM was specifically designed with students in mind and demonstrated effectiveness for this subgroup, it did not perform as well for full-time employees. The structure of the tool emphasizes flexibility and interactive engagement, and it aligns well with the learning habits and needs of students who are accustomed to using digital platforms for their studies. However, full-time employees, who may have different learning preferences or time constraints, did not benefit from SAM as much. These findings suggest that while SAM is a powerful tool for enhancing student learning, its design might need to be adjusted to accommodate different user groups better, such as full-time professionals who require more tailored educational resources.

The evaluation in our study focused on a single topic, neural networks, which is highly structured and well-suited for AI-driven mentoring. The tool’s applicability to disciplines requiring more abstract or discussion-oriented learning remains unexplored. Testing SAM in these diverse areas could provide insights into its versatility and effectiveness across different disciplines. In general, SAM’s flexible design allows for adjustments to accommodate various subjects and lecture materials, making it adaptable to diverse educational contexts. Another limitation of SAM is the need for a thorough investigation into how lecture video quality impacts learning outcomes both with and without SAM’s support. The present study relied on high-quality instructional videos with clear audio, well-organized content, and accompanying slides. However, the impact of varying video quality on SAM’s effectiveness has not yet been systematically examined. Factors such as audio clarity, visual fidelity, and the coherence of lecture materials may influence both user engagement and the AI’s ability to provide accurate, contextually relevant responses.

Additionally, although SAM is flexible in its use, it is currently optimized for videos available on YouTube. This is a limitation given that many educational institutions utilize other platforms, such as Panopto
(
Getenet et al., 2022
)
, for hosting and streaming lecture content. Future iterations of SAM should consider integrating with a broader range of video-watching tools to enhance its applicability and reach within academic environments. Expanding SAM’s compatibility with widely used educational platforms would not only increase its utility but also support its adoption across various learning contexts, ensuring that more students and educators can benefit from its capabilities.

While this study primarily measured short-term knowledge gains and user satisfaction, future research should assess SAM’s impact on long-term knowledge retention and higher-order cognitive skills, such as critical thinking and problem-solving. Metrics such as retention rates, frequency of subsequent interactions with SAM, and the transferability of learned concepts to novel scenarios would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the tool’s educational value. Additionally, longitudinal studies that track learners’ progress over time could elucidate the sustained impact of SAM on academic achievement and self-directed learning behaviors. These investigations would not only validate SAM’s effectiveness but also contribute to the design of adaptive learning systems that promote lifelong learning.

Although SAM currently utilizes GPT-4o
(
OpenAI, 2024
)
as its underlying LLM, this model is easily exchangeable, allowing for flexibility in integrating other commercial or open-source LLMs. Our primary focus has been on SAM’s long-term impact in fostering personalized, real-time learning support rather than being tied to a specific model. Investigating the most reasonable LLM for SAM’s continued development is a future direction for this project.

Overall, SAM introduces a novel approach to real-time support by delivering highly accurate answers combined with personalized learning experiences tailored to individual needs. This innovation showcases the potential of adaptive AI systems to enhance engagement and effectiveness in educational tools.

7.
Conclusion

We introduced SAM, a context-aware AI chatbot that allows students to ask questions and explore unclear parts of the lecture. User studies with a total of 105 participants (80 in the main and 25 in the pilot study) revealed its effectiveness as an educational tool focusing on proactive teaching. SAM enhances learning outcomes by encouraging students to take full ownership of their learning experience and fosters a more interactive and engaged learning environment. The real-time, personalized assistance provided by SAM allows learners to delve deeper into complex topics, reinforcing their understanding of the lecture content. The studies highlighted SAM’s potential as an effective learning tool, and the detailed analysis of demographic groups revealed that SAM has a great potential, especially for young learners and individuals in flexible working environments, such as students, to increase knowledge gains. We also found that participants who had intermediate-level prior knowledge asked more questions during lecture watching. According to expert raters, SAM maintained a high answer correctness rate of 97.6%, and overall, participants provided positive feedback regarding user satisfaction, response quality, and the tool’s helpfulness, with many expressing a willingness to use SAM in the future. These findings underscore SAM’s potential to transform education by promoting interactivity and learner independence. Future versions could expand its use beyond STEM subjects to areas that focus on discussion, interpretation, and critical thinking, while also incorporating features like summarizing argumentative texts to support learning in non-STEM disciplines.

Linear Regression Summary from the Pre-Test Questionnaire

The following text appeared for each participant before watching the video:

“Linear regression is a statistical method used to model the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables by fitting a linear equation to the observed data. The simplest form, simple linear regression, involves a single independent variable and models the relationship with the equation
y=m\cdot x+b
, where
y
is the predicted value,
x
is the independent variable,
m
is the slope of the line (indicating the relationship strength and direction between
x
and
y
), and
b
is the y-intercept (the value of
y
when
x
is zero). In multiple linear regression, the model includes multiple independent variables and extends to

(1)

y=b_{0}+b_{1}\cdot x_{1}+b_{2}\cdot x_{2}+...+b_{n}\cdot x_{n}.

The coefficients
b_{i}
are estimated using methods such as Least Squares, which minimizes the sum of the squared differences between the observed values and the values predicted by the model. The goal is to find the best-fitting line that explains the variation in the dependent variable based on the independent variables, enabling predictions of
y
for given values of
x
.

Linear regression connects to classification tasks by providing a foundation for understanding how inputs (such as images of cats and dogs) can be used to predict outputs (labels - "cat" or "dog"). While linear regression predicts continuous values, classification tasks involve predicting discrete class labels. Techniques similar to linear regression, such as logistic regression, extend this concept by applying a transformation to the linear equation to handle binary or multiclass classification. This approach models the probability that a given input belongs to a particular class, enabling the assignment of class labels based on the highest predicted probability.”
