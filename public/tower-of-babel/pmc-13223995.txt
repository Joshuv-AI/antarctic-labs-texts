Genomic insights into the tunicamycin-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress response in peripheral blood mononuclear cells: A comprehensive RNA sequencing analysis
Authors: Laura S Mahl, Axel H Meyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTunicamycin induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress by inhibiting N-glycosylation of newly synthesized proteins, leading to accumulation of misfolded proteins in the ER. This activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), a conserved adaptive pathway aimed at restoring proteostasis. A key mediator of this response is the kinase PERK which activates a signal cascade that leads to a global reduction in translation, while selectively enhancing the expression of stress-associated genes. This PERK-mediated response also constitutes a major arm of the integrated stress response (ISR). Under persistent ER stress, these response pathways may trigger pro-apoptotic programs in addition to adaptive responses. In this study, we used next-generation RNA sequencing to profile transcriptional changes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) after 24 h of tunicamycin exposure, alone or with the PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 or the eIF2B activator ISRIB, to assess stress responses at distinct regulatory points within the PERK-ISR pathway. Key findings were validated by qRT-PCR, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Gene Ontology Resource and the Reactome database. ER stress altered gene expression, with 4.80% of genes upregulated and 12.67% downregulated. GSK2606414 suppressed a broader subset of tunicamycin-induced genes than ISRIB. However, both inhibitors effectively reversed upregulation of the top two stress-responsive genes, TNC and WNT5A. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed overrepresentation of pathways related to ER stress responses, unfolded protein homeostasis, ER chaperone complex assembly, amino acid biosynthesis and interconversion, and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase-mediated protein translation. These findings provide mechanistic insights into chronic ER stress in PBMCs and identify WNT5A and TNC as candidate biomarkers, pending further validation.Keywords: UPR, ISR, ER stress, PERK, PBMCs, eIF2α, Tunicamycin, Transcriptomics

Introduction

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is crucial for maintaining protein homeostasis in the cell. Under conditions of prolonged stress, misfolded proteins, which are normally degraded in the ubiquitin-proteasome, accumulate in the ER and activate the unfolded protein response (UPR) as a response mechanism. Induction of the UPR is detected by three ER-based molecules, ATF6, IRE1 and PERK, activating several signaling pathways including the integrated stress response (ISR), an adaptive intracellular mechanism that plays a critical role in maintaining [1,2]. Four eIF2α kinases (PKR, PERK, GCN2, and HRI) respond to distinct stress stimuli by undergoing dimerization and autophosphorylation, which phosphorylate the α subunit of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) at serine 51 [3]. eIF2α plays a key role in translation initiation, and eIF2 phosphorylation acts as a potent noncompetitive eIF2B inhibitor, suppressing eIF2B-mediated exchange of eIF2-GDP for eIF2-GTP [4]. This leads to inhibition of tertiary complex assembly, resulting in attenuated overall translation efficacy and decreased global protein synthesis. However, eIF2a phosphorylation paradoxically increases the translation of a restricted set of mRNAs via upstream open reading frame (uORF)-mediated control, including those encoding activating transcription factor 4 and 5 (ATF4 and ATF5), growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible protein 34 (GADD34), tribbles pseudokinase 3 (TRIB3), and C/EBP homologous protein [5,6].

Triggering gene expression reprogramming by ISR activation is essential to ensure a rapid and effective cellular stress response. ISR activation represses global protein synthesis, aids the conservation of energy and nutrients, and reduces the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) proteostatic burden, offering temporary stress alleviation [7]. However, to respond to stress in a more targeted manner, ISR activation also triggers increased ATF4 expression, which acts as a master transcription factor and increases the transcription of its target genes [8].

The ISR pathway serves primarily as a cytoprotective and pro-survival program but may also promote pro-apoptotic gene expression in cases of severe or prolonged stress. The fact that the ISR is part of a larger cellular stress response makes it difficult to connect downstream effects specifically to the ISR [9].

There are only a few compounds to specifically activate the ISR. A recently described example is A8, a small molecule that activates the ISR kinase HRI [10]. In fact, many studies use less selective compounds like tunicamycin to induce ER stress. On the other hand, selective compounds like GSK2606414, a selective PERK inhibitor [11,12] or the eIF2α activator ISRIB allow for more selective manipulation of the ISR.

Persistent activation or dysregulation of the ISR signaling network has been linked to several neurogenerative diseases [2]. In vanishing white matter disease, loss-of-function mutations in eIF2B subunits impair ISR termination [13,14], while in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and Huntington's disease, chronic ISR hyperactivation contributes to neuronal dysfunction [[15], [16], [17]]. Pharmacological modulators and inhibitors of the ISR have been investigated to gain insight into the physiological and pathophysiological role of the ISR [2]. Understanding the effects of ISR modulators and inhibitors may aid the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and clinical drug development [18].

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) have been investigated as a promising biological matrix for biomarker measurement [19]. They can be obtained easily and minimally invasively from peripheral blood samples, making them highly accessible for both preclinical models and clinical studies. They are well-suited for ex vivo culture, which allows for controlled pharmacological manipulations such as treatment with ISR inducers or inhibitors [20,21]. To explore the molecular changes within PBMCs, advanced RNA analysis techniques are essential. Among these methods, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) - a next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology - offers comprehensive whole transcriptome identification with high resolution [22]. RNA-Seq profiling has provided insight into PBMC transcriptome changes in various medical conditions or after different pharmacological treatments [[23], [24], [25]]. However, to the best of our knowledge, a comprehensive transcriptomic study investigating changes in PBMCs following activation and inhibition of the ISR has not been published to date.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore transcriptional changes driven by ER stress induction and concomitant pharmacological manipulation either by PERK inhibition or eIF2α activation in the entire transcriptome of PBMCs using RNA-Seq. The study also aimed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the underlying biological processes utilizing pathway enrichment analysis. In the context of UPR/ISR activation, the efficacy of GSK2606414 and ISRIB was examined to evaluate the reversibility of UPR/ISR-driven transcriptional changes.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human PBMCs, derived from a single, healthy Caucasian donor, were purchased from HemaCare/Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA). PBMCs were thawed and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with GlutaMAX™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Pharmacological treatments modulating the ISR

PBMCs were treated with an ISR inducer and/or ISR inhibitors after they had been cultured for at least 24 h. Tunicamycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), which is a naturally occurring mixture of nucleoside antibiotics obtained from various Streptomyces species, was employed as an UPR/ISR inducer [26,27]. Treatment with 2 μg/mL tunicamycin for 24 h was selected, as preliminary experiments demonstrated that, under these conditions, the ISR was robustly activated, without affecting PBMC viability. In preliminary experiments, two ISR inhibitors were examined for their potential to inhibit tunicamycin's effects on ISR markers in PBMCs: GSK2606414 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and ISRIB (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). GSK2606414 (hereafter referred to as GSK), which is a potent PERK inhibitor [28], demonstrated the most pronounced ISR inhibitory effects, and a 2.5 μM concentration was selected for further experiments. ISRIB, which enhances the activity of eIF2B [15], reduced the expression of a subset of ISR markers, and a concentration of 1 μM was chosen. The following six treatment conditions of PBMCs were included in the study: control, 2 μg/mL tunicamycin, 2 μg/mL tunicamycin +2.5 μM GSK, 2 μg/mL tunicamycin +1 μM ISRIB, 2.5 μM GSK, and 1 μM ISRIB. Cells were harvested after 24 h of incubation. Cell pellets and medium were separated by centrifugation at 600 × g for 10 min at room temperature and were cryopreserved until downstream analysis.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from PBMC samples using the MagMAX™ mirVana Total RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on the KingFisher Apex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA integrity, size, and quantity were assessed with RNA ScreenTape (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Agilent TapeStation Controller Software of the 4200 Agilent TapeStation system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

RNA-seq and differential gene expression analysis

Six biological replicates were subjected to RNA-Seq for each of the six treatment conditions. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, mRNA was isolated from total RNA, second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed, library fragments were generated, cDNA fragments exceeding 150–200 bp were purified, and double-stranded cDNA templates were amplified on the SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Following clean-up with magnetic beads, the final library concentration and quality were assessed with the Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Qubit dsDNA HR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Next, libraries were pooled, denatured using 0.2 N NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich) and diluted to a final loading concentration of 1.7 pM. RNA-Seq was carried out on the two-channel, fluorescence-based Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using sequencing by synthesis technology.

Primary analysis was performed using the integrated Illumina Real-Time Analysis v2 software, which filtered reads with low-quality scores. Subsequent analyses were performed using an AbbVie proprietary bioinformatic pipeline and a high-performance computing cluster. This includes the Trim-Galore tool [29] which was used to trim adapter sequences from the reads and to remove bad-quality reads and reads shorter than 18 nucleotides. Alignment of filtered and trimmed reads were conducted with STAR [30] against the human hg38 reference genome (GRCh38, homo sapiens) downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive. To assign aligned reads to genes FeatureCounts [31] was used. Genes expressed in fewer than two biological replicates per condition or with fewer than five assigned reads were excluded from further analysis. Raw counts were log-transformed and normalized using the trimmed mean of M values method [32]. Comprehensive quality control of all counts and alignments was performed with FastQC [33] and MultiQC software [34]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess the data structure and to identify patterns or trends among the samples.

Normalization was subsequently carried out using the counts per million method to standardize expression values by accounting for differences in sequencing depth (total read counts) across samples. Next, the data were subjected to a variance-stabilizing transformation utilizing the Voom method [35]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the gene expression count matrix and the Limma package [36].

Reverse transcription and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

cDNA synthesis was performed utilizing the SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master-Mix with ezDNase enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). qRT-PCR was carried out using the Quant Studio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Design and Analysis Software 2.6.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The reaction mixture for each sample contained 10 μL 2 × PCR buffer, 1 μL primer, 1 μL cDNA, and 8 μL nuclease-free water. The PCR reaction encompassed an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 1 s and 60 °C for 20 s. The housekeeping gene GAPDH served as an internal control. The 2^(-ΔΔCt) calculation method was used to compare transcription levels of target genes across different treatment conditions [37].

Pathway enrichment analysis

A pathway enrichment analysis was performed using an internal AbbVie application based on the clusterProfiler package (R package 4.2.2.). A gene list derived from the differential gene expression analysis was utilized as an input list. It was filtered based on a log2 fold change of ≥1 for genes upregulated by tunicamycin stimulation and ≤ −1 for downregulated genes and a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of <0.05. Visualization parameter thresholds were adjusted p-values of <0.05 and a q-value of <0.05. The minimum gene set size was set to 10.

The Gene Ontology (GO) Resource (version July 2023) (https://geneontology.org/) categories biological processes (GO-BPs), cellular components (GO-CCs), and molecular functions (GO-MFs) as well as the Reactome database (version 86, September 2023) (https://reactome.org/) were used in the current study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., California, USA). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis for multiple comparisons among replicates of the control and treatment groups was performed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey post-hoc test. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.005; ∗∗∗p < 0.0005).

Results

Evaluation of transcriptomic variability

Overall, 36 samples were analyzed with RNA-Seq, including six samples from each of the following groups: PBMCs treated with tunicamycin (2 μg/mL), PBMCs treated with tunicamycin (2 μg/mL) + ISRIB (1 μM), PBMCs treated with tunicamycin (2 μg/mL) + GSK (2.5 μM), PBMCs treated with ISRIB (1 μM), PBMCs treated with GSK (2.5 μM), and untreated PBMCs. The concentrations used for the different treatments were based on the findings of preliminary experiments. Among these 36 samples, 34 met the quality standards. Two biological replicates of PBMC + ISRIB-treated samples failed to match the quality requirements and were excluded from subsequent differential gene expression analysis. On average, 16,500–19,500 genes were identified across the different treatment conditions.

The discriminative power of gene expression patterns in differentially treated PBMCs was examined using PCA. The first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 42.03% and 14.70% of the total variance in gene expression across samples, respectively. Clusters of different treatment conditions were distinguished by PC1 and PC2, indicating considerable variability in gene expression patterns across treatment groups. Moreover, a high degree of consistency was observed within biological replicates, which are presented in tight clusters. The PCA analysis confirmed variance in gene expression of tunicamycin-treated cells compared to untreated cells, as they grouped separately. GSK-treated samples were shifted on the PC1 axis, indicating noticeable transcriptional changes in response to GSK. Comparing the positions of samples treated with tunicamycin + GSK with samples treated with tunicamycin + ISRIB relative to the position of tunicamycin-treated samples indicated that GSK caused more variations in the data set, whereas ISRIB had a small effect on gene expression patterns. Notably, GSK2606414 treatment alone caused observable transcriptional shifts distinct from untreated controls, whereas ISRIB-treated samples clustered closely with untreated controls. (Fig. 1A).

A hierarchical clustering heatmap of the 500 most variable genes revealed distinct clusters of genes and samples consistent with the experimental conditions. Notably, there were minimal variations in gene expression patterns within biological replicates, emphasizing the consistency and reliability of experimental data. Stark contrast in expression patterns was detected when comparing untreated and tunicamycin-treated PBMCs. Interestingly, tunicamycin + GSK partially counterbalanced the effect of tunicamycin, although it did not fully revert gene expression to the basal levels observed in the control group. Moreover, tunicamycin + GSK upregulated a cluster of genes that were not upregulated in either tunicamycin-treated or untreated PBMCs. Further, GSK treatment led to less pronounced gene activation of most genes than in untreated samples, suggesting that GSK may attenuate gene expression even in the absence of tunicamycin. Untreated and solely ISRIB-treated PBMCs clustered together, highlighting their similar expression patterns and suggesting that ISRIB might not alter gene expression in PBMCs. Likewise, the expression patterns of tunicamycin- and tunicamycin + ISRIB-treated samples aligned closely, suggesting that ISRIB might have limited efficacy in reversing tunicamycin-induced changes in gene regulation (Fig. 1B).

Differential gene expression

A comprehensive differential gene expression analysis for pairwise comparisons of the RNA-Seq data was carried out across the different treatment conditions. Upon tunicamycin treatment, 754 (4.80%), 1991 (12.67%), and 12,965 (82.53%) genes out of 15,709 annotated genes were significantly upregulated, downregulated, and unchanged, respectively, in comparison to control cells. Tenascin C (TNC) and Wnt family member 5A (WNT5A) were the most highly upregulated genes after tunicamycin treatment according to their log2 FC values. Notably, the ISR-associated genes DDIT3 and TRIB3 were among the significantly upregulated genes (Fig. 2A). A summary of the top upregulated genes in response to ER stress is presented in Table 1. Next, the ability of ISR inhibitors to counteract the effects of tunicamycin on gene expression was investigated. Genes that were upregulated by tunicamycin and suppressed when cells were treated with tunicamycin in combination with GSK included CLGN, SLC6A9, DMGDH, PSAT1, and DDIT3 (Fig. 2A). Overall, with the exception of ANKRD1, all of the top 11 genes upregulated by tunicamycin were downregulated by GSK (Fig. 2B). In cells co-treated with ISRIB and tunicamycin, only a small number of genes showed significant downregulation, whereas the transcription of the majority of genes remained unchanged compared with cells treated with tunicamycin alone (Fig. 2C).

Tunicamycin treatment resulted in significant upregulation of 754 genes. Concurrent treatment with the PERK inhibitor GSK reversed this response for 236 genes, whereas ISRIB co-treatment suppressed 19 genes (fold change <1, p < 0.05). GSK thus exhibited substantially more pronounced suppression than ISRIB, consistent with their mechanistically distinct modes of intervention. A total of 13 genes were downregulated by both GSK and ISRIB treatments. Additionally, 9 genes were suppressed by both inhibitors without prior upregulation by tunicamycin (Fig. 3A).

Validation of genes of interest

A subset of nine of the most pronouncedly upregulated genes in tunicamycin-induced cells compared to untreated cells, as well as the ISR-associated genes TRIB3, ATF4, DDIT3, and GDF15, were validated using qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR-derived expression of the 13 genes was consistent with the results of the RNA-Seq experiment. Specifically, TNC, WNT5A, and CLGN, which were most markedly upregulated in the RNA-Seq dataset, demonstrated upregulation with log2 fold changes ≈8 in qRT-PCR. ANKRD1, SLC6A9, ASS1, NDUFA4L2, PSAT1, and CHAC1 exhibited qRT-PCR log2 fold changes in the range 4.5–6.5. The ISR-related genes TRIB3, GDF15, DDIT3, and ATF4 demonstrated relatively modest qRT-PCR log2 fold changes of 2.3, 3.3, 1, and 0.6, respectively (Fig. 4).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Biological processes, cellular locations, and molecular pathways enriched in genes that were expressed differentially after tunicamycin treatment were identified using a pathway enrichment analysis. Most of the top enriched GO-BP pathways were closely associated with ER stress mitigation, handling of unfolded or misfolded proteins, and amino acid metabolic processes. The top three enriched GO-BP terms were “Response to endoplasmic reticulum stress”, “Response to topologically incorrect protein” and “Response to unfolded protein” (Supplementary Table 2). The results of GO-CC pathway enrichment highlighted the association of differentially expressed genes with cellular components, including the ER lumen, ER chaperone complexes, DNA packaging complexes, and nucleosomes. The top three enriched GO-CC terms were “Endoplasmic reticulum lumen”, “DNA packaging complex”, and “Endoplasmic reticulum chaperone complex” (Supplementary Table 3). Further, GO-MF (Supplementary Table 4) and Reactome (Supplementary Table 5) pathway enrichment analyses corroborated the involvement of pathways related to ER stress and the UPR. In addition, an overrepresentation of pathways related to tRNA aminoacylation, protein heterodimerization activity, and ligase activity was also detected. The top three enriched GO-MF terms were “Aminoacyl-tRNA ligase activity”, “Ligase activity, forming carbon-oxygen bonds tRNA binding” and “Ligase activity” (Supplementary Table 4). The top three enriched terms in the Reactome database analysis were “Unfolded protein response (UPR)”, “Response of EIF2AK1 (HRI) to heme deficiency” and “PERK regulates gene expression” (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of this transcriptome study provided insights into the specific gene regulatory program initiated upon chronic ER stress activation and/or concomitant PERK inhibition or eIF2B activation in PBMCs. Pathways enriched after tunicamycin treatment included those related to ER stress, unfolded proteins, the ER chaperone complex, amino acid synthesis and interconversion, and tRNA aminoacylation for protein synthesis.

RNA-Seq followed by differential gene expression analysis revealed that chronic ER stress induction via tunicamycin treatment in PBMCs resulted in significant transcriptional remodeling, with 17.47% of genes exhibiting altered transcription (12.67% downregulated, 4.80% upregulated).

It is important to recognize that tunicamycin, as an ER-stress inducer, broadly initiates the UPR, engaging all three major signaling branches: PERK, ATF6, and IRE1. The use of tunicamycin in this study was predicated on its well-documented capacity to robustly activate the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 axis, considered central to ISR signaling, as supported by numerous fundamental studies [[38], [39], [40]]. To ensure that our analysis remained focused on ISR-mediated transcriptional responses, we evaluated canonical ISR markers (ATF4, DDIT3, TRIB3, GADD34, ASNS) and employed ISRIB, a selective ISR inhibitor and GSK, a potent PERK inhibitor, to delineate ISR-specific contributions. Our results confirm substantial ISR involvement in the observed transcriptomic alterations. Nevertheless, complete dissociation of ISR signaling from other UPR branches is not experimentally feasible with tunicamycin treatment. Consequently, transcriptional overlap between the ISR and the ATF6 or IRE1 pathways cannot be definitively excluded.

ATF4 regulation occurs predominantly at the translational level

Interestingly, although ATF4 is recognized as a central transcriptional regulator of the ER stress response, our analysis revealed that ATF4 mRNA levels did not exhibit significant upregulation following tunicamycin treatment and were not reduced by concomitant GSK or ISRIB treatment. This observation indicates that ATF4 regulation predominantly occurs at the translational rather than transcriptional level, consistent with the established mechanism whereby ATF4 protein synthesis is selectively enhanced during ISR through uORF-mediated translation control. In this mechanism, the inhibitory uORFs in the ATF4 5′ untranslated region are bypassed under conditions of reduced ternary complex availability [41]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that ISR activation robustly increases ATF4 protein abundance without concomitant elevation of ATF4 mRNA levels, underscoring the primacy of translational control mechanisms within the ISR regulatory architecture [[42], [43], [44]]. Consistent with this, our data demonstrated substantial upregulation of ATF4 target genes, including those involved in amino acid metabolism and serine biosynthesis (ATF3, ASNS, TRIB3, PSAT1, PHGDH), antioxidant and glutathione metabolism (CHAC1), apoptotic regulation (DDIT3), ER chaperone function (HSPA5), stress-responsive growth factors (GDF15), and ISR feedback regulators (PPP1R15A), confirming functional ATF4 pathway activation despite unchanged ATF4 mRNA levels [45].

Novel ISR-responsive genes: TNC and WNT5A

Notably, TNC and WNT5A emerged as the most significantly upregulated genes in response to ISR activation, despite neither previously being associated with ER stress. WNT5A encodes a secreted glycoprotein involved in tissue homeostasis [46], whereas TNC encodes an extracellular matrix protein linked to tissue repair and chronic inflammation [47]. Intriguingly, TNC has been identified as a potential plasma biomarker for neurodegenerative diseases, with elevated levels in peripheral blood from patients with Alzheimer's disease [48]. Considering the connection between neurodegenerative diseases and ISR, future studies are required to investigate TNC in the context of ISR signaling. While the interplay of WNT5A and TNC in PBMCs is currently unclear, WNT5A has been shown to significantly increase TNC expression in osteoblasts, suggesting a potential mechanistic link that requires further exploration [49]. From a translational medicine perspective, these highly transcribed genes have the potential to serve as peripheral biomarkers for monitoring ISR activity and assessing disease progression, therapeutic responses, and pharmacodynamic readouts for ISR-modulating interventions. Nonetheless, several confounding variables may influence their expression in PBMCs, including inter-individual genetic heterogeneity and epigenetic variation, underlying comorbidities, immune cell composition, and concomitant medical interventions. Furthermore, while our transcriptomic data establish a correlative relationship between ISR activation and TNC/WNT5A upregulation, the functional significance of these changes remains unresolved. Key unaddressed questions include whether TNC and WNT5A proteins are efficiently translated and secreted during ISR or remain uncoupled from mRNA levels due to translational repression, whether their induction is mediated directly via canonical ISR transcription factors (ATF4, CHOP, ATF3) or through secondary pathways (NF-κB, STAT, AP-1), whether WNT5A and TNC signaling modulates ISR outcomes in PBMCs and whether suppressing TNC or WNT5A expression alters stress tolerance, apoptosis, or inflammatory responses in disease models. Addressing these questions will require integrative approaches combining transcriptomics, proteomics, functional genomics, and in vivo modeling to establish causal relationships between ISR activation and TNC/WNT5A regulation.

Comparative efficacy of PERK inhibition vs. eIF2B modulation

Two mechanistically distinct modulators were employed to elucidate PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway contributions to the tunicamycin-induced transcriptional response. Activation of PERK via ER stress induces the activation of the transcription factor NF-E2-related factor-2 (Nrf2) which attenuates the translation of most cellular proteins. However, this also increases the transcription of Nrf2 target genes [50]. GSK is a potent PERK inhibitor that binds to the ATP-binding site of PERK and effectively blocks its autophosphorylation [28]. This inhibits downstream signaling events and restores global translation rates to approximately 90% of control levels in vitro. In addition, PERK has been shown to interact with filamin A, thereby eliciting a remodeling of F-actin through a UPR-independent mechanism [51,52]. This interaction, which is crucial for the regulation of ER-plasma membrane contact formation, may also influence the effect of PERK on ER stress.

ISRIB acts downstream of eIF2α by enhancing eIF2B activity through stabilization of the eIF2B decamer, but achieves only partial translational restoration, reaching approximately 50% of control levels when used at its saturation concentration of 1 μM [53]. Since ISRIB targets eIF2α, it specifically inhibits the ISR. However, ISRIB efficacy is constrained by eIF2α phosphorylation levels. It effectively inhibits ISR when eIF2α phosphorylation remains below 45–70% of maximal levels, but its inhibitory capacity decreases substantially when this threshold is exceeded [54]. In the current study, GSK suppressed 236 of the 754 tunicamycin-upregulated genes, whereas ISRIB suppressed only 19 genes. This difference in inhibitory efficacy reflects both their mechanistically distinct modes of action and the intensity of ER stress induction under our experimental conditions. Tunicamycin treatment at 2 μg/mL for 24 h induced robust and prolonged ER stress, likely driving eIF2α phosphorylation levels beyond the critical threshold reported by Rabouw et al., thereby substantially limiting ISRIB efficacy. In contrast, GSK, which acts upstream by preventing PERK autophosphorylation altogether, retained substantial inhibitory activity even under these conditions of intense ISR activation. These findings demonstrate that the extent of eIF2α phosphorylation, determined by the magnitude and duration of stress, critically determines the relative efficacy of upstream versus downstream ISR inhibitors.

Although kinase selectivity profiling by Axten et al. [11] demonstrated that GSK exhibits high selectivity for PERK, with the second-most sensitive kinase being 385-fold less potent (IC50 = 154 nM for c-KIT versus IC50 = 0.4 nM for PERK), off-target kinase inhibition cannot be excluded at the 2.5 μM concentration employed in this study. This broader kinase engagement may contribute to the pleiotropic transcriptional effects observed in PCA analysis in GSK-treated samples in the absence of tunicamycin-induced ER stress. These findings suggest that the transcriptional signature of GSK reflects both PERK-dependent ISR modulation and compound-specific off-target activity, necessitating careful interpretation when distinguishing ISR-mediated from ISR-independent gene expression changes.

Notably, ISRIB and GSK have both been found to prevent neurodegeneration in murine models [55,56]. However, GSK exhibits strong toxicity and is therefore not considered a drug candidate [56,57]. ISRIB itself is not sufficiently soluble for clinical administration, but ISRIB derivatives are currently undergoing clinical trials for neurological indications [58,59].

Pathway enrichment analysis: coordinated UPR and ISR activation

To gain comprehensive insight into the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions associated with tunicamycin-induced UPR/ISR activation in PBMCs, we conducted a pathway enrichment analysis using the GO and Reactome databases. As expected, the pathway enrichment analysis revealed that tunicamycin treatment predominantly enriched pathways related to ER stress and protein misfolding. Importantly, both the IRE1-and ATF6-mediated UPR pathways as well as the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 branch, which functions within both the ISR and UPR, were markedly enriched. These results underscore the parallel activation of ISR and UPR branches, highlighting coordinated roles of the PERK, ATF6, and IRE1α pathways in regulating cellular responses to ER stress [60]. This coordinated activation complicates the attribution of specific transcriptional changes solely to the ISR (PERK/eIF2α/ATF4) versus other UPR branches, as discussed below in the limitations section. However, the suppression of the two most highly tunicamycin-regulated genes WNT5A and TNC by ISRIB suggests that these genes are involved in the ISR.

The GO term “endoplasmic reticulum chaperone complex” was also enriched after UPR/ISR activation. This complex plays a pivotal role in the folding and assembly of proteins within the ER lumen and includes the enriched heat shock protein (HSP)-encoding genes HSP90B1, HSPA5, and HYOU1 as well as the heat shock client gene SDF2. HSPA5 encoding the ER chaperone BiP is a central regulator of ER homeostasis responsible for the recognition and processing of misfolded proteins [61]. Furthermore, chaperones are closely related to the “endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation pathway,” which was also enriched in our dataset and is pivotal for eliminating irreversibly misfolded proteins from the ER [62]. The enrichment of ERAD and chaperone-related genes reflects the cellular attempt to restore ER proteostasis through enhanced protein folding capacity and clearance of terminally misfolded proteins, representing a canonical adaptive response to ER stress.

Overrepresentation of genes encoding proteins involved in amino acid metabolism processes and increased transcription of genes encoding enzymes involved in amino acid synthesis and interconversion were detected. The GO term “tRNA aminoacylation for protein synthesis” was also enriched after tunicamycin treatment, with genes encoding different cytoplasmic-localized aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases being upregulated. This enrichment is consistent with ATF4-mediated transcriptional upregulation of amino acid biosynthetic and transport genes, which serves to replenish depleted amino acid pools under conditions of reduced global translation and increased demand for stress-response proteins. The coordinate upregulation of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases further suggests a compensatory mechanism to maintain translational fidelity and capacity despite reduced ternary complex availability.

Study limitations and future directions

This study provides a first comprehensive overview of transcriptomic alterations in PBMCs following UPR/ISR modulation. While these findings substantially advance our understanding of stress-induced transcriptional programs, cellular phenotypic outcomes are ultimately governed by regulatory mechanisms operating at post-transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels. Since the ISR primarily regulates translation via eIF2α phosphorylation rather than transcription, our transcriptome-centric approach captures only one dimension of ISR-mediated cellular regulation. Future studies integrating ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq) and quantitative proteomics are essential to comprehensively assess ISR effects in PBMCs. Ribo-seq will enable direct quantification of translational efficiency, distinguishing translational upregulation from transcriptional upregulation and identifying transcripts subject to translational repression despite stable mRNA levels. Phosphoproteomic analysis would further elucidate eIF2α phosphorylation kinetics, PERK activation, and downstream signaling cascades, complementing our transcriptomic findings.

The selected conditions for ER stress induction ensured sufficient activation of the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 axis and downstream transcriptional responses, as confirmed by the upregulation of canonical ISR target genes including DDIT3, TRIB3, and GADD34. However, it is recognized that transcriptomic responses to UPR/ISR modulation are dynamic and dose-dependent. Alternative concentrations or time points may reveal distinct temporal phases of the stress response, early adaptive versus late maladaptive signatures, or differential sensitivity of specific gene sets. For instance, shorter exposure durations may capture immediate-early stress genes, whereas prolonged treatments could reflect sustained remodeling or cell fate decisions such as apoptosis. Similarly, lower or higher doses of tunicamycin and UPR/ISR modulators may influence the magnitude and specificity of transcriptional changes. Future studies employing dose-response curves and time-course analyses will be valuable to map the kinetics and dose-dependence of UPR/ISR-driven transcriptomic alterations in PBMCs, thereby refining the understanding of ISR regulation and improving the translational relevance of these findings.

Furthermore, future investigations would benefit from the use of selective ISR activators that target individual eIF2α kinases, thereby enabling more precise resolution of ISR-specific gene expression changes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our transcriptome study, employing differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analyses, yielded comprehensive insights into the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions impacted by chronic UPR/ISR activation in human PBMCs. The PERK inhibitor GSK and the eIF2B activator ISRIB partially inhibited ER stress-related gene expression, with a considerably stronger impact of GSK than of ISRIB. The two most robustly upregulated genes following tunicamycin treatment, WNT5A and TNC, represent promising candidates as peripheral markers for ISR activation. Nevertheless, the implementation of WNT5A and TNC as clinical biomarkers demands extensive validation across larger and more diverse patient cohorts, as well as careful evaluation of potential confounding factors that may influence their gene expression in PBMCs.

To comprehensively assess the effects of UPR/ISR activation on translation in PBMCs, future studies integrating ribosome profiling and quantitative proteomics are required. These approaches will enable the distinction between transcriptional changes and translational reprogramming, and the validation of the functional significance of WNT5A and TNC upregulation.
