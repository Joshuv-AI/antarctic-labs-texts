Fast, Accurate Numerical Evaluation of Incomplete Planck Integrals
Whit Lewis and Ryan G. McClarren

Abstract
Methods for computing the integral of the Planck blackbody function over a finite spectral range, the so-called incomplete Planck integral, are necessary to perform multigroup radiative transfer calculations. We present a comparison, in terms of speed and accuracy, of a wide array of approaches to numerically evaluating these integrals. Our results indicate that a direct rational polynomial approximation to these integrals has the best combination of accuracy and efficiency. We also present for the first time a derivation of the polylogarithm form of these integrals and show that modern approaches to polylogarithm evaluation are suitable for numerically evaluating incomplete Planck integrals. This article is dedicated to Prof. B.D. Ganapol, the Transport Cowboy, on the occasion of his retirement.

Introduction

Radiative transfer calculations are important in a variety of physics applications. These include the modeling of inertial confinement fusion experiments
[
1
,
2
]
and other high energy density phenomena
[
3
,
4
]
. Benchmark solutions for several multifrequency radiative transfer problems have also been given by Ganapol (e.g.,
[
5
]
) and others
[
6
]
.

In such high energy density radiative transfer calculations the linear transport equation for photons is coupled to a material energy equation through the absorption and emission of light. In local thermodynamic equilibrium, the emission of light is proportional to the Planck distribution,
B(\nu,T)
, a function of the medium temperature
T
and the light frequency
\nu
.
In these calculations often one must use a model of radiative transfer that accounts for the different photon energies. A popular approach to doing this is the multigroup method where one solves for integrals of the radiation intensity over energy.
In such a calculation, one must routinely compute integrals of the Planck function over a finite range of photon energies, also known as incomplete Planck integrals. These integrals are sometimes called “band emission” as well
[
7
]
.

The calculation of these integrals was treated in detail by Clark
[
8
]
. That work was first to the present, as a result from computer algebra software, that incomplete Planck integrals can be expressed as a combination of polylogarithm functions
[
9
]
. Clark then proceeds to use two approaches to calculate the incomplete Planck integral. The first uses a Taylor series representation of the integrand, and the other uses a power series representation of the polylogarithm. Each of these approaches has a limited range of validity and through an elegant analysis, Clark proposes a method that smoothly combines the two approaches to calculate Planck integrals.

In the intervening years there have been advances in calculating polylogarithm functions
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. These have the potential to change the conclusions of Clark as to which calculation approaches are most efficient and accurate.

In this paper we compare recent approximations to polylogarithms, previously reported algorithms, and a new piecewise-rational polynomial approximation. Our analysis considers both the accuracy of the methods and the computational cost. We also give the first derivation of the polylogarithm form of the incomplete Planck integral whereas previous work
[
8
,
13
]
relied on computer algebra software to realize this form
deus ex machina
.

The paper is laid out as follows. In Section
2
we detail the incomplete Planck integral that we are interested in calculating. We then give an overview of the theory of polylogarithms and their evaluation including their application to incomplete Planck integrals in Section
3
. Section
4
compares the speed and accuracy of different approaches to evaluating Planck integrals, followed by a conclusions section.

2
The Planck Function and Its Integrals

The Planck function,
B(\nu,T)
, gives the intensity (or radiance) at a frequency
\nu
of a blackbody at temperature
T
. This function is given by

B(\nu,T)=\frac{2h\nu^{3}}{c^{2}}\left(e^{h\nu/kT}-1\right)^{-1},

(1)

where
h
is the Planck constant,
k
is the Boltzmann constant, and
c
is the speed of light. The units of
B(\nu,T)
are energy per area per steradian per time per frequency. Commonly, in high energy density systems, the Planck function is written in terms of photon energy,
E_{\nu}=h\nu
. Because
B(\nu,T)
is a distribution in frequency, we can convert it to a function of
E_{\nu}
via the relation

B(\nu,T)\,d\nu=B(E_{\nu},T)\,dE_{\nu}.

(2)

Using
dE_{\nu}/d\nu=h
, we get

B(E_{\nu},T)=B(\nu,T)\,\frac{d\nu}{dE_{\nu}}=\frac{2E_{\nu}^{3}}{h^{3}c^{2}}\left(e^{E_{\nu}/kT}-1\right)^{-1}.

(3)

Another way to write the Planck function is to define a reduced-frequency variable,
x=h\nu/kT
. This makes
dx/d\nu=h/kT
and leads to

B(x,T)=\frac{2k^{4}T^{4}}{h^{3}c^{2}}\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1}.

(4)

In any of the above forms the integral of the Planck function over all positive frequencies/energies surprisingly has a simple closed form. To see this we first note that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1}\,dx=\frac{\pi^{4}}{15}.

(5)

This makes the integral of the Planck function

\int_{0}^{\infty}B(x,T)\,dx=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}}{\pi}T^{4},

(6)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant,

\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}=\frac{2\pi^{5}k^{4}}{15h^{3}c^{2}}\approx 0.102831\>\frac{\text{GJ}}{\text{cm}^{2}\cdot\text{ns}\cdot\text{keV}^{4}}.

(7)

The result of this integral can also be written in terms of the radiation constant,

a_{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{4\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}}{c}\approx 0.01372\>\frac{\text{GJ}}{\text{cm}^{3}\cdot\text{keV}^{4}},

(8)

as

\int_{0}^{\infty}B(x,T)\,dx=\frac{a_{\mathrm{r}}c}{4\pi}T^{4}.

(9)

The results in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) naturally lead to the definition of a normalized Planck function

b(x)=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\frac{x^{3}}{e^{x}-1},

(10)

so that

B(x,T)=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}}{\pi}T^{4}b(x).

(11)

2.1
Rosseland Function

We also note that the derivative of
B(\nu,T)
with respect to temperature is often needed for the linearization of the Planck spectrum and in diffusion approximations. This derivative, sometimes known as the Rosseland function, is written as

R(\nu,T)\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial T}B(\nu,T)=\frac{2h^{2}\nu^{4}e^{\frac{h\nu}{kT}}}{c^{2}kT^{2}\left(e^{\frac{h\nu}{kT}}-1\right)^{2}}.

(12)

Changing variables to
x
from
\nu
using the same procedure as above leads to

R(x,T)=\frac{4\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}}{\pi}T^{3}r(x),

(13)

where

r(x)=\frac{15}{4\pi^{4}}\frac{x^{4}e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{2}}.

(14)

As we will see, integrals of
r(x)
are related to integrals of
b(x)
.

2.2
Incomplete Planck Integrals

In many numerical methods for radiative transfer one needs to compute integrals of the Planck function over a finite range. This occurs in particular in the multigroup method where one divides the frequency range into a finite number of intervals and integrates the radiation intensity over those intervals
[
14
,
6
]
. That method requires computing integrals of the form

b_{g}\equiv\int_{x_{g}}^{x_{g+1}}b(x)\,dx=\Pi(b)-\Pi(a),

(15)

where

\Pi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}b(z)\,dz.

(16)

The values
x_{g}
and
x_{g+1}
are the reduced frequency values of the lower and upper bounds, respectively, of group
g
.

We should note that the function
\Pi(x)
is tabulated in many heat transfer texts. Nevertheless, it does have a closed form expression first noted by Clark
[
8
]
and later by Lampret, et al.
[
13
]
. Both of those works used computer algebra software to perform the integration. Below we present a straightforward derivation of this formula. To find that expression will require becoming acquainted with a family of special functions called the polylogarithm.

3
Polylogarithm Functions

Here we will sketch out the necessary theory of polylogarithms to compute the incomplete Planck integral,
\Pi(x)
. For a richer treatment of these functions, the reader should consult Lewin’s monograph
[
9
]
.

One definition of the logarithm is given by the integral

-\log(1-z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dt}{1-t}.

(17)

This function also has a series representation given by

-\log(1-z)=\frac{z}{1}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{z^{3}}{3}\dots,\qquad|z|\leq 1.

(18)

The idea behind the dilogarithm,
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)
, is to generalize these formulas. For the series representation we would make the power in the denominator 2 instead of 1:

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)=\frac{z}{1^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{2^{2}}+\frac{z^{3}}{3^{2}}+\dots,\qquad|z|\leq 1.

(19)

Similarly, if we take Eq. (
18
) and divide by z and integrate we get

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}dt\,\left(1+\frac{t}{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{3}\dots\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{z}{1^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{2^{2}}+\frac{z^{3}}{3^{2}}\dots\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z).

(20)

Note that while the series representation is only valid for
|z|\leq 1
, the integral can be defined for any value of
z
by introducing branch cuts, etc.

This gives us a simple way to define a general polylogarithm
\mathrm{Li}_{n}(z)
. We notice that the dilogarithm has the same series representation as
-\log(1-z)
with the power in the denominator of each term increased from 1 to 2. Changing the power to another value,
n
, defines the polylogarithm

\mathrm{Li}_{n}(z)=\frac{z}{1^{n}}+\frac{z^{2}}{2^{n}}+\frac{z^{3}}{3^{n}}+\dots,\qquad|z|\leq 1.

(21)

Note that
\mathrm{Li}_{1}(z)=-\log(1-z)
. As already stated, the
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)
function is known as a dilogarithm. The third-order polylogarithm is known as the trilogarithm. Though the fourth-order version does not have a name, we dub it the tetralogarithm (pronouncing that word aloud perhaps explains why it has not been previously named).

Using the same procedure as above (dividing by the argument and integrating) gives the integral definition of the polylogarithm:

\mathrm{Li}_{n}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{n-1}(t)}{t}\,dt.

(22)

From this definition we can show that
\mathrm{Li}_{0}(z)=z/(1-z)
, and continue to determine polylogarithms of negative order. Additionally, we notice that the derivative of a polylogarithm can be determined from Eq. (
22
) to be

\mathrm{Li}_{n}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{n-1}(z)}{z}.

(23)

(b)

z\rightarrow 1
.

3.1
Polylogarithms and Planck Integrals

The detail of the polylogarithm functions above was necessary to understand how these functions are related to Planck integrals. We obtain the same result as
[
8
]
, but have considerably simpler algebra by resorting to complex numbers at the outset.

Taking the definition of
\Pi(x)
from above and integrating once by parts we get

\displaystyle\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{z^{3}}{e^{z}-1}\,dz

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\left(x^{3}\log\left(1-e^{x}\right)-x^{4}-\int_{0}^{x}3z^{2}\left(\log\left(1-e^{z}\right)-z\right)\,dz\right).

(24)

We can use the relation that
\log(1-e^{x})=x+\log(1-e^{-x})+\pi i
, where
i=\sqrt{-1}
, to get

\displaystyle\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\left(x^{3}\left(\log(1-e^{-x})+\pi i\right)-\int_{0}^{x}3z^{2}\left(\log\left(1-e^{-z}\right)+\pi i\right)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\left(x^{3}\left(\log(1-e^{-x})\right)-\int_{0}^{x}3z^{2}\log\left(1-e^{-z}\right)\,dz\right).

(25)

To continue we note that a simple application of the chain rule and Eq. (
23
) leads to

\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{Li}_{n}(e^{-z})=-\mathrm{Li}_{n-1}(e^{-z}).

(26)

Therefore, we can apply integration by parts again to the integral in Eq. (
25
) to get

\int_{0}^{x}3z^{2}\log\left(1-e^{-z}\right)\,dz=3x^{2}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(e^{-x})-\int_{0}^{x}6z\mathrm{Li}_{2}(e^{-z})\,dz.

(27)

Finally, we apply integration by parts to the integral on the RHS of Eq. (
27
)
yielding

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}6z\mathrm{Li}_{2}(e^{-z})\,dz

\displaystyle=-6x\mathrm{Li}_{3}(e^{-x})+\int_{0}^{x}6\mathrm{Li}_{3}(e^{-z})\,dz

\displaystyle=-6x\mathrm{Li}_{3}(e^{-x})-6\mathrm{Li}_{4}(e^{-x})+\frac{\pi^{4}}{15}.

(28)

This last integral used
\mathrm{Li}_{4}(1)=\pi^{4}/90.

Combining Eqs. (
25
)-(
28
) gives

\Pi(x)=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\left(x^{3}\log(1-e^{-x})-3x^{2}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(e^{-x})-6x\mathrm{Li}_{3}(e^{-x})-6\mathrm{Li}_{4}(e^{-x})\right)+1.

(29)

This is the equation that we need to numerically evaluate. Next, we discuss the subtleties of evaluating polylogarithms.

3.1.1
Integral of Rosseland function

By a similar procedure we can integrate
r(z)
from
z=0
to
x
. The result is
\Pi(x)
plus an additional term. Following
[
8
]
, we define the partial integral as

\displaystyle\Upsilon(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{x}r(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\Pi(x)-\frac{15}{4\pi^{4}}\frac{x^{4}}{e^{x}-1}.

(30)

Therefore, any procedure we have for evaluating
\Pi(x)
can be used directly to evaluate
\Upsilon(x)
because the additional term contains integer powers of
x
and exponentials.

3.2
Evaluating polylogarithms

If we want to get numerical values for polylogarithms in the range
z\in[0,1]
, it seems natural to use the power series representation. However, as
z\rightarrow 1
we would expect the series to converge slowly, especially for the dilogarithm due to the fact that its terms decay the slowest as the series becomes the harmonic series at
z=1
. This is important for evaluating Planck integrals because computing
\Pi(x)
for
x
near zero, we will be evaluating polylogarithms of the form
\mathrm{Li}_{n}(e^{-x})\rightarrow\mathrm{Li}_{n}(1)
as
x\rightarrow 0
.

Fortunately, there is an identity for the dilogarithm that can be used to evaluate near
z=1
. This relation
[
9
, Eq. (1.11)]
is

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)=-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-z)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\log(1-z)\log z.

(31)

The idea advanced in
[
11
]
is to find a rational polynomial approximation to
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)
over the range
z\in[0,1/2]
and use Eq. (
31
) to evaluate
z\in(1/2,1]
. In
[
11
]
a degree
\{5,6\}
rational polynomial is used. The approximation has a maximum relative error of about
5\times 10^{-17}
. The coefficients for the rational polynomial approximation are given in
A.1

There are similar paths to evaluating the trilogarithm. We use the identity
[
9
, Eq. (6.10)]

\mathrm{Li}_{3}(z)=-\mathrm{Li}_{3}(1-1/z)-\mathrm{Li}_{3}(1-z)+\\
\zeta(3)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}}\log(z)-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(z)\log(1-z)+\frac{1}{6}\log^{3}(z),

(32)

where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function;
\zeta(3)\approx 1.202056903
. This relation allows us to evaluate
\mathrm{Li}_{3}(z)
for
z\in(1/2,1]
by evaluating a trilogarithm with a negative argument and a trilogarithm with argument in [0,1/2]. A rational polynomial approximation for each of these is given in
A.2
. The rational approximations are accurate to a similar level as that for the approximation to the dilogarithm.

Finally, we turn to the tetralogarithm. This function does not have as useful of a relation to shift the argument away from 1 as we have for the di- and trilogarithm. Nevertheless, the tetralogarithm function can be computed using 2 rational polynomial approximations
[
15
]
over the ranges
[0,1/2]
,
(1/2,8/10]
, and a 10-term power series for
(8/10,1]
. These approximations are given in
A.3
.

4
Rational Approximations to the Incomplete Planck Integral

As noted above, rational approximations to the individual polylogarithm functions have been successful in providing numerical values to double precision. However, to accomplish the calculation of
\Pi(x)
, inspection of Eq. (
29
) indicates we must evaluate at least one exponential, one logarithm, and three rational polynomials. The reason for the caveat of “at least” is due to use of polylogarithm identities in Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) to expand the domain of the rational approximations.

An alternative is to construct a rational approximation for
\Pi(x)
directly. Evaluating a rational polynomial would obviate the need to compute any exponentials or logarithms, potentially giving a large reduction in the evaluation time. Additionally, we can control the accuracy to match that of the direct evaluation of the polylogarithms discussed above.

Inspired by rational approximations to the polylogarithms, we break the function
\Pi(x)
up into 3 regions. The first region, from
x=0
to 1, we use a polynomial to estimate the function. This polynomial has powers of
x
from 3 to 12 and is not the Taylor series of
\Pi(x)
to ensure near-uniform error over the range while maintaining the property of the function going to 0 at
x=0
.

We then break the domain of
x>1
into two regions based on the behavior of
\Pi(x)/x
. That function, as shown in Figure
2
, will go to zero as
x\rightarrow\infty
and that behavior can be engineered into the rational polynomial. The function
\Pi(x)/x
also has a maximum,
x_{\mathrm{max}}
, at
x\approx 4.608345478
. This led us to break the domain above
x=1
into two regions at this maximum.

The resulting approximation to
\Pi(x)
is

\Pi_{\mathrm{rp}}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{n=3}^{12}\alpha_{n}x^{n}&0\leq x\leq 1\\
\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{14}p_{n}x^{n}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{15}q_{n}x^{n}}&1<x\leq x_{\mathrm{max}}\\
1+x\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{15}u_{n}x^{n}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{18}v_{n}x^{n}}&x_{\mathrm{max}}<x\end{cases}.

(33)

Note that when evaluating
\Pi(x_{g+1})-\Pi(x_{g})
as in Eq. (
15
), the
1
for large
x
cancels. In implementation, it is important to perform this cancellation algebraically to maintain precision as
\Pi(x)
approaches 1. Furthermore, we mention that in practice often the last group goes from
x=O(10)
to
x=\infty
. For this last group, we recommend that one use

\Pi(x_{G})-\Pi(x_{G-1})=1-\Pi(x_{G-1}),

(34)

to take advantage of the known value of
\Pi(\infty)
.

5
Other Approximations to the incomplete Planck integral

5.1
Combined Taylor/Power Series Approach

The paper by Clark
[
8
]
contains several approximations to
\Pi(x)
that can be numerically evaluated. The first of these uses a Taylor series approximation for
x
having a small magnitude, and evaluates the power series definition of the polylogarithms given in Eq. (
21
) for large values of
x
, and therefore small values of the argument
e^{-x}
of the polylogarithms.

To switch between the approximations, Clark uses the following formula:

\Pi_{\mathrm{C}}^{N,L}(x)=\mathrm{min}\left(\Pi_{L}(x),\tilde{\Pi}_{N}(x)\right).

(35)

Here
\Pi_{L}(x)
uses an
L

21

\Pi(x)
, and
\tilde{\Pi}_{N}(x)
is the
N
term Taylor expansion of
\Pi(x)
around
x=0
. This Taylor expansion is

\tilde{\Pi}_{N}(x)=\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{x^{3}}{3}-\frac{x^{4}}{8}+\frac{x^{5}}{60}-\frac{x^{7}}{5040}+\frac{x^{9}}{272160}-\right.\\
\left.\frac{x^{11}}{13305600}+\frac{x^{13}}{622702080}-\frac{691x^{15}}{19615115520000}+\dots+c_{N}x^{N}\right).

(36)

Note that in this approximation the number of terms is chosen to be
N=3,5,9,13,17,21,\dots
so that the truncation error in the Taylor series is positive (i.e.,
\tilde{\Pi}_{N}(x)
goes to positive infinity as
x
goes to infinity). This makes the selection of the minimum in Eq. (
35
) choose the approximation with the smaller error because
\Pi_{L}(z)<\Pi(z)
for
z>1
.

5.2
The “Cold War” approximations

Zimmerman at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
[
8
]
and Goldin at the Keldysh Institute of Applied Mathematics of USSR Academy of Sciences
[
16
]
each developed fast approximations in the 1980s for the incomplete Planck integral.

The Goldin approximation,
\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}
, is a simple, but effective approximation:

\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{15x^{3}}{\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{5}{312}x^{2}-\frac{1}{8}x+\frac{1}{3}\right)&x\leq 2\\
\\
\displaystyle 1-\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}e^{-x}\left(x^{3}+3x^{2}+6x+7.28\right)&x>2\end{cases}.

(37)

The maximum relative error in this approximation is 0.274% at
x\approx 1.383
.

The Zimmerman approximation,
\Pi_{\mathfrak{z}}
, is more complicated, using more terms to achieve higher accuracy. Also, the branch taken depends on the upper bound of the range of the definite integral in Eq. (
15
),
b
. This approximation is

\Pi_{\mathfrak{z}}(x)=\begin{cases}\tilde{\Pi}_{3}(x)&\text{if }b<10^{-3},\\
\displaystyle 1-\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{3}x^{3}+a_{4}x^{4}+a_{5}x^{5}}{1+b_{1}x+a_{5}x^{2}}e^{-x}&\text{if }b\geq 10^{-3},\end{cases}

(38)

where

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=6.493939402267,

(39)

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=8.317008834543,

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=5.570970415031,

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=2.161761553097,

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=0.5194172986679,

\displaystyle a_{5}

\displaystyle=-0.07713864107538,

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=0.2807339758744.

The maximum relative error in this approximation is 0.07822% at
x\approx 1.467

These two approximations both use a polynomial for low values, but the Goldin approach changes the coefficient for the

term in the Taylor series from

Additionally, the Goldin approximation for

in the integrand and changing the bounds:

\displaystyle\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=1-\int^{\infty}_{x}\frac{15}{\pi^{4}}\frac{z^{3}}{e^{z}-1}\,dz

The Goldin approximation changes the constant in the polynomial multiplied by

5.3
Quadrature-based Approximations

Rather than approximating the function
\Pi(x)
and then taking a difference to evaluate the incomplete Planck integral, a reasonable approach is to use quadrature rules directly on the integral of
b(x)
. Here we consider Gauss-Legendre quadrature rules shifted to the range of interest, i.e.,

\int_{a}^{b}b(x)\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}b(x_{n}),

(41)

where the weights,
w_{n}
, and abscissae,
x_{n}
, are computed from the standard Gauss-Legendre quadrature sets with weights and abscissae
\{\hat{w}_{n},z_{n}\}
via

x_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(z_{n}+1)(b-a)+a,\qquad w_{n}=\frac{\hat{w}_{n}}{2}(b-a).

(42)

We note that other types of quadrature would be possible, e.g., Romberg integration or tanh-sinh rules, but in our numerical experiments, Gauss-Legendre rules were superior in terms of accuracy per function evaluation.

When using quadrature rules, the accuracy of the calculation depends on the width of the range of integration. Therefore, the number of groups used in a radiative transfer calculation will determine the number of quadrature points used, and, therefore, the cost of computing the incomplete Planck integral. However, with more groups, as we will see, one can get a high level of accuracy with only a few quadrature points.

7
Conclusions

We have presented a wide array of methods for evaluating incomplete Planck integrals. From our results on the accuracy and speed of the different methods we remark

1.

The rational approximation we presented is the best combination of speed and accuracy of all the methods tested.

2.

Efficient, robust polylogarithm functions are available to directly evaluate the integral. This approach is faster and more accurate than the approximation
\Pi_{\mathrm{C}}^{21,10}
that was recommended for high accuracy evaluation in
[
8
]
.

3.

The Goldin and Zimmerman methods are the two fastest evaluation methods with sub-nanosecond evaluation times. The Zimmerman method, while slower, does give an extra digit of accuracy.

4.

Gauss-Legendre quadrature can be an efficient and accurate approach, but the appropriate number of quadrature points depends on the width of the intervals.

We believe that the results above give clear guideposts for evaluating incomplete Planck integrals. If speed is of paramount importance, the Zimmerman and Goldin approaches are good choices. If high accuracy is desired, the rational approximations that we presented give a high degree of accuracy and speed.

Further optimization of the calculations may be possible. For instance, we did not attempt to optimize the number of intervals in the rational approximations. For instance if, instead of 3 different approximations, we used 5 intervals, it is possible that the degree of the polynomials in the approximation could be reduced, decreasing computational cost. Furthermore, automatically choosing a number of quadrature points based on the width of the group could prove to be the most efficient approach to high accuracy. This can be seen in our results because, for small enough group widths, the 16 point Gauss-Legendre quadrature gets 14 digits of accuracy and is nearly twice as fast as the rational approximation. However, to guarantee a particular accuracy, more points may be needed when the group bounds are wide.

Finally, we mention that the Gauss-Legendre quadrature approach can also be beneficial when computing multigroup opacities with either the Planck or Rosseland weightings. For example, the Planck absorption opacity for group
g
is

\sigma_{\mathrm{a}g}^{\mathrm{P}}(T)=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\nu_{g-1}}^{\nu_{g}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\nu,T)B(\nu,T)\,d\nu}{\displaystyle\int_{\nu_{g-1}}^{\nu_{g}}B(\nu,T)\,d\nu}.

(43)

This function cannot be evaluated exactly, but the numerator can be evaluated using quadrature and the denominator using any of the methods discussed above. Similarly, for the Rosseland weighting,

\sigma_{\mathrm{a}g}^{\mathrm{R}}(T)=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\nu_{g-1}}^{\nu_{g}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}(\nu,T)\frac{\partial}{\partial T}B(\nu,T)\,d\nu}{\displaystyle\int_{\nu_{g-1}}^{\nu_{g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial T}B(\nu,T)\,d\nu},

(44)

the denominator is a difference of
\Upsilon(x_{g})-\Upsilon(x_{g-1})
and the numerator can be computed with Gauss-Legendre quadrature. Exploring the efficiency and accuracy of these approaches are natural follow-on work.
