Enhancing Autologous Fat Graft Retention: The Impact of Inactive Vitamin D3 on Immune Modulation and Extracellular Matrix Remodeling in a Swine Model
Authors: Rebecca Hohsfield, Amr Elmeanawy, Alexandra M Vagonis, Rachel E Ricketts, Andreea Gavrilescu, Yusuf Surucu, Wayne Vincent Nerone, Jose Antonio Arellano, Hamid Malekzadeh, Fuat Baris Bengur, Yadira Villalvazo, Shengyang Jin, Bahaa Shaaban, Shawn J Loder, Jeff Gusenoff, Lauren E Kokai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutologous fat grafting (AFG) is a widely utilized surgical technique, but graft survival rates remain inconsistent and unpredictable. The aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of oral vitamin D3 (VD3) on long-term AFG retention in a porcine model and examine its mechanisms. Inguinally harvested adipose tissue from 3 female Yucatán pigs was autologously grafted into 16 dorsal sites (5 cc per graft). Pig 1 received no treatment; Pig 2 received oral VD3 (100 K IU) thrice weekly postoperatively; Pig 3 received oral VD3 (100 K IU) thrice weekly for 2 weeks preoperatively and continuously postoperatively. Pigs were sacrificed at 3 months. Grafts were extracted and evaluated for histological, volumetric, and molecular changes. Hematoxylin and eosin, Sirius red, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and quantitative polymerase chain reaction tests were performed. VD3 improved AFG volume retention at 3 months (P = .01). VD3 enhanced adipocyte viability and vascular migration into the graft core, measured by perilipin+ (P = .003) and CD31+ (P = .003) IHC, respectively. VD3-treated grafts showed a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor-α expression compared with the control (P = .0005) and an increase in pro-regenerative M2 macrophages (P = .002). VD3 increased graft extracellular matrix (ECM) composition (P = .03) and Collagen III (P = .001). No significant differences were observed in fibrosis markers (transforming growth factor beta and Collagen I) or adiposity biomarkers (adipocyte count and adiponectin). In this pilot study, oral VD3 significantly increased fat graft retention, likely through modulation of inflammation, enhanced ECM remodeling, and improved vascular migration into the graft core. These findings support the potential of VD3 to improve AFG outcomes in large animal models. Autologous fat grafting (AFG) is a surgical procedure in which adipose tissue is harvested from a donor site and transferred to areas requiring volume restoration. Although historically utilized for aesthetic and reconstructive purposes, fat grafting has gained prominence across various surgical specialties, including orthopedic, oncologic, colorectal, otolaryngologic, neurologic, and trauma surgery. Despite its broad utility, graft viability and retention remain unpredictable. Clinical outcomes are often compromised by high resorption rates, fibrosis, and limited long-term volume maintenance.1,2 These limitations frequently necessitate multiple procedures, emphasizing the need for improved strategies to enhance graft survival.

One of the primary barriers to long-term fat graft retention is the nonvascularized nature of transplanted adipose tissue. Mature adipocytes are minimally adaptable and particularly vulnerable to hypoxic stressors.3 Numerous studies, including our own, have demonstrated rapid loss of adipocyte viability postengraftment, with particularly high levels of necrosis at the graft core. In contrast, adipocytes at the graft periphery, adjacent to vascularized host tissue, are more likely to survive. This highlights the critical role of rapid revascularization and the delivery of oxygen and nutrients in ensuring graft survival.3,4

In previous xenograft mouse models, we demonstrated that both active (calcitriol) and inactive (cholecalciferol) forms of vitamin D3 (VD3) significantly improved fat graft survival. These effects appeared to result from enhanced adipose stromal cell viability and mitigation of hypoxic stress. This led to more effective fat remodeling and regeneration following the initial period of ischemia and necrosis. Importantly, calcitriol and VD3 also significantly improved angiogenesis. This was mediated in part by increased endothelial migration and invasion.5,6

To build on these findings and assess clinical scalability, we conducted a follow-up study using a large animal model. Our primary aim was to determine whether oral administration of inactive VD3 to pigs would significantly improve long-term fat graft retention in a clinically relevant surgical model. Secondary objectives included assessing changes in graft health and viability. Based on our promising preliminary results in the xenograft model, we hypothesized that oral VD3 would enhance graft retention and survival.6

METHODS

Using a previously established swine AFG model, we evaluated retention and viability of clinically relevant fat volumes (5 cc per graft) at 16 dorsal sites per animal.7 We then compared retention and graft health outcomes between animals receiving oral VD3 and the untreated control. For graft preparation, inguinal adipose tissue was harvested and manually processed to achieve a consistency comparable to lipoaspirate using the Coleman technique. We employed the fanning-type injection method, which involves withdrawing the cannula while injecting, to ensure uniform distribution and integration of fat. Two strategies were compared: oral VD3 given postoperatively or both preoperatively and postoperatively. Ultimate retention was assessed over a 3-month period, a timeframe previously established as representative of long-term outcomes.8

This study was conducted in accordance with guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA) and received ethical approval (IACUC protocol no. 00022796). All procedures were performed in compliance with the National Institute of Health guidelines for ethical animal treatment. The pigs were provided with 12 h light and dark cycles, water ad libitum, and food per the Department of Laboratory Animal Research schedule according to their weights. They were socially housed apart from the acute postsurgical periods, when single housing was required to avoid wound contamination and ensure adequate wound healing.

Study Design and Surgical Model

Three female Yucatán pigs (Premier BioSource, formerly S&S Farms, Ramona, CA) were randomly assigned to 3 experimental groups: (1) control, (2) postsurgical VD3, and (3) pre- and postsurgical VD3. Fat-soluble VD3 (Zahler 50,000 IU capsules, GNC Item #: 582857) was given orally in lipid-rich foods to optimize absorption. Pig 1 received lipid-rich food without VD3 capsules, Pig 2 received oral VD3 (100 K IU) on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday for 3 months postoperatively, and Pig 3 received oral VD3 (100 K IU) on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday for 2 weeks before surgery and 3 months postoperatively.

The surgical model adhered to previously established protocols. All procedures, including selection of optimal adipose donor sites, development of recipient sites, tissue harvesting and processing, and fat grafting, were performed as previously described, with the exception that we utilized 5 cc injections instead of the previously stated 3 cc (Figure 1).7 Longitudinal ultrasound measurements for estimation of fat graft volume were not utilized because previous studies reported no correlation with actual graft volume measurements.7

Postoperative Care

Pain control following surgery was achieved with buprenorphine injections for 1 to 2 days and oral meloxicam for 5 to 7 days. Additional doses and/or longer treatment courses were provided as necessary, based on assessments by the surgical and veterinary teams.

Following surgery and after they had fully recovered, the pigs were transferred back to their original housing, with feeding resuming as soon as possible. They were maintained in single housing for 2 to 3 weeks to allow for proper wound closure. On postoperative Day 3, the animals were assessed for donor-site complications under general anesthesia, and surgical intervention was performed as necessary. The animals were monitored daily for the first week postengraftment and then twice weekly for the remainder of the study. They were assessed for general activity level, weight loss, changes in eating habits, wound healing complications, or signs of infection.

Outcome Analysis

Blood samples were obtained at the time of surgery and at 1, 2, and 3 months postengraftment. Blood samples were centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min, and serum samples were stored at −80°C until the completion of the study. Samples were analyzed for 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (25(OH)D3) by a clinical laboratory using high-performance liquid chromatography.

The pigs were sacrificed at 3 months by phenobarbital injection (IV, 120 mg/kg). The entire dorsum, down to the muscle interface, was surgically removed. Grafts were extracted and measured using liquid displacement in graduated cylinders or gas pycnometry (for samples 1 cc or less). The excised tissue was cut into 2 equal halves and either formalin-fixed or paraffin-embedded for histology or snap-frozen for genetic analysis. Samples embedded for histology were sectioned and visualized with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Immunohistochemical Assessment

CD31+ endothelial cells and perilipin+ adipocytes were semiquantified in tissue sections using IHC. Briefly, samples were fixed in 10% formalin for 24 to 28 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm (2 slides per sample). Antigen retrieval was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 30 min, followed by permeabilization and blocking with buffer containing bovine serum albumin (BSA), normal serum (same species as secondary antibody), cold water fish skin gelatin, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.05% Tween-20 for 2 h at room temperature.

Tissue sections were then incubated with either Perilipin 1 Polyclonal Antibody (PA5-72921) or CD31 Antibody (NB100-2284) at 5 µg/mL in 0.1% BSA at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody, goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G antibody (H + L), biotinylated (BA-1000-1.5) at a dilution of 1:2000 for 45 min at room temperature, followed by Cy5-conjugated streptavidin at room temperature for 1 h (NorthernLights Streptavidin NL637, Cat# NL998). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Quantification of viable adipocytes was performed by counting perilipin+ cells in representative 10× cross-sectional fields using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Endothelial cell density was evaluated as the proportion of CD31+ cells relative to total DAPI+ nuclei.

Quantitative Reverse Transcriptase Polymerase Chain Reaction

To isolate RNA, ∼25 mg of tissue was cut from frozen fat graft samples stored at −80°C. Samples were immediately transferred into clean 5 mL round-bottom tubes and weighed. RNA was then isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Cat# 74134) with the following modifications: tissue homogenization was performed in 1 mL of RLT Plus buffer (Bio-Gen Pro200), followed by serial mechanical lysis through progressively smaller gauge needles until the homogenate passed through a 25 G needle. Proteinase K (0.6 mg) and RNase-free water were added to reach a final volume of 2.8 mL. The solution was incubated in a 55°C water bath and then returned to an ice bath. Tissue debris was pelleted by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 6 min at room temperature. The cleared lysate (∼2.7 mL), free of oil and debris, was transferred to a new 5 mL tube, and RNA isolation was then performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The final RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using Nanodrop spectrophotometry and gel electrophoresis, respectively.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11766050), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to a control (non-VD3-treated) sample for each of the TaqMan primers listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Given the pilot nature of this study with 1 animal per treatment condition, statistical analyses were performed in an exploratory and descriptive manner. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. Data distribution was assessed using normality testing. Because of the small sample size, formal inferential statistical comparisons at the animal level were limited, and nonparametric methods (Kruskal–Wallis test) were applied to graft-level summary data for exploratory group comparisons.

For image-based analyses involving large numbers of individual measurements (eg, adipocyte diameter), the biological unit of analysis was defined at the slide level to avoid pseudoreplication. Individual measurements were therefore summarized within each slide, and slide-level means were used for group-level comparisons. Adipocyte diameter values are reported in relative units because of the absence of spatial calibration at the time of image acquisition.

Statistical significance was defined as P < .05, and exact P-values are reported to 2 decimal places where applicable. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

RESULTS

Surgical Outcomes

All animals tolerated the procedure and follow-up period without serious adverse events. Daily postoperative monitoring revealed no signs of distress or infection at the incision sites. One pig (control) developed loosened sutures, which resolved without complication or intervention. Minor issues, such as dryness and irritation of the dorsal skin, were noted across subjects and alleviated with biweekly application of an oatmeal-based lotion.

Successful Administration of VD3 in Treatment Groups

Serum 25(OH)D3 levels were measured at the time of surgery and at 1, 2, and 3 months postgrafting to confirm successful administration of VD3 in treatment groups. All VD3-treated pigs achieved and maintained significant serum elevation compared with the control. Pig 1 (control) maintained low, stable levels throughout the study (15 ng/mL at surgery, peaking at 52 ng/mL at 2 months, then declining to 19 ng/mL; Figure 2). Pig 2, which received VD3 postoperatively, showed a marked elevation in serum levels by 1 month (329 ng/mL), which remained elevated through study completion (335 ng/mL at 2 months, 183 ng/mL at 3 months; Figure 2). Pig 3, which received VD3 both preoperatively and postoperatively, exhibited the highest initial level (305 ng/mL), reflecting presurgical loading. Levels remained elevated at all time points (166 ng/mL at 1 month, 182 ng/mL at 2 months, 224 ng/mL at 3 months; Figure 2).

VD3 Improved Long-Term Graft Volume Retention

Fat graft retention was significantly higher in the postsurgical VD3-treated animal compared with the untreated control animal (P = .01). Mean graft retention in the post-VD3 animal was 48.6% (SEM = 6.8%), compared with 23.2% (SEM = 5.1%) in the control animal (Figure 3A). Grafts from the pre/post-VD3 animal also demonstrated higher mean retention compared with the control (43.1%, SEM = 6.6%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .05; Figure 3A). This suggests that the timing of VD3 administration may influence the degree of graft retention achieved. The anatomic location of the grafts across dorsal sites did not significantly influence volume retention (Figure 3B).

Histologic Analysis Revealed Distinct Graft Remodeling Patterns

On histologic evaluation, all grafts demonstrated preserved adipose architecture with varying degrees of necrosis, oil cyst formation, and early collagen deposition. However, notable heterogeneity was observed both within and between groups. Control grafts typically exhibited broad central necrosis, accompanied by diffuse oil cysts and patchy collagen infiltration. Post-VD3 grafts showed greater preservation of tissue integrity and fewer, more scattered necrotic and cystic voids. Clusters of viable-appearing adipocytes were observed alongside interstitial collagen strands. Pre/post-VD3 grafts displayed a more heterogeneous architecture. Although viable adipose tissue was present, these grafts contained localized regions of cystic degeneration and potential calcification, interspersed with elongated, band-like collagen structures that segmented otherwise intact areas (Figure 4).

Adipocyte Number and Size Were Similar Across Vitamin D3 Treatment Conditions

ImageJ analysis using the AdipoSoft plug-in was performed to quantify adipocyte number and size at the graft level. Although the mean adipocyte count was lower in pre/post-VD3 grafts, inter-sample variability was high, and the overall difference between groups was not statistically significant (control: 35,707 ± 9329; post-VD3: 32,329 ± 11,569; pre/post-VD3: 22,080 ± 16,596; P = .15). Similarly, mean adipocyte diameter, reported in relative units, did not differ significantly across treatment conditions (control: 5.63 ± 0.61; post-VD3: 5.46 ± 0.21; pre/post-VD3: 5.10 ± 0.66; P = .31). Given the absence of spatial calibration and slide-level aggregation of measurements, adipocyte size data were interpreted descriptively and were not used to support inferential conclusions regarding treatment effects.

Vitamin D3 Increased Density of Viable Adipocytes

Viable adipocytes were quantified through immunohistochemical staining for perilipin, a lipid droplet-associated protein expressed in intact adipocytes. Semi-quantitative analysis of 10× cross-sectional images revealed a significantly greater number of perilipin+ cells in post-VD3 grafts (40.9 ± 21.1 cells/field) compared with control grafts (31.2 ± 24.3; P = .003; Figure 5A-D). Pre/post-VD3 grafts also exhibited higher mean perilipin+ cell counts relative to control grafts (39.1 ± 27.1); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .08; Figure 5A-D).

To complement histological quantification, we analyzed AdipoQ gene expression as a molecular measure for total adipocyte content using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Although VD3-treated grafts showed a trend toward increased AdipoQ expression (post-VD3 grafts: 3.4 ± 3.3; pre/post-VD3 grafts: 2.3 ± 2.0) compared with control grafts (0.4 ± 1.8), this difference was not statistically significant (P = .17; Figure 5E). The modest correlation between AdipoQ expression and perilipin staining may reflect differential sensitivity of these markers to metabolic or inflammatory cues within the graft microenvironment.

Vitamin D3 Enhanced Graft Extracellular Matrix Remodeling

Given the comparable adiposity across grafts on gross histological assessment, we next investigated structural tissue as a potential contributor to graft retention. Picro Sirius Red staining revealed a significantly greater extracellular matrix (ECM) area in pre/post-VD3 grafts (17.0% ± 8.0%) relative to control grafts (8.0 ± 3.7%; P = .03; Figure 6A). Post-VD3 grafts also demonstrated higher ECM area compared with control grafts (15.6 ± 6.7%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .07; Figure 6A). Correlation analysis between graft weight and ECM area revealed a weak, nonsignificant positive relationship (r = 0.4, P = .06; Figure 6C), suggesting that ECM remodeling may have supported, but did not solely account for, the improved graft retention.

To further characterize the nature of ECM remodeling, transcriptional markers of collagen expression and fibrosis were assessed. Expression of Collagen III (COL3A1) was significantly higher in both post-VD3 (2.1 ± 1.5) and pre/post-VD3 (1.6 ± 0.8) grafts relative to control grafts (−0.2 ± 1.6; P = .001 and P = .04, respectively; Figure 6D). No significant differences were observed in expression of Collagen I (COL1A1), IV (COL4A1), or VI (COL6A1) in VD3-treated grafts compared with control grafts (P > .05 for all; Figure 6E-G). Expression of transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), a key driver of pathological fibrosis, remained unchanged in VD3-treated grafts relative to control grafts (P > .999; Figure 6H), suggesting that ECM enrichment occurred in the absence of sustained fibrotic signaling.

Vitamin D3 Modulated Inflammation and Promoted Regenerative Immune Signaling

To assess the impact of VD3 on local immune activity, key inflammatory mediators and macrophage markers were analyzed using relative qPCR and IHC. Pro-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) was significantly downregulated in both post-VD3 (−11.8 ± 10.3) and pre/post-VD3 (−5.7 ± 1.9) grafts compared with control grafts (−1.7 ± 1.3; P = .0005 and P = .007, respectively; Figure 7A). Interestingly, expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) was also significantly reduced in post-VD3 grafts (−3.2 ± 1.3) relative to control grafts (−0.4 ± 1.4; P = .0008; Figure 7B).

DISCUSSION

The success of AFG is frequently hindered by high rates of resorption. This compromises both aesthetic and functional outcomes. A primary contributor to graft loss is inadequate blood perfusion following engraftment, resulting in ischemia-induced hypoxia.3,4 The resulting adipocyte necroptosis, a form of programmed cell death, triggers a destructive inflammatory response culminating in fat necrosis and fibrosis—both of which are detrimental to long-term graft retention.9 Various strategies, such as stem-cell enrichment and tissue preconditioning, have shown promise in improving fat graft retention.10 However, their broader clinical application is limited by high costs, regulatory barriers, patient burden, and inconclusive results. In previous work, we demonstrated that inactive VD3 is a mechanistically promising and cost-effective approach to improving fat graft survival in a xenograft model.6 Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate whether VD3 could improve fat graft retention in a clinically relevant pig model. We also sought to explore the underlying mechanisms driving this effect.

We found that administration of oral VD3 postoperatively significantly improved graft volume retention. A modest improvement was also observed in the pre/post-VD3 group. Across both treatment groups, we observed enhanced adipocyte viability, reduced inflammatory signaling, greater structural support through ECM remodeling, and increased vascular migration to the graft core. To better understand the biological foundation of these outcomes, we examined cellular fate, immune signatures, and structural remodeling.

The observed improvements in VD3-treated groups did not appear to result from increased adipocyte proliferation or replacement. Quantification of adipocyte number from raw imaging revealed no significant differences between groups. In fact, the pre/post-VD3 group actually trended lower. These findings suggest that the total quantity of adipocytes alone is not the primary determinant of graft success. Previous studies have shown that even decellularized adipose tissue scaffolds, devoid of viable adipocytes, can support graft regeneration. They achieve this by providing an inductive microenvironment that facilitates host cell infiltration, angiogenesis, and matrix remodeling.11,12 Rather than replacing damaged adipocytes, VD3's therapeutic benefit in this model may lie in influencing the initial fate of cells and the biochemical and structural consequences that follow.

The improvement in fat graft retention with postsurgical administration of VD3 may therefore stem from its ability to maintain functional integrity of existing adipocytes during the critical hypoxic window postengraftment. Our analysis revealed a significantly higher density of perilipin+ cells in post-VD3 grafts compared with control grafts. This suggests enhanced preservation of viable, metabolically active adipocytes.13 This finding aligns with previous studies on endothelial and skeletal muscle cells, demonstrating that VD3 supports cellular energy homeostasis. This may occur through the protection of mitochondrial function under metabolic stress.14-16 Similarly, in vitro work from our lab demonstrated that calcitriol significantly enhances the mitochondrial reserve capacity of stromal vascular fraction (SVF) cells under hypoxic conditions. This enables cells to respond more effectively to energy stressors without compromising their function or viability.5

However, this increase in perilipin+ cells was not paralleled by a significant rise in relative AdipoQ expression. This discrepancy may reflect the complex regulation of adiponectin, which is influenced not only by the number of adipocytes but also by local inflammation, metabolic activity, and systemic factors.17,18 Previous studies have shown that VD3 may promote adiponectin expression in certain contexts, such as in individuals with diabetes or obesity. However, findings have been inconsistent and appear to vary based on BMI, race, and other host characteristics.18,19 In contrast to perilipin, which serves as a structural marker of adipocyte integrity, AdipoQ is a functional adipokine whose expression may be suppressed or altered despite preservation of cellular viability.20 These findings suggest that adipocyte preservation may precede or occur independently of functional adipokine secretion in the local graft environment.

Our findings in the pre/post-VD3 group highlight how treatment timing influences VD3's effects on mitochondrial function and cell fate. Both VD3-treated groups showed improved markers of cellular viability and regenerative remodeling. However, the effect was notably diminished in grafts that were preexposed to VD3 before surgery. This pattern likely reflects a calcium-driven mitochondrial vulnerability induced when high-dose VD3 is present before the onset of ischemia.21-23 VD3 and its metabolites are known to transiently increase cytosolic calcium levels. These levels are typically restored by ATP-dependent pumps in healthy cells.21,24 Under hypoxic conditions, however—when ATP is scarce—cells must rely on mitochondrial calcium buffering mechanisms. This leads to mitochondrial depolarization, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.23

Previous in vitro studies from our lab support this model: SVF cells pretreated with VD3 exhibited significantly reduced mitochondrial polarization and an increased apoptosis-to-viability ratio following 6 h of hypoxia, as confirmed by elevated caspase activity.6 Interestingly, although both pretreated and untreated cells showed reduced viability at this time point, only the pretreated group demonstrated increased apoptosis. This suggests that pretreatment with VD3 sensitized cells to early apoptotic loss. Although this could limit long-term cell survival, it may still confer benefit by shifting the mode of cell death toward apoptosis, a more immunologically silent process.25,26

A key mechanism through which these divergent effects—pro-survival after postsurgical exposure and pro-apoptotic after presurgical exposure—may confer graft protection is by limiting necroptosis. Necroptosis is a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death triggered by ischemic injury and oxidative stress.9,27 In freshly grafted adipose tissue, hypoxia-induced energy depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction lead to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species. These trigger the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). TNF-α, a well-established DAMP, is a potent inducer of necroptosis through the activation of receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3) and mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL).28,29 Once initiated, this pathway amplifies local inflammation, promotes additional cytokine expression, and perpetuates tissue injury beyond the initial ischemic insult.30

Thus, the improved graft outcomes observed in our VD3-treated groups—although to varying degrees—may reflect attenuation of this necroptotic cascade. In the postsurgical VD3 group, enhanced mitochondrial resilience may have helped preserve membrane integrity, thereby limiting DAMP release, suppressing TNF-α-mediated necroptosis, and reducing immune-mediated tissue destruction.

In contrast, the addition of a presurgical VD3 treatment phase may have redirected cells toward calcium-induced apoptosis upon engraftment, thereby mitigating inflammation and preserving elements of regenerative signaling despite greater early cell loss. However, these benefits appeared to come at a cost, as evidenced by the mosaic tissue response observed in the pre/post-VD3 grafts. Interspersed among otherwise preserved adipose architecture were localized zones of cystic degeneration, some with features suggestive of calcification. This pattern may reflect a complex interplay between early apoptosis and impaired apoptotic cell clearance because of VD3's immunomodulatory effects.31,32 In the absence of timely clearance, apoptotic cells can undergo secondary necrosis. This leads to the accumulation of saponified lipid remnants and the development of oil cysts with dystrophic calcification.33,34

The changes observed in the local inflammatory environment in VD3-treated grafts lend further support to these proposed mechanisms. We observed a marked reduction in TNF-α expression within the grafts, consistent with attenuation of pro-inflammatory signaling and necroptosis.35,36 Interestingly, IL-10 was also significantly decreased in the post-VD3 group. Although IL-10 is traditionally considered an anti-inflammatory cytokine, its role in tissue remodeling is both complex and context dependent.37 In myeloid cells, IL-10 expression is induced by upstream pro-inflammatory signals. This provides a compensatory response to ongoing tissue damage and inflammation.38 Additionally, IL-10 has been shown to suppress thermogenic gene expression in adipose tissue through chromatin remodeling, potentially limiting mitochondrial function and metabolic adaptability.39 Thus, the concurrent reduction in both TNF-α and IL-10 may reflect a balanced immune response and metabolically competent microenvironment rather than a blunt suppression of inflammation.

IHC analysis further revealed a significant increase in Arg1+ M2 macrophages in pre/post-VD3 grafts, with a more moderate increase observed in the post-VD3 group. Notably, this shift occurred without a corresponding increase in CCR7+ M1 macrophages. This suggests that VD3 did not broadly amplify macrophage infiltration but rather influenced polarization toward a reparative phenotype. Although the M1-to-M2 classification is a commonly used framework to distinguish between pro-inflammatory and pro-regenerative responses, its simplicity belies the functional complexity of these cells in adipose tissue. Some studies have linked persistent M2 activity to pathological fibrosis—particularly in necrotic regions where unabsorbed oil droplets remain.4,33 Others demonstrate that M2 polarization can promote angiogenesis and adipogenesis and improve graft integration.40,41 These divergent roles likely reflect differences in microenvironmental signals, tissue integrity, and the extent of inflammatory resolution.

A recent study further underscores the importance of M2 macrophages in ECM synthesis during graft remodeling. It demonstrated compromised matrix deposition and adipocyte survival following M2 depletion.42 The authors emphasize that ECM remodeling is a critical, yet often overlooked, determinant of long-term graft viability—beyond adipocyte survival alone. This provides important context for our findings, which show that VD3-treated grafts exhibited increased collagen deposition at 3 months, including a significant upregulation of Collagen III and a nonsignificant increase in Collagen I.

Although increased collagen is traditionally associated with fibrosis and poor graft outcomes, the broader context of our results suggests a more nuanced interpretation. The pronounced M2 macrophage enrichment and elevated Collagen III in pre/post-VD3 grafts may reflect an early-stage fibrotic response, particularly where clearance of larger oil droplets remained incomplete by the 3-month window assessed in this study. However, such a fibrotic trajectory would typically be accompanied by elevated levels of TGF-β, a central profibrotic cytokine that drives the activation of fibroblasts and the overproduction of ECM.43,44 Given that TGF-β expression remained low at this time point, the observed response may represent neither degenerative fibrosis nor successful remodeling, but rather an incomplete attempt to resolve injury and stabilize the graft.

Further, despite increased ECM content, post-VD3 grafts demonstrated improved viability and volume retention. Because pathological fibrosis is generally preceded by a sustained inflammatory response, we propose that VD3-mediated immune modulation supported constructive, rather than fibrotic, matrix remodeling—preserving structural integrity while facilitating cell survival, migration, and integration.45-47 This is further reinforced by selective alterations in collagen composition and organization. Collagen III, in particular, plays a key role in matrix organization, serving as a pliable scaffold that promotes neovascularization and limits wound contraction.48,49 In contrast, excessive Collagen VI—of which no significant increase was observed—has been associated with dysregulated ECM turnover and fibrotic remodeling.50-52 In post-VD3 grafts, thin, reticulated collagen is threaded between adipocytes. It appears as a supportive network within viable tissue rather than a compensatory replacement of necrotic voids. Even denser collagen bands displayed linearized alignment, offering mechanical support for vascular ingrowth and tissue cohesion without disrupting architecture.49,53 These findings indicate that it is not merely the presence of collagen but the magnitude and specificity of its deposition that dictate graft outcomes.54 Controlled tissue remodeling, marked by balanced immune signaling and selective collagen expression, appears to distinguish regenerative healing from fibrotic failure.

Previous studies have demonstrated that both Collagen III and M2 macrophages play a role in the formation of a supportive extracellular niche. This niche facilitates endothelial cell migration and arteriogenic remodeling.49 ,54 This mechanism is consistent with our findings. VD3-treated grafts exhibited increased M2 macrophages and Collagen III deposition, along with a greater presence of CD31+ endothelial cells within the graft core. Interestingly, these histological changes were not accompanied by a significantly elevated expression of angiogenic genes, such as PECAM-1 or MCAM, although MCAM did exhibit a modest upward trend. This discrepancy may reflect the temporal dynamics of vascular remodeling, where transcriptional activity peaks early in vessel formation, whereas later stages are characterized by stable protein expression as the new vasculature matures and integrates.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although each animal received 16 fat grafts, the total number of animals was small. This increased susceptibility to outlier effects and limited assessment of intragroup variability. This design should therefore be interpreted as a pilot investigation, providing preliminary but clinically relevant data to support future large-scale studies. Furthermore, our initial analytical focus on collagen-based ECM deposition limited the assessment of the broader ECM environment. Other ECM constituents, such as elastin, fibronectin, and matrix metalloproteinases, were not assessed. Thus, the full scope of VD3-mediated matrix remodeling remains to be characterized. Functional outcomes, such as graft vascular integration and biomechanical properties, were also not assessed.

Finally, the long-term safety of high-dose VD3 administration was not assessed beyond 3 months, and dose–response relationships were not systematically explored. Vitamin D toxicity and its clinical manifestation, severe hypercalcemia, are rare but serious considerations, typically associated with chronic intakes far exceeding recommended limits.55,56 Long-term supplementation studies suggest that relatively high daily intakes can be well tolerated, although isolated cases of toxicity have been reported with extreme dosing errors.56-58 In this swine model, supraphysiologic dosing produced elevated serum concentrations without overt adverse effects. Nevertheless, larger, longer-term studies will be critical to establish clinical durability, refine dosing protocols, and guide safe translation to human practice.

CONCLUSIONS

This pilot study demonstrates that inactive VD3 may enhance long-term fat graft retention in a clinically relevant large-animal model. Mechanistically, VD3 promotes conditions favorable for regenerative healing. It does so by enhancing adipocyte viability, modulating immune activity, supporting matrix remodeling, and facilitating neovascularization. Importantly, the timing of administration played a critical role. Postsurgical VD3 delivery produced the most robust improvements. The addition of a presurgical treatment phase appeared to sensitize cells to early apoptotic loss, resulting in a hybrid phenotype characterized by both regenerative activity and maladaptive remodeling. These findings underscore the importance of aligning therapeutic delivery with the evolving metabolic demands of engrafted tissue.
