Enhanced color stability in blueberry-grape blended fruit wines: Insight into synergistic copigmentation of epigallocatechin gallate and ferulic acid
Authors: Yu Wang, Wen-Bo Xia, Yue Zhu, Chen Bian, Xiao-Hui Yang, Xin-Yu Wang, Shuang Xu, Zheng-Guo Zhu, Zhi-Qing Xu, Xiu-Bing Lu, Hai-Wei Zhang, Xue-Ling Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The significant anthocyanin loss in blueberry wine can lead to color degradation during aging. This study showed that blending blueberries with ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes notably enhanced acylated anthocyanins and copigmentation levels, delaying the decrease in the copigment anthocyanin ratio and significantly increasing the polymerized anthocyanin ratio over time. A model blueberry wine system was applied to evaluate the copigmentation effects of primary copigments from grapes on anthocyanins. The thermodynamic parameter analysis and molecular docking showed that epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) exhibited superior copigmentation effects compared to other compounds. Additionally, the copigmentation effects of EGCG and ferulic acid (FA) together were more pronounced than those of EGCG alone, suggesting a synergistic enhancement of copigmentation. These findings provide valuable insights for the blueberry wine industry, indicating that blending techniques and the use of composite copigments can significantly enhance color stability.

Introduction

Blueberry wine is known for its high concentrations of organic acids, mineral elements, anthocyanins, and phenols, all of which contribute to its potent significant nutritional value, satisfying the growing demands of consumers for healthy drinking. The appearance of wine, particularly its color characteristics, plays a vital role in its sensory quality and significantly affects consumer preference and evaluation. However, the color degradation of blueberry wine during storage significantly affects its shelf life and economic value (Sun et al., 2022; Sun, Luo, et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024), thereby making the enhancement of color stability in blueberry wine a focal point of recent research (Li, Zhang, et al., 2020; Sun, Yan, et al., 2019).

Monomeric anthocyanins, which provide the red hue in freshly fermented fruit wines, undergo transformations during the aging period. This transformation involves addition or polymerization reactions that cause a shift in the maximum absorption wavelength of the anthocyanin derivatives, consequently altering the wine color (He et al., 2012; Monagas et al., 2005). Specifically, these monomeric anthocyanins transition into derivative pigments, including copigments and other polymeric phenols, during aging process, causing the red-purple hue of young red wine to mature into the red-orange color of aged wine (Boulton, 2001; Zhang et al., 2018). During this process, the majority of monomeric anthocyanins combine or condense with other phenolic compounds to form more complex and stable pigments, while some fade through degradation, oxidation, precipitation, or by turning into other colorless compounds (Gutiérrez et al., 2005; Zhang, Li, et al., 2020).

Notably, anthocyanins are not the only factor determining fruit wine color. Research suggests that other colorless non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds, such as phenolic acids, flavonols, tannins, and catechins, also play a crucial role in the expression of fruit wine color (Montealegre et al., 2006; Pérez-Magariño & González-San José, 2006; Zhang et al., 2017). These compounds, which contain hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, can diversify color intensity by forming hydrogen bonds or charge transfer interactions with anthocyanins. The resulting copigmented compounds can then block water molecules from attacking anthocyanins, thereby preventing the formation of the chalcone structure and safeguarding anthocyanins from degradation (Cheng et al., 2023). Most of the current research focuses on the stabilizing effect of single copigments on fruit wine anthocyanins and the enhancement of fruit wine color (Gordillo et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2020). However, since multiple phenolic copigments coexist in the real fruit wine matrix, the potential synergistic interactions among these coexisting copigments in real wine systems remain underexplored.

Blending techniques have emerged as effective strategies to balance the composition of phenolic compounds in fruit wines and enrich their color characteristics. By incorporating other fruit varieties abundant in copigments, the stability of anthocyanins in fruit wines can be enhanced, leading to a synergistic effect (Heras-Roger et al., 2016). Studies on wines blended from different varieties have demonstrated a more robust and long-lasting coloration compared to single varietal wines. For instance, blending ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ with other varieties significantly improved the red hue and phenolic content of wines, likely due to copigments such as flavanols and flavan-3-ols from the added varieties (Li, Zhao, et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, the unstable coloration of the blueberry wines might be mitigated by using blending techniques to balance the chemical components of different fruit wines, thereby resolving color deterioration issues during the aging process.

Given that ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes are rich in copigments such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and ferulic acid (FA), we hypothesize that blending blueberries with these grapes introduces copigments that synergistically stabilize anthocyanins. To validate this, both blending experiments and molecular-level copigmentation analyses were integrated to elucidate the mechanism of color stabilization. To be specific, (1) the effects of blending blueberries and ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ wine grapes in different proportions on the physicochemical properties, anthocyanin composition and content, and color parameters of the resulting wines, aiming at determining the optimal blending ratio and aging time for enhancing wine color stability; (2) based on model blueberry wines, the copigmentation effects of the main copigments derived from grapes and their pairwise interaction on anthocyanins were explored.

Material and methods

Chemicals

The food-grade syringic acid (SA, CAS: 530-57-4), quercetin (QG, CAS: 849061-97-8), epicatechin (EC, CAS: 490-46-0), ferulic acid (FA, CAS: 1135-24-6), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, CAS: 989–51-5) and gallic acid (GA, CAS: 149-91-7) with a purity of ≥ 98 %were purchased from Shanghai Kangting Bio-Technique Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The anthocyanins (ACNs) extracted from blueberries (36% purity) were obtained from Skyherb Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China), which contain two main ACNs of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and peonidin-3-O-glucoside (Fig. S1). All other common chemical reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Blending experiments with blueberries and grapes

Blended fruit wine winemaking process

Vaccinium corymbosum L. ‘Lanmei 1’ blueberries and Vitis vinifera L. ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes were employed for winemaking in 2022. The ripe grapes (with the total soluble solids reaching at ∼20°Brix) were harvested from Jinzhai county in Anhui province, and ripen blueberries were obtained from Huaning county in Anhui province. Fresh blueberries and grapes were respectively mixed in the proportions of 6:4 (B6G4), 7:3 (B7G3), 8:2 (B8G2), and 9:1 (B9G1) based on the fresh fruit weight. A control group was also set up with 100% blueberries (CK). Each group was established with three biological replicates, each replicate consisting of 1 kg of berries. The fruit wines were prepared according to the procedures described by Wang et al. (2023). Upon the completion of alcoholic fermentation, the fruit wines were filtered and stored at 16 °C. Wine samples were taken respectively on the 0, 90, 180, 270, and 360 days of aging, and were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Physiochemical compositions in fruit wines

The total soluble solids (TSS) content was measured using a handheld refractometer (Atago PAL-1, Tokyo, Japan). The pH value of the fruit wines was measured using a pH meter (Mettler-Toledo S220, Greifensee, Switzerland). The residual sugar, titratable acidity, total SO2, and alcohol content of the fruit wines were determined following the analytical methods outlined in GB/T 15038-2006 “General Analysis Method for Wine and Fruit Wine” (the national standard of Peoples's Republic China). Total anthocyanins were determined using the pH differential method (Liu et al., 2018) and were expressed as equivalent of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (mg/L). Total phenols were determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu phenol spectrophotometric method (Martín-Gómez et al., 2021) and were expressed as equivalent of gallic acid (mg/L). The CIELab parameters were used for colorimetric measurements of fruit wines, following the procedure outlined by Gordillo et al. (2012). CIElab parameters included lightness (L⁎), green/red component (a⁎), blue/yellow component (b⁎), chroma (C⁎ab), hue (hab) and overall colorimetric difference (ΔE⁎).

Analysis of monomeric anthocyanins in fruit wine

The monomeric anthocyanin content in blueberry wine was analyzed using liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS). Blueberry wine sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube and vortexed for 60 s. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C), the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred to a vial for analysis (Wang et al., 2024).

The analysis was performed on a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with a Thermo Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) under the following conditions: flow rate of 0.25 mL/min, column temperature of 50 °C, and injection volume of 2 μL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water containing 1% (v/v) formic acid and (B) methanol containing 1% (v/v) formic acid. A gradient elution program was applied: 0.0–2.0 min, 5% B; 2.0–15.0 min, 5%–70% B; 15.0–15.1 min, 70%–95% B; 15.1–18.0 min, 95% B; 18.0–20.0 min, 95%–5% B; 20.0–25.0 min, 5% B.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using the Thermo Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with an ESI source operated in positive ion mode. Key parameters included: spray voltage of 3.5 kV, sheath gas flow rate of 47 arbitrary units (arb), auxiliary gas flow rate of 15 arb, capillary temperature of 320 °C, full scan range of m/z 220–1300 at a resolution of 70,000, normalized collision energies of 10 eV, 50 eV, and 60 eV, and MS/MS resolution of 17,500.

Analysis of major non-anthocyanin phenols in fruit wine

For the analysis of non-anthocyanin phenolics, an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system coupled with an Agilent 6410 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was employed as described by Li et al. (2017). Prior to injection, each wine sample (1 mL) was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone membrane, and an aliquot of 1 μL was injected in triplicate. The non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds were separated by a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at 55 °C. The mobile phases consisted of water (A) and a 50:50 (v/v) mixture of acetonitrile and methanol (B), each containing 0.1% formic acid. A gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min as follows: 10–46% B over 28 min, followed by a return to 10% B within 1 min, and finally a 5-min re-equilibration with 10% B. The mass spectrometer operated in negative electrospray ionization mode with a spray voltage of 4 kV. The source temperature was set to 150 °C, the desolvation gas temperature to 350 °C, the gas flow rate to 12 L/h, and the nebulizer pressure to 35 psi. The Mass Hunter software (version B.04.00, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for data processing.

Copigmentation rate

Determination of the proportions of copigmented anthocyanins (CA%), free anthocyanins (FAN%) and polymeric anthocyanins (PA%) followed the methods described by Wang et al. (2018) with appropriate modifications. Specifically, the pH of wine sample was adjusted to 3.1 using 1 M HCl or NaOH. Four mL of wine sample was mixed with 40 μL of 10% (v/v) acetaldehyde solution, vortexed until homogeneous, and allowed to react for 45 min. The maximum absorption was recorded during full wavelength scanning, expressed as Aacet. For a 4 mL wine sample combined with 320 μL 5% (v/v) SO2, the solution was vortexed until uniform, and the peak absorption was recorded during full wavelength scanning, expressed as ASO2. For the wine sample without adding either acetaldehyde or SO2, the maximum absorption was recorded during full wavelength scanning, expressed as Awine. The values of CA% were calculated following the formula (Aacet - Awine)/Aacet * 100%. The values of FAN% were calculated following the formula (Awine – ASO2)/Aacet * 100%. The values of PA% were calculated following the formula ASO2/Aacet * 100%.

Percent polymeric color (PPC)

The determination of the percentage of polymeric color utilized the methods described by Hager et al. (2020), with appropriate modifications. Initially, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 ×g at 4 °C for 15 min and then diluted with distilled water to measure the color density (CD) by measuring the absorbance at 420, 510 and 700 nm according to the formula [(A420-A700) + (A510-A700)] × K, where K represented the dilution factor. In order to analyze the polymeric color (PC) of wine samples, 0.3 mL of 0.09 M potassium metabisulfite were mixed with 4.2 mL of the diluted wine sample. After equilibrating for 30 to 60 min, the absorbances at 420, 510 and 700 nm were recorded, respectively, and polymeric color was calculated following the formula [(A420-A700) + (A510-A700)] × K, where K represented the dilution factor. The percent polymeric color (PPC) was calculated following the formula PC/CD * 100%.

Copigmentation effect of different copigments in model blueberry wines

Preparation of model blueberry wines

The formulation of model blueberry wines was conducted according to the method described by Wang et al. (2024). Briefly, the model blueberry wine comprised of 8 g/L citric acid, 12% (v/v) ethanol, and 0.2 mM anthocyanin (ACNs) extracts (expressed as cyanidin 3-O-glucoside). The pH of the solution was calibrated to 3.1 using 1 M NaOH or HCl. The addition of NaCl facilitated the adjustment of ionic strength to 0.2 M.

Effects of different molar ratio, temperature and pH on absorption spectra of the copigmented model blueberry wines

Five monomeric phenolic copigments, including epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), quercetin (QG), ferulic acid (FA), and syringic acid (SA), and four composite copigments, including EC + EGCG, QG + EGCG, FA + EGCG and SA + EGCG, were applied for copigmentation in model blueberry wines. To investigate effects of the molar ratios of ACNs to copigments on copigmentation, the molar ratio of ACNs to copigments, except for QG, were set at 1:0, 1:1, 1:10, 1:20, 1:40 and 1:80, respectively. Considering the characteristic of quercetin being slightly soluble in water, the molar ratio of ACNs to QG was adjusted to 1:0, 1:0.5, 1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2 and 1:2.5. To investigate the effects of temperature on copigmentation, the copigmentation reaction temperature was set at 4, 10, 20, 30, and 40 °C, respectively. To investigate the effects of pH on copigmentation, the pH of the model blueberry wine was adjusted to 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0 and 5.0, while the ionic strength was maintained at 0.2 M using NaCl. When the above samples were prepared, the mixed solutions were stored for 30 min in the dark for equilibration. Subsequently, with distilled water as a blank control, a full wavelength scan of the solutions was carried out using a 10 mm quartz cuvette in the range of 400–700 nm, with a scanning interval of 1 nm.

Thermodynamic parameter analysis

Based on the copigmentation reaction between anthocyanins and monomeric/composite copigments in model blueberry wines, the equilibrium constant (K), Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), enthalpy change (ΔH°), and entropy change (ΔS°) were calculated according to the following formulas (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2020):(1)lnA−A0A0=lnK+nlnCP0(2)ΔG=−RT×lnK(3)ΔG=ΔH−TΔS(4)lnA−A0A0=−ΔHRT+ΔSR+nlnCP0

Herein, A and A0 represent the absorbance values at 520 nm of the model blueberry wine with and without the addition of copigments, respectively; n represents the stoichiometric ratio of copigments to anthocyanins; [CP]0 stands for the concentration of copigments. The equilibrium constant (K) is expressed as ([AH(CP)+n]/([AH+][CP]n)); R represents the gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K); T represents the Kelvin temperature.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to serve as the target molecule for molecular docking analysis. The 3D structure rendering and energy minimization of anthocyanins were accomplished using the Chem 3D Pro software (PerkinElmer Inc., Massachusetts, USA). The preparation of copigments and anthocyanin pdbqt files was completed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.7 (Scripps Research, California, USA). The molecular docking of copigments and anthocyanins was conducted using AutoDock 4.2.6 software (PerkinElmer Inc., California, USA), with the Lamarckian genetic algorithm as the searching method. The optimal results, characterized by the lowest binding energy and the highest binding numbers, were selected, and the output complex underwent rendering using PyMol 2.5.2 software (Schrodinger LLC., New York, USA).

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the effects of blending ratio on physicochemical parameters, total phenolics, total anthocyanins, color parameters (CIELab), and anthocyanin proportions (CA%, FAN%, PA%), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was performed for each aging time point separately using SPSS 25 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Heatmaps were created using Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/), while all other plots were generated with Origin 2021 (OriginLab, MA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, MA, USA). The CIELab parameters were imported into Adobe Photoshop CS6 software (Adobe Systems Incorporated, CA, USA) to create a simulated color panel of the fruit wines.

Results and discussion

Effects of blending treatments on physiochemical composition of the juice and fruit wines

The blended fermentation with blueberries and grapes significantly decreased total acidity of the juice while increasing its pH value. As for the fruit wines after alcoholic fermentation, blending treatments had a consistent effect on total acidity and pH as they did with the juice, with no significant impact on the residual sugar content (Table S1). Due to the lower total anthocyanin and total phenol content in grapes compared to blueberries, the composite juice was affected by the dilution effect, resulting in significantly lower total anthocyanins and total phenol content than the control group (Table S1). Moreover, with an increase in the proportion of grapes, there was a downward trend in the total phenol and total anthocyanin content in the composite juice. Upon the completion of alcoholic fermentation, the total anthocyanin and total phenol content in the fruit wine markedly decreased compared to the juice, with an average decrease of 58.57% in total anthocyanins and 17.98% in total phenols (Table S1). This can be attributed to the absorption of anthocyanins in the fruit wines by yeast during fermentation, as well as the degradation, oxidation, precipitation, or conversion of anthocyanins, leading to a decrease in anthocyanin content (He et al., 2012). Notably, the blending treatments had a consistent impact on the total anthocyanin and total phenol content in fruit wines as they did with the juice.

The effects of blending treatments on phenols and anthocyanins in fruit wines during aging process

As the aging process progressed, there was a noticeable decrease in total anthocyanin and total phenol content in the fruit wines. This reduction in phenolic content could be attributed to the interaction of phenolic compounds with polysaccharides, proteins, and other substances, or the polymerization reaction (Li & Duan, 2019). At the start of aging (0 days), the total anthocyanin and phenol contents in the blended fruit wines were significantly lower than in the controls. However, the total phenol content in the B6G4 group was significantly higher than the control group after 360 days of aging (Fig. 1), indicating that the loss of phenolic content in the blended fruit wines during aging was significantly lower than in the control group. Comparatively, the loss rate of anthocyanins and total phenols in fruit wines decreased with the increasing grape proportions during 360 days of aging (Fig. 1). The control group had the highest loss rates of anthocyanins and total phenols, at 82.01% and 26.34% respectively. However, the B6G4 group had the lowest loss rates, at 68.99% and 13.00% respectively (Fig. 1). This suggested that blending grapes with blueberries can protect anthocyanins from degradation. However, the inappropriate high grape proportions could lead to a low anthocyanin content in the blended fruit wines, which remained lower than the control group even after the aging process. Overall, it was crucial to strike a balance between maintaining the content of anthocyanins and minimizing the loss of anthocyanins in the blended fruit wines. To investigate the role of anthocyanin profiles in determining fruit wine color, this study compared the compositional differences between the B9G1 group (with the highest anthocyanin content after 180-day aging) and the control group at aging day 180 (Fig. S2). The results demonstrated that the primary anthocyanins in the fruit wine comprised seven monomeric anthocyanins and four acylated anthocyanins (Fig. 2). Notably, the B9G1 group exhibited significantly lower levels of monomeric anthocyanins (except for delphinidin-3-O-galactoside) compared to the control group, while its acylated anthocyanin content was markedly higher. Previous studies have reported that Cabernet Sauvignon grapes typically contain abundant acylated anthocyanins, predominantly p-coumaroylated acylated anthocyanins (He et al., 2012). Consistent with these findings, our study detected elevated levels of p-coumaroylated acylated anthocyanins in the B9G1 group (Fig. 2). To further elucidate the compositional basis for the enhanced color stability observed in blended wines, the contents of major non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds were compared between the control (CK) and B9G1 wines after 180 days of aging (Fig. S3). Overall, the B9G1 groups had higher levels of phenolic acids, flavan-3-ols and flavonols compared to the control groups. To be specific, both ferulic acid and (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) are the characteristic of ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes and have been reported as effective copigments for anthocyanins (Šikuten et al., 2020). In addition, the levels of (+)-catechin and (−)-epicatechin were also substantially elevated in B9G1, which are known to enhance copigmentation through π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding with anthocyanin flavylium cations (Zhao et al., 2020). Notably, p-coumaric acid glycoside became a dominant phenolic component in B9G1 compared to the control group. It can serve as the precursor or stabilizer for acylated anthocyanins, consistent with the increased proportions of acylated anthocyanins observed in B9G1 wines (Fig. 2). In general, these results indicate that blending blueberries with ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes not only diversifies the phenolic profile but also introduces potent copigments such as EGCG and ferulic acid. The enrichment of these compounds in the blended wine provides a molecular basis for the enhanced copigmentation, reduced anthocyanin loss, and improved color stability observed during aging.

The color of fruit wines can also be characterized by the proportions of free, copigmented and polymerized anthocyanins relative to the total anthocyanins. The copigmented anthocyanin ratio and the percent polymeric color (PPC) of the fruit wines can be employed as indicators for assessing the resistance of pigments to SO2 bleaching, thereby reflecting the degree of anthocyanin polymerization (Wang et al., 2024). In this study, the copigmented anthocyanin ratios of fruit wines showed a significant declining trend during the aging process. It was observed that the copigmented anthocyanin ratios of B6G4, B7G3, B8G2, and CK showed a decrease of 41.58%, 47.96%, 44.25%, and 51.33%, respectively, after 360 days of aging (Fig. 3). The polymerized pigments in fruit wines can be transformed from free and copigmented anthocyanins. In this study, the ratio of polymerized pigments showed an upward trend during the aging period (Fig. 3), which can potentially be resulted from the transition of copigment complexes with anthocyanins, formed after fermentation, into more stable polymerized pigments during the aging process. In terms of the percent polymeric color (PPC) of the fruit wines, the PPC decreased as the grape proportion increased at the onset of aging (0 day), with the control group showing the highest PPC of 62.11% (Fig. S4). As the aging progressed, PPC gradually increased in all groups, which was aligned with the increasing polymerized anthocyanin proportions. At 360 days of aging, the PPC in the blended fruit wines was significantly higher than in the controls (Fig. S4), indicating a stronger bleaching resistance and color stability.

The effects of blending treatments on the color of fruit wines during aging process

The trend in the shift of CIELab parameters in the five groups of fruit wine was fundamentally similar during the aging process (Fig. 4). The L⁎ value of the wine increased significantly with the prolonged aging time (Fig. 4a), while the a⁎ and b⁎ values showed a decreasing trend, primarily due to anthocyanin degradation in the wine (Fig. 4b, c). Notably, the decrease in the b⁎ value indicates a reduction in yellow tones and a relative increase in blue tones within the color matrix. In the context of red wine, where the base color is red (positive a⁎), a shift towards blue (negative shift in b⁎) does not result in a purely blue appearance. Instead, it combines with the red base to produce deeper, purplish, or more vibrant ruby hues, which are visually perceived as a more intense and stable red color. The ∆E⁎ values of all the blended wines exceeded 3.0, indicating that compared to the control group (Fig. 4f), blending effectively produced distinguishable differences recognizable by the human eye. At 360 days of aging, the L⁎ values of the five groups of wine increased by 5.3% (B6G4), 3.1% (B7G3), 1.9% (B8G2), 16.5% (B9G1), and 40.1% (CK), respectively, compared to day 0, while the a⁎ values decreased by 37.8% (B6G4), 47.7% (B7G3), 32.0% (B8G2), 29.8% (B9G1), and 32.2% (CK), respectively. Notably, the a⁎ values of the blended wines at the end of aging were significantly higher than the control group. Visual analysis of the wine color based on these color parameters (Fig. 4g) indicated that at 360 days of aging, blended wines, especially group B9G1, exhibited a deeper and more vibrant red hue. These results suggested that although the color expression of the blended wine was weaker than the control group at the onset of aging, the color change was relatively smaller during aging, culminating in a deeper and more vibrant hue. Correlation analysis between color parameters and copigmentation rate revealed that L⁎ values demonstrated a positive correlation with copigmented anthocyanins and free anthocyanins and a negative correlation with polymerized anthocyanins, while a⁎ values indicated the contrary (Fig. 4h). Overall, the above results showed that blending treatments can enhance the level of copigmentation in fruit wines during the aging process. This was possibly due to the ability of phenolic compounds in grapes to form π-π complexes with anthocyanins (Zhao et al., 2020), which increased the absorbance values and maximum absorption wavelength. As the aging progressed, these complexes gradually transformed into more stable substances, contributing to the stability of fruit wine color.

Copigmentation effect of different monomer copigments on anthocyanins in model blueberry wines

Research indicated that the primary copigment molecules in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes included epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, presented in grape seeds), quercetin (QG, presented in grape skins), ferulic acid (FA), and syringic acid (SA, presented in grape pulp) and so on (Gutiérrez et al., 2005; Li et al., 2010; Obreque-Slier et al., 2010). To further elucidate the mechanism by which blending blueberries with ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes enhances the color stability of blueberry wine, this study selected the aforementioned phenolic substances as copigments and investigated their copigmentation effects with anthocyanins (ACNs) in blueberries in a model blueberry wine system.

Effects of molar ratio, temperature and pH on the copigmentation in model blueberry wine

As the concentration of each copigment in the model wines increased, the absorbance at λ520 correspondingly increased, demonstrating a typical color enhancement effect. Concurrently, the λmax of each solution gradually shifted towards longer wavelengths, exhibiting a trend of redshift (Fig. S5). Notably, when the molar ratio of anthocyanin to co-pigment was 1:80, the absorbance at the λmax of the model wines of ACNs+SA, ACNs+EC, ACNs+FA and ACNs+EGCG increased by 77.79%, 51.72%, 139.9%, and 146.3%, respectively (Fig. S5). Similar concentration-dependent hyperchromic effects have been observed in other fruit wine systems. For instance, in blackberry wine residue anthocyanins (mainly cyanidin-3-O-glucoside), ferulic acid and rutin were the most effective copigments among phenolic acids and flavonoids, respectively, and the hyperchromic effect also intensified with increasing copigment concentration (Fan et al., 2019). It is noteworthy that, our results further demonstrate that EGCG, a flavan-3-ol with a galloyl moiety, can outperform phenolic acids and other flavonoids in color enhancement, resulting in the highest redshift of λmax, reaching up to 12 nm (Fig. S5), significantly altering the hue of the model wines. It is consistent with the superior copigmentation ability of flavonols and flavanols reported by Lyu et al. (2025) in a wine model containing malvidin-3-O-glucoside.

Research suggested that the copigmentation interaction between copigments and anthocyanins was primarily driven by van der Waals forces, which required the support of a large planar structure (Zhang et al., 2015). Among the five copigments, EGCG demonstrated the most potent effect. This superiority can be attributed to its specific molecular structure, which aligns with the structure-activity relationships established for monomeric flavan-3-ols (Zhang et al., 2024). Specifically, EGCG possesses both the critical B-ring pyrogallol group (tri-hydroxyl substitution) and a galloyl moiety esterified at the C3 position, structural features that have been directly linked to strong copigmentation effects (Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, EGCG exists predominantly in the 2,3-cis configuration, which, according to recent findings, allows for a synergistic enhancement when the B-ring and the 3-O-galloyl group are positioned on the same side, facilitating a more effective association with anthocyanins (Zhang et al., 2024). This combination of optimal substitution patterns and a favorable stereochemical configuration endows EGCG with an extensive, relatively planar π-conjugated system, enhancing π-π stacking with anthocyanins and promoting the formation of stable complexes, thereby resulting in the pronounced bathochromic shift and color enhancement observed.

As the temperature increased, the absorbance at the λmax of the model wines gradually decreased, exhibiting a rather noticeable decolorization effect (Fig. S6) accompanied by a certain blueshift effect. When the temperature increased from 4 °C to 40 °C, the Amax of the model wines containing SA, QG, EC, FA, and EGCG decreased by 19.6%, 18.3%, 6.3%, 18.9%, and 15.1%, respectively. In this study, the copigmentation reaction between epicatechin and anthocyanin was significantly less affected by high temperatures than the other groups, which could be attributed to its stability relies more on robust π-π stacking and hydrophobic interactions, which are less disrupted by heat, rather than on the more temperature-sensitive hydrogen bonds that dominate the complexes of other copigments like EGCG (Li & Duan, 2019). As the pH gradually increased, the Amax of the model wines significantly decreased (Fig. S7). Variations in pH alter the anthocyanin forms in the model wines, promoting their transformation into colorless chalcone structures. The pH-dependent behavior is consistent with studies on bayberry and blackberry anthocyanins, where acidic conditions (pH 3.0–3.4) favor the flavylium cation form (Fan et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2020). However, the addition of copigments shifts the equilibrium towards colored groups, effectively preventing the degradation of anthocyanins at different pH values. At pH = 3 (the pH of blueberry wine is between 3.1 and 3.4), the Amax of the model wines containing SA, QG, EC, FA, and EGCG increased by 43.70%, 39.62%, 15.41%, 46.72%, and 70.81% respectively, compared to the blank group (Fig. S7). This indicated that under the same pH conditions, the copigmentation effect of the aforementioned co-pigments followed the trend: EGCG > FA > SA > QG > EC.

Copigmentation reaction is the dynamic equilibrium between the copigmentation process and hydrolysis process. This equilibrium is reliant on the non-covalent interactions between copigments and anthocyanin molecules, which effectively obstruct the binding of water molecules with anthocyanins. Therefore, equilibrium constant (K) and thermodynamic parameters, such as Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), enthalpy change (ΔH°), and entropy change (ΔS°), can be employed to quantify the extent of the copigmentation reaction.

The negative ΔG° of the copigmentation reactions of the five copigments, indicated that the copigmentation reaction was a spontaneous exothermic process. ACNs+EGCG has the lowest ΔG° (−7.32 kJ/mol), further indicated that its copigmentation reaction is more likely to occur than other copigments (Table 1). ΔH° is considered a quantitative indicator for assessing changes in molecular binding energy during the association process. In this study, EGCG has the lowest ΔH° (−20.17 kJ/mol) compared to other complexes (Table 1), indicating a stronger exothermic process and more stable complex formation. This is consistent with EGCG's optimal structure for copigmentation, as its B-ring pyrogallol group and C3-galloyl moiety provide multiple sites for forming extensive van der Waals contacts and hydrogen bonds with anthocyanins and with surrounding solvent water molecules, leading to a more favorable enthalpy change (Wang et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2024). The ΔS° value could be an effective indicator for the reorganization of anthocyanin-copigment complexes caused by solvent molecules. The lowest ΔS° in the interaction between EGCG and anthocyanins suggested that EGCG can form more densely packed and highly ordered complexes than other anthocyanin-copigment complexes. The structural “pre-organization” afforded by EGCG's planar, rigid galloyl group and favorable cis-configuration likely reduces the entropic penalty upon binding, resulting in a tighter and more structured final assembly (Zhang et al., 2024).

Overall, the extent of the copigmentation reaction depended on the chemical structure of the copigments, and the interaction ability between anthocyanin and copigments follows the order of EGCG > FA > SA > QG > EC.

Molecular docking analysis

Considering the diversity of molecular structures, the aforementioned copigments exhibited varying degrees of steric hindrance, which might affect the binding sites and affinity between anthocyanins and copigment molecules. To delve further into the interactions between the copigment molecules and the main anthocyanin (cyanidin-3-O-glucoside) in blueberries (Li et al., 2024), the molecular binding energy was calculated, and molecular docking studies were conducted.

Based on the molecular binding energy between five phenolic copigments and cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (Fig. 5), it can be observed that EC and C3G have the highest binding energy (−2.16 kcal/mol), followed by QG (−2.47 kcal/mol), SA (−2.95 kcal/mol), FA (−3.62 kcal/mol), and EGCG (−4.88 kcal/mol). The lower the binding energy, the stronger the binding ability between the copigment molecules and anthocyanins. Therefore, EGCG has a stronger affinity for C3G, which is consistent with the affinity trends between copigments, and anthocyanins derived from thermodynamic analysis. The superior binding affinity of EGCG, as revealed by the lowest binding energy, is a direct computational manifestation of its advantageous structure. The presence of the galloyl group significantly expands the molecule's planar, hydrophobic surface area for π-π stacking, while the multiple hydroxyl groups on both the B-ring and the galloyl moiety provide numerous sites for hydrogen bonding. Moreover, the prevalent 2,3-cis configuration of EGCG positions these key functional groups synergistically for optimal interaction with the anthocyanin, minimizing steric repulsion and maximizing contact, as suggested by Zhang et al. The five spatial conformations simulated by molecular docking all demonstrated the interaction between the five copigments and C3G (Fig. 5). Typical non-covalent interactions readily form π-π stacking structures, which serve as the main driving force of intermolecular copigmentation between phenolic rings and anthocyanins (Zhao et al., 2020). Generally, the larger the extension of the π-conjugated system of copigments, the stronger their binding ability with anthocyanins. Moreover, the docking results also indicated that intermolecular hydrogen bonds were the primary interaction force between copigment molecules and anthocyanins. In this study, compared with other phenolic compounds, EGCG molecules are rich in hydroxyl groups, which can explain their stronger non-covalent π-π interactions with C3G.

Copigmentation effect of different composite copigments on anthocyanins in model blueberry wines

This study revealed the most potent copigmentation effect of EGCG through investigating the copigmentation reaction of monomer copigments and anthocyanins. The research indicated that the content of EGCG in ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes was generally higher than in blueberries, which could partly explain why blending blueberries with ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes significantly enhanced color stability during the aging process. However, in the actual wine system, a variety of phenolic copigments coexist, and the interaction effects among them and their influence on the copigmentation effect still require further investigation. To elucidate the inherent mechanism of how copigmentation interactions influence the color of fruit wine, this study conducted simulation experiments with four composite copigments, each formed by combining EGCG with either SA, QG, EC, or FA respectively.

Effects of molar ratio, temperature and pH on the copigmentation in model blueberry wine

When the molar ratio of anthocyanin to composite copigments was 1:80 (the molar ratio of anthocyanin to composite copigments QG + EGCG was 1:2), the treatments of EGCG mixed with SA, QG, EC, and FA have resulted in an increase of Amax of the model blueberry wines by 109.1%, 4.85%, 85.52%, and 164.4% respectively (Fig. S8). Besides, compared to monomer copigments, the composite copigments have led to an increase in Amax in all cases. Notably, the combination of ferulic acid with EGCG has enhanced the Amax by 7.4% compared to EGCG alone, demonstrating a superior copigmentation effect. When the system temperature is elevated from 4 °C to 40 °C, the treatments of EGCG combined with SA, QG, EC, and FA result in a decrease in the Amax of the model blueberry wines by 37.43%, 32.19%, 24.89%, and 28.19% respectively (Fig. S9). Relative to the monomer copigment groups, the decreases in Amax were more pronounced with increasing temperature, which indicated that the composite copigments were more significantly affected by temperature. The copigmentation interaction between composite copigments and anthocyanins primarily occurs in a low pH environment (Zhang et al., 2017). When the pH = 3, the treatments of EGCG combined with FA, QG, EC, and SA led to an increase in the Amax, as compared to the control group, by 83.13%, 54.73%, 28.34%, and 92.84% respectively (Fig. S10). This outcome suggested that under identical pH conditions, the trend of copigmentation effects of the four composite copigments is as follows: FA + EGCG > SA + EGCG >QG + EGCG > EC + EGCG.

Thermodynamic properties of the copigmentation reactions

The thermodynamic parameters of different phenolic compound copigment combinations were calculated and compared with the copigmentation effect of using EGCG alone (Table 1). The results indicated that, although the combined effects of SA, QG, and EC with EGCG increased compared to individual copigments, this enhancement was not significantly different compared with EGCG alone. Therefore, the interactive effects between these copigments and EGCG are not significant. However, when ferulic acid is combined with EGCG, both its equilibrium constants and thermodynamic parameters significantly increased. Compared to other composite copigments, FA + EGCG composite copigments have a stronger binding affinity with C3G, which can be described as a synergistic effect. By combining FA with EGCG, the resulting compound can provide a stable binding site for the chromophoric group of C3G in the simulated solution. This binding effectively protects the chromophoric group of C3G, preventing its hydration reaction, and thereby inhibiting the formation of colorless structures (Chen et al., 2023; Fenger et al., 2019).

Molecular docking analysis

The molecular binding energies between the four composite copigments and anthocyanins were calculated (Fig. 6). Among the four copigments tested, only the composite copigment obtained after combining ferulic acid and EGCG had a molecular binding energy with C3G (−14.38 kcal/mol) lower than that of EGCG. This suggested stronger binding interaction and enhanced copigmentation effect, consistent with the results obtained from the thermodynamic analysis. Research showed that the interactions among certain polyphenols exhibited a synergistic effect on their individual antioxidative properties, reflecting the existence of interrelated structures among certain phenolic compounds (Hidalgo et al., 2010). The molecular docking results indicated that a more symmetrical structure can be formed between ferulic acid and EGCG (Fig. 5), facilitating interactions with anthocyanin molecules. Moreover, after combining ferulic acid and EGCG, a potential π-π stacked arrangement may exist between the aromatic gallate ester and the catechol ring within the formed composite. The pronounced enhancement of its copigmentation action can be attributed to the presence of a planar π-π stacking system, engendering a form wherein anthocyanins are encapsulated. These findings underscore the crucial role of gallate esterification in its interactive capacity with anthocyanins. Conclusions

This study showed that blending treatments of blueberries and ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ can reduce the loss of anthocyanins and total phenols during fruit wine aging, thereby stabilizing the color of the fruit wine. The optimal blueberry-to-grape weight ratio was 9:1, which yields the highest total anthocyanin content after 180 days of aging. The improved stability could be partially attributed to an increased proportion of acylated anthocyanins and strong copigmentation effects, with EGCG from grapes having the strongest copigmentation ability due to its favorable molecular structure for π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding. Notably, this study reveals, for the first time, a significant synergistic copigmentation interaction between ferulic acid and EGCG, suggesting potential complementary mechanisms in ternary complex formation. These findings could provide a theoretical basis and technical support for the application of blending techniques and composite copigments to improve the color stability of blueberry wine.
