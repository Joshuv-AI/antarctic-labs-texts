Introduction

DNA damage response (DDR) is crucial for maintaining genome integrity and determining cell fate. Poly ADP-ribose polymerases (PARPs), also known as NAD⁺ ADP-ribosyltransferase or poly [ADP-ribose] synthase, serve as DNA damage sensors that rapidly activate the DDR in a manner dependent on the degree and complexity of the damage. PARPs play a key role in the initiation of chromatin organization at the damage site and the selection of DNA repair pathways [[1], [2], [3]]. PARP1, which belongs to the first PARP family, is the most important and well functionally investigated PARP enzyme. The activity of PARP1 relies on NAD⁺ as the donor of ADP-ribose. Therefore, many functions of PARP1 are closely associated with nuclear NAD+ metabolism and the metabolic patterns in cells [4]. PARP enzymes regulate the metabolism of carbohydrates and lipids energy via highly-ordered organismal and cellular processes [3]. PARP1 activity increases with DNA damage accumulation and repair, leading to irreversible depletion of NAD+ and subsequent reduction of its levels. This results in changes in cell metabolism and function, which could promote degenerative diseases [5]. In this context, PARPi can at least partially prevent these harmful events by disrupting the essential roles of PARP in DNA repair, particularly for single-stranded DNA breaks. While treatment with PARPi alone is not lethal to most types of cells, it induces the death of tumor cells carrying mutations of breast cancer genes (BRCA, including BRCA-1 and BRCA-2) or other genes involving homologous recombination (HR), a concept known as synthetic lethality. BRCAs are a family of genes responsible for homologous recombination repair (HRR). Their mutations cause homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) in tumor cells, a phenomenon commonly seen in breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancer [6,7], arguing PARP1 as a rational target for these types of cancer harboring BRCA mutations. In addition, PARP1 activity is associated with many other diseases, including stroke, circulatory shock, myocardial ischemia, nervous system disorders, and liver-related diseases [8,9], suggesting that PARP1 may also serve as a potential target for these non-malignant diseases as well [5,8]. Indeed, the therapeutic response to PARPi has been observed in non-malignant diseases, including inflammatory and immune-related diseases involving the liver, lungs, and heart. As a consequence, the clinical application of PARPi is increasing rapidly, including both cancer and non-malignant diseases [6–9].

Metabolic reprogramming represents a key mechanism for cells to change their metabolic patterns to meet energy needs during proliferation and growth, which involves the metabolism of various energy substances such as glucose, lipids, and amino acids. Metabolic reprogramming also occurs in a variety of diseases, including inflammation and cancer [10,11]. According to the latest findings, metabolic reprogramming is necessary for malignant transformation and tumorigenesis, as well as tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance [10,12]. Because PARP requires NAD+ as a substrate, the anti-tumor activity of PARPi is closely associated with metabolism [12–14]. However, it remains poorly understood whether and how PARPi affects cellular metabolism. In this review article, we summarize the effects of PARPi on metabolism and their underlying mechanisms, aiming to explore how the body maintains the balance of energy metabolism during PARPi therapy.

NAD+ and energy metabolism

Synthesis and catabolism of NAD+

Enzymatic degradation of NAD+ for signal transduction requires a continuous supply of dinucleotides, while the in vivo half-life of NAD+ is relatively short, varying from 15 min to 15 h upon tissue type. There are three main pathways for NAD+ synthesis: de novo synthesis, Preiss-Handler, and salvage pathways. Among them, the salvage pathway is considered to be the most important pathway for NAD+ synthesis in maintaining its normal intracellular levels, which starts from food intake and NAD+ consumption byproducts. The Preiss-Handler pathway starts from the molecules derived from nicotinic acid (NA) or tryptophan. The de novo synthesis, also known as the kynurenine pathway, is a prior step for tryptophan production (Fig. 1A) [4,15,16]. However, the relative contribution of the de novo synthesis pathway to NAD+ levels is still unclear. In humans, almost all types of cells use the salvage pathway to synthesize NAD+, except liver cells that use the de novo synthesis. As an electron carrier for energy production, NAD+ is consumed as a substrate by PARP, sirtuins, and CD138 (Fig. 1B) [15–17]. NAD+ affects the activity of NAD+-dependent deacetylases, PARP, and transcription factors in a broad spectrum of cellular functions. ADP-ribosylation is a process to transfer ADP-ribose (ADPR) to protein. In this reaction, NAD+ acts as a substrate to be enzymatically cleaved and then partially adds ADPR to amino acid receptors. This event involves either a single ADPR unit (mono ADP ribosylation) or multiple units through the formation of ADPR polymers (poly ADP ribosylation). PolyADP ribosylation plays an important role in various cellular processes, mainly transcriptional regulation, DNA repair, and initiation of cell death pathways [18,19]. In response to DNA damage, PARP, as a major consumer of NAD+, rapidly catalyzes a large amount of ADP-ribosylation at the site of DNA damage [4,17]. NAD+ is also required by NAD+-dependent deacylation mediated by sirtuins. Sirtuins cleave NAD+ and transfer acyl groups to ADPR. Sirtuins 1–7, as NAD+-dependent deacetylases, deacetylate histones at specific lysine residues, leading to transcriptional silencing. The dependency of sirtuins on the coenzyme NAD+ at the cellular level links sirtuin activity with energy metabolism [15–17,20]. NAD+ also acts as a precursor of calcium mobilization signaling produced by CD38 /157 and TIR motif-containing 1 (Sarm1) [4]. The conversion of NAD+ to cyclic ADP-ribose (cADPR) occurs at neutral pH, which is then converted to NADP via base exchange of nicotinamide (NAM) with NA at acidic pH. These products act as calcium mobilizers [17,21].

Roles of NAD+ in metabolism

NAD+ plays an important role in cellular signaling and metabolic regulation, including glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and the TCA cycle, thus serving as a core molecule in energy metabolism. NAD+ and its reduced form NADH act as acceptor and donor molecules for two electrons and one proton, respectively [4,15,17,20]. The concentration of NAD+ is not even within different cellular organelles, with mitochondria as the largest pool of NAD+ (∼250 μM), followed by the nuclear pool (70–100 μM), while cytoplasmic NAD+ pool serves as a linker and regulator for other NAD+ pools. In the cytoplasm, NAD+ is reduced to NADH during glycolysis when glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is oxidized to 1,3-biphosphoglycerate. When pyruvate is converted to lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase, NADH is oxidized to NAD+ (Fig. 1C) [22]. NADH enters the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) via shuttling glycerol-3-phosphate or malate-aspartate. In the mitochondria, NAD+ is reduced to NADH through the TCA cycle, and NAD+ can also be regenerated from NADH through ETC complex 1 (Fig. 1D). The nuclear NAD+ plays a key role in PARP-mediated ADP-ribosylation and SIRT-mediated deacetylation (Fig. 1E). NAD+ can be converted to NADP+ by NAD+ kinase in the cytosol and mitochondria, and consumed by PARPs and SIRTs in different subcellular compartments [4].

Fatty acid beta-oxidation (FAO) is another major source of acetyl-CoA, which reduces NAD+ to NADH [23]. In the OXPHOS pathway, glutamine is involved in the production of the electron donor NADH or FADH2 following the synthesis of guanosine triphosphate (GTP) and ATP [16]. NADH and FADH2 provide the electron gradient, which is required for ATP synthesis during OXPHOS [16]. NAD+ is a critical intermediate in energy metabolism, which serves as an enzyme cofactor in oxidation–reduction reactions involving glycolysis, OXPHOS, TCA, FAO, and glutamine metabolism. In these reactions, the NAM part of NAD+ receives high-energy electrons in the form of hydrides to produce NADH, which then acts as an electron donor for ATP synthesis via mitochondrial OXPHOS.

The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), which branches out from glycolysis, provides essential compounds for anabolic reactions, nucleotide synthesis, and detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and at the same time, the PPP provides substrate and NADPH for nucleotide biosynthesis during tumor cell division [24]. NADPH is required for reducing oxidized glutathione and can also directly act as an antioxidant, therefore preventing oxidative stress in the reductive biosynthesis pathway, which is necessary for cell survival. Inhibition of NADPH production e.g., by knocking down the rate-limiting enzyme 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6PGD) of the PPP pathway, slows down cell growth and reduces lactic acid production (a sign of metabolic reprogramming). Moreover, inhibition of 6PGD activity can simultaneously reduce NADH levels and the enzymatic activity of oxidized NADH-dependent sirtuin-1 [13]. It is worth noting that pharmacological inhibition of discoidin domain receptor 2 (DDR2), a tyrosine kinase receptor, impairs DNA repair by preventing PPP activation and reducing PARP1 activity. This increases the sensitivity to olaparib in HR-proficient ovarian cancer [25]. In addition, PARP is also closely related to the metabolism of amino acids and hormones [26]. PARP-1, PARP-2, PARP-5, and PARP-16 control the intricate network of glucose homeostasis via hormone regulation by improving beta cell function or regulating insulin sensitivity. Pharmacological PARP inhibition disrupts insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-1 signaling and induces cell death [27]. In neuronal ischemia-reperfusion injury, castration and low androgen levels reduce PARP activity [28], while estradiol induces the expression of PARP-1 in the uterus [29].

In the NAD+ theory, SIRT1 is designated as a universal molecular regulator of synthesis- and secretion-related factors, while PARP1 is a major consumer of NAD+ [15,17,20]. The activity of SIRT1 depends on the ratio of NAD+/NADH in the cytoplasm. SIRT1 with deacetylation activity is involved in the regulation of redox state and metabolic balance [15]. There is an interaction between PARP1 and SIRT1. The activity of PARP1 can be directly regulated by SIRT1 deacetylase activity. Alternatively, PARP1 can bind to and be regulated by a SIRT1-binding protein, deleted in breast cancer 1 (DBC1). The interaction between SIRT1 and DBC1 or PARP1 and DBC1 is dynamically regulated by the levels of NAD+ in cells [7]. PARP1 activation limits the activity of SIRT1, while PARPi can alleviate this limitation. Because increased activity of PARP1 consumes more NAD+, loss of PARP1 elevates NAD+ levels and thus activates SIRT1 [30]. Similarly, loss of PARP2 also increases SIRT1 activity. However, this increase is not due to the changes of NAD+ levels, rather via enhancing expression of SIRT1, because PARP-2 is a direct negative regulator of SIRT1 promoter [3]. Therefore, although NAD+ is considered a major regulator of SIRT1 activation, the deacetylase activity of SIRT1 can also be enhanced via a process independent of NAD+ increase [3,30].

Pyruvate, a product of glycolysis, is metabolized to lactic acid under the anoxic condition. Under normoxia, acetyl-coA is produced and enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Glycolytic products are also involved in PPP, Glycerol, Serine, glycine, one-carbon metabolism, etc. The intermediates of TCA are associated with metabolic pathways of fatty acid, glutamine, heme, aspartate, asparagine, among many others. The NADH produced by glycolysis, PPP, and TCA is coupled with OXPHOS to generate a large amount of ATP (Fig. 1C-D) [10,12,16]. The availability of NAD+ in the cytoplasm determines the rate of glycolysis and also is a key determinant of the rate of subsequent NADH and pyruvate entry into the mitochondria. Severe reduction of cytoplasmic NAD+ levels blocks glycolysis, leading to metabolic collapse and cell death. At multiple steps of the TCA cycle, NAD+ is reduced to NADH by acquiring two electrons and a proton from the substrate [31], which is required for maintaining OXPHOS and ATP levels.

Role of PARP1 in metabolism and metabolic diseases

PARP is a large family of at least 17 enzymes (PARP1–17), which play various roles in DNA repair, chromatin structure modulation, transcription, translation, protein degradation, cell division, immune processes, metabolism, mitochondrial biology, oxidative stress biology, cell death, differentiation, aging, tumorigenesis, etc. [1–3] (Fig. 2). Among them, PARP1 has multiple functions in the stress response (e.g., DNA repair and cell death), which use NAD+ as the donor of the ADP-ribose unit. If DNA damage is mild, PARP1 catalyzes a reaction that transfers ADP-ribose units from NAD+ to receptor proteins for repairing SSB (Fig. 3A). If a large amount of DNA is damaged, overactivation of PARP1 depletes NAD+, resulting in energy expenditure and cell death [32,33] (Fig. 3B). NAD+ depletion also causes mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase-dependent apoptosis [34]. The reaction produces PAR that can induce Parthanatos, also known as PARP1-dependent cell death. This new form of programmed cell death is activated by PARP1 in response to DNA damage. Overactivation of PARP1 by extensive DNA damage mediates the decomposition of intracellular NAD+ into nicotinamide and PAR while consuming a large amount of NAD+, which inhibits the activity of mitochondrial oxidative respiratory chain complex enzyme I-III, thereby blocking TCA cycle and impeding mitochondrial energy metabolism. Apoptosis-inducing factor is released and carries macrophage migration inhibition factor into the nucleus, inducing Parthanatos (e.g., chromatin agglutination and large DNA fragmentation). Parthanatos has been reported in many diseases such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, and other neurological diseases [35,36]. Therefore, the function of PARP1 is closely related to nuclear NAD+ metabolism and cellular metabolic profile [4].

It also consumes a large amount of NAD+, thereby reducing intracellular NAD+ levels, inhibiting glycolysis and mitochondrial respiratory chain, and disrupting SRIT1, etc., consequently leading to a decline in the ATP level in the cell and thus inability to maintain normal physiological processes, eventually necrosis.

PARP decomposes NAD+ into NAM and ADP-ribose, resulting in irreversible depletion of NAD+. Overactivation of PARP may induce cell death by depleting cellular NAD+. Since NAD+ is required for both glycolysis and OXPHOS metabolism in mitochondria, its depletion causes a breakdown of the energy supply and subsequent cell death. This mechanism has previously been referred to as metabolic catastrophe i.e., cell death in response to alkylating agents [32]. After nuclear DNA damage, PARP-dependent NAD+ depletion induces a decrease in NAD+ levels but a rapid increase in the binding of NADH with proteins. Phasor approach using fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy reveals an increase in protein-bound NADH and proportionally a decrease in free NADH in response to DNA damage. The increase in bound NADH fraction entirely depends on PARP1 activity, which is sensitive to OXPHOS inhibition and NAD+ supplementation. This is associated with a shift in metabolic balance toward OXPHOS rather than glycolysis. Due to the rapid deprivation of PARP-dependent ATP, inhibition of OXPHOS, but not glycolysis, induces parthanatos, reflecting a novel pro-survival effect of PARP activation through cellular metabolism [32]. Of note, PARP1-induced NAD+ depletion is not the only mechanism for inducing metabolic breakdown and cell death. Other mechanisms include nuclear-to-mitochondrial communication via PARP1 activation and DNA damage-induced defects in glycolysis and OXPHOS (Fig. 3B). For example, loss of hexokinase 1 (HK1) activity due to changes in subcellular localization after DNA damage-induced PARP1 activation blocks glycolysis and reduces ATP production. Interestingly, PARP1-mediated PAR synthesis, rather than direct NAD+ depletion, impairs glycolysis and results in ATP decrease. HK1 is identified as a PAR-binding protein. HK1 activity is inhibited by the binding of PAR following nuclear ARTD1 activation [37]. Restriction of energy substrate in the medium (glucose is the only exogenous energy substrate for astrocytes) induces PARP1 activation, leading to rapid but incomplete depletion of cytoplasmic NAD+ in astrocytes, accompanied by almost complete blockade of glycolysis, ultimately cell death. Consistently, the supplement of NAD+ can restore glycolysis, thereby preventing cell death. Similarly, the addition of non-glucose substrates (e.g., pyruvate, glutamic acid, or glutamine) to the medium also prevents the death of astrocytes following PARP1 activation [35,38]. These substrates increase pyruvate availability for mitochondria, thereby partially counteracting mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cell death induced by blockade of pyruvate production by glycolytic inhibition due to PARP-1 activation. Activation of PARP1 first causes a rapid depletion of the NAD+ pool in the cytoplasm, but not mitochondria. Depletion of the cytoplasmic NAD+ pool prevents cells from using glucose as a metabolic substrate, thus blocking glycolysis and reducing pyruvate production, subsequently impairing the TCA cycle. It can induce rapid cell death due to energy breakdown if glucose is the solely available metabolic substrate. Glucose serves as the metabolic substrate that rapidly transports across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Thus, this cell death pathway is closely associated with brain disorders [35]. In addition, PARP1 can also inhibit the activity of GAPDH, thereby inhibiting anaerobic glycolysis and thus reducing ATP production, leading to injury and subsequent necrosis of proximal tubule cells [39,40].

Lipid metabolic disorders are one of the most serious threats to human health worldwide, with obesity and high cholesterol as the top risk factors associated with chronic diseases as defined by the WHO [41]. PARPs play an essential role in fat metabolism, adipocyte differentiation, adipocyte function regulation, and energy homeostasis, as well as in obesity and its related diseases [19]. The main roles of PARP1 in lipid metabolism are discussed as follows. (1) Fatty acid metabolism: ultraviolet irradiation induces PARP1-dependent autophagy, a source of triglycerides [42]. Pharmacological inhibition or deletion of PARP1 alters the composition of membrane lipids [43,44]. Furthermore, PARP1 can also regulate the levels of inflammatory polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolites [43]. (2) Cholesterol homeostasis: Numerous studies have revealed that PARP1 is closely associated with cholesterol homeostasis [45]. PARP1 inhibition decreases the level of total serum cholesterol but increases the ratio of high-density and low-density lipoprotein (HDL/LDL ratio). Thus, PARP1 inhibition has a protective effect under some harmful conditions such as high-fat or high-cholesterol diet and diabetes [46–48]. (3) Liver metabolism: PARP1 activation mediates the PARylation and thus inactivation of peroxisome proliferator activating receptor (PPAR), a transcription factor, resulting in lipid accumulation in the liver due to inhibition of lipid oxidation [14,47,49]. PARP1 inhibition significantly reduces endoplasmic reticulum stress [14], resulting in down-regulation of de novo lipid biosynthesis [50], and simultaneously alters other cellular processes such as up-regulation of lipid uptake and lipolysis [47]. PARP1 also plays an important role in the pathology of lipid metabolism in the liver, such as promoting lipid accumulation in fatty liver diseases, either non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or alcoholic liver disease [51].

Increasing the activity of PARP1 may also be involved in the regulation of metabolic homeostasis indirectly by affecting sirtuins [15–17]. Sirtuins regulate the acetylation status and activity of numerous enzymes and transcription regulators, including PPAR, sterol regulatory element-binding proteins, cAMP response element-binding proteins, Atgs, and p53. These proteins in turn affect the expression of genes involved in energy homeostasis (the metabolism of carbohydrate, fat, or protein) and inflammation. Because the activation of both sirtuins and PARP1 depends on NAD+, PARP over-activation can reduce the activity of sirtuins by competing for the NAD+ pool, thereby disrupting cellular energy homeostasis [18,30] (Fig. 1B and Fig. 3C). PARP1 also interacts with insulin or IGF-1 to regulate metabolism and cell survival. IGF-1 protects cells against oxidative stress by preserving mitochondrial integrity and inhibiting PARP cleavage by activated caspase-3, in association with the activation of the PI3K/PDK1/Akt pathway [52]. Similarly, PARP1 inhibition reduces hyperglycemia-induced apoptosis by preventing caspase cleavage through activating the IGF-1R/Akt pathway [53].

In a model with in vitro and in vivo loss of PARP14 function, it has been demonstrated that PARP14 promotes aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) in hepatocellular carcinoma by maintaining low activity of the pyruvate kinase M2 subtype (PKM2), an event associated with inhibition of Thr365 phosphorylation and activation of PKM2, dependently on the pro-apoptotic kinase c-Jun N-terminal kinase 1 (JNK1). PARP14 depletion attenuates glycolysis without affecting mitochondrial OXPHOS. Moreover, targeting PARP14 increases the sensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma cells to anti-tumor drugs [53].

Together, PARPs, as the key mediators of stress responses, are closely related to cellular metabolism via the consumption of NAD+. PARP1 activation reduces mitochondrial complex I, therefore decoupling ETC, producing superoxide, blocking glycolysis, and consequently, reducing ATP production. Prolonged activation of PARP1 impairs energy homeostasis in cells, leading to severe ATP depletion and apoptosis. In contrast, PARP1 inhibition improves energy homeostasis and mitochondrial function. The relationship between PARPs and lipid metabolism seems bidirectional. On the one hand, the activity of PARPs is regulated by various lipid species and relevant drugs. On the other hand, PARPs regulate lipid metabolism and are thus involved in the pathogenesis of many lipids’ metabolic diseases [54].

The interactions between PARP1, NAD+ and SIRT1 in cancer

SIRT1 is often considered an oncogene that plays an important role in drug resistance [55] SIRT1 is overexpressed in most types of ovarian cancers [56,57]. High expression of SIRT1 is associated with malignant transformation of human ovarian tissue and poor prognosis of patients with ovarian cancers [58]. SIRT1 deacetylates p53 and inhibits its activity, thereby maintaining cell cycle and proliferation in response to stress [59]. However, SIRT1 also deacetylates proteins of the FOXO family, transcriptionally inhibiting pro-apoptotic and stress-related genes [59]. In many circumstances, SIRT1 plays a dual role in oncogenesis as a tumor suppressor or promoter by activating the different signaling pathways, probably in a cancer-type-dependent manner. Deoxypodophyllotoxin simultaneously activates PARP1 and SIRT1 in glioma, inducing malignant transformation of glioma cells [60]. Nicotinamide mononucleotide promotes the function of pancreatic islets in mice after burns through the SIRT1 pathway [60]. Ginsenoside Rh2 targets SIRT1-mediated deacetylation overcome endocrine therapy resistance in prostate cancer [59]. Inhibition of PARP 1 or SIRT1 can increase the NAD+/NADH ratio in breast adipose fibroblasts, while the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio in turn controls the interaction between PARP1 and SIRT1. In the case of a low NAD+/NADH ratio (known as the inverse Warburg effect), the balance between acetylation and deacetylation can be modulated to treat estrogen-dependent breast cancer [61].

Relationship between HR deficiency and energy metabolism

The principle of synthetic lethality is based on the findings that while base-excision repair, the main mechanism for DNA break repair in normal cells, depends on PARP (Fig. 3A), HRR relies on BRCA1/2 for high-fidelity repair. In normal cells, PARPi can fix PARP on damaged DNA, causing the replication fork to stall, which in turn activates the BRCA1/2-dependent HRR pathway to repair damage and reactivate the replication fork. However, in BRCA1/2 deficient (i.e., HRD) tumor cells, stalled replication fork following PARPi can only be repaired via non-homologous end junctions (NHEJ), with a higher error rate. Thus, this process often leads to high levels of genomic instability, therefore causing death of tumor cells [1,3,10,12]. The ability to detect and repair DNA damage is in harmony with the metabolic changes in cells [10]. Metabolic alterations influence DDR by regulating the availability of substrates required for DNA repair as well as the function of proteins involved in DNA repair. In turn, DDR-related proteins indirectly regulate metabolic flux by fixing metabolic enzymes associated with major metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, TCA cycle, PPP, and ROS production [62].

Recent findings have demonstrated the relationship between DNA damage and cell metabolism, and also the interaction between HRD and energy metabolism as well. BRCA1, a key HR protein, is involved in energy metabolism such as fatty acid (FA) synthesis [63]. In addition, BRCA1 also functions to maintain energy homeostasis in skeletal muscle [64,65]. BRCA1-knockout mice exhibit an increase in total body energy expenditure but a decrease in exercise tolerance, accompanied by reduced mitochondrial respiration rate, together with enlarged mitochondria [64,65]. BRCA also significantly affects myocardial metabolism. Ablation of BRCA1 in cardiomyocytes is responsible for significant alterations in glucose and FA metabolism, with reduced FAO [66]. These metabolic changes lead to a decrease in overall ATP production[64–66]. BRCA1 also affects the metabolism in tumor cells. It’s down-regulation increases adipogenesis in breast cancer cells [67]. Recombinant BRCA1 induces global transcriptional changes, resulting in down-regulation of glycolytic enzymes but up-regulation of genes involving antioxidant response and TCA cycle [68]. Overexpression of BRCA1 can reverse the Warburg effect, thereby inhibit the growth and migration of breast cancer cells and improve the sensitivity to anti-tumor therapy, in association with reduced expression of PKM2 via the PI3K/AKT signaling [69]. Interestingly, deletion of BRCA2 or radiation sensitive 51 (RAD51) does not resemble these phenotypic changes, highlighting the specificity of BRCA1 in metabolic regulation [70]. Unlike BRAC1, the effects of BRCA2 on metabolism remain inconsistent. For example, BRCA2 can regulate tumor cell metabolism after cisplatin treatment, characterized by inhibition of mitochondrial function and increased glucose uptake, without change of glycolysis [71]. In line, NAM phosphate ribose transferase-dependent glycolysis is enriched in a group of cells lacking the BRCA1/2 mutations [72]. Thus, BRCA2 may act as a regulator of intracellular metabolic response.

It is worth noting that because tumor cells with HRD rely on NAD+ and ATP-consuming PARP enzymes for DNA repair, alterations in the activity of these enzymes affect cell viability and metabolism. Such metabolic reprogramming may have an important implication for the prevention and treatment of this kind of cancer [73].

PARPi targets energy metabolism

PARPi has been widely used for the treatment of cancer with BRCA mutations and for enhancing sensitivity to radiotherapy or other therapies against HR-proficient tumors and non-malignant diseases [73]. Antitumor effects of PARPi via metabolic interference

Different mechanisms for DNA repair depend on specific metabolites, which in turn determine the sensitivity of tumor cells to different treatments. OXPHOS is important for maintaining high ATP levels and PARP activity in HRD cells, which may partially explain why metformin can block the growth of HR-deficient tumors in vivo and in vitro [32]. The metabolic switch from OXPHOS to hyper-glycolysis also affects the sensitivity of HRD cells to PARPi [73]. As HRD cells are more sensitive to OXPHOS inhibition, the combination of OXPHOS inhibitors (e.g., metformin) and PARPi (e.g., olaparib) has then been investigated. Surprisingly, no synergistic, rather antagonistic, activity has been observed in Trp53/BRCA2-deficient ID8 cells, suggesting that both OXPHOS inhibition and glycolytic restriction may be required for susceptibility of HRD cells to PARPi. Olaparib significantly changed the metformin pharmacokinetics, which may be of clinical importance [73]. Olaparib achieves sensitivity of chronic lymphocytic leukemia to BTK and BCL2 targeted therapies by disrupting mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction that is independent of PARP1 [74]. Reversing Olaparib resistance in OC by targeting glucosamine metabolism [75]. Moreover, Olaparib regulates glutamine metabolism and acts on ubiquitin D, thereby promoting autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma [76]. However, co-targeting glycolysis and specific RAD51 function show increased efficacy, compared to standard-of-care treatments, in leukemia [74]. In addition, reduced levels of glutamine in olaparib-treated cell lines indicate increased utilization of glutamine [77].

Metabolic profiling of the BRCA1 mutant PDX model shows that the expression of OXPHOS and respiratory chain complex I gene sets is higher in olaparib-sensitive than -resistant tumors, while an opposite phenomenon is observed in the case of MCT4 expression. Thus, the efficacy of olaparib may depend on the metabolic characteristics of HRD cells, suggesting increased glycolysis as a potential mechanism for PARPi resistance [73]. Enhanced OXPHOS in HRD cells predicts the vulnerability of tumors to PARPi, while Trp53/Brca2 loss is associated with differential cell sensitivity to metformin. Metformin affects cell viability and cell cycle of Trp53- or Trp53/Brca2-deficient cells but does not induce apoptosis. In contrast, the administration of olaparib results in a large increase in the number of apoptotic cells. Compared with HR-proficient ovarian cancer cells, the combination of olaparib and metformin more significantly inhibits cell proliferation and colony formation of wild-type cells [78]. Therefore, because the efficacy of olaparib is influenced by the metabolic characteristics of tumor cells, metabolic profiling may help predict the response or resistance to PARPi. Blockade of glycolysis can resensitize tumors to PARPi, representing an alternative mechanism to overcome drug resistance (Fig. 4).

Effects of PARPi on tumor microenvironment via metabolic interference

PARPi interferes with DNA repair in tumor cells, resulting in tumor antigen release and changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [5]. For example, PARPi can increase the M1/M2 ratio of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and inhibit the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) by improving the hypoxic condition in TME, inhibiting the uptake of glucose by macrophages, and regulating the expression of related cytokines [79]. PARP1 is essential for the activation, stabilization, and accumulation of HIF-1 under hypoxia, a common event associated with tumorigenesis [79,80]. In HeLa cell and melanoma models, treatment with olaparib reduces HIF-1 stability and its protein level [81]. In PARP1 knockout mice and human renal carcinoma-derived cells treated with PARPi, PARP1 inhibition markedly reduces HIF-2 transcription and its protein stability [80]. HIFs promote the expression of macrophage-derived VEGF-A [82]. The inhibition of HIF by PARPi reduces the sensitivity of macrophages to an anoxic environment to a certain extent, thus maintaining the activity of macrophages. PARPi may negatively regulate TME by impairing DNA repair in immune cells and up-regulating PD-L1 in tumor cells [79]. Persistent DNA damage induced by PARPi promotes chronic inflammation, thereby inhibiting the function of dendritic cells [83]. Moreover, PARPi has multifunctional effects on T cells. For example, PARPi promotes the activation, migration, and cell-killing activity of CD8+ T cells via increasing the production of type I IFN and inhibiting the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. However, PARPi reduces the number of T cells to a certain extent, while increasing the proportion of Tregs and up-regulating CD80 expression on DCs that impairs DC-induced T cell activation [79]. Although the direct lethality of PARPi toward TAMs is weak, olaparib can cause metabolic reprogramming of TAMs, contributing to its high cytotoxicity and phagocytosis. PARPi treatment results in NAD+ surge, glycolysis increase, OXPHOS inactivation, and induction of mitochondrial reverse electron transfer through increasing ROS and transcriptional reprogramming. Such reprogramming occurs in either the absence or presence of PARP1/PARP2, which can be partially resembled by adding the NAD+ derivative methyl-NAM [84]. Together, the effects of olaparib on TAMs contribute to the anti-tumor activity of PARPi in vivo and in vitro.

PARPi may have dual effects on TME i.e., either promoting or inhibiting the anti-tumor immune response, although the latter prevails in most circumstances. The net effect of PARPi on macrophages in TME may vary among different types of cancer. Further studies to determine the potential effect of PARPi on the TME specific for certain types of cancer are needed to inform the selection of combination regiments and patients who may benefit from these therapies.

Effects of PARPi on neurological diseases via metabolic interference

PARPi has been extensively studied in various neurological disorders. In brain tissue, glucose is the major source of energy supply due to the presence of the blood-brain barrier. The anti-seizure effect of the ketogenic diet (KD) has long been documented. In rats fed with KD, the levels of hippocampal sirtuins, PARP, and the oxidation-induced DNA damage marker show an immediate and persistent increase, compared with those fed with regular chow. KD modulates energy metabolism by affecting NAD+-dependent enzymes and their downstream pathways [85]. PARPi affects the metabolism by influencing NAD+ levels in neurological diseases, including tumors and non-malignant diseases such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease (Fig. 4A-D).

Neurological cancer

The combinations of PARPi play an increasing role in the treatment of numerous cancer types, including brain tumors [86]. Clinical studies of FDA-approved PARPi as monotherapy or combination therapy in primary or metastatic brain tumors are summarized in Table 4. Among them, the treatment of glioma accounts for the largest proportion. Patients with glioblastoma (GBM), the most common neuroepithelial tumor, often have poor prognosis. Temozolomide (TMZ), an alkylating agent, is currently the first-line chemotherapy for GBM, which induces tumor cell death by causing DNA damage at multiple sites via alkylation [87]. However, approximately 50% of patients with GBM do not benefit from TMZ treatment [88]. PARPi has been extensively studied in the treatment of GBM [89]. Olaparib resensitizes TMZ-resistant GBM cells to TMZ [90], due to its selective effects on ETC inhibition of complex I [91]. Because GBM patients have few BRCA mutations, it is speculated that the anti-GBM activity of olaparib may be at least partially independent of DNA repair. As fitter mitochondria in TMZ-resistant GBM cells are directly responsible for chemoresistance, olaparib reduces mitochondrial metabolism specifically by inhibiting ETC complex I in TMZ-resistant cells. This response is selective and dose-dependent [91]. Since NADH is the core component of complex I, olaparib acts as an NAD+ analog to compete with NADH. Thus, the mechanism for olaparib to resensitize TMZ-resistant GBM cells involves the combinational effects of OXPHOS and DNA repair inhibition. PARPi also restores TMZ cytotoxicity toward O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT)+ glioblastoma stem cells (GSC) in vitro and in vivo, independently of the classical base-excision repair function of PARPi. PARP regulates MGMT activity via PARylation which enhances the binding of MGMT and DNA in response to TMZ. In contrast, PARPi inhibits MGMT PARylation and eliminates O6-MetG repair, thereby sensitizing MGMT+ GSC to TMZ [92].

Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant tumors account for approximately 12% of all glioma cases in the US [93]. IDH mutation disrupts the conversion of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and thus facilitates the production of large amounts of the "cancer metabolite" D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2HG) [94]. In addition, IDH1 mutation alters the canonical metabolic pathway [95], including glutamine catabolism and the TCA cycle. IDH1-mutant tumor cells often have a high density of mitochondria and increase mitochondrial activity [95]. As mitochondrial inhibitors, olaparib and metformin can cause a breakdown in metabolic homeostasis in IDH-mutant cells, leading to metabolic lethality [91]. The introduction of mutant IDH1 reduces the expression of the rate-restricting enzyme Naprt1 in the NAD+ salvage system, leading to a reduction in basal NAD+ levels [96]. Notably, PARPi can counteract NAD+ biosynthesis inhibition [91,97], providing a potential rationale for combining TMZ and PARPi to treat IDH-mutant tumor cells.

The classical immune checkpoint inhibitors (namely anti-PD-1/PDL-1 and anti-CTLA4 antibodies) have no therapeutic effect on GBM. The potentially effective strategy is to target TAMs, including promoting phagocytosis against CD47, regulating TAM phenotype by nanoimmunocouples, and inhibiting the recruitment of TAMs by aptamers [98]. Olaparib induces differentiation, maturation, and activation of macrophages as well as causes macrophage accumulation in triple-negative breast cancer [99]. Similarly, olaparib induces CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activation in vivo, while CD8+ T-cell depletion severely compromises the anti-tumor efficacy of PARPi [100]. The metabolic reprogramming of TAMs induced by olaparib includes glycolysis increase, OXPHOS passivation, induction of mitochondrial reverse electron transfer, and ROS increase, which promote the cytotoxicity and phagocytosis of TAMs. Such reprogramming of TAMs contributes to the anti-tumor efficacy of PARPi in BRCA1-associated breast cancer [84]. However, the combinational effects of TMZ and PARPi on the immune microenvironment need to be further explored, which may represent a direction for future research involving drug resistance (Fig. 4A).

Ischemic stroke

Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide [101], which can cause irreversible neuronal damage due to ischemia and hypoxia in brain tissue. Neurons and glial cells can only survive for up to several hours by using ATP produced by glycolysis in the absence of oxygen [102]. Of note, the lack of oxygen and glucose supply results in an almost complete depletion of ATP in a few minutes [103]. Thus, unlike most other tissues, brain tissue almost entirely relies on glucose for its energy supply. PARP1 enzyme activity is significantly increased in brain tissue in response to transient (∼5 min) cerebral ischemia and ischemia-reperfusion [104]. Excessive activation of PARP1 can deplete NAD+ and ATP, leading to necrosis [34]. Indeed, PARP1 activation is a major cause of mitochondrial depolarization and subsequent mitochondrial-triggered cell death in ischemia-reperfusion. PARP1 depletes cytoplasmic NAD+, thereby blocking the production of glycolytic ATP and the transport of glucose carbon to mitochondria for OXPHOS [105]. Ischemia‒reperfusion increases poly-ADP-nucleotidylation in the brain, in association with the pathophysiological consequences of stroke [106]. In a mouse model of middle cerebral artery ischemia, treatment with the PARPi PJ34 improves recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA)-induced reperfusion and promotes vascular protection, reflected by reduced vascular inflammation, hemorrhagic transformations, and middle cerebral artery vasospasm. In addition, combined treatment with PJ34 and rt-PA significantly reduces edema, cortical damage, and sensorimotor deficits [107]. Prolonged incubation with PJ-34 (>1 hour) can effectively enhance baseline NADH before hypoxia and increase NADH hyperoxidation and ATP production, improving neuronal recovery after severe hypoxia and reoxygenation. In the models of acute severe neurological dysfunction, prolonged incubation with niacinamide or PJ-34 before hypoxia also enhances NADH reduction and ATP production, thereby improving the recovery of neuronal function. Unfortunately, during or after prolonged periods of hypoxia and reoxygenation, neither treatment restores the function of neurons [105]. Early treatment with another PARPi JPI-289 has also been demonstrated to reduce infarct size and improve long-term behavior in a rat model of ischemic stroke [108]. These effects may also involve the modulation of the immune microenvironment around ischemic tissues [109]. In monkey models with transient and permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), MP-124 (a PARP1 inhibitor) significantly reduces total infarct volumes, involving the cortex, white matter, and striatum by 22%, 54%, and 64%, respectively. This significantly reduces overall neurological deficits [110]. The use of PARPi in patients with stroke may also provide long-term benefits, possibly via reducing apoptosis and providing neuronal protection through reducing ATP depletion (Fig. 4D).

Alzheimer's disease

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, one of the deadliest diseases [111]. Immunostaining has revealed high levels of PARP1 and PAR in the brain tissue of AD patients [112]. Hyperactivation of PARP1 is also observed in primary cultured mouse microglia and rat hippocampal area [36,113].

AD is featured by the release of Aβ polypeptides in the form of monomers/oligomers, leading to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic loss, neuroinflammation, and thus PARP1 activation [114]. Several studies have suggested that β-amyloid-induced neuronal death is mediated by PARP, while mitochondrial dysfunction plays an important role in the onset and development of AD and is considered a primary event in the pathogenesis of AD, representing a therapeutic target [35,115]. The ability of PARPi to counteract bioenergy impairment indicates that PARPi may be used as an early intervention [116]. The presence of PARP1 in mitochondria is related to the maintenance of DNA integrity and mitochondrial energy depletion [117]. Activation of PARP1 enhances age-related endothelial dysfunction and neurovascular decoupling by reducing the availability of NAD+, thus exacerbating cognitive decline. The cerebral microvascular protective effect of PARP1 inhibitors highlights their preventive and potentially therapeutic property via restoring NAD+ homeostasis in vivo, suggesting an effective intervention for patients at risk of vascular cognitive impairment [118]. Exposure to β-amyloid reduces oxygen consumption, glucose uptake, and hyperactivation of PARP1 in cortical astrocytes. PARPi (e.g., DPQ and 3-AB) not only limits β-amyloid-induced NAD(P)H depletion and restores oxygen consumption, but also prevents slow mitochondrial depolarization induced by β-amyloid and restores NADH pools in hippocampal neurons [36]. DPQ also significantly reduces amyloid-induced neuronal cell death. In SH-SY5Y and 7PA2 cells treated with Aβ25–35, there is a significant decrease in COX IV protein levels, with inhibition of OXPHOS and an increase in anaerobic glycolysis. These metabolic changes can be partially reversed by PARPi (e.g., olaparib and MC2050), probably due to the reactivation of OXPHOS [116]. In addition, flavonoids can also prevent the progression of AD by reducing NAD+ degradation and affecting the NAD+-sirtuin axis [119]. Pyruvate inhibits Aβ oligomer-induced PARP1 activation while ameliorating Aβ oligomer-induced reduction in NAD+ levels, thereby preventing neuronal death and cognitive impairment in a rat model of AD [120]. These findings indicate a strong correlation between PARPi, NAD+, and AD. Genetic, molecular, and biochemical approaches have been integrated to investigate two different PARPi (olaparib and MC2050) in a Drosophila model of AD, to explore their in vivo neuroprotective and therapeutic activity. Pharmacological inhibition or gene inactivation of PARP1 is effective in significantly prolonging the life span and improving the climbing ability of transgenic AD Drosophila. Consistently, PARPi significantly reduces Aβ42 aggregates and partially salvages AD-related epigenetic changes in the brain. Interestingly, PARPi also inhibits abnormal activation of AD-associated transposable factors in neuronal tissue of AD Drosophila [121]. In PARP1 knockout mice, injection of Aβ fails to promote microglial activation, while treatment with PJ34 attenuates microglial activation [113]. The effect of PARPi on AD involves both energy metabolism and the immune microenvironment.

Considering the important effect of PARP1 on energy metabolism, PARPi has the potential ability to enhance mitochondrial function and thus alleviate the impairment of energy metabolism in AD brain tissue (Fig. 4C). Thus, PARPi may represent an effective therapeutic strategy for AD patients.

Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative motor disease caused by partial failure of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. Its typical neuropathological features include loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and abnormal accumulation of protein α-synuclein (α-syn) in the cytoplasm and neurogenic inclusions [122]. Polyadenosine 5′-diphosphate-ribose (PAR) production accelerates the formation of pathological α-syn, leading to cell death via parthanatos, while PARPi or genetic PARP1 deletion prevents the toxicity of pathological α-syn [35]. PAR levels are elevated in cerebrospinal fluid and brain tissue of PD patients, suggesting the role of PARP activation in the pathogenesis of PD [35]. PARP gene mutation improves mitochondrial function and neuroprotection in a PINK1-mutant model of PD in Drosophila. PARP gene mutation reduces the overall PARP activity and attenuates the up-regulation of protein PARylation in this model. Moreover, PARP mutation also inhibits the loss of Δψm, restores complex I-mediated respiration, and reduces defects in mitochondrial morphology in PINK1-mutant Drosophila [123]. Whereas Sarm1, a major regulator of programmed axonal degeneration, can induce energy consumption by decomposing NAD+, PARPi (e.g., PJ34) restores the activity of mitochondrial complex I by supplementing NAD+ and preventing intracellular Sarm1 activation induced by rotenone. In Drosophila, PARPi reduces rotenone-mediated motor loss and Sarm1 expression, suggesting the repurposed use of PARPi to treat mitochondrial complex I-deficient diseases [124]. Impaired energy metabolism and reduced ATP levels due to mitochondrial damage are common features of PD. Terazosin (TZ) can stimulate glycolysis and thus increase cellular ATP levels. In toxin-induced and genetic PD models in mice, rats, fruit flies, and pluripotent stem cells, TZ increases ATP levels in the brain, slowing down or stopping the loss of neurons. Consistently, individuals who received TZ and its related agents have slow PD progression, reduced PD-related complications, and less PD diagnosis [125]. These findings suggest that increasing glycolysis may slow down neurodegeneration during PD. Thus, it is worth anticipating that the administration of PARPi may improve glycolysis and ATP in PD patients [126]. Together, inhibition of PARP activation as a potential therapy holds promise for preventing the loss of dopamine neurons in PD (Fig. 4B).

Although olaparib [127], veliparib, niraparib [92], talazoparib, and pamiparib [128] (most of them have entered clinical trials) are known to be able to cross the BBB, permeabalization of small molecule inhibitors remains a challenge in the treatment of neurological diseases. Given the fact that better results for PARPi in combination with TMZ have been obtained from in vitro experiments or animal models of subcutaneously-grafted tumors, it is anticipated that using nanomaterials to deliver these agents would improve BBB permeability and increase therapeutic efficacy for treating GBM and other neurological diseases such as stroke, AD, and PD.

Effects of PARPi on cardiovascular diseases via metabolic interference

Metabolic syndrome and diabetes significantly increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality [129]. Cardiac energy-metabolic disorders also markedly influence the heart structure and function. In cells (particularly cardiomyocytes), the NAD+/NADH ratio is crucial for maintaining reductive oxidation (redox) homeostasis and regulating energy metabolism [130]. Moreover, inhibition of PARP prevents the activation of multiple vascular injury-related pathways induced by hyperglycemia [131]. Thus, both PARPi and NAD+ have significant preventive and therapeutic effects on metabolic syndrome, heart failure, ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, arrhythmia, and hypertension [132] (Fig. 4G).

Hyperglycemic injury in aortic endothelial cells is derived from GAPDH inhibition via its poly (ADP-ribosyl) ation by PARP. Notably, hyperglycemia increases the polymerization (ADP-ribosylation) of GAPDH in the aorta of wild-type mice, but not PARP1-deficient mice. Blockade of PARP activity by its competing inhibitors PJ34 or INO-1001 prevents the activation of the hyperglycemia-induced vascular injury pathway [131]. In contrast, increased PARP activity reduces cardiac NAD+ levels and increases mitochondrial protein acetylation, thereby exacerbating cardiac damage [132]. Consistently, 25-HC protects cardiomyocytes from IR damage by inhibiting PARP activity [133]. Immunohistochemical staining has shown that PARP1 activity in myocardial cells is significantly higher in ischemic myocardium after myocardial infarction than in the sham control [134]. Diabetes exacerbates IR-induced myocardial dysfunction through down-regulation of BAG3, Bcl-2, Nrf-2, and HO-1, while increasing p22, p67, caspase 3/PARP-mediated apoptosis, and oxidative damage, but impairing microvascular reactivity [135]. NAD+ and ATP levels are decreased in myocytes exposed to various forms of oxidative stresses as well as in the ischemic/reperfused heart, events attenuated by PARP1 deficiency or PARPi [136]. PARPi prevents the development of left ventricular hypertrophy in the animal model of spontaneous hypertension. Treatment with the PARP inhibitor l-2286 is beneficial to the structural stability of mitochondria, reflected by decreased fragmentation of the interfibrous mitochondrial network, increased mean size of mitochondria, and higher ridge density. l-2286 can also preserve mitochondrial genome integrity. In addition, mitochondrial biogenesis is enhanced, leading to an overall increase in ATP production and an improvement in stressed cell survival [137]. PARP activation as well as free oxygen and nitrogen radicals also play important roles in arterial restenosis after angioplasty. In a rat model of carotid intima balloon-induced vascular injury, daily administration of PJ34 inhibits leukocyte infiltration through vascular endothelial cells, de novo intima formation, and carotid restenosis (40%), while increasing NO levels and improving functional recovery [138].

In line, PARPi is beneficial in the treatment of acute and chronic cardiovascular diseases in both preclinical and clinical settings [139], although the safety (particularly chronic side effects) of PARPi and its potential interactions with other therapies remain to be defined.

Effects of PARPi on digestive system diseases via metabolic interference

The functions of the gastrointestinal tract and liver, such as absorption and metabolism, are crucial for maintaining energy homeostasis. Impaired barrier integrity and increased gut permeability, typical signs of inflammatory bowel disease, are associated with intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) death, mitochondrial dysfunction, and depleted energy metabolism in IECs. PARP1 activation blocks glycolysis [37], a "last safe way" of terminating energy production, which triggers the death pathway to cause intensive mucosal damage with severely impaired barrier function and ulceration. Olaparib reduces morphological changes and barrier permeability to maintain barrier integrity in H2O2-treated Caco-2 cell monolayers, in association with enhanced glycolysis (e.g., the rate of extracellular acidification) and improved mitochondrial function (e.g., mitochondrial coupling efficiency, maximum respiration and reserve respiration) in epithelial cells. Olaparib also improves the integrity of the intestinal barrier by preventing energy breakdown in experimental celiac disease, suggesting a repurposed use for treating Crohn's disease (Fig. 4F) [126]. In addition, PARPi has also been used to treat colorectal cancer. The PARPi talazoparib can induce cell senescence by inhibiting p53 ubiquitination and thus activating p21. The combination of talazoparib and palbociclib is synergistic through both tumor cell killing and immunomodulatory mechanisms. Furthermore, the combination with PD-L1 antibodies may be a promising "one-two punch" therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer [140].

Liver-specific metabolic adaptation leads to increased fat load in the peripheral blood [141], which may promote hepatocytic mitochondrial dysfunction due to chronic fat accumulation. A negative correlation between PARP1 and β-oxidizing gene transcripts has been observed in the liver [74]. Using a high-fat high-sucrose (HFHS) diet to induce NAFLD in mice induces protein PARification, in association with decreased NAD levels and impaired mitochondrial function, correlating with increased liver fat content. Administration of PARPi together with the HFHS diet reverses NAFLD by supplementing with NAD, thereby increasing mitochondrial biogenesis and beta-oxidation while reducing ROS, ER stress, and fibrosis in the liver [142]. The effect of PARPi treatment has also been demonstrated in mice fed with methionine- and choline-deficient diets or with lipopolysaccharide-induced hepatitis. In addition to improving mitochondrial function, PARPi also inhibits fat synthesis and enhances beta-oxidation, while improving HFHS-induced fatty acid metabolism (fatty liver) and systemic glucose homeostasis (Fig. 4E). In both models, PARP activation is inhibited, with a delay of liver injury [50].

Conclusion and future perspectives

PARPs are involved in metabolic signaling, DNA damage, oxidative stress, mitochondrial stress, and many other metabolic events. PARP enzymes regulate metabolism-related transcription factors and energy sensors. The activation of PARP leads to the depletion of NAD+, reducing energy production by impairing both glycolysis and OXPHOS. Thus, PARP is involved in the regulation of high-order metabolism and the pathogenesis of various metabolic diseases. With the rapidly increasing use of PARPi in the clinic, the effects of PARPi on energy metabolism have attracted more attention. In this review, we summarize the studies that have used PARPi to target energy metabolism, including glycolysis, OXPHOS, and fat metabolism. These studies have mainly focused on cancer and non-malignant diseases involving cardiovascular, nervous, digestive systems, and other organs.

PARPi can enhance glycolysis and thus be repurposed to treat mitochondrial complex I-deficient disorders. A PARPi-related surge in NAD+ increases glycolysis and has the potential to promote fatty acid β oxidation and improve mitochondrial function. On the one hand, OXPHOS is important for maintaining high ATP levels and PARP activity in HRD cells, and thus HRD cells are more sensitive to OXPHOS inhibition. In ovarian cancer cells with genetic inactivation of BRCA1, metformin can restrict the production of mitochondria-dependent biosynthetic intermediates, providing a new avenue for "starvation" as a chemoprophylaxis strategy in patients with BRCA1 mutations. On the other hand, either inhibition of OXPHOS or enhancement of glycolysis reduces the sensitivity of HRD cells to PARPi. The efficacy of PARPi depends on the metabolic characteristics of cells, with increased glycolysis as an alternative mechanism for PARPi resistance. Therefore, analyzing the metabolic profile of tumor cells may help predict the response or resistance to PARPi. Blockade of glycolysis resensitizes tumor cells to PARPi, providing a potential alternative strategy to reverse PARPi resistance. Olaparib has a specific inhibitory effect on the ETC complex I, which provides a potential rationale for combining olaparib and TMZ to treat GBM with IDH mutations or TMZ-resistant GBM. For chronic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, the negative effect of PARP activation on mitochondrial function may be an important mechanism for PARP-mediated diseases. Therefore, a better understanding of the complex network connecting PARPs and mitochondria will have clear therapeutic implications and thus warrants further investigation.
