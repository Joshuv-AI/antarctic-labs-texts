A Case of Slow Progressive Cutaneous Angiomatosis on a Cat Metatarsal Pad Treated by a Topical Beta‐Blocker
Authors: Adrien Accard, Rita Bitar, Maria Dolores Sanchez, Philippe Blumstein, Olivier Albaric, Nora Bouhsina, Emmanuel Bensignor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 5‐year‐old female cat presented with a purple depressed lesion on a metatarsal pad inducing ulceration and pain. Histopathological results revealed a non‐neoplastic vascular proliferation, a rare condition in cats. Topical beta‐blocker successfully prevented recurrence of ulceration. This case highlights the emerging use of topical beta‐blockers in both veterinary and human dermatology.Keywords: angiomatosis, beta‐blocker, cat

Introduction

In veterinary medicine, non‐neoplastic tumour‐like vascular proliferations are subdivided into angiomatosis and hamartomas. Angiomatosis refers to a heterogeneous group of vascular proliferative lesions, including both vascular proliferations and vascular malformations. In humans, the main dermatological clinical entity is “progressive cutaneous angiomatosis”, which is very rare in cats and uncommon in dogs. Vascular hamartoma is also a tumour‐like vascular proliferation, the main clinical entity being scrotal‐type hamartoma, which is reported occasionally in dogs. The definitions of angiomatosis and vascular hamartoma overlap considerably in the veterinary literature and are expected to become more precise as additional documented cases are reported [1]. Here, we describe the first case of slow progressive cutaneous angiomatosis successfully treated with a topical beta‐blocker in a cat.

Case Description

A 5‐year‐old neutered female domestic short hair cat was referred with a recurrent ulcerative, haemorrhagic lesion measuring 1 cm × 1 cm on the central metatarsal paw pad of the right pelvic limb, causing moderate pain and lameness. The precise time course was unknown. The referring veterinary surgeon prescribed compressive bandaging, twice‐daily topical antiseptic applications using chlorhexidine, and oral amoxicillin‐clavulanate (Clavaseptin; Vetoquinol) at 20 mg/kg twice daily for 14 days. A good clinical response was observed, with healing of the ulcerated lesion and resolution of haemorrhage and pain. At the 1‐month follow‐up, the resulting scar tissue was described as a 1 cm × 1 cm purple, depressed lesion.

Histopathological examination of a skin biopsy sample showed nonspecific active chronic proliferative and ulcerative dermatitis. Prednisolone (Dermipred; CEVA) at 1 mg/kg orally twice daily was prescribed for 30 days, yet showed no clinical improvement. Although the precise onset of the lesion is unknown, it had been identified by the referring veterinary surgeon 9 months before our examination.

Physical examination was normal. Dermatological examination revealed a unique dark red‐to‐purple depressed lesion as described previously (Figure 1a). Differential diagnoses included neoplasia (plasmacytoma, haemangioma, and haemangiosarcoma), deep fungal infection, eosinophilic granuloma complex, vasculitis, and vascular proliferation. Owner consent was granted for all subsequent examinations. Complete blood count (CBC) was within normal limits, and rapid testing for feline immunodeficiency virus and feline leukaemia virus yielded double negative results. Cutaneous cytological results showed no signs of infectious agents. A cutaneous biopsy was collected under general anaesthesia using a 6 mm diameter biopsy punch and submitted for histopathological evaluation.

Photographs of the metatarsal lesion (a) at first presentation, showing a well‐delimited dark red‐to‐purple and depressed, lesion on the paw pad, and (b) when treatment was stopped by the owner after 4 months therapy with timolol gel 0.1% (Geltim LP 1 mg/g [Thea Pharma] applied twice daily as a thin layer over the entire lesion) showing recurrence of ulceration and haemorrhage.

Histopathological examination showed a medium and deep proliferative, multilobulated dermal lesion whose growth pattern could not be precisely defined owing to the absence of peripheral healthy tissue on the examined sample. A proliferation of vascular structures of variable appearances was observed: anfractuous lacunae with irregular contours (Figure 2a), capillaries, and papillary‐type intravascular structures (sometimes with intravascular arterioles or venules). These structures regularly contained small numbers of red blood cells and were delineated by a well‐differentiated endothelium with low mitotic activity, fewer than one mitotic figure per 10 fields (×400; Figure 2b). On the basis of these findings, a diagnosis of angiomatosis was established [1, 2].

(a,b) Photomicrographs of sections from the biopsied tissue sample showing medium and deep proliferative dermal lesions, consisting of a proliferation of vascular structures of different sizes and types. Capillaries, arterioles, venules (arrowheads), and papillary‐type intravascular structures (black star), often observed within large, irregular lacunae (arrows). All vascular structures are lined by very well‐differentiated endothelial cells, without atypia or mitotic figures. Haematoxylin & eosin staining, ×10 (a) and ×40 (b).

A computed tomography (CT) scan was performed using a Siemens Somatom goUp helical device (130 kVp, 150 mAs), centred on both distal pelvic limbs. Pre‐ and postintravenous contrast series were acquired with a bone and soft tissue reconstruction filter. CT images revealed significant, irregular soft‐tissue thickening of the metatarsal pad wall in the laterodistal region, measuring approximately 3.4 mm in the medio‐lateral axis, 7.7 mm in the proximo‐distal axis and 9 mm in the dorso‐plantar axis. After injection, it enhanced markedly and homogeneously (Figure 3). Vascular structures were normal, in particular the dorsal arteries. These observations confirmed that the lesion did not invade adjacent tissues and was confined to the pad.

Sagittal plane computed tomography image of post‐contrast right hind limb at the level of the metatarsal pad in soft tissue window. Note the marked and homogeneous enhancement (red arrow) with normal adjacent vascular structures.

Noninvasive therapy was then initiated with a topical beta‐blocker timolol gel 0.1% (Geltim LP 1 mg/g; Thea Pharma) applied twice daily as a thin layer over the entire lesion for 2 months. At the 2 month follow‐up, the lesion remained stable with no evidence of progression or new ulceration. The cat exhibited no clinical signs of lameness or bleeding during this period. However, 4 months after starting therapy, treatment was discontinued by the owner, and recurrence of ulcers and lameness were noted 2 weeks later (Figure 1b). Reintroduction of the same protocol resulted in good but partial remission at the 4 week follow‐up. Unfortunately, the patient died from an unknown cause before achieving complete clinical resolution of the recurrence.

Discussion

Progressive cutaneous angiomatosis is very rarely described in cats, with only three documented cases to date, to the best of our knowledge [3, 4, 5]. The case reported here is unusual because of its noninvasive behaviour. This distinctive feature, along with the associated clinical signs, led us to try a topical beta‐blocker agent, which has been an approach increasingly adopted in human dermatology as an alternative to the more frequently reported medical treatments and surgery [6].

The lesion in this case was diagnosed as cutaneous progressive angiomatosis according to diagnostic criteria reported by Gross et al. and Abrama et al. [1, 2] These include: involvement of the deep dermis, a lobular pattern, endoluminal papillary growth, normal connective tissue interposed between vascular lesions, multiple types of vascular structures, and endothelium devoid of atypia and mitotic figures. Additional criteria, such as infiltrative growth or arteriovenous shunts, could not be ruled out, as only a biopsy sample was submitted for histological examination.

Digital progressive cutaneous angiomatosis typically exhibits a progressive and destructive course, with bone lysis and rapid proximal progression. In other locations, angiomatosis appears less aggressive yet remains progressive and may become generalised after several years. All previously reported cases of cutaneous angiomatosis in cats were successfully managed with surgical resection (two amputations and one resection of the metatarsal pad) with no sign of recurrence during follow‐up time [3, 4, 5]. In our case, complete resection of the metatarsal pad with or without pad transposition was discussed, but not accepted as a first‐line procedure, given the noninvasive and very slow progressive nature of the lesion. Other therapeutic options reported in dogs were also discussed with the owner. Laser photocoagulation therapy could have been an interesting noninvasive option as it appears to yield good clinical results. However, it requires frequent sessions and recurrences are common once treatment is discontinued [7]. Radiotherapy has been used as an alternative to surgery in cases of inoperable progressive cutaneous angiomatosis, resulting in complete or partial remission, with stable lesions at 12 month follow‐up [8]. One canine case with multicentric, disseminated, and infiltrative lesions did not exhibit progressive behaviour, remaining stable for 6 years without any treatment [9].

Other alternatives have been reported in human medicine, including the use of timolol gel or propranolol gel, both showing good results on vascular proliferations (haemangioma and vascular hamartoma) [6, 10]. Among these, the use of topical beta‐blockers is increasingly used in infantile haemangioma, with a growing number of indications. The suspected mechanisms of beta‐blockers in angiomatosis are vasoconstriction and decreased angiogenesis, primarily through the inhibition of vascular endothelial growth factor and basic fibroblast growth factor. Topical beta‐blockers provide significant clinical improvement with moderate local adverse effects in 90% of cases. Reported concentrations range from 1%, 2%, or 4% propranolol gel or cream to 0.5% timolol gel. It has been reported that absorption can vary significantly depending on the galenic formulation used. Hydrogel is particularly used for infantile haemangioma, whereas eye drops are preferred for chronic or acute wound healing, pyogenic granulomas, and tufted angiomas [10]. Typically, twice‐daily application is required, yet protocols vary greatly, and remission is gradual (partial remission after 12 weeks, maximum effect reached after several months) [6, 10].

Even though this treatment was proven safe for infantile haemangiomas, its potential adverse effects and contraindications are not to be overlooked. Contraindications include severe asthma, bradycardia, atrio‐ventricular block, cardiac failure, ischaemic heart disease, and venous obstruction [10].

In our case, we observed a good response to topical beta‐blocker gel therapy, with no ulceration relapse during the treatment. However, discontinuation after 4 months led to a rapid recurrence, which responded well to the re‐administration of the same protocol. No adverse effects were observed in our case. In conclusion, further studies are needed to appreciate the relevance of topical beta blockers in cases of progressive cutaneous angiomatosis with non‐ or minimally invasive behaviour.
