Long-time behavior of solutions to fluid dynamic shape optimization problems via phase-field method
Michael Hinze, Christian Kahle, John Sebastian H. Simon

Abstract
We investigate the long time behavior of solutions to a shape and topology optimization problem with respect to the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations. The sought topology is represented by a stationary phase-field that represents a smooth indicator function. The fluid equations are approximated by a porous media approach and are time-dependent. In the latter aspect, the considered problem formulation extends earlier work. We prove that if the time horizon tends to infinity, minima of the time-dependent problem converge towards minima of the corresponding stationary problem. To do so, a convergence rate with respect to the time horizon, of the values of the objective functional, is analytically derived. This allowed us to prove that the solution to the time-dependent problem converges to a phase-field, as the time horizon goes to infinity, which is proven to be a minimizer for the stationary problem. We validate our results by numerical investigation. Keywords. Navier–Stokes equations, shape optimization, phase-field method, long-time behavior MSC Codes. 35Q93, 35Q30, 76D55, 35R35

Introduction

We study the long-time behavior of solutions to shape and topology optimization problems governed by the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations. The optimization problem is formulated on a finite time horizon, whereas the shape variable is assumed to be time independent. Our primary interest lies in the convergence properties of the optimal shapes as the time horizon tends to infinity.

Shapes are represented implicitly by a phase-field variable
\varphi
defined on a fixed hold-all domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Within this phase-field framework, we consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u},\varphi}J_{T}(\mathbf{u},\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varphi+1}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right]+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)

subject to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\mathbf{u}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}&&\text{ in }\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\dive\mathbf{u}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\mathbf{u}&=\mathbf{g}&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{T}=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}(0)&=\mathbf{u}_{0}&&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}

This formulation can be interpreted as a tracking-type optimization problem for a target velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{d}
in the space
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, augmented by a perimeter regularization term expressed through the Ginzburg–Landau energy functional
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}
. The control acts as coefficient in the state equation.
For a detailed description of the model and assumptions, we refer to Section
2
.

Contribution

The highlight of this article is the inquiry of the asymptotic behavior of the optimal values of
J_{T}
. To do so, we compare the evaluations of
J_{T}
on their global optimizers against the optimal value of a corresponding problem with a stationary flow field, see Theorem
6.1
.
As a consequence, we show that global minimizers of
J_{T}
converge to a global minimizer of the stationary problem as
T\to\infty
, see Theorem
6.4
.

In addition, by incorporating time-dependent fluid dynamics, we extend earlier results from the stationary setting to the fully time-dependent framework.

Literature Review

Foundational contributions in the direction of optimization involving fluids were made in
[
2
]
and in
[
15
]
, where first-order optimality conditions for optimal control problems constrained by the Navier–Stokes equations were derived. These works laid the groundwork for gradient-based algorithms for approximating optimal controls in time-dependent fluid systems. Since then, a substantial body of literature has developed around optimization problems constrained by fluid dynamics, see, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
25
,
26
]
.

A particular class of such problems concerns the optimization of the shape of the fluid domain, commonly referred to as
shape optimization problems
. One of the earliest and most influential works in this direction is
[
40
]
, establishing first-order necessary conditions for drag minimization constrained by the Stokes equations. This seminal result inspired subsequent investigations, including
[
6
,
5
]
, where the differentiability of the drag functional with respect to domain variations were analyzed. More recent works, such as
[
16
,
30
,
32
,
41
]
, have further advanced this line of research by considering numerical implementations.

A defining feature of shape optimization problems is the variability of the underlying domain, which introduces both analytical and numerical difficulties. Analytically, the variation of the domain affects the functional spaces in which solutions are defined, thereby complicating convergence analyses. Numerically, modifying the domain during the solution process often necessitates specialized adaptations, making the problem more intricate and less tractable. To address these challenges, several authors have proposed reformulations in which the computational domain is fixed while the geometry of interest is embedded within it, i.e.,
the geometry is implicitly described through an auxiliary function. Among such approaches, the phase-field method has gained considerable attention. A series of works
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
applied the phase-field method to shape optimization problems governed by the stationary Navier–Stokes equations, demonstrating in particular its ability to accommodate topological changes in the fictitious domain.

A particular property that the previously mentioned works dealing with the phase-field approach to shape optimization is that the fluid is governed by the stationary Navier–Stokes equation. Although these works provide vital information for solving topology and shape optimization problems, the nonlinear nature of fluid dynamics gives rise to phenomena only observable in the unsteady case. A few examples of such are vortex shedding, transient separation and wakes
[
45
]
. We also mention the case where the fluid equations do not possess a stable equilibrium point
[
38
,
43
]
.

Finally, particular attention is drawn to the role of the design function
\varphi
in the state equation, that appears as a control in the coefficient. In the typical case where
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
depends linearly on
\varphi
, the problem resembles bilinear optimal control problems,
which have been applied, for example, to the modeling of ecological phenomena
[
34
,
35
]
and of the Bose-Einstein condensates and non-relativistic bosonic atoms and molecules
[
28
,
14
]
.

Motivation

The main advantage of using phase fields is that the shapes and the inherent topology of the domain are quantified by a design function. It is thus quite natural to deal with topological changes during the optimization process. Hence, the initial value for the optimization process need not necessarily reflect the topology of the found optimal shape.

Furthermore, we emphasize that the phase-field method, together with the present investigation, provides a pathway toward understanding the turnpike property in shape optimization. Essentially, the turnpike property in optimization is the phenomenon that the optimal trajectory spends a long-time near a steady-state, see, e.g.,
[
46
]
.

The phase-field approach offers a mathematically and numerically tractable representation of shape and topology.
Specifically, the current formulation leads to a first-order optimality system that directly links the optimal shape and optimal state. This does not only facilitate numerical solution but also enables a systematic quantification of shapes and topology, which may support future proofs of the turnpike property in shape optimization.

With respect to the convergence of shape with respect to time we are essentially building up on the works of
[
42
]
and
[
33
]
. In the former, the shape optimization problem is posed in the
sharp
setting where the analysis yields only a sensitivity analysis suitable for a steepest-descent algorithm. The latter attempted to establish the turnpike property for a heat-equation-constrained shape optimization problem and achieved numerical evidence.

The phase-field formulation resembles a bilinear optimal control problem, in which the control and state appear multiplicatively in the governing equation, as seen in Section
2
. The turnpike property for such bilinear problems is an active area of research, see, e.g.,
[
36
]
. Our work will also contribute to investigating the turnpike property in bilinear optimal control problems constrained by the Navier–Stokes equations.

Additionally, for this work we extend results for topology optimization using a phase field formulation for the stationary Navier–Stokes equation from
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
17
,
21
]
to the setting with
time-dependent
Navier–Stokes equations.
We analyze both the time-dependent optimization problem and its long-time behavior, thereby establishing a methodological framework with broader applicability. Notably, the analytical tools developed herein are not restricted to the specific problem at hand; they may also be adapted to alternative objective functionals or to other physical models, such as those studied in
[
23
,
22
,
4
]
, or to simpler systems such as the heat and Poisson equations.

2
Setup and model

We describe the shape and topology optimization problem using a phase field approach, following e.g. in
[
18
,
21
]
and we refer for a decent introduction of the model to this literature. We consider the problem with both time-dependent and stationary state equations.

Let
\Omega
denote a hold-all domain that is supposed to be divided into a fluid domain and an obstacle.
To utilize the so-called phase field method, we consider a smooth indicator function, that serves as a design function,
\varphi:\Omega\to[-1,1]
such that the set
\{x\in\Omega:\varphi(x)=1\}
indicates the fluid domain, while
\{x\in\Omega:\varphi(x)=-1\}
approximates the obstacle with a porous medium. Furthermore, the design function is assumed to change its value rapidly but continuously from
+1
to
-1
, which leads to an interface of thickness proportional to
\varepsilon>0
at the boundary of the fluid domain where
|\varphi|<1
. This thickness
\varepsilon
is a given modeling parameter.
In Figure
1
a sketch of the situation is drawn.

The introduction of the design function
\varphi
, which implicitly describes the fluid domain, calls us to introduce a model for the fluid equations that are now posed on the hold-all domain
\Omega
.
For this we consider a porous media approximation following
[
8
]
by assuming that the obstacle has a very small permeability
(\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\ll 1
.
Moreover we introduce a function
\alpha_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(-1)=\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon}
to interpolate between the fluid domain and the porous media.
Consequently, the state equations are defined in the whole domain
\Omega

and we respectively use the following state equations governing the time-dependent velocity-pressure pair
(\mathbf{u},p)
and the stationary velocity-pressure pair
(\mathbf{v},\pi)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\mathbf{u}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}&&\text{ in }\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\dive\mathbf{u}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\mathbf{u}&=\mathbf{g}&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{T}=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}(0)&=\mathbf{u}_{0}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\mathbf{v}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}+\nabla\pi&=\mathbf{f}_{s}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\dive\mathbf{v}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\mathbf{v}&=\mathbf{g}_{s}&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

Here
\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}
and
\mathbf{f}_{s},\mathbf{g}_{s}
are given external volume force and Dirichlet data for the time-dependent and stationary equations, respectively,
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is a fixed initial data, and
\mu>0
is the kinematic viscosity.

Figure 1
:
The geometrical setup using a phase field function
\varphi
.

The objective functionals that we consider is of tracking-type within the fluid domain, where we track a given stationary target velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{d}
in the
L^{2}
-norm within some given observation domain
\omega
, for which
\chi_{\omega}
denotes its indicator function.
The corresponding integral is extended to
\Omega
by using
\frac{\varphi+1}{2}
as indicator function for the fluid domain.

For the minimizers
\varphi
of the objective functionals to have the form of a phase-field, we shall consider the Ginzburg–Landau energy functional

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2c_{0}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Psi(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\right),

with some regularization constant
\gamma>0
to the objective.
Here
\Psi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a given potential with equal minima at
-1
and
1
, and
c_{0}>0
is a constant dependent only on
\Psi
in the sense that

\displaystyle c_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2\Psi(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Furthermore, from the definition of
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}
we see that admissible design functions belong to

\displaystyle\Phi_{ad}:=\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega):|\varphi(x)|\leq 1\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\}

and by definition, we have for any
\varphi\in\Phi_{ad}

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1.

(3)

From the introduction of the porous media approximation, we note that the contribution of fluid velocity on the interface may not be at all negligible. For this reason, we also consider a penalization for this contribution on the objective functional. Let us consider a function
{\beta}_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
possessing the same properties as
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
above, i.e., there exists
\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\ll 1
from which we define
\beta_{\varepsilon}
as
\beta_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}(-1)=\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
.

More precisely, we respectively consider the following objective functions
J_{T}
, for the time-dependent setting, and
J_{s}
, for the stationary setting:

\displaystyle J_{T}(\mathbf{u},\varphi)=\frac{1}{T}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varphi+1}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right]+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi),

(4)

\displaystyle J_{s}(\mathbf{v},\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varphi+1}{2}\right)|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi).

(5)

The main result of this work is the proof of the following theorem.

Claim 2.1
.

Let
\varphi^{T}
and
\varphi^{s}
be global minimizers of (
4
) and (
5
), respectively, and suppose that some meaningful assumptions are imposed on the data, then

\displaystyle|J_{T}(\varphi^{T})-J_{s}(\varphi^{s})|\leq\mathcal{F}(T)

for some function
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying
\mathcal{F}(T)\to 0
as
T\to+\infty
.

Remark 2.2
.

Problems (
4
) and (
5
) are approximations of the corresponding formal sharp problems

\displaystyle\min_{B}\,J_{T}(\mathbf{u},B)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{E}|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\gamma\mathrm{P}_{\Omega}(B)

subject to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\mathbf{u}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f}&&\text{ in }E_{T}:=E\times(0,T),\\
\dive\mathbf{u}&=0&&\text{ in }E_{T},\\
\mathbf{u}&=0&&\text{ on }\partial B_{T}:=\partial B\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}&=\mathbf{g}&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{T}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathbf{u}(0)&=\mathbf{u}_{0}&&\text{ in }E,\end{aligned}\right.

and

\displaystyle\min_{B}\,J_{s}(\mathbf{v},B)

\displaystyle:=\int_{E}|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x+\gamma\mathrm{P}_{\Omega}(B),

subject to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mu\Delta\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}+\nabla\pi&=\mathbf{f}_{s}&&\text{ in }E,\\
\dive\mathbf{v}&=0&&\text{ in }E,\\
\mathbf{v}&=0&&\text{ on }\partial B,\\
\mathbf{v}&=\mathbf{g}_{s}&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
B
denotes the obstacle,
E
denotes the fluid domain and
P_{\Omega}(B)
denotes the perimeter of
B
.
Formal matched asymptotics for
\varepsilon\to 0
are carried out for the stationary setting in
[
17
]
.
Especially,
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)

\Gamma
-convergences to the perimeter functional
P_{\Omega}(B)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, see
[
37
]
.

3
Preliminaries and standing assumptions

Let
X
be a Banach space with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, we denote its dual as
X^{*}
and we write
\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X}
for the dual pairing of
x\in X
and
x^{*}\in X^{*}
.
We denote by
L^{p}(D)
the space containing Lebesgue measurable and
p
-integrable functions on a measurable domain
D
. We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
the norm in
L^{p}(D)
, whenever the domain
D
is known. The
L^{2}
-inner product in a domain
D
shall be denoted as
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
, the Sobolev spaces in a domain
\Omega
will be denoted as
W^{m,p}(\Omega)
, with
H^{m}(\Omega)=W^{m,2}(\Omega)
. The norm in
W^{m,p}(\Omega)
is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{W^{m,p}}
.
We also consider zero trace Sobolev spaces which we denote as
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
or
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for
p=2
.
We write in bold letter, for instance
\boldsymbol{L}^{p}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{W}^{m,p}(\Omega)
, to emphasize spaces consisting of vector-valued functions.

To take into account the incompressibility of the fluid we consider the following solenoidal spaces

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}:=\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega):\dive\boldsymbol{\psi}=0\text{ in }\Omega\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}:=\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega):\dive\boldsymbol{\psi}=0\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega),\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

The spaces
\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
are respectively endowed with the norm

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}=\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\text{ and }\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}=\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}.

The pair
(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{H})
is known to satisfy the Gelfand triple, i.e., the embeddings
\boldsymbol{V}\hookrightarrow\boldsymbol{H}\hookrightarrow\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
are dense, continuous and compact. In fact, the compactness follows from Rellich–Kondrachov embedding and Schauder theorems.

We recall that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}
is equivalent to the norm in
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
due to Poincaré inequality, i.e., there exists a constant
c_{P}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\leq c_{P}\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\text{ for any }\mathbf{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

We explicitly write the Poincaré constant since it is necessary to keep track of the constants when proving the main result. We also recall the Ladyzhenskaya constant
c_{L}>0
that satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{4}}\leq\sqrt{c_{L}}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{1/2}\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{1/2}\text{ for any }\mathbf{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

For the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations, we consider functions which map
I_{T}=(0,T)
to Banach spaces
X
. The space of functions from an interval
I_{T}
to
X
that can be continuously extended to the closed interval
\overline{I_{T}}
will be denoted by
C(\overline{I_{T}};X)
.
The space
L^{p}(I_{T};X)
consists of functions
\psi:I_{T}\to X
such that
t\mapsto\|\psi(t)\|_{X}
belongs to
L^{p}(0,T)
, the norm in such space will be denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(X)}
.
Note that the elements of
L^{p}(I_{T};L^{p}(\Omega))
can be identified as elements in
L^{p}(\Omega_{T})
.

The spaces that we are particularly interested in are the following:

\displaystyle W_{T}^{p}(X):=\{v\in L^{2}(I_{T};X):\partial_{t}v\in L^{p}(I_{T};X^{*})\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}^{2,1}_{p,T}:=\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in L^{p}(I_{T};\boldsymbol{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)\cap\boldsymbol{V}\,):\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{p}(\Omega_{T})\}.

We see by the Aubin–Lions–Simon embedding theorem that
W_{T}^{2}(\boldsymbol{V}\,)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{I_{T}};\boldsymbol{H})
.
From Amman embedding theorem
[
3
, Theorem 3]
,
we get the compact embedding
\boldsymbol{W}^{2,1}_{2,T}\hookrightarrow C(\overline{I_{T}};\boldsymbol{V})
.

We conclude this section with standing assumptions. We start with the given data influencing the fluid equations.

Assumption 3.1
.

The given data acting on the fluid are assumed to satisfy

(i)

homogeneous Dirichlet boundary data, i.e.,
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}_{s}=0
;

(ii)

the given external forces satisfy
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(I_{T};\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega))
and
\mathbf{f}_{s}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

It is well-known that uniqueness of weak solutions to the stationary Navier–Stokes equations is not guaranteed unless smallness of external force or sufficient viscosity is imposed. We shall therefore impose the following assumption for the said uniqueness.

Assumption 3.2
.

The external force
\mathbf{f}_{s}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and the kinematic viscosity
\mu>0
satisfy

\displaystyle{2}c_{P}^{2}c_{L}\|\mathbf{f}_{s}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}<\mu^{2}.

(6)

To facilitate the convergence analysis required in our main result, we assume additional properties for the source functions.

Assumption 3.3
.

There exists
K>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{\gamma t}\|\mathbf{f}(t)-\mathbf{f}_{s}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq K,

for all
T>0
, where
\gamma:=\frac{\mu^{4}-2c_{L}^{2}c_{P}^{4}\|\mathbf{f}_{s}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}}{c_{p}^{2}\mu^{3}}>0
.

A direct computation shows that this implies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{L}^{2})}\leq\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{T}\|\mathbf{f}_{s}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}.

(7)

For the functions
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
, that describe the porous medium, we impose the following properties.

Assumption 3.4
.

The functions
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
satisfy

(i)

The functions
\alpha_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are nonnegative, satisfy
\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(1)=\beta_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
,
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(-1)=\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon}>0
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}(-1)=\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon}>0
, where
(\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon},\overline{\beta}_{\varepsilon})\to+\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(ii)

As operators on
\Phi_{ad}
,
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
are Fréchet differentiable in the
L^{2}
-norm. Specifically, we assume the existence of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}:\Phi_{ad}\to L^{p}(\Omega)
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}:\Phi_{ad}\to L^{p}(\Omega)
for
p\geq 4
, such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi)\delta\varphi=\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\delta\varphi\text{ and }\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi)\delta\varphi={\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\delta\varphi

for any
\delta\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and such that

\displaystyle\max\{\|{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\|_{L^{p}},\|{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\|_{L^{p}}\}\leq c_{\!\mathcal{A}}

for any
\varphi\in\Phi_{ad}
.

A consequence of Assumption
3.4
(i)
is that for any
\varphi\in\Phi_{ad}
both
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)
are in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Indeed, for
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)
, if
x\in\Omega
is such that
|\varphi(x)|\leq 1
then

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi(x))|=|\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi(x))-\alpha_{\varepsilon}(1)|\leq c|\varphi(x)-1|\leq c(|\varphi(x)|+1)\leq 2c

(8)

where the Lipschitz constant
c>0
is dependent neither on
x\in\Omega
nor on
\varphi\in\Phi_{ad}
. The same reasoning can be used for
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)
.

Additionally, the Fréchet differentiability of
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
implies their continuity on
\Phi_{ad}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm.

To analyze the optimization problem, we assume that the target profile and the double-obstacle potential satisfy the following properties.

Assumption 3.5
.

(i)

The target profile
\mathbf{u}_{d}
satisfies the regularity
\mathbf{u}_{d}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\omega)
.

(ii)

The potential
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is the double-obstacle potential defined as

\displaystyle\Psi(s)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Psi_{0}(s)&&\text{if }|s|\leq 1,\\
&+\infty&&\text{if }|s|>1.\end{aligned}\right.\quad=\Psi_{0}(s)+I_{[-1,1]}(s)

where
\Psi_{0}(s)=(1-s^{2})/2
and
I_{[-1,1]}(s)
is the convex indicator function of the admissible range
[-1,1]
.
The constant
c_{0}
has consequently the value
c_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Remark 3.6
.

We restrict our analysis to the two-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations due to the uniqueness and regularity issues for the three-dimensional (3D) case. To be precise, despite the weak-strong uniqueness results for solutions of the 3D Navier–Stokes, such result holds only locally in time.

4
Solutions of the governing equations

In this section we investigate and collect existence results of solutions to the time-dependent and the stationary Navier–Stokes equation, i.e., (
1
) and (
2
).

To simplify the notations, we introduce the bilinear operator
a_{\varphi}:\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\displaystyle a_{\varphi}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\mu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}x

and the trilinear operator
b:\boldsymbol{L}^{4}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{L}^{4}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, emanating from the nonlinearity in the Navier–Stokes equations, defined as

\displaystyle b(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=\int_{\Omega}[(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}]\cdot\mathbf{w}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The operator
a_{\varphi}
is continuous on
\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{V}
and coercive in
\boldsymbol{V}
, i.e. there exists
c>0
such that

\displaystyle c\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}^{2}\leq a_{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\text{ for any }\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{V}.

To establish such inequality, one would have to utilize the nonnegativity of
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)
.

On the other hand, the operator
b
is continuous on
\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{V}\times\boldsymbol{V}
. Furthermore, given
\mathbf{u}\in\boldsymbol{V}
the bilinear operator
b(\mathbf{u},\cdot,\cdot):\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
is antisymmetric, i.e.

\displaystyle b(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=-b(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})\text{ for any }\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Theorem 4.1

Aside from the energy estimates (

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that the assumptions in Theorem
4.2
hold. Assume further that there exists

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}(t)\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}^{2}\leq e^{A(t)}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}^{2}+\frac{2c_{P}^{2}}{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t)-A(\tau)}\|\mathbf{F}(\tau)\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}^{2}\leq\frac{c_{P}^{2}}{\mu}\|\mathbf{F}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{c_{L}^{2}}{\mu}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}^{2}

A direct application of Gronwall lemma gives us (

Proposition 4.6

Suppose that Assumption

(i)
hold,

Proof (Sketch).

The proof of Proposition

[

24

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi)(\nabla\varphi\otimes\mathbf{u}):\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{p_{1}}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{p_{2}}}\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\|\Delta\mathbf{u}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}},

6
Long time behavior of minima

The results above have now put us in a position to prove our main result. In Theorem

The steps required to prove such result utilize the established energy estimates of the involved state equations in Section

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{n}}\left[\left|\int_{\Omega_{T_{n}}}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}-\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega_{T_{n}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{T_{n}})(\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}})|\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\right.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{n}}(J_{1,1}+J_{1,2}+J_{1,3})+\frac{\gamma}{2\varepsilon c_{0}}J_{1,4},

where
\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}=\mathcal{S}_{T}(\varphi^{T_{n}})
and

) to get the following estimate for
J_{1,1}
using a generic constant
C_{1,1}
.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}J_{1,1}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega_{T_{n}}}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega_{T_{n}}}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1,1}\|\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}\|_{L^{2}}\left|\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\|\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{4}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|+T_{n}\left|\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}}{2}\right)|\mathbf{u}_{d}|^{2}\chi_{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

Now the first term tends to zero by the strong convergence of

in

.
For the second term we use

and the convergence (

) to show that in summary

as

.
Similar arguments show that
\frac{1}{T_{n}}J_{1,2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

, and the estimates (

) and (

), and proceed using a generic constant

.

\displaystyle J_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega_{T_{n}}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{T_{n}})(\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}})\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{T_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{T_{n}})(\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}})\|_{L^{2}}\|\mathbf{u}^{T_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{L}^{4})}\|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{T_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{L}^{4})}

where

\displaystyle J_{1,3}\leq C_{1,3}\|\varphi^{\infty}-\varphi^{T_{n}}\|_{L^{q}}(1+\sqrt{T_{n}}+T_{n})

where

is a constant not dependent on

. We thus have

as

.
Lastly, we have

as

by virtue of (

Similarly, we can again use the first order optimality

7
Numerical implementation and examples

This section is dedicated to numerically illustrate the convergence of solutions proved in Section
6
.
The equations involved in the optimization problems are solved using finite element method with the aid of the free software
FreeFem++

[
27
]
.

The state and adjoint equations are discretized using
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bubble finite elements for the velocity component, while we use
\mathbb{P}^{1}
elements for the pressure, i.e. we use the MINI-element.
To solve the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations we utilize an Implicit/Explicit method: we uniformly partition the interval
[0,T]
into
N_{T}
subintervals
(t^{n-1},t^{n})
and recursively solve the approximations
(\mathbf{u}^{n},p^{n})\approx(\mathbf{u},p)(t^{n})
using the difference equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\Delta t}(\mathbf{u}^{n}-\mathbf{u}^{n-1})+\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\mathbf{u}^{n}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}^{n}+(\mathbf{u}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}^{n}+\nabla p^{n}&=\mathbf{f}^{n}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\dive\mathbf{u}^{n}&=0&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\mathbf{u}^{n}&=\mathbf{g}^{n}&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

with
\mathbf{u}^{0}=\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\Delta t=t^{n}-t^{n-1}
. This method is a stable time-discretization method which in fact satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta t}\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\boldsymbol{V}}\leq c\left(\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}+\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{L}^{2})}+\|\mathbf{g}\|_{L^{2}(\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega))}\right)

where
c>0
continuously depends only on
\frac{1}{\mu\Delta t}
,
c_{P}
, and the continuity constant of the trace operator
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\boldsymbol{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.
An appropriate adjoint equation is also formulated that corresponds to the optimization problem and the semi-discretization above.

The stationary problem, on the other hand, is solved using Newton’s method for the nonlinear Navier–Stokes equations. The routine is terminated when the
\boldsymbol{V}
-norm of the difference between two consecutive approximations reaches a given tolerance
\texttt{tol}>0
.

All of the linear systems due to the finite element discretizations are solved with the sparse linear solver UMFPACK
[
11
]
.

To solve the discretized versions of the optimization problems (

P
s

) and (

P
T

), we use the
“variable metric projection-type”
(VMPT) method to circumvent the fact that the Fréchet derivative of
J_{T}
and
J_{s}
is defined on
\mathbb{X}:=H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
which is a non-reflexive Banach space, which prevents us from using a Riesz representative of the derivatives, commonly called as the gradient, which in turn prevents us from designing a gradient descent method to solve the optimization problem.

In each optimization iteration, the VMPT method calculates suitable descent directions by solving a projection type subproblem. In this way a descent direction is found, that corresponds to the gradient in case of a Hilbert space setting.
In our case these subproblems are linear-quadratic with bound constraints and thus are solved by
a primal-dual active set (PDAS) method. We base the VMPT method from
[
7
]
, while the PDAS method is inspired by
[
29
]
and use it both for the time-dependent problem (

P
T

) and the stationary problem (

P
s

). The iterative process is terminated when
the
H^{1}
-norm of the difference between two consecutive approximations reaches a given tolerance
\texttt{tol}>0
.

In all iterative processes, including the Newton’s method for the stationary Navier–Stokes equations and the approximation of the optimal phase-field, we use the value
\texttt{tol}=1\times 10^{-6}
for the tolerance.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the long-time behavior of solutions to a shape and topology optimization problem governed by the time-dependent Navier–-Stokes equations. The topology of the fluid domain was represented by a stationary phase-field variable acting as a smooth indicator function, while the fluid equations were formulated on a fixed hold-all domain using a porous media approximation. This setting extends earlier phase-field-based approaches for stationary flows to a fully time-dependent framework.

The main analytical result shows that, as the time horizon tends to infinity, global minimizers of the time-dependent optimization problem converge in objective value to a global minimizer of the corresponding stationary problem. Moreover, an explicit convergence rate with respect to the time horizon was derived.

The theoretical findings are complemented by numerical investigations, which confirm the predicted convergence behavior and illustrate the practical relevance of the asymptotic analysis. From a numerical point of view, the results suggest that stationary or long-time-averaged models may serve as effective surrogates for large-horizon time-dependent optimization problems, thereby offering potential reductions in computational cost. Overall, this work advances the analytical and numerical understanding of phase-field-based shape optimization for unsteady fluid flows and provides a foundation for further developments at the interface of variational methods, optimal control, and numerical optimization.

From a methodological perspective, the problem formulation exhibits a bilinear structure, as the design function enters the governing equations as a coefficient. The analysis therefore contributes to the understanding of asymptotic regimes in bilinear PDE-constrained optimization problems and provides a basis for investigating turnpike-type behavior in shape and topology optimization. The analytical tools developed herein are not limited to the specific tracking-type functional considered, but can be adapted to alternative objective functionals and to other evolutionary PDE models.
