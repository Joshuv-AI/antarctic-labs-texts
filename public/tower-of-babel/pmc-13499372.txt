Evaluation of alternative blood culture systems in detecting pathogenic bacteria: a multi-site, observational prospective study - author's reply
Authors: Yanyan Hu, Chang Cai, Rong Zhang, Timothy R. Walsh
Published: 2026

Firstly, we agree that overall concordance can be influenced by the high proportion of negative blood culture results, and that Positive Percent Agreement (PPA) may be informative as a complementary agreement measure. However, the interpretation of PPA depends on the role assigned to the comparator. In our study, no blood culture platform was designated as an independent gold standard, and discordant positive results were not equivalent to independently confirmed bloodstream infections (BSIs). Therefore, PPA should not be interpreted as sensitivity or as a direct missed-infection rate. The statement that a PPA of approximately 70% implies that one-third of detectable infections were missed is therefore not supported by the design of our study.

Secondly, this is the first study to independently and objectively evaluate blood culture (BC) bottles and instruments using real-world clinical specimens. Our study was a large-scale, real-world paired comparison rather than an in-vitro spiked-bottle experiments. Blood from each patient was collected simultaneously and distributed equally among bottles, ensuring that prior antibiotic exposure affected both systems equally. Therefore, although antibiotic use may influence absolute recovery rates, it is unlikely to bias comparative performance. We acknowledge the reduced recovery of fastidious and anaerobic organisms. However, these pathogens account for only a very minor proportion of BSIs, with anaerobes representing <1% of positive cultures,1,2 suggesting that the absolute clinical impact of differential detection is considerably limited.

Thirdly, blood volume (8–10 mL/bottle), simultaneous collection, and standardised transport and loading procedures were specified across sites, minimising pre-analytical bias. Our study evaluated the BACT/ALERT 3D workflow used during the study period; extrapolation to VIRTUO is beyond the study scope.

Fourthly, although guidelines recommend two to three blood culture sets, our objective was a paired comparison between systems rather than estimation of overall diagnostic yield.

Fifth, we agree that our study was not a formal non-inferiority trial.

Finally, we have re-checked Fig. 1 and supplementary Tables and corrected these minor numerical inconsistencies, which do not affect the study outcomes or our conclusions.

We do not agree that discordant results should be attributed solely to the use of non-validated bottles or interpreted as evidence of unreliable results. We have clarified that our conclusions apply to the participating hospitals, tested systems, bottle types, and workflows evaluated in this study, rather than representing a universal claim of interchangeability across all laboratory settings. It should also be noted that our team had no conflict of interest, were completely agnostic and treated all data dispassionately.
