Dual targeting of glutamine metabolism and lysosomal function in eliminating drug-tolerant KRAS-mutant cancer cells
Authors: Hiroki Furukawa, Keitaro Umezawa, Yuchen Sun, Hinata Chiba, Marin Kubonishi, Hayato Naito, Yuri Miura, Kousei Ito, Shigeki Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
KRAS inhibitors are reshaping the cancer-treatment landscape; however, durable responses remain limited by drug-tolerant persister cells that survive initial therapy and drive relapse. We show that KRAS-mutant pancreatic and lung cancer cells enter a reversible drug-tolerant (TR) state upon KRAS inhibition, marked by proliferative arrest and extensive metabolic adaptation. Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyses reveal lysosome-linked remodeling and relatively broad metabolic reprogramming in TR cells. Dual blockade of glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes selectively compromises TR-cell viability under KRAS inhibition, which is rescued by α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). N-acetyl-L-cysteine phenocopies the rescue, and α-KG supplementation lowers intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, supporting a model in which α-KG acts predominantly as a redox-supportive metabolite rather than a Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle intermediate, in the TR state, with lysosome-associated processes contributing to redox balance. These findings define drug-tolerant redox vulnerability and provide a rationale for co-targeting glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes during KRAS inhibitor therapy.

Introduction

Recent advances in molecular oncology have fundamentally reshaped the therapeutic landscape of cancer1,2. Targeted therapies directed at oncogenic driver mutations have catalyzed a paradigm shift from broadly cytotoxic regimens to precision medicine, allowing enhanced specificity and delivering markedly improved clinical outcomes2–4. Inhibitors targeting actionable alterations, such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and v-Raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF), have demonstrated profound clinical efficacy across multiple malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), melanoma, and hematologic cancers5–8. Comprehensive genomic profiling has substantially accelerated the discovery of oncogenic drivers, paving the way for developing personalized therapeutic strategies3,9,10. However, the emergence of drug resistance persists as a formidable and near-universal challenge4,11. Despite high initial response rates, most patients eventually experience relapse4,11. This often-transient efficacy is frequently driven by secondary genetic alterations, rewiring of adaptive signaling, or persistence of rare drug-tolerant cell subpopulations11,12. Although initially undetectable, these cells form the reservoir from which resistance emerges13,14. Therfore, understanding the mechanisms underlying their survival remains a critical frontier in oncology13,15.

Oncogenic KRAS is a highly frequently mutated driver in human cancers and has recently become a tractable therapeutic target16,17. Activating KRAS mutations, most notably G12C in NSCLC and G12D or G12V in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), lead to constitutive activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide-3-kinase signaling pathways, thereby driving tumor aggressiveness16,17. This progress has been enabled by breakthroughs in designing covalent and non-covalent inhibitors16,18. Selective inhibitors, such as sotorasib and adagrasib, have demonstrated clinical efficacy, particularly in KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC19,20. However, treatment responses are typically short-lived, with resistance arising via secondary KRAS mutations, pathway reactivation, or adaptive cellular states21,22. These challenges highlight the urgent need for strategies targeting the oncogenic driver and survival mechanisms that allow cancer cells to endure therapy15,21,23.

Genetic resistance mechanisms of cancer cells have been well-characterized; therefore, increasing attention has focused on non-genetic and reversible drug-tolerant states13,14. A central contributor to relapse is a rare subpopulation of cancer cells called drug-tolerant persister cells (DTPs) that survive initial drug exposure in the absence of resistance-conferring mutations13. DTPs adopt a slow-cycling and epigenetically plastic state that enables survival under sustained therapeutic pressure14,15. While distinct from terminal senescent or apoptotic cells, DTPs retain the capacity to re-enter the cell cycle and generate stably resistant clones14,15. These cells have been identified across multiple oncogene-targeted therapies, including those against EGFR, ALK, and BRAF, and have recently been implicated in resistance to KRAS inhibition23–25. Notably, post-treatment biopsies from patients relapsing on KRAS inhibitors frequently lack detectable resistance mutations, implicating DTP-like populations behind relapse21,23. Several molecular features of DTPs have been characterized including dependence on oxidative phosphorylation, autophagy, and chromatin remodeling; however, the precise mechanisms enabling their persistence under KRAS inhibition remain incompletely understood13,14,26. Most studies have focused on characterizing DTPs or delaying their progression to stable resistance13–15. In contrast, whether DTP-like states can be functionally eliminated while targeted therapy is maintained remains unresolved, and addressing this issue may be key to improving therapeutic durability. Tackling this challenge requires an in-depth understanding of DTP biology and identification of exploitable vulnerabilities that distinguish them from the bulk tumor population13,26.

In this study, we assessed whether KRAS inhibitor-tolerant cells can be selectively eliminated by targeting state-specific survival dependencies. Using in vitro models of KRAS-mutant NSCLC and PDAC, we established a reversible drug-tolerant (TR/DTP-like) state under KRAS inhibition and defined its metabolic dependencies. Whereas prior strategies have focused on suppressing residual disease or delaying the onset of resistance13,15, we intended to decipher whether the drug-tolerant reservoir itself represents an actionable therapeutic target prior to the emergence of stable resistant clones.

Results

KRAS G12C-mutant cancer cells enter a reversible drug-tolerant state under inhibitor treatment

To characterize the drug-tolerant reservoir that persists under KRAS G12C inhibition, we treated four KRAS G12C-mutant cancer cell lines, MiaPaCa2 (pancreatic), H358, H23, and H1792 (lung), with the covalent inhibitor sotorasib or adagrasib. In line with reported half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50; approximately 9 nM for sotorasib and 5 nM for adagrasib)18,27, both agents dose-dependently suppressed proliferation of MiaPaCa2, H358, and H23 cells (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Notably, substantial numbers of cells remained adherent and viable at ≥ 100 nM, suggesting a drug-tolerant subpopulation (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Across 30–1000 nM, population expansion was largely halted over 7 days; however, viable cells still persisted (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b). We defined TR cells as those surviving continuous exposure to 100 nM sotorasib or adagrasib for 7 days (schematized in Fig. 1c). In MiaPaCa2, H358, and H23 cells, TR populations showed delayed cell-cycle progression (Fig. 1d) and sustained suppression of MAPK and Akt signaling (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1c). Overt apoptosis or widespread cell death was not detected under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2), whereas senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity increased in a subset of cells (Fig. 1f). Importantly, upon drug withdrawal, TR cells gradually resumed proliferation and recovered parental growth kinetics (Fig. 1g). TR cells exhibit lysosome- and metabolism-enriched proteomic remodeling

To characterize adaptive features of TR cells under continuous KRAS inhibition, we performed quantitative liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry proteomics on TR cells and compared them with their parental MiaPaCa2 and H358 cells (Fig. 2a). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between TR and parental populations, indicating extensive proteomic remodeling (Fig. 2b). Differential expression analysis identified more than 200 upregulated proteins for each inhibitor (sotorasib or adagrasib) across both cell lines (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 3a). Among them, 168 (MiaPaCa2) and 185 (H358) proteins were commonly upregulated by both inhibitors, with 47 shared across both lines (Fig. 2d, e; Supplementary Table 1). Pathway enrichment of the shared set highlighted the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Lysosome pathway as the top hit, together with prominent enrichment of metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the full dataset corroborated lysosome- and metabolism- associated signatures in TR cells (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Consistent with these proteomic signatures, TR cells displayed increased lysosomal abundance, evidenced by elevated lysosome associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) staining, increased nuclear localization of TFEB (Fig. 2g, h), and enhanced LysoTracker signals (Supplementary Fig. 4). TR cells undergo metabolic reprogramming with enhanced mitochondrial features

To explore metabolic adaptations in TR cells, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling of glycolytic and mitochondrial intermediates. Lactate levels were reduced in both MiaPaCa2 and H358 TR cells, accompanied by accumulation of upstream glycolytic metabolites (e.g., glucose 6-phosphate and fructose 6-phosphate) in MiaPaCa2 and decreased Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA)-cycle intermediates (e.g., citrate and malate) in both cell lines (Fig. 3a, b). Consistently, direct quantification of lactate production revealed a marked reduction of glycolytic output in TR cells relative to that in parental counterparts (Fig. 3 c, Supplementary Fig. 5). TR cells exhibited increased mitochondrial membrane potential and a relatively highly fused/elongated network consistent with mitochondrial remodeling (Fig. 3d, e). As an ancillary functional test, TR cells did not display increased sensitivity to inhibition of ATP synthase by oligomycin (Supplementary Fig. 6), which was not necessarily indicative of increased reliance on oxidative phosphorylation. TR-cell survival is supported by α-KG-rescuable vulnerability under KRAS inhibition

To identify metabolic liabilities in TR cells, we performed a focused inhibitor survey targeting key nodes of mitochondrial metabolism (Fig. 4a). Across a panel of seven compounds, TR cells exhibited marked sensitivity to epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) that inhibits glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) (Fig. 4b). EGCG dose-dependently reduced TR-cell viability, whereas parental cells were relatively less affected (Fig. 4c). Notably, exogenous α-KG robustly and completely restored TR-cell viability (Fig. 4c, d), indicating that TR-cell survival under these conditions is strongly supported by α-KG-linked functions. EGCG did not markedly change steady-state abundances of TCA-cycle intermediates, including α-KG (Supplementary Fig. 7). Combined disruption of glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes selectively compromises TR-cell survival

To test whether TR cells rely on glutamine-supported metabolism for persistence under KRAS inhibition, we challenged them with glutamine withdrawal. Cell viability declined; however, a residual population persisted, indicating that glutamine deprivation alone was insufficient to eliminate TR cells (Fig. 5a, b). Given the lysosome-enriched proteomic signature of TR cells together with increased lysosomal programs (Fig. 2), we next assessed whether lysosome-associated processes contribute to TR-cell survival. Pharmacologic lysosomal inhibition with chloroquine (CQ) induced LC3 accumulation, consistent with impaired autophagy–lysosome degradation/flux (Fig. 5c). Similar results were obtained by substituting glutamine withdrawal with glutaminase 1 (GLS1) inhibition by CB-839 (Fig. 5a, e), whereas parental cells were relatively less affected under the same conditions (Fig. 5d, e). Comparable sensitization to dual inhibition was also observed in TR cells derived from H23 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8). To genetically corroborate the glutamine axis implicated by CB-839, we generated GLS1-knockout cells (Fig. 5f) and established TR populations in this background. CQ progressively reduced the viability of GLS1-knockout TR cells, and this effect was fully rescued by exogenous α-KG, whereas CQ had little impact on parental GLS1-knockout cells that had not been rendered drug tolerant (Fig. 5g).

We next examined the lysosome-associated processes that support TR cells. Because CQ can affect the autophagy–lysosome flux, we perturbed the core autophagy regulator ATG7. ATG7 knockdown reduced autophagic flux and modestly decreased the number of TR cells; however, this effect was neither specific to GLS1 deficiency nor rescued by α-KG (Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting that lysosome-associated support of TR-cell survival cannot be fully explained by canonical autophagy alone.

Finally, because dual inhibition robustly eliminated established TR cells in an α-KG-rescued manner, we assessed whether applying the same co-targeting regimen from the onset of KRAS inhibition could prevent the formation of the tolerant reservoir. Parental cells were treated from day 0 with KRAS inhibitors together with either glutamine withdrawal and CQ, or CB-839 and CQ, and viability was longitudinally monitored. Under these conditions, the combined treatments reduced overall cell numbers early; however, they did not eliminate the population (Supplementary Fig. 10). Notably, exogenous α-KG supplementation could not restore cell viability in this upfront co-treatment setting (Supplementary Fig. 10), in contrast to the complete α-KG-mediated rescue observed when the same perturbations were applied to established TR cells.

Collectively, these data indicate that the α-KG-linked vulnerability is state-specific, becoming apparent after cells transition into the KRAS inhibitor-induced TR state. Under upfront co-treatment, surviving cells were not α-KG-rescuable, consistent with engagement of a distinct survival program.

Suppression of oxidative stress is critical for TR-cell survival under KRAS inhibition

As a supplementary observation, blockade of glutamine metabolism combined with CQ treatment attenuated the fused/elongated mitochondrial network typically observed in TR cells, and this morphology was not restored by exogenous α-KG (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that α-KG was not a primary determinant of mitochondrial remodeling in this setting.

Because TR-cell killing by co-targeting glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes was fully rescued by α-KG, and given reported links between α-KG and redox homeostasis28,29, we next assessed whether α-KG supports TR-cell survival by buffering oxidative stress. Notably, steady-state α-KG levels decreased in TR cells; however, exogenous α-KG robustly restored viability, whereas GDH inhibition by EGCG did not measurably change intracellular α-KG abundance (Fig. 3b, c; Supplementary Fig. 7). These observations suggest that the α-KG-rescued phenotype may reflect an α-KG-supported function that is not readily captured by measuring the steady-state metabolites.

Consistent with a redox-related mechanism, the antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) phenocopied the survival-promoting effect of α-KG in TR cells under glutamine-metabolism blockade combined with CQ treatment and GLS1-knockout TR cells (Supplementary Fig. 12a). We next examined whether TR-state vulnerability identified in KRAS G12C models was also observed in the KRAS G12D context. TR populations established in AsPC-1 and A427 cells under continuous MRTX1133 exposure were challenged with co-disruption of glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes under continued KRAS inhibition. This co-perturbation reduced TR-cell viability and was robustly rescued by exogenous α-KG (Fig. 7c-e), whereas parental cells were relatively less affected (Fig. 7d, e). In PK-1 and PANC-1 cells, which showed limited persistence under continuous MRTX1133 exposure, sensitization to glutamine/lysosome co-disruption was modest (Supplementary Fig. 13e, f).

Consistent with a redox-linked component of this TR vulnerability, loss of viability in AsPC-1 TR cells was also rescued by NAC, and combined metabolic perturbation increased intracellular ROS, which was attenuated by α-KG supplementation (Fig. 7f, g). Collectively, these data indicate that KRAS G12D-mutant models can access drug-tolerant states that, once established, exhibit a state-dependent susceptibility to glutamine/lysosome co-disruption associated with impaired redox homeostasis during KRAS inhibition.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that KRAS-mutant cancer cells entered a reversible drug-tolerant (TR/DTP-like) state during sustained KRAS inhibition. Similar persister populations have been observed following EGFR or ALK inhibition, which are implicated in relapse and resistance12,14,31. TR cells transiently halted proliferation and exhibited senescence-like features, including elevated SA-β-Gal activity in a subset of cells; however, they regained proliferative capacity after drug withdrawal. This reversibility distinguished them from terminal senescence and was consistent with our proteomic profiling, which did not show clear induction of canonical senescence-associated markers, such as p16INK4a and p2132,33. These findings support a reversible, DTP-like adaptation to KRAS inhibition and provide a framework for identifying state-specific vulnerabilities to target the drug-tolerant reservoir.

TR cells displayed a remodeled mitochondrial network with a relatively highly fused/elongated morphology under KRAS inhibition. Notably, co-disruption of glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes attenuated this morphology, and exogenous α-KG did not restore it. Therefore, α-KG-induced survival can be dissociated from restoration of TR-associated mitochondrial morphology under these conditions.

Metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a component of adaptive survival under oncogene-targeted therapies, including KRAS inhibition34,35. Our metabolomic profiling revealed reduced lactate production together with the accumulation of upstream glycolytic intermediates and altered levels of TCA-cycle intermediates, indicating broad reconfiguration of central carbon metabolism during the tolerant state. In parallel, TR cells showed elevated mitochondrial membrane potential and remodeled morphology. Such mitochondrial remodeling is frequently observed under metabolic stress and has been linked to altered substrate handling and redox regulation36,37. However, TR cells were not sensitive to inhibition of ATP synthase by oligomycin, which is not necessarily consistent with a simple shift toward heightened dependence on oxidative phosphorylation. Under therapeutic stress, mitochondrial metabolism supports functions beyond ATP production, including biosynthesis and redox-associated processes38–40. Together, these observations are consistent with a stress-adaptive metabolic state.

α-KG has largely been discussed as an anaplerotic metabolite, which sustains TCA-cycle flux and growth-associated biosynthesis41,42. Consistent with that framework, co-inhibition of glutamine supply/utilization and autophagy/lysosome-dependent recycling induces cytotoxic metabolic stress in pancreatic cancer cells, and α-KG supplementation can rescue cell viability43. In our setting, however, α-KG-dependent rescue emerged most clearly after cells had transitioned into the KRAS inhibitor-induced TR state, and was accompanied by attenuation of intracellular ROS signals. Moreover, α-KG did not restore TR-associated mitochondrial morphology under co-perturbation conditions, arguing against a simple global restoration of mitochondrial bioenergetics. Together, these observations indicate that, in the TR state under KRAS inhibition, α-KG contributes to survival at least in part through redox-supportive functions.

Our results further indicate that redox homeostasis in TR cells is supported by coordinated glutamine metabolic and lysosome-associated processes. Glutamine metabolism supplies glutamate, which can support antioxidant buffering through α-KG-linked reactions and via glutathione synthesis38,44. Therefore, under the stress of KRAS inhibition, limiting glutamine utilization (CB-839 or GLS1 knockout) may restrict glutamate availability. Lysosome-associated recycling can further shape redox balance through multiple mechanisms, including the reuse of amino acids for the maintenance of glutathione homeostasis45, control of iron- and organelle-quality pathways46,47, and recycling of redox-active metabolites48, thereby complementing cellular redox buffering. Consistent with this framework, co-disruption of glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes triggered pronounced ROS accumulation and selective killing of TR cells, which were reversed by α-KG or NAC. These findings support a model in which TR cells rely on a layered redox-buffering capacity that becomes vulnerable when glutamine-derived inputs and lysosome-linked recycling are simultaneously constrained.

Building on these observations, we propose a model in which KRAS inhibition drives a reversible TR state sustained by layered redox-buffering capacity (Fig. 8). In proliferating KRAS-mutant cancer cells, mitochondrial metabolism and TCA-cycle intermediates largely serve growth-associated demands38,49, whereas under KRAS inhibition, TR-cell survival becomes critically dependent on redox homeostasis. In this context, glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes jointly support oxidative-stress control, and co-disruption of these processes overwhelms this buffering network to trigger lethal ROS accumulation and selective elimination of established TR cells. Robust rescue by α-KG and NAC, together with α-KG-mediated suppression of ROS, is consistent with a redox-linked mechanism rather than simple restoration of mitochondrial bioenergetics. Notably, this α-KG-linked vulnerability appears state-specific and becomes evident after establishment of the TR state, as the same co-perturbations from treatment onset do not eliminate surviving cells, and α-KG-dependent rescue is not observed, consistent with engagement of a distinct α-KG-independent survival program.Fig. 8Model of redox-dependent survival in KRAS inhibitor-tolerant cells.Under KRAS inhibition, cancer cells enter a reversible drug-tolerant (TR) state, in which survival becomes highly dependent on intracellular redox homeostasis. KRAS-targeted therapies often lead to transient tumor regression as surviving TR cells contribute to relapse and resistance23. Therefore, defining and eliminating the drug-tolerant persister reservoir is important for improving the durability of response13,14. A key limitation of this work is its reliance on defined two‑dimensional in vitro systems that do not capture the full complexity of in vivo tumor microenvironments, including stromal interactions, vascularization, and immune modulation. Therefore, careful separation of in vitro efficacy from in vivo outcomes will be important for translation. Our findings highlight redox regulation as a potentially actionable, state-specific vulnerability in established TR cells during KRAS inhibition. Selective killing of TR cells was achieved by co-disrupting glutamine metabolism and lysosome-associated processes under KRAS inhibition, which was consistent with a redox-linked mechanism. Modulation of the glutamine pathway influences the tumor microenvironment and antitumor immunity50,51, and multiple agents targeting these pathways are under clinical and preclinical development52–54. Together, these considerations support the feasibility of testing redox-linked metabolic co-targeting strategies to reduce the persister reservoir during KRAS inhibitor therapy.

Methods

Reagents

CQ, puromycin, N-acetyl-L-cysteine (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and EGCG (Funakoshi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were dissolved in Milli-Q water obtained from a Direct-Q 3 UV system (Millipore, Berlin, Germany). Sotorasib, adagrasib, MRTX1133 (all from TargetMol, Boston, MA, USA), carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone, oligomycin, rotenone, and antimycin A (all from Sigma-Aldrich), devimistat (CPI-613; Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA), CB-839 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), etomoxir (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), and bafilomycin A1 (FUJIFILM Wako, Tokyo, Japan) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). Dimethyl malonate and dimethyl 2-oxoglutarate (α-KG) (FUJIFILM Wako) were obtained as ready-to-use solutions and used without further dilution.

Cell culture

MiaPaCa2, PK-1, and PANC-1 cells were obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Research Center (Tsukuba, Japan), H358, H23, H1792, and A427 cells from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA), and AsPC-1 cells from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Nacalai Tesque), and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere under 5% CO2. Cells were grown in either normal-glucose (2 g/L) or low-glucose (0.2 g/L) medium.

To establish TR cell populations, parental MiaPaCa2, H358, H23, and H1792 cells were exposed to 100 nM sotorasib or adagrasib for 7 days, and AsPC-1 and PK-1, PANC-1, and A427 cells were treated with 1000 nM MRTX1133 for 7 days. Cells that remained adherent after treatment were defined as TR cells.

293FT cells were obtained from Life Technologies (Carlsbad, CA, USA) and maintained separately in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (high glucose, 4.5 g/L, containing L-glutamine and sodium pyruvate; Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, MEM non-essential amino acids, and antibiotic–antimycotic solution (all from Nacalai Tesque).

All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed to be negative.

Measurement of intracellular ATP concentration

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessment of cell number

Cell number was assessed using an MTT assay. Cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/mL of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) for 2 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO M Plex; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Absorbance values were used to estimate the cell number.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing, cells were treated with RNase A (100 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at 25 °C and then stained with 7-aminoactinomycin D (Sony, Tokyo, Japan) for 5 min in the dark. DNA content was analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Colter, Brea, CA, USA). Cell-cycle distribution was determined using the FlowLogic software (Inivai Technologies, Mentone, VIC, Australia) based on the Dean–Jett–Fox model. Representative gating strategies for flow cytometric analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in PBS containing 1% Triton X-100 (Nacalai Tesque) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) on ice. Equal amounts of protein were heated at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Nacalai Tesque) at 25 °C for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phosphorylated (phospho) mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) 1/2 (Ser217/221, clone 41G9), MEK1/2, phospho-Akt (Thr308, clone D25E6), Akt, microtubule-associated proteins 1 A/1B light chain 3B (clone D11), GLS1 (clone E4T9Q), ATG7 (clone D12B11) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), or β-actin (clone AC15) (Sigma-Aldrich). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK) and developed using an ECL Prime reagent (GE Healthcare). Signals were detected using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 system (GE Healthcare). Uncropped and unedited western blot images are provided in Supplementary Fig. 15.

Analysis of cellular apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V–FITC and Zombie dye (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in a binding buffer (10 mM of 4-[2-hydroxyethyl]-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid, 140 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4). Annexin V–FITC and Zombie dye were added to the cell suspension and incubated for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. After washing with cold PBS, samples were immediately analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Colter). Data were processed using FlowLogic (Inivai Technologies). Representative gating strategies for flow cytometric analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14.

SA-β-gal staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque) for 10 min at 25 °C, washed, and incubated in a staining solution (0.1% X-gal [Apollo Scientific, Cheshire, UK], 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide [MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA], 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM MgCl2 in 40 mM citric acid/sodium phosphate buffer [pH 6.0]) for 14 h at 37 °C. After staining, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Bright-field images for SA-β-gal staining and corresponding fluorescence images for nuclear staining were acquired from identical fields using a BZ-X1000 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). For quantification, total cell numbers were determined by automated nuclear counting using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health [NIH], Bethesda, MD, USA). SA-β-gal–positive cells were defined as nuclei with perinuclear regions exhibiting SA-β-gal signal exceeding a fixed threshold determined using control samples. The percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was calculated from three randomly selected fields per group.

Proteomic sample preparation, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis, and data processing

For proteomic profiling, 10 µg protein from each lysate was precipitated using acetone and resuspended in 25.5 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. Reduction and alkylation were performed by sequential addition of 1.5 µL of 100 mM dithiothreitol and 3 µL of 100 mM iodoacetamide, each incubated for 30 min at 60 and 25 °C, respectively. Samples were then digested with 0.5 µg Trypsin Platinum (Promega) overnight at 37 °C. After acidification (pH < 3) with trifluoroacetic acid, samples were desalted using GL-Tip SDB (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan), vacuum-dried, and resuspended in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for LC–MS/MS analysis (500 ng on-column).

Peptides were separated using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a nano electrospray ionization source. A trap column (C18 PepMap 100; 0.3 × 5 mm, 5 µm; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an analytical column (NTCC-360/75-3-125; Nikkyo Technos, Tokyo, Japan) were used. Peptides were separated using a 90-min gradient of water containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The measurement parameters were set as follows: 0–3 min, 2% B; 3–93 min, 2%–40% B; 93–95 min, 40%–95% B; 95–105 min, 95% B; 105–107 min, 95%–2% B; and 107–120 min, 2% B. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. Mass spectrometric parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 2.0 kV; capillary temperature, 275 °C; S-lens RF level, 50; scan type, full MS; scan range, m/z 350–1500; resolution, 70,000; polarity, positive; automatic gain control target, 3 × 106; and maximum injection time, 100 ms. MS/MS parameters were set as follows: resolution, 17,500; automatic gain control target, 1 × 105; maximum injection time, 60 ms; normalized collision energy, 27; dynamic exclusion, 15 s; loop count, 10; isolation window, 1.6 m/z; and charge exclusion, unassigned, 1, 8, >8.

Peptide identification and label-free quantification were performed using Proteome Discoverer v.3.1 SP1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Chimerys node (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The analytical parameters used for data processing were as follows: parent mass error tolerance, 10.0 ppm; fragment mass error tolerance, 0.02 Da; search engine, Inferys v.3.0; protein database, SwissProt (Homo sapiens); enzyme name, trypsin (full); maximum number of missed cleavages, 2; FDR confidence < 0.01; dynamic modification, oxidation (methionine); and static modification, carbamidomethyl (cysteine). Label-free quantification parameters for detected peptides were set as follows: precursor quantification, precursor abundance based on area; normalization mode, total peptide amount; protein ratio calculation, pairwise ratio based; and hypothesis test, t-test (background based). Among the identified proteins, those meeting the following criteria were used for comparative data analysis: proteins with a “Protein FDR Confidence” score ranked as “High”; proteins with a “Found in sample” score assigned as “Peak found” or “High” in at least three sample lots out of the replicate samples (n = 4) in each group; and proteins with a numerical value recorded for “Abundance” in at least one sample lot out of the replicate samples (n = 4) in each group.

PCA was performed using the prcomp function in R v.4.2.3 with Z-score–normalized abundance values and visualized using the ggplot2 package. Differentially expressed proteins were identified based on |log2 fold change | ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05, and visualized in volcano plots. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost) with the KEGG Pathway category. The abundance of proteins in TR and parental cells was used for analysis using GSEA v.4.4.0, with the following settings: number of permutations = 1000, permutation type = gene set, enrichment statistics = weighted, and metric for ranking genes = ratios of classes. Complete proteomic datasets are provided in jPOST under the accession code JPST003998 and in ProteomeXchange under the accession code PXD067380.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on collagen (Corning, Corning, NY, USA)-coated glass coverslips and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque), permeabilized with 0.01% Triton X-100 (Nacalai Tesque) in PBS, and blocked with 1% BSA (Nacalai Tesque) in PBS. Samples were incubated with rabbit anti-LAMP1 (Sigma-Aldrich) and rabbit anti-TFEB (Cell Signaling Technology) primary antibodies (1:200), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 546–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclei were counterstained with TO-PRO-3 iodide (642/661) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Coverslips were mounted using a Dako Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and imaged using an LSM700 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Three randomly selected fields per condition were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH). For quantitative analysis of LAMP1 immunofluorescence, nuclear regions were identified based on TO-PRO-3 staining and expanded by a fixed number of pixels to approximate cell boundaries. LAMP1 fluorescence was quantified as corrected total fluorescence per cell after background subtraction. Three randomly selected fields per condition were analyzed. For quantitative analysis of TFEB immunofluorescence, nuclear regions were identified based on TO-PRO-3 staining using single optical sections. Nuclear TFEB intensity was quantified as the background-corrected mean fluorescence intensity within nuclear regions. For each condition, three randomly selected fields were analyzed.

Measurement of extracellular lactate levels

Extracellular lactate levels were quantified using a Lactate Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Lactate concentrations in the resulting supernatants were assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO M Plex, Tecan).

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using JC-1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated with 500 nM JC-1 in complete medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed twice with PBS, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Colter). JC-1 monomers and aggregates were detected at 525 (green) and 585 (red) nm, respectively, with excitation at 488 nm. The red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio was used to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. Data were processed using FlowLogic (Inivai Technologies). Representative gating strategies for flow cytometric analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14.

Fluorescence microscopy

Cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes (MATSUNAMI, Osaka, Japan). For mitochondrial imaging, cells were stained with 100 nM MitoTracker Green FM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 µg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in complete medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. For lysosomal imaging, cells were incubated with 50 nM LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 in complete medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. For assessing intracellular ROS, cells were incubated with 10 μM of 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA; Funakoshi Co., Ltd.) and 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 in FBS-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by gentle washing and replacement with fresh medium prior to imaging. Fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM780 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Three fields of view per condition were selected without bias and analyzed using ImageJ (NIH). Mitochondrial morphology was assessed by skeleton-based image analysis, and mitochondrial network length was quantified on a per-cell basis. For lysosomal imaging, the mean fluorescence intensity of LysoTracker per cell was quantified. For ROS imaging, the mean fluorescence intensity of DCFDA was quantified per cell.

Targeted metabolomic analysis of glycolytic and TCA cycle metabolites

Polar metabolites were extracted from MiaPaCa2 and H358 cells by sequential removal of proteins and lipids. Cells were resuspended in 80 μL saline and mixed with 400 μL methanol containing an internal standard (2 μM hippuric acid-d5; Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Canada). After vigorous vortexing for 2 min, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was passed through an Ultrafree-MC 0.22-μm filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and a MonoSpin C18 column (GL Sciences) for removing proteins and lipids. The final eluate (350 μL) was evaporated to dryness and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Targeted metabolomic profiling of glycolytic and TCA cycle intermediates was conducted using a high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q-Exactive; Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an ion-chromatography system (Dionex ICS-5000 + ; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL water and filtered through an Ultrafree-MC 0.22-μm filter before injection. A 10-µL aliquot was used for each measurement. Chromatographic separation was conducted under previously described conditions55. MS analysis was performed in a parallel reaction monitoring mode, with a quadrupole isolation window set to 0.4 m/z. MS/MS parameters included an AGC target of 2 × 105 and maximum injection time of 80 ms. Mass spectrometric transition values for each target analyte were as follows: lactate (m/z 89.0243 → 89.0245); pyruvate (m/z 175.0249 → 87.0089); fructose 6-phosphate (m/z 259.0222 → 96.9695); 2-phosphoglycerate, 3-phosphoglycerate (m/z 184.9856 → 78.9590); fructose-1,6-phosphate (m/z 338.9888 → 96.9697); glucose 6-phosphate (m/z 259.0222 → 96.9695); phosphoenolpyruvate (m/z 166.9749 → 78.9590); fumarate(m/z 115.0037 → 71.0138); malate (m/z 133.0141 → 115.0036); citrate (m/z 191.0197 → 111.0087); isocitrate (m/z 191.0197 → 111.0087); α-KG (m/z 145.0142 → 101.0245); and hippuric acid-d5 (m/z 183.0823 → 139.0921). The normalized collision energy values were set to 30 for all analytes. Data were processed using TraceFinder v.4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with mass tolerance of 10 ppm. Peak areas were normalized to those of the internal standard and subsequently used for statistical analyses.

Establishment of GLS1 knockout cells

GLS1 knockout cells were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing approach. A single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the human GLS1 locus (5′-CCCGGGGAGACGGACGCGTT-3′; PAM: CGG) was designed and cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (52961; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) that co-expresses Cas9 and sgRNA under the U6 promoter. A non-targeting scramble sgRNA construct (SHC016; Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a control. Lentiviral particles were produced in 293FT cells by co-transfecting lentiCRISPRv2-sgGLS1 or scramble control plasmids with the packaging plasmid pCMV-dR8.91 and envelope plasmid pCMV-VSV-G using PEI MAX (Warrington, PA, USA). Supernatants containing viral particles were collected, filtered, and concentrated by precipitating with polyethylene glycol. Target cells (MiaPaCa2) were infected with lentiviral particles and selected with puromycin (2.5 μg/mL) to obtain stable GLS1 knockout and scramble control cell populations. Puromycin was withdrawn after complete elimination of non-infected cells, and surviving cells were expanded under standard culture conditions. Knockout efficiency was validated by immunoblot analysis of GLS1 levels.

Transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNA)

Cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA oligonucleotides (Japan Bio Services Co., Ltd., Saitama, Japan) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). For MiaPaCa2 SotTR cells, 150 nM siRNA was used. The sequence of ATG7-targeting siRNA was as follows: sense, 5′-GCCAGAGGAUUCAACAUGATT-3′; antisense, 5′-UCAUGUUGAAUCCUCUGGCTT-3′. A non-targeting siRNA (Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a negative control.

Cell-free DCFDA oxidation assay

A cell-free assay was performed to assess the direct ROS-scavenging activity of α-KG by monitoring H2O2-induced oxidation of DCFDA. DCFDA (final concentration, 10 μM) was incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS at 37 °C in the dark. Oxidation was initiated by adding 100 μM H2O2 in the presence or absence of α-KG (final concentration, 1–10 mM). Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO M Plex; Tecan) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 525 nm, respectively, for 60 min at 37 °C.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc test, as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v.8.4.3 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA).

For cell viability, proliferation, and cell-free fluorescence assays, triplicate wells were used for each condition within each experiment, and the experiments were independently repeated at least three times unless otherwise stated. For imaging-based quantification, biological replicates were defined as independent experiments performed on separate days; values obtained from individual cells or fields were averaged to generate one value per biological replicate, as described in the corresponding figure legends. For targeted metabolomic analyses, independent samples were used as described in the figure legends. For proteomic analyses, biological replicates were defined as independently prepared cell samples and were analyzed as described in the Methods. Representative microscopy images, flow cytometry plots, and immunoblots are shown from experiments that were independently repeated with similar results, unless otherwise stated.
