Observation of resonant doublet and variable finesse in a tabletop meter-scale linear three-mirror cavity Journal: opticajournal
Paul Stevens, Pierre-Emmanuel Bonningues, Théo Lesieur, François Glotin, Vincent Loriette, Manuel Andia, Angélique Lartaux-Vollard, Nicolas Leroy, Aymeric van de Walle

Abstract
Fabry-Perot cavities are widely used in current gravitational-wave detectors. In particular, they play a key role in frequency-dependent squeezing systems, enabling broadband quantum noise reduction. However, their ability to precisely control squeezing properties may be insufficient for the next generation of detectors. In this context, theoretical studies on linear three-mirror cavities have revealed promising features, such as resonance peak splitting and their equivalence to two-mirror cavities with variable finesse. In this paper, we report experimental observations of both the resonant doublet and variable finesse using a meter-scale implementation of a linear three-mirror cavity.

2
Optical characteristics of linear three-mirror cavities

A linear three-mirror cavity consists of three mirrors arranged in series, with the input and end mirrors facing each other as in a standard two-mirror Fabry-Perot cavity. The central mirror is positioned between them and can be oriented towards either direction. A schematic illustration of the three-mirror cavity is depicted in the upper part of Fig.

.

2.2
Variable finesse

Another key feature of the three-mirror cavity is its equivalence to a Fabry-Perot cavity with variable finesse. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the first sub-cavity in the three-mirror system can be interpreted as a virtual mirror, whose effective reflection and transmission coefficients vary with its tuning
[
14
]
. The reflectivity and transmissivity of the virtual mirror
\text{M}^{\prime}_{1}
correspond to those of the Fabry-Perot cavity formed by
\text{M}_{1}
and
\text{M}_{2}
. As a result, the optical properties of
\text{M}^{\prime}_{1}
can be dynamically tuned by making microscopic adjustments to the length
L_{1}
. Either the first or second sub-cavity can act as a virtual mirror. However, the choice may not be equivalent for two reasons. First, when the three mirrors have different reflectivity coefficients, it affects the virtual mirror’s reflectivity range and, consequently, the achievable finesse interval. Second, even if all mirrors share the same reflectivity coefficient, the selection remains significant, as it influences the ratio between the input and output mirror reflectivities in the virtual two-mirror cavity, resulting in under-coupled, optimally-coupled, or over-coupled states.

(r^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{1})
. Both configurations share the same second cavity length
L_{2}
and mirror parameters
(r_{3},t_{3})
.

4
Experimental observation of the resonance frequency doubling

4.1
Method

The splitting of the resonance peak can be experimentally observed by simultaneously performing microscopic length scans of both sub-cavities. Fig.
4
presents the transmission through the cavity as a function of the microscopic detuning of each sub-cavity length from its individual resonant condition. The resulting map displays a distinctive cross-like pattern, where the two prominent resonance branches correspond to the peaks of the resonant doublet. Specifically, the lower-left and upper-right branches represent, respectively, the variation of left and right maxima of the doublet presented in Fig.
1
. To experimentally probe this structure, identical displacement ramps are applied to both piezoelectric stages controlling the sub-cavity lengths. This synchronized actuation causes both sub-cavities to contract and expand simultaneously by equal amounts. Consequently, the three-mirror cavity is scanned along a diagonal direction parallel to the solid green line shown in Fig.
4
. However, since the initial microscopic position of each piezoelectric stage is not controlled, the horizontal and/or vertical shift relative to this green line cannot be determined. Nevertheless, such shifts only affect the spacing between the two maxima of the double-peak. It is important to note that, due to the fully symmetric configuration of our three-mirror cavity, with identical mirror reflectivities and equal sub-cavity lengths, the resulting theoretical resonant doublet exhibits a perfect symmetry. This symmetry is reflected in terms of peak width, amplitude, and spacing with respect to the central resonance condition
[
12
]
.

4.2
Results

A distinct splitting of the resonant peaks was observed, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. As discussed earlier, the expected profile of these doublets in our specific configuration is symmetric, with the two peaks exhibiting equal widths, amplitudes, and spacing relative to the resonance condition. In practice, however, the measured spectra reveal a random asymmetry in the amplitude ratio between the peaks of individual doublets. This irregularity can be attributed to two main factors. First, small discrepancies in the reflectivity of the cavity mirrors can lead to uneven optical coupling between the sub-cavities. Second, the central mirror, although fixed, is not actively stabilized, and its position may drift slightly over time. Residual deviations are likely caused by external mechanical perturbations such as vibrations of the optical bench or fluctuations in the ambient air flow, both of which can induce sub-wavelength shifts in the mirrors’ positions. Since the amplitude ratio is highly sensitive to these parameters, even minimal shifts in the central mirror’s position as a function of time, combined with small deviations from the designed reflectivities of the mirrors, can lead to noticeable random fluctuations in the observed peak intensities. For the data presented in Fig.
5
, we reconstructed both the average intra-doublet peak spacing and the separation between adjacent doublets using the calibrated displacement constant of the piezoelectric stage
$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$/$\text{\,}\mathrm{V}$
. The measured mean peak spacing within a doublet is of the order of
42\pm 6\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
, which is in agreement with the expected value of
54\pm 6\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
derived from the theoretical reflectivities
R_{\text{th}}=$90\pm 2\text{\,}\%$
. Additionally, the separation between successive doublets, defined as the distance between adjacent doublet centers, is of the order of
555\pm 4\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
, close to the expected
532\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
based on the
1064\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
laser wavelength. These discrepancies can arise from mechanical perturbations affecting the entire system or non linearity in the piezoelectric stages scans. Finally, additional higher-order resonance peaks are observed around the doublets. This is due to imperfect mode matching between the beam and the cavity geometry, along with slight misalignments of the mirrors.

5
Experimental observation of finesse variation

5.1
Method

To measure the variation of finesse, one of the sub-cavities can be kept at a fixed length in order to define the effective reflectivity of the resulting virtual mirror, while the other sub-cavity’s length is scanned. In practice, it is more convenient to implement this measurement strategy by slowly scanning the length of one sub-cavity to gradually adjust the virtual reflectivity, and simultaneously performing a rapid scan of the other sub-cavity to record the corresponding finesse. This dual-scan method enables efficient data collection, on the timescale of just a few minutes, thereby reducing the influence of perturbations and maintaining a coherent relationship between the positions of the two piezoelectric stages throughout the measurement. In our setup, the length
L_{1}
is scanned at a relatively high frequency of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, whereas
L_{2}
is scanned more slowly at
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, allowing the effective reflectivity of the second sub-cavity, treated as the virtual mirror, to be modulated over time. The Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of the transmitted resonance peaks is measured and recorded as a function of time using a high-resolution oscilloscope
Teledyne Lecroy HDO6401
. These measured peak widths are then converted into finesse values using the free spectral range (FSR) of the cavity.

5.2
Results

Fig.
6
shows the variable finesse, reconstructed from our measurement of FWHM, as a function of the displacement of mirror
\text{M}_{3}
. The transition between low and high finesse regimes, as well as the corresponding minimum and maximum finesse values, are governed by the reflectivity coefficients of all three mirrors. By fitting the finesse data, assuming identical mirrors and hence equal reflectivity for each, we extract a reflectivity coefficient of
R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{3}=R=$86\text{\,}\%$
. Taking into account the data variations around the fit, we evaluated the uncertainty to be
\Delta R=$1\text{\,}\%$
. The observed discrepancy between the fitted and theoretical reflectivity
R_{\text{th}}=$90\pm 2\text{\,}\%$
can also be translated in terms of finesse: while the expected theoretical maximum finesse is
\mathcal{F}_{th,\text{max}}\approx 58.1^{+15.7}_{-10.5}
, our fit yields a maximum finesse of only
\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}}\approx 39.7^{+0.2}_{-0.2}
. Despite this difference, the fitted value closely aligns with the theoretical value, showing no significant variation. The analyzed data display multiple periods of finesse variation, with the maximum finesse of the three-mirror cavity occurring when the sub-cavity formed by the third mirror and the central mirror (acting as a virtual mirror) is at anti-resonance. This condition is met when the displacement of mirror
\text{M}_{3}
from resonance satisfies
\frac{(2N+1)\lambda}{4}
, where
N\in\mathbb{Z}
. At these positions, the effective reflectivity of the virtual mirror is maximized. Conversely, as this sub-cavity approaches resonance, its transmissivity is maximized, resulting in the lowest finesse. In our case, the theoretical minimum finesse is
\mathcal{F}_{\text{min}}=0
. This value is reached when both sub-cavities are simultaneously at resonance, resulting in complete transmission through the entire three-mirror cavity as the two sub-cavities are optimally coupled (i.e.
R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{3}
). Over all the data, we observe finesse instabilities that can be attributed to the mechanical sensitivity of the setup to vibrations. Furthermore, as the finesse approaches zero, the FWHM increases and theoretically becomes infinite, making measurements highly sensitive and leading to the observed instabilities for
F=0
. In addition to the main finesse drops, smaller drops, slightly shifted from the main ones but with the same period, are also observed. They are due to the presence of a residual high-order mode in the cavity.

6
Conclusion

Building upon established theoretical studies of three-mirror cavities, we have successfully designed and implemented a meter-scale experimental prototype, enabling direct investigation of its optical properties. Our experimental observations confirmed the expected resonance peak splitting, which shows good agreement with simulation predictions. Although the simulations are based on idealized assumptions, such as the plane-wave approximation and perfectly lossless mirrors, the close match between theoretical and experimental results validates the robustness of the model. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that the three-mirror cavity can effectively be interpreted as a two-mirror system with a finesse that is variable by adjusting one sub-cavity away from its resonant condition. This controllable finesse introduces a high degree of flexibility, allowing the cavity response to be adapted for different experimental needs. However, the precision with which a target finesse can be achieved in practice may be limited by several factors, including mechanical stability, alignment sensitivity, and mirror losses. In this study, we focused on a fully symmetric configuration, with all three mirrors having identical reflectivities and both sub-cavities set to equal macroscopic lengths. Future investigations will aim to explore more advanced and varied configurations. This includes asymmetric setups, featuring unequal sub-cavity lengths or differing mirror reflectivities, as well as larger-scale implementations. Additionally, employing suspended mirrors or operating the cavity under vacuum conditions would reduce environmental noise, enabling more precise measurements. Another challenge lies in developing techniques to control such system and lock it in a precise operating regime. Such studies could contribute to the development of filtering systems for the reduction of quantum noise in advanced interferometric gravitational wave detectors.
