Antibacterial Immunotherapy: Mechanistic Insights, Emerging Therapeutic Strategies, and Clinical Translation
Authors: Hamdi Al-Azzani, Hanane Aliouat, Hongshi Cheng, Omer M A Dagah, Musaed Hamood Al-subari, Wenhu Zhou, Shugen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) continues to compromise the effectiveness of conventional antibacterial therapy, driving the development of therapeutic strategies that extend beyond direct antibiotic-mediated bacterial killing. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens evade treatment through diverse mechanisms, including enzymatic drug inactivation, target modification, efflux pump overexpression, biofilm formation, and persisters development. AMR results in chronic and recurrent infections, prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and elevated morbidity and mortality, underscoring the need for innovative therapeutic approaches that target both the pathogen and the host. To bridge the dynamic interplay between bacterial pathogens and the host immune system with emerging therapeutic innovations, this narrative review first examines the biological mechanisms underlying bacterial resistance. It then explores therapeutic strategies beyond conventional antibiotics, providing an overview of current approaches and their limitations, including drug repurposing, bacteriophage therapy, and CRISPR-Cas technology. The review subsequently focuses on antibacterial immunotherapy, discussing a broad range of emerging approaches, including probiotics, monoclonal antibodies, cell-based therapies, host-directed therapies, aptamers, nanotechnology-based platforms, cytokine-based therapies, and antimicrobial peptides. An integrated overview of preclinical evidence, clinical studies, and FDA-approved therapies is presented to assess the translational potential of immunotherapy strategies in combating AMR. Scientific, regulatory, manufacturing, and implementation challenges that influence their successful translation into clinical practice are discussed throughout. By integrating the biological basis of host-pathogen interactions with emerging antibacterial therapeutics and their translational development, this review provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating innovative strategies against antimicrobial resistance. In contrast to modality-focused reviews, it offers a unified perspective that highlights the complementary roles of pathogen-targeted and host-directed interventions and identifies future opportunities to improve the prevention and management of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, antibacterial immunotherapy, nanotechnology, aptamers, bacteriophage therapy, immune evasion

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the 21st century. The emergence and rapid spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) bacteria have significantly exacerbated this crisis, limited treatment options and increasing morbidity and mortality worldwide. Among the most problematic pathogens are the ESKAPE organisms. It is a group of bacteria distinguished by their remarkable adaptability, ability to form biofilms, capacity to evade host immune defenses, and resistance to multiple classes of antimicrobial agents.1 AMR constitutes a global crisis as it knows no geographical boundaries and spreads across both developed and low-income countries. The burden of AMR is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), due to limited healthcare system capacity, deterioration and lack of infrastructure, limited resources, medical deserts, and weaknesses in the health insurance system. Moreover, the rapid spread of AMR is facilitated by several factors, including poor water access and quality, hygiene deterioration, low sanitation capacity in medical services, low education level and awareness toward good antibiotic usage and infection preventive measures, leading to inappropriate self-medication, less adherence to correct antibiotic dosage and duration, vaccination escape, and late medical diagnosis, promoting multi-infections and interspecies exchange of resistance genes.2 Additionally, The lack of specialized diagnostic laboratories drives probabilistic infectious disease management and increases antibiotic misuse. Collectively, these factors accelerate the development and dissemination of resistance, prolong infectious disease transmission within communities, and contribute to the disproportionately high burden of AMR-related mortality observed in LMICs.2,3

From a One Health perspective, environmental factors are implicated in the development of AMR. Humans, animals, and the environment are interconnected, and the spread of resistant strains in animals or in water, soil, food, and agricultural products contaminates humans.4 The misuse of anti-infective treatments in farming, use of pesticides in agriculture, and improper disposal of pharmaceutical waste into soil and water expose commensal bacteria to selective pressures, driving resistance. This cycle is further intensified by ecological burdens and climate change, which function as stressors for bacteria, prompting their evolution and resistance. In addition, social practices and the dynamics of living in close communities increase the spread capacity.5 More importantly, globalization has transformed individuals into global citizens and facilitated the large-scale spread of resistant pathogens across borders, as illustrated by the COVID-19 pandemic.

While bacterial acquisition of resistance is undergoing rapid expansion, antibacterial discovery is going backward.6 Identifying new and effective hit and lead molecules for antibacterial pipelines remains a real challenge as it is hindered by the diversity of escape mechanisms. Furthermore, the successful translation of candidate molecules into clinically useful therapies is constrained by the need to achieve favorable pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and toxicological profiles.7

Contemporary trends are emerging in the global antibacterial pipeline, driven by advances in genomic and proteomic techniques that refine target definitions and pave the way for new therapeutic approaches. More importantly, antimicrobial resistance is no longer viewed solely as a microbiological phenomenon driven by bacterial mutations and horizontal gene transfer. Increasing evidence indicates that resistant pathogens actively modulate host immunity, inflammatory signaling, and tissues microenvironments, thereby contributing to persistent infection and therapeutic failure. These observations have accelerated interest in host-directed and multimodal therapeutic strategies.

Immunotherapy is a promising approach that leverages host immunity to target resistant pathogens and drives a shift from pathogen-centric to host-centric therapies.8 Instead of directly targeting bacterial structures, these approaches modulate host immune pathways and improve pathogen clearance and therapeutic responsiveness. Promising approaches within this research area include microbiota modulation, phage-based agents, and immunomodulation strategies.9 Among the most actively investigated alternatives to conventional antimicrobial agents are antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, and nanoparticle-drug conjugates, which offer enhanced targeting capabilities and improved treatment efficacy against resistant pathogens.10 As such, multimodal therapeutic systems combining immune modulation with bacteriophage engineering, antimicrobial peptides and nucleic acid-based targeting platforms have attracted increasing attention.11–13

Despite the rapid growth of research in antimicrobial immunotherapy and nanotechnology, the available literature remains highly fragmented, often focusing on specific therapeutic modalities or individual pathogens. To the best of our knowledge, no review has yet provided a broad and integrative perspective that combines host-directed immune modulation with advanced therapeutic and delivery platforms for the treatment of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections, highlighting a critical gap in the field.

To address this unmet need, this narrative review examines emerging trends in antibacterial immunotherapy and explores novel therapeutic opportunities arising from the modulation of host-pathogen interactions, including the targeting of key host and pathogen effectors. Furthermore, it evaluates the potential of nanomaterial-based delivery systems and nucleic acid-based therapeutic platforms to enhance treatment efficacy and overcome bacterial resistance mechanisms. It provides an overview of host-pathogen interactions, key molecular effectors, and multimodal antibacterial immunotherapeutic strategies, both at the pre-clinical and clinical state. This review aims to bridge existing knowledge gaps and establish a conceptual framework for future research. Given the escalating global burden of AMR, the development of coordinated, multidisciplinary, and integrative therapeutic strategies is urgently needed.

Bacterial Resistance Pathophysiology

Antibacterial Natural and Acquired Resistance

Antibacterial agents exert therapeutic activity through diverse mechanisms targeting bacterial cell walls, membranes, nucleic acid synthesis, and protein translation. However, bacterial pathogens possess both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms that substantially reduce antimicrobial efficacy and contribute to persistent infection. Bacterial species exhibit intrinsic resistance.14 For example, cell wall morphology may provide natural protection against Gram-negative bacteria.15 The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria acts as a selective permeability barrier that restricts the penetration of many antibacterial agents, particularly hydrophilic and high-molecular-weight molecules. In addition, efflux pump systems actively eject toxic compounds and further reduce intracellular antibiotic accumulation, thereby contributing to intrinsic resistance and therapeutic failure.16 Many pathogenic bacteria form structured multicellular communities known as biofilms, which consist of extracellular polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA. Biofilm formation promotes bacterial adhesion, limits antibiotic penetration, impairs immune-cell access, and contributes to chronic and recurrent infections. In addition to enhancing antimicrobial tolerance, biofilms facilitate bacterial persistence and inflammatory dysregulation within host tissues. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a clinically important example, particularly in cystic fibrosis, where biofilm-associated persistence markedly compromises therapeutic efficacy and contributes to disease progression.17 Bacteria are highly modifiable microorganisms that evolve and acquire resistance to antibiotics. Two main mechanisms are implicated in this transformation: mutational processes and horizontal gene transfer (HGT).18 Genetic modifications induced by the acquisition of newly expressed sequences alter metabolic pathways. Several mechanisms emerge to preserve bacterial viability in hostile environments, such as decreased permeability of bacterial cell walls, overexpression of efflux pumps, and the production of enzymes that degrade or modify the drug target. In addition to drug resistance, bacteria have developed potent survival strategies, including intracellular persistence and dormancy.19 This survival process involves the formation of a new subpopulation called persisters. This was observed in many bacterial pathogens. Persister-cell formation has been described in several clinically important pathogens, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Chlamydia spp., and Staphylococcus aureus.19,20 For example, S. aureus can survive within keratinocytes and the skin immune system, including macrophages and dendritic cells, and overcomes the acidic degradation of phagosomes to proliferate within these cells, contributing to chronic wounds.21 This phenomenon is induced by genetic variability within the bacterial accessory gene regulator (agr) system. In S. aureus, this system controls virulence and induces cytotoxic effects against host cells. Its expression is inhibited by the persisting colonies, inducing an agr-deficiency phenotypic switch. Bacteria hiding within host cells promote their escape from both antibiotics and immune surveillance.

Bacterial resistance mechanisms not only diminish the efficacy of antimicrobial agents but also profoundly reshape host-pathogen interactions. The reduced permeability of bacterial envelopes, particularly the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, limits the entry of host-derived antimicrobial peptides, thereby weakening innate immune killing mechanisms. Similarly, the overexpression of efflux pumps not only expels antibiotics but can also reduce the intracellular concentration of host-produced toxic metabolites and immune effector molecules. Biofilm formation represents a particularly important resistance-associated strategy, as the extracellular matrix physically impedes both antibiotic penetration and immune-cell access, while also creating localized microenvironments that dampen phagocytic activity, alter cytokine signaling, and promote chronic inflammation. In addition, genetic adaptation through mutation and horizontal gene transfer enables pathogens to rapidly remodel virulence and metabolic pathways, often resulting in immune escape phenotypes and reduced recognition by host immune surveillance systems. Finally, intracellular persistence and the formation of dormant persister subpopulations allow bacteria to evade both antimicrobial treatment and immune-mediated clearance, contributing to infection relapse and chronic disease states. All these interactions have a wide impact on the healthcare system, inducing persistent infections and death in patients. However, research in this field remains slow. Thus, it is urgent to improve antibacterial strategies using innovative therapeutic systems.

Pathogen- Host Interactions and Therapeutic Insights

Bacterial host colonization begins with inoculation through wounds or natural orifices, such as the nose, vagina, and urinary tract.22 The interaction between the pathogens and host cells determines bacterial growth within the organism. These interactions occur through both secreted and factors surface-associated virulence determinants and effectors.23 Virulence-associated determinants, such as surface adhesins, pili, fimbriae, capsules and outer membrane proteins, in addition to secreted virulence factors and immune-modulating effectors allow bacterial survival in adverse host environments and determine bacterial attachment, pathogenicity, tissue invasion, toxin-mediated injury, immune escape, persistence and therapeutic failure.23–26 Other factors produced by bacteria, such as exotoxins, endotoxins, proteases, and type III secretion systems, affect host-pathogen interactions by destroying host tissues, breaking down epithelial barriers, interfering with cytokine signaling, and inhibiting immune cell function.27,28 Certain pathogens produce highly potent immunomodulatory toxins; for example, superantigens produced by Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes cause excessive and non-specific T-cell activation with subsequent cytokine-mediated tissue injury and immune dysregulation.29,30 Likewise, LPS from Gram-negative bacteria induces pattern recognition receptor pathways and systemic inflammation in severe infection.28,31 Collectively, these virulence mechanisms not only facilitate bacterial survival and dissemination within the host but also profoundly influence disease severity, treatment outcomes, and the effectiveness of host immune responses. Owing to their persistence and their critical role in pathogenesis, virulence-associated pathways have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets and are increasingly being explored through the development of anti-adhesion therapies, vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, aptamers, and nanocarrier-based targeting systems.32–34

Bacterial Cell-Surface Effectors

Infestation relies on adhesion molecules that enable bacterial insertion and/or penetration into host cells.25 These molecules, called adhesins, are non-polymeric structures, including collagens, proteoglycans, laminins, hyaluronans, elastins, vitronectin, and fibrinogen, and are subdivided into fimbriae and non-fimbriae subcategories. They interact with human host cell adhesion molecules, including integrins, E-cadherins, selectins, fibronectins, and CEACAMs (carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecules).26 For instance, Neisseria spp initiates attachment to host cells through lipooligosaccharides and type IV pili and outer membrane proteins, such as Opa and Opc.32,33,35 Thus, it forms microcolonies on epithelial surfaces and contribute to its virulence.

The immune system, supported by a diverse array of cellular defenses, plays a crucial role in preventing pathogen adhesion. Ciliated epithelial cells within mucosal barriers facilitate pathogen clearance through mucociliary mechanisms, thereby limiting microbial attachment and colonization.36,37 The integrity of these epithelial barriers is maintained by specialized junctional complexes composed of glycoproteins, lipids, and adhesion molecules that regulate intercellular cohesion and restrict pathogen penetration.

A key component of epithelial defense is the glycocalyx, a gel-like carbohydrate-rich layer that is particularly prominent on gastrointestinal and urinary tract surfaces. The glycocalyx is composed primarily of membrane-bound glycoproteins, proteoglycans, especially glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), and glycolipids, which interact with soluble lectins, galectins, and extracellular matrix components. Together, these structures form a protective interface that limits microbial adhesion, regulates host-pathogen interactions, and contributes to the maintenance of tissue homeostasis37–39 (Figure 1).

GAGs are highly negatively charged structures with a protein core anchored in epithelial cells’ membrane and a variable extra-pending structure, which could be heparin sulfate (50–90%), chondroitin sulfate, keratin sulfate, hyaluronic acid, and dermatan sulfate.39 Alongside the physical barrier role, the moisturizing ability, and the molecular filter function, the glycocalyx prevents bacterial pathogenesis through its role as a sensing wire.40 Indeed, it interacts with cellular receptors, hormones, and pathogens and transfers information about the extracellular environment to the intracellular space, leading to diverse responses such as the release of the nitric oxide vasodilator, the rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton, and cell polarization.41,42

To adhere and proliferate, pathogenic bacteria need to degrade the glycocalyx first. It is the case of Salmonella that secretes forty-eight (48) types of glycosyl hydrolase, an enzyme able to create a breach in the glycocalyx structure and to promote bacterial invasion.43 Similarly, Streptococcus pneumoniae harbors a hyaluronate lysate that targets pulmonary hyaluronan sulfate and enhances the pathogen’s invasiveness.44,45 Staphylococcus aureus takes advantage of the ocular glycocalyx Syndecan-1 and Perlecan, two proteoglycans from the GAG family, which are coated with heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate. The bacteria induce shedding and prevent neutrophil response, leading to ocular keratitis.46 Instead of degrading it, some bacteria can also hijack the proteoglycan interaction to favorize their adhesion and penetration.47 Heparin sulfate extensions are the main structures implicated in bacterial exploitation. For example, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Helicobacter, Listeria, and Lactobacillus use these structures to attach GAG proteins and to adhere to the cell surface, forming colonies.48,49

Given the importance of the glycocalyx and GAGs as one of the first immune defenses in modulating bacterial pathogenicity and preventing infection, their therapeutic potential has been investigated.48,50 Exogenous administration of GAG analogs appears to restore mucosal barrier function in the urinary tract during urinary tract infections. Heparinoid and chondroitin analogues can inhibit pathogen growth through agonist/antagonist interactions. From the same perspective, synthetic glycol polymers emerge as glycans mimetics with optimized affinity, selectivity, multivalency, and stability, capable of targeting and neutralizing pathogens and toxins.50 Besides GAGs, mucins are glycocalyx glycoproteins composed of heavily O-glycosylated proteins with terminal sialic acids, secreted or anchored at the cell surface.50,51 These components also serve as a frontline defense against pathogens, functioning as a thick barrier and being shed from the cell surface to hinder adhesion.52,53

Alongside with adhesion strategies, bacteria have also developed modalities to counteract innate immune cells’ cytotoxicity. For example, the multiple peptide resistance factor protein (MprF) provides Staphylococcus aureus with physical avoidance through electrostatic repulsion. Moreover, bacteria use a hiding and disguising strategy to veil their PAMPs. Usually, the lipopolysaccharide motifs are recognized by the innate immune system through the lipid A fragment.54 Modifications of lipid A structure to less-acylated forms have been observed in resistant bacterial species, leading to reduced immune stimulation. Another example of structure-based immune evasion is given by Staphylococcal protein A (SpA).55 Which, rather than hiding, acts as a B-cell superantigen, triggering a strong, nonspecific immune response. Hence, the immune system is dispersed and unable to eliminate the pathogen effectively. Unlike capsule-mediated immune evasion, SpA functions as a B-cell superantigen by binding the Fc region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) and the variable heavy-chain 3 (VH3) domain of B-cell receptors. This interaction induces polyclonal B-cell activation, followed by apoptosis or functional inactivation of VH3-expressing B cells, thereby impairing the development of effective humoral immunity. By disrupting opsonization and antibody-mediated bacterial clearance, SpA promotes bacterial persistence and facilitates immune evasion.55

Collectively, bacterial cell-surface effectors constitute the first line of interaction with the host, facilitating adhesion, colonization, immune evasion, and persistence by modulating pathogen recognition and impairing innate and adaptive immune responses. However, successful infection extends beyond surface-associated virulence factors. Many bacterial pathogens further reshape the host environment through the secretion of specialized effector molecules that manipulate intracellular signaling pathways, alter inflammatory responses, disrupt tissue integrity, and promote bacterial survival. Understanding the coordinated actions of these secreted effectors is therefore essential for elucidating host-pathogen interactions and identifying novel targets for antibacterial intervention.

Bacterial Secreted Effectors

In addition to cell-surface interactions, bacteria and the host also interact through secreted molecules.22 Bacteria can excrete toxins. We enumerate two kinds in this category: endotoxins and exotoxins.27 Exotoxins are structures with a peptidic nature, while endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides.28 Exotoxins mostly come from Gram-positive bacteria. However, some Gram-negative bacteria include exotoxin producers such as Shigella dysenteriae and E. coli, which produce Shiga-like toxins with host-killing effects and enable neuro- and enterotoxicity.29,30

The exotoxin family also includes superantigens, which can spread throughout the general circulation, engender sepsis, and trigger severe T-cell reactivity, sometimes responsible for chronic autoimmune inflammatory diseases.28,31 Two well-characterized exotoxins in this category are the pyrogenic superantigens of Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and beta-hemolytic Streptococcus pyogenes. They are non-glycosylated pyrogenic and highly toxic proteins with low molecular weight and high resistance to heat and to biological and acidic proteolysis. S. aureus has more than 20 super-antigens, which play a major role in toxic shock syndrome occurrence. Moreover, superantigens bind outside the normal antigen-recognition site of the T-cell receptor (TCR) and specifically interact with certain Vβ (variable β-chain) families of the TCR, triggering Vβ-specific activation rather than antigen-specific activation, leading to massive activation of polyclonal T-cells, cytokine storm, and immune dysregulation and auto-immune reactivity.34 Several autoimmune diseases have been related to S.aureus super-antigens, such as Multiple Sclerosis (MS).56 A similar phenomenon is observed with Streptococcal superantigens.57 Although the role of these superantigens is still not clearly understood, they are implicated in an immune escape strategy, as the lack of specificity of the immune storm fails to block their proliferation. Therapeutic strategies were suggested to target the unspecified T cell activation and minimize it. The strategy includes antitoxin antibodies that bind and neutralize superantigens, immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory drugs to reduce cytokine storm and tissue damage, and appropriate antibiotics to eliminate the causative bacteria.31,58

A second bacterial exotoxin category includes extracellular proteases secreted by bacteria that may act within host cells or within the tissue stromal microenvironment.59,60 Proteases have two main functions: protein hydrolysis to provide nutrients for bacteria, and modulation of the microenvironment to support bacterial proliferation and dissemination. For example, Vibrio species produce extracellular proteolytic enzymes, including Vibrio-lysin, metalloproteases, and chymotrypsin-like serine proteases.61 Proteolytic enzymes play several roles in bacterial pathogenesis. They have the conventional role of exotoxins, which implicates toxicity against host cells and tissues. There, they induce tissue damage, bleeding, and bacterial dissemination. In septicemia, proteases are implicated in disrupting the organism’s homeostasis. And through the digestion of the mucosal matrix, bacteria could attach and proliferate. Moreover, in the case of wounds infected with bacteria such as Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species, the presence of Aureolysin, Staphopain, Staphylococcal serine protease, Streptopain, Streptolysin, and Cysteine proteinase may impact the wound environment, leading to healing delay and chronicity.60 Furthermore, bacteria use proteases to escape immune clearance by degrading host immune components, including immunoglobulins, complement proteins, antimicrobial peptides, and cytokines, thereby impairing opsonization, phagocytosis, chemotaxis, and inflammatory signaling.62,63

On the other hand, endotoxins are lipopolysaccharidic, pyrogenic molecules generated after bacterial lysis, cell division, or extracellular vesicle formation.64 Basically, lipopolysaccharides are components of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria and include the antigen O, Lipid A, and the core oligosaccharide.64,65 Once in the general circulation, they become pro-inflammatory inducers through the Toll-like receptor (TLR) recognition pathway.64,66 TLRs are members of the pattern recognition receptor (PRR) family, which recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). PAMPs are conserved parts of pathogens, such as membranes, DNA, and RNA, while DAMPs are stress-induced components, such as heat shock proteins. Therefore, the innate immune system, which expresses TLRs, is activated, implicating macrophages and antigen-presenting dendritic cells. Although this immune activation is necessary to eliminate the pathogens, it induces severe systemic inflammation and acute toxicity.65

Bacteria’s evading strategy also implicates the secretion of immune-modulating effectors. Shigella flexneri secretes Type III effector OspI, which induces NF-κB signaling inhibition to promote a mild immune reaction.67 Similarly, enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC) produces potent inhibitors of Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-κB) and tumor necrosis factor signaling: non-LEE-encoded effector E and B (NleE/NleB).68 Recruiting regulatory T cells is another part of the bacterial immune evasion strategy.69 Bacteria produce molecules that preferentially interact with Toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5), such as flagellin, thereby increasing Foxp3 transcription factor expression and reinforcing the suppressive activity of Treg cells.

New therapeutic modalities using anti-LPS antibodies or peptidic structures have emerged to prevent high adverse events induced by LPS persistence in the bloodstream, such as endotoxemia-induced cardiometabolic disorders.70–72 On the other hand, some bacteria may secrete virulence peptide effectors such as Ssek1 and Ssek3, which are injected into host cells through the type III secretion system (T3SS), where they catalyze the glycosylation of intracellular proteins.73,74 SseK1 and SseK3 secreted by Salmonella enterica in macrophages induced Arginine-GlcNAcylation of host cells’ death domain. This reaction implicates a non-canonical glycosylation in which an N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) sugar is covalently bound to the guanidinium group of an arginine residue. Moreover, the T3SS system encoded by Salmonella pathogenicity island-2 (SPI-2) enables the translocation of Salmonella effector proteins, promoting their proliferation within macrophages.74 An experimental study by Figueira et al demonstrated that cumulative T3SS mutations inhibit bacterial proliferation.

Overall, bacterial secreted effectors facilitate immune evasion and promote bacterial survival by disrupting host defense mechanisms. These virulence strategies are further reinforced by multicellular adaptations, including biofilm formation, granuloma development, and quorum sensing, which collectively enhance bacterial persistence and therapeutic tolerance. The following section examines the contribution of these adaptive processes to host–pathogen interactions.

Biofilm, Granulomas, and Quorum Sensing

In contrast to the planktonic bacterial state, biofilms are microbial communities attached to an organ surface, protected by a dense extracellular polymeric matrix.53 Biofilm formation is a major adaptive strategy that enhances bacterial persistence and contributes to antimicrobial tolerance. Within the biofilm matrix, genetically diverse bacterial populations coexist, facilitating horizontal gene transfer and the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance determinants. The extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix acts as a physical and biochemical barrier that limits the penetration of antibiotics and immune effectors, while the presence of metabolically inactive persister cells further reduces antimicrobial susceptibility. In addition, biofilms actively impair host immune responses by inhibiting phagocytosis, limiting complement-mediated opsonization, and modulating inflammatory signaling. For example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms consume complement opsonins, reducing macrophage recognition and activation, while extracellular polysaccharides sequester divalent cations, including calcium, thereby interfering with complement activation. Furthermore, the biosurfactant rhamnolipid, secreted within the biofilm matrix, induces lysis of neutrophils and macrophages, further compromising host defense. Similarly, Staphylococcus aureus biofilms secrete the metalloprotease aureolysin, which cleaves complement component C3 and other complement proteins, impairing opsonization and facilitating bacterial persistence.

Some bacterial pathogens, particularly Mycobacterium tuberculosis, promote the formation of granulomas as a hallmark of chronic infection.75 Granulomas are organized host immune structures composed predominantly of infected macrophages, epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells, and surrounding T lymphocytes. Although their primary function is to contain the infection and prevent bacterial dissemination, M. tuberculosis has evolved to exploit this microenvironment as a protected niche that facilitates immune evasion by impairing phagosome maturation, modulating antigen presentation, and promoting long-term persistence within host tissues.76 Two major granuloma phenotypes have been described: necrotizing granulomas, characterized by a central caseous necrotic core containing predominantly extracellular bacilli, and non-necrotizing granulomas, in which bacteria remain largely intracellular within macrophages. Non-necrotizing granulomas are commonly associated with latent tuberculosis infection, enabling prolonged bacterial persistence in a dormant state while avoiding complete immune clearance.77 Clinically, it is referred to as latent tuberculosis infection.78

Another example of bacterial persistence is Staphylococcus aureus, which can establish intracellular reservoirs that contribute to chronic and recurrent infections, particularly in skin and soft tissue wounds.21 Following internalization by professional and non-professional host cells, S. aureus undergoes phenotypic adaptation, including the formation of small-colony variants, enabling it to evade immune surveillance, tolerate antibiotic exposure, and persist intracellularly. These adaptive mechanisms impair bacterial clearance, delay wound healing and increase the risk of infection recurrence.

To survive in a community and display different escape mechanisms, bacteria rely on cell-to-cell communication. This ability is named quorum sensing. It allows the adjustment of their gene expression according to the environment, and this conversation significantly affects the host’s immune response.79 Hence, bacteria exhibit multicellular behavior, increasing population density, forming biofilms, and employing survival and resistance strategies.80 It helps bacteria turn virulence up or down, including the production of toxins and enzymes to invade tissues and evade the immune system.81 The core mechanism of this feature is the ability of bacteria to release and sense small extracellular molecules and effectors, and to translate these signals into behavior.82

Quorum-sensing molecules not only promote cellular organization inside the community but also interfere with host immune cells. Some bacteria use quorum sensing to downregulate the immune response by reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.83 For example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa utilizes the quorum-sensing signaling molecule 2-aminoacetophenone to promote persistence within macrophages by suppressing autophagy through epigenetic reprogramming of host lipid biosynthesis,84,85 while Vibrio cholerae quorum sensing promotes the activation of the host intestinal innate immune cells and shifts the host-pathogen interaction toward commensalism.86

Biofilm formation, intracellular persistence, immune evasion, granuloma formation and quorum-sensing communication are interconnected mechanisms that, together, allow bacterial survival under immune and antimicrobial pressure. These mechanisms are central to chronic infection, therapeutic failure and antimicrobial resistance, emphasizing the importance of combined therapeutic strategies directed at bacterial fitness and host-pathogen interactions.

Recent advances in biofilm disruption have increasingly focused on targeting the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which serves as a major barrier to antimicrobial penetration. Matrix-degrading enzymes such as DNase I, dispersin B, alginate lyase, glycoside hydrolases, and bacteriophage-derived depolymerases have demonstrated the ability to degrade extracellular DNA, polysaccharides, and structural matrix components, thereby enhancing antibiotic access and immune clearance. Bacteriophage-derived lysins and depolymerases are particularly promising because they simultaneously disrupt biofilm architecture and facilitate bacterial killing. In parallel, combined phage-antibiotic strategies have gained considerable attention through the concept of phage-antibiotic synergy (PAS). Recent studies have shown that combinations of bacteriophages with antibiotics such as tobramycin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, and β-lactams significantly improve biofilm eradication, reduce bacterial burden, and limit the emergence of resistant subpopulations. For example, phage T4 combined with tobramycin enhanced clearance of resistant Escherichia coli biofilms, while phage-amoxicillin combinations effectively inhibited Klebsiella pneumoniae biofilm formation. Furthermore, engineered phages expressing depolymerases or lysins have shown superior penetration into mature biofilms, highlighting the potential of integrated enzyme-phage-antibiotic approaches as next-generation therapeutics against biofilm-associated multidrug-resistant infections.

Differences Between Gram-Positive and Gram-Negative Bacteria in Host Immune Cell Interactions

Bacteria are classified into two main categories: Gram-positive and Gram-negative. These groups have a significant effect on how bacteria affect the immune system. The structural differences between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria fundamentally shape how they interact with host immune cells. Lipopolysaccharide is the main component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria and triggers a strong inflammatory response. This interaction is crucial for initiating the immune response and is often associated with sepsis in severe infections.54 On the other hand, Gram-positive bacteria have a strong peptidoglycan layer containing teichoic and lipoteichoic acids, which are important for activating immune cells, especially via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that trigger cytokine production and inflammation.87

These structural variations determine the pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) displayed to the host immune system. Gram-positive bacteria present lipoteichoic acid (LTA), peptidoglycan fragments, and lipoproteins, which are primarily recognized by TLR2 and (NOD2) receptors.88 Gram-negative bacteria, however, display LPS, a potent endotoxin recognized by TLR4; flagellin, recognized by TLR5; and porins, which trigger a generally stronger inflammatory response.89–91

Once internalized by host cells, both bacterial types employ distinct intracellular survival strategies. Gram-positive bacteria, such as Listeria monocytogenes, escape the phagosome using listeriolysin O to replicate in the cytosol.92 While Gram-negative bacteria, such as Salmonella enterica, modify their phagosomal compartment to create a replicative niche (the Salmonella-containing vacuole).93

The secreted immune evasion factors further illustrate this divergence: Gram-positive bacteria release superantigens that nonspecifically activate T cells, leading to immune dysregulation; cytolysins that destroy immune cells; and proteases, such as the S. aureus V8 protease.94 Gram-negative bacteria secrete proteases that degrade immune proteins, including T3SS effectors (ExoS, ExoT), which inhibit phagocytosis and NF-κB signaling.95

In summary, the key distinction lies in recognition and evasion mechanisms: Gram-negative bacteria are primarily sensed by TLR4 via LPS, triggering intense inflammation, and they frequently employ secreted proteases to cleave intracellular immune components directly. Gram-positive bacteria are mainly sensed by TLR2 and NOD2, eliciting moderate responses, and they rely more on extracellular exotoxins that destroy immune cells or dysregulate T-cell responses. Collectively, these differences underscore the diverse evolutionary strategies employed by Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria to manipulate host immunity and promote persistence. Understanding these pathogen-specific interactions provides a mechanistic foundation for the development of targeted immunotherapies and other next-generation antibacterial strategies.

The Evolving Landscape of Antibacterial Therapeutics Challenges and Perspectives

The discovery of antibacterial agents began in the late nineteenth century with early observations of microbial antagonism. In 1871, Joseph Lister reported the antimicrobial properties of Penicillium glaucum, suggesting its potential therapeutic value.96 In 1893, Bartolomeo Gosio isolated mycophenolic acid from Penicillium species, representing one of the earliest naturally derived antibacterial compounds. The early twentieth century marked the emergence of synthetic antimicrobial chemotherapy with arsenical compounds, most notably Salvarsan, developed by Paul Ehrlich for the treatment of syphilis. A major breakthrough occurred in 1928 when Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin after observing the inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus growth by Penicillium. Subsequent efforts by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, et al enabled the purification, large-scale production, and clinical application of penicillin during the Second World War.

Building on the pioneering work of René Dubos, who demonstrated that soil microorganisms produce antibacterial compounds, Selman Waksman established a systematic soil-screening strategy that led to the discovery of streptomycin and numerous additional antibiotics. This “Waksman platform” inaugurated the golden age of antibiotic discovery, during which more than twenty major antibiotic classes were identified, many of which remain integral to modern clinical practice. Pharmaceutical companies subsequently expanded antibiotic discovery through large-scale natural-product screening and medicinal chemistry programs. However, the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens, the scarcity of new antibiotic classes, escalating development costs, and increasingly stringent regulatory requirements have substantially slowed antibacterial innovation. Although antibiotics revolutionized twentieth-century medicine, the current antibacterial development pipeline remains insufficient to address the escalating global burden of antimicrobial resistance96 (Figure 2).

The Scientific and Regulatory Burden in Antibiotic Development

The antibiotic pipeline experienced a golden era in the twentieth century. However, the growth cycle declined rapidly. Unfortunately, the current situation indicates a loss of interest and a decrease in funding for antibiotic discovery by large firms. Efforts are limited to small startups and academic work. The reticence of major pharmaceutical companies to invest in new antibiotic discovery, combined with the rapid emergence of resistance, has played a critical role in diminishing pipeline efficacy.97 The economic losses during new antibiotic development and the non-profitable outcomes due to the high diversity of bacterial resistance and rapid evolution make it less attractive to investors. Moreover, the need to preserve new antibiotics from resistance limits their use in clinical care, leading to low economic benefit after their approval.

In addition to the struggle to develop new large-spectrum antibiotics that are economically profitable, regulatory and health agencies are fighting to preserve the financial capacity of the countries’ health insurance system. Thus, price negotiations usually lead to the lowest possible price and are not correlated with financial investment. For example, in France, each new drug is submitted for evaluation and ranked on a scale of improved medical service or clinical added value, called ASMR, which stands for “Amélioration du service médical rendu” in French. Only drugs situated within ASMR 1, 2, or 3 can have a price in the European corridor, which should be higher than the usual comparative drug.98 Unfortunately, reaching these categories is quiet difficult.

Furthermore, numerous scientific challenges exist in the development of antibiotics. The pipeline extension focused mostly on the chemical modification of empirical treatments, as this approach is less expensive, which limits its scope.99 Natural bacterial features constitute another challenge in drug development.99,100 Indeed, the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria blocks the activity of hydrophobic antibiotics, and efflux pumps expel drugs from the cells. Moreover, the adaptation ability of bacteria within an aggressive environment and the genetic modulation capability increase target variability and decrease the killing effect of novel molecules. Bacteria undergo spontaneous and random mutational changes, particularly under selective pressure, which increases the generation of resistant strains.101,102 Acquired resistance is mainly mediated by horizontal gene transfer (HGT). HGT facilitates the rapid dissemination of resistance determinants through plasmids, bacteriophages, transposons, and integrative genetic elements. These mobile resistance platforms accelerate bacterial adaptation, promote multidrug resistance, and substantially complicate therapeutic development and long-term infection control.103

Antibiotic Repurposing and Adjuvant Therapies

Recent studies have focused on repurposing antibiotics as a potential approach to combat MDR bacterial pathogens.104 Antibiotic repurposing involves the assessment of drugs already on the market for their efficacy against pathogens. One major advantage of this approach is that the drugs have already been tested for safety. For example, loperamide, an anti-diarrhea drug, has shown antibacterial properties. A mechanistic study revealed that this molecule promotes the overexpression of glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B in host cells, leading to autophagy and elimination of intracellular bacteria.105 N-acetylcysteine, a drug used to treat acetaminophen poisoning, has antibacterial properties and can destroy bacterial biofilms in respiratory tract infections.106 Reproposing the strategy has implicated a long list of conventional treatments, such as mitoxantrone, a cytostatic used in oncology; omeprazole, a proton pump inhibitor; and amlodipine, used for cardiovascular disorders.107

The second strategy to combat AMR is the addition of adjuvants to antibiotic formulations. A well-known example is the combination of Amoxicillin and Clavulanic acid. This β-lactamase inhibitor prevents the inactivation of β-lactam antibiotics by bacterial β-lactamases.108 Similarly, Sulbactam, Avibactam, and Relebactam act as beta-lactamase inhibitors adjuvants. Adjuvants also include efflux pump inhibitors, such as carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone, thereby increasing the concentration of antibiotics inside the bacterial cytoplasm.109 Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone can also demonstrated the capacity to disrupt the proton motive force of the bacterial cell membrane, leading to the destruction of Methicillin-resistant S. aureus.110 Membrane permeabilizers, such as polymyxins and cerium oxide nanoparticles, can hinder antibiotic resistance by increasing antibiotic concentrations within bacteria.108 The rationale for using antibiotic adjuvants is to create a synergistic effect.111 This approach not only improves drug efficacy but also reduces the development of pathogen resistance.1 Indeed, lowering the antibiotic dosage while improving its potency slows bacterial evolution toward resistant strains by diminishing the selection pressure.

Although antibiotic repurposing and adjuvant therapies have shown considerable potential for restoring the efficacy of existing antimicrobial agents, their effectiveness remains constrained by the continued evolution of bacterial resistance and the limited lifespan of antibiotic-based interventions. These challenges have stimulated interest in alternative antibacterial strategies, including the re-emergence of bacteriophage therapy as a highly specific approach for targeting multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Re-Emerging Bacteriophages Approach and Its Burdens

Given the limitations encountered in the development of new antibiotics, bacteriophage therapy has re-emerged as a promising approach for managing resistance.112 This strategy exploits viruses that replicate within bacteria, leading to bacterial lysis at the end of the cycle113 (Figure 3).

Bacteriophages are classified into two principal categories based on their life cycle. Lysogenic phages, also known as temperate phages, insert their genetic material into bacterial DNA and replicate in host cells without destroying the host or producing virions.114 And the lytic phages that are more adapted to therapy.115 The advantages of phages in combating microbial resistance have increased interest in their clinical application against resistant strains. The safety and efficiency of bacteriophages depend on their ability to target specific bacterial strains. This narrow activity range is beneficial because it prevents off-target effects on human cells and beneficial microbiota.116 However, this highlights one of the main obstacles in using bacteriophages as therapeutics; the narrowed targeting range of many phages limits activity against heterogeneous bacterial communities within infected tissues, thereby, detering clinical applicability, and reducing therapeutic consistency and reproducibility.117 The co-existence of genetically diverse bacterial populations and phages as environmental stressors may allow the emergence of phage-resistant variants through receptor modification and membrane adaptation.113,118,119

Although bacteriophage therapy primarily acts through direct bacterial lysis, it can indirectly influence host immune responses. The disruption of biofilm and release of bacterial antigens during phage-mediated lysis enhances immune recognition and facilitate pathogen clearance. Therefore, phage-based approaches can be viewed not only as pathogen-targeted therapies but also as complementary components of integrated antibacterial immunotherapeutic strategies.120 Yet, host immune responses, including antibody-mediated phage neutralization, can further limit repeated administration and long-term therapeutic efficacy.121,122

One way to select bacteriophages pools is the isolation from environmental sources.123 Abebe et al have investigated the isolation, characterization, and therapeutic potential of lytic bacteriophages against multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae. Researchers isolated 102 bacteriophages from wastewater, hospital sewage, and soil samples. The isolated phages displayed variable host ranges, lysing 8.7% to 63% of 46 clinical K. pneumoniae isolates.

The study comprehensively characterized the phages by measuring their host range, efficiency of plating, titers, optimal multiplicity of infection, replication dynamics, and environmental stability. Many phages exhibited high titers (up to 4.3 × 1011 PFU/mL), short latent periods (15–35 minutes), large burst sizes (up to 323 PFU per infected cell), and remained stable at physiological temperatures (25–40°C) and across a pH range of 5–9, indicating favorable properties for therapeutic use.

Using a computational approach through Phage Cocktail algorithm, the authors designed a minimal four-phage cocktail (TTM-TASP117, GKM-TASP92, ZHS-TASP18, and BYM-TASP37) that achieved 100% lysis coverage of all 42 susceptible clinical isolates.

Another promising phages against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, the lytic phage “phiMR003” exhibited broad activity against clinical MRSA isolates and significantly reduced bacterial burden, inflammation, and accelerated wound healing in experimental wound infection models.124 Similarly, the two-phage cocktail “NOV012”, composed of phages P68 and K710 demonstrated broad lytic activity against S. aureus, including MRSA strains, and was shown to be safe following repeated topical administration in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis.

To promote the clinical potential of bacteriophages in combating resistant bacterial strains and resistant biofilms, several approaches are investigated, such as using phage cocktails or engineered bacteriophages.125,126 Engineering methods are explored to create the next generation of phages with a broader host range and enhanced efficacy.119 The editing tools include homologous recombination, in vitro assembly, and CRISPR-Cas-mediated editing.127 Besides, chemical treatment modifies reactive chemical groups within the phage capsid. It allows combining antibacterial molecules or nanoparticles, such as silver nanomaterials and antibiotics, to enhance and orient the killing effect against resistant bacteria.128,129 This last strategy enables the potential of non-lytic phages as drug delivery agents.130 A relevant example of a recombinant drug-conjugated phage was introduced by Yang et al, who engineered a M13 filamentous non-lytic phage, naturally targeting Escherichia coli, to target Pseudomonas aeruginosa and deliver conjugated Polymyxin B bacteriotherapy.131

Furthermore, phages with double-stranded DNA synthesize lytic enzymes that destroy bacterial membranes through amidase, glycosidase, depolymerase, lytic transglycosylase, and endopeptidase activities.132,133 These lysin molecules are gaining interest as anti-microbial factors due to their impressive selectivity. For example, Staphylococcus-specific endolysin “HY-133” targets specific cell-surface epitopes and enable a precise, destructive bactericidal effect. Still, the clinical application of these molecules is challenged by the allergic immune response they mediate in the host organism and their narrow target range. Hence, engineering recombinant endolysins provides a new avenue to explore the endolysin landscape.

Phage therapy is particularly attractive for biofilm-associated infections because phage-derived enzymes degrade extracellular matrix components and facilitate penetration into dense bacterial communities.134 In addition to disrupting biofilm architecture, phages may reduce bacterial burden, limit persisters survival, and enhance the activity of conventional antibacterial agents.135

Recent clinical studies in the USA, Europe and Asia have investigated the safety and therapeutic potential of bacteriophage therapy for the treatment of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.136 Initial clinical trials, such as the CYPHY and PhagoDAIRI studies, showed promising safety profiles and preliminary therapeutic efficacy, particularly for persistent and biofilm-associated infections.137,138 Clinical evidence has expanded through compassionate-use programs and early-phase trials. A notable example is the Intravenous Phage Therapy (IPATH) program at the University of California San Diego, which reported a 70% clinical success rate (7 of 10 patients) among its first compassionate-use cases involving severe multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections, with no major adverse events observed.139 These treatments targeted pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Staphylococcus aureus.

Significant progress has also occurred in engineered phage therapeutics. CRISPR-based phage products have entered human clinical testing. LBP-EC01, a CRISPR-Cas3-enhanced bacteriophage cocktail, completed Phase I/II evaluation for uncomplicated urinary tract infections and demonstrated favorable safety, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic profiles.140 Similarly, SNIPR001 completed a Phase I randomized, double-blind, first-in-human dose-escalation study.141 Despite these advances, no CRISPR-based phage therapeutic has yet received approval from major regulatory agencies such as the FDA or EMA as of 2026, and these therapies remain investigational.

Indeed, large-scale clinical validation, standardized manufacturing protocols, optimized dosing strategies and regulatory harmonization are major hurdles that hinder widespread clinical implementation.142,143 In October 2025, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) released guidelines for phage quality assessment. European regulation seems very cautious about phage therapy, as the workshops began in June 2015.144 Indeed, for regulatory authorities, the subject is new and raises several concerns regarding safety and efficacy assessment, with a large scientific knowledge gap. In USA, the regulatory framework for phages is more advanced. The therapy is classified as a biological product, and the FDA has already approved the only Investigational New Drug (IND) in the world for an adaptive phage therapy bank, allowing extensive clinical trials.145 The principle of this concept is to use a phage cocktail adapted to the patient’s bacterial isolate sensitivity.146 However, there is still no commercialization authorization in the USA for any therapeutic phage, highlighting the regulatory and efficacy hurdles this therapy still faces.

Overall, recent regulatory innovation, expanding GMP manufacturing capacity, and encouraging early clinical results support the growing role of bacteriophage therapy as a complementary strategy against MDR bacterial infections. Nevertheless, larger randomized controlled trials and greater regulatory harmonization are still required before phage therapy can become a routine standard of care.

Challenges related to host range specificity, bacterial resistance to phages, and variable clinical outcomes continue to limit its widespread application.

CRISPR-Based Antibacterial Therapy

CRISPR-Cas technology has emerged as one of the most transformative platforms for combating AMR, offering unprecedented precision to selectively eliminate pathogenic bacteria, disrupt virulence determinants, or reverse antibiotic resistance while preserving the commensal microbiota. Unlike conventional broad-spectrum antibiotics, CRISPR-based antimicrobials employ programmable guide RNAs to direct Cas nucleases toward specific chromosomal or plasmid-borne DNA sequences, enabling species- or strain-specific bacterial eradication. Two principal antibacterial strategies have been developed: a pathogen-centric approach, which cleaves essential chromosomal genes leading to bacterial death, and a gene-centric approach, which selectively targets antibiotic resistance genes located on mobile genetic elements, thereby restoring bacterial susceptibility without necessarily eliminating the organism. Examples include CRISPR-mediated removal of IncF resistance plasmids in Escherichia coli, knockout of resistance genes such as mecA in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and silencing of the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump in E. coli, which significantly increased susceptibility to rifampicin, erythromycin, and tetracycline.147–149

Despite these remarkable advances, several scientific, ethical, and regulatory challenges continue to limit widespread clinical implementation. The most significant safety concern remains off-target genome editing, where Cas nucleases cleave unintended DNA sequences due to imperfect guide RNA specificity, potentially resulting in unpredictable mutations, genomic instability, or undesirable biological effects. Efficient delivery of CRISPR components also remains challenging because viral vectors, bacteriophages, lipid nanoparticles, and polymeric nanocarriers each possess limitations related to immunogenicity, cargo capacity, tissue specificity, and long-term stability. Another emerging concern is the evolution of bacterial resistance to CRISPR therapeutics. Pathogens may evade CRISPR-mediated killing through point mutations within target sequences, deletion or disruption of Cas genes, loss of targeting spacers, or acquisition of anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins, more than fifty families of which have already been identified.150 Moreover, the remarkable diversity of microbial communities and horizontal gene transfer may complicate the sustained efficacy of CRISPR-based antimicrobial strategies, emphasizing the need for combination approaches with antibiotics or bacteriophages.

Ethical considerations surrounding CRISPR extend beyond technical safety and have generated intense international debate. Although somatic genome editing for therapeutic purposes is increasingly accepted, human germline editing remains highly controversial because genetic modifications introduced into embryos, sperm, or oocytes can be inherited by future generations. Future clinical applications should prioritize somatic editing, rigorous evaluation of off-target effects, standardized long-term safety monitoring, validated delivery systems, and robust informed consent procedures. International organizations increasingly recommend harmonized regulatory frameworks, biosecurity measures to prevent misuse, and multidisciplinary collaboration among scientists, clinicians, ethicists, and policymakers. Continued integration of artificial intelligence and bioinformatics for guide RNA design, together with improved delivery technologies and highly precise editing platforms such as base and prime editors, is expected to further enhance specificity while minimizing safety risks. Hence, CRISPR-based antibacterial strategies represent a paradigm shift in the management of antimicrobial resistance. Nevertheless, their successful clinical translation will ultimately depend not only on technological innovation but also on careful ethical governance, rigorous clinical validation, and globally coordinated regulatory oversight to ensure that these powerful genome-editing technologies are implemented safely, responsibly, and equitably.

These limitations have reinforced the need for broader therapeutic approaches that not only target the pathogen but also strengthen host defense mechanisms. In this context, bacterial immunotherapy has emerged as a holistic strategy that seeks to modulate host immune responses, enhance pathogen clearance, and improve treatment outcomes while reducing the selective pressures associated with conventional antimicrobial therapies.

Bacterial Immunotherapy as a Holistic Approach

Host immune responses are critical for resolving bacterial infections and become an important target for therapeutic modulation.151 The body is programmed to handle microbial threats through the skin barrier and the innate and adaptive immune systems. The first and most common approach leveraging immune system against infective agents is vaccination. Routine vaccination programs are deployed under the supervision of the World Health Organization (WHO) worldwide against high-risk bacteria such as Neisseria meningitides, Bordetella pertussis, Bordetella parapertussis, Haemophilus Influenzae type b, Leptospira interrogans, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Clostridium tetani, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Corynebacterium ulcerans, and Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis.152,153 Despite the efforts to vaccinate a large population, over 20 million children are under-vaccinated, comprising a high range of possible bacterial hosts susceptible to having severe infections and to deploy antibiotic resistance.154,155 The hurdles to maximizing vaccination rates are mainly related to the social conditions of the population, including limited access to the health care system and, on the other hand, strong ideological anti-vaccination convictions and concerns about potential side effects.

Successful vaccination stimulation of the immune system paved the way for rapid immunomodulation to combat bacterial infections. Therefore, while anti-tumoral immunotherapy discovery received the Nobel Prize in Medicine, bacterial Immunotherapy emerged as a holistic approach that enhances host immunity to tackle both extracellular and intracellular bacterial pathogens.156

Despite the high integration of antibacterial vaccines into the healthcare system, the FDA has approved only three antibacterial antibodies to date: Bezlotoxumab, Raxibacumab, and Obiltoxaximab.157 Alongside Eflapegrastim, a granulocyte growth factor, has also been approved in the USA since 2022 to prevent bacterial infections in neutropenic patients with non-myeloid malignancies.158,159

Despite their high specificity and favorable safety, monoclonal antibody-based therapies face several limitations that may restrict their widespread application in antibacterial treatment. Antigenic variability among bacterial strains reduces target recognition and compromise therapeutic efficacy, particularly when virulence factors or surface antigens exhibit significant genetic diversity. In addition, the high costs associated with antibody development, manufacturing, and administration may limit accessibility, especially in resource-constrained settings. Furthermore, the pathogen-specific nature of monoclonal antibodies results in a relatively narrow spectrum of activity, necessitating accurate pathogen identification and potentially limiting their utility in polymicrobial or rapidly evolving infections. These challenges highlight the need for broader-spectrum targets, cost-effective production strategies, and combination approaches to maximize the clinical impact of antibody-based antibacterial therapies.

Overall, the slow pace of antibacterial therapeutic development and the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens highlight the urgent need for novel immune-based and multimodal therapeutic platforms. Continued advances in monoclonal antibodies, immune modulation, engineered biologics and combination therapeutic systems may provide new opportunities to augment antibacterial efficacy and to prevent resistance-associated therapeutic failure.160,161 The development of effective immunotherapeutic strategies relies on a comprehensive understanding of the complex interactions between pathogens and the host immune system. Bacterial survival, persistence, and disease progression are governed by a diverse array of virulence determinants and immune-evasion mechanisms that enable pathogens to colonize host tissues, modulate immune responses, and resist clearance. Therefore, elucidating the molecular and cellular pathways underlying host-pathogen interactions is critical for identifying novel therapeutic targets and designing next-generation antibacterial immunotherapies.

Anti-Bacterial Immunotherapy Fundamentals

The pharmaceutical market is experiencing significant expansion in immune modulators. In contrast to immunosuppressing drugs used in transplantation or anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic drugs that lead to immune depletion, immunotherapy is an innovative therapeutic approach that targets the immune system to modulate and restore the organism’s defenses.162,163 This approach is widely applied against tumoral pathologies and emerges as a promising approach for infectious diseases.

The general overview of immunotherapy is organized into active modalities that stimulate immune effector function and passive modalities, such as antibody, lymphocyte, and cytokine therapies. These systems target both innate and adaptive immune cells and include both biological and chemical actors.164

Antibacterial immunotherapy works by potentiating innate and adaptive immune responses to stop the growth of pathogens. Herein, we explore approaches developed in the field of antibacterial Immunotherapy.

Probiotic-Driven Approaches in Immunotherapy

The microbiome is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of host-pathogen interactions and immune responses, thereby influencing susceptibility to infection and the efficacy of antibacterial immunotherapies.165 Commensal microorganisms contribute to colonization resistance by competing with pathogens for ecological niches, producing antimicrobial molecules, and promoting host antimicrobial defenses. In addition, microbiota-derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, modulate innate and adaptive immune responses by regulating inflammatory pathways and maintaining immune homeostasis. Conversely, microbial dysbiosis may impair barrier integrity, alter immune regulation, and increase susceptibility to infection and excessive inflammation. These observations suggest that host-microbiome interactions may influence responses to immunotherapeutic interventions and should be considered in the development of future antibacterial treatment strategies.

Probiotic-driven approaches in antibacterial immunotherapy have emerged as a promising strategy to complement conventional antibiotic treatment, particularly in the face of increasing antimicrobial resistance. Rather than directly replacing antibiotics, probiotics enhance the host’s immune defenses while simultaneously suppressing pathogenic bacteria through multiple interconnected mechanisms. According to the review by Adejumo et al, probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species inhibit bacterial infections by producing antimicrobial compounds, including bacteriocins, organic acids, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which reduce intestinal pH and create an unfavorable environment for pathogenic microorganisms.166 They also compete with harmful bacteria for nutrients and adhesion sites, preventing pathogen colonization and biofilm formation. In addition, probiotics strengthen epithelial barrier integrity by increasing the expression of tight-junction proteins such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudins, thereby reducing bacterial translocation across mucosal surfaces. Their immunotherapeutic effects are equally important, as probiotics activate pattern-recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD receptors, leading to enhanced production of antimicrobial peptides and balanced cytokine responses that promote effective pathogen clearance while limiting excessive inflammation. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated beneficial effects against several bacterial pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Listeria monocytogenes, Helicobacter pylori, and Staphylococcus aureus. In urinary tract infections, probiotic strains such as Lactobacillus crispatus, L. rhamnosus, and L. reuteri reduce bacterial adhesion, inhibit biofilm formation, restore healthy urogenital microbiota, and lower recurrence rates, particularly when used alongside antibiotics. In bacterial diarrheal diseases, probiotics shorten disease duration, reduce pathogen shedding, and enhance mucosal immunity by restoring microbial balance and reinforcing the intestinal barrier. Furthermore, probiotics can neutralize bacterial toxins and modulate inflammatory pathways through increased production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 while maintaining appropriate innate and adaptive immune responses. These combined antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities make probiotics valuable adjuncts to antibacterial therapy, helping to improve treatment outcomes without promoting antibiotic resistance. However, their clinical effectiveness remains strain-specific, and further research is needed to establish optimal strains, dosages, treatment duration, and long-term safety before widespread implementation in antibacterial immunotherapy. These findings suggest that probiotic-based approaches may complement emerging antibacterial platforms by modulating host immunity, reducing pathogen virulence, interfering with quorum-sensing pathways, and limiting biofilm formation.167 However, there are a number of important caveats. A large percentage of the research has been conducted in vitro or in animal models, where the different results obtained from the different probiotics may prove problematic for application. Host-Directed Immunotherapy

Host-directed immunotherapy (HDT) aims to modulate host biological pathways that are exploited by bacterial pathogens to establish infection, evade immune clearance, and persist within host tissues. Rather than directly targeting bacterial structures, HDT seeks to restore effective host immune responses while limiting excessive inflammation and tissue damage.177 By reprogramming the host microenvironment, these approaches enhance pathogen clearance, improve antibiotic penetration into infected tissues, and reduce pathological processes such as granuloma formation, fibrosis, and chronic inflammation that contribute to treatment failure. Consequently, HDT represents a complementary strategy to conventional antimicrobial therapy, with the additional advantage of exerting minimal selective pressure for the emergence of antimicrobial resistance.

Tuberculosis is one of the most extensively investigated models for host-directed immunotherapy. Despite prolonged multidrug treatment with pyrazinamide, isoniazid, rifampicin, and ethambutol, Mycobacterium tuberculosis survives by manipulating host immune responses, inhibiting phagosome maturation, persisting within macrophages, and exploiting granulomatous lesions that limit drug penetration. HDT therefore aims to counteract these host-mediated mechanisms by restoring antimicrobial immune functions and remodeling the infected tissue microenvironment.178 For example, mesenchymal stem cell-based therapies have been investigated for their capacity to modulate immune responses, promote tissue repair, and enhance macrophage antimicrobial functions, including autophagy and nitric oxide production, thereby restricting intracellular bacterial replication.179 Likewise, epigenetic modulation has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue. RGFP966, a selective histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) inhibitor, reverses M. tuberculosis-induced epigenetic alterations, enhancing macrophage antimicrobial activity, autophagy, and pro-inflammatory responses while reducing intracellular bacterial survival.180

Although most host-directed immunotherapies remain at the preclinical or early clinical stage, accumulating evidence suggests that combining immune modulation with conventional antimicrobial therapy may improve treatment outcomes while reducing the likelihood of resistance development. Nevertheless, further clinical trials are required to establish their long-term efficacy, safety, and optimal integration into antibacterial treatment regimens.

Antibodies and Cellular Immunomodulatory Strategies

Antibodies are the most widespread Immunotherapy. Since their approval in 1986, they become a leading element of immunotherapies.181 Nowadays, several types of antibodies are engineered such as the chimeric, the humanized, the fragmented, and the drug antibody conjugates.182 Antibacterial antibodies have been explored to hijack bacterial resistance,183 used alone or conjugated to antibiotics.184 Chen et al have engineered human immunoglobulins targeting Staphylococcus aureus, thereby blocking protein A (SpA) evasion and enabling effective opsonization. Indeed, Staphylococcus aureus evades the immune system using protein A, which binds the fragment crystallizable region (Fc) of IgG antibodies and blocks opsonization. The Fc region normally recruits immune cells, while binding to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) protects antibodies from degradation and prolongs their half-life. Chen et al engineered human antibodies with modified Fcγ regions that avoid SpA binding while retaining FcRn interaction, thereby enhancing bacterial clearance and maintaining long-lasting antibody activity.185

Recent research has highlighted the importance of immune checkpoint regulation as a host-directed strategy for combating bacterial infections.186 Immune checkpoints such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing protein 3 (TIM-3), T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) are increasingly recognized as key modulators of host-pathogen interactions. Bacterial infections consistently induce upregulation of immune checkpoint pathways, suggesting that pathogens exploit these mechanisms to suppress protective immune responses.186,187 Immune checkpoint blockade has shown promising results in restoring antimicrobial immunity. Across the reviewed animal studies, it reduced bacterial burden in 60% of experiments, particularly in polymicrobial infections and infections caused by Pseudomonas and Escherichia species.186 Therapeutic administration after infection was more effective than prophylactic treatment, indicating that checkpoint inhibition may be most beneficial during established infections.

Mechanistically, checkpoint inhibition enhances host defense by reinvigorating exhausted T cells, increasing CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses, reducing immune-cell apoptosis, and modulating cytokine production. Emerging evidence also suggests that checkpoint pathways regulate innate immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells. Recent studies have identified TIGIT blockade enhanced the cytotoxic activity of human CD8⁺ T cells by increasing their production of effector molecules and improving their ability to eliminate intracellular bacteria. Furthermore, inhibition of TIGIT significantly reduced the pulmonary bacterial burden in mice infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, highlighting its potential as a host-directed immunotherapeutic strategy.188 Moreover, genetic ablation or antibody blockade of TIGIT improves immune responses during bacterial infection and polymicrobial sepsis through CD4+ regulation.189 TIM-3 antibody inhibition enhanced clearance of Chlamydia psittaci and reduced T-cell apoptosis in sepsis models.190 PD-1/PD-L1 antibody mediated blockade has also shown beneficial effects in models of Staphylococcus aureus osteomyelitis infection by reversing immune dysfunction and restoring antimicrobial activity.191

Translation of antibacterial antibodies into human studies remains limited but preliminary studies are encouraging. Early-phase clinical trials of anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies in patients with sepsis-induced immunosuppression demonstrated acceptable safety profiles, no evidence of hypercytokinemia, and improved markers of immune function, including increased monocytic expression.192

Overall, recent developments indicate that immune checkpoint regulation represents a promising host-pathogen modulation strategy that could complement conventional antimicrobial therapies, particularly in the context of multidrug-resistant infections and sepsis.

Cellular immunotherapy has emerged as a treatment for tumoral diseases. Several approaches have been enumerated, including infiltrating T cells, Engineered T-cell receptor (TCR), and Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T).164 These strategies are gaining interest for resolving infectious resistance in viruses, fungi, and bacteria.193 However, applying this strategy against bacteria faces several challenges. The main problem is that CART cells need immunodepression to favor their activation; this situation can worsen the ongoing infection.194

Cytokines and Antimicrobial Peptides Immunotherapeutics

Other biological antibacterial Immunotherapeutics consist of biomolecules such as cytokines and antimicrobial peptides, which enhance the host immune response or directly target pathogens.

Cytokines are critical regulators of the immune system and have emerged as promising antimicrobial immunotherapeutic agents because of their ability to enhance host defense mechanisms against infectious pathogens. Unlike conventional antibiotics that directly target microorganisms, cytokine-based therapies strengthen the host immune response by activating innate and adaptive immune cells, promoting pathogen clearance, and restoring immune homeostasis. Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) is one of the most extensively studied cytokines and plays a central role in activating macrophages, enhancing antigen presentation, and stimulating intracellular killing of pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Listeria monocytogenes, and Salmonella species.195–197 Early in 1991, a clinical use of recombinant IFN-γ has shown benefits as an adjunctive therapy in chronic granulomatous disease and refractory mycobacterial infections by improving phagocytic function and reducing infection severity.198 Interleukin-7 (IL-7) has also gained attention for its capacity to reverse infection-induced lymphopenia by promoting T-cell survival, proliferation, and restoration of adaptive immunity, particularly in patients with sepsis-induced immunosuppression.199 Despite their therapeutic potential, cytokine-based immunotherapies require careful administration because excessive cytokine signaling may trigger systemic inflammation, tissue damage, or cytokine release syndrome.200 Consequently, current research focuses on optimizing cytokine dosage, timing, and combination therapies with antibiotics, vaccines, or immune checkpoint inhibitors to maximize antimicrobial efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. As antimicrobial resistance continues to threaten global health, cytokine immunotherapy represents an important host-directed therapeutic strategy capable of enhancing immune competence, improving pathogen clearance, and complementing conventional antimicrobial treatments.

On the other hand, anti-microbial peptides (AMPs) are naturally occurring molecules produced by diverse organisms as part of innate immune defense.201 Unlike conventional antibiotics, AMPs often exert rapid antibacterial activity through membrane disruption, intracellular targeting, and immunomodulatory functions. Their broad-spectrum activity, low propensity to develop resistance, and ability to interfere with biofilm formation have attracted considerable interest for the treatment of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections. Beyond direct antibacterial activity, many AMPs modulate host immunity by recruiting immune cells, enhancing pathogen clearance, regulating cytokine production, and promoting tissue repair.202

Although AMPs were initially regarded as endogenous antibiotics because of their membrane-disruptive activity against bacteria, increasing evidence demonstrates that they also function as potent immunomodulators, making them promising candidates for antibacterial immunotherapy. At physiological concentrations, many AMPs contribute to bacterial clearance primarily by regulating host immune responses rather than by directly killing pathogens. They act as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity by altering host gene expression, inducing chemokine production, modulating dendritic cell and T-cell responses, suppressing excessive inflammation, and promoting tissue repair, thereby facilitating infection resolution while minimizing host damage. For example, the human cathelicidin LL-37 enhances leukocyte recruitment by inducing chemokines such as CXCL8 (IL-8), CCL2 (MCP-1), and IFN-α, leading to the accumulation of neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and T lymphocytes at infected tissues.203–205 Similarly, human α-defensins (HNP-1 and HNP-2) recruit monocytes, hBD2 attracts mast cells, while hBD2, hBD3, and hBD4 promote the migration of immature dendritic cells, memory T cells, monocytes, and macrophages through CCR6-dependent signaling, thereby strengthening antigen presentation and adaptive immune activation.206–208 In addition to recruiting immune cells, AMPs finely regulate inflammatory signaling. LL-37 suppresses excessive Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated cytokine production in dendritic cells and macrophages, inhibits lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory responses, and prevents uncontrolled inflammation while preserving antimicrobial immunity.209 Beyond immune regulation, AMPs also accelerate tissue regeneration following bacterial infection. LL-37 promotes keratinocyte migration, re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, and wound closure, whereas human β-defensin-2 stimulates endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation, supporting vascularization and tissue repair. Collectively, these multifunctional activities demonstrate that AMPs not only eliminate bacteria but also orchestrate protective immune responses, limit damaging inflammation, and enhance tissue healing. This dual antimicrobial-immunomodulatory mechanism positions AMPs as highly attractive therapeutic agents for antibacterial immunotherapy, particularly against multidrug-resistant bacterial infections where restoration of effective host immunity is essential.202,210

AMPs are presented as a rational answer to antimicrobial resistance because their membrane-centred, multi-target action is assumed to be hard for bacteria to evade.211,212 Yet despite roughly 4000 catalogued peptides in Antimicrobial Peptide Database (version 3), hosted at the University of Nebraska Medical Center and decades of work since gramicidin in 1939, fewer than ten peptide antibiotics have reached the clinic, including colistin, polymyxin B, daptomycin, vancomycin, gramicidin, bacitracin. Despite their strong conceptual appeal, antimicrobial peptides are limited by a series of fundamental biological and translational constraints that have hindered their clinical success. These include rapid proteolytic degradation, poor pharmacokinetic stability, reduced activity under physiological salt and serum conditions, host cytotoxicity linked to their amphipathic membrane-disruptive mechanism, and emerging bacterial adaptive responses that can attenuate susceptibility. In addition, manufacturing complexity and high production costs further restrict their scalability and clinical deployment. Collectively, these limitations explain why only a small number of peptide-based antibiotics have reached clinical use despite decades of research and thousands of identified candidates. These challenges have, in turn, driven the development of engineering-based strategies aimed at improving AMP stability, selectivity, and in vivo performance, including bioinformatic and machine-learning-guided design, and bottom-up de novo and multivalent peptide design.213

Engineered peptides and peptide-based delivery systems are currently being explored to improve stability, reduce toxicity, and enhance therapeutic efficacy.214 Although challenges related to proteolytic degradation, pharmacokinetics, and manufacturing remain, AMPs represent one of the most promising classes of next-generation antibacterial immunotherapeutics. Engineered AMPs have been developed to overcome the intrinsic limitations of natural peptides and represent a major focus of current antimicrobial design strategies.213 Approaches such as amino-acid substitution, cyclization, truncation, stapling, incorporation of D-amino acids, hybrid peptide construction, and computational or machine-learning-guided de novo design have demonstrated clear improvements in proteolytic stability, membrane affinity, and in vitro antimicrobial potency. In several cases, these modifications have expanded activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens, including ESKAPE organisms, and enhanced efficacy against biofilm-associated infections and dormant persister cell populations. However, despite these advances, a critical gap remains between optimized in vitro activity and clinically relevant in vivo performance. A major limitation of engineered AMPs is the persistent trade-off between potency and selectivity. Modifications that increase cationicity and hydrophobicity often enhance antimicrobial activity but simultaneously increase cytotoxicity toward mammalian membranes, reducing therapeutic indices. Similarly, strategies that improve serum stability do not necessarily address reduced efficacy under physiological ionic strength or complex infection microenvironments such as biofilms and polymicrobial communities. Furthermore, many engineered peptides are evaluated primarily under simplified laboratory conditions, which may overestimate their effectiveness in clinically relevant settings. Although computational and AI-driven approaches have accelerated peptide discovery, they remain constrained by limited predictive power for mechanism of action, toxicity, and in vivo behavior.

Another key concern is that most evidence of synergy is derived from simplified in vitro assays that do not adequately capture the complexity of in vivo infection environments, including polymicrobial communities, host immune interactions, and dynamic tissue barriers. Manufacturing complexity, scalability issues, and increased production costs further complicate clinical translation, particularly for multi-component or chemically modified constructs. Importantly, there is also a risk that partial biofilm disruption or sublethal exposure may select for more resilient bacterial subpopulations, potentially undermining long-term efficacy. Collectively, these limitations highlight a gap between promising synergy observed in experimental systems and the requirements for clinically robust, reproducible, and scalable antibacterial therapies.

Peptide-nanoparticle and peptide-antibiotic conjugates have emerged as a prominent strategy to enhance the therapeutic performance of AMPs and conventional antibiotics.215,216 By integrating AMPs with nanocarriers or small-molecule antibiotics, these platforms aim to improve pharmacokinetic stability, increase local drug concentration at infection sites, enhance membrane permeabilization, and overcome bacterial defense mechanisms such as efflux pumps and biofilm barriers. Nanoparticles offer new perspectives for anti-bacterial immunotherapy that are explored in the following section.

Nano-Immunotherapy

Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative platform for antibacterial immunotherapy by enabling the simultaneous enhancement of antimicrobial efficacy, immune activation, and targeted drug delivery. Unlike conventional antibiotics, nanomaterials can be engineered to function as immunomodulatory carriers, vaccine platforms, and stimuli-responsive delivery systems that precisely target infected tissues while minimizing systemic toxicity. Their tunable physicochemical properties, including size, surface charge, composition, and functionalization, facilitate efficient interaction with immune cells, improved antigen stability, prolonged circulation, and controlled therapeutic release. Consequently, nanomaterials have become attractive candidates for overcoming antibiotic resistance while promoting host-directed immune responses. Recent advances have demonstrated the successful application of diverse nanoplatforms, including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, niosomes, carbon-based nanomaterials, exosomes, and outer membrane vesicles, in combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

To reinforce immune activity against resistant bacteria, the concept of antigen Nano vaccines has emerged as the next generation of vaccine strategy, displaying strong adjuvant characteristics and inducing consistent immune activation while avoiding the toxicity of traditional aluminum adjuvants.217 Recent advances in nano vaccines have demonstrated significant potential against antimicrobial-resistant pathogens. These nano systems use resistance effectors within pathogens, such as antibiotic-degrading enzymes, efflux pumps, and super-virulence factors as immune targets.218 Hence, they triggers specific immune destruction of the bacteria’s resistance features. For example, specific protective immunoglobulins were generated after mice were immunized with Pseudomonas aeruginosa flagellin incorporated into gold nanoparticles.219 A membrane-coated nanosponge vaccine (Nanotoxoid) developed against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) neutralizes multiple pore-forming toxins and provides broad protection in preclinical models.220 Chitosan nanoparticle formulations carrying antigens from enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli (EHEC) have elicited strong mucosal and systemic immune responses, while PLGA-based nanoparticles encapsulating the protective antigen of Bacillus anthracis have induced durable antibody responses.221,222 Outer membrane vesicle-based Nano vaccines targeting multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii have also shown promising protective efficacy in animal models.223 Clinically, the Outer Membrane Vesicle -based vaccine Bexsero® against meningitidis has validated the clinical feasibility of nanoparticle vaccine platforms, and the success of lipid nanoparticle-based mRNA vaccines has accelerated the translation of similar technologies for bacterial pathogens. These advances support nano vaccines as a promising strategy for preventing infections.

In addition to triggering an immune response, the nanoplatforms confer high antigen stability and function as antigen-presenting agents, facilitating uptake and recognition by the adaptive immune system and provide adjuvant properties.

The effect of immune potentiation using nano-adjuvants was observed with liposomal nanoparticles.224 This adjuvant effect is achieved by modulating liposomes’ physicochemical properties, including size, lamellarity, and surface charge. Large liposomes measuring 225 nm elicit T cell responses. The cationic nature of the liposomes promotes their attachment to DNA and proteins, facilitating immunological recognition, while their splitting ability releases anchored antigens. GlaxoSmithKline (GSK) has launched clinical trials to market a subunit vaccine using tuberculosis dual-antigen M72 instead of the whole bacteria, which is adjuvanted by AS01E monophosphoryl lipid (MPL) and Quillaja saponaria composed liposome. This innovation reached the phase IIb and has demonstrated a 49.7% efficacy in preventing tuberculosis infection, thereby achieving the World Health Organization’s criteria for TB vaccines.225

CpG adjuvant effect is due to the CpG-TLR9 agonist activity.226 This feature was explored in nanoparticles to achieve a successful tuberculosis subunit vaccine. Hence, Orr et al developed a dual adjuvant vaccine using glucopyranosyl lipid, CpG, and recombinant fusion protein ID93.227 The subjunction to CpG sequences elicited a strong immunological response by co-producing Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF), and Interleukin-2 (IL-2).

Niosomes are synthetic vesicles composed of non-ionic surfactants and cholesterol that have emerged as promising nanocarriers for targeting intracellular bacterial pathogens.228,229 Unlike conventional antibiotics, which struggle to penetrate host cells and are often inactivated by acidic intracellular environments, niosome-encapsulated drugs can be taken up directly by macrophages through phagocytosis, delivering their antimicrobial payload precisely where pathogens like Mycobacterium tuberculosis reside and multiply. Their advantages over liposomes include greater stability, higher drug entrapment efficiency, lower cost, and longer shelf life and make them particularly attractive for sustained antibacterial immunotherapy. Surface functionalization with antibodies, aptamers, or PEG further enhances their targeted delivery to specific immune cells, while inhalable or nebulized formulations enable direct lung deposition, reducing systemic toxicity. In vivo studies have demonstrated that niosome-encapsulated first-line anti-tuberculosis drugs achieve significantly greater bacterial load reductions in lung tissue compared to free drugs, underscoring their potential to improve treatment outcomes for difficult-to-treat intracellular lung infections.

Another compelling example of nanoparticle-based systems for antibacterial immunotherapy is the use of copper oxide-stigmasterol nanoparticles.230 The copper oxide core generates reactive oxygen species that disrupt microbial cell membranes and induce oxidative stress, while the conjugated stigmasterol enhances efficacy through specific binding to pathogen adhesion proteins, inhibiting biofilm formation on tooth surfaces and medical device surfaces. Critically, biofilm disruption unveils protected bacteria to the immune system, enabling recognition and clearance by host defenses that would otherwise be blocked by the biofilm matrix.

Nanofibers are ultrafine polymeric or composite fibers typically ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers in diameter. They have emerged as a versatile platform for wound healing and infection management owing to their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, tunable physicochemical properties, and capacity for controlled drug or bioactive agent release. Beyond their conventional role as scaffolds for tissue regeneration, nanofibers are increasingly being engineered to exert immunomodulatory functions, enabling them to actively reshape the host immune microenvironment rather than merely providing structural support. One of particular therapeutic interest is their ability to sequentially orchestrate antibacterial activity and macrophage polarization, transitioning the wound from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-regenerative state. Nanofiber systems functionalized with photothermal agents, immunomodulatory cytokines, or polarizing biomolecules offer a spatiotemporally controlled means of first eliminating bacterial burden and subsequently steering macrophage phenotype toward tissue-reparative functions, representing a compelling paradigm shift in the treatment of biofilm-associated and antibiotic-resistant wound infections.231

Polymeric nanoparticles have emerged as one of the most promising and versatile platforms. Crucially, polymeric materials are not merely passive carriers; they have demonstrated intrinsic antibiotic properties, with the ability to accumulate on bacterial cell membranes and promote the destruction of entire bacterial cells, conferring a multitarget advantage when combined with loaded antimicrobial agents. Beyond direct bactericidal action, PNPs can be precisely engineered for immunomodulatory purposes, modifying their surface with ligands, antigens, or adjuvants to actively engage innate and adaptive immune pathways. Nanoparticles have demonstrated capacity to promote host immunity through inhibition of virulence factors, reduction of resistance development, and combination with other compounds to generate synergistic antimicrobial activities. Collectively, these properties position polymeric nanoparticles not merely as drug carriers but as sophisticated immunotherapeutic platforms capable of simultaneously eradicating bacterial pathogens and reprogramming the host immune response, offering a compelling paradigm shift in the management of refractory bacterial infections.232

Among the rapidly expanding repertoire of carbon-based nanomaterials developed to combat bacterial infections, carbon quantum dots have emerged as a particularly versatile and multifunctional platform that transcends conventional antimicrobial paradigms to actively engage and modulate host immune responses. They are zero-dimensional carbon-based nanomaterials typically smaller than 10 nm, characterized by their exceptional biocompatibility, tunable surface chemistry, ease of functionalization, and intrinsic fluorescent properties that enable simultaneous therapeutic and diagnostic applications. Unlike conventional antibiotics, which act through a single bactericidal mechanism and are increasingly undermined by resistance development, they can be engineered to exert multi-target antibacterial activity, disrupting bacterial membranes, inducing intracellular protein leakage, degrading RNA, and interfering with bacterial metabolic and resistance pathways, while simultaneously functioning as immunomodulatory agents capable of reshaping the host immune microenvironment.233 An illustration of this dual functionality is provided by Qi et al, who developed polyethyleneimine-EDTA-2Na carbon quantum dots, synthesized via a hydrothermal method, demonstrating that these particles operate in a pH-responsive manner that is dynamically aligned with the evolving biochemical phases of wound healing.234 In the acidic environment characteristic of active bacterial infection, it exerts potent bactericidal effects against both Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative Escherichia coli through electrostatic adsorption onto bacterial surfaces, enhanced membrane permeability, protein leakage, and RNA disruption, while genomic analysis revealed downregulation of antibiotic resistance genes. As the wound microenvironment transitions toward neutral and alkaline pH following infection resolution, the nanodots shift their functional role toward antioxidant and immunomodulatory activity, scavenging reactive oxygen species through cascade superoxide dismutase and catalase like nanozyme activities, and critically promoting the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. In vivo validation in both diabetic wound and bacteremia mouse models confirmed that quantum dots significantly reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, enhanced angiogenesis through upregulation of CD31, VEGF, and α-SMA, promoted collagen deposition, and markedly improved survival rates in systemic infection models. Furthermore, by integrating the system into a gelatin methacrylate hydrogel scaffold, real-time non-invasive pH monitoring of wound status was achieved through pH-dependent fluorescence, representing a convergence of diagnostics and therapy within a single nano system. Collectively, these findings position carbon quantum dots as a sophisticated next-generation antimicrobial-immunotherapeutic platform that simultaneously eliminates pathogens, resolves inflammation, and actively orchestrates the host immune response to accelerate tissue regeneration, a capability that is particularly valuable in the context of chronic wounds complicated by multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

Complementing the therapeutic applications of carbon quantum nanodots and their immunomodulatory capacity, carbon-based nanomaterials modulation of the innate immune cells is highlighted, particularly in the context of monocyte and macrophage biology.235 Indeed, this category, including carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide, and carbon nanodiamonds, significantly enhance the reactivity of primary human monocytes toward a range of clinically relevant bacterial species, augmenting phagocytic activity and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in response to bacterial stimuli. These findings carry important implications for the rational design of carbon-based nanotherapeutics, suggesting that beyond their direct bactericidal properties, they possess an underappreciated capacity to prime and reprogram innate immune cells at the earliest stages of the immune response, a property that, when harnessed deliberately, could be exploited to bolster host-directed immunity against antibiotic-resistant pathogens and to fine-tune the inflammatory milieu of infected tissues toward resolution and repair.

Beyond synthetic nanomaterial platforms, naturally derived extracellular vesicles have emerged as a biologically sophisticated and increasingly promising modality for immunotherapy against bacterial infections. Exosomes are endosome-derived vesicles of 30–100 nm secreted by virtually all eukaryotic and prokaryotic cell types, carrying a diverse molecular cargo encompassing proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and carbohydrates that mediate intercellular communication in both homeostatic and pathological contexts.236 During bacterial infections, exosomes occupy a dual and context-dependent role: on one hand, pathogen-derived outer membrane vesicles can package and deliver virulence factors, toxins, β-lactamases, and antibiotic resistance determinants to host cells, thereby contributing to immune evasion and disease progression; on the other hand, host-derived exosomes, particularly those released by macrophages and dendritic cells, carry bacterial antigens and danger-associated molecular patterns that potently activate both innate and adaptive immune responses. The immunotherapeutic potential of exosomes is increasingly being harnessed through deliberate engineering, as demonstrated by studies showing that macrophage-derived exosomes loaded with bacterial antigens or antibiotics can stimulate antigen-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses and effectively combat intracellular methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections in vivo. Furthermore, outer membrane vesicle-based vaccines against Neisseria meningitidis, Bordetella pertussis, Escherichia coli, and other clinically significant pathogens have demonstrated protective efficacy in preclinical models, with some formulations already approved for human use, underscoring the translational readiness of exosome-based antibacterial immunotherapy.

In addition to their capacity to trigger strong immune responses, nanoparticles are used in antibacterial therapy to target bacteria and deliver antibiotics and immunotherapeutic agents specifically. The precise delivery and efficient cellular uptake enable strategies to overcome physical barriers, such as mucus, biofilms, and epithelial layers, for localized infection treatment. Moreover, the development of stimuli-responsive nanomaterials that release therapeutic agents in response to bacterial signals, such as toxins or pH changes in infected tissues, improved the precision delivery of Immunotherapeutics to infection sites, minimizing systemic toxicity.237 One of the studies investigating nanomedicines delivery ability and immune clearance activation against bacteria was conducted by the Ge et al group against Staphylococcus aureus. This last can persist within host cells, such as osteoblasts, thereby evading both antibiotic treatments and immune defenses, contributing to chronic infections like osteomyelitis. Standard antibiotics, including vancomycin, are largely ineffective against these intracellular bacteria. The approach employs nanoparticles that carry perforin and granzyme B, mimicking natural killer (NK) cells. Toxins are released in response to the altered redox environment in infected cells to destroy both bacteria and host cells. This strategy not only clears vancomycin-resistant S. aureus but also activates innate and adaptive immune responses, promotes bacteria-specific memory T cells, and offers a promising, safe, and scalable option for clinical use.238 The concept of targeted delivery was also stated with antibiotics encapsulated in diverse nanostructures.239 For example, Rifampicin was loaded into solid lipid nanoparticles with a mannose-modified surface. Mannose is a relevant ligand because it targets the active mannose receptors on alveolar macrophages, the preferred site of tuberculosis infection. Furthermore, nanoplatforms offer environment triggered delivery. pH reduction induced by bacterial acids’ production at the infection site is targeted to achieve precise antibacterial therapy and mitigate off-target toxicity.240 Several pH-sensitive cross-linkers were used to achieve successful antibiotic delivery, such as acetals, ketals, and imine linkages, which degrade under acidic conditions to release antibacterial agents on demand.

Inspired by polymeric systems, a pH-responsive design was elaborated to undergo controlled degradation, enabling the timed release of encapsulated antibacterial Ceftazidime. This broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotic interferes with bacterial cell wall synthesis.241 The nanoparticle is a self-assembled vesicle like polymersome, which are similar to liposomes but are made from synthetic polymers rather than lipids. The polymers used to make these nanoparticles are: 2-Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) (hydrophilic monomer), Poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (O950) (hydrophilic, PEG-based monomer), Dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA), a pH-responsive monomer that can change protonation with pH, and N-(2-hydroxypropyl) methacrylamide (HPMA) (hydrophilic, biocompatible monomer). The Study has confirmed that such systems maintain structural integrity at neutral pH while releasing antibiotics effectively under acidic conditions, demonstrating potent antibacterial activity. Furthermore, Zhao et al have introduced an impressive control-activated nano-immuno-therapeutics against MRSA-induced bone infection.242 As mentioned before, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) osteomyelitis is a severe infection due to bacterial resistance, the tendency toward biofilm formation, which hinders immune cells’ killing effect, and induces the extreme local inflammatory response, which causes bone tissue destruction. Herein, the nanoparticle was designed as a multifunctional system composed of gold nanorods, Baicalin, and a positively charged surface coating. The positively charged coating enables the nanoparticle to target and penetrate biofilms actively. Gold nanorods act as photothermal agents, absorbing near-infrared light and converting it into localized heat. The heat kills bacteria but also disrupts biofilms, allowing better penetration of therapeutic agents. Baicalin provides immunomodulatory and antibacterial activity. It promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, suppresses excessive inflammation, and supports bone tissue repair. Upon Near-Infrared (NIR) light stimulation, the nanoparticle releases baicalin in a spatiotemporally controlled manner. At the same time, the photothermal effect synergizes with the drug to eradicate MRSA, disperse biofilms, and modulate the local immune environment. This integrated design addresses both the bacterial infection and the bone tissue damage, offering a comprehensive and precise photo-immunotherapeutic strategy.

Likewise, a novel gas-sensitized hyperthermia strategy was developed using a multifunctional nanoplatform, composed of a Prussian blue core, metal-organic framework (MOF) shell, and diallyl trisulfide as a hydrogen sulfide (H2S) donor.243 The Prussian blue core serves as a photothermal agent, converting near-infrared light into localized heat to directly kill bacteria. The MOF shell is pH-responsive, degrading specifically in the acidic biofilm microenvironment, which triggers the release of diallyl trisulfide. This last reacts with glutathione in the biofilm to generate H2S gas, which induces DNA damage in bacteria and disrupts the biofilm. Upon NIR irradiation, H2S-sensitized hyperthermia synergistically eradicates biofilms via both thermal bacterial killing and H2S-mediated destabilization. Additionally, H2S acts as an immunomodulatory signal, promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby reducing inflammation and stimulating the secretion of tissue-regenerative cytokines. This process reverses the infection-induced pro-inflammatory environment, accelerates tissue repair, and remodels the immune microenvironment.

Recent studies increasingly demonstrate that nanotechnology enables combination therapeutic strategies that simultaneously enhance antibacterial efficacy, restore host immune responses, and improve antibiotic delivery. Rather than functioning solely as drug carriers, multifunctional nanoparticles combine conventional antibiotics with immunomodulatory molecules, photothermal agents, vaccines, or immune-cell mimetics to overcome bacterial resistance and biofilm-associated immune evasion. These integrated approaches improve antibiotic accumulation at infection sites while stimulating both innate and adaptive immunity (Table 2).

Nanomaterials offer a multifunctional platform that combines diagnostic and therapeutic functions. Thanks to their structural malleability, they enhance bacterial detection and eradication, as well as immune system activation. They can be loaded with targeting moieties to avoid off-target effects and can be configured to activate in response to bio-stimuli, such as toxins, or to Near Infra-Red light. Despite the remarkable therapeutic promise of nanomaterial-based platforms in combating bacterial infections, a rigorous appraisal of their clinical translatability must confront the substantial and unresolved concerns surrounding their toxicological profiles, biodistribution kinetics, and long-term biosafety. The physicochemical properties that render nanomaterials therapeutically advantageous, including their small size, high surface-area-to-volume ratio, surface charge, and functional group density, are the same properties that govern their interactions with biological systems and determine their potential for unintended harm. Nanoparticle size and surface charge profoundly influence cellular uptake, with cationic nanoparticles in particular demonstrating enhanced membrane penetration, while beneficial for antibacterial activity, may also disrupt the integrity of host cell membranes and trigger cytotoxicity at concentrations approaching therapeutic doses.

Carbon-based nanomaterials, including carbon quantum dots and carbon nanotubes, have shown encouraging biocompatibility profiles in vitro, for instance, PEI-EDTA-2Na carbon quantum dots maintained cell survival rates above 85% in human keratinocyte lines even at concentrations as high as 2500 μg mL−1, and demonstrated hemolysis rates below 5% across all tested concentrations,234 Yet, such in vitro assays are acknowledged to incompletely capture the complexity of in vivo physiological environments. In vivo biodistribution remains a particularly critical consideration, as nanomaterials administered systemically may accumulate in off-target organs including the liver, spleen, kidneys, and lungs, where prolonged retention can provoke chronic inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, or organ dysfunction. The biodistribution of PECDs administered intraperitoneally in bacteremia models, for example, was shown to be renally cleared within 24 hours, providing preliminary evidence of favorable elimination kinetics; however, the fate of nanoparticles administered by other routes or across extended treatment regimens remains incompletely characterized. Polymeric nanoparticles, widely studied for antibiotic delivery and immunomodulation, have demonstrated safety and biodegradability profiles for materials such as PLGA and chitosan in preclinical models, yet concerns persist regarding the accumulation of degradation byproducts, the immunogenicity of surface-conjugated ligands, and the potential for complement activation following repeated administration.232 For exosome-based platforms, while their endogenous origin confers inherent biocompatibility advantages and low immunogenicity, the scalability of production, batch-to-batch consistency, and the potential for unintended transfer of immunosuppressive or pro-inflammatory cargo from donor cells to recipient tissues remain open challenges. Across all nanomaterial classes, the absence of standardized long-term toxicology protocols specifically designed for nanomedicine represents a significant regulatory and scientific gap. Future research must therefore prioritize chronic toxicity studies employing physiologically relevant animal models, comprehensive organ-level histopathological assessment, systematic evaluation of nanoparticle clearance mechanisms, and the development of validated in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation frameworks, in order to establish the safety boundaries within which nanomaterial-based antibacterial immunotherapies can be responsibly advanced toward clinical application.

While nanotechnology-based systems offer significant advantages for improving the stability, biodistribution, controlled release, and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents, their effectiveness largely depends on the availability of highly specific targeting moieties. In this regard, aptamers have emerged as promising molecular recognition tools owing to their high affinity, specificity, ease of synthesis, and low immunogenicity. The integration of aptamers with nanomaterials has further expanded their therapeutic potential, enabling precise targeting of bacterial cells, virulence factors, and host-pathogen interaction pathways. Consequently, aptamer-based strategies have gained increasing attention as innovative components of next-generation antibacterial immunotherapies.

Aptamers in Bacterial Immunotherapy

Aptamers are short single-stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides that fold into unique three-dimensional structures, enabling highly specific target recognition and functioning functionally analogous to antibodies.244 Aptamer selection is based on the Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential enrichment (SELEX) technology. The process begins with a large library of randomized nucleic acid sequences, which are incubated with a target protein, followed by a separation step that isolates and amplifies bound sequences. Progressive selection improves aptamers’ affinity toward the target, and the final enriched pool is sequenced and characterized to identify the highest-affinity aptamers.

Aptamers are employed in numerous therapeutic applications, including identification, targeting, agonism, and antagonism, and may even exhibit catalytic activity. In antibacterial applications, aptamer mechanisms can be broadly classified into three functional categories: neutralizing aptamers, which block bacterial toxins or virulence factors; targeting aptamers, which bind bacterial surface structures and guide antimicrobial agents or nanocarriers; and immune-modulating aptamers, which interact with host immune pathways to enhance antibacterial immune responses. Aptamers offer several advantages over antibodies, including high specificity, lower production costs and complexity, greater stability, longer shelf life, reduced immunogenicity, better tissue penetration, and possible cellular uptake due to their smaller size and broader range of targetable molecules, including small molecules and ions. They are chemically synthesized, ensuring high batch-to-batch uniformity, and can be easily modified for enhanced functionality.245 Overall, aptamers represent a versatile and highly specific class of molecules with broad potential in diagnostics and therapeutics, which has raised interest in their potential for treating bacterial infections. Importantly, their programmable binding properties also enable integration into host-directed antibacterial immunotherapy strategies, in which aptamers can either neutralize bacterial virulence determinants or modulate host immune responses.

The application of aptamers in infectious diseases implies targeting bacterial virulence factors. Hence, aptamers were used to disrupt bacterial toxin production, biofilm formation, induce biofilm disassembly, and hinder adherence mechanisms.246 They also serve as bacteriostatic agents.247 Examples of aptamers’ efficiency include DNA aptamers targeting α-toxin from Staphylococcus aureus, namely AT-27, AT-33, AT-36, and AT-49, which have shown significant inhibition of α-toxin-mediated cell death in human cells and reduced upregulation of TNF-α, IL-17, and other inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting immunomodulatory effects.248

Similarly, Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K88, a major cause of post-antibiotic diarrhea, was targeted using two fimbria-specific DNA aptamers: the K88-Apt A04 and the K88-Apt 37. The study showed that aptamers inhibited bacterial adhesion in a concentration-dependent manner, preventing ETEC K88 attachment and colonization. Additionally, the aptamers reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the cytotoxic effects of ETEC K88, demonstrating their potential to mitigate bacterial pathogenicity and inflammation in vitro.249

Aptamers act as immune modulators and can enhance immune system activation through targeted delivery or modulation of immune checkpoints. A tuberculosis-targeting aptamer was identified and labeled to bind specifically to the virulence factors of the H37Rv strain. This interaction induced enhanced IFN-γ production by CD4 T cells, reduced bacterial load in the spleen, improved survival in infected mice, and reduced lung tissue damage.250

Aptamers that stimulate the immune system against bacteria are a cutting-edge therapeutic strategy that warrants further exploration. Applicable aptamers in clinical phases require improved strategies to enhance aptamer efficacy in challenging physiological environments, including modifications to increase resistance to nuclease degradation.251 Aptamer engineering can encounter stability issues in vivo and requires chemical modifications of nucleic bases and their incorporation into nanoparticles.251 Indeed, their small molecular weight and nucleic acid nature decrease their resistance toward nucleases. Moreover, it exhibits low bioavailability and rapid renal filtration.252 (Figure 6) Despite these challenges, continuous advances in aptamer engineering, chemical modification, and nanocarrier integration are expected to improve their pharmacokinetic properties and accelerate their translation toward clinical antibacterial immunotherapies. The combination of aptamers with nanocarriers, antimicrobial peptides, and targeted immunotherapies represents an emerging area of translational research. Such integrated platforms may improve pathogen specificity while simultaneously enhancing antibacterial efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity.

A survey of current clinical trials involving nucleic acid-based aptamers, as comprehensively documented by Matthew Stephens,253 reveals a landscape that is rich in immunomodulatory and diagnostic applications yet conspicuously devoid of any trial specifically targeting bacterial infections. Among the aptamers that have advanced to human clinical evaluation, several carry direct relevance to immune system modulation that could, in principle, inform future antibacterial immunotherapeutic strategies. Most notably, ApTOLL, a DNA aptamer targeting Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), the principal innate immune receptor for bacterial lipopolysaccharide, has advanced to Phase Ib/IIa clinical evaluation for acute ischemic stroke (NCT04742062), representing the closest clinical approximation to antibacterial immune modulation, given that TLR4 is a central mediator of Gram-negative bacterial sepsis and endotoxin-driven inflammatory cascades. Similarly, another immunologically significant aptamer is AON-D21, targeting complement factor C5a, which has entered Phase I evaluation in healthy controls (NCT05018403), with complement modulation being directly pertinent to bacterial opsonization and innate immune clearance mechanisms. NOX-H94, an RNA aptamer targeting hepcidin evaluated in Phase I/II trials for anaemia in end-stage renal disease (NCT02079896), is also noteworthy given hepcidin’s emerging role as an acute-phase iron-regulatory protein that profoundly influences the nutritional immunity response to bacterial infection by limiting iron bioavailability to pathogens. The collective picture that emerges from this clinical trial inventory is unambiguous: while aptamer technology has demonstrated sufficient safety and target engagement to advance through multiple phases of human clinical evaluation across diverse disease areas, and while several of these candidates modulate immune pathways of direct relevance to antibacterial defense, none has been developed or investigated specifically as an antibacterial immunotherapeutic agent in human subjects, representing both the most significant gap in the current aptamer clinical development pipeline and, simultaneously, the most compelling opportunity for future translational investment in this rapidly evolving field.

Clinical Translational Framework for Antibacterial Immunotherapy

To elucidate the progress of antibacterial immune-based technologies, it is beneficial to assess their translational maturity. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) primarily neutralize bacterial toxins or virulence factors and promote immune-mediated elimination of pathogens.254 Bacteriophage-derived therapeutics directly lyse bacteria and modulate host immune responses by disrupting biofilms and regulating inflammatory pathways. Nanomedicine platforms can make it easier to deliver antimicrobials, more precisely modulate immune signaling, and improve targeting of pathogens.255 Aptamer-based systems serve as highly specific molecular ligands that can target bacterial structures or deliver immune-modulating cargos. These technologies should not be seen as competing solutions, but as parts of a larger immunotherapy system that work together to fight antimicrobial resistance. Understanding the mechanistic relationships and complementary nature of these platforms provides a foundation for their translational development and clinical evaluation.256

From Bench to Bedside: Current Immunotherapies in Clinical Development

Preclinical progress in antibacterial immunotherapy is impressive, demonstrating innovative strategies to combat multidrug-resistant pathogens, biofilms, and bacterial virulence factors. Among these, aptamer-based approaches and targeted nanoparticles have emerged as versatile and highly specific platforms capable of neutralizing toxins, inhibiting adhesion and biofilm formation, and modulating host immune responses. By leveraging this progress, a promising avenue for clinical translation emerges in the fight against persistent and resistant bacterial infections.

Herein, we explore examples of anti-bacterial Immunotherapeutics in clinical development or clinical trials, including monoclonal antibodies and vaccine candidates. For example, the Suvratoxumab (MEDI4893 / AR-320) NCT05331885 trial, investigating a human monoclonal antibody against Staphylococcus aureus Alpha Toxin, was terminated due to administrative issues.257 Similarly, the Phase 3 vaccine against E. coli was aborted due to a lack of results. Their greatest translational potential may arise from integration into multimodal therapeutic platforms that combine immune modulation, targeted pathogen elimination, biofilm disruption, and precision drug delivery.

The clinical development of anti-virulence therapies has highlighted several important challenges and lessons for future research. Despite encouraging preclinical data, multiple late-stage programs have failed to demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit. For example, the anti-Staphylococcus aureus α-toxin monoclonal antibody Suvratoxumab (MEDI4893) and the phage-derived lysin Exebacase (CF-301) showed strong mechanistic rationale but did not achieve their primary efficacy endpoints in Phase 3 studies. Similarly, the ExPEC9V vaccine was discontinued because interim analyses indicated insufficient efficacy. These outcomes suggest that neutralizing a single virulence factor may be insufficient to significantly alter disease progression in complex infections, where multiple pathogenic pathways often act simultaneously.

Several lessons emerge from these experiences. First, future trials should incorporate biomarker-based patient selection to ensure that the targeted virulence factor is actively expressed in enrolled patients, thereby maximizing the likelihood of observing a therapeutic effect. Second, anti-virulence agents may be most effective when administered early in infection, before extensive tissue damage and systemic inflammation become established. Third, because bacterial pathogens frequently possess redundant virulence mechanisms, multi-target approaches-including bispecific antibodies, antibody cocktails, engineered bacteriophages, or combination regimens targeting toxins, biofilms, and quorum-sensing pathways simultaneously may provide greater clinical benefit than single-target interventions. Fourth, anti-virulence therapies should be evaluated primarily as adjuncts to standard-of-care antibiotics rather than as replacements, since their role is to attenuate pathogenicity and enhance host clearance rather than directly eradicate bacteria. Finally, future studies should place greater emphasis on patient stratification, pathogen genomics, and pharmacodynamic biomarkers to identify responder populations and improve clinical trial sensitivity.

Collectively, recent trial failures do not diminish the promise of anti-virulence strategies; rather, they underscore the need for precision-medicine approaches, rational combination therapies, and improved clinical trial design to fully realize the therapeutic potential of virulence-targeted interventions.

Clinical Reality: FDA-Approved Bacterial Immunotherapies and Their Limitations

The range of FDA-approved bacterial Immunotherapeutics includes mostly vaccines. The implicated pathogens are anthrax, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae type b (DPT; Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Bordetella pertussis, Clostridium tetani), Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella enterica, and Yersinia pestis. In the realm of non-vaccine immunotherapeutic agents, we find bezlotoxumab (ZINPLAVA), one of the rare monoclonal antibodies approved by the FDA for the treatment of bacterial disease. This highlights that while preventive vaccination is the cornerstone of bacterial immunotherapy, the use of monoclonal antibodies targeting bacteria and other immunomodulatory agents for therapeutic purposes is relatively uncommon, presumably due to scientific challenges in targeting living bacteria and their toxins. A short review of approved therapies targeting bacterial infections is explored in Table 4. Examining the real-world efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions in conditions such as sepsis, pneumonia, and chronic wound infections poses significant challenges for the application of innovative preclinical discoveries. Addressing issues such as patient variability, immune tolerance, and bacterial heterogeneity and ensuring the stability, targeted delivery, and consistent bioavailability of immunotherapeutic agents could help with successful clinical translation. Despite these regulatory successes, approved antibacterial immunotherapies remain limited compared with therapies available for cancer and autoimmune diseases. This highlights the considerable scientific, clinical, and regulatory challenges that continue to hinder broader implementation of antibacterial immunotherapeutic approaches.

Bridging the Gap: Translational and Clinical Challenges in Antibacterial Immunotherapy

Antibacterial immunotherapy has received increasing attention in recent years, yet its translation into clinical practice remains limited, this gap can be largely attributed to the reliance on simplified preclinical models and the fragmented nature of current development strategies.260 In clinical settings these limitations are particularly apparent in complex conditions such as sepsis, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and chronic wound infections where treatment outcomes are often unpredictable, In patients with underlying conditions, including cystic fibrosis, immune dysfunction further complicates therapeutic responses and reduces overall treatment effectiveness.261,262 In addition, these approaches are constrained by a relatively narrow spectrum of activity and the rapid development of antimicrobial resistance. Their effectiveness is further reduced by limited tissue penetration and suboptimal pharmacokinetic behavior, especially within the challenging microenvironment of biofilm associated infections.263 The absence of standardized dosing protocols and reliable biomarkers for assessing treatment response further limits their widespread clinical application.

While nanotechnology- and aptamer-based platforms offer promising opportunities, they still face several practical challenges, particularly in terms of safety, delivery efficiency, and scalability.264 For example, nanoparticle-based systems may be affected by instability, unintended interactions, and difficulties in large-scale production. Similarly although aptamer-based approaches are highly specific, their performance in complex in vivo systems requires further investigation, host-directed therapies also present challenges, as limited control over immune modulation may lead to unintended immune dysregulation.265

More broadly, progress in developing integrated and coordinated therapeutic frameworks remains limited as many strategies are still pursued independently rather than as part of combined multimodal approaches. Future antibacterial strategies are likely to rely on multimodal therapeutic platforms that integrate complementary mechanisms of action to overcome the limitations of single-agent therapies. Combining immunotherapy to bacteriophages and nanotechnology-based delivery systems may improve therapy stability, targeted delivery, and biofilm penetration while reducing bacterial resistance. Similarly, antibiotic conjugates offer the potential for highly specific bacterial targeting, enhancing antimicrobial efficacy while minimizing off-target effects and toxicity. Other promising combinations include antimicrobial peptides, host-directed therapies with bacteriophages, and CRISPR-Cas systems, Immunotherapeutics and nanoparticles that selectively eliminate antimicrobial resistance. The integration of these approaches with precision medicine, biomarker-guided patient stratification, and advanced drug delivery technologies may further optimize therapeutic efficacy.

Although all these platforms remain largely at the preclinical stage, continued advances in bioengineering, nanomedicine, and immunology are expected to accelerate their translation into clinically viable strategies for combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

Furthermore, although host-directed immunotherapies offer promising opportunities to enhance pathogen clearance, their implementation requires careful consideration of potential safety concerns. Excessive activation of innate or adaptive immune responses may result in immunopathology, including uncontrolled inflammation, tissue damage, and cytokine dysregulation. Therefore, achieving an optimal balance between immune enhancement and immune homeostasis remains a key challenge in the development of effective and safe immunotherapeutic interventions. Indeed, this is major hurdle in the clinical application of antibacterial immunotherapy.266 While host-directed therapies (HDTs) are designed to enhance protective immune responses, inappropriate or excessive immune stimulation can worsen disease outcomes by triggering hyperinflammation, cytokine storms, and tissue damage. For example, the phase I trial of an agonistic anti-CD28 monoclonal antibody caused a life-threatening cytokine storm, highlighting the potential toxicity of immune-activating therapies. Similarly, cytokine-based treatments such as interferon-α have demonstrated beneficial effects when administered early during infection but become detrimental when given later, underscoring the importance of the therapeutic window. Immune pathways, including cGAS-STING and pyroptosis, also exhibit dual roles: activation during the early stages of infection enhances pathogen clearance, whereas sustained activation during disease progression contributes to severe inflammation and organ damage. Consequently, successful clinical implementation of antibacterial immunotherapies requires precise control over the magnitude and timing of immune modulation, supported by biomarkers and immune profiling to identify the appropriate stage of infection and minimize immunopathological complications.

Regulatory considerations represent an additional barrier to translation. Emerging antibacterial immunotherapies frequently involve complex biological products, engineered bacteriophages, nucleic acid-based therapeutics, or multifunctional nanomaterials, all of which require rigorous evaluation of safety, manufacturing consistency, long-term stability, and quality control. Furthermore, standardized regulatory pathways for several of these technologies remain under development, creating additional uncertainty for clinical implementation. Addressing these issues will require more cohesive and clinically validated strategies that integrate immunotherapy with improved delivery systems and more precise patient stratification to enhance treatment outcomes.267

Conclusion, Future Directions, and Challenges

The global emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens has imposed a substantial burden on healthcare systems, resulting in treatment failure, prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and mortality. In response, antibacterial immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy that complements or replaces conventional antibiotics by enhancing pathogen-specific immune responses, limiting tissue-destructive inflammation, and promoting long-term immune protection. Current immunotherapeutic modalities encompass monoclonal antibodies, cytokines, antimicrobial peptides, immune agonists, and host-directed therapies, each targeting distinct aspects of host immunity and bacterial pathogenesis.

To further improve therapeutic efficacy, researchers have increasingly combined Immunotherapeutics with advanced drug delivery platforms. Nanotechnology-based systems, including liposomes and other nanoparticles, enable targeted drug delivery, controlled release, enhanced tissue penetration into difficult-to-reach sites such as the brain and bone, and reduced systemic toxicity. Likewise, aptamers have emerged as versatile targeting molecules capable of inhibiting bacterial adhesion, exerting bacteriostatic effects, and modulating immune cell activation. Although promising, their susceptibility to nuclease degradation and limited in vivo stability remain important challenges that require further optimization before widespread clinical application.

Future antibacterial strategies will likely rely on integrated therapeutic platforms that combine the complementary advantages of multiple modalities rather than single-agent interventions. Examples include phage-nanoparticle hybrid systems that improve bacteriophage stability and targeted delivery, aptamer-antibiotic conjugates for pathogen-specific drug release, CRISPR-Cas systems delivered by bacteriophages or nanoparticles for precision genome editing of resistance determinants, and multifunctional nanocarriers capable of co-delivering antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and anti-biofilm agents. Similarly, integrating host-directed immunotherapies with pathogen-targeted approaches may enhance bacterial clearance while reducing selective pressure for antimicrobial resistance. Advances in synthetic biology, biomaterials engineering, and precision medicine are expected to accelerate the development of these multimodal therapeutic platforms.

Despite their considerable promise, the successful clinical translation of these next-generation antibacterial therapies remains constrained by substantial economic and manufacturing challenges. Personalized bacteriophage therapy requires rapid pathogen identification, phage isolation, characterization, and patient-specific manufacturing under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions, making production labor-intensive, time-consuming, and more expensive than conventional antibiotics. CRISPR-Cas antibacterial therapeutics similarly face high production costs associated with guide RNA synthesis, Cas protein manufacturing, and the development of efficient, safe, and scalable delivery systems. Immunotherapeutic biologics, including monoclonal antibodies and antimicrobial peptides, require complex manufacturing, purification, formulation, and quality-control processes that substantially increase production costs. Likewise, nanotherapeutics-particularly liposomal formulations depend on sophisticated manufacturing technologies to ensure reproducible particle size, encapsulation efficiency, sterility, and long-term stability. Clinically approved liposomal drugs, such as liposomal amphotericin B, remain considerably more expensive than conventional formulations because of these manufacturing complexities. Collectively, these economic barriers may limit global accessibility, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, emphasizing the need for standardized manufacturing platforms, scalable production technologies, and sustainable funding models.

Emerging technologies are expected to further transform antibacterial immunotherapy. Synthetic biology and engineered immune cells, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells and other engineered lymphocyte platforms, offer exciting opportunities to selectively target bacterial pathogens and resistant infections. However, their clinical implementation remains limited by target specificity, manufacturing complexity, immunodepleting preconditioning requirements, safety concerns, and regulatory challenges. In parallel, CRISPR-based immune engineering continues to expand beyond direct bacterial killing toward precision manipulation of resistance genes, virulence determinants, and bacterial plasmids.

Artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and advanced bioinformatics are poised to become indispensable tools in combating antimicrobial resistance by accelerating therapeutic discovery and enabling precision medicine. Transformer-based genomic language models, including DNABERT, ProkBERT, FGeneBERT, and GenSLM, facilitate functional annotation of previously uncharacterized genes, identification of novel antimicrobial targets, and prediction of resistance evolution from large-scale genomic and multi-omics datasets. Furthermore, ML models integrating whole-genome sequencing with transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and clinical data have substantially improved the prediction of antimicrobial resistance genes, minimum inhibitory concentrations, and pathogen-specific resistance phenotypes. Emerging knowledge graph- and large language model-based frameworks further integrate genomic, clinical, and epidemiological information to support real-time surveillance and personalized antimicrobial therapy. Future progress will depend on the integration of explainable AI, standardized FAIR-compliant databases, multimodal omics, and prospective clinical validation to translate these computational advances into clinically actionable tools.

Ultimately, overcoming antimicrobial resistance will require multidisciplinary collaboration among microbiologists, immunologists, clinicians, engineers, computational scientists, regulatory agencies, policymakers, and industry partners. Continued investment in translational research, harmonized regulatory pathways, equitable funding mechanisms, and global public health initiatives will be essential to accelerate the development, accessibility, and clinical implementation of safe, effective, and affordable next-generation antibacterial immunotherapies.
