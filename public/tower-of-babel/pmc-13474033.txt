Associations of Plasma Total Tau Level and MMSE Score With Polysomnographic Data in Middle‐Aged Men With Severe Obstructive Sleep Apnea: A Pilot Study
Authors: Irina M. Madaeva, Alena A. Pytkina, Olga N. Berdina, Nadezhda A. Kurashova, Natalia V. Semenova, Luibov I. Kolesnikova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is one of the most common sleep disorders. Intermittent nocturnal hypoxia and sleep fragmentation in OSA may be among the main mechanisms triggering cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration. The study was to identify the relationships among plasma total tau (t‐tau) levels, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, and sleep parameters in middle‐aged men with severe OSA.MethodsTwenty‐three men (mean age, 52.3 ± 2.2 years) with severe OSA were included. Nineteen age‐matched men without OSA served as controls. All participants underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG). Global cognition was assessed using the MMSE. Plasma t‐tau levels were measured by enzyme immunoassay. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among plasma t‐tau levels, MMSE scores, and PSG data.ResultsPlasma t‐tau levels were significantly negatively correlated with non‐rapid eye movement sleep stage 3 (N3) duration and mean oxygen saturation (SaO2) and positively correlated with the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and arousal index (AI). MMSE scores were negatively correlated with AHI, ODI, and AI.ConclusionReduced deep sleep duration, nocturnal intermittent hypoxia, and sleep fragmentation were associated with a greater t‐tau burden and lower MMSE performance in severe OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder. Recent estimates indicate that the prevalence of OSA has increased by 30% over the past 20 years. Large‐scale epidemiological studies have shown that 34% of men and 17% of women have OSA of varying severity [1]. Nocturnal intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation cause neuronal damage, leading to neurodegenerative processes and cognitive impairment [2]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that OSA is frequently associated with impairments in attention, memory, and executive function [3]. Tau proteins, particularly total tau (t‐tau), are well‐established markers of neurodegeneration; some authors have described t‐tau as a molecular indicator of brain degeneration that may be associated with cognitive impairment in adults with OSA [4, 5]. Recent studies of age‐related changes in the human proteome have led to the development of strategies to improve proteostasis. Therefore, sleep research, particularly studies of proteostasis in OSA, may facilitate the early prevention of potential neurodegenerative brain damage.

Tau is widely expressed in the central nervous system and is primarily responsible for stabilizing microtubules and promoting their assembly in axons. Physiological tau states, including phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, are tightly regulated. However, tau can form intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) in cerebral cortical neurons; these tangles are major components of insoluble fibers known as paired helical filaments (PHFs). An ever‐growing group of neurodegenerative diseases associated with NFTs is collectively known as tauopathies [6]. A predominant feature of tauopathies is brain atrophy caused by neuronal death, and understanding the different aspects of their pathogenesis may help guide the development of effective therapies.

An earlier pilot study assessing t‐tau levels in patients with OSA suggested that neurodegenerative processes may begin in severe OSA [7]. Elevated t‐tau levels are known to be associated with neuronal damage in OSA. Bhuniya et al. (2022) proposed an integrated approach to cognitive assessment, including the simultaneous analysis of t‐tau and beta‐amyloid [5]. Relevant evidence also indicates that sleep fragmentation affects cognitive performance. Tan et al. (2024) showed that apnea episodes during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep have a pronounced negative effect on cognitive function [8, 9]. Studies have examined associations between tau protein levels and cognitive status, as well as between sleep parameters and cognitive performance, in patients with OSA. However, the relationships among objective sleep data, tau protein levels, and cognition in patients with severe OSA remain unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify the relationships among t‐tau levels, MMSE scores, and sleep parameters in middle‐aged men with severe OSA.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Design

Twenty‐three men (mean age, 52.3 ± 2.2 years) with severe OSA participated in the study. All patients reported snoring, breathing pauses during sleep, frequent nighttime awakenings, and excessive daytime sleepiness. They were prospectively recruited from the Somnological Center between December 2025 and April 2026.

The inclusion criteria were age 46–55 years, ≥ 10 years of education, an apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 30 events/h, mean oxygen saturation (SaO2) < 89%, and an OSA symptom duration of at least 4 years. The exclusion criteria were acute disease or exacerbation of a chronic condition; previous or current OSA treatment; sleep disorders other than OSA; shift work; a concomitant neurological disorder associated with cognitive dysfunction or a family history of Alzheimer's disease; consumption of more than 10 alcoholic drinks per week; use of neuroactive medication; and refusal to participate in the study or provide informed consent. Nineteen age‐matched men without OSA or other sleep disorders served as controls. They were volunteers. The groups were matched for age and education level. All participants completed the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Excessive Sleepiness Scale (ESS) questionnaires [10, 11]. They also completed the MMSE and underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG). Morning blood samples were collected to determine plasma t‐tau levels. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Local Committee on Biomedical Ethics (reference no. 9; December 15, 2025). All participants provided written informed consent.

Methods

Sleep Questionnaires

The PSQI assesses sleep quality and sleep disturbances over the previous month. It comprises 19 items across seven components, each scored from 0 to 3. The total score is calculated by summing all component scores and ranges from 0 to 21; higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. The ESS assesses excessive daytime sleepiness across eight common situations. The likelihood of dozing is rated from 0 to 3 for each item. The total ESS score ranges from 0 to 24. An ESS score > 10 indicates subjective excessive daytime sleepiness, with higher scores reflecting greater sleepiness [12]. The questionnaires were completed at the Somnological Center immediately before PSG.

Polysomnography

Overnight PSG was performed in a dedicated room at the Irkutsk Somnological Center. PSG began between 10:00 and 11:00 p.m. and ended between 6:00 and 7:00 a.m. the following day. Participants underwent overnight PSG using a GRASS Telefactor Comet AS40 amplification system (Astro‐Med, Inc., USA), with continuous video and audio monitoring. The standard PSG protocol included continuous monitoring by electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), chin and leg electromyography (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), airflow, chest and abdominal respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, snoring, and body position. Sleep was scored manually by certified sleep physicians according to the latest American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) criteria [13]. Severe OSA was defined as an AHI ≥ 30 events/h. A mean SaO2 of ≤ 89% lasting ≥ 10 s was defined as a desaturation event. The oxygen desaturation index (ODI) was defined as the number of desaturation events per hour.

Cognitive Assessment

The MMSE was used to assess cognitive function. It consists of questions evaluating cognitive domains, including attention, visuospatial ability, calculation, language, praxis, and memory. The MMSE is a screening tool for cognitive impairment, with a maximum score of 30. Normal cognition was defined as an MMSE score ≥ 28, mild cognitive impairment as a score of 25–27, and global cognitive impairment as a score ≤ 24. A trained researcher administered the MMSE in the morning after PSG.

Assessment of Plasma t‐Tau Levels

Fasting blood samples (5 ml) were collected between 7:00 and 8:00 a.m. after PSG and MMSE testing. Venous blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes, centrifuged at 2000g for 10 min, and the plasma samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. Plasma t‐tau levels were measured by enzyme immunoassay using an ELx808 microplate reader (BioTek, USA) and a Human TAU enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Fine Biotech, China). The kit uses sandwich ELISA technology. An anti‐tau antibody was precoated onto a 96‐well plate, and a biotin‐conjugated anti‐tau antibody was used for detection. The standards and study samples were then added to the wells. After incubation, unbound conjugates were removed using the wash buffer. The biotinylated detection antibody was then added to bind to tau captured by the coated antibody. After unbound conjugates were washed away, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐streptavidin was added. Following a third wash, 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added to visualize the HRP enzymatic reaction. HRP catalyzed TMB to produce a blue product that turned yellow after addition of the stop solution. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The t‐tau concentration in each sample was calculated from a standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA version 10 (StatSoft Inc., USA). The normality of variable distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for small samples. All variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between the two groups were assessed using Student's t test. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations among plasma t‐tau levels, MMSE scores, and PSG data. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Significant between‐group differences were observed in PSQI and ESS scores, AHI, ODI, mean SaO2, MMSE scores, N3, AI, and t‐tau (all p < 0.05). Age, body mass index (BMI), education level, and REM sleep duration did not differ significantly between the groups (all p > 0.05).

More than 80% of participants with severe OSA had PSQI scores ≥ 5, indicating poor overall sleep quality. Almost all patients reported subjective excessive daytime sleepiness (ESS > 10). PSG showed severe OSA in all patients, with high AHI and ODI values and low mean SaO2, indicating substantial nocturnal intermittent hypoxia. Shorter non‐rapid eye movement sleep stage 3 (N3) duration, a predominance of stages 1 and 2 (N1–N2), and a high arousal index (AI) indicated sleep fragmentation. Patients with severe OSA had lower MMSE scores. Plasma t‐tau levels were significantly higher in the OSA group than in the control group. Pearson correlation analysis identified several significant associations: plasma t‐tau levels were negatively correlated with N3 duration (p = 0.002) and mean SaO2 (p < 0.001) and positively correlated with AHI (p < 0.001), ODI (p < 0.001), and AI (p < 0.001). Discussion

In the present study, we provide additional evidence of a potential link between OSA and neurodegenerative processes through the accumulation of pathological tau in the central nervous system. Our findings are consistent with previous evidence. A meta‐analysis by Huang et al. (2023) showed that circulating t‐tau and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) levels were significantly higher in patients with OSA (n = 306) than in controls (n = 233) [14]. We previously obtained similar results in a sample of men and women with severe OSA [7]. However, larger studies are needed to evaluate the relationship between OSA and circulating tau proteins across age groups and sexes.

Evidence suggests an association between elevated t‐tau levels and lower MMSE scores under hypoxic conditions. Hypoxia causes tau hyperphosphorylation, which alters its structure. Tau proteins aggregate to form NFTs and PHFs, leading to neuronal apoptosis and necrosis and subsequent synaptic loss [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Oxygen is essential for organismal survival, and the brain is particularly sensitive to hypoxia because of its high oxygen demand [20]. Chronic intermittent nocturnal hypoxia in OSA disrupts proteostasis and cellular metabolism, subsequently contributing to the development of neurodegenerative disorders. The principal neurodegenerative processes result from protein aggregation under conditions of proteotoxic and oxidative stress [21, 22]. The negative associations of t‐tau levels with N3 duration and mean SaO2, together with the positive associations with AI and ODI, are consistent with current concepts regarding the effects of sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxia on neurodegenerative processes. Recently, results from a longitudinal study of 223 cognitively normal older adults with a family history of sporadic Alzheimer's disease were published [23, 24, 25]. Since 2011, the study has collected sleep data and measured tau and amyloid levels, demonstrating an association between increasing sleep variability, assessed by questionnaires and actigraphy, and tau burden. The investigators also measured amyloid and tau levels using positron emission tomography (PET) and assessed sleep quality using actigraphy and the PSQI.

In contrast, we used overnight PSG to objectively assess sleep architecture, including quantification of N1, N2, N3, and REM sleep. In this pilot study, MMSE scores differed significantly between patients with OSA and controls. Our findings are consistent with those of investigators who suggested that hypoxia and sleep fragmentation increase the risk of cognitive impairment even in younger patients [26]. The MMSE is commonly used to screen for cognitive deficits and dementia [27]. In our study, the changes were modest; although scores did not fall below 27, they were significantly lower in the OSA group than in the control group. Therefore, these findings can be interpreted as lower MMSE performance. The negative association between MMSE scores and OSA severity was expected. Notably, a previous study reported reductions in amyloid and tau protein levels in patients with OSA after treatment with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [28].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The main limitations are the small sample size and the inclusion of only middle‐aged men. Larger studies including participants of both sexes and assessment of sex hormones are warranted. Another limitation is that cognition was assessed using only the MMSE. Future studies should use additional measures of global cognitive status, such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Clinical Dementia Rating scale (CDR), and other tests.

Conclusion

The results of this pilot study demonstrated statistically significant associations of plasma t‐tau levels and MMSE scores with several PSG parameters in middle‐aged men with severe OSA. Reduced deep sleep duration, sleep fragmentation, and nocturnal intermittent hypoxia were associated with a greater t‐tau burden and lower MMSE performance in severe OSA.

Prolonged untreated OSA‐associated intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation may trigger incipient neurodegeneration followed by cognitive decline. This hypothesis requires confirmation in larger studies across diverse ethnic, sex, and age groups and will be investigated in future research.
