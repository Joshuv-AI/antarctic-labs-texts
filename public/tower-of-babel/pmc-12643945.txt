Rapidly Progressive Multiple System Atrophy—Cerebellar Type in a 52‐Year‐Old Woman: Diagnostic Challenges in a Resource‐Limited Setting
Authors: Khushi V Singh, Sai Priya Nimmagadda, Ameesh Panda, Rarigela Rohith, Tirth J Patel, Sonika Gopathoti, Prajesh V Patel, Mihirkumar P Parmar, Saleh Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultiple system atrophy (MSA) is a progressive adult‐onset neurodegenerative disorder that manifests as a variable combination of cerebellar, parkinsonian, and autonomic dysfunctions. We report a case of a 52‐year‐old woman who presented with rapidly progressive gait ataxia, dysarthria, orthostatic hypotension, and urinary urgency. MRI of the brain demonstrated pontocerebellar atrophy with the characteristic “hot cross bun” sign, supporting a diagnosis of Multiple System Atrophy–cerebellar type (MSA‐C). Despite receiving levodopa and supportive therapy, the symptoms progressed over 8 months, highlighting the unusually rapid disease course. This case highlights the diagnostic value of targeted clinical assessment and MRI when advanced confirmatory tests are inaccessible, underscoring the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary symptom management in resource‐constrained settings.Keywords: autonomic dysfunction, cerebellar ataxia, hot cross bun sign, MSA‐C, multiple system atrophy

Introduction

Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) is a rare neurodegenerative disorder that affects approximately 0.6–3.0 individuals per 100,000 annually. This condition is characterized by neuronal loss and gliosis in various regions of the central nervous system (CNS). MSA typically presents in the sixth decade of life, with an average age of onset of 56 ± 9 years. The disease exhibits a higher prevalence in males, with a male‐to‐female ratio of 1.3:1 [1].

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is characterized by the presence of oligodendrocytic glial cytoplasmic inclusions that are positive for α‐synuclein, commonly referred to as Papp‐Lantos bodies. This phenomenon has also been observed in neuropathological studies. Glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) are intracellular aggregates of proteins, predominantly composed of α‐synuclein (α‐syn), specifically within oligodendrocytes. These inclusions are among the most significant microscopic markers of this disease [2, 3, 4, 5].

The hallmarks of MSA include putamen, pontine, and cerebellar atrophy. The two distinct types of MSA are Parkinsonian and cerebellar, as indicated by their primary symptoms. MSA‐P is characterized by extensive striatonigral degeneration, whereas MSA‐C is characterized by modest to moderate symptoms. Due to the disease, there are substantial modifications in the caudate nucleus and dorsolateral caudate putamen. GABAergic medium spiny neurons are particularly susceptible to this phenomenon [6]. MSA‐C affects specific brain regions. They consist of cerebellopontine fibers, pontine bases, inferior olive nuclei, and dentate. MSA‐C affects various brain regions. They consist of cerebellopontine fibers, pontine bases, inferior olive nuclei, and dentate nuclei [2].

Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremors, posture, and balance disorders are the characteristics that distinguish MSA‐P, which is more prevalent than MSA‐P. In general, patients with MSA‐C exhibit slurred language, impaired vision, impaired movement and coordination, and difficulty in imbibing or ingesting food. Although they differ, MSA‐P and MSA‐C exhibit overlapping indications, including urinary incompetence, erectile dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension, and mental disorders [7]. Initially, the disease progressed and deteriorated at an accelerated rate. Within 5 years of disease onset, patients with MSA typically become unable to walk and die within 10 years [8].

Currently, no effective treatments are available for MSA. However, it is possible to employ various evidence‐based therapies to reduce the severity of symptoms and enhance the quality of life [9, 10, 11].

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A 52‐year‐old woman presented to the neurology outpatient department with progressive ambulatory difficulty and slurred speech of one‐month duration. Her medical history was notable for a hysterectomy in 2014 and Pott's spine, for which she had undergone spinal surgery and had been receiving antitubercular therapy since June 2023. She had no history of head trauma, toxin exposure, or psychiatric illnesses. There was no family history of inherited or progressive neurological disorder. She did not smoke or consume alcohol and had previously maintained a good general health.

Clinical Findings

During the preceding three to 4 months, the patient had noticed increasing unsteadiness while walking, difficulty in initiating gait, and repeated retropulsive (backward) falls occurring six to eight times. She reported slow, slurred, and monotonous speech, dizziness while standing, stiffness in the right shoulder, and urinary urgency with increased frequency.

On examination, the patient was conscious, alert, and fully oriented to time, place, and person. The vital signs were stable. Neurological examination revealed dysarthric speech and hypermetric saccadic eye movements in the patient. Muscle strength was preserved (Medical Research Council grade 5/5) in all limbs. Muscle tone was increased with mild spasticity, and the deep tendon reflexes were brisk. The sensory modalities were intact. The Romberg sign was positive, with falls in multiple directions when her eyes were closed, and the finger‐to‐nose test showed dysmetria. Plantar responses were bilaterally flexor, and Hoffmann and Babinski signs were absent. The gait was broad‐based and ataxic, requiring bilateral support.

A tilt‐table test revealed neurogenic orthostatic hypotension, with blood pressure decreasing from 135/90 mmHg in the supine position to 105/70 mmHg on standing after 3 min, a drop of 30/20 mmHg. Routine laboratory investigations, chest radiography, echocardiography, and abdominal ultrasonography were unremarkable. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, mycobacterial culture, and GeneXpert testing were negative, ruling out infectious relapse of spinal tuberculosis (TB).

Diagnostic Assessment

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain demonstrated cerebellar and pontine atrophy with cruciform hyperintensity in the pons, the classical “hot cross bun” sign on axial T2‐weighted and FLAIR images (Figure 1). The basal ganglia appeared normal, and no supratentorial abnormalities were observed.

A whole‐body fluorodeoxyglucose positron‐emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET‐CT) scan was performed to exclude paraneoplastic or inflammatory etiologies of hypercalcemia. The scan showed physiological tracer distribution without FDG‐avid lesions or malignancy. Mild hepatomegaly and degenerative vertebral changes due to Pott's spine were observed (Figure 2).

Management

The patient was admitted to the neurology department for further evaluation and management. A therapeutic trial of long‐term levodopa–carbidopa combination therapy was initiated to assess dopaminergic responsiveness and manage parkinsonian features. Ropinirole, a dopamine agonist, was administered to stabilize levodopa levels and reduce nocturnal symptoms. Amitriptyline was prescribed to address sleep disturbances, and betahistine with vitamin D supplementation was provided for vestibular support and bone health.

Given the patient's history of Pott's spine, antitubercular therapy consisting of rifampicin, isoniazid, and ethambutol was continued. Midodrine was used to manage orthostatic hypotension, and pyridoxine supplementation was maintained. Additional medications included an antihypertensive combination (telmisartan, cilnidipine, and chlorthalidone), rosuvastatin for dyslipidemia, and amantadine as adjunct therapy for extrapyramidal symptoms.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Despite optimized medical and supportive care, the patient's condition deteriorated rapidly. Within 8 months of symptom onset, she became wheelchair dependent. The combination of progressive cerebellar ataxia, autonomic failure, poor levodopa response, and the MRI “hot cross bun” sign established the diagnosis of Multiple System Atrophy‐Cerebellar type (MSA‐C) according to the 2022 Movement Disorder Society diagnostic criteria (Table 3).

Conclusion and Results

Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by complex presentation and rapid progression, posing significant treatment challenges due to the ineffectiveness of levodopa. The patient, a 52‐year‐old woman, exhibited dysarthria, ataxia, and Parkinsonism. Neurological assessments and brain MRI revealed cerebellar atrophy and the hot cross bun sign in the pons, leading to a diagnosis of MSA‐C. We advocate for prioritizing patient quality of life through treatments that emphasize rapid and accurate diagnosis.

Discussion

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that affects multiple regions of the central nervous system (CNS), including the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem. Clinically, MSA may present with a combination of extrapyramidal, cerebellar, and autonomic symptoms that evolve as the disease advances. The pathological process involves neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra, caudate, putamen (particularly its posterior portion), globus pallidus, inferior olives, pontine nuclei, cerebellar Purkinje cells and intermediolateral spinal cord columns. Unlike idiopathic Parkinson's disease, post‐encephalitic parkinsonism, or progressive supranuclear palsy, MSA lacks Lewy bodies and neurofibrillary tangles [12].

Two major phenotypes are recognized: parkinsonian (MSA‐P) and cerebellar (MSA‐C). In men, autonomic dysfunction, such as erectile failure, commonly precedes motor symptoms, whereas in women, urinary urgency or incontinence may occur early. Parkinsonian features, such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability, predominate in MSA‐P, whereas cerebellar ataxia and dysarthria characterize MSA‐C [4]. The coexistence of cerebellar signs and autonomic failure, as in our patient, should raise suspicion for MSA‐C, particularly when the progression is rapid and the response to levodopa is minimal.

Pathologically, degeneration occurs in the striatonigral and olivopontocerebellar pathways, accompanied by glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) composed of fibrillated α‐synuclein [13, 14, 15], which are considered neuropathological hallmarks of MSA, although their exact pathogenic mechanisms remain uncertain. α‐Synuclein aggregates may also appear as neuronal cytoplasmic or nuclear inclusions, contributing to axonal degeneration and neuroinflammation [16, 17, 18, 19]. Current evidence supports both SNCA gene overexpression and intercellular propagation of α‐synuclein as potential mechanisms of GCI accumulation [20, 21, 22, 23].

In clinical practice, however, MSA diagnosis is based on the integration of clinical features and neuroimaging rather than histopathology. MRI remains a pivotal tool, and the “hot cross bun” sign, which is a cross‐shaped hyperintensity in the pons, is highly suggestive of MSA‐C, reflecting pontocerebellar tract degeneration [1, 7, 8]. In our patient, the characteristic imaging findings, combined with progressive cerebellar ataxia and neurogenic orthostatic hypotension, supported the diagnosis according to the 2022 Movement Disorder Society criteria.

Cognitive impairment is increasingly recognized in patients with MSA, particularly in the cerebellar subtype. Prior studies demonstrated significantly lower Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores in MSA‐C compared with healthy controls and MSA‐P [24]. Although cognitive testing was not formally performed in our patient, mild behavioral changes were reported, which is consistent with existing evidence.

This case highlights several diagnostic challenges. The early overlap of cerebellar and autonomic features can mimic Parkinson's disease, spinocerebellar ataxias, or paraneoplastic cerebellar syndromes, often leading to a delayed recognition. In resource‐limited settings, where advanced assays such as seed‐amplification tests or functional imaging are unavailable, reliance on careful clinical assessment and conventional MRI is crucial. Awareness of the rapid course of MSA‐C, often leading to wheelchair dependence within a few years, enables timely counseling, multidisciplinary support, and realistic goal setting for patients and families.
