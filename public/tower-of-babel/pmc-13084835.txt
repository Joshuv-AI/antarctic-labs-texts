Cardioprotective effect of hydroalcoholic extract stem bark of Xeroderris stuhlmannii on cadmium chloride-induced hypertension and its possible vasorelaxant mechanism on isolated aortic rats
Authors: Jacquy Joyce Wanche Kojom, Willifred Dongmo Tekapi Tsopgni, Russelle Camelie Nguemnang Tchatchouang, Anatole Guy Blaise Azebaze, Timo D Stark, Alain Bertrand Dongmo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundXeroderris stuhlmannii is a medicinal plant, widely used in Cameroonian folk medicine to treat hypertension.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the beneficial effect of X. stuhlmannii in hypertensive rats and to explore the vasorelaxant mechanisms of its hydroalcoholic extract and to determine the compounds responsible of the activities.Materials and methodsHydroalcoholic extract, fractions, subfractions and isolated compounds were screened for the antihypertensive activity in Wistar rat of either sex using a noninvasive method to record blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR). At the end of the treatment, the level of nitrite in the tissues and the histological analysis of the heart and aorta were determined. Vasorelaxant activity of the plant was carried out in vitro, on the contractile responses of aortic rings induced by phenylephrine (10−6 M) or KCl (60 mM). Mechanisms involved vasorelaxation effect of X. stuhlmannii were assessed using various antagonists.ResultsTreatment of Cadmium-hypertensive rats with extract significantly (p ˂ 0.001) reduced BP and HR, improved nitrite level, ameliorated heart and aorta alterations. Moreover, the hydroalcoholic extract, fractions, subfraction and odoratin-induced vasorelaxant effect. The preincubation with NG-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester, methylene blue and tetraethylammonium partially inhibited hydroalcoholic extract/fraction-induced relaxation. The extract completely inhibited the effects on CaCl2-induced contracted rings and reduced the contraction induced by phenylephrine in a Ca2+-free solution.ConclusionThese results support the traditional use of X. stuhlmannii in the treatment of hypertension. This effect may be due in part to the plant’s vasorelaxant activity and the presence of compounds such as phenol in plant extracts.Keywords: Xeroderris stuhlmannii, fractions, isolated compound, hypertension, vasorelaxant effect

Introduction

Environmental contamination by heavy metals is gradually becoming one of the main causes of distress and mortality in the world due to its harmful effects on human health (Jessica et al. 2020; Al-Khayri et al. 2022). Metals such as arsenic, lead, copper and cadmium are common in ever-growing industrial operations, and can play a major role in the onset and development of cancer, pulmonary edema, renal and liver dysfunctions (Satarug et al. 2010; Jaishankar et al. 2014). Exposure to heavy metals has been also related to an increased risk of coronary heart disease and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) (Lee and Kim 2012; Cai et al. 2017).

CVD is defined as disease of the heart and coronary blood vessels (Alves-Silva et al. 2016). Among the risk factors for cardiovascular disease, hypertension is the main cause of cardiovascular mortality, affecting 1.39 billion people worldwide (Mills et al. 2020; Fodem et al. 2021). The rise in the prevalence of hypertension is due to a number of factors, including physical inactivity, smoking, alcoholism, overweight and, more recently, chronic exposure to heavy metals such as cadmium (Tellez-Plaza et al. 2013; Weber et al. 2014).

Cadmium (Cd) is an important environmental pollutant due to its high degree of toxicity (Tchounwou et al. 2012). Upon absorption, cadmium is transported by blood where it accumulates in various tissues for a long-term (Mohamed et al. 2009). The kidney is a primary target organ after chronic exposure to Cd (Roels et al. 1999). Aside a kidney, Cd is predominantly stored in the liver, the first site of its metabolism and accumulation (Attia et al. 2022).

As the cardiovascular system is not the main target of Cd toxicity, the precise biological mechanisms by which the metal affects the cardiovascular system have not been clearly elucidated (Oyinloye et al. 2016). However, experimental reports indicate that oxidative stress and endothelial damage may be an important process in the development of hypertension induced by Cd (Wang et al. 2004; Prabu et al. 2013).

Considering the mechanisms of Cd toxicity, many types of chelators and metal antagonists have been used to counteract its toxic effects. However, a number of undesirable side effects have limited their application (Fagerberg et al. 2013; Kukongviriyapan et al. 2016). Natural products are being efficiently used to treat the adverse consequences (Tchounwou et al. 2012; Bhattacharya 2018). Recently, there has been growing interest in new antihypertensive agents derived from plant extracts, and bioactive compounds from natural sources are becoming the new method of choice for the treatment of hypertension (Oyinloye et al. 2016; Jayalalita et al. 2021). In addition, regulating vascular tone is one of the most recent approaches to reducing arterial pressure (Salahdeen et al. 2016; Takashima et al. 2017).

Xeroderris stuhlmannii Taub. (Fabaceae) is a plant commonly used in traditional medicine for the treatment of icteria, nausea, intestinal parasitosis and fever (Kaoru et al. 2011). It is also used as an analgesic, anti-inflammatory and diuretic (Bruschi et al. 2011; Maroyi 2012). Ethnobotanical studies mention the use of this plant in the treatment of high blood pressure (Kaoru et al. 2011). Besides the scientifically proven antihypertensive effect of the leaves of this plant (Kuinze et al. 2024), there is little information available on any possible cardioprotective effect of the bark, which is the most commonly used part of X. stuhlmannii. As this plant is widely used in Cameroonian’s traditional medicine to treat cardiovascular affections and as it has shown great biological potential (Mekuete et al. 2023), we considered it worthwhile to study the therapeutic efficacy of the stem bark extract of X. stuhlmannii as well as some molecules that have been isolated from it. Therefore, this study was designed to assess the possible cardioprotective activity of X. stuhlmannii hydroalcoholic extract against cadmium-induced hypertension. Furthermore, given the vascular tone is directly involved in the regulation and management of blood pressure, the vasorelaxant effect of X. stuhlmannii extract, fractions, subfractions and major isolated compounds was evaluated in rat aortic rings.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and drugs

Cadmium chloride salt (CaCl2), amlodipine, NG-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME), indomethacin, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), phenylephrine hydrochloride (PE), acetylcholine chloride (Ach), glibenclamide, barium chloride (BaCl2), methylene bleue (MB), potassium chloride (KCl), pyrilamine maleate (Pyr), atropine (Atr), propranolol (Prop), yohimbine, nifedipine were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Tetraethylammonium (TEA) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were obtained from VWR (Darmstadt, Germany). All the chemicals and reagents used to prepare the physiological saline solutions and other analyses were of analytical grade.

All compounds were dissolved in distilled water, except isolated compounds, indomethacin and glibenclamide which were dissolved in aqueous DMSO 5% and sonicated just before use.

Plant collection, extraction, and isolation of bioactive compounds

Xeroderris stuhlmannii stem barks were harvested in Tonga, west region, Republic of Cameroon, in November 2023. Botanical identification was made by Dr. Tacham Walter (University of Bamenda, Cameroon) and compared to a voucher specimen (N°6011/SFR/CAM) deposited at the National Herbarium of Cameroon.

Afterwards, the dried stem bark powder (100 g) of X. stuhlmannii was suspended in a mixture of ethanol/water (600 mL, 70/30, v/v), ultrasonicated (10 min), stirred at room temperature (30 min), and filtered. Ethanol was removed from the filtrate under reduced pressure, and the remaining aqueous extract was repeatedly defatted with n-pentane (3 × 300 mL, each), then both fractions were freeze-dried. The yields of the corresponding extracts were between 5 and 9 (ethanol/water) as well as 2%–4% (pentane).

The hydroalcoholic extract, which first demonstrated a drop in blood pressure and then vasorelaxant activity (more than 80%) on PE- or KCl-precontracted aortic fragments, was further fractionated using ultrafiltration (cut-off 5 kDa). The low molecular weight fraction (<5KDa filtrate) was repeatedly separated by RP (reversed phase) medium pressure liquid chromatography (MPLC), yielding nine fractions (M1-M9). All subfractions were combined, freed from the solvent under vacuum and freeze-dried (Mekuete et al. 2023). Fraction M9 was the most active fraction in the vasorelaxant assay; therefore, M9 was further subdivided in the following MPLC runs into M9-M1 to M9-M4. According to the previous vasorelaxant test, M9-M1 was the most active subfraction showing a percentage of relaxation of about 108%. This subfraction M9-M1 was analyzed via ultra-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray-ionization-travelling wave ion mobility separation-time-of-flight-mass spectrometry (UPLC–ESI–TWIMS–TOF–MS) (Kuinze et al. 2024), as well as separated by means of high pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) (M9-M1-H1 to M9-M1-H6) for starting the isolation of bioactive compound (Mekuete et al. 2023).

Animals

Adult male and female Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old, weighing 200–220 g) were provided by the animal house of the Faculty of Science, University of Douala, Cameroon. The procedures involving the animals were in compliance with the guidelines established for the care and use of animals in biomedical research and were approved by the Institutional Ethic Committee of the University of Douala (N°4006 CEI-UDo/09/2023/T). The animals were housed in plastic cages under standard laboratory conditions for 12 h light/dark cycles in room temperature, with food and water ad libitum. They were acclimatized to adapt to their surrounding environment and hemodynamic measures for 2 weeks before the commencement of the experiments.

Antihypertensive activity

Experimental protocol

Fifty-six rats were divided into two groups. Group A, control group (n = 6) received intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) of saline solution (1 mL/100g b.w) and group B, hypertensive group (n = 50) was treated (i.p.) with CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w) daily for 21 days. After three weeks of CdCl2-injection, only rats with a blood pressure greater than or equal to 140/90 mmHg were used for further studies. The selected animals (n = 6 rats/group) were randomly divided into different groups as follows:

Group 1 (control group): normotensive rats received i.p. injection of 0.9% saline solution (1mL/100 b.w) concomitantly with distilled water per OS.

Group 2 (hypertensive group): hypertensive rats received i.p. of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w)

Group 3 (standard group): hypertensive rats received oral administration of amlodipine (10 mg/kg b.w) followed by i.p. injection of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w)

Group 3 (standard group): hypertensive rats received oral administration of amlodipine (10 mg/kg b.w) followed by i.p. injection of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w)

Groups 4 and 5 (assay groups): hypertensive rats received oral administration of X. stuhlmannii (100 and 200 mg/kg b.w., respectively) followed by i.p injection of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w).

Groups 4 and 5 (assay groups): hypertensive rats received oral administration of X. stuhlmannii (100 and 200 mg/kg b.w., respectively) followed by i.p injection of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg b.w).

The control group received tap water as drinking water, while the CdCl2-treated group received drinking water supplemented with NaCl (1%) continuously for a period of 2 weeks. All drugs were dissolved in distilled water and administered at the same time of day between 08.00 am and 09.00 am. Bodyweight was also monitored weekly after the baseline value was taken.

Rats were restrained and acclimatized in the holder for 10–20 min/day for 5 days prior to recording of blood pressure. Acclimated animals should provide faster blood pressure measurements than non-acclimated animals and then minimizing stress during the experiment. Systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) were recorded in conscious animals by CODA noninvasive blood pressure system (Kent Scientific Co., USA) at baseline and twice weekly for two weeks after starting treatment (Kojom et al. 2022). Rats were kept on an immobilized support and, after a 30-min warm-up period (32–35 °C), the parameters were registered via tail-cuff transducer by using a computer-assisted monitoring device (CODA 4.1 software, USA). For each session, blood pressure was measured 10 times consecutively, composed of four (4) preliminary sets to allow the rats to adapt to the rodent holder and cuff and, six (6) experimental measurements. After that, around four readings were selected and recorded on average for every trial of the animal and calculated.

Estimation of oxidative stress parameter

At the end of the experimental period, rats were anesthetized with a combination of diazepam (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (50 mg/kg) given intraperitoneally. After that, the rats were killed by decapitation. The heart and aorta of each animal were rapidly removed, cleared of fatty tissue and weighed after washing in normal saline. Representative fragments of each organ were crushed in Mac Even solution to prepare 20% homogenate. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 30 min, the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C for biochemical analyses. Nitric oxide (NO) assay was measured using the Griess method (Ikeda et al. 2003).

Histopathological examination

The other parts of the heart and aortic tissues from all experimental groups were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated in alcohol series, impregnated with xylene, and then embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared and colored with hematoxylin–eosin (Xu et al. 2015). The histological analysis of the stained sections was carried out under an optical microscope. Using the Image J software, the resulting aorta images was used for the media size measurement.

Vasorelaxant action mechanisms

Aorta isolation and mounting

Rats of either sex (8–12 weeks old, weighing 200–300 g) were anesthetized with urethane and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Superior part of the thoracic aorta was carefully dissected out and immersed in ice-cold oxygenated Krebs–Henseleit (K–H) solution containing (mM): NaCl (118); KCl (4.7); CaCl2 (2); KH2PO4 (1.2); MgCl2 (1.2); NaHCO3 (25) and glucose (11.5). Aortic rings (3–4 mm in length) were obtained free of connective tissue and fat and, introduced in an organ chamber as described by Vierling et al. (2003). Rings were gently suspended horizontally between two stainless steel hooks and introduced. The first set of experiment (involvement of K+ and Ca2+ channels) was conducted on endothelium-denuded aorta as described by Dongmo et al. (2011) and Kojom et al. (2024).

The second set of experiments was carried out on aorta fragments in the presence of endothelium. Endothelium-intact vessel was assessed by measuring the relaxation response to acetylcholine (ACh, 10 μM), after precontraction with phenylephrine (10−6 M). Endothelium was considered intact when ACh caused a relaxation equal or greater than 80% (Fodem et al. 2021). After this state was achieved, the bath solution was removed until the resting tone was recovered before beginning experiments. Aortic rings with or without endothelium were incubated with L-NAME (100 μM), methylene blue (10 μM), indomethacin (10 μM), propranolol (5 µM), pyrilamine (10 μM), and atropine (10 μM) for 20 min prior to pre-contraction with PE in order to determine the mechanisms underlying the relaxing effects of X. stuhlmannii.

Data from the both sets of experiments were recorded continuously on the iox2 data acquisition software version 2.9.10.1 from Emka Technologies.

Effects of hydroalcoholic extract, fractions, subfractions and isolated compounds of X. stuhlmannii on the PE-induced contraction

Endothelium-free aortic rings were pre-contracted with PE (10−6 M). After the contractile response became steady, concentration-response curves of the extract or fractions (M1-9), subfractions (M9-M1-4) (1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300 and 700 μg/mL) and isolated compounds (1, 3, 10, 30, 44 μg/mL) were obtained by adding cumulative concentrations of samples to the bath. The vasorelaxant effects of X. stuhlmannii extract, fractions, subfractions and isolated compounds were expressed as a percentage of relaxation in response to PE (Dongmo et al. 2011).

In another series of experiment, denuded aortic rings were incubated with hydroalcoholic extract (300 𝜇g/mL), fraction M9 (100 𝜇g/mL) or not, during 20 min before the cumulative addition of PE (10−9–10−5 M). Finally, the contractile effects induced by PE were recorded and the corresponding concentration-response curves were plotted.

Effects of hydroalcoholic extract and fractions of X. stuhlmannii on the contraction induced by KCl

The extract or fractions (1 μg/mL to 700 μg/mL) were added cumulatively to the organ bath when a steady contraction induced by KCl (60 mM) in rings without the endothelium was reached. Then, the vasorelaxant effect of X. stuhlmannii extract and fractions was expressed as a percentage of relaxation in response to KCl (Zhou et al. 2019).

Assessment of potassium channels/NO/cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)/prostaglandin/histamine H1/muscarinic and adrenergic and calcium channels pathway in the vasorelaxant effect of extract and fractions of X. stuhlmannii

In order to evaluate the involvement of potassium (K+) channels on the extract or M9 induced relaxation, endothelium-denuded aortic rings were incubated with TEA (10 mM), a nonselective K+ channel blocker or BaCl2 (100 µM), an inwardly rectified K+ (Kir) channel blocker or glibenclamide (10 µM), an ATP-dependant K+ (KATP) channel blocker, for 20 min prior to contraction by PE (10−6 M). After that, the extract (700 µg/mL) or M9 (300 µg/mL) was added and the results obtained were compared with those registered with rings not pre-incubated with the blockers above mentioned (Dongmo et al. 2014; Zhou et al. 2019).

To assess the involvement NO/cGMP/prostaglandin/adrenergic/H1/muscarinic and calcium channels pathway in the vasorelaxant effect of hydroalcoholic extract and M9, the following inhibitors were used: L-NAME (100 𝜇M), a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor; methylene blue (10 𝜇M), a guanylate cyclase inhibitor; indomethacin (10 μM), a nonselective cyclooxygenase inhibitor; propranolol (5 µM), a β2-adrenergic antagonist; pyrilamine (10 μM), a histamine H1-receptor antagonist; or atropine (10 μM), a muscarinic receptor antagonist, were used to incubate endothelium-intact rings 20 min before the contraction induced by PE (10−6 M). The extract of X. stuhlmannii (700 µg/mL) or M9 (100 µg/mL) was then added and the concentration-response curves were constructed for the inhibitory responses (Dongmo et al. 2011; Lais et al. 2012; Tan et al. 2017).

This experiment was undertaken to demonstrate the role of hydroalcoholic extract on calcium channel. Firstly, the endothelium denuded rings were contracted with KCl (60 mM) for 30 min in normal Krebs solution to ensure the organ’s viability and functionality. When the contraction reached its peak, the rings were cleaned in normal Krebs for 30 min and then placed into Ca2+-free Krebs solution (containing EDTA and 60 mM KCl) for another 30 min. Then, to assess the intracellular calcium involvement, aortic rings were pre-incubated with hydroalcoholic extract (300 μg/mL), before the induction of a contraction with PE (1 μM). Aortic rings without pretreatment with X. stuhlmannii were considered as the control group (Tan et al. 2017).

In another experimental set, the influence of X. stuhlmannii on calcium influx was evaluated using aortic rings subjected with high K+ (60 mM) containing Ca2+-free Krebs solution for 30 min. Afterwards, the rings were incubated with hydroalcoholic extract (300 µg/mL) for 20 min, and subsequently, CaCl2 (1, 3 and 10 mM) was added cumulatively at 20 min intervals to obtain concentration-response curves. The contractile responses were expressed as the percentage of the maximum contraction (=100%) obtained with 10 mM CaCl2. The same procedure was applied with nifedipine (10 μM), used as the standard control (Dongmo et al. 2014).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5.0. The data were expressed as mean ± SEM and n refers to the number of animals or aorta rings used. Differences of continuous variables between treatment groups were assessed by one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test followed by Tukey post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Two-way ANOVA repeated measures followed by the Bonferroni post-hoc test were used to analyze data with two variables. Concentration-response curves were plotted and fitted and EC50 was determined using sigmoidal non-linear regression. The value of p < 0.05 was considered as significant.

Effect of hydroalcoholic extract of X. stuhlmannii on blood pressure and heart rate

As shown in Figure 2, chronic administration of CdCl2 (0.5 mg/kg) + NaCl (1% in water drink) caused a significant (p ˂ 0.001) increase in SBP, DBP and MBP, reaching to 30.33%, 35.83% and 32.30%, respectively, after 2 weeks of treatment when compared to the control group. The treatment with X. stuhlmannii (100 and 200 mg/kg) or amlodipine (10 mg/kg) significantly (p ˂0.001) reduced the increase of SBP (25.79%, 25.21% and 27.30%), DBP (28.94%, 26.34% and 32.29%) and MBP (26.16%, 25.71% and 28.99%), when compared to hypertensive group after 2 weeks of treatment (Figures 2(a–c)).

In addition, CdCl2-treated rats exhibited 33.11% higher HR than the control group. Hydroalcoholic extract of X. stuhlmannii significantly (p ˂0.001) decreased HR by 19.05% and 21.85% at 100 and 200 mg/kg, respectively, when compared to the hypertensive group. Amlodipine (10 mg/kg) used as standard also decreased the rise in heart rate (22.19%, p ˂ 0.001) at the end of the treatment (Figure 2(d)).

Effect of hydroalcoholic extract of X. stuhlmannii on nitrite level

Tissue level of NO was decreased in CdCl2-induced hypertensive rat models compared to the NO levels in the normotensive group (control). Amlodipine (10 mg/kg) and X. stuhlmannii extract (200 mg/kg) significantly reversed the effect of CdCl2 by significantly (p < 0.01) increasing the nitrite level when compared with the hypertensive group (Figure 3).

Vasorelaxant effect of X. stuhlmannii hydroalcoholic extract, fractions and isolated compounds

X. stuhlmannii extract induced a concentration-dependent relaxant activity on PE (10−6 M) precontracted aortic rings with an EC50 = 316.50 µg/mL and Emax value of 83.22% at the highest concentration (700 µg/mL). Only fractions M3, M8 and M9 showed a significantly vasorelaxant effect among nine fractions tested when compared to control. The maximum relaxant effect was, respectively, of 58.01% (EC50 = 568.61 µg/mL at 700 µg/mL), 102.8% (EC50 = 102.80 µg/mL at 300 µg/mL) and 103.4% (EC50 = 17.30 µg/mL at the 100 µg/mL), respectively, for fractions M3, M8 and M9 (Figure 6(a)).

The relaxant effect was calculated as a percentage of the contraction in response to PE or KCL. Values are mean ± S.E.M (n = 4). ***p < 0.001 significantly different in comparison with the control.

As shown in Figure 6(b), cumulative concentrations of X. stuhlmannii significantly produced concentration-dependent relaxation on denuded aortic rings precontracted with 60 mM KCl, resulting in an EC50 = 738.40 µg/mL and Emax value of 61.99% at 700 µg/mL as compared to the control. Fractions M8 (Emax: 97.34% at 700 µg/mL, EC50 = 11.53 µg/mL) and M9 (Emax: 94.76% at 700 µg/mL, EC50 = 63.39 µg/mL) showed the highest vasorelaxant activities on the KCl-induced contraction.

Discussion

Cd is a toxic heavy metal found in the environment that affects many systems in humans and animals (Ijaz et al. 2021). Several reports indicate that Cd exposure has been associated with hypertension (Tellez-Plaza et al. 2013). In this study, chronic administration of CdCl2 in rat resulted in an increase in blood pressure and heart rate. The precise mechanisms that linking cadmium exposition and hypertension are not clear. However, reports have highlighted that the hypertensive effect of cadmium exposure may be due to endothelial and smooth muscle cell dysfunction (Prozialeck et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2004). Thus, in vascular smooth muscle cells, Cd, by mimicking the Ca2+ ion as a partial agonist, leads to a direct contractile effect (Qu and Zheng 2024). The antihypertensive activity X. stuhlmannii hydroalcoholic is thought to be due to interference with calcium channels, resulting in vasodilation. In addition, the presence of flavonoids compound, like formononetin, an O-methylated isoflavone, acting via NO/Ca2+ channel pathway could contribute the vasodilation and antihypertensive activity of the extract (Wu et al. 2010; Sun et al. 2011). Similarly, concomitant administration of CdCl2 with amlodipine, used as a standard drug provoked a significant decreased in heart rate and blood pressure in hypertensive rats. Indeed, amlodipine is a dihydropyridine calcium antagonist which acts through blocking calcium influx into vascular smooth cells and cardiac muscle leading to a decrease in peripheral vascular resistance and blood pressure (Toba et al. 2005; Kojom et al. 2022).

The potential mechanism of cadmium-induced endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress is well-known (Wang et al. 2004; Prabu et al. 2013). There is strong evidence that Cd reduces the NO bioavailability and leads to an elevation in blood pressure (Skoczynska and Martynowicz 2005). The present study revealed that Cd-induced arterial hypertension is related to a decrease in nitrite levels. Administration of X. stuhlmannii extract significantly increases the bioavailability of nitrite, which would contribute to the vasodilatory and antihypertensive effects. These effects are thought to be linked to the presence of (poly) phenols such as irisolidone, odoratin, afromosin and formononetin, which have been recognized as excellent free radical scavengers (Shirataki et al. 1997; Patel 2022; Mekuete et al. 2023) and NO releasing agents (Sun et al. 2011). Similar results have already been reported by Ahmed and Hassanein (2013). Additionally, amlodipine has been shown to increase the release of NO in arteries, cardiac tissue and isolated cells (Toba et al. 2005; Celik et al. 2015).

CdCl2 is known to be a cardiotoxic agent (Oyinloye et al. 2016), leading to tissue injury by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and depleting antioxidants inducing oxidative stress (Adi et al. 2016; Battacharjee et al. 2019). Previous results reported that an increase of nitrite levels in heart and aorta homogenates indicating damage in these organs. In Cd-hypertensive rats histological analysis of the myocardium revealed myofibrillar necrosis, leukocytic infiltration and impairment of the integrity of the histological architecture of the aorta, as evidence of hypertrophic of the aortic wall (tunica media). Hydroalcoholic extract as well as amlodipine improved all the damage, indicating its cardioprotective effect against oxidative damage and, corroborate several uses of medicinal plants and bioactive compounds which possess antioxidant property for the treatment of hypertension induced by cadmium (Oyinloye et al. 2016; Jayalalita et al. 2021).

Vasomotricity plays a key factor in the physiopathology of the cardiovascular system. Thus, vasorelaxant activities of hydroalcoholic extract, fractions, subfractions as well as isolated compounds from X. stuhlmannii were carried out on rat aortic rings.

Hydroalcoholic extract, fractions (M3, M8 and M9), subfraction (M9-M1) and compound 4 (odoratin) elicited relaxation on aortic rings pre-contracted with KCl or PE, suggesting that, they are able to inhibit the permeation of extracellular Ca2+ [Ca2+]i both voltage-dependent (VDCC) and receptor operated (ROCC) Ca2+ channels since these two types of Ca2+ channels are presents in smooth muscle. Moreover, these substances may also act on the vascular endothelium or/and vascular smooth muscle by stimulating vasoactive compounds and/or by interfering with blood vessel channels, receptors or enzymes (Ch’ng et al. 2017).

Besides Ca2+ channels, K+ channels play an essential role in the regulation of the membrane potential (Ko et al. 2008). The opening of K+ channels in vascular smooth cells results in membrane hyperpolarization which provides on arteries dilatation (Estrada-Soto et al. 2010). Consequently, drugs that stimulate these channels could be useful in the management of hypertension (Lawson 2000). The presence of glibenclamide, a KATP channel blocker and BaCl2, a Kir channel blocker (Novakovic et al. 2006), did not affect the vasorelaxant effects of the hydroalcoholic extract and fraction M9. Nevertheless, incubation with TEA, a nonselective K+ channel blocker (Novakovic et al. 2006), significantly decreased the relaxant effects of the hydroalcoholic extract and fraction M9, suggesting that relaxation on vascular smooth cells is partially mediated by the KCa channels (Gan et al. 2016).

It is well known that, vascular endothelium plays an essential role in cardiovascular functions (Dayang et al. 2018), mainly by regulating vascular tone through synthesis and releasing vasodilator agents, including NO, COX (cyclooxygenase)-derived products, and endothelial-derived hyperpolarizating factors (EDHFs) (Furchgott and Zawadzki 1980; Félétou et al. 2012). In order to determinate the involvement of these EDRFs in the vasorelaxant effect caused by the hydroalcoholic extract, the rings were incubated with L-NAME (a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor) or methylene blue (a guanylate cyclase inhibitor) (Zhou et al. 2019). Both blockers partly prevented the vasorelaxant effect of the hydroalcoholic extract. So, NO/cGMP signaling pathway seems to be an important mechanism underlying the X. stuhlmannii exerts vasorelaxant effects. The same results have been obtained with fraction M9 indicating also a vasorelaxant effect mediated NO/cGMP. This effect may be due to the presence in this extract of formononetin, a compound whose vasorelaxant activity has been demonstrated via the release of NO. In addition, there could be a number of vasoactive compounds present that reinforce the vasorelaxant effect of extract via various pathways (Wu et al. 2010; Sun et al. 2011).

Following these findings, additional investigations were carried out in the presence of indomethacin; a cyclooxygenase inhibitor, propranolol; a nonselective β-adrenoceptor blocker, atropin; a muscarinic receptor antagonist and pyrilamine maleate; a histamine H1 antagonist (Lais et al. 2012; Tan et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2019), all did not affect significantly the vasorelaxation effect of extract in aortic rings precontracted by PE, which suggest that the effect was may not be mediated via these pathways. Further work will be carried out to support these initial results. Aortic rings pretreated with X. stulhmannii produced a shift of the PE-induced concentration-response curves to the right, with a significant reduction suggests a noncompetitive inhibition by the extract or its fraction.

In Ca2+-free solution, pretreatment with the hydroalcoholic extract (300 µg/mL) significantly reduced the magnitude of phenylephrine-induced contraction. It seems that the hydroalcoholic extract blocked the Ca2+ release from intracellular Ca2+store and subsequently reduced the PE induced transient vascular contraction (Gan et al. 2016). On the other hand, the influence of extract on extracellular Ca2+ influx-dependent vasoconstriction was studied. X. stuhlmannii also markedly inhibited cumulative addition of Ca2+-induced vasoconstriction in Ca2+-free solution likewise nifedipine, a selective L-type calcium channel VOCC blocker (Hockerman et al. 1997). This result suggested that X. stuhlmannii could inhibit the permeation of extracellular Ca2+ via blocking L-type VDCC. Dongmo et al. (2014), also obtained the same result with tetra-acetylajusgasterone, a component of Vitex cienkowskii.

Finally, of the 11 isolated compounds of the hydroalcoholic extract from X. stuhlmannii, four compounds were tested for their vasorelaxant properties. The results obtained demonstrated that only odoratin (4), induced concentration-dependent relaxation in aortic rings precontracted by PE. Odoratin is an isoflavone belonging to the flavonoid family (Khan et al. 2000), and many reports indicated that isoflavones provoke vascular relaxation by diverse signaling pathways (Henrique 2021), but this is the first time that odoratin vasorelaxant activity is elucidated. In addition, we suggest that the remarkable antihypertensive and vascular relaxant effects of X. stuhlmannii extract/fraction are probably due to its phytoconstituents in particular high flavonoids content were shown to induce vasorelaxant, antioxidant and hypotensive effects (Viktor et al. 2016). So, additional experiments are necessary for clarifying the molecular mechanisms by which these isolated compounds from X. stuhlmannii act on vascular smooth muscle cells leading to blood pressure lowering.

These results demonstrated that the hydroalcoholic stem bark extract, rather than the aqueous leaf extract of X. stuhlmannii, previously investigated by Kuinze et al. (2024) may has potential applications in the management of cardiovascular disorders, especially hypertension. Both extracts significantly reduced CdCl2-induced hypertension in rats at a comparable extent when administered at the same dose (100 mg/kg), and both improved structural alterations in the heart and aorta. However, the hydroalcoholic stem bark extract showed a more pronounced efficacy, likely due to its higher phenolic content, which may enhance its cardioprotective and antioxidant activities (Azimi et al. 2021).

Conclusions

The results showed the antihypertensive effect of the stem bark of the plant. This effect may be partly due to the interference of the compounds in the extract with the various mechanisms involved in regulating vasomotricity, such as the eNOS/cGMP pathway. In addition, the antioxidative compounds may also contribute to the antihypertensive and vasorelaxant activities of plant extracts.

Further investigations are underway using bio-guided fractionation to identify more compounds present in the extract that may be involved in the observed activities of the plant extract. In order to confirm the described mechanisms of action and to identify additional ones, further pharmacological tests will be carried out. X. stuhlmannii may be an interesting candidate for new or complementary strategies regarding the treatment of cardiovascular disorders, such as hypertension.
