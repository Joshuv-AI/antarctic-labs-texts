Effects of electron correlation on resonant Edelstein and inverse-Edelstein effects
Mojdeh Saleh, Abhishek Kumar, Dmitrii L. Maslov, Saurabh Maiti

Abstract
Spin-orbit coupling in systems with broken inversion symmetry gives rise to the Edelstein effect, which is the induced spin polarization in response to an applied electric field or current, and the inverse Edelstein effect, which is the induced electric current in response to an oscillatory magnetic field or spin polarization. At the same time, an interplay between spin-orbit coupling and electron-electron interaction leads to a special type of collective excitations–chiral-spin modes–which are oscillations of spin polarization in the absence of a magnetic field. As a result, both Edelstein and inverse Edelstein effects exhibit resonances at the frequencies of spin-chiral collective modes. Here, we present a detailed study of the effect of electron correlation on the Edelstein and inverse Edelstein effects in a single-valley two-dimensional electron gas and a multi-valley Dirac system with proximity-induced spin-orbit coupling. While the chiral-spin modes involve both in-plane and out-of-plane oscillations of spins, we show that only the in-plane modes are responsible for the above resonances. In the multi-valley system, electron correlation splits the in-plane modes into two. We also study the spectral weight distribution between the two resonances over a large parameter space of intra- and inter-valley interactions.

v_{\rm D}
being the Dresselhaus coupling constant. Correspondingly, the SO part of the velocity becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\bf v}_{\rm SOC}=v_{\rm D}(k_{ux}\hat{s}_{x}\hat{\bf x}-k_{uy}\hat{s}_{y}\hat{\bf y}).

(22)

One can now proceed along the same lines as for the Rashba case. For electric-field driving we find:

\displaystyle\bf J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\sigma_{0}}{\tau}\left[\frac{1}{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\Omega}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm D}}{4\mu}\right)^{2}D_{\rm JE}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)\right]{\bf E}_{0},

\displaystyle M_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{i\sigma^{\rm ME}_{0}}{\Omega\tau}D_{\rm ME}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)E_{x},\;M_{y}=-\dfrac{i\sigma^{\rm ME}_{0}}{\Omega\tau}D_{\rm ME}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)E_{y}.

(23)

where
D_{\rm JE}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)=D_{\rm ME}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)
is same as
R_{\rm JE}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)=R_{\rm ME}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)
in Eq. (
14
), but with
v_{\rm R}\rightarrow v_{\rm D}
, which results in
\lambda_{\rm R}\rightarrow\lambda_{\rm D}
. The same applies to the definition of
\sigma^{\rm ME}_{0}
in Eq. (
17
). For Zeeman-field driving, we get

\displaystyle J_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\rm ME}}{\tau}D_{\rm JB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)B_{x},\;J_{y}=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\rm ME}}{\tau}D_{\rm JB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)}_{\chi_{\rm JB}}B_{y},

\displaystyle\bf M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\chi_{0}}{2}D_{\rm MB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)}_{\chi_{\parallel}}{\bf B}_{0},\;M_{z}=\underbrace{-\chi_{0}D_{\rm MB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)}_{\chi_{\perp}}B_{z},

(24)

where
\chi_{0}
is same as in Eq. (
19
) and,
D_{\rm JB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)=D_{\rm MB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)
are also same as
R_{\rm JB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)=R_{\rm MB}^{\rm 2D}(\Omega)
[Eq. (
18
)], but, again, with
v_{\rm R}\rightarrow v_{\rm D}
.

In summary, the cross-response tensors for pure Rashba and pure Dresselhaus cases are:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm ME}_{ab}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{-i\sigma_{0}^{\rm ME}}{\Omega\tau}R^{\rm 2D}_{\rm ME}(\Omega)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},~\text{for Rashba SOC},\\
\frac{-i\sigma_{0}^{\rm ME}}{\Omega\tau}D^{\rm 2D}_{\rm ME}(\Omega)\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},~\text{for Dresselhaus SOC}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\chi^{\rm JB}_{ab}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\chi_{0}^{\rm JB}R^{\rm 2D}_{\rm JB}(\Omega)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},~\text{for Rashba SOC},\\
-\chi_{0}^{\rm JB}D^{\rm 2D}_{\rm JB}(\Omega)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},~\text{for Dresselhaus SOC}.\end{cases}

(25)

A characteristic difference in the tensor structure of the cross-response functions in the two cases allows for an unambiguous identification of the dominant type of SOC in a given material. This is to be contrasted with the tensor structure of direct response functions, i.e., conductivity and spin susceptibility, which are longitudinal for both Rashba and Dresselhaus cases. The difference in the tensor structure of cross-response functions could be exploited in transport measurements to extract different components of SOC.

IV
Multi-valley Dirac system: non-interacting limit

As a specific realization of the multi-valley system, we consider gated monolayer graphene with two valleys at time-reversal symmetric points of the Brillouin zone. Although intrinsic SOC in graphene is very weak, a strong SOC can be induced by placing graphene on a heavy-element substrate, e.g. TMD. It is known that SOC in this case is of two types: Rashba and valley-Zeeman (VZ), the latter acting as an out-of-plane Zeeman field of polarity alternating between the
K
and
K^{\prime}
valleys
[
25
]
. For energies much smaller than the chemical potential, one can project out the band that does not contain the chemical potential, which leads to the following
4\times 4
Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=v_{\rm{F}}k\hat{\tau}^{0}\hat{s}^{0},~~~\hat{H}_{\rm SOC}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2}\hat{\tau}^{0}({\bf k}_{u}\times\hat{\bf s})\cdot{\bf z}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm Z}}{2}\hat{\tau}^{3}\hat{s}^{3}.

(26)

Here, in addition to the
2\times 2
spin matrices
\hat{s}
, there are also
2\times 2

\hat{\tau}
matrices that act in the valley space, and
\lambda_{\rm R}
and
\lambda_{\rm Z}
are the Rashba and VZ spin-orbit couplings, respectively, with units of energy. Since a gated system has a finite
k_{\rm{F}}
, we can introduce a velocity
v_{\rm R}\equiv\lambda_{\rm R}/2k_{\rm{F}}
. When comparing the Hamiltonian (
26
) with Eq. (
7
), it is worth noting that the SOC term depends only on the direction of
{\bf k}
but not on its magnitude, which is the result of projecting out fully occupied bands in the Dirac system. Consequently, the structure of spin-orbit part of the velocity operator

\displaystyle\hat{\bf v}_{{\bf k}}=v_{\rm{F}}{\bf k}_{u}\hat{\tau}^{0}\hat{s}_{0},~~~\hat{\bf v}_{\rm SOC}=v_{\rm R}({\bf k}_{u}\cdot\hat{\bf s})({\bf k}_{u}\times\hat{z})\hat{\tau}^{0},

(27)

differs from that in Eq. (
8
). Next, the non-equilibrium part of the distribution function is modeled as
[
36
,
19
]

\displaystyle\delta\hat{n}=-\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{F})\left[u_{0}\hat{\tau}^{0}\hat{c}^{0}+u_{i}\hat{\tau}^{0}\hat{c}^{i}+W_{i}\hat{\tau}^{i}\hat{c}^{0}+M_{ij}\hat{\tau}^{i}\hat{c}^{j}\right],~~~~i,j\in\{1,2,3\},

(28)

where
u_{i}
’s describe the spin-chiral sector in the same way as for 2DEG [cf. Eq. (
9
)],
W_{i}
’ describe the valley sector, and
M_{ij}
describe coupled fluctuations in the spin-valley sector. We then perform the harmonic decomposition of these degrees of freedom as

u_{i}(\theta)=\sum_{m}u^{(m)}_{i}e^{im\theta},~W_{i}(\theta)=\sum_{m}W_{i}^{(m)}e^{im\theta},~M_{ij}(\theta)=\sum_{m}M_{ij}^{(m)}e^{im\theta}.

The electric current and magnetization are still obtained from Eqs. (
6a
) and (
6b
), respectively. For the current, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\nu^{T}_{F}\left[v_{\rm{F}}\frac{u_{0}^{(-1)}+u_{0}^{(1)}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2k_{\rm{F}}}\left\{\frac{u^{(-1)}_{2}-u^{(1)}_{2}}{2i}\right\}\right],

\displaystyle J_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\nu^{T}_{F}\left[v_{\rm{F}}\frac{u_{0}^{(-1)}-u_{0}^{(1)}}{2i}-\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2k_{\rm{F}}}\left\{\frac{u^{(-1)}_{2}+u^{(1)}_{2}}{2}\right\}\right],

(29)

where
\nu_{F}^{T}\equiv 2k_{\rm{F}}/\pi v_{\rm{F}}
is the total density of states at the Fermi level. The expressions for the components of magnetization are same as in the single-valley case [Eq. (
11
)] with
\nu_{F}^{T}
corresponding to the multi-valley case now.
Comparing Eq. (
29
) with Eq. (
10
), we see that while the Drude part of the current (
\propto v_{\rm{F}}
) remains the same, the SOC induced terms are modified due to a different form of the SOC part of the velocity operator [cf. Eqs. (
8
) and (
27
)].

The equations of motion for
E
-field driving are:

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ev_{\rm{F}}}{2}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})E_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})E_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}u_{2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\rm R}u_{1}^{(m)}+i\Omega u_{2}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm Z}M_{3,3}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ie\lambda_{\rm R}}{4k_{\rm{F}}}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})E_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})E_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{3}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm Z}M_{3,2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega M_{3,1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}M_{3,2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\rm R}M_{3,1}^{(m)}+i\Omega M_{3,2}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm Z}u_{3}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega M_{3,3}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm Z}u_{2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(30)

When compared to the single valley case, even in the absence of VZ SOC, we see that there are differences in how the degrees of freedom couple to the electric field, e.g., the
u^{(m)}_{3}
component does not couple to the electric field. This is a consequence of the modification of the velocity operator [Eq. (
27
)]. Despite this, as we will see soon, the current and magnetization will not be sensitive to this change. Further, with no VZ SOC, the spin-valley sector is also decoupled from the electric field. The presence of VZ SOC, however, couples the spin-chiral and spin-valley degrees of freedom. This suggests that in the presence of VZ SOC, one can use electric field to access fluctuations in the spin-valley sector.

For Zeeman-field driving, the equations of motion read

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}u_{2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{g\mu_{\rm{B}}\lambda_{\rm R}}{4}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})B_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})B_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\rm R}u_{1}^{(m)}+i\Omega u_{2}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm Z}M_{3,3}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{g\mu_{\rm{B}}\lambda_{\rm R}}{2}\delta_{m,0}B_{z},

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{3}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm Z}M_{3,2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega M_{3,1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}M_{3,2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\rm R}M_{3,1}^{(m)}+i\Omega M_{3,2}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm Z}u_{3}^{(m)}

\displaystyle-\frac{ig\mu_{\rm{B}}\lambda_{\rm Z}}{4}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})B_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})B_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega M_{3,3}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm Z}u_{2}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{g\mu_{\rm{B}}\lambda_{\rm Z}}{4}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})B_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})B_{y}\Big].

(31)

Without VZ SOC, the spin-chiral sector couples to the Zeeman field in the same way as in the single-valley case, while the spin-valley sector remains decoupled. However, VZ SOC again couples these degrees of freedom. Thus, VZ SOC plays an important role in allowing both the
E
and
B
fields to couple to the spin-valley fluctuations.

Solving for the
u^{(m)}_{i}
and
M^{(m)}_{ij}
components and substituting the results into the definitions of the electric current and magnetization, Eqs. (
6a
) and (
6b

\displaystyle{\bf J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{i\sigma_{0}}{\tau}\left[\frac{1}{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\Omega}\left(\frac{\Omega}{2\mu}\right)^{2}R^{\rm Di}_{\rm JE}(\Omega)\right]}_{\sigma(\Omega)}{\bf E}_{0},\,\,\,\,R_{\text{JE}}^{\rm Di}(\Omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}-\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\bf M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\dfrac{i\sigma^{\rm ME}_{0}}{\Omega\tau}\left(\frac{\Omega}{\lambda_{\rm R}}\right)^{2}R^{\rm Di}_{\rm ME}(\Omega)}_{\sigma^{\rm ME}(\Omega)}{\bf E}_{0}\times\hat{z},\;M_{z}=0,\;R^{\rm Di}_{\rm ME}(\Omega)=R^{\rm Di}_{\rm JE}(\Omega),

(32)

where superscript Di stands for “Dirac”,
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma^{\rm ME}_{0}
are the same as in Eqs. (
15
) and (
17
), with
\nu_{F}^{T}
re-defined appropriately.

For Zeeman-field driving, we obtain

\displaystyle\bf M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\chi_{0}}{2}R^{\parallel\rm Di}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)}_{\chi_{\parallel}(\Omega)}{\bf B}_{0},\,\,\,\,\,\,R^{\parallel\rm Di}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}+2\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}-\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}},

\displaystyle M_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\chi_{0}R^{\perp\rm Di}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)}_{\chi_{\perp}(\Omega)}B_{z},\,\,\,\,\,\,R^{\perp\rm Di}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}-\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}},

\displaystyle\bf J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\chi_{0}^{\rm JB}R^{\rm Di}_{\rm JB}~{\bf B}_{0}\times\hat{z},\,\,\,\,\,\,R^{\rm Di}_{\rm JB}(\Omega)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}+\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}-\lambda_{\rm Z}^{2}}.

(33)

where
\chi_{0}
and
\chi_{0}^{\rm JB}
are the same as in Eqs. (
19
) and (

), respectively. Thus, the cross-responses persist in multi-valley systems.
In fact, comparing the results in Eqs. (

33

14

(which is expected as that is the new spin splitting in the system). This means that although the resonance factor [
R_{AA^{\prime}}(\Omega)
] in the cross-response survives when
\lambda_{\rm R}=0
but
\lambda_{\rm Z}

V
Dynamic response in the presence of electron-electron interaction

Electron-electron interaction (
eei
) affects the dynamic response of a system with SOC in a number of ways. The most important one is that it endows electron spins with rigidity with respect to finite-
q
perturbations. As a result, the various resonances both in the direct and cross sectors become dispersive modes of a FL with SOC
[
21
,
37
,
38
]
. At
q=0
(which is the subject of this paper),
eei
renormalizes the resonance frequencies. For weak SOC, such renormalization can be expressed in terms of the Landau parameters of the underlying Fermi liquid in the absence of SOC
[
17
,
32
,
19
]
. In addition,
eei
leads to damping of the resonances even in the absence of disorder
[
39
]
. Finally,
eei
is responsible for splitting of both ESR and EDSR peaks in a two-valley Dirac system
[
19
]
.

While the effects of
eei
on direct responses (ESR and EDSR) have been studied extensively in the past (see review
[
26
]
and references therein), here we extend the analysis to cross-responses. To do so, we come back to Eqs. (
1
–
3
) and restore the Landau interaction functional,
\hat{\epsilon}_{\rm LF}
. Since the Landau functionals are different for a single- and multi-valley cases, we will treat the two separately, in Secs.
V.1
and
V.2
, respectively. Furthermore,
eei
also renormalizes the parameters of a free Hamiltonian, i.e.,
v_{F}\to v_{F}^{*}
,
v_{\rm SOC}\to v_{\rm SOC}^{*}
and
g\to g^{*}
. However, as stated earlier, we shall suppress the asterisks for brevity with the understanding that all parameters in what follows are already renormalized.

V.1
Single-valley system

We start with the standard form of the Landau function for a single-valley system in the absence of both SOC and Zeeman field
[
27
]

\displaystyle\nu_{F}^{T}F_{\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2};\varsigma_{3},\varsigma_{4}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=F^{S}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\delta_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\delta_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}}+F^{A}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\cdot\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}},

(34)

where
F^{S/A}
are the symmetric and antisymmetric parts describing the interaction in the charge and spin sectors, respectively, and
\vec{A}
denotes a vector
A
with three Cartesian components. The angles
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
are the azimuthal angles of momenta
{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
3
) with magnitude
k_{\rm F}
;
in a rotationally-invariant system, the Landau function depends only on
\theta-\theta^{\prime}
. The components of the Landau function can be decomposed into angular harmonics according to
X(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{m}X_{m}e^{im(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}
, where
X\in\{F^{S},F^{A}\}
and
m
is the
z
-component of the angular momentum. It will also be convenient to introduce the following combinations of the Landau parameters:

\displaystyle f^{S,(m)}=1+F^{S}_{m},~~~f^{S,(m)}_{+}=1+\frac{F^{S}_{m-1}+F^{S}_{m+1}}{2},~~~f^{S,(m)}_{-}=\frac{F^{S}_{m-1}-F^{S}_{m+1}}{2};

\displaystyle f^{(m)}=1+F^{A}_{m},~~~f^{(m)}_{+}=1+\frac{F^{A}_{m-1}+F^{A}_{m+1}}{2},~~~f^{(m)}_{-}=\frac{F^{A}_{m-1}-F^{A}_{m+1}}{2}.

(35)

In the presence of
eei
, one needs to use the full form of the electric current with the Landau functional taken into account, as given by Eq. (
6a
). The (temporal Fourier transform of) current density, modified according to Eq. (
6a
), is then evaluated as

\displaystyle J_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\nu^{T}_{F}\left[v_{\rm{F}}f^{S,(1)}\frac{u^{(-1)}_{0}+u^{(1)}_{0}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2k_{\rm{F}}}f^{(0)}\left(\frac{u^{(-1)}_{2}-u^{(1)}_{2}}{2i}-\frac{u^{(-1)}_{3}+u^{(1)}_{3}}{2}\right)\right],

\displaystyle J_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\nu^{T}_{F}\left[v_{\rm{F}}f^{S,(1)}\frac{u^{(-1)}_{0}-u^{(1)}_{0}}{2i}-\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2k_{\rm{F}}}f^{(0)}\left(\frac{u^{(-1)}_{2}+u^{(1)}_{2}}{2}+\frac{u^{(-1)}_{3}-u^{(1)}_{3}}{2i}\right)\right].

(36)

The definition of magnetization in Eq. (
6b
) does not involve the Landau function, hence its explicit form remains the same as in the non-interacting case, Eq. (
11
) (modulo “silent” renormalization of the Hamiltonian parameters).

Next, we follow the same steps as for the non-interacting limit to derive the equations of motion in the presence of
eei
. For electric-field driving those read

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ev_{\rm{F}}}{2}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})E_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})E_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}u_{2}^{(m)}f^{(m)}_{+}+i\lambda_{\rm R}u_{3}^{(m)}f^{(m)}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{2}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm R}u_{1}^{(m)}f^{(m)}

\displaystyle ie\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{4k_{\rm{F}}}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})E_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})E_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{3}^{(m)}

\displaystyle e\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{4k_{\rm{F}}}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})E_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})E_{y}\Big],

(37)

while for Zeeman-field driving we obtain

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{0}^{(m)}

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{1}^{(m)}+\lambda_{\rm R}u_{2}^{(m)}f^{(m)}_{+}+i\lambda_{\rm R}u_{3}^{(m)}f^{(m)}_{-}

\displaystyle\dfrac{g\mu_{\rm{B}}\lambda_{\rm R}}{4}\Big[(\delta_{m,1}+\delta_{m,-1})B_{x}-i(\delta_{m,1}-\delta_{m,-1})B_{y}\Big],

\displaystyle i\Omega u_{2}^{(m)}-\lambda_{\rm R}u_{1}^{(m)}f^{(m)}

Compared to the non-interacting case,

\displaystyle\bf J

and for Zeeman-field driving

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\chi_{0}^{\rm JB}R^{\rm 2D}_{\rm JB}(\Omega)}_{\chi^{\rm JB}(\Omega)}{\bf B}_{0}\times\hat{z},~~R^{\rm 2D}_{\rm JB}(\Omega)=\dfrac{f^{(0)}f^{(1)}\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-f_{+}^{(1)}f^{(1)}\lambda^{2}_{R}},

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\chi_{0}}{2}R^{\parallel 2D}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)}_{\chi^{\parallel}(\Omega)}\mathbf{B},~~R^{\parallel 2D}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)=\dfrac{f^{(1)}\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-f_{+}^{(1)}f^{(1)}\lambda^{2}_{R}},

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\chi_{0}R^{\perp 2D}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)}_{\chi^{\perp}(\Omega)}B_{z},~~R^{\perp 2D}_{\rm MB}(\Omega)=\dfrac{f_{+}^{(0)}\lambda_{\rm R}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}-f^{(0)}f_{+}^{(0)}\lambda^{2}_{R}}.

, but differently so for the in- and out-of-plane components of

V.2
Two-valley system

The Landau function for a two-valley system can be written as
[
36
,
19
]

\displaystyle\nu_{F}^{T}F_{(\upsilon_{1}\upsilon_{2};\upsilon_{3}\upsilon_{4});(\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{2};\varsigma_{3}\varsigma_{4})}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{S}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\delta_{\upsilon_{1}\upsilon_{3}}\delta_{\upsilon_{2}\upsilon_{4}}\delta_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\delta_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}}

(41)

\displaystyle+F^{A}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\delta_{\upsilon_{1}\upsilon_{3}}\delta_{\upsilon_{2}\upsilon_{4}}(\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\cdot\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}})+G^{A}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})(\vec{\tau}_{\upsilon_{1}\upsilon_{3}}\cdot\vec{\tau}_{\upsilon_{2}\upsilon_{4}})(\delta_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\delta_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}})

\displaystyle+H(\theta,\theta^{\prime})(\vec{\tau}_{\upsilon_{1}\upsilon_{3}}\cdot\vec{\tau}_{\upsilon_{2}\upsilon_{4}})(\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{1}\varsigma_{3}}\cdot\vec{s}_{\varsigma_{2}\varsigma_{4}}).

In addition to the usual charge (
F^{S}

G^{A}
and
H

eei
is much larger than the lattice spacing, which we assume to be the case here.

As for the single-valley case, the components of the Landau function can be decomposed into angular harmonics according to
X(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{m}X_{m}e^{im(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}
, where
X\in\{F^{S},F^{A},G^{A},H\}
and

\displaystyle J_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\nu^{T}_{F}\left[v_{\rm{F}}f^{S,(1)}\frac{u_{0}^{(-1)}+u_{0}^{(1)}}{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm R}}{2k_{\rm{F}}}\left(f^{(1)}_{+}\frac{u^{(-1)}_{2}-u^{(1)}_{2}}{2i}-f^{(1)}_{-}\frac{u^{(-1)}_{3}+u^{(1)}_{3}}{2}\right)\right],

VII
Conclusions

In this article, we analyzed the effect of electron-electron interactions on resonances in
the electric current and magnetization induced by oscillatory electric and magnetic fields in single and multi-valley systems. As an example of the former, we considered a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) with Rashba or Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling (SOC), while as an example of the latter, we considered a monolayer graphene with proximity-induced Rashba and valley-Zeeman SOC. In both cases, a combination of SOC and electron-electron interaction gives rise to chiral-spin modes (CSMs), which are collective excitations of a Fermi liquid with SOC. In addition to direct responses, i.e., optical conductivity
\sigma(\Omega)
and dynamical spin susceptibility
\chi(\Omega)
we have shown that
CSMs also lead to a resonant behavior in the cross-response functions, i.e. magnetoelectric (Edelstein) conductivity
\sigma^{\rm ME}(\Omega)
, which relates the magnetization to an oscillatory electric field, and the current-Zeeman susceptibility
\chi^{\rm JB}(\Omega)
, which relates the electric current to an oscillatory Zeeman field. The last two quantities describe the resonant versions of the Edelstein effect
[
11
]
and its inverse
[
12
]
. The resonances in cross-responses are induced only by those types of SOCs that couple the vector potential to the electron spin, such as in the Rashba and Dresselhaus types of SOC. Finally, it is shown that out of the three possible CSMs (two with in-plane polarization and one with out-of-plane polarization), it is the in-plane modes that contribute to the cross-responses.

In a single valley system, the effect of electron correlations is to renormalize the resonance frequency. In multi-valley system, correlations also split a single CSM into two, corresponding to coupled excitations in the spin-chiral and spin-valley sectors. While there is a resonant enhancement of the cross-response at both these frequencies, the spectral weight associated with these modes are shown to vary significantly. The higher energy mode is shown to carry a lower spectral weight for a wide range of parameters corresponding to intra- and inter-valley interactions. We add here that although the specific form of the SOC perturbation will depend on the particular system (like the Rashba SOC form for the multi valley system is specific to the Dirac Hamiltonian), the fact that there will be CSMs and that they will be split in the presence of inter-valley interaction remains a generic feature of multi-valley systems, and all of our conclusions should apply.

To conclude, one can state that a general response of spin-orbit coupled metal to oscillatory
E
and
B
fields can be written as:

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)}_{\text{Drude+EDSR+Hall}}E_{\beta}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\chi^{\rm JB}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)}_{\text{Resonant inverse Edelstein}}B_{\beta},

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sigma^{\rm ME}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)}_{\text{Resonant Edelstein}}E_{\beta}

\displaystyle+~~~~~~~~~~\underbrace{\chi_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)}_{\text{CSR}}B_{\beta}.

(56)

The underbraces below the response functions denote the effects hosted by them. The tensor structure of the cross-response functions depends on the type of SOC [
vide
Eq. (
25
)] and this fact may be used to identify various types of SOC present in a material. With respect to the polarization of the oscillatory fields, we showed that the in-plane
E
and
B
fields induce both direct and cross-responses, while the out-of-plane
B
-field induces only the direct response. These calculations offer a way to track the evolution of the resonances identified in Ref.
[
23
]
and their related effects in different materials.
