A Non-Wellfounded and Labelled Sequent Calculus for Bimodal Provability Logic
Justus Becker

Abstract
We present a labelled and non-wellfounded calculus for the bimodal provability logic CS . The system is obtained by modelling the Kripke-like semantics of this logic. As in [ 8 ] , we enforce the second-order property of converse wellfoundedness by using techniques from cyclic proof theory. We will prove soundness and completeness of this system with respect to the semantics and provide a primitive decision procedure together with a way to extract countermodels.



1
Introduction

Modal provability logics originate from interpreting the
\Box
-modality as “it is provable that” in a sufficiently strong (usually arithmetic) theory.
In
[
29
]
, Robert Solovay proved completeness of the modal logic
GL
with respect to the provability of Peano Arithmetic (
\mathsf{PA}
) as well as the completeness of the modal logic
S
(also referred to as
\mathsf{GLS}
) with respect to the true provability of
\mathsf{PA}
.
Since then, the concept of provability logic has been extended towards more and more complex logics: for instance, Japaridze’s polymodal provability logic
\mathsf{GLP}

[
16
]
or logics of non-standard provability
[
10
,
13
,
24
,
31
]
.
Bimodal provability logics extend unimodal logics, such as
GL
, by also incorporating the provability of a second theory.

A useful technique in the proof theory of modal logic is the usage of
labelled sequents
, which are syntactically enriched sequents utilising the Kripke frame correspondence of modal logics (see
[
19
,
26
,
30
]
).
Here, we use a two-sorted approach to labels by using two kinds of relational atoms (see
[
12
]
for a multi-labelled system), which allows us to capture two modalities simultaneously. As the labelled structure internalises part of the semantics, we can sometimes read out countermodels from the leaves of failed proof search, which is a common feature of labelled calculi.

Cyclic and more generally
non-wellfounded proofs
were originally developed for the modal
\mu
-calculus
[
20
]
and later also adapted for first-order logic handling induction
[
5
,
6
]
. In the recent decade non-wellfounded proof theory has been also adapted for Arithmetic
[
27
]
and other logics, such as propositional dynamic logic
[
9
]
.

In
[
23
]
, Daniyar Shamkanov provides the first cyclic and non-wellfounded proof systems for
GL
. Since then, a lot of variants of cyclic systems for similar logics, such as
\mathsf{iGL}

[
14
,
25
]
and
\mathsf{Grz}

[
22
]
, have been developed. For bimodal provability logic, however, no sequent system has been introduced thus far.

We will combine these notions of non-wellfounded proofs together with labelled sequents, similarly to
[
8
]
, to obtain a proof system for the basic bimodal provability logic Carlson-Smoryński (
CS
), named after Tim Carlson and Craig Smoryński due to their foundational work in the area
[
7
,
28
]
.
Like in
[
8
]
, we utilise the progress condition of our proofs to implement the second-order condition for models of the logic (i.e. converse wellfoundedness).

In the following section, we introduce the syntax and Kripke-like semantics for
CS
. In section
3
, we introduce the rules of a non-wellfounded and labelled proof system for this logic, and prove semantic soundness and completeness. The latter is obtained by a countermodel construction, which we get by a primitive proof search strategy, giving us a decision procedure for
CS
.

3
A Bi-Labelled Sequent Calculus

In this section, we present our bi-labelled non-wellfounded calculus
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
, and show that it is sound and complete with respect to the logic
CS
.

3.1
The Calculus
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}

The calculus we present is based on the relational semantics introduced in the previous section, using labels. Labelled systems can be traced back to Kanger
[
17

, who used them to introduce the first cut-free system for the modal logic
\mathsf{S5}

[
11

19
,
26
,
30

The second proof theoretic tool we use are

\mu

[

and later also adapted for other logics, such as propositional dynamic logic
[

]

While wellfounded proofs are often seen as top-down inductively definable trees, non-wellfounded proofs are defined as coinductive trees, allowing for infinitary branches.
Our system is inspired by the labelled, non-wellfounded systems for

One way to read the progress condition is to consider countermodel extractions. A desired feature of a calculus is the ability to read out countermodels from a failed proof. This is made even easier by incorporating labels. A countermodels can usually be read out from a single branch that is failed. This is either the case by that branch getting stuck (i.e. no rules can be applied any more) or by that branch continuing indefinitely. Considering example

In non-wellfounded proof theory, there is usually a special focus on

In this work, regular proofs do not play a role as we do not consider effectiveness. Instead, we focus on logical and proof-theoretic aspects, such as showing soundness and completeness.

3.2
Soundness

We will prove both soundness and completeness with respect to Carlson semantics. To do so, we generalise the semantic truth of formulas to sequents.

Definition 3

(Sequent interpretation)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
be a sequent and
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\prec,M_{0},M_{1},V\rangle
a Carlson model.
Let
I:Lab(\mathfrak{S})\to W
be a function from labels of the sequent to points in the model.
We call
I
a
sequent interpretation
of
\mathfrak{S}
on
\mathcal{M}
if the following condition is fulfilled: If
xRy\in\mathcal{R}
then
I(x)\prec I(y)
and
I(y)\in M_{0}
, and if
xSy\in\mathcal{R}
then
I(x)\prec I(y)
and
I(y)\in M_{1}
.

Intuitively, a sequent interpretation maps every label to a point in a Carlson model such that the meaning of the relational atoms match their interpretation in the Carlson model.
From here on, we refer to sequent interpretations as interpretations, which we use to determine whether a sequent holds in a model.

Definition 4

(Semantic Truth for Sequents)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\prec,M_{0},M_{1},V\rangle
be a model. We say that a sequent
\mathfrak{S}=\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
is true on
\mathcal{M}
(written
\mathcal{M}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}
) if the following holds for all interpretations
I
of
\mathfrak{S}
on
\mathcal{M}
:
If
\mathcal{M},I(x)\Vdash A
for all
x:A\in\Gamma
, then there is some
y:B\in\Omega
with
\mathcal{M},I(y)\Vdash B
.

Similarly to the validity of formulas, we write
\textnormal{{CS}}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}
if the sequent
\mathfrak{S}
is valid on all Carlson models, and
\textnormal{{CS}}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
if it is not.
We now proceed to prove soundness of
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
using the following crucial local soundness lemma. This lemma essentially tells us that truth is always preserved in a rule application, both upwards and downwards. Note that downwards preservation of truth always holds for sound sequent systems.

Lemma 5

(Local Soundness)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Carlson model and let

\frac{\mathfrak{S}_{1}...\mathfrak{S}_{n}}{\mathfrak{S}}

\mathcal{M}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}

\mathcal{M}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}_{i}

I
is also an interpretation of
\mathcal{R},x\circ y,y\bullet z,x\bullet z,\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
, and it therefore witnesses that
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathcal{R},x\circ y,y\bullet z,x\bullet z,\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
.

The other direction follows immediately just like in the previous case.
∎

The following observation follows directly from the proof and will be helpful for our soundness argument.

Corollary 6

Given an instance of a rule of
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
, with conclusion
\mathfrak{S}
and premisses
\mathfrak{S}_{1},...,\mathfrak{S}_{n}
: if an interpretation
I
witnesses that
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
, then there is ab interpretation
I^{\prime}
witnessing
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{i}
which is either equal to
I
or extends
I
.

Notice that the proof does not use the fact that our models are conversely wellfounded. This will be used to prove the next theorem when considering the infinite branches of a proof, which also have not played a role yet.

Theorem 3.7

(Soundness of
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
)

For any sequent
\mathfrak{S}
: If
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\vdash\mathfrak{S}
then
\textnormal{{CS}}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}
.

Proof.

Assume
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\vdash\mathfrak{S}
and suppose for a contradiction that
\textnormal{{CS}}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
.
Then, there is some Carlson model
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\prec,M_{0},M_{1},V\rangle
that falsifies
\mathfrak{S}
, hence
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
.
As
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\vdash\mathfrak{S}
, it must be witnessed by some proof
\mathcal{T}
, where every branch of
\mathcal{T}
is either finite or progressing. We now extract an infinite branch
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
from
\mathcal{T}
which will not be progressing, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{T}
is a proof. We define the branch inductively by
\mathfrak{S}_{0}:=\mathfrak{S}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{i+1}
is a premiss of
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
in
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{i+1}
. By
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
and local soundness (lemma
5
) this branch is well defined.

Note that
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
cannot be finite as it would have to end with an initial sequent (either
\mathcal{R},\Gamma,x:p\Rightarrow x:p,\Omega
or
\mathcal{R},\Gamma,x:\bot\Rightarrow\Omega
) as
\mathcal{T}
is a proof and an initial sequent cannot be falsified by a model. Thus,
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
is an infinite branch, which must therefore be progressing (again, because
\mathcal{T}
is assumed to be valid).

Call
I_{i}
the sequent interpretation of
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
which is guaranteed by
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{i}
. Note that
I_{i+1}
can be obtained by possibly just extending
I_{i}
(corollary
6
), which allows us to define the limit interpretation
I
for all
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
over
\mathcal{M}
.

We show that
\prec
cannot be conversely wellfounded.
Consider some label
x_{n}
occurring in the progressing trace
(x_{i})_{i<\omega}
of
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
together with its corresponding point in the model
I_{n}(x_{n})
. By the trace being progressive we can find some
m>n
s.t.
x_{n}\circ x_{m}
(for
\circ\in\{R,S\}
), thus
I_{n}(x_{n})\prec I_{m}(x_{m})
.
Further, this shows that for any label
x
occurring in the trace, there is some distinct label
x^{\prime}
also occurring in the trace such that
I(x)\prec I(x^{\prime})
as all
I_{i}
are contained in
I
.

We can then find a chain of points
I(x)\prec I(x^{\prime})\prec I(x^{\prime\prime})\prec...
with
x=x_{1}
. Either this chain will have infinitely many distinct elements or it must eventually repeat an element. If it has infinitely many elements, that means
\prec
has an infinite non-cyclic path. If elements do repeat, there must be some loop in
\prec
.
In both cases,
\prec
is not conversely wellfounded, meaning that
\mathcal{M}
is not a Carlson model, a contradiction.
∎

3.3
Completeness

We continue with the proof of completeness. There are generally two ways to establish completeness for a sequent-style calculus. One can either show that the system is complete wrt. the Hilbert axiomatisation by introducing the cut rule – which is then shown to be admissible – or wrt. semantics, by considering failed proofs and showing that one can read out a countermodel from them.
We will do the latter here.
As we have a fully invertible calculus, we are able to consider a technique which is relatively simple and has been inspired by an unpublished (personally communicated) completeness proof for the labelled and non-wellfounded system for classical
GL
from
[
8
]
.

Let us define a proof search procedure which we define in two phases. Dividing the procedure like this makes it easy to show that the strategy is well defined. It also allows us to adapt the strategy to other logics, for example if we have a relational rule that can fire infinitely often such as directedness for the modal logic
\mathsf{KD}
. We divide the phases by considering firstly rules that do not introduce new labels, which we will call
saturation
; secondly, we consider all rules that introduce new labels in a single phase, the
label phase
.
We will show that the whole proof search is well defined by showing that each phase terminates.
The important thing here is that we do not rely on blind proof search as we want to ensure that every rule that we can apply (without creating duplicates) has to be applied eventually. This will simplify our countermodel construction as we can read out the valuation and the relational structure directly from the propositional and relational atoms.

Definition 8

(Saturation)
.

Let
\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
be a sequent that contains a labelled formula
x:A
. We call
x:A

saturated
in
\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
if the following conditions hold based on the form of
A
:

-

A
is a propositional atom
p\in\mathtt{Prop}
or
A=\bot
;

-

A=A_{1}\to A_{2}
and
x:A\in\Gamma
, then either
x:A_{1}\in\Omega
or
x:A_{2}\in\Gamma
;

-

A=A_{1}\to A_{2}
and
x:A\in\Omega
, then
x:A_{1}\in\Gamma
and
x:A_{2}\in\Omega
;

-

A=\Box A_{1}
and
x:A\in\Gamma
, then for all
y
with
xRy\in\mathcal{R}
,
y:A_{1}\in\Gamma
;

-

A=\triangle A_{1}
and
x:A\in\Gamma
, then for all
y
with
xSy\in\mathcal{R}
,
y:A_{1}\in\Gamma
.

A sequent is saturated, if all its formulas are saturated and
\mathcal{R}
is closed under
\textnormal{trans}_{\circ\bullet}
.

We call a tree of sequents (finite or infinite) formed by rules of
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
(possibly containing some leaves that are not closed by axioms) a
derivation
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a derivation with some open leaves. The
saturation phase
over
\mathcal{T}
is then defined by applying the rules
\to
L,
\to
R,
\Box
L,
\triangle
L, and
\textnormal{trans}_{\circ\bullet}
as long as possible to the open leaves which are not saturated.
It is not important in which order we apply these rules, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 9

Any saturation phase yields a finite tree whose open leaves are saturated.

Proof.

By construction, the open leaves of a saturation phase will be saturated. Let
\mathfrak{S}
be some open leaf at the start of the saturation phase.
We show that the saturation phase terminates by showing that only finitely many formulas can be added during saturation.
Observe that
\mathfrak{S}
only contains finitely many formulas and every new formula that gets added to
\mathfrak{S}
must be a subformula of another formula in
\mathfrak{S}
. Therefore, for every label
x
occurring in
\mathfrak{S}
there can only be finitely many new formulas
x:A
that can be added. As the amount of labels is finite and does not increase, the total amount of formulas that are added in the saturation phase is finite.
∎

Next, we define the second phase, the
label phase
, of our proof search as simply applying the rules
\Box
R and
\triangle
R continually as long as possible.
We call a sequent to which no such rules can be applied
label saturated
.

Lemma 10

Any label phase yields a finite tree whose leaves are label saturated.

Proof.

The lemma follows directly from the observation that sequents contain only finitely many formulas and any formula can only have a finite amount of nested
\Box
and
\triangle
.
∎

Both lemmas show us that the following
proof search strategy
is well defined:
Alternatingly execute saturation phases and label phases while applying Id and
\bot
eagerly (i.e. as soon as possible).

We will use this proof search strategy to obtain a countermodel from an unprovable sequent.
A sequent which is saturated and label saturated and to which neither Id nor
\bot
can be upwards applied is called
fully saturated
. Note that any open leaves which our proof search strategy creates must be fully saturated. Let us show in the following lemma how to extract a countermodel from such a sequent.

Lemma 11

If
\mathfrak{S}
is a fully saturated sequent, then there is a countermodel
{\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}}
.

Proof.

Let us first consider a fully saturated sequent
\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega
.
Define a model
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\prec,M_{0},M_{1},V\rangle
as follows:

W:=Lab(\mathcal{R},\Gamma\Rightarrow\Omega)
;

M_{0}:=\{x\in W\;|\;\text{there is some }y\text{ such that }yRx\in\mathcal{R}\}
;

M_{1}:=\{x\in W\;|\;\text{there is some }y\text{ such that }ySx\in\mathcal{R}\}
;

{\prec}:=\{(x,y)\in W\times W\;|\;xRy\in\mathcal{R}\text{ or }xSy\in\mathcal{R}\}
;

V(p):=\{x\in W\;|\;x:p\in\Gamma\}
for any
p\in\mathtt{Prop}
.

First, observe that
\langle W,\prec,V\rangle
is a
GL
-model, as
\mathcal{R}
constitutes a finite and transitive tree by closure under
\textnormal{trans}_{\circ\bullet}
, hence
\mathcal{M}
is also a Carlson model.

Claim:
\mathcal{M}
satisfies all formulas from
\Gamma
and falsifies all formulas from
\Omega
under the identity interpretation
I(x)=x
.
Consider any
x:A
occurring in the sequent. We prove the claim by simultaneous induction on the complexity of
A
.

A\in\mathtt{Prop}
: If
x:A\in\Gamma
, the claim follows by definition. If
x:A\in\Omega
, it follows from the fact that the sequent is saturated, so not an identity sequent.

A=\bot
: We know that
x:A\in\Gamma
cannot happen because of full saturation, whereas if
x:A\in\Omega
the formula is falsified by definition.

A=A_{1}\to A_{2}
: If
x:A\in\Gamma
, then by full saturation either
x:A_{1}\in\Omega
or
x:A_{2}\in\Gamma
. So, by induction hypothesis
\mathcal{M},x\nVdash A_{1}
or
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash A_{2}
, thus in both cases
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash A_{1}\to A_{2}
. If
x:A\in\Omega
, then
A_{1}\in\Gamma
and
A_{2}\in\Omega
. By induction hypothesis
\mathcal{M},y\Vdash A_{1}
and
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash A_{2}
, so
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash A_{1}\to A_{2}
.

A=\Box A_{1}
: If
x:A\in\Gamma
, then
z:A_{1}\in\Gamma
for any
z
with
xRz\in\mathcal{R}
.
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash\Box A_{1}
holds vacuously if no such
z
exists. By induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{M},z\Vdash A_{1}
and by the definition of
\prec
and the set
M_{0}
:
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash\Box A_{1}
. The case for
x:A\in\Omega
cannot occur due to label saturation.

The case of
A=\triangle A_{1}
works similarly to the previous one.
∎

Now, we apply our proof search to show how to create a countermodel from a failed proof that is obtained by the previously introduced strategy.

Theorem 3.12

(Completeness of
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
)

For any sequent
\mathfrak{S}
: If
\textnormal{{CS}}\Vdash\mathfrak{S}
then
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}

\vdash\mathfrak{S}
. Furthermore, if a sequent is not provable in
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}
, there is a countermodel
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive.
Assume
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\nvdash\mathfrak{S}
. Perform the proof search described above on
\mathfrak{S}
to obtain a derivation
\mathcal{T}
. Consider some branch
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
in
\mathcal{T}
such that it either ends with a fully saturated sequent, or it is infinite but not progressing. Such a branch must exist by
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\nvdash\mathfrak{S}
as we otherwise would have shown the opposite.
Write
\mathfrak{S}_{i}=\mathcal{R}_{i},\Gamma_{i}\Rightarrow\Omega_{i}
and define a sequence of models
(\mathcal{M}_{i})_{i<\omega}
with
\mathcal{M}_{i}=\langle W_{i},M_{0\,i},M_{1\,i},\prec_{i},V_{i}\rangle
as follows.

W_{i}:=Lab(\mathfrak{S}_{i})

M_{0\,i}:=\{x\in W_{i}\;|\;\text{there is some }y\text{ such that }yRx\in\mathcal{R}_{i}\}

M_{1\,i}:=\{x\in W_{i}\;|\;\text{there is some }y\text{ such that }ySx\in\mathcal{R}_{i}\}

{\prec_{i}}:=\{(x,y)\in W_{i}\times W_{i}\;|\;xRy\in\mathcal{R}_{i}\text{ or }xSy\in\mathcal{R}_{i}\}

V_{i}(p):=\{x\in W_{i}\;|\;x:p\in\Gamma\}

If
(\mathfrak{S}_{i})_{i<\omega}
is finite and ends with a fully saturated sequent
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, by lemma
11

\mathcal{M}_{n}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then, by lemma
5
also
\mathcal{M}_{n}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
.

If
(\mathfrak{S}_{n})_{n<\omega}
is infinite, then any potential trace in
(\mathfrak{S}_{n})_{n<\omega}
must be eventually constant. Consider the limit model
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\prec,M_{0},M_{1},V\rangle
with
W:=\bigcup_{i<\omega}W_{i}
,
{\prec}:=\bigcup_{i<\omega}\prec_{i}
,
M_{0}:=\bigcup_{i<\omega}M_{0\,i}
,
M_{1}:=\bigcup_{i<\omega}M_{1\,i}
, and
V(p):=\bigcup_{i<\omega}V_{i}(p)
.
By every trace of
(\mathfrak{S}_{n})_{n<\omega}
being eventually constant,
\prec
must be conversely wellfounded, otherwise an infinite
\prec
-chain would allow to obtain a progressing trace. (We do not consider the case for
\prec
having a cycle as it must form a tree if the root of the derivation is cycle free.) Therefore,
\mathcal{M}
must be a Carlson model. We now show that for any
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
in the branch we have
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{i}
. We do this similarly to the proof of lemma
11
by induction over the complexity of formulas occurring in
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
, for all
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
simultaneously.

Consider any sequent
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in the branch and a formula
A
such that
x:A
occurs in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. It then suffices to show that
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash A
if
x:A\in\Gamma_{n}
and
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash B
if
x:A\in\Omega_{n}
.

A\in\mathtt{Prop}
: If
x:A\in\Gamma
, the claim follows by definition. If
x:A\in\Omega
, it follows from the fact that the branch did not close during the proof strategy, thus Id cannot be applied.

A=\bot
: We know that
x:A\in\Gamma
cannot happen because then the rule
\bot
could have been applied, making the branch finite in the proof search strategy. If
x:A\in\Omega
, the formula is falsified by definition.

A=A_{1}\to A_{2}
: By the strategy
A
must be eventually principal.
If
{x:A\in\Gamma}
, there is a sequent
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with
{n<m}
such that either
{x:A_{1}\in\Omega_{m}}
or
{x:A_{2}\in\Gamma_{m}}
.
By induction hypothesis
\mathcal{M},x\nVdash A_{1}
or
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash A_{2}
, thus in both cases
{\mathcal{M},x\Vdash A_{1}\to A_{2}}
.
If
x:A\in\Omega
, there is a sequent
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with
n<m
such that
A_{1}\in\Gamma_{m}
and
x:A_{2}\in\Omega_{m}
. By induction hypothesis
\mathcal{M},y\Vdash A_{1}
and
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash A_{2}
, so
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash A_{1}\to A_{2}
.

A=\Box A_{1}
: If
x:A\in\Gamma
, there must be some
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with
n<m
such that
z:A_{1}\in\Gamma_{m}
for any
z
with
xRz\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
.
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash\Box A_{1}
holds vacuously if no such
z
exists. By induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{M},z\Vdash A_{1}
and by the definition of
\prec
and of the set
M_{0}
:
\mathcal{M},x\Vdash\Box A_{1}
.
If
x:A\in\Omega
, there is some
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
with
n<m
such that
z:A_{1}\in\Omega_{m}
and
xRz\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
. By induction hypothesis
\mathcal{M},z\nVdash A_{1}
,
y\prec z
, and
z\in M_{0}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{M},y\nVdash\Box A_{1}
.

The case of
A=\triangle A_{1}
works similarly to the previous one.

As
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}_{i}
for all
i<\omega
, we gain as a corollary
\mathcal{M}\nVdash\mathfrak{S}
.
∎

This proof also yields directly a decision procedure for the logic
CS
as we can apply the strategy to a given sequent
\Rightarrow x:A
and either obtain a valid proof or extract a countermodel by the procedure described in the proof above.

We remark that we can make the infinite countermodel
\mathcal{M}
, which we obtained from a non-progressing branch, into a finite model:
observe that due to the model being infinite but conversely wellfounded we must have some points with infinitely many
\prec
-children; however, we consider only finitely many subformulas. Thus, if we quotient these
\prec
-children into equivalent points (meaning they satisfy the same formulas and are in the same subset of either
M_{0}
or
M_{1}
), we can always obtain a finite set of
\prec
-children for every point without changing the valid formulas of the model.

Corollary 13

For any formula
A\in\mathcal{L}^{\Box\triangle}
:
\textnormal{{CS}}\vdash A
iff
{\textnormal{labCS}^{\infty}}\vdash A
.

Proof.

Apply theorems
3.7
and
3.12
for the sequent
\Rightarrow x:A
and apply theorem
2.3
.
∎

4
Conclusion and Future Works

We have introduced a non-wellfounded calculus for the bimodal provability logic
CS
by a two-sorted labelled approach. We have shown soundness and completeness for this system, the latter of which relies on a primitive decision procedure and a countermodel construction.

For the future, we want to investigate modal extensions of this calculus. This should be easy for extensions that have a first-order Kripke correspondence, as one can simply add the semantic conditions as rules for the relational atoms.
For Kripke-incomplete logics, such as
\mathsf{ER}
(see
[
7
,
32
]
), we conjecture that one could introduce a different progress condition which can capture the generalised semantics.

Building on that, it might be interesting to see whether these labelled proof systems might be reducible to simple sequent calculi without labels in a similar way to
[
18
,
21
]
, except that we would consider non-wellfounded proofs. We expect to find a system similar to the non-wellfounded one in
[
23
]
.
For applications of these calculi, it will be interesting to see how they enable one to obtain results about these logics, such as interpolation (see
[
1
,
23
]
).

Another line of further research could include the development of calculi for other provability logics such as Feferman provability
[
10
,
24
,
31
]
, slow provability
[
13
]
, intuitionistic provability
[
2
]
, or polymodal provability logic
\mathsf{GLP}

[
15
]
.
