GSpaRC: Gaussian Splatting for Real-time Reconstruction of RF Channels
Bhavya Sai Nukapotula, Rishabh Tripathi, Seth Pregler, Dileep Kalathil, Srinivas Shakkottai, Theodore S. Rappaport

Abstract
Channel state information (CSI) is essential for adaptive beamforming and maintaining robust links in wireless communication systems. However, acquiring CSI incurs significant overhead, consuming up to 25% of spectrum resources in 5G networks due to frequent pilot transmissions at sub-millisecond intervals. Recent approaches aim to reduce this burden by reconstructing CSI from spatiotemporal RF measurements, such as signal strength and direction-of-arrival. While effective in offline settings, these methods often suffer from inference latencies in the 5–100 ms range, making them impractical for real-time systems. We present GSpaRC: Gaussian Splatting for Real-time Reconstruction of RF Channels, the first algorithm to break the 1 ms latency barrier while maintaining high accuracy. GSpaRC represents the RF environment using a compact set of 3D Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by a lightweight neural model augmented with physics-informed features such as distance-based attenuation. Unlike traditional vision-based splatting pipelines, GSpaRC is tailored for RF reception: it employs an equirectangular projection onto a hemispherical surface centered at the receiver to reflect omnidirectional antenna behavior. A custom CUDA pipeline enables fully parallelized directional sorting, splatting, and rendering across frequency and spatial dimensions. Evaluated on multiple RF datasets, GSpaRC achieves similar CSI reconstruction fidelity to recent state-of-the-art methods while reducing training and inference time by over an order of magnitude. By trading modest GPU computation for a substantial reduction in pilot overhead, GSpaRC enables scalable, low-latency channel estimation suitable for deployment in 5G and future wireless systems. The code is available here: GSpaRC .

Introduction

Machine learning (ML) is increasingly transforming wireless communication systems by replacing or augmenting traditional rule-based elements with approaches that learn to model and adapt to complex propagation environments. This shift coincides with architectural innovations in 5G systems, particularly the emergence of GPU-accelerated software-defined stacks. Platforms like NVIDIA Aerial
(
11
)
now enable entire 5G baseband stacks to run on GPUs, delivering low-latency, high-throughput processing by leveraging GPU memory and parallelism. With GPUs available to the radio access network, ML models for the physical layer could potentially be deployed without violating the tight latency constraint of 1 ms or less per transmission time interval (TTI).

A promising opportunity for ML integration lies in wireless channel estimation, a core function that typically consumes 10–25% of spectrum resources in 5G systems. Traditionally, channel state information (CSI) is acquired via pilot transmissions and feedback. However, wireless propagation is inherently a 3D process, with signals interacting with the environment through reflection, diffraction, and scattering. This motivates viewing channel estimation as a 3D reconstruction problem. ML models could leverage spatiotemporal data from past transmissions, device locations, and environmental context to learn such representations. Given the success of 3D reconstruction techniques like neural radiance fields (NeRF) and Gaussian splatting (GS) in the visual domain, and the fact that RF signals are also electromagnetic waves, it is appealing to explore similar methods for wireless scenarios. Such models could enable predictive channel reconstruction, reducing estimation overhead and freeing spectrum for user data, thereby improving spectral efficiency.

Recently, Gaussian splatting (GS)
(
6
)
has emerged as a faster alternative for 3D reconstruction. Unlike NeRF, GS uses explicit 3D Gaussian primitives and avoids neural networks, enabling direct rendering through projection and blending. This greatly reduces latency and allows efficient GPU parallelization, making GS a promising approach for real-time 3D wireless channel modeling. However, off-the-shelf GS is not directly applicable to the wireless domain. First, wireless environments, such as large indoor spaces or outdoor areas, are structurally more complex than typical 3D scenes, while each RF sample carries far less information than an image. However, since the goal is to model signal propagation rather than visual detail, a smaller number of carefully tuned Gaussians may suffice. Second, unlike vision pipelines that rely on foveated rendering from a viewer’s perspective, RF reconstruction must be performed at discrete receiver locations using equirectangular projections. Third, while cameras operate largely via line-of-sight and require only a few rays per view, antennas receive energy from all directions, necessitating full hemispherical rendering. Finally, RF propagation is governed by physical effects such as attenuation and reflection—phenomena that are almost negligible in light-based GS models. Thus, effective wireless Gaussian splatting must incorporate RF-specific physics into both model construction and rendering.

To address these challenges, we introduce
GSpaRC—Gaussian Splatting for Real-time Reconstruction of RF Channels
—a novel ML-driven framework that is the first to break the 1 ms latency barrier while maintaining high reconstruction accuracy. GSpaRC is explicitly designed for execution within the tight timing constraints of the TTI window, making it suitable for deployment on GPU-based radio access networks. Our approach integrates several key innovations. First, we model the environment using a compact set of 3D Gaussian primitives that capture spatial signal behavior, with domain-specific attributes learned through a lightweight ML model and augmented by physics-informed features such as distance-based signal decay. Second, we introduce a equirectangular projection mechanism that splats these Gaussians onto a hemispherical surface, matching the directional reception geometry of multi-antenna RF systems and enabling efficient parallel rendering. Finally, we implement a custom CUDA pipeline for fully parallelized directional sorting, splatting, and rendering, achieving sub-millisecond inference even in complex, cluttered environments.

We contrast GSpaRC with recent parallel work exploring Gaussian splatting for RF modeling. RF-3DGS
(
20
)
adapts vision-based Gaussian splatting with image-based initialization and by approximating RF behavior using signal strength in decibels within a traditional 3DGS pipeline. While this appears effective for RF imaging and sensing, it is not well-suited for accurate, low-latency channel estimation. WRF-GS+
(
16
)
introduces deformable Gaussians and integrates a large neural network to capture RF-specific propagation effects, improving accuracy but incurring significant rendering latency. In contrast, RFSPM
(
18
)
attaches smaller MLPs to each Gaussian for directional emission but does not explicitly model attenuation, requiring more Gaussians and increasing computational cost. Typical examples of GSpaRC’s channel reconstruction accuracy are shown in Figure

Related Work

Recent efforts have extended Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
(
9
)
to model radio signal propagation in complex 3D environments.
NeRF2

(
21
)
is a notable example, leveraging a NeRF-like architecture composed of two neural networks. It takes as input the position of a virtual transmitter, the direction from receiver to transmitter, and the transmitter’s location. The network predicts signal strength and attenuation, incorporating the attenuation term into the rendering equation as opacity. This formulation adapts the volumetric rendering equation from computer graphics to RF propagation, where accumulated opacity modulates the received signal along sampled paths. While NeRF2 demonstrates strong generalization from sparse measurements, it requires several hours of training and incurs high rendering latency—often in the range of hundreds of milliseconds per frame—thus limiting its suitability for real-time applications.

NeWRF

(
8
)
adapts NeRF for wireless channel prediction by integrating a
1/d
path loss term into the volumetric rendering equation and leveraging direction-of-arrival (DoA) information to model RF propagation. It introduces a ray-casting mechanism guided by DoA estimates and a ray-searching algorithm for generalization to unvisited receiver locations. While effective for fixed-transmitter, mobile-receiver scenarios, NeWRF incurs high computational costs, requiring hours of training and hundreds of milliseconds per inference, limiting its suitability for real-time applications.

WRFGS

(
17
)
extends Gaussian splatting to wireless signal modeling by representing the radio field with spatial Gaussians and using a neural network to predict signal value and attenuation. In the same work, the authors also introduce
WRFGS+
, an enhanced variant of WRFGS that incorporates a more expressive network capable of estimating opacity, scaling, and rotation, thereby improving reconstruction quality. However, this increased complexity results in high computational cost, requiring hours of training and around 8 ms per query at inference, thereby undermining the core efficiency advantages of Gaussian splatting.

RFSPM

(
18
)
adapts Gaussian splatting for RF by attaching a small MLP to each 3D Gaussian to model directional emission. It performs splatting to identify the relevant Gaussians along a given direction, but diverges from usual 3DGS by subsequently sampling each of these Gaussians and blending the samples. This hybrid rendering strategy introduces additional computational overhead and latency. While RFSPM achieves high fidelity, it appears to compromise some efficiency benefits of 3DGS, making it less suited for applications like real-time channel estimation.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Wireless Signal Propagation

Let the transmitter emit a complex signal
u(t)
at carrier frequency
f_{c}
, which propagates through the environment. We describe the propagation and reception of this signal using a complex-valued formulation that captures key physical effects, including path loss, delay, and phase shift.

In an ideal free-space environment where a direct line-of-sight path exists between the transmitter and receiver, the signal received at a single antenna after traveling a distance
d
can be modeled as

r(t)=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}}\,u(t-\tau),

where
\lambda
is the carrier wavelength,
\tau=d/c
is the propagation delay with
c
denoting the speed of light,
\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}
captures the free-space path loss, and the exponential term accounts for the phase shift introduced by the signal traveling the distance
d
.

In realistic environments, the transmitted signal typically reaches the receiver through multiple propagation paths due to environmental interactions such as reflection, diffraction, and scattering. In such cases, the received signal is modeled as a superposition of
L
distinct paths:

r(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\alpha_{l}\lambda}{4\pi d_{l}}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi d_{l}}{\lambda}}\,u(t-\tau_{l}),

where
d_{l}
is the length of the
l
-th path,
\tau_{l}=d_{l}/c
is the associated propagation delay, and
\alpha_{l}\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex coefficient that accounts for the amplitude attenuation and phase distortion introduced by the medium and the interaction with surfaces along the path.

When the receiver is equipped with
M
spatially distributed antennas, each antenna receives a different combination of these multipath components, depending on its position and orientation relative to the incoming signals. The signal received at the
m
-th antenna is given by

r_{m}(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\alpha_{l,m}\lambda}{4\pi d_{l,m}}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi d_{l,m}}{\lambda}}\,u(t-\tau_{l,m}),

where
\alpha_{l,m}
is the complex gain of the
l
-th path arriving at the
m
-th antenna,
d_{l,m}
is the path distance to that antenna, and
\tau_{l,m}=d_{l,m}/c
is the corresponding delay.

3.2
3D Gaussian Splatting

Gaussian Splatting (GS) replaces the implicit volumetric MLP of NeRF with an explicit set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives
(\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k},\alpha_{k},c_{k})
. Each primitive is defined by: (i) a mean
\mu_{k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{3}
giving the 3D center of the Gaussian; (ii) a covariance
\Sigma_{k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
encoding its spatial extent, orientation, and anisotropy; (iii) an opacity
\alpha_{k}\!\in\![0,1]
determining the accumulation weight along the viewing ray; and (iv) a color
c_{k}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{3}
specifying the RGB radiance emitted by the primitive. The density field contributed by the
k
-th Gaussian is
G_{k}(x)=\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu_{k})^{\top}\allowbreak\Sigma_{k}^{-1}(x-\mu_{k})]
. To ensure that
\Sigma_{k}
remains positive semi-definite and numerically stable during optimization, it is parameterized as
\Sigma_{k}=R_{k}S_{k}S_{k}^{\top}R_{k}^{\top}
, where
R_{k}\!\in\!SO(3)
is a rotation matrix controlling orientation and
S_{k}
is a diagonal scale matrix controlling the principal-axis radii.

Under a camera projection
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\!\rightarrow\!\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each 3D Gaussian induces a 2D elliptical footprint on the image plane. This footprint is obtained by locally linearizing
\Pi
around the Gaussian mean and propagating the 3D covariance through the projection. The resulting 2D covariance is
\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}\propto J_{\Pi}(\mu_{k})\,\Sigma_{k}\,J_{\Pi}(\mu_{k})^{\top}
, where
J_{\Pi}(\mu_{k})
is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projective transformation. The corresponding normalized image-plane kernel is
\mathcal{G}_{k}(u)=\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}(u-\Pi(\mu_{k}))^{\top}\allowbreak\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(u-\Pi(\mu_{k}))]
, which determines how strongly the Gaussian contributes to pixel
u
.

Rendering is performed by depth-sorting the projected Gaussians and applying visibility-aware front-to-back
\alpha
-compositing. The color at pixel
u
is
\hat{C}(u)=\sum_{k}[T_{k}\,\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{G}_{k}(u)\,c_{k}]
, where the transmittance is
T_{k}=\prod_{j<k}(1-\alpha_{j}\,\mathcal{G}_{j}(u))
. This explicit, differentiable representation provides high-fidelity novel-view synthesis with real-time rendering performance and significantly reduced computational cost compared to volumetric ray marching.

4
Gaussian Splatting for Wireless Scene Reconstruction

4.1
Problem Formulation

Indoor environments, such as rooms furnished with objects like tables and chairs, present complex propagation conditions for radio signals emitted by an omnidirectional transmitter. As these signals interact with surrounding structures, they undergo frequency-dependent phenomena including reflection, refraction, and diffraction, resulting in intricate multipath effects. These interactions significantly distort the electromagnetic field captured by a receiver equipped with an antenna array. To account for the cumulative effects of the environment on the received signal, we model these interactions using the concept of virtual transmitters represented as gaussians, which serve as abstract representations of dominant multipath contributions. Our goal is to reconstruct a spatially consistent
spectrum image
from sparsely sampled spectral measurements at the receiver. To address the challenges of sparse sampling and frequency-selective multipath distortion, we leverage
Gaussian splatting
—a continuous, differentiable rendering technique that enables efficient and high-fidelity spectrum reconstruction. In the further part of this section, we present our algorithm
1
for wireless field reconstruction in complex indoor settings.

4.2
Initialization

Each virtual transmitter is represented as an anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitive, providing a differentiable and spatially continuous parameterization of its radiative contribution. A virtual transmitter
i
is described by the tuple
(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i},\sigma_{i},s_{i})
: the center
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
specifies its spatial location; the covariance
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
controls the Gaussian’s anisotropic support and orientation; the opacity
\sigma_{i}
determines its volumetric contribution in the splatting pipeline; and the complex coefficient
s_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
encodes the baseband signal emitted by the transmitter, with its real and imaginary parts corresponding to amplitude and phase.

The covariance follows the standard rotation–scale decomposition introduced earlier, providing a guaranteed positive semi-definite form while enabling independent control of anisotropy and orientation. Rotations are parameterized using unit quaternions to ensure valid and differentiable updates during training, and the diagonal scale parameters modulate the spatial extent of each Gaussian along its principal axes. The Gaussian density
G_{i}(x)
is defined using this parameterization and governs how each transmitter’s contribution decays with spatial displacement.

To model directional variation in signal propagation, each Gaussian is equipped with a lightweight neural predictor that produces a complex-valued signal coefficient conditioned on both the transmitter location and the viewing direction. Let
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{tx}}
denote the transmitter position and
(\theta_{i},\phi_{i})
the azimuth and elevation from the receiver to the Gaussian center. A compact MLP
f_{\vartheta}
maps these descriptors to

s_{i}=f_{\vartheta}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{tx}},\theta_{i},\phi_{i}),

(1)

allowing the representation to capture angular and distance-dependent propagation behavior directly from data, without relying on explicit electromagnetic wave models.

We initialize the Gaussians by uniformly distributing their centers across the spatial domain to form a well-conditioned and geometry-agnostic starting configuration. In contrast to prior 3D Gaussian Splatting systems that depend on Structure-from-Motion for stable initialization, our uniform strategy enables training to proceed without any external geometric priors. The scale parameters are set to a constant value to ensure broad scene coverage at initialization, while the rotation matrices are set to identity, corresponding to isotropic Gaussians with no directional preference. Opacity parameters are initialized to a small constant, enabling the optimization to gradually modulate volumetric influence. The MLP weights are initialized using standard normal distributions to promote unbiased directional predictions at the start of training. Together, these initialization choices provide a stable foundation for end-to-end optimization, allowing the Gaussian field to adapt to multipath, attenuation, and environment-specific propagation characteristics.

4.3
Splatting Mechanism

A viewing transformation is applied to the Gaussians to align the coordinate system with the receiver’s (Rx) perspective. This transformation reorients all Gaussian centers and their orientations such that the Rx is positioned at the origin and the viewing direction is aligned with the
z
-axis:

{\mu}_{i}^{\prime}=W({\mu}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{\text{rx}}).

Here,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the viewing transformation matrix that rotates world coordinates into the local coordinate frame of the receiver. It ensures that the scene is represented relative to the receiver’s position and viewing direction.

To compute the directional signal spectrum at a given receiver (Rx) position, we simulate rays emanating from the Rx into the environment at a fixed angular resolution of
1^{\circ}
. These rays uniformly sweep across azimuth angles from
0^{\circ}
to
360^{\circ}
and elevation angles from
0^{\circ}
to
90^{\circ}
, effectively forming a dense hemispherical sampling grid centered at the receiver.

This hemispherical distribution of directions is then projected onto a flat 2D image using an
equirectangular projection
, which maps each direction—defined by its azimuth and elevation angles—to a corresponding pixel in the image plane. Specifically, the azimuth angle is linearly mapped to the horizontal axis, while the elevation angle is linearly mapped to the vertical axis of the image. As a result, the 2D image serves as a panoramic representation of the upper hemisphere, where each pixel encodes the signal arriving from a specific direction in the Rx’s field of view.

Given a 3D point
(x,y,z)
in the receiver’s coordinate frame and output image dimensions
w
(width) and
h
(height), the corresponding pixel coordinates
(p_{x},p_{y})
in the equirectangular projection are computed as:

\begin{bmatrix}p_{x}\\
p_{y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\frac{\arctan 2(x,z)}{\pi}+1\right)\cdot w/2\\
2\cdot\arcsin\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}}\right)\cdot h/\pi\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
\arctan 2(x,z)
computes the azimuth angle (longitude),
\arcsin(y/r)
gives the elevation angle (latitude), and
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}
is the radial distance from the receiver. The result defines a pixel location on the upper-hemisphere equirectangular projection centered at the receiver.

To facilitate efficient rendering, all Gaussians are transformed into the receiver’s coordinate frame using the same viewing matrix
W
, enabling their parallel projection ("splatting") onto the 2D image plane using a custom CUDA kernel. Splatting here refers to the process of projecting a 3D Gaussian onto the image plane, forming an elliptical 2D Gaussian footprint.

The 2D covariance of each Gaussian in the image plane is computed as:

\Sigma^{\prime}=JW\Sigma W^{\top}J^{\top},

where
W
is the viewing transformation matrix applied to both Gaussian centers and their spatial extent, and
J
is the Jacobian of the affine approximation of the projection. The final 2D footprint is obtained by discarding the third row and column of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, corresponding to the depth dimension.

4.4
Differentiable Rendering

To render the signal field, we identify all Gaussians that intersect each viewing ray (i.e., direction of interest in azimuth–elevation space), sort them by their distance from the receiver, and splat their contributions onto the image. For each intersecting Gaussian
i
, a lightweight neural network (MLP) is queried using the transmitter’s position
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tx}}
to predict its signal intensity contribution
s_{i}
. This formulation allows the network to model spatial signal propagation patterns conditioned on the transmitter’s location.

The final signal value
S
observed in a given direction is computed using a volumetric rendering equation adapted for signal propagation:

S=\sum_{i\in N}\alpha_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_{j})\frac{s_{i}}{d}.

(2)

Here,

s_{i}
is the predicted signal strength from the MLP for Gaussian
i
,

\alpha_{i}=G^{\prime}_{i}\sigma_{i}
is the opacity-weighted contribution, where
G^{\prime}_{i}
represents the projected visibility of the Gaussian and
\sigma_{i}
its learned opacity,

d
is the Euclidean distance from the transmitter
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tx}}
to the Gaussian center
\mu_{i}
, modeling free-space path loss. This rendering formulation is structurally similar to point-based rendering, where compositional contributions are integrated along rays using alpha blending. Additional ablation studies evaluating the impact of this formulation are provided in Appendix

GSpaRC: Training and Signal-based Rasterization

4.5
Optimization

Following the GPU-accelerated optimization practices established in prior work
(
6
)
, which builds on techniques such as custom CUDA kernels for performance-critical steps
we adapt these strategies to the radiance field (RF) domain and implement tailored CUDA kernels for rasterization. This targeted acceleration significantly improves rendering efficiency, addressing the main computational bottleneck and enabling faster optimization and real-time feedback during training.

Positions
\mu
, covariance
\Sigma
, opacity
\sigma
of the Gaussians and parameters of MLP that capture signal propagation effect are optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), where gradients are computed by comparing the rendered signal against ground truth data over successive iterations.
After volumetric rendering, loss is calculated as:

\mathcal{L}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{1}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{SSIM}.

(3)

Combining the loss
\mathcal{L}_{1}
with the loss of the structural similarity index (SSIM) results in a more perceptually meaningful and balanced loss function. Integrating SSIM into the loss function promotes the preservation of high-frequency components in the image spectrum by penalizing structural distortions rather than just pixel-wise deviations.

Following loss computation, pixel-wise gradients are first derived from the loss equation. These are then used to compute gradients with respect to each contributing Gaussian parameter through a custom backward CUDA kernel, enabling efficient parallel execution. The resulting gradients are passed to the optimizer, which updates the Gaussian parameters accordingly.

5.1
Spectrum Synthesis - RFID Dataset

We evaluate our method using the publicly available RFID Spectrum NeRF2 dataset. In this setup, the receiver remains fixed, operates at 915 MHz, and is equipped with a
4\times 4
antenna array. The transmitter is an RFID tag that emits RN16 reply signals. Ground truth measurements are obtained from 6,123 distinct transmitter locations, recorded as spatial spectrum responses by the receiver.

The loss function used during optimization is a weighted combination of the L1 loss and the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM). A single weighting parameter,
\lambda_{1}=0.2
, controls the trade-off between pixel-wise accuracy and perceptual quality. This formulation ensures that the model not only minimizes numerical errors but also preserves the structural fidelity of the reconstructed signals relative to the ground truth. All hyperparameter settings used for optimization are provided in Appendix

Conclusion

We presented
GSpaRC
, a framework for real-time wireless channel reconstruction that combines the speed and differentiability of 3D Gaussian splatting with domain-specific insights from RF propagation. GSpaRC models the environment using a compact set of learned Gaussian primitives, enhanced with physics-informed neural features, and employs a custom CUDA-based rendering pipeline optimized for hemispherical RF signal reception. This design enables efficient, sub-millisecond inference and supports high-fidelity spectrum reconstruction from sparse RF measurements.

Our evaluations demonstrate that GSpaRC achieves competitive accuracy across SSIM, PSNR, and MSE metrics while reducing training and inference times by an order of magnitude compared to state-of-the-art methods such as NeRF2 and WRF-GS+. Importantly, GSpaRC is the first splatting-based approach to operate within the strict 1 ms processing window required by 5G transmission time intervals (TTIs), making it a practical and scalable solution for modern wireless systems.

By aligning fast, differentiable 3D reconstruction techniques with the physical requirements of RF propagation and antenna reception, GSpaRC lays the groundwork for real-time, data-driven channel estimation in latency-sensitive wireless applications. Its potential extends to digital twins, adaptive beamforming, and mobility-aware communication in 5G and future networks.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
TTI-Scale Channel Estimation and the Role of GSpaRC

In 5G cellular systems (called 5G New Radio or 5G NR), data transmission follows a structured pipeline: user data is first encoded and modulated into radio frequency (RF) signals, which are then transmitted over a wireless channel, where they experience distortion due to multipath propagation, Doppler shifts, shadowing, and interference. At the receiver, the incoming signal undergoes synchronization, channel estimation, demodulation, and decoding to reconstruct the original information. Among these stages, accurate and timely channel estimation is essential, as it enables coherent demodulation and supports adaptive techniques such as beamforming and link adaptation
(
4
;
14
)
.

5G NR employs flexible Transmission Time Intervals (TTIs), ranging from 0.125 ms (for mm-Wave) to 1 ms (typical in the sub-6 GHz band). Crucially, channel estimates are required at every TTI to maintain reliable communication, through the choice of the modulation and coding schemes, beamforming etc.. This is achieved through a combination of reference signals:
Demodulation Reference Signals (DMRS)
embedded in each TTI support fine-grained estimation,
Phase Tracking Reference Signals (PTRS)
provide sub-TTI phase correction, and
Channel State Information Reference Signals (CSI-RS)
enable longer-term channel tracking over many TTIs. Collectively, these reference signals typically consume 10–25% of time-frequency resources—an overhead that scales with mobility and frequency.

The coherence time
T_{c}
, which defines the duration over which the channel remains approximately constant, is inversely proportional to the Doppler spread and decreases rapidly with mobility. For instance, at 2.4 GHz, a pedestrian user has
T_{c}\approx 35~\mathrm{ms}
, while a user moving at highway speed (65-75 mph) experiences
T_{c}\approx 2.4~\mathrm{ms}
, necessitating frequent estimation updates. In such cases, pilot signaling must be densely inserted to ensure valid estimates within each coherence interval.

Our proposed method,
GSpaRC
(Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Reconstruction of RF Channels), offers a complementary solution: by producing accurate channel reconstructions at each TTI from spatiotemporal RF context, GSpaRC enables predictive estimation that can guide dynamic pilot allocation. Rather than inserting DMRS and PTRS at fixed densities, a GS-based estimate can inform when and where reference signals are truly needed, allowing systems to adaptively reduce pilot overhead while still meeting latency and fidelity constraints.

Appendix E
Limitations

While
GSpaRC
demonstrates promising performance in real-time RF channel reconstruction, several limitations remain. First, like other radiance field-based approaches, GSpaRC requires accurate localization of the receiver. This is essential to correctly orient the Gaussian primitives during rendering and affects the accuracy of the resulting spectrum prediction. Although localization data is commonly available in many practical scenarios (e.g., from IMUs or infrastructure-based tracking), inaccuracies in receiver position could degrade reconstruction fidelity. Second, our current work on GSpaRC assumes a relatively static environment during training and inference. Rapid changes in geometry, such as moving objects or people, may violate this assumption and reduce accuracy unless the model is frequently retrained or augmented with temporal dynamics. Finally, while GSpaRC achieves sub-millisecond inference using modest hardware, it still relies on GPU acceleration and a pre-training phase that may not be feasible for extremely constrained edge devices. Addressing these limitations, particularly in dynamic environments and under localization uncertainty, is an important direction for future work toward making GSpaRC more robust and widely deployable.
