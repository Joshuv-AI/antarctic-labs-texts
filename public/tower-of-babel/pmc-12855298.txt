Assessment of gamma and neutron shielding features of Se80−yTe20My (M = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, y = 0 and 2) alloys for radiation safety applications
Authors: Nisha Kumari, Vishnu Saraswat, Shiv Kumar Pal, Ishu Sharma, Z Y Khattari, Neeraj Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ternary transition-metal chalcogenide system possesses high potential in various fields of science and technology. The present report highlights a comparative study on the high-energy radiation shielding properties of transition metals as chemical modifiers in the Se80 − yTe20My (M = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, y = 0 and 2) chalcogenide series, spanning a broad photon energy range of 15 keV to 15 MeV. The radiation shielding capability of the studied materials against high-energy radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, was evaluated using Phy-X/PSD software and experimentally validated. Various parameters essential for evaluating radiation shielding performance were investigated, including MFP, HVL, LAC, MAC, Zeff, Neff, Zeq, RPE, TF, EABF, EBF, and FNRCS (ΣR). Among the studied samples, the Fe incorporated sample (STFe) exhibited the most effective shielding performance. At 0.015 MeV, it recorded the highest LAC (~ 413.87 cm⁻¹) and a MAC of 87.498 cm²/g, values significantly higher than those of standard shielding materials. STFe showed the lowest HVL and MFP at 0.015 MeV, confirming its strong gamma-ray shielding. It also had the highest FNRCS (0.076 cm⁻¹), indicating excellent neutron protection. Its excellent performance arises from its higher density.Keywords: Transition metals, Chalcogenides, Attenuation, Density, Radiation shieldingSubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

In recent years, optimizing the composition of chalcogenide glassy materials has emerged as a vital research direction in radiation physics, particularly for shielding applications in nuclear medicine1, space exploration2, and radiation therapy3–5. This interest has grown because studies show that gamma radiation can significantly alter the structural and physical properties of glasses and glass-ceramics6–9. Ionizing radiation is used extensively in medicine, energy, industry, and research. Still, its hazards require effective shielding materials10, and there is an increasing demand for the development of efficient and reliable radiation shielding materials11–13. These radiation-induced effects are highly dependent on parameters such as chemical composition, density, radiation dose, and exposure time14. Glasses and ceramics attract attention in nuclear science because they are more radiation-resistant than crystalline materials15–17. Chalcogenide glasses are known for their radiation resistance properties and are promising materials for space, nuclear, and reactor applications that require long-term shielding and operational efficiency18. Previous studies have shown that selenium-rich glasses are effective gamma-ray shields, with their performance depending on composition and radiation intensity. Adding tellurium (Te) strengthens the Se-based network, enhancing its elastic, thermal, optical, and electrical properties for advanced photonic and radiation-shielding applications15. Combined with selenium’s excellent glass-forming ability and the improved thermal stability of Se-Te samples, these alloys have attracted significant research interest due to their potential in radiation protection technologies19–23. In recent years, transition metals (TMs) incorporated glasses have attracted significant interest in physics, materials science, and condensed matter physics due to the unique behavior of their 3d electrons, which can significantly enhance the properties of the glass. Transition metals also play a significant role in both conventional and emerging technologies. While traditionally used as coloring agents in glass production, they now serve more advanced functions in various alloys.

Adding transition metals not only alters the glass structure but also enhances gamma-ray shielding through scattering and absorption. The use of metallic alloys as alternative protective shielding materials has attracted significant attention due to the limitations of conventional shielding materials24. TMs are valuable for tailoring the shielding performance of glass and glass-ceramics, as well as for their other functional roles. Elements such as Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Y, Zr, Nb, Mo, Ag, Cd, and W have significantly enhanced the radiation shielding capabilities of various alloys. Like other glasses, transition-metal-incorporated chalcogenide glasses can provide some degree of radiation shielding due to their atomic structure and composition25–36. Alzahrani et al. reported radiation protection efficiency (RPE) values at 0.284 MeV of 64.57% (Ni-based), 59.05% (Fe-based), 98.33% (Pb-based), and 99.32% (W-based); thus, Pb and W exhibit the highest γ-ray shielding efficiencies in that study, while the neutron cross-sections showed no significant changes among these alloys. However, they did not observe significant changes in the neutron cross-sections of these alloys25,37,38. Copper (Cu)-based alloys are widely used across numerous fields owing to their superior electrical and thermal conductivity, structural durability, and corrosion resistance39,40. Seven et al. investigated CoCuNi alloys and reported the presence of a photoelectric absorption edge, along with measurements of the MAC at discrete photon energies ranging from 11.88 to 26 keV41. Hamad et al. evaluated the shielding capabilities of FexSe0.5Te0.5 alloys against ionizing radiation, identifying Fe0.95Se0.5Te0.5 as the most effective sample, while alloys with higher iron concentrations showed lower attenuation efficiency27.

Elias et al. studied the optical and radiation-shielding behavior of lithium tetraborate (Li2B4O7) glasses doped with Mn, Cu, and Cr, finding that the LBO: Cu5 sample provided superior radiation shielding30. Nazrin et al. synthesized copper oxide-doped borotellurite glasses via melt-quenching and observed that higher copper oxide content decreased the density but increased the molar volume31. Using Phy-X/PSD software, Zahran et al. determined the shielding parameters of the quaternary compounds Cu2CoGeS4, Cu2CoGeSe4, and Cu2CoGeTe435. Nefzi et al. used cobalt-60 as a radiation source to investigate the effects of gamma-ray doses (20–100 kGy) on Cu2InSnS4 (CITS) thin films produced by spray pyrolysis33.

Sabry et al. investigated the radiation shielding characteristics of compounds such as Cu2CdSn3S8, Cu2MnGeS4, Cu2MnGeSe4, Cu2MnGeTe4, as well as binary compounds such as CuS, CdS, and SnS, spanning the energy range from 15 keV to 15 MeV. Their results identified Cu2MnGeTe4 as an excellent candidate for gamma-ray and neutron shielding, while CuS exhibited the highest FNRCS value (= 0.102 cm− 1) among the studied materials34,35. Studies have also explored the shielding capabilities of glass and composite systems containing ferric oxide42–46. Babeer et al. observed that increasing the PbO/BaO ratio in PbO–Fe2O3–SrO–B2O3–BaO glasses led to higher density and optical band gaps but reduced linear and nonlinear refractive indices, making these glasses promising candidates for radiation protection42. The optical, mechanical, and shielding properties of CaO-Na2O-B2O3 glasses and Fe2O3-reinforced hydroxyapatite composites were also documented43,44. Bawazeer et al. examined sodium borate glasses doped with Fe2O3 and Sm2O3, highlighting their optical and shielding properties45. Along these lines, Negm et al.47,48 investigated clays containing nano-oxides to improve the shielding effectiveness of these composite materials. Their findings aligned well with the results discussed above39–46. The same group also studied metallic glasses based on the chemical formula B2O3 + TeO2 mixed with various mol% of P2O5, ZnO, Na2O, Fe2O3, and K2O. Their results suggested that higher-density metallic glasses provide superior radiation shielding against γ-rays, neutrons, and charged particles, making them excellent candidates for advanced radiation shielding applications49.

The literature survey reveals that very limited data are available on the radiation shielding of transition-metal-incorporated chalcogenide glasses. Thus, this article explores the photon, neutron, and proton shielding capabilities of the Se80 − yTe20My (M = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu; y = 0, 2) alloys and compares them with those of other standard concretes and glasses. Different radiation shielding parameters in the energy range from 15 keV to 15 MeV were analyzed using Phy-X/PSD software. These results were also compared with those of the parent binary glassy Se80Te20 alloy.

Scientific (experimental/theoretical) basis

The samples of the Se80Te20 (ST) and Se78Te20M2 (STM) alloys (where M = Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) were synthesized in bulk form using the well-known melt-quenching method50,51. The melt-quench technique is considered cost-effective because it enables glass formation in a single step, requiring only conventional raw materials, a melting furnace, and rapid cooling, thereby minimizing the need for specialized equipment, controlled environments, or complex processing procedures. Sakar et al.52 developed the Phy-X/PSD online software, a web-based simulation tool for calculating key radiation shielding parameters for various materials. The accuracy of the Phy-X/PSD online software has been verified by comparison with values reported in the literature. The software is freely available for academic use with proper citation. In this study, a user-friendly software available at https://phy-x.net/PSD has been used to compute a wide range of shielding and dosimetric parameters. Built on the NodeJS platform, the software performs calculations efficiently, completing approximately 377,600 calculations in about 500 s for different materials. In the software, the sample thickness is predefined, ranging from 1 to 3 cm, providing a comprehensive description of the simulation setup and process. After this, the software offers two predefined energy ranges: 15 keV–15 MeV and 1 keV–100 GeV, along with a selection of well-known radioactive sources (e.g., 22Na, 60Co, 137Cs) and characteristic X-rays from elements (e.g., Cu, Mo, Ag). Then, we select the shielding parameters and energies relevant to our study and input the additional required parameters. Once the parameters are selected, the calculation is performed, and the data generation process starts. After completing these steps, the results are saved in an Excel file for further analysis. The file includes key radiation shielding parameters like mean free path (MFP), half-value layer (HVL), tenth-value layer (TVL), linear and mass attenuation coefficients (LAC and MAC), radiation protection efficiency (RPE), transmission factor (TF), effective atomic number (Zeff), effective electron density (Neff), atomic cross-section (ACS), electronic cross-section (ECS), effective conductivity (Ceff), and buildup factors (R and Zeq). Additionally, it calculates energy absorption and exposure build-up factors (EABF and EBF) and the fast-neutron removal cross-section (FNRCS) for any selected material. It efficiently evaluates the shielding performance of different materials across a wide energy range. One of the main advantages of this tool is its ability to analyze an unlimited number of material compositions simultaneously, delivering accurate results quickly. This software is expected to help researchers develop reliable, low-cost shielding materials efficiently. To validate the theoretical results, experimental measurements were carried out on all samples at photon energies of 0.06 MeV, 0.08 MeV, and 0.1 MeV, using a sample thickness of 4 mm and a tungsten-source X-ray system equipped with a stereotactic field diode detector. Due to experimental limitations, the measurements were restricted to these three energies.

In the present study, we have limited our investigation to the photon energy range of 15 keV to 15 MeV, which comprehensively encompasses the majority of practical and widely encountered scenarios in medical diagnostics and therapy, nuclear industry, radiation shielding design, and industrial radiography. These energy levels are well-aligned with standard shielding requirements and are supported by reliable photon interaction cross-section databases. We acknowledge that environments such as space, high-energy physics laboratories, or advanced accelerator facilities involve photon and particle interactions at energies beyond 15 MeV. However, such high-energy domains require different modelling techniques as well as extended cross-section datasets that incorporate high-energy physics models.

The current study is designed as a foundation for understanding the radiation shielding potential of ST and STM alloys within the well-established and most commonly used energy range. We consider this a necessary and practical first step. We are currently planning future extensions of this work to cover higher energy ranges (up to 100 MeV and beyond) using appropriate simulation tools and theoretical models, specifically focusing on space and accelerator-based applications. We have also performed experimental measurements at photon energies of 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1 MeV and plan to extend this energy range for further investigation in the future.

Results and discussions

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on the fabricated powder specimens to investigate the thermal stability of ST and STM samples at a heating rate of 15 °C/min over 25–600 °C. The sample weight was recorded as a function of increasing temperature, with measurements carried out in a nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL/min). A continuous gas flow was maintained throughout the experiment to ensure environmental stability and minimize the effects of moisture. The inset figure clearly compares the onset temperature of degradation for all samples. The onset temperature is the specific temperature at which the TGA curve first deviates from a flat baseline, indicating the beginning of mass loss and material decomposition. The onset temperature for the STM samples is lower than that of the parent sample. The TGA curves indicate a single-stage decomposition for all samples, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, a minute weight loss of approximately 0.02% has been observed for all samples within a temperature range of 25 °C to 297.44 °C. After that, from a temperature range of 297.44 °C to 381.71 °C, each sample exhibits a slow weight loss. For the temperature range 381.71 °C to 491.03 °C, STCu, STCo, and STFe exhibit a similar mass-degradation pattern. Meanwhile, ST and STNi samples follow nearly identical decomposition patterns up to 510.51 °C. Approximately 60% mass degradation is observed for ST and STM (M = Co, Cu, Fe) from 381.71 °C to 600 °C, while the Ni-containing sample shows only about 48% mass degradation. These results indicate that the prepared samples exhibit good thermal stability up to 381 °C.

When applied to the preparation of radiation-shielding glassy or glass-ceramic alloys, the melt-quench technique offers significant advantages in terms of manufacturability and cost. The process involves the direct melting of constituent elements or compounds in a conventional furnace, followed by rapid quenching, which simplifies fabrication and enables the production of homogeneous bulk samples. Because it does not require high-vacuum systems, sophisticated deposition setups, or prolonged processing cycles, the overall production cost remains relatively low. Moreover, the method can be scaled to larger batch sizes, making it highly suitable for developing radiation-shielding glasses where both material uniformity and economic feasibility are essential.

Density is a crucial factor in determining the radiation shielding efficiency of glass. For denser materials, more radiation attenuation has been reported in the literature61. The density is governed by the internal structure of the glass, which is defined by the arrangement of atoms and the nature of chemical bonding. Incorporating additional elements into the ternary STM alloys alters the glass network structure, thereby influencing the density and ultimately enhancing or reducing the shielding performance. Table 1 shows that the STFe alloy possesses the highest density among all the prepared samples. As a result, it demonstrates superior radiation attenuation compared to the other investigated alloys. Three primary photon interaction mechanisms dominate at different energy ranges: Photoelectric absorption (PA), Compton scattering (CS), and Pair production (PP). Photoelectric absorption is predominant at low photon energies and is directly proportional to Z4–5 and inversely proportional to E3. At intermediate energies, Compton scattering becomes the dominant interaction, with its probability varying directly with the atomic number Z and E−1. At higher energies, attenuation is mainly governed by pair production, which depends on Z2 and increases logarithmically with energy (log E). Using Eq. Using Eq. (2), we have determined the MAC values for the present samples. The variation of MAC with energy is quite similar to the LAC due to their mutual interrelationship for all alloys (see Fig. 4). The MAC values exhibit a sharp decline between 0.015 and 0.1 MeV, followed by a gradual decrease with increasing photon energy67, reaching their minimum around 6 MeV for all selected alloys. A slight increase in the MAC value is observed in the higher-energy range between 8 and 15 MeV. Figure 4 reveals that the MAC results of the prepared samples vary with incident photon energy and are largely independent of their chemical composition. A sharp decline in MAC values is observed up to 0.1 MeV, primarily due to the dominance of Compton scattering in this energy range. From 0.15 to 6 MeV, the values decrease more slowly. However, a noticeable increase appears between 8 and 15 MeV, where pair production becomes the prevailing photon interaction process at higher energies. The bar diagram in Fig. 4 indicates that the parent alloy (ST) exhibits the highest MAC value at 15 keV, whereas the Fe incorporated sample shows the lowest value at the same energy. Figure 5 shows the bar graph comparing the theoretical and experimental MAC values for the ST, STFe, STCo, STNi, and STCu samples. At a photon energy of 0.08 MeV, the experimental results closely follow the theoretical data, confirming the reliability and accuracy of the measurements. The experimental MAC values are 2.692, 2.647, 2.679, 2.611, and 2.720 cm²/g for ST, STFe, STCo, STNi, and STCu, respectively. These higher experimental values indicate that the prepared samples provide better photon absorption than predicted by the theoretical calculations. The comparative analysis of MAC values (0.015–15 MeV) confirms that the present samples (ST, STFe, STCo, STNi, STCu) exhibit superior or comparable radiation shielding performance compared to other reported materials, including Se78 − xTe20Sn2Mx (where M = Cd, Ag, or Zn, and x = 2)62; (50-x)V2O5–10Bi2O3–40B2O3-xPbO with x = 5 (VBBPx)68; lead–tungsten–boron glass matrix doped with bismuth (PWB-Bi)23; borosilicate (BS) glasses with 5% Cr2O3 (BS-Cr2O3)69; and sodium metaphosphate glasses modified with molybdate and tungstate, denoted as (100-x)%NaPO3-x%MoO₃/WO₃ with x = 20, corresponding to samples S1 and S4, respectively70, as shown in Table 4. At a very low photon energy of 0.015 MeV, the current glass samples (ST, STFe, STCo, STNi, STCu) show much higher MAC values, ranging from 87.498 (STFe) to 88.162 (ST). These values are far better than those of other known shielding materials. Although density generally enhances shielding, at very low photon energies, such as 15 keV (0.015 MeV), the MAC is strongly influenced by the atomic composition and the photoelectric effect. Therefore, the parent ST glass, with its specific Se/Te ratio, exhibits a higher MAC. At the same time, the Fe atoms in STFe contribute less to photoelectric absorption at this energy, resulting in a lower MAC despite its higher density. For instance, VBBPx has a MAC of 47.441 cm²/g, while PWB-Bi shows 73.134 cm²/g, both lower than those of the current samples. Even lower MAC values are observed in BS-Cr2O3 (9.324 cm²/g) and for phosphate glasses such as S1 and S4 (43.677 cm²/g), clearly proving the excellent shielding ability of the present materials at low energy. At intermediate energies (0.03–0.06 MeV), the current glasses show superior attenuation capabilities. Specifically, at 0.05 MeV, the MAC values for the present samples are consistently around 6.03–6.04 cm2 g− 1, which is significantly higher compared to VBBPx (3.043 cm²/g), PWB-Bi (5.199 cm²/g), and BS-Cr2O3 (0.326 cm²/g), reflecting a stronger photon interaction response. Even at higher photon energies (1–15 MeV), the present samples maintain competitive performance. At 15 MeV, their MAC values remain in the range of 0.036–0.03653 cm2 g− 1, which is better than those of BS-Cr2O3 (0.024 cm²/g) and sample S1 (0.030 cm²/g) and closely comparable to PWB-Bi (0.042 cm²/g). These results confirm the strong and consistent radiation shielding effectiveness of the present alloys across a broad energy range. Hence, the highest MAC values are observed at lower photon energies, primarily because photoelectric absorption dominates71. In contrast, the lowest values appear at higher energies, where Compton scattering becomes the predominant interaction mechanism.

As described in Eq. 5(a) and (6), the ability of the chosen samples to minimize the shielding effectiveness of radiation can be expressed using the HVL and MFP factors72. The variation of these parameters with photon energy for prepared ST and STM (M = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) composites is shown in Fig. 6. At lower photon energies, the PE dominates, leading to greater photon absorption and smaller HVL and MFP values. As the photon energy increases, gamma photons can penetrate deeper into the glass because they interact less with electrons. This reduces the LAC, thereby increasing both the parameters. In this study, HVL and MFP were analyzed within the energy range of 0.015–15 MeV. As shown in Fig. 6, HVL and MFP rise sharply with increasing energy up to 5 MeV, reaching their maximum values at around 6 MeV (~ 4.66 cm for HVL and ~ 6.73 cm for MFP). However, as the energy increases toward 15 MeV, both parameters decline significantly, reflecting reduced photon interactions at higher energies. The MFP and HVL values for the alloys show distinct variations. The MFP decreases from ~ 5.951 cm to 5.791 cm, and the HVL decreases from around 4.125 cm to 4.014 cm at high energy (15 MeV). The STFe alloy exhibits the lowest values compared to other alloys like ST, STCo, STNi, and STCu. At 6 MeV, the MFP values follow this order: ST (6.733 cm) > STCu (6.717 cm) > STCo (6.661 cm) > STNi (6.558 cm) > STFe (6.548 cm). At 0.015 MeV, the lowest HVL values are observed in the following order: STFe (0.00167 cm) < STNi (0.00168 cm) < STCo (0.00170 cm) < STCu (0.00171 cm) = ST (0.00171 cm). Additionally, the Fe-based alloys exhibit the minimum MFP at this energy, with STFe showing the lowest value (0.00242 cm), followed by STNi, STCo, STCu, and ST, as shown in Fig. 6. Hence, the lower HVL and MFP values indicate that the STFe sample is the most effective shielding material among the prepared alloys. The experimental HVL measurements further confirm these observations. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the HVL values for ST and STM alloys vary with photon energy. As expected, the HVL increases with photon energy for all samples, indicating that higher-energy photons require a greater material thickness to achieve equivalent attenuation. This behavior aligns with the general principle that higher-energy photons have greater penetration power. For ST and STM alloys, the HVL ranges from approximately 0.49 to 0.61 cm within the 60–100 keV energy range, as presented in Table 3. In Fig. 7(b), the bar graph compares the experimental and theoretical HVL values for the five prepared alloys at an incident energy of 0.08 MeV. The experimental HVL values are lower than the theoretical ones for all alloys. In the theoretical calculations, HVL values are estimated for thicknesses ranging from 1 to 3 cm. However, the experimental measurements are performed using a much thinner sample of 4 mm. This difference in thickness contributes to the variation between the theoretical and experimental results, with the experimental data showing lower HVL values, thereby indicating better shielding efficiency in the prepared samples. Among the alloys, STFe consistently exhibits the lowest HVL values in both theoretical and experimental cases, confirming its superior photon-shielding capability.

The Zeff defined in Eq. (7), represents the average atomic number influencing photon interactions within a material. It is a crucial parameter for identifying the dominant photon-interaction mechanism at a specific photon energy. The parameter on which photon attenuation depends is the Zeff of prepared materials; i.e., the more gamma radiation is attenuated by those materials possessing a high value of Zeff. The influence of the atomic numbers of the constituent elements on the photon interaction and absorption capacity of the studied alloys was illustrated by their effective atomic numbers. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the photoelectric effect causes the value of Zeff to increase rapidly from 0.015 MeV to 0.06 MeV, then abruptly decrease from 0.06 MeV to 0.5 MeV, and finally fluctuate steadily from 0.5 MeV to 15 MeV as a result of the CS and PP processes, respectively. The newly prepared Fe-incorporated alloys exhibit the highest Zeff values, whereas STCu shows the lowest. Because of their mutual interaction, as described in Eq. (7), the variations of the Neff and Ceff with photon energy are similar to those of Zeff. The variations of Neff and Ceff with the photon energy for the studied alloys are shown in Figs. 8(b) and 8(c). The Fe alloy exhibits the highest Neff and Ceff values compared to the other alloys.

As emphasized by several research communities, the design of novel radiation shielding materials requires knowledge of buildup factors, namely the EABF and the EBF57,77,78. Using the Zeq and the corresponding G-P fitting parameters79, these buildup factors can be evaluated. Figure 10 presents the variation of EABF with photon energy at four penetration depths (0.5, 5, 20, and 40 mfp). The highest EABF value is observed for the ST sample in the low-energy region. This can be attributed to the predominance of the photoelectric effect, where photon energies are comparable to the binding energies of the K, L-I, and L-II electron shells, producing the peaks evident in the energy–EABF curve80. Figure 11 shows the dependence of EABF on penetration depth at selected photon energies (15 keV, 0.15 MeV, 1.5 MeV, and 15 MeV) for ST and STM (M = Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) alloys. For all samples, EABF decreases with decreasing penetration depth. At higher energies (15 MeV), the variation follows an exponential trend due to the dominance of pair creation. The ST glass consistently exhibits higher EABF values at low energies and larger penetration depths, which can be attributed to its relatively lower density compared to the transition-metal-containing samples. A lower density promotes greater photon buildup because photons undergo more successive scatterings before absorption. In practical shielding design, this implies that the ST sample may offer higher buildup, which could also result in increased secondary radiation and therefore require thicker shielding layers compared to denser doped samples.

Similarly, Fig. 12 illustrates the variation of EBF with photon energy for the ST and STM alloys at four penetration depths (0.5, 5, 20, and 40 mfp). The EBF values peak in the low-energy region due to strong photon absorption, and then decrease sharply. A secondary increase appears in the intermediate energy range, where Compton scattering predominates. At higher energies, where pair production becomes dominant, the EBF values become nearly constant, especially at larger penetration depths. The buildup factor increases with penetration depth, reaching its maximum at 40 mfp due to enhanced multiple scattering. Although the EBF follows the same general trend as the EABF, its consistently higher values underscore the importance of accounting for buildup effects in shielding design. High buildup factors, particularly for the ST, indicate that the uncollided photon flux can be significantly exceeded by the scattered radiation flux, which must be accounted for when evaluating shielding efficiency and radiation safety. The incorporation of transition metals (Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) slightly reduces the buildup, suggesting that density and atomic number are beneficial in suppressing excessive secondary photon buildup. Similar to the EABF, the EBF exhibits a clear dependence on penetration depth. However, the EABF values are consistently lower than the corresponding EBF values, as shown in Figs. 11 and 13. Therefore, studying their attenuation properties is essential to ensure adequate protection for personnel and surrounding equipment.

In this study, the ΣR of the prepared samples was calculated using the PSD software. The software automatically determines ΣR based on the material’s chemical composition, which is provided as input in the form of weight fractions or atomic fractions of each constituent element. Using its built-in database of elemental fast-neutron removal cross-sections, it calculates the effective ΣR of the material by taking a weighted sum of all the aspects. This automated method provides an accurate and reliable method to calculate the neutron shielding ability of the samples. The attenuation of these fast neutrons, represented by the removal cross-section, is illustrated in Fig. 14. The FNRCS values for the studied samples are 0.073, 0.076, 0.074, 0.075, and 0.074 cm⁻¹ for ST, STFe, STCo, STNi, and STCu, respectively. These values follow the order: ST (0.073 cm⁻¹) < STCo = STCu (0.074 cm⁻¹) < STNi (0.075 cm⁻¹) < STFe (0.076 cm⁻¹). Although the STFe shows the largest FNRCS value in this series, the overall variation between the samples is minimal (within ~ 4%). Therefore, claiming a clear Fe advantage will require more analysis in future experiments by considering Fe’s specific interaction mechanisms with fast neutrons. Instead, these slight variations suggest that transition-metal incorporation in general has only a modest influence on the fast neutron attenuation ability of Se–Te-based glasses. The relatively high ΣR observed for the STFe sample may be primarily attributed to its slightly higher density, emphasizing that density remains the dominant factor governing fast neutron shielding in this alloy.

Finally, the superior radiation shielding performance of the STFe alloy, coupled with its relatively low density compared to traditional lead-based materials, highlights its potential for weight-sensitive applications. For instance, in spacecraft or satellite design, where minimizing weight is critical, STFe could serve as an effective gamma-ray and neutron shielding material. Moreover, its high attenuation efficiency at low and medium photon energies suggests that it can be used in radiotherapy rooms to provide compact, reliable protection for medical personnel and sensitive equipment, offering a promising alternative to conventional heavy shielding materials (see Table 2).

Conclusions

The present study explores the radiation shielding efficiency of alloys containing selenium and tellurium, with the addition of TMs (Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) to the chemical composition of Se80 − xTe20Mx (x = 0 and 2), using the open-source Phy-X/PSD software and experimental validation. The incorporation of TMs into the parent ST sample results in monotonic variation in key shielding parameters, including MAC, LAC, HVL, TF, and ΣR. Among all samples, the STFe exhibits the highest density (4.73 g/cm³), contributing to its superior radiation attenuation performance. At a photon energy of 15 keV, the LAC follows the ascending order: STCu (404.24 cm⁻¹) < ST (405.545 cm⁻¹) < STCo (407.07 cm⁻¹) < STNi (413.76 cm⁻¹) < STFe (413.86 cm⁻¹). At an extremely low energy of 0.015 MeV, the MAC values are remarkably high for all samples, ranging from 87.498 cm²/g (STFe) to 88.162 cm²/g (ST), in the following sequence: ST > STCu > STNi > STCo > STFe.

The HVL and MFP reach their maximum values (~ 4.66 cm and ~ 6.73 cm, respectively) at 6 MeV. However, the STFe maintains the lowest HVL and MFP among all samples. Specifically, the MFP at 6 MeV follows the trend: ST (6.733 cm) > STCu (6.717 cm) > STCo (6.661 cm) > STNi (6.558 cm) > STFe (6.548 cm). Similarly, at 0.015 MeV, the HVL values are lowest in the following order: STFe (0.00167 cm) < STNi (0.00168 cm) < STCo (0.00170 cm) < STCu = ST (0.00171 cm) and are better than those of standard shielding materials. Furthermore, the STFe alloy exhibits the highest values of the Zeff, Neff, Ceff, and radiation absorption-related parameters. For fast neutron attenuation, STFe shows the highest removal cross-Sect. (0.076 cm⁻¹). Thus, based on the above results, we conclude that the STFe alloy exhibits better shielding capabilities against gamma radiation and fast neutrons.

Limitations and future work

This study is primarily based on computational simulations using the Phy-X/PSD software, with experimental validation conducted only at selected photon energies of 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1 MeV. To assess the reliability of the Phy-X/PSD simulations, the computed shielding parameters were compared with experimental measurements at 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1 MeV. The results indicate that the predicted MAC agrees with the experimental values within 2–4%. In comparison, the RPE values differ by less than 5%, confirming the software’s robustness in estimating photon absorption and shielding efficiency. In contrast, the LAC and HVL exhibit slightly larger deviations, typically 8–15% and 7–12%, respectively. These differences are primarily attributed to the thinner experimental samples (4 mm) compared to the standard simulation thickness (10 mm). For neutron shielding, the fast neutron removal cross-section (ΣR) shows only minor variation across alloys (≤ 4%), highlighting the consistency of Phy-X/PSD predictions. Overall, the close correspondence between simulated and experimental results demonstrates that Phy-X/PSD provides reliable estimates of shielding parameters, with uncertainties generally confined to within 5% for MAC and RPE, and up to ~ 15% for LAC and HVL due to experimental constraints. Thus, this study has certain limitations, primarily due to the restricted availability of sample material, which limited the range of experimentally tested energies. Moreover, the absence of high-energy gamma-ray facilities meant that validation beyond 1.5 MeV relied solely on computational predictions. Future work will address these challenges by scaling up sample production for broader experimental validation, especially at higher photon energies. Future studies will focus on evaluating the long-term stability and mechanical durability of these materials under radiation, as well as on investigating the effects of varying dopant concentrations on gamma and neutron shielding, which could further optimize these materials for practical applications. Compositional tuning and the development of hybrid or multilayered alloys can further enhance shielding efficiency across a wide range of photon energies. Moreover, integrating these materials with flexible or transparent matrices may expand.
