Self-Explaining Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Network Resource Allocation
Konrad Nowosadko, Franco Ruggeri, Ahmad Terra

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods that incorporate deep neural networks (DNN), though powerful, often lack transparency. Their black-box characteristic hinders interpretability and reduces trustworthiness, particularly in critical domains. To address this challenge in RL tasks, we propose a solution based on Self-Explaining Neural Networks (SENNs) along with explanation extraction methods to enhance interpretability while maintaining predictive accuracy. Our approach targets low-dimensionality problems to generate robust local and global explanations of the model’s behaviour. We evaluate the proposed method on the resource allocation problem in mobile networks, demonstrating that SENNs can constitute interpretable solutions with competitive performance. This work highlights the potential of SENNs to improve transparency and trust in AI-driven decision-making for low-dimensional tasks. Our approach strong performance on par with the existing state-of-the-art methods, while providing robust explanations.



I
Introduction

Interest in
Explainable Artificial Intelligance (XAI)
has been rapidly growing, facilitated by the need for transparency.
Although powerful,
Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)
models often operate as black boxes, making it difficult to interpret their decisions, leading to a lack of trust among stakeholders and consequently hindering their applicability. The idea behind the
XAI
extends onto the
Reinforcement Learning (RL)
through
Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL)
, which is especially important as
RL
systems operate in dynamic and often high-stakes environments
[
1
]
.

The most commonly used explanation methods for
DNNs
are model-agnostic, feature-attribution methods such as LIME or SHAP, which compute feature attributions representing the impact of each feature input on the output of the model
[
2
]
. The problem of induction from
XAI
perspective, which states that past observations generalise on the assumption of the uniformity of the environment, discussed in
[
2
]
, shows that causal dependencies between the decision and input can be
reliably inferred, given that inference data accurately reflects the deployment environment. Thus, the intrinsic
XAI
methods are more robust as they provide explanations derived from the model’s internal mechanism.

The analogically similar division into intrinsic and post-hoc extends into
XRL
solutions with similar implications. Many intrinsic
XRL
specific methods were proposed to date
[
1
]
. Furthermore, knowing that state-of-the-art
RL
algorithms are model-free, the black box aspect in those methods comes from utilising black box models such as
DNNs
. Thus, improving the explainability of models used within the
RL
system can improve the explainability of the whole system.
A method that combines high-capacity modelling and introduces intrinsic explainability is
Self-Explaining Neural Networks (SENNs)
. A type of neural network that maintains the high expressiveness of
DNNs
while offering an adjustable level of interpretability
[
3
]
.
SENNs
can be applied to the
RL
problem through
RL
algorithms, thereby constituting for the
XRL
solution.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Proposing a self-explaining reinforcement learning framework that incorporates
SENNs
into
Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)
, a state-of-the-art
RL
algorithm, to achieve trustworthy predictive results with reliable explanations to solve sequential decision problems.

•

Modifying the architecture of
SENNs
to improve expressivity of the network and explainability.

•

Proposing a methodology to extract global explanations from the local
SENNs
’ explanations.

.

III
Background

SENNs
consists of three modules: conceptizer, parametrizer, and aggregator. The conceptizer acts as a feature extractor, providing the concept vector, a dimensionality-reduced input data representation
[
3
]
. The parametrizer takes in the input and produces a relevance score vector for each output class that weights each element of the concept vector. Lastly, the aggregator implements a function that aggregates the concept vector and relevance score matrix. Authors of
SENNs
implement an aggregator as an inner product between relevance scores and concept vectors, resulting in the final output, expressed as:

\displaystyle f(x)=g(\theta(x),h(x))=\theta(x)^{T}h(x)

(1)

where,
\theta
are relevance scores matrix,
h
are concepts vector and
g
is aggregating function.

Loss expression for
SENN
is:

\mathcal{L}(x,y)=\mathcal{L}_{y}(f(x),y)+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(f(x))+\xi\mathcal{L}_{h}(x,\hat{x}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
is robustness loss, and
\lambda
is a robustness loss factor.
\mathcal{L}
is total loss,
\mathcal{L}_{y}
is classification loss,
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is conceptizer loss, which for the identity conceptizer is zero. Furthermore,
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
in Equation (
2
), guarantees that relevance scores behave linearly locally, imposing local stability, an idea central to the
SENNs
, a measure of ensuring consistency among explanations. It can be understood as the indifference of relevance score values to small changes in the concept vector.
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
is defined by:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(f(x))=\left\|\nabla_{x}f(x)-\theta(x)^{\top}J_{x}^{h}(x)\right\|\approx 0\text{,}

(3)

where
J^{h}_{x}(x)
is a Jacobian matrix of
h(x)
with respect to
x
.
For further discussion of loss terms, see
[
3
]
.

Local stability is evaluated using the Lipschitz constant:

L=\max_{x\in X}\frac{\|f(x)-f(x_{0})\|}{\|h(x)-h(x_{0})\|}

(4)

f(x)
is a
SENN
model,
h(x)
is a conceptizer and
L
is Lipschitz constant. Lipschitz constant measures the effect of robustness loss from Equation (
3
). Due to the number of data samples for which evaluation is accurate and representative of the model’s behaviour, calculating the exact Lipschitz constant is time-intensive; thus,
[
3
]
authors use stochastic gradient descent to estimate it.

The explainability of
SENNs
originates from the relevance scores. Each concept is weighted by the relevance scores, quantifying its contribution to the model’s final decision. They provide interpretability; however, it comes at the cost of performance as the local stability loss term introduced in Equation (
3
) is also a strong regularizer, worsening the model’s predictive performance
[
3
]
. The strength of local stability can be increased by increasing the robustness loss factor
\lambda
.

IV
Self-Explaining Reinforcement Learning

In this section, first we discuss modifications we introduce to the
SENNs
. Furthermore, we discuss two types of explanations provided by the
SENNs
: local explanations, which clarify individual predictions, and global explanations, which reveal the model’s overall behaviour and how to generate both.

IV-A

Model architecture

The SENN model we apply has two changes compared to its default form
[
3
]
. The concept explanations in
[
3
]
, which are clear for the visual data, become less explicit in non-visual domains.
Due to the low-dimensional character of the approached problem, we decided to utilise an identity conceptizer, as there is no need for feature extraction. We also introduced a trainable bias vector in the aggregator to measure the model’s systematic bias and improve the expressivity of the model. We choose to train the model using
PPO
algorithm, because of its proven effectiveness
[
9
]
.

These two modifications allow us to rewrite Equation (

f^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(\theta(x),x)=\theta(x)^{T}x+b,

(5)

where b stands for the bias term. We aim to obtain an explainable policy learned by the actor, leaving value prediction uninterpretable as it is not used during inference. Thus, the actor is implemented via
SENN
and the critic via

.

IV-B

Local explanations

SENNs
provides local intrinsic explanations, which are sourced from the parametrizer’s relevance score. Relevance scores weigh the input features, providing a numerical measure of the feature’s contribution towards the final decision, similar to linear models.
For each decision, the local explanation consists of (i) the relevance scores
\theta(x)
and (ii) the effect scores. The effect scores are defined as follows, inspired by linear models
[
11
]
:

E_{i}=\theta(x_{i})\odot x,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,m\},

where
\theta\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n}
and
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times 1}
,
n
is input size and
m
is number of output classes and
\odot
is the Hadamard product. The effects are relevance scores scaled by the feature values incorporating information of direct feature’s contribution.

Relevance scores reflect feature importance, while effects capture their direct contribution to the predictions, together they offer a more complete explanation of model’s decision. Furthermore, the lack of black-box conceptizers improves the clarity of the explanations, discarding the need for often dubious concept explanations
[
4
]
.

IV-C

Global explanations

While local explanations can clarify individual decisions made by the model, they fall short of providing a comprehensive view of the model’s overall behaviour, which is essential for understanding and predicting its responses. Global explanations are necessary to grasp the model’s general behaviour better. In this work, we propose two techniques of extrapolating global explanations based on the local explanations naturally provided by
SENNs
:
Effects Distributions (ED)
and clustering. We form global explanations using the local explanations extracted for a set of decisions
\mathcal{D}
.

In effect distributions, we plot the distribution of effect vectors
E^{(i)}
of all samples of some action
A
,
\{d_{t}\in\mathcal{D}|a^{(t)}=A\}
. Plotting the distribution and mean of effects corresponding to each feature gives insights into the expected effect values for the given action. It is a simple yet effective approach; however, averaging effect’s value density function introduces vagueness, thus we came up with clustering based method.

Clustering complements the effect distributions by providing a representative input samples for each action. We cluster the dataset
\mathcal{D}
using the K-means method
[
12
]
. The value of
k
in K-means is tuned depending on the cluster metrics such as Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index and the cluster’s purity of actions. The more homogeneous the cluster is, regarding the actions assigned to the data points in the cluster, the better the clustering from the standpoint of the explanations. Clustering is done on the dataset
D
. The set of centroids for action
a
is given by,

\mathbf{C}(a)=\left\{C_{i}\mid\arg\max_{j}M_{ij}=a,\,M_{i,a}>\tau\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}\right\}\\

(6)

where,
M
is contingency matrix counts datapoints

i
taking action
j
,
\tau
is a threshold,
n
is number of actions and
C_{i}
is centroid of
i
th cluster. We set
\tau
to
0.6
. Every centroid reflects a typical activation of the parametrizer expressing the types of attention it pays to the input for the given action. However, it comes with the cost of higher visual complexity.
Additionally, we can aggregate the clustering results to create importance scores that enhance explanations, see Subsection
V-C
. This allows us to validate our method by comparing it against state-of-the-art post-hoc techniques. Furthermore, the bias term introduced in the aggregator can be leveraged to generate explanations. Visualizing the bias would help understand the model’s inherent bias towards each action.

V
Experimental setup

V-A

Mobile-env

We apply our method to mobile-env, an environment that implements a resource allocation problem in mobile networks
[
13
]
.
It consists of user equipment (UE) and base stations (BSs). BSs, distributed over an area, provide mobile connection to the UEs. At the same time, UEs move randomly around the area at a constant speed, connecting to the BSs over a limited number of time steps, which determines the length of the simulation run, episode. The initial position of the UEs is sampled from the uniform distribution bounded by the simulation area. The position of the BSs is predetermined and remains static throughout the simulation run. For each run, the goal is to find a sequence of BSs and UEs assignments that would maximize the utility, a metric describing the quality of experience aggregated over all UEs. Finding the exact solution to this problem is a challenge for even the small instance; thus, existing solutions rely on simple heuristics or deep reinforcement learning
[
14
]
. Similar to existing DRL solutions, we train the model using PPO on the data provided by all UEs from the simulation. We insert the trained model into all UEs, which act independently of each other.
We approach a small instance of the problem with three UEs and three BSs.

V-B

Experiments

We train the
SENN
model using the
PPO
algorithm for 2 million timesteps across 4 parallel, randomly seeded simulations each consisting of 3 UEs, and then evaluate it on a 30,000 timesteps. This training–evaluation cycle is repeated 10 times with different random seeds to ensure statistically robust results. Local explanations were taken from arbitrarily chosen time step. For the effects distribution we gather effect values from
18000
time steps, while clustering, through K-means aggregates
36000
relevance scores into
k=14
different clusters. Bias vector is taken from one out of training cycles. Stability is measured through the Lipschitz constant estimate calculated from
800
randomly selected samples, using routine provided in
[
3
]
. Lastly, we perform an analysis of predictive performance, stability trade-off. We fixed unique seeds for trainings and evaluation for each
\lambda
value and compare them against the Lipschitz constant estimation and episodic reward.

V-C

Evaluation

Before evaluating the quality of the model’s explanations, verifying that the model achieves a satisfactory level of predictive accuracy is essential. Since mobile-env does not have ground truths that we could compare to our model performance, we compare it to the existing solutions. We utilize notions identified in
[
8
]
such as cognitive relief, causality, explicitness, correctness and stability for the local explanations and global explanations evaluation. Cognitive relief measures a cognitive dissonance between explanations and the user’s pre-existing beliefs. Predictability measures how well the user can predict the model’s behaviour. Casuality is the capacity of the method to show the relationship between input and output. Explicitness is the ability to provide immediate and understandable explanations. Stability is the consistency of providing similar outputs for similar inputs and for global explanations, we evaluate it by estimating the Lipschitz value, given by Equation (
4
). Furthermore, to have a more objective measure of the quality of global explanations, we extract from clustering and effects distribution input features’ attributions that could be compared with the attributions provided by the alternative methods
[
7
,
6
,
15
,
16
]
.

For the effect distribution, the mean value of each effect becomes an attribution; clustering attribution calculation is more complex. We average the centroid values of the clusters that belong to the given action. Action cluster assignment is based on the action content of each cluster; this is the reason behind obtaining relatively pure clusters.
The importance calculation of input features given set of centroids
C(i)
, see equation (
6
), is expressed by,

\mathbf{I}(a)=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{C}(a)|}\sum_{C_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(a)}C_{i}
, where
\mathbf{I}(a)
is importance vector for action
a
. Calculated attributions are compared with an output of post-hoc explanation methods such as GradSHAP, InputXGradient or DeepLift
[
7
,
6
,
15
]
.

VI-D

Performance and local stability

In SENNs, local stability regulates robustness of model’s explainability. Local stability can be improved by increasing the importance of robustness loss during training. However, robustness loss, Equation
3
is a strong regularizer that constrains the model’s flexibility. Consequently, in SENNs, the trade-off between explainability and predictive performance can be regulated with
\lambda
.
We also try to examine the effect of the robustness loss term from Equation (
3
) on the output of the model to confirm its indirect impact on the local stability of the model.
Figures
7
presents the relation between the robustness loss scaler and its influence on the episodic reward and Lipschitz estimate. The evaluation results for different robustness loss scalers seem to be dispersed. Nonetheless, the tendency of higher
\lambda
values to underperform in terms of episodic return can be observed. Showing that the regularization effect introduced with robustness loss can be detrimental towards the predictive performance. The training with robustness loss
0.001
seems to be an outlier as it outperforms the unconstrained model (
\lambda=0.0
) on episodic reward and achieves a Lipschitz constant estimate almost on par with model
\lambda=0.1
. Presented results show that local stability can be significantly improved by the introducing the robustness loss. In conclusion, the presented results demonstrate the existence of
\lambda
regulated explainability and accuracy trade-off in SENNs.

VII
Conclusions

Self-explaining reinforcement learning seems to achieve results that are on par with state-of-the-art methods on both approached problems, reaching almost a dense neural network performance and confidently outperforming the heuristic approach on resource allocation problems
[
14
]
. Bias introduced to the SENNs improved the model’s accuracy, demonstrating better performance aggregated over multiple runs. Furthermore, the bias vector can be visualized, providing insightful information about the model’s systematic bias, thus making it more explainable despite the slight decline in local stability.

Local explanations provided by the SENNs model seem to be accurate and explicit. Additionally, the lack of a DNN conceptizer removes the need for concept explanations, reducing the explanations’ explicitness. The proposed methods for extraction of global explanations performed reasonably well but did not achieve particularly strong results.

ED
is the simpler of the two methods and slightly more explicit. Looking at the violin plots delivers immediate information on the contribution of each feature and its distribution. However,
ED
does not retain as much information as clustering, a more versatile and complete method. All methods have a similar degree of cognitive relief.
Furthermore, clustering attributions seem to agree more with the results of existing methods than
ED
. Both methods provide insight into the model from a more general outlook.

In future work, the method of evaluating provided explanations, especially its subjective evaluation, should be more rigid, for instance, by conducting survey research.
SENNs
could be applied on a larger array of RL problems, especially high variance ones similar to mobile-env.
