‘They Absolutely Love It, or They're Terrified of It’. The Self‐Perceived Competence of Australian Speech‐Language Pathologists in Augmentative and Alternative Communication
Authors: Clancy Conlon, Robyn Preston, Barbra Zupan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAugmentative and alternative communication (AAC) requires speech‐language pathologists (SLPs) to have unique skills that may not be easily transferred from other practice areas. The introduction of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) also created a distinctive work context for Australian SLPs within the disability sector.AimTherefore, this study aimed to investigate the self‐perceived competence of Australian SLPs in AAC.Method and ProceduresThis study followed a sequential explanatory mixed methods approach. First, 203 SLPs completed a quantitative online survey. Next, 16 SLPs of those surveyed, participated in a one‐on‐one, semi‐structured interview over Zoom.Outcome and ResultsCompetency ratings were higher for those SLPs who received pre‐professional training in AAC, worked in a metropolitan area, had spent more time in the workforce and had a higher proportion of AAC users on their caseload. Competency ratings also differed by service delivery task; delivering therapy received the highest rating, while conducting assessments received the lowest. When asked about barriers and facilitators to clinical competence in AAC, four themes were identified (1) access to training and information, (2) AAC is a quickly evolving and changing field, (3) the impact of workplace context and (4) the attitudes of key stakeholders regarding AAC.Conclusions and ImplicationsSLPs in Australia report mixed levels of competence in AAC and experience many barriers to further developing their competence. Considerations should be made for how to reduce these barriers at a national level to ensure AAC users are accessing high quality supports.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on the subject Previous literature has highlighted that speech‐language pathologists (SLPs) internationally have varied levels of self‐perceived competence in augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). Most of this research has been conducted in the United States and has been quantitative. What this paper adds to the existing knowledge This mixed methods study highlights that the self‐perceived competence of Australian SLPs is impacted by university training, geographical location, time in the workforce, caseload and service delivery task. SLPs also feel that workplace context, attitudes of key stakeholders, technological advances and access to post‐professional training impacts their ability to further develop competence in AAC. What are the potential or actual clinical implications of this work? This study highlights the critical role of key stakeholders such as university staff, funding bodies and employers in the development of clinical competence in AAC for SLPs.

Introduction

The concept of competence is widely used across the health sector, yet remains inconsistently defined. In medicine, competence has been described as the integration of knowledge, skills, communication, values and reflection within clinical practice (Epstein and Hundert 2002). Nursing and allied health literature similarly conceptualises competence as multidimensional, encompassing clinical skills, professional standards, judgement, experience and confidence, alongside broader organisation and personal factors (Axley 2008; Valizadeh et al. 2019; Walker et al. 2013). These varied conceptualisations reflect the complexity and context‐specific nature of competence, posing challenges for its application to speech pathology.

Despite its centrality to professional practice, the concept of competence and competency development has received relatively limited attention in the international speech pathology literature. Most of what is currently understood about competence development in the profession stems from the seminal work of Sue McAllister (McAllister 2006) and subsequent publications (McAllister et al. 2010, McAllister et al. 2011), which focus primarily on competency growth in speech‐language pathology (SLP) students (e.g. Hill et al. 2014; Sheepway et al. 2014). This body of work informed the development of the Competency‐Based Occupational Standards (CBOS) (Speech Pathology Australia 2017), which were later superseded by the Professional Standards in Australia (Speech Pathology Australia 2020b). These documents outline the knowledge, skills, and values SLPs should possess to be a competent clinician in the Australian context and adopt a performance‐based perspective, defining competence in terms of observable professional actions. Importantly, competence is conceptualised not as a binary state but as a dynamic, multidimensional construct that evolves along a continuum shaped by knowledge, skills and attitudes (McAllister et al. 2011). While these standards aim to balance the need for profession‐wide consistency with the flexibility to accommodate diverse practice areas (Ferguson 2006), they do not provide detailed guidance on how competence is enacted within specific domains of practice. Furthermore, performance‐based standards do not necessarily capture clinicians’ self‐perceived competence or confidence, nor the complex relationship between knowledge acquisition, skill enactment and professional judgement. As a result, the generalised nature of these frameworks may limit their applicability to certain areas such as augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), where unique knowledge and skills are required (Conlon et al. 2024).

AAC refers to all forms of communication used in addition to or in place of speech. AAC includes unaided systems, such as gestures and keyword sign, and aided systems, which may be low‐tech (e.g. communication boards) or high‐tech (e.g. speech‐generating devices) (Beukelman and Light 2020). AAC has been recognised globally as within an SLPs scope of practice (e.g. American Speech‐Language‐Hearing Association 2020; Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists 2021; Speech Pathology Australia 2020b). Despite this, a scoping review conducted recently by Conlon et al. (2024) highlighted that SLPs report mixed levels of competence in AAC globally. No studies from Australia were identified in this scoping review, as prior Australian research has focused on related factors such as knowledge (Balandin and Iacono 1998) or perceptions/attitudes (Conlon et al. 2025; Iacono and Cameron 2009).

Similar to broader healthcare literature, competence in AAC has been conceptualised as a complex interaction between knowledge, skills, confidence and values (Axley 2008; Conlon et al. 2024; Epstein and Hundert 2002). Notably, this interaction is not linear. For instance, high levels of knowledge do not necessarily translate to clinical skill, and confidence alone does not equate to competence, particularly in complex areas of practice such as AAC, where multiple contextual and experiential factors influence knowledge, confidence and attitudes (Barman et al. 2023; Conlon et al. 2025; Conlon and Zupan 2024; Gohsman and Johnson 2023; Johnson and Gohsman 2023; Sanders et al. 2021). These factors include university training in addition to practicum experiences, post‐graduate training, workplace, clinician age, years in workforce, caseload and geographic location (Barman et al. 2023; Biggs et al. 2022; Conlon and Zupan 2024; Johnson and Gohsman 2023; King 1998; Sanders et al. 2021; Simpson et al. 1998; Ward et al. 2023).

While these factors, which influence knowledge, confidence, attitudes, and aspects of AAC practice have been identified, it cannot be assumed that these factors translate directly into clinical competence. Much of the existing literature examining AAC‐related competence for qualified SLPs has been conducted in the United States and has relied predominantly on quantitative survey methodologies (e.g. King 1998; Kovacs 2021; Marvin et al. 2003; Simpson et al. 1998). The applicability of these findings to Australian SLPs therefore remains uncertain, given differences in university training pathways, service delivery models and national policy contexts that shape AAC practice. Therefore, this mixed methods research project aimed to answer three questions: What is the self‐perceived competence of Australian SLPs in AAC?What factors impact the self‐perceived competence of Australian SLPs in AAC?What are the barriers and facilitators to Australian SLPs developing competence in AAC?

Method

Research Design

A mixed methods sequential explanatory approach was used as described by Creswell and Plano Clark (2018). This approach was selected to provide both breadth and depth in the exploration of competence in AAC among SLPs (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). Consistent with a sequential explanatory design, interview data were used to extend, contextualise and further explain the survey findings (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018; Tashakkori et al. 2021). A survey was created as part of a broader research project that aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitudes, confidence, competence, and training experiences of SLPs in AAC (Conlon and Zupan 2024; Conlon et al. 2025). The present study focuses on a subset of this data, along with semi‐structured interviews. A detailed description of this method has been provided in the companion paper to this study, which focused on training in AAC (Conlon et al. 2025). An overview of the method and any components specific to this study has been provided subsequently. This study was approved by the CQUniversity Human Research Ethics Committee (reference number 23676).

Positionality

Given the inclusion of qualitative methods within this mixed‐methods study, consideration of the author positionality was undertaken to acknowledge the interpretive nature of qualitative analysis (Saldana 2021). At the time of the research, Author 1 was a qualified and registered SLP and a doctoral candidate, trained in Australia. Their professional roles included teaching AAC to undergraduate SLP students within an Australian university program, alongside ongoing clinical work with children with disabilities, including individuals who use AAC. Author 2 had extensive expertise in qualitative research within public health and entered the project without prior professional experience in the field of AAC, contributing an external analytical perspective. Author 3 was a qualified and registered SLP, trained in Canada and practising in Australia, with substantial experience in quantitative and mixed‐methods research. Collectively, these complementary backgrounds informed the analytic process and supported reflexive consideration of assumptions brought to data interpretation.

Participants

Participants were Australian‐trained SLPs practising in Australia with paediatric or mixed caseloads and eligible for registration with Speech Pathology Australia. Internationally trained clinicians were excluded due to the study's focus on Australian training pathways.

Survey Design and Distribution

The survey was designed by the first author and reviewed by the second and third authors. The survey was piloted by two SLPs and one SLP student; no changes were identified as necessary. The survey questions relevant to this study consisted of 11 demographic questions, 5 Likert Scale statements pertaining to service delivery competence (4‐point scale), 5 Likert Scale statements pertaining to AAC system competence (4‐point scale) (see Supporting Information for survey questions) and 8 Likert scale statements pertaining to pre‐professional training (4‐point scale) (see Conlon et al. 2025). The survey was disseminated online via Qualtrics using random (purposive) sampling to target eligible participants and was open from the 25 September 2022 to 10 November 2022.

Survey Data Analysis

All survey data were exported into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS). Data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Quantitative analyses were selected to align with the research questions, with descriptive statistics used to address Research Question 1 by characterising patterns of competence, and inferential statistics used to address Research Question 2 by examining relationships and/or differences between variables. Likert Scale statements were collapsed into two domains: AAC service delivery competence and AAC system competence; a third domain from part one of this study ‘pre‐professional training adequacy’ was also included in analysis (Conlon et al. 2025) made up of eight Likert Scale statements. The pre‐professional training adequacy domain was included in the current analysis to allow examination of the relationship between perceived adequacy of pre‐professional training and reported competence in AAC, as addressed in Research Question 2. These domain scores formed the basis of statistical analyses for this study. The internal consistency of each domain was tested using Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient. An alpha above 0.70 was calculated for pre‐professional training adequacy (α = 0.960), AAC service delivery competence (α = 0.852) and AAC system competence (α = 0.830), indicating good internal consistency and reliability for each domain (Morgan et al. 2020). Parametric testing included paired t‐tests, Pearson correlations and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Semi‐Structured Interview Design and Implementation

Upon completion of the survey, participants were asked if they consented to participate in a semi‐structured interview. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted online via Zoom until data saturation was reached (n = 16). The interview protocol consisted of one open‐ended question pertaining to work experiences and six questions pertaining to competency.

Data Analysis of Semi‐Structured Interviews

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using abductive analysis (Palmer and Coe 2020). Abductive analysis was used to enable iterative movement between quantitative survey findings and qualitative interview data, supporting both explanatory depth and openness to unexpected phenomena. This approach facilitates the development and refinement of theoretical insights through a flexible interaction between data and existing knowledge (Palmer and Coe 2020).

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using NVivo. Analysis followed established qualitative procedures adapted for a sequential explanatory design (Creswell 2014). Initial familiarisation was supported through reflexive memo‐writing following each interview (Saldaña 2011). To establish analytic consistency, Authors 1 and 2 independently coded three interviews, compared coding decisions, and jointly agreed on preliminary codes informed by both qualitative data and relevant quantitative findings. Author 1 subsequently coded the remaining interviews, with codes and themes iteratively refined as analysis progressed. Codes were visually organised and depicted in themes for each of the following concepts: (1) self‐perceived competence, (2) factors impacting competence and (3) barriers and facilitators to competence using NVivo visualisation tools, with attention to convergence, divergence, and outlier cases. As question three (pertaining to barriers and facilitators) was a qualitative only question, these codes/themes were depicted via a mind map (see Supporting Information), which were reviewed by Authors 2 and 3. Theme development was reviewed and confirmed through discussion among the research team. Data collection and analysis continued until sufficient analytic depth and conceptual coherence were achieved across research questions.

Triangulation

In this sequential explanatory mixed‐methods design, qualitative findings were used to elaborate on and interpret quantitative results through triangulation (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018). Triangulation involved an iterative process in which Author 1 examined quantitative and qualitative data for each research question, identified points of convergence, divergence, and complementarity, and determined appropriate modes of integration (e.g. narrative or joint display). Resulting interpretations were reviewed and refined through team discussion to ensure they accurately represented the data.

Question 1: What Is the Self‐Perceived Competence of Australian SLPs in AAC?

AAC Service Delivery

SLPs were asked to rate their competence in delivering AAC services on a scale from one to four (1 = not competent, 2 = somewhat competent, 3 = competent, 4 = very competent). Ratings of competence were low across all services. The highest competency rating was found for delivering therapy (M = 2.87, SD = 0.83); conducting assessments received the lowest rating (M = 2.50, SD = 0.93) (see Table 2). Interview participants felt that the assessment process was particularly difficult in AAC due to the need to access AAC resources and complete feature matching, ‘… the therapy component I've always been relatively confident with, it's just that assessment and having the resources to feature match’ (P7). In comparison, SLPs felt they could use transferable skills when completing therapy, ‘… it's language intervention, just by a different name’ (P4).

Question 2: What Factors Impact the Self‐Perceived Competence of Australian SLPs in AAC?

The following section reports the inferential statistical analyses conducted to address Research Question 2, followed by qualitative findings that further explain and contextualise these results. For all quantitative analyses, AAC competence domain scores were treated as the dependent variables. Independent variables included geographical location, AAC caseload, and years of professional experience and pre‐professional training adequacy. All statistical assumptions required for the inferential tests were assessed and met.

Geographical Location

Geographical location was found to impact SLPs’ ratings of competence in AAC systems, F(1, 199) = 14.33, p = <0.001, and delivering AAC services, F(1, 198) = 15.09, p = <0.001, with SLPs living in metropolitan areas reporting higher levels of competence in comparison to those living in rural/remote areas. Interview participants felt those living in rural/remote areas had more barriers to developing competence, including access to professional development, access to AAC equipment suppliers, as well as being a sole clinician with more of a generalist role. Participant 12 described their experience as a rural clinician trying to support AAC users, ‘In X, there aren't any suppliers, our suppliers are in Melbourne and Adelaide…that's a really long way away and as much as they are super super helpful, Zoom isn't amazing… unless you have a tech savvy brain to you, that's really hard’.

AAC Caseload

The proportion of AAC users on an SLP's caseload was found to significantly impact their ratings of competence in AAC systems, F(4, 188) = 28.58, p = <0.001, and delivering AAC services, F(4, 189) = 29.66, p = <0.001, with higher ratings of competence for those SLPs with a higher proportion of AAC users on their caseload. There was one exception to this—service delivery competence was lower for SLPs with 81%–100% of users on their caseload (M = 3.22) in comparison to those with 61%–80% of AAC users on their caseload (M = 3.28) (see Figure 1).

Service delivery competence by proportion of AAC users on caseload. Participants were asked to rate their competence on a scale of one to four (1 = not competent, 2 = somewhat competent, 3 = competent, 4 = very competent).

Interview participants felt that the clients on their caseload heavily influenced their competence as it impacted their exposure to AAC systems as well as their training needs, ‘I had quite a number of kids that needed AAC and I had to upskill very quickly and in a lot of areas of AAC’ (P14). Those SLPs with less AAC users on their caseload felt it was difficult to balance training in AAC with training needed in other areas, such as Participant 13, ‘I think a lot of people might only have one or two AAC users on their caseload… do they upskill in that area when they have… a large proportion of people that are not using AAC’. Overall, SLPs felt more AAC users on their caseload increased competence as it gave them ‘… the opportunity to put it into practice…’ (P15).

Years of Experience

An SLP's years’ of experience was found to impact their ratings of competence in AAC systems, F(3, 197) = 4.43, p = 0.005, and delivery of AAC services, F(3, 198) = 6.60, p = <0.001. There was a significant difference in service delivery competence ratings for those SLPs with less than five years of experience in comparison to those with 6 to 10 years of experience (p = 0.005) and 11 to 15 years of experience (p = 0.004), but not those with more than 15 years of experience (see Figure 2).

Service delivery competence by years’ of experience as an SLP. Participants were asked to rate their competence on a scale of one to four (1 = not competent, 2 = somewhat competent, 3 = competent, 4 = very competent).

Interview participants felt that experience in AAC supported their competence as it increased their exposure to different AAC systems, ‘Experience—definitely being able to like see a lot of different clients using different systems…’ (P8). Hands on experience in AAC also allowed SLPs to hone their clinical skills, as shared by Participant 15, ‘Experience definitely made a big difference, like the more you use something and particularly if you experience success with it… the more you can tweak your performance…’. However, participants also noted, that work experience as an SLP doesn't necessarily equate to experience in AAC, ‘I think it's really dependant on opportunity… depending on the type of caseload that people have…they may or may not have that opportunity and experience’ (P13).

Pre‐Professional Training

SLPs were asked to rate their pre‐professional training in AAC, and these results have been reported in the companion paper to this study (Conlon et al. 2025). This study considered those results in relation to ratings of competence. Pearson correlations indicated a strong positive correlation between ratings of pre‐professional training and AAC system competence [r(199) = 0.544, p = <0.001] as well as service delivery competence [r(200) = 0.523, p = <0.001]. Interview participants primarily felt that their pre‐professional training did not prepare them to support AAC users once entering the workforce, ‘I think the competence that I came out of uni[university] with was extremely basic’ (P2). Part 1, the companion paper to this study, further describes the training SLPs felt SLP students should receive in AAC at university to better support competence in this practice area (Conlon et al. 2025).

Question 3: What Are the Barriers and Facilitators to Australian SLPs Developing Competence in AAC?

When discussing barriers and facilitators to developing competence in AAC, four main themes were identified: (1) access to training and information about AAC; (2) the attitudes of key stakeholders regarding AAC; (3) the impact of workplace context and (4) AAC is a quickly evolving and changing field.

Access to Training and Information

SLPs felt that training acted as a facilitator to developing clinical competence, however many barriers to accessing training were identified, including time and cost. While most SLPs felt that there are now many post‐professional training opportunities in AAC, such as those provided by the Australian Group Supporting Communication Inclusion (AGOSCI) and AAC equipment suppliers (such as Liberator), the caveat highlighted was AAC is just one of many areas that SLPs require training in, ‘There is lots of PD available, but obviously speechies [SLPs] have to be very selective about where they put their time and their money when doing PDs…’ (P5). This was a particularity pertinent consideration for those SLPs who only see AAC users for a small portion of their caseload as described by Participant 5, ‘… if the option is um you know, I spend $500 on a training that…we'll learn how to use one device that may not be applicable to all their clients versus… feeding therapy that's going to apply to 10 of their clients’. Although cost and time were perceived as barriers to all SLPs, the impact appeared substantial for those located in rural areas with online training creating a reprieve, ‘…being rural, the travel to professional development opportunities, you know, often they're in the city. I must say COVID has been fantastic for that, like so many more opportunities to do online training’ (P15).

The Attitudes of Key Stakeholders Regarding AAC

Interview participants felt that the attitudes of key stakeholders, including parents, teachers and other SLPs can impact the development of clinical competence in AAC. Some SLPs felt that parents could make them second‐guess their own clinical knowledge and capabilities, particularly when parents placed emphasis on verbal communication, ‘If you're looking to introduce AAC and a parents, like, [says] “it's going to stop them talking”, I think that can make a bit of a barrier and go, oh okay, maybe we just look at doing something else…’ (P2). Participant 3 emphasised it is particularly difficult in the current workforce model ‘because it's client‐driven, client choice and control kind of model’. This means that SLPs need to be confident in their own clinical knowledge and skills in AAC to engage in education and capacity building activities, ‘… you're dealing with having to try and educate parents that we're going to need a robust, you know AAC system for this client and they just want them to talk verbally, and it's it's such a constant battle. So, you need to know your stuff if you're going to provide this AAC’ (P9). SLPs felt they faced similar barriers when collaborating with teachers, ‘… teachers as well, a lot of them are like [say], “oh no, they can talk” and so I think sometimes it de‐values AAC and you're like, oh, maybe there isn't such a need…’ (P2).

Interview participants also highlighted that the attitudes of SLPs can impact on their own competence, ‘There's definitely a relationship between attitude and competence. In that yeah, the speechies who have that attitude of being interested and wanting to learn about it, have been the speechies who I've worked with, who've sought … more information about it’ (P3). Competence of those around them was also identified as having influence, ‘…the opinions of the other speechies around them in regards to not liking specific programs…’ (P9). The impact of negative attitudes and feelings unfortunately, leads SLPs to avoid working with AAC users, ‘So I think there's a real aversion to doing AAC… speech pathologists join the team and want to refer to someone else for AAC… everyone knows there's a whole lot of training and it's a really complex area’ (P7). These feelings also seemed to perpetuate the idea that AAC is a ‘specialty’ area, ‘I feel a lot of speechies are very anxious about AAC and see it as a very specialised area’ (P5).

The Impact of Workplace Context

SLPs heavily emphasised the impact of their workplace on developing competence due to factors such as time, caseload, key performance indicators (KPIs), funding models, access to resources, the service model and access to a team.

Service Model, Caseload, KPIs and Funding

The service model often dictates a therapist's role, which SLPs felt impacted their ability to carry out AAC‐related tasks. Participant 11 described their experience working in a school, ‘… we're very much a visitor… an outsider… I know as an early career therapist it was really hard to be a voice in the wilderness…’. The service model also impacted SLPs exposure to working with clients with disabilities, Participant 14 described the limitations of working in the health system, ‘… all you're doing is assessing and without disability services anymore, you're not getting those really complex kids, like they're in private practice now’. Those SLPs working in private practice highlighted the impact of the NDIS on service delivery, including potential financial incentives, ‘I think with the NDIS now the workforce is shifted… there's more clients with funding, so that's where the money is’ (P7). While SLPs commented positively on the funding now available to people with disabilities, SLPs felt this shift has resulted in two problems on the opposite ends of a spectrum. One end of the spectrum includes people providing AAC services despite a lack of competency, ‘… there's lots of cowboys [someone who ignores rules] out there in the NDIS system and lots of people who shouldn't be prescribing devices are prescribing devices…’ (P11). On the other end of the spectrum, there are practices refusing services to AAC users due to their perceived complexity, ‘… parents being on really long waiting lists and then they'd get to the top of the waiting list and then they'd [the service] say, “oh, you're actually too complex for us”’ (P2).

Time, Resources, and Access to a Team

SLPs strongly believed the NDIS impacts the time provided to develop their competence due to the privatisation of therapy, ‘time, funding [are barriers]… in an NDIS kind of space where time is money and billable hours are KPIs’ (P3). Participant 13 explained how these factors have a ripple effect, ‘…in the NDIS world… everybody works very siloed… there's a much bigger push on billable hours, just go and see your client and come back, not working alongside other practitioners…AAC users are often really complex clients and having that chance to learn alongside somebody else [is a facilitator].’ Finally, SLPs felt that access to resources was more important in AAC than other practice areas, ‘…needing resources is much more important for AAC because if you're unresourced [sic], you're left with gesture and sign… other aspects of speech therapy, we can kind of do it with very little’ (P16). However, AAC resources can be costly, both financially and in relation to a therapists’ time, which SLPs described as a barrier across different workplaces. A lack of resources was linked to competence due to the hands‐on nature of AAC, which requires familiarisation and practice, ‘I feel like I could learn quickly with exposure and practice’ (P10). In this aspect, SLPs felt that equipment suppliers acted as facilitators if they were able to secure an equipment loan/trial, although this was variable between participant experiences.

AAC Is a Quickly Evolving and Changing Field

Participants felt that there was an overwhelming amount of information to learn to become competent in AAC, ‘I still think there's a huge amount to learn, there is a lot I don't know because… we work in a complex needs practice’ (P4). This challenge was exacerbated by the fact that AAC practices are constantly changing, which makes it hard to maintain up‐to‐date knowledge. SLPs also felt that technology has heavily influenced the AAC landscape, which can pose a barrier to maintaining competence, ‘I think because it's so technical… you can't just do a little bit of training and be across it. It's ongoing because the technology changes… there are so many options now, which is great, but it is a whole lot more you have to be across and aware of’ (P11). Participant 11 felt that the influx of high‐tech AAC systems available has created an impossible task for SLPs, ‘there was this absolutely massive explosion of devices… I'm not fluent with them because everything's in different places and my brain goes [says] oh my gosh, where is it in this one? … I can't keep my thoughts in order… learning to master all the electronic systems has been the hardest part and I don't think I will ever master them all’. This means to be competent in AAC, SLPs needs to be able to continuously reflect and autonomously seek out new information to ensure their knowledge and skills are up to date because ‘… it always tends to come back to the motivation factors and the willingness to learn’ (P12).

Discussion

The following section aims to integrate and triangulate the findings from both the quantitative survey data and qualitative interview responses to provide a comprehensive understanding of SLPs self‐perceived competence in AAC. By examining patterns across these data sources, the discussion will contextualise the results within existing literature, explore the implications for clinical practice and training, and identify systemic factors that influence competence development. This approach allows for a more nuanced interpretation of the findings and highlights areas for future research and policy consideration.

What Is the Self‐Perceived Competence of SLPs in AAC and What Factors Impact Competence?

Survey results demonstrated that SLPs rated themselves as somewhat competent across all service delivery tasks and types of AAC systems. However, competence varied between SLPs with mean values for AAC service delivery competence and AAC system competence ranging from one (not competent) to four (very competent). As described by interview Participant 1, ‘… they [SLPs] absolutely love it, or they're terrified of it…’. This variance in general AAC competence is commensurate with the literature globally (Conlon et al. 2024). However, while prior research has also identified discrepancies in competence between service delivery tasks, most research has made comparisons between assessment and intervention only (Chua and Gorgon 2018; Kovacs 2021) or focused on confidence or self‐efficacy rather than competence (Biggs et al. 2022; Burke et al. 2002). To better understand these discrepancies, the following sections examine competence across specific service delivery tasks and AAC system types, drawing on both survey data and interview insights.

Service Delivery

SLPs rated their competence highest for intervention and lowest for assessment which is consistent with recent findings in the United States (Kovacs 2021). Interview participants in this study described that intervention for AAC users could utilise knowledge transfer from principles of language intervention. In comparison, there are aspects of the assessment process unique to AAC users, such as feature matching and device prescription, which may not be easily supported by knowledge transfer. As competence was positively correlated with pre‐professional training, lower ratings for assessment competence could relate to the content covered in AAC at universities. In the United States, 43.8% of programs reported that the majority of class time was spent covering intervention in comparison to 42.2% that spent more time on assessment (Sauerwein and Burris 2022). While these ratings were similar, the focus of Australian university programs on AAC content is currently unknown. Australian programs could be focusing more on therapy than assessment however, this requires further investigation.

Another consideration is available research evidence. There is currently ample, recent evidence describing intervention approaches for AAC users, including systematic and scoping reviews (Dada et al. 2021; Langarika‐Rocafort et al. 2021; Muttiah et al. 2022), which could guide SLPs in their decision‐making during intervention planning. Although there are now a variety of resources available to support the assessment of AAC users, such as the SETT framework (Zabala 2005), Dynamic AAC Goals Grid—3rd Edition (Tobii Dynavox 2024) and Communication Matrix (Rowland and Fried‐Oken 2010), the research describing assessment processes for AAC users is not as extensive. Furthermore, SLPs would need to be aware of these assessments, where to find them and how to use them, which creates a barrier as described by Participant 16, ‘I just didn't really know what I didn't know’.

AAC Systems

Although SLPs reported themselves as most competent in low/light technology systems, there was minimal variation between system ratings overall (range = 2.47–2.95). Interestingly, SLPs rated themselves as more competent in high‐technology systems (M = 2.80) than in unaided systems (M = 2.74). This represents a shift in skillset when compared to Australian data from 1998, where SLPs reported they had the most knowledge of unaided AAC, followed by low‐technology AAC and high‐technology AAC (Balandin and Iacono 1998). This could be due to the ‘… absolutely massive explosion of devices’ (P11) that has occurred as a result of advances in technology (McNaughton and Light 2013).

Pre‐Professional Training

Ratings of pre‐professional training were positively correlated with self‐perceived competence in AAC service delivery (i.e. more pre‐professional training equates to more competent clinicians in the workforce). This is an important finding highlighting the importance of university education for SLPs and the long‐term impact it can have on competence throughout their professional careers. For a full discussion on pre‐professional training, please see the companion paper to this study (Conlon et al. 2025).

Experience and Caseload

Survey results indicated a relationship between AAC caseload and years of experience as an SLP with self‐perceived competence. These results are consistent with international literature (Conlon et al. 2024). In the current study, competency increased as an SLP's caseload of AAC users increased, with one exception—SLPs with caseloads comprised of 81%–100% AAC users reported lower mean service delivery competency scores (M = 3.22) in comparison to those with caseloads of 61%–80% AAC users (M = 3.28). This anomaly could be due to sample bias; SLPs with caseloads of 81%–100% of AAC users only made up 4.9% of the sample (n = 10). Alternatively, these SLPs may have a better understanding of the sheer volume of knowledge and skills required to service a diverse caseload of AAC users and therefore have a differing perception of competence in AAC. Participant 3 described how their perception of competency has changed with experience in AAC, ‘[I've] gone through these different learning phases of thinking that competence needs to be proficiency with a particular device, and then proficiency with a range of devices… then proficiency supporting language… proficiency with communication partners, it just keeps growing and changing as I learn more’.

SLPs who have AAC users for the majority of their caseload reported higher competency ratings, reinforcing the idea that AAC users should be serviced by clinicians with relevant experience. This raises the question of whether clinicians should be encouraged to specialise in AAC or refer clients to those with specialised knowledge. While countries such as the United States offer post‐graduate qualifications and certifications in assistive technology (Rehabilitation Engineering and Assistive Technology Society of North America 2024) and supports the notion of speciality areas of practice (American Speech‐Language‐Hearing Association 1997), at the time of writing this paper, Australia does not have these initiatives (Speech Pathology Australia 2021). This absence has prompted ongoing debate regarding the feasibility and necessity of specialisation within the Australian context. Some interview participants supported a specialisation approach, ‘… we need to get better referring on to specialists like in the UK’ (P4), while others felt competent in AAC despite being in a generalist role, ‘I feel relatively competent, but without being a specialist’ (P12). Research suggests that specialisations may enhance service quality, for example, Dietz et al. (2012) identified that SLPs who specialised in AAC followed a more holistic and person‐centred assessment in comparison to generalist SLPs. Furthermore, SLPs in the United States who identified as a specialist reported higher self‐confidence in AAC (Sanders et al. 2021). However, implementing specialisations is complex and may introduce challenges, including regulatory requirements and additional costs, which were already cited as barriers to AAC practice. Moreover, SLPs who work in regional, rural, and remote areas in Australia often work as generalists to meet diverse community needs (Millar et al. 2025) and introducing specialisation could inadvertently reduce access to AAC services in these regions.

Geographical Location

In this survey, those SLPs living in rural/remote areas reported lower competence levels in AAC systems and service delivery than those living in metropolitan areas; they also indicated AAC users form a lower proportion of their caseload. Geographical location is a particularly pertinent factor in Australia given that 28% of Australians live in regional, rural or remote areas (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023) and the need for paediatric SLP services is generally higher in these areas (McCormack and Verdon 2015). While AAC is the focus for this study, it is possible SLPs in these areas experience lower self‐perceived competency in other practice areas as well due to external barriers such as generalist roles (O'Toole et al. 2010), difficulties accessing professional development (O'Toole et al. 2010), staff retention issues (Russell et al. 2017) and poor public awareness of a SLP's role (Janes et al. 2021). Clinical competency for those SLPs living in regional, rural, and remote Australia is a complex topic that requires further investigation across all areas of practice.

What Are the Barriers and Facilitators to SLPs Developing Competence in AAC?

SLPs reported experiencing a range of barriers to developing their competence in AAC, including accessing training and information, staying up‐to‐date with frequent changes in the field, workplace complexities, and the attitudes of stakeholders. While reducing all of the barriers discussed in this study seems insurmountable, there were two key areas identified by participants where systemic change would have considerable influence. These are each discussed below.

Fair, Equitable and Transparent Financial Remunerations

SLPs reported a range of workplace restrictions which impact their ability to develop competence. While these were widespread (training, resources, time, KPIs, etc.), they all have the same root cause—financial viability. The introduction of the NDIS privatised the disability sector, whereby all organisations run a fee‐for‐service model based on the NDIS price guide (National Disability Insurance Agency 2023b). At this current time, the price for therapy services has not increased since 2019 (National Disability Insurance Agency 2023a). While this price guide does allow for therapists to bill for client facing and administration (non‐face to face) time associated with delivering services, there seems to be a lack of stakeholder awareness of the time taken to deliver high quality therapy services, particularly for those clients with complex needs. Recently, allied health professionals in Australia have come under media fire regarding ‘price gauging’ NDIS participants (Daniel 2023). In contrast, participants within this study, felt that they either spent a considerable amount of non‐billed time supporting AAC users (i.e. the therapists’ personal time) or they were not given the time by their employer (due to billing restrictions) and therefore provided a less comprehensive service.

This friction between consumers and therapists was highlighted by Foley et al. (2025) who reported consumers felt like dollar signs; in contrast, therapists and managers felt they were constantly juggling the fiscal aspects of therapy in opposition to their desire for providing quality care. This finding from Foley et al. (2025) highlights a disconnect between what stakeholders perceive to be the time taken to deliver a comprehensive service and the actual time taken, which impacts financial viability for organisations, SLP workloads and quality of service for AAC users (i.e. everybody loses out). Advocating for fair remuneration from the NDIS and providing organisations with a guide to fair costing would require a better understanding of the time currently taken to deliver AAC supports which is highly variable. For example, a new graduate will take longer than an experienced SLP to write an AAC prescription, therefore, what is a fair amount of time to bill? An investigation into the provision of AAC supports in Australia, including face‐to‐face hours, administration hours and billing practices is required.

Development of Behavioural Objectives to Support Self‐Assessment of AAC Competence

Most SLPs struggled to keep up with the technological advances within the field of AAC, which impacted their self‐perception of competence, particularly in the face of providing AAC training to other stakeholders. Since AAC is a technology reliant field where advances are made on a daily basis, it would be nearly impossible for someone to know every AAC option without careful thought or consideration. However, it should be possible to identify a core set of competencies or behaviours expected of an Australian SLP which would constitute competent delivery of AAC services. This would support SLPs to accurately reflect on their clinical competencies to identify transferable skills and inform future professional development needs. This may result in more targeted training, which could have positive cost implications. Conlon et al. (2024) suggests that competence in AAC is achieved through integration of knowledge and skills specific to AAC layered with confidence and an SLPs beliefs and values. While this definition provides a foundation, research translation would require a specific set of behavioural objectives which SLPs could use to reflect on their own clinical knowledge, skills and attributes and extend upon these where required. Australia is well‐placed for the creation of such a resource given that the CBOS were based on behavioural objectives (Speech Pathology Australia 2017) and the Augmentative and Alternative Communication Guideline outlines the expertise Australian SLPs should have in AAC (Speech Pathology Australia 2020a).

Limitations

At the time of this survey, no definition of clinical competence specific to AAC was available. Therefore, within the survey, SLPs were asked to rate their self‐perceived competence however, competence was not defined. This may have impacted how each participant interpreted the question if they had differing perceptions on what competence in AAC means. Post‐professional training has been found to have a relationship with clinical confidence (Conlon et al. 2024) however, the relationship between competence and post‐professional training was not investigated as part of this study. Further research into the frequency and effectiveness of AAC training in Australia as it pertains to clinical competence is needed.

Conclusion

This study examined Australian SLPs self‐perceived competence in AAC, revealing moderate overall ratings across service delivery tasks and AAC system types. Competence was highest in intervention and lowest in assessment, suggesting that knowledge transfer from general language therapy may support intervention more readily than the specialised processes required for AAC assessment. SLPs reported greater competence in high‐tech AAC systems than unaided systems, reflecting a shift in clinical exposure and technological advancement. Pre‐professional training was positively correlated with competence, reinforcing the importance of AAC education at the university level. Experience and AAC caseload also influenced competence, with higher ratings among clinicians with greater exposure to AAC users. Geographical location emerged as a significant factor, with SLPs in rural and remote areas reporting lower competence and fewer AAC users on their caseloads. These findings highlight the need for targeted training, improved access to AAC resources, and consideration of systemic factors such as workforce distribution and service models. Addressing these areas is essential to ensure that all AAC users receive support from clinicians who are competent and equipped with the resources to meet their communication needs.
