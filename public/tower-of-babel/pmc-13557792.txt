Pilot Study: Facial Trauma Reconstruction Outcomes: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmed Nadeem-Tariq, Jonnby La Guardia, Evan Childers, Richard Baynosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Facial trauma reconstruction involves a range of surgical approaches aimed at restoring function and aesthetics following injury. Understanding the prevalence of complications and treatment outcomes is crucial for enhancing patient care, even as surgical techniques continue to advance. We aimed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and outcomes of various surgical interventions for facial trauma reconstruction.Methods:PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched through December 1, 2024, for studies reporting outcomes after surgical facial trauma reconstruction in adults. Quantitative meta-analysis was restricted to orbital fracture studies reporting diplopia and/or enophthalmos, using a random-effects model. Other outcomes were synthesized narratively due to clinical heterogeneity.Results:A total of 1218 records were identified; 9 studies met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review, and 7 contributed data to the orbital outcomes meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of diplopia was 0.16 (95% confidence interval, 0.00–0.33; I2 = 80%), whereas enophthalmos had a prevalence of 0.15 (95% confidence interval, 0.05–0.26; I2 = 0%). Complications reported in nonorbital facial fractures, including infection, nonunion, and wound dehiscence, demonstrated wide variability across studies and were summarized narratively due to clinical and anatomical heterogeneity. Additional complications reported by individual studies included malunion, gross malocclusion, and nerve paresthesia. Overall, complication reporting varied substantially across nonorbital injuries, supporting anatomy-specific interpretation.Conclusions:Orbital fracture reconstruction demonstrated descriptively low pooled prevalence estimates for diplopia and enophthalmos, although heterogeneity was substantial for diplopia and limits inferential interpretation.

Takeaways

Question: Among adults undergoing surgical reconstruction for facial trauma, what are the rates of key postoperative complications, and which outcomes allow quantitative synthesis?

Findings: Across 9 studies (660 patients), quantitative pooling was limited to orbital fracture outcomes. The pooled prevalence of diplopia was 16% and enophthalmos was 15%. Mandibular, orbital, and mixed facial injuries inconsistently reported techniques and were synthesized narratively rather than pooled.

Meaning: Facial trauma outcomes should be interpreted using an anatomy-specific framework. Orbital fractures permit pooled estimates, whereas cross-site pooling produces misleading conclusions. Standardized reporting of operative techniques is necessary to enable reliable comparison of reconstructive outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Facial trauma, commonly resulting from road traffic accidents, interpersonal violence, or sports injuries, poses a substantial clinical and public health challenge globally. Facial trauma encompasses a wide variety of injuries involving both soft tissue and skeletal structures. Common examples are mandibular fractures, zygomaticomaxillary complex fractures (which disrupt midfacial contour), Le Fort fractures (which involve separation of the maxilla from the cranial base), and orbital floor or blowout fractures.1 These injuries often lead to significant morbidity and disfigurement, with far-reaching economic and psychological implications for both patients and society.1,2 In addition to the immediate physical damage, facial trauma can severely impair essential functions such as vision,3 breathing,3,4 and mastication,5 while also compromising aesthetic appearance.6 These factors are crucial to a patient’s quality of life and social reintegration. Consequently, achieving acceptable functional and cosmetic outcomes through timely and effective reconstruction is an essential component of recovery following initial life-saving interventions.

Surgical reconstruction of facial trauma encompasses a range of techniques, including open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), bone grafting, and soft tissue repair.7,8 Recent advancements, such as 3-dimensional (3D) printing and computer-aided surgical planning, have transformed the field, enabling greater precision and personalization.9 Despite these innovations, achieving optimal outcomes remains challenging due to complications such as infection, malunion, and variability in surgical expertise.10,11

METHODS

This review was carried out according to the guidelines in the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) statement.12

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar for articles published up to December 1, 2024. The search strategy was designed to identify studies focusing on surgical interventions for the reconstruction of facial trauma. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms included combinations of “facial trauma,” “reconstruction,” “surgical intervention,” “mandibular fractures,” “orbital fractures,” and “soft tissue repair.” Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”) were used to maximize the retrieval of relevant studies. Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Each article included in the review was summarized in a table for study characteristics. The extracted attributes included study information (author, year, and country), population characteristics (sample size, age, and gender distribution), trauma and intervention types (type of facial trauma and reconstructive surgical techniques used), outcomes measured (functional and aesthetic results, complication rates), patient-reported outcomes, and follow-up period. Due to variations in how studies defined and measured outcomes (eg, infection, nonunion, diplopia, enophthalmos), outcomes were extracted according to each study’s specific definitions and follow-up periods.

For quality assessment, appropriate tools were used based on the study design. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of cohort studies.13 For case series, the Joanna Briggs Institute tool was applied.14 Randomized controlled trials were appraised using the Risk of Bias 2 tool.15

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was done using R software version 4.4.2. To estimate the reported outcomes after facial trauma reconstruction, a single proportion was calculated for each study. These proportions were pooled as events/total number of patients at assessment. Pooled prevalence was then computed using random-effects models. Given the anatomical and procedural heterogeneity inherent to facial trauma reconstruction, quantitative synthesis was restricted to homogenous fracture subtypes with comparable outcome definitions, while remaining outcomes were evaluated via structured narrative synthesis. Given the small sample sizes of several included studies, pooled estimates were interpreted descriptively, with particular attention to the influence of sparse data on heterogeneity and confidence interval (CI) precision.

RESULTS

Search Results

Following a thorough search of PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, we identified 1218 records. After removal of 107 duplicates, 1111 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Eighty-five articles were retrieved for full-text review, of which 76 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 9 studies were included in the systematic review and 7 were included in the meta-analysis. The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) summarizes the study selection process.

Study Characteristics

This article included 9 studies, 2 prospective cohort studies, 2 randomized controlled trials, 2 retrospective case series, and 3 retrospective cohort studies. The 9 studies included in this review provided data on a combined total of 660 patients, with a mean age of 38 years, who had undergone facial trauma reconstruction.16–24 The mean age of patients ranged from 29.8 to 48.6 years, with a median follow-up period of 6 months across the studies. The studies were conducted in various countries, including the United States,16,18,22,24 China,17 Iraq,19 Finland,20 Israel,21 and Brazil,23 with patients predominantly male. The injuries included both fractures (mandibular, orbital floor, and orbital wall fractures) and soft tissue injuries, with the most common reconstruction methods being ORIF, bone grafts, cartilage grafts, and tissue repair. Reconstruction Outcomes

Diplopia

The meta-analysis of diplopia outcomes following facial trauma reconstruction included 4 studies.17,20–22 All studies were on orbital fractures. The prevalence of diplopia was lowest in Lee et al22 at 0.0 and highest in Wu et al17 at 0.67. Because only 2 of the 4 studies were specific to the site of the orbital fracture (Zunz et al21 and Lee et al22 reported on orbital floor fractures), it was not possible to examine how the rate of diplopia varied across different orbital sites. Patients who underwent orbital reconstruction had a diplopia rate of 0.67.17 Reported complication proportions varied across studies; however, outcomes could not be attributed to specific surgical techniques due to overlapping interventions and inconsistent reporting. The rates varied for different follow-up periods. At the 6-month follow-up, the rates were 0.6717 and 0.19.20 At 12.5 months, they were 0.19,21 and at 20.4 months, they were 0.0.22 Differences in reported prevalence across studies likely reflect variation in study populations, injury severity, and follow-up duration rather than temporal trends within individuals. The pooled prevalence of diplopia across all 4 studies was 0.16 (95% CI, 0.00–0.33) (Fig. 2); however, this estimate was characterized by substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 80%) and wide CIs, reflecting the influence of small study sample sizes and variability in study populations and follow-up.

Forest plot of the prevalence of diplopia after facial trauma reconstruction. Note: see Wu et al.17

Enophthalmos

The analysis of enophthalmos following facial trauma reconstruction included data from 3 studies.17,19,22 All studies focused on orbital fractures; therefore, this outcome could not be compared across different fracture locations. The highest rate of enophthalmos was recorded at 0.20.22 The lowest was 0.12 for several techniques, including ORIF, grafting, flaps, orbital reconstruction, and soft tissue reconstruction.19 Orbital reconstruction had an enophthalmos rate of 0.16.17 At 20.4 months, the prevalence of enophthalmos was 0.20,22 compared with 0.16 at 6 months.17 Differences in reported prevalence across studies likely reflect variation in study populations and follow-up duration rather than temporal trends. The pooled results demonstrated a prevalence of 0.15 (95% CI, 0.05–0.26) (Fig. 3), indicating a low occurrence of enophthalmos as an outcome. Although heterogeneity was low, the precision of this estimate remains limited by the small number of contributing studies and modest sample sizes. Notably, no heterogeneity was observed across the studies (I2 = 0%).

Forest plot of the prevalence of enophthalmos. Note: see Wu et al17 and Lee et al.22

Infection/Nonunion/Dehiscence

Due to substantial heterogeneity in fracture location, surgical approach, and outcome definitions, complications, including infection, nonunion, and wound dehiscence, were not quantitatively pooled. Among mandibular fracture studies, reported infection rates ranged from 6% to 23%, and nonunion rates ranged from 1% to 14%. Wound dehiscence was reported inconsistently across fracture types and follow-up periods. In mixed facial trauma cohorts, complication profiles varied widely based on injury mechanism and reconstructive strategy. These findings underscore the anatomical dependence of complication risk and preclude clinically meaningful pooled estimates across disparate facial fracture types.

DISCUSSION

Summary of Evidence

The findings of this study highlighted significant advancements and challenges in the reconstruction of facial trauma. Outcomes consistently demonstrated improvements in functional and aesthetic recovery following surgical interventions. In this review, quantitative synthesis was restricted to orbital fracture outcomes. The pooled prevalence of diplopia after orbital fracture reconstruction was 16% (I2 = 80%), and enophthalmos was 15% (I2 = 0%). For nonorbital facial fractures, complication reporting was heterogeneous and could not be pooled. Infection, nonunion, and wound dehiscence demonstrated wide variability across mandibular and mixed facial trauma cohorts, underscoring the anatomy-dependent nature of complication risk. It was not possible to substantially evaluate how these complications varied across different types of fractures due to the small number of studies, with most of them (6 out of 9 studies) focusing on 1 type of fracture, namely, orbital fractures. Because nonorbital cohorts differed substantially in anatomical zone, mechanism, reconstructive strategy, and outcome definitions, between-study comparisons across fracture types were not performed.

Patient-reported outcomes assessed via the FACE-Q scales showed diminished HRQoL in the early postoperative phase, influenced by factors such as smoking, complex fractures, and postoperative immobilization.16 Advanced techniques, such as single-stage repair and 3D computed tomography (CT) imaging, facilitated precise reconstruction, notably improving orbital alignment in cases involving both bony and soft tissue injuries.17 Various reconstruction materials and methods, such as autologous bone grafts, auricular cartilage, and absorbable polyacid copolymers, demonstrated comparable efficacy in functional and cosmetic outcomes.21,23

Our findings generally align with established practices that emphasize early intervention and multidisciplinary approaches. Wu et al17 observed significant improvements in orbital alignment with single-stage repair, corroborating the broader evidence base that supports the integration of soft tissue and bony reconstruction in a single surgical session to enhance outcomes. Similarly, the safety and effectiveness of autologous bone grafts, as observed by Zunz et al,21 are consistent with their established role in the management of orbital floor fractures. However, specific results challenge conventional assumptions. For instance, Saman et al18 reported no clear advantage of postoperative MMF in managing noncomminuted mandibular fractures, contrasting with traditional reliance on MMF as a standard adjunct. The diminished HRQoL observed in the early postoperative phase is consistent with findings from prior studies, which highlight the psychological and physical burdens patients face following facial trauma surgery. For example, studies by Priya et al,3 Sahni,25 Gironda and Lui,26 and Walshaw et al27 emphasized the significant impact of facial injuries on mental health and self-esteem during the recovery process, further reinforcing the need for integrated mental health support in postoperative care.

Keeling et al28 reported that MMF can improve occlusion stability in select patient populations, particularly those with complex fracture patterns. This finding indicates that the use of MMF during surgical intervention for facial trauma should be based on fracture pattern and fixation strategy (rigid versus semirigid).28 The techniques in imaging and reconstruction used in the included studies draw parallels with existing literature. The use of 3D CT imaging to guide orbital reconstruction is supported by Schmutz et al,29 who found that advanced imaging significantly enhances surgical precision and reduces the likelihood of postoperative complications. This review provided new insights that extend beyond traditional surgical considerations.

Implications for Practice

The findings of this review underscore the importance of individualized treatment strategies in facial trauma reconstruction to optimize patient outcomes. Tailored approaches that consider the unique characteristics of each injury and patient profile can enhance functional and aesthetic results while minimizing complications. The significant improvement in orbital alignment achieved with advanced imaging techniques, such as 3D CT reconstruction, underscores the transformative role of technology in enhancing surgical precision. These tools not only facilitate preoperative planning but also provide surgeons with real-time insights during complex reconstructions, reducing the risk of residual defects such as diplopia and enophthalmos. Clinicians should prioritize evidence-based reconstruction methods, such as autologous bone grafting for orbital floor fractures, which have demonstrated safety by minimizing postoperative complications such as infection and malunion.

Addressing patient-reported outcomes, including HRQoL and satisfaction, should be integral to postoperative care plans, ensuring a holistic approach to recovery. Validated tools, such as the FACE-Q, offer valuable insights into patients’ perspectives and can inform interventions aimed at enhancing their quality of life during recovery. Factors such as active smoking and the use of MMF, identified as predictors of poorer outcomes, should prompt clinicians to implement targeted preoperative interventions, such as smoking cessation programs, to mitigate risks. Multidisciplinary collaboration among surgeons, radiologists, anesthesiologists, and rehabilitation specialists is essential for comprehensive care, particularly in complex cases involving self-inflicted injuries or combined bony and soft tissue trauma. The inclusion of mental health professionals in the care team can also address the psychological impacts of facial trauma, ensuring a patient-centered approach to recovery. Moreover, integrating emerging technologies into clinical practice, such as augmented reality for surgical navigation or artificial intelligence–driven algorithms for risk prediction, could further refine treatment strategies and improve outcomes. Study Limitations

Our review has several limitations to be acknowledged. First, the small number of studies limited the meta-analysis that could be performed. We were thus unable to analyze the outcomes substantially for different fracture locations, surgical techniques, injury severity, and follow-up periods. This limitation, due to the number of studies, also made it impossible to assess publication bias for the meta-analysis results using either funnel plots or the Egger test. Several pooled estimates were derived from studies with very small sample sizes, which likely contributed to wide CIs and, in the case of diplopia, substantial between-study heterogeneity. Despite this, the small number of relevant studies, with most being single-center studies that focus on 1 type of fracture, leads us to believe that probable publication bias is present across the included studies. Second, the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measures poses challenges for direct comparisons and generalizability. Technique-based outcome comparisons were not performed due to inconsistent reporting granularity across included studies. Accordingly, this review does not attempt to infer comparative effectiveness between reconstructive techniques or across anatomical fracture types. Mechanism of injury and reconstructive technique represent distinct, nonmutually exclusive variables, and this review does not infer comparative effectiveness between these domains.

Future Direction

Standardized reporting of patient outcomes, particularly using validated tools such as the FACE-Q, would enhance the comparability of studies and facilitate a deeper understanding of patient-centered outcomes. Prospective, multicenter trials with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of different reconstruction methods across diverse populations. Furthermore, research should delve into the long-term psychosocial impacts of facial trauma and reconstruction, focusing on interventions aimed at improving HRQoL during recovery and addressing factors that influence patient satisfaction. Investigations into emerging technologies hold significant promise for transforming practice.

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review found descriptively low pooled prevalence estimates for diplopia and enophthalmos following orbital fracture reconstruction; however, heterogeneity and limited sample sizes constrain inferential interpretation. For mandibular and mixed facial fractures, complication reporting (including infection, nonunion, and wound dehiscence) was anatomically dependent and too heterogeneous for quantitative pooling, supporting narrative interpretation. Future multicenter studies with standardized outcome reporting are needed to enable technique- and anatomy-specific comparisons. Factors such as active tobacco smoking, MMF, and Le Fort fractures were associated with diminished satisfaction and HRQoL. Advanced imaging techniques, such as 3D CT reconstruction, have demonstrated significant improvements in orbital alignment. Autologous bone grafting for orbital floor fractures has proven both safe and effective. These findings underscore the importance of tailored surgical approaches, patient-centered care, and the integration of advanced technologies to enhance outcomes. The insights gained from this review contributed to the growing body of evidence guiding the optimization of facial trauma reconstruction practices while identifying areas requiring further exploration to address ongoing challenges.
