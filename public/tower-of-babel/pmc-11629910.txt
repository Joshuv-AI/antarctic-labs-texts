Linking Iris Cis-Regulatory Variants to Primary Angle-Closure Glaucoma Via Clinical Imaging and Multiomics
Authors: Jiaying Li, Yun Chen, Wenbin Wang, Ye Zhang, Guangsong Su, Sean K. Wang, Yuanyuan Zhang, Yilong Yao, Shen Wu, Wange Lu, Kunlin Zhang, Chunyan Qiao, Shuning Li, Hengtong Li, Ching-Yu Cheng, Yuwen Liu, Ningli Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo elucidate the genetic basis of primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) by identifying pathogenic tissue and critical tissue-specific variants.MethodsThe correlations among PACG susceptibility, axial length (AL), and anterior chamber depth (ACD) were evaluated using meta-analyses. Propensity score matching was utilized on 2161 participants from the Handan Eye Study to determine the risk factors independent of ACD and AL for PACG. Subsequently, we employed the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) and allele-specific self-transcribing active regulatory region sequencing (STARR-seq) to screen 202 PACG genome-wide association study (GWAS) variants for chromatin accessibility and functional roles.ResultsThe meta-analysis found that PACG susceptibility loci are not associated with ACD or AL. However, abnormal iris phenotypes emerged as significant independent risk factors for primary angle-closure disease (PACD), unrelated to ACD and AL. Substantial enrichment of PACG heritability was observed in the open chromatin regions of the human iris. Within the iris-relevant cellular context, 22 out of the 202 PACG GWAS variants could influence enhancer activity. Two variants in the iris open chromatin regions were implicated in the modulation of PLEKHA7 and C10orf53 expression. The downregulation of these two genes affects cytoskeletal organization.ConclusionsOur findings underscore the importance of the iris in the pathogenesis of PACG and identified iris-specific, enhancer-modulating variants that may influence disease risk. Our approach also provides a generalizable framework for studying ocular diseases from the perspective of enhancer-modulating variants.

Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) is a leading cause of permanent vision loss and is expected to affect 32 million worldwide by 2040. Asia currently accounts for more than three-quarters of the PACG population.1 Characterized by anatomical narrowing of the drainage angle of the eye, PACG impedes the normal outflow of aqueous humor within the eye. This accumulation leads to elevated intraocular pressure, which subsequently results in the death of retinal ganglion cells, the neurons that convey visual input from the eyes to the brain. Previous research has indicated a strong genetic predisposition for PACG,2 with pedigree studies demonstrating a heritability rate of around 60%.3 Supporting this, two pivotal genome-wide association studies (GWASs) encompassing diverse populations have identified eight susceptibility loci for PACG.4,5

Nonetheless, translating GWAS signals to the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying PACG remains challenging. The challenges arise from the fact that most of the PACG susceptibility loci overlap noncoding regions and encompass many highly linked non-coding DNA variants, complicating the identification of causal cis-regulatory variants and their target genes.6 An additional hurdle in PACG GWASs stems from difficulty in determining the pathogenic tissue in the eye ultimately responsible for PACG from a clinical standpoint.7 This not only complicates correlating genetic effects of DNA variants with molecular or physiological phenotypes but also presents obstacles in identifying suitable cell models to validate hypotheses regarding PACG etiology.8 Collectively, these knowledge gaps significantly impede revealing the genetic basis of PACG by integrating clinical risk factors, GWASs, and functional genomics.

To tackle these challenges, we first conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate whether PACG genetic susceptibility could be explained by the most significant phenotypes of PACG in clinical settings, such as short anterior chamber depth (ACD) and axial length (AL). This analysis revealed no significant correlation among these risk factors and PACG susceptibility loci, suggesting that other factors might play more critical roles underlying PACG genetic susceptibility. Consequently, we focused on identifying other independent risk factors for angle closure within a large Chinese cohort using propensity score matching, a method proven to effectively reduce the impact of confounding variables.9 Subsequently, in contexts relevant to these risk factors, we applied the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) and high-throughput enhancer screening (allele-specific self-transcribing active regulatory region sequencing [STARR-seq]) to discern enhancer-modulating variants from PACG susceptibility loci. Our findings helped us to further identify and prioritize potential causal cis-regulatory variants underlying PACG, thereby advancing our understanding of its etiology.

Methods

Meta-Analysis

We conducted a literature search in PubMed with no language restrictions for studies published before October 29, 2023, using the terms (“anterior chamber depth” OR “axial length”) AND (“angle closure glaucoma” OR “angle closure”) AND (“SNP” OR “variant” OR “loci” OR “locus” OR “genetic association”). Four studies explored the relationship between PACG and ocular parameters, including ACD and AL10–13; of these, one study was excluded because of incomplete public data. The meta-analysis combined data that included participants from five population-based studies: Anyang University Students Study (AUSES, n = 7971), Singapore Malay Eye Study (SiMES, n = 1690), Singapore Indian Eye Study (SINDI, n = 2209), Singapore Chinese Eye Study (SCES, n = 2520), and Beijing Eye Study (BES, n = 826). Also included was a case–control association study conducted in Ningxia, China (NC, n = 1220).

For each study, we obtained the regression coefficients (β), standard errors, and P values associated with associations among PACG susceptibility loci, ACD, and AL. All loci were tested in multiple cohorts, yielding the meta-analysis results. The meta-analysis was conducted using the metafor package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Q statistics and I2 statistics were calculated to assess heterogeneity across studies. We used a fixed-effects model for the primary analysis and verified it with a random-effects model when significant heterogeneity was observed. For the meta-analysis, combined point estimates of the overall effect size (β coefficient) and the corresponding P values were obtained and adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Inclusion, Exclusion, and Propensity Score Matching of Subjects

Clinical parameters were based on a cross-sectional follow-up study of the Handan Eye Study (HES).The HES follow-up study involved 5394 rural Chinese adults 30 years or older across 13 villages in Yongnian County, Handan City, Hebei Province, Northern China.14 The study was conducted from 2012 to 2013.15 Exclusion criteria for the study were rigorously defined to ensure clarity of the results, as detailed in previous research.16

To enhance comparability between the two groups, we conducted propensity score matching using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper set at 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity scores for 1:1 pairing. Baseline characteristics, including age, anterior chamber depth, and axial length, were integrated into the propensity score-matching model to ensure similarity between matched individuals in these baseline features. All participants provided written informed consent. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, and the study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definition of Primary Angle-Closure Disease Subjects

In our research, we adopted the classification and criteria for primary angle-closure disease (PACD) as outlined by the International Society for Geographical and Epidemiological Ophthalmology.17 Glaucoma diagnosis within the HES dataset was determined by a consortium of glaucoma experts using the three-step process described in our previous investigations.18

Clinical Examinations and Statistical Analysis

The questionnaires and examination protocols that were employed have been detailed in previous investigations.15 For cases in which bilateral eyes or the right eye alone developed angle closure, the ocular factors of the right eye were utilized. Conversely, ocular factors of the left eye were exclusively considered in individuals with angle closure. Differences in variables between eyes with PACD and controls were assessed using the independent t-test (for variables with a normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U test (for variables without a normal distribution) for continuous variables. Adjustments to P values were made using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance was determined as adjusted P < 0.01.

Cell Culture

ARPE-19 cells (CL-0026) were provided by Priscilla Life Sciences (New Delhi, India). The ARPE-19 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Gibco penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C in a 95% air and 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was changed every other day.

ATAC Sequencing

Postmortem adult human irises were procured from donors with consent by Beijing Tongren Eyebank under protocols approved by the Beijing Tongren Hospital ethical committee. The human tissue experiments complied with the guidelines of the ARVO Best Practices for Using Human Eye Tissue in Research. None of the donors had a history of ocular disease, and no abnormalities were noted during dissection by ophthalmologists. Deidentified eye globes were refrigerated (but not frozen) in ice bottles with a maximum death-to-preservation interval of 12 hours and were then sent to the lab for processing. Donor information is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Iris tissues and ARPE-19 cells were treated according to the ATAC-seq protocol.19 Transposition was performed at 37°C for 60 minutes with gentle shaking. DNA was purified using a DNA MiniElute column (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and amplified for 16 cycles using indexed S5 and N7 primers. The final library included products between 200 and 600 bp as determined by using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and they were subjected to paired-end sequencing (PE150) on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

ATAC-seq data were processed as previously described; three biological replicates were collected for the iris tissue and ARPE-19 cell line. Following quality control by Fastp,20 150-bp paired-end ATAC-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using Bowtie221 with default parameters. After filtering reads from mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and removing duplicates, we included properly paired reads with high mapping quality (MAPQ score > 10) using SAMTools for subsequent analysis.22 Then, deepTools was used to check the Pearson correlation among three biological replicates and to summarize signal intensities across transcriptional starting sites.23 The binary alignment map (BAM) files were grouped for further calling peaks using MACS2 with the parameters –nomodel –shift -100 –extsize 200.24 We performed enrichment analysis using the basal regulatory domain method implemented in the Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT).25 This analysis entailed comparing ATAC-seq peaks identified in the iris against the entire genome to determine enrichment patterns. Each gene was assigned a basal regulatory domain extending a predefined distance upstream and downstream of its transcription start site (TSS). Genomic regions were then associated with nearby genes based on their overlap with these regulatory domains and enriched for Gene Ontology (GO) terms.

To assess whether there is a significant enrichment of GWAS candidate variants that overlap open chromatin regions (OCRs) in specific eye tissue or cell types, including iris, retina26 (GSE137311), choroid26 (GSE137311), scleral fibroblast cells (unpublished), retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)19 (GSE99287), and limbal stem cells27 (GSE156273), we applied the random background model. All mentioned tissues and cells were sequenced using 150-bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data for these samples were processed using the same protocol.

For the random background model, we generated random OCRs for each eye tissue or cell type by randomly selecting 150-bp sites across the genome. The number of sites selected on each chromosome equaled the number of OCR identified chromosomes in the actual dataset. This random selection was repeated 1000 times. For each permutation, we recorded the number of GWAS candidate variants within these random background sites and calculated the average across all 1000 permutations, which we defined as the expected count. This expected count was then compared to the actual number of variants observed within the paired OCRs, referred to as the observed count. The enrichment fold for each tissue or cell type was calculated as the ratio of the observed count to the expected count in each tissue or cell type. Statistical significance was evaluated via binomial test using the stat package in R (binom.test) with the option alternative = “greater.”

Correlation Between ARPE-19 and Human Iris

For the gene expression correlation assessment, we utilized transcriptome data from human iris tissue28 and ARPE-19 cells cultured for 4 days and 4 months.29 The raw gene count tables were obtained from previous studies, with four replicates for the human iris transcriptome and triplicates for ARPE-19 at both time points. We filtered out genes with counts less than 10 and then performed normalization using DESeq. The normalized gene counts were subsequently used to calculate Pearson correlation coefficients. For the open chromatin region correlation assessment, we employed the deepTools multibamsummary function, which is traditionally used to evaluate correlations between replicates, to examine the correlation between ATAC-seq data from ARPE-19 cells and human iris tissue using BAM files.

Generation of Variants Pools

Candidate variants, were identified as index variants of eight independent PACG association signals and proxies (r2 ≥ 0.5, ±100 kb). Linked variants were selected based on the 1000 Genomes phase 3 panel (European and Asian, based on the GWAS population) using LDProxy.30 We subsequently filtered out indels and multi-allelic variants. The variant lists were further filtered if they were not replicated in large Asian or European populations (unpublished). This yielded a comprehensive pool of 202 candidate variants. To increase the complexity of the libraries, 400 random variants were added from the extensive linkage block of MAPT–KANSL131 (see Supplementary Data S3).The MAPT–KANSL1 locus was chosen because it is characterized by high linkage disequilibrium, such that typically only a few variants are truly causal. The majority of these random variants are expected to exhibit minimal or no effect on allelic imbalance, thus serving as a control for the functional assessment of the candidate PACG variants. Both alleles of each variant were centered in a 152-bp genomic context using bedtools. Finally, we appended 5′(acatttctctatcgatacgtac) and 3′(GCTAGCCATTGCGTGTAGAG) PCR adaptor sequences to each oligo and synthesized a library utilizing Azenta Life Sciences (Burlington, MA, USA).

Generation of Input Plasmid Library

Plasmid libraries were generated based on a previously described method.32 In summary, we conducted sequence amplification from the Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA) array with gia-starrseq-1-f (5′-ATTAGATTGATCTAGAGCATGCACCGGTacatttctctatcgataggtac-3′) and gia-starrseq-1-r(5′-TCGAAGCGGCCGGCCGAATTCGTCGANNNNNNNNNNCTCTACACGCAATGGCTAGC-3′) primers. Amplification employed KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA) in a 50-µL reaction with 2.5 ng of DNA, utilizing a real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The process was halted before reaching the plateau phase, after 13 cycles. This reaction amplified our library, appending a 10-nucleotide (nt) degenerate barcode to each sequence, and added integrating adapters for subsequent cloning into the human STARR-seq vector. The PCR product (homology arms added to DNA fragments) was integrated into the vector backbone, which had been linearized by Agel and SalI restriction enzymes, using Gibson Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), followed by transformations into MegaX DH10B T1R Electrocompetent Cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, transformations were performed. All transformed Escherichia coli cells were pooled to grow in 4 L of Luria–Bertani medium with ampicillin and harvested when the optical density reached 1.0. The input plasmid library was extracted using a QIAGEN Plasmid Mega Kit and dialyzed prior to transfection. The input plasmid library was electroporated into ARPE-19 cells using the Nucleofector platform (Lonza Bioscience, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's protocol (1 µg DNA/1 million cells). A total of 10 million cells were electroporated into each replicate, and two biological replicates were generated. After transfection, the cells were cultured for 24 hours until harvesting.

Allele-Specific STARR-Seq Library Preparation

Total RNA was isolated from transfected ARPE-19 cells using the M5 HiPer Universal RNA Mini Kit (Mei5 Biotechnology, Beijing, China) followed by poly(A) tail mRNA isolation using the Dynabeads Oligo(dT) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). mRNA was treated with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and purified using RNAClean XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Target-specific first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with SuperScript III (1.5 µg mRNA per reaction; Thermo Fisher Scientific) to specifically reverse transcribe plasmid transcribed mRNAs (RT primer: 5′-CAAACTCATCAATGTATCTTATCATG-3′). All mRNA from 10 million transfected cells were used for reverse transcription. After reverse transcription, the reactions were treated with RNase A and RNase H and pooled. Then, for the first round of amplification, every 5-µL cDNA reaction was used as a template in every 50-µL PCR reaction using the Roche KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix. Target amplification primers were used (gia-starrseq-2-f: 5′-ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTattagattgatctagagcatgcaccggt-3′; gia-starrseq-2-r: 5′-GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCtcgaagcggccggccgaattcgtcga-3′). In the final round of amplification, the enzymes used were the same as those used in the first round, but four sets of primer combinations with different indices were used to complete the final construction of the library. Illumina HiSeq platform–compatible primers were used (forward: 5′-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-3′; reverse: 5′-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGAT-index-GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTG-3′).

Four duplicated PCR reactions were performed for each indexing PCR. The PCR products were cleaned using pure DNA beads (VDO Biotech, Suzhou, China) and subjected to Illumina sequencing. An input library for sequencing was generated using a similar PCR setup. Products from four duplicate PCRs were pooled (10 ng of plasmid per reaction, 16 cycles of PCR). All primers used in previous research are summarized in Supplementary Table S5.

Analysis of the Allele-Specific STARR-Seq

Screening consisted of two sets of data with different types of readouts. The input library was generated by directly amplifying the inserts from the plasmid DNA used for cell transfection, serving as a reference for the original representation of the insert fragments in the starting plasmid pool. The output library measured the abundance of self-transcribed mRNA from the insert fragments of the transfected plasmid pool. The paired-end 150-bp reads were merged into single amplicons using Flash 1.2.11.33 Oligo sequences from the passing reads were then aligned back to the expected oligo sequences using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA)–MEM version 0.7.12.34 Multimapped reads were discarded. Reads were filtered if they did not meet the following criteria:1.The adaptor sequence GCTAGCCATTGCGTGTAGAG must have a perfect match.2.There should be an exactly 10-bp barcode sequence between adaptors.3.Only primary alignment reads (-F 256) could be included.

To maintain barcode integrity, we generated oligo–barcode association tables from the sequencing results and confirmed that these associations in the output libraries matched those in the input libraries. Oligos with fewer than five unique barcodes in each replicate were discarded. The abundance of unique barcode counts for each oligo per replicate was provided as input to DESeq235 for normalization and enhancer activity calculations.

Based on adjusted P values and log2 fold changes derived from the DESeq results, oligos exhibiting enhancer activity were defined as the log2 ratio of output to input library barcode counts greater than 0.585, with an adjusted P < 0.01. Normalized unique barcode counts were then averaged in the input and output libraries. For each variant, Fisher's test was applied to assess the association between the input and output counts of oligo-containing reference alleles and the alternate alleles. A Manhattan plot was created using the CMplot package in R.36

Human Samples

Human ocular tissues were obtained postmortem from the Eye Bank of the Tongren Eye Center (Supplementary Table S4). The acquisition and use of human tissues were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital. No ocular disease was reported in any of the human donors, and no abnormalities were noted during dissection. For each eye, the iris was separated under direct visualization using a dissection microscope.

Variant Annotation

The Somatic Binding Sequence Annotator (SBSA) webtool37 and SNP2TFBS tool38 were employed to predict the change in transcription binding affinity. To quantify the impact of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) on transcription factor binding, we utilized the SNP2TFBS tool. This output included Scorediff, which represents the change in binding affinity of a transcription factor (TF) when comparing the alternative allele to the reference allele at the same genomic position. For each variant, we recorded the absolute value of the Scorediff to represent the magnitude of the disruption in TF binding, regardless of whether the binding affinity increased or decreased. In cases where a variant was associated with multiple TFs (and therefore multiple Scorediff values), we selected the highest absolute Scorediff to represent the maximum potential disruption caused by that variant. This absolute value of the Scorediff of each variant was then used in our subsequent analyses to quantify the extent of the predicted TF binding disruption.

We proceeded to investigate whether functional variants were situated within the anchor regions of Hi-C (chromatin conformation capture) data. The Hi-C data were reanalyzed using high-throughput sequencing data obtained from the hTERT RPE cell line.39 Hi-C data processing was performed using HIC-PRO40 with the hg38 genome. Loops were identified using FitHiC41 on the merged Hi-C datasets at a 5-kb resolution with a false discovery rate threshold of 0.01 using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For a variant target gene, one foot of the loop should overlap with the variants and the second foot of the loop should overlap with the promoter region of the gene (defined as 2 kb upstream of the transcription starting site).

Candidate Gene Distribution and Function

Single-cell expression profiles from the anterior segment of the eyes were derived from the previous paper42 and used to identify the distribution of casual gene expression. To assess the impact of reduced expression of PLEKHA7 and C10orf53, we employed a small interfering RNA (siRNA) approach to individually knock down each gene in ARPE-19 cells, followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses. All transfections, qRT-PCR, and RNA-seq experiments were conducted in triplicate. We used si-PLEKHA7, si-C10ORF53, and si-NC (Supplementary Table S5) synthesized by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). Transient transfection of ARPE-19 cells was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, total RNA was extracted using the M5 HiPer Universal RNA Mini Kit, with each sample divided for either qRT-PCR or RNA-seq analysis. In the following analysis, the expression profiles of ARPE-19 treated with si-PLEKHA7 or si-C10ORF53 are compared with ARPE-19 treated with si-NC.

For qRT-PCR, cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. qRT-PCR was carried out on a 7500 FAST Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Without ROX) (Vazyme). The reaction conditions were 95°C for 10 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 5 seconds at 95°C and 30 seconds at 60°C. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. All primers used in the qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S5.

For RNA-seq, the extracted RNA samples were sent to Novogene (Tianjin, China) for library preparation and sequencing for 6 billion base pairs (6G) per library. Clean reads were mapped to the hg38 genome using Hisat243 with default parameters. Gene reads were counted by Htseq.44 Fold changes (FCs) were computed as the log2 ratio of normalized reads per gene using the DEseq2 package in R.35 Genes with at least a twofold increase or decrease in expression and adjusted P < 0.01 were considered to show significant expression change. Clusterprofiler was used for GO analysis.45 Bubble charts were constructed using the ggplot2 package. A link to the raw data can be found in the Data and Code Availability section.

Results

Meta-Analysis Found PACG Susceptibility Loci are not Associated With ACD or AL

Given that short ACD and AL are the most prominent phenotypes for PACG screening,7 it is plausible that these phenotypes could mediate the genetic effects identified by PACG GWAS loci. Previous investigations into the relationship between PACG susceptibility and ACD or AL have primarily utilized small or isolated cohorts,10,12,13 yielding no significant findings. To enhance the power to detect potential correlations, we performed a comprehensive meta-analysis by analyzing genetic data across six cohorts, encompassing three ethnic groups and totaling 16,436 genotyped participants. The cohorts included five population-based studies—AUSES (n = 7971), SiMES (n = 1690), SINDI (n = 2209), SCES (n = 2520), and BES (n = 826)—along with the case–control association study NC (n = 1220) (Supplementary Table S1). Despite the substantially increased sample size, neither ACD nor AL was associated with any of the eight PACG GWAS loci (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S2). A summary of the association statistics among the eight loci with each condition (PACG, AL, and ACD) is provided in Figure 1B.

Abnormal Iris Phenotypes as a Risk Factor Contributing to PACD Independent of ACD and AL

As neither ACD nor AL showed an association with PACG susceptibility loci, we aimed to identify other independent risk factors that may represent alternative pathogenic mechanisms underlying PACG. To this end, we utilized propensity score matching to mitigate potential confounding factors. Further, to enhance our ability to detect such factors, we explored the entire spectrum of PACD,17 which includes stages such as primary angle-closure suspect, primary angle closure, and the final stage, PACG. This exploration was conducted in the Handan Eye follow-up study cohort (Fig. 1C), comparing patients at various stages of the disease to normal subjects (Fig. 1C).

From the initial pool of 5394 participants, 2262 completed the comprehensive ophthalmic assessments. Subsequently, we excluded 101 eyes due to concurrent ophthalmic conditions. After these exclusions, 2161 subjects’ unilateral eyes remained eligible for analysis. This cohort was divided into 715 normal eyes and 1446 with PACD. Following 1:1 propensity score matching for ACD, AL, and age, the study continued with 571 PACD eyes and an equal number of normal eyes for further analysis.

We compared the distribution of ACD and AL before and after propensity score matching (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table S3). Normal eyes had a median AL of 22.80 mm (interquartile range [IQR], 22.19–23.33), differing significantly from PACD eyes at 22.40 mm (IQR, 21.97–22.99; adjusted P = 1E-19). Median ACD also varied, with normal eyes at 2.53 mm (IQR, 2.36–2.71) versus PACD eyes at 2.24 mm (IQR, 2.08–2.39 mm; adjusted P = 1E-114). Through propensity score matching, we adjusted for factors such as age, ACD, and AL to balance between the treatment and control groups. The post-matching data showed no significant statistical differences in key variables between the groups (median age, 62 years; AL = 22.57 mm for controls and 22.47 mm for PACD; ACD = 2.31 mm for controls and 2.29 mm for PACD; all adjusted P > 0.05), indicating that the matching process effectively reduced baseline disparities.

Subsequently, we compared ocular parameters of PACD eyes and control eyes both before and after propensity score matching (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table S3). Before matching, notable differences were observed in various factors such as average keratometry and spherical equivalent, as well as parameters related to the anterior chamber, lens, and iris (all adjusted P < 0.01). After matching, notable differences emerged in parameters related to angle and iris (mostly with adjusted P < 0.01). Clinical images illustrating typical PACD versus normal eye conditions with similar ACD and AL are presented in Figure 1F. Overall, we proposed that the abnormal iris phenotype stood out as risk factor of PACD, independent of ACD or AL.

Landscape of Chromatin Accessibility in the Iris and its Potential Implications in PACG

Recognizing the potentially critical role of the iris in PACG in clinical settings and the strong heritability of PACG,46,47 we speculated that genetic variations affecting gene regulatory networks in the iris might contribute to PACG etiology. Because numerous studies have suggested prominent contributions of variants in open chromatin regions to the risk of complex diseases, we performed ATAC-seq to investigate the global chromatin accessibility landscape of the human iris (Fig. 2A). We identified 56,774 high-confidence open chromatin regions across the iris samples (Supplementary Data S1). The dataset is of high quality, as evidenced by the enriched distribution of signals around TSSs and a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.4 between chromatin accessibility and the expression of potential target genes (Fig. 2C).28

(G) The enrichment of OCRs was significant in the iris, RPE, and fibroblast, in comparison to other ocular tissues or cells.

We defined iris OCRs as those not present in ocular stem cells to highlight the differentiated and specialized functions of the iris.27 In our GO analysis, genes assigned to the nearest iris-specific OCRs exhibited enrichments in biological processes, including responses to muscle inactivity, layer formation in cerebral cortex, and ventricular cardiac muscle cell action potential. Additionally, these genes demonstrated significant enrichment in pathways related to lysophosphatidic acid acyltransferase activity and adenylate cyclase binding (Figs. 2B, 2D). These findings are consistent with the functional role of the iris in receiving neural signals to contract or dilate the pupil.48 To identify potential transcription factors binding to iris OCRs, we performed de novo motif enrichment analysis. The top enriched motifs in iris-specific OCRs included CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF), nuclear factor 1A (NFIA), and specificity protein 1 (SP1) (Figs. 2E, 2F). Notably, the enrichment of MITF motifs aligned with its well-established role in melanocyte development and differentiation, the key processes for iris pigmentation.49

In the iris OCRs, we observed a 3.63-fold enrichment of candidate PACG GWAS variants. We also observed significant fold enrichment in the OCRs of the RPE19 and scleral fibroblast (unpublished). In contrast, enrichment was not significant in OCRs of other ocular tissue, including limbal stem cells,27 retina,26 and choroid26 (Fig. 2G). These findings further highlight the potential critical role of the iris in the pathogenesis of PACG.

High-Throughput Screening in Cellular Environment Reflective of Human Iris Identified PACG GWAS Variants That Modulate Enhancer Activity

Non-coding DNA variants have the potential to influence disease risk by modulating enhancer activity, which in turn, affect the expression of genes implicated in disease progression.31,50 To comprehensively evaluate the cis-regulatory activity of PACG-associated variants, we implemented allelic-specific STARR-seq, a high-throughput enhancer screening technique that enables the efficient identification of enhancer-modulating variants.51,52

Given that STARR-seq requires a cell line with efficient transfection capabilities, we chose ARPE-19 as an appropriate model to simulate the native cellular environment of the iris. We based our selection of ARPE-19 on several factors. Primarily, ARPE-19 is an immortalized cell line of the RPE, which shares its origin with the pigmented cells of the iris. Additionally, strong correlations were found between the transcriptome of ARPE-19 and the iris (Pearson's r = 0.93), as well as between the chromatin accessibility profiles (Pearson's r = 0.73) (Supplementary Figs. S2D, S2E). Consequently, ARPE-19 proves to be a reliable cell model for exploring enhancer activity associated with the iris.

Our workflow for screening 602 variants with allele-specific STARR-seq is depicted in Figure 3A. This variants pool was comprised of 202 candidate variants, filtered from PACG variants and their linkage disequilibrium counterparts (r2 > 0.5), and 400 random variants selected from the extensive linkage block of MAPT–KANSL1. For each variant, we synthesized two 152-bp DNA sequences that differed only in the alleles they carried—one with the major allele and the other with the minor allele. These sequences were also flanked by adaptor sequences for cloning into the STARR-seq screening vector. To reduce PCR duplication bias during library creation, we modified the STARR-seq by incorporating 10 nucleotide random barcodes into each oligonucleotide. This modification followed methodologies utilized in prior studies.52–54 After transfecting the input plasmids into ARPE-19 cells, we produced STARR-seq output libraries by performing RNA-seq on transcripts derived specifically from the STARR-seq plasmid libraries, 24 hours post-transfection. The enhancer-modulating effects of the alleles of each variant were then assessed by comparing allelic imbalances between the input and output libraries.

The STARR-seq experiments demonstrated high reproducibility, with consistent normalized barcode counts across replicates (Pearson's r = 0.99) (Supplementary Figs. S3A, S3C). The complexity of the oligonucleotide libraries contributed to effective detection of enhancer activity. Specifically, each oligonucleotide in the input library had an average of 1661 unique barcodes per replicate, whereas the output library averaged 202 per replicate (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

We identified STARR-seq enhancers showing significantly higher expression in output compared with input with adjusted P < 0.01 and an at least 1.5-fold increase (log2FC > 0.585) (Supplementary Fig. S3D).52 These enhancers, comprising 27% of the library elements, showed rates similar to those reported in previous studies.31,55 These active enhancers were significantly enriched in OCRs of the iris and RPE with odds ratios of 2.6 (P < 0.05) and 2.2 (P < 0.01), respectively, in comparison with non-enhancers in the oligo pool, underscoring their relevance in regulating gene expression within an endogenous genomic context.

In these STARR-seq enhancers, we identified 22 enhancer-modulating PACG variants that exhibited significant differences in enhancer activity between alleles (adjusted P < 0.01), with each showing at least a 25% variation in activity levels56,57 (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S3E, Supplementary Data S2) spanning across five loci of PACG (Fig. 4E).

Aligning with prior studies,31,50 the allelic effects identified in STARR-seq correlated with allelic differences in predicted transcription binding disruption scores (Fig. 3F). Also, compared with variants with lower allelic imbalances, those with higher allelic imbalances in enhancer activity were enriched in variants predicted to change transcription binding affinity (odds ratio = 1.4; P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S3F). Notably, variants associated with PACG are more likely to exhibit allelic imbalances compared to random variants under testing, suggesting the functional relevance of these variants (odds ratio = 2.22; P = 1.494E-05) (Fig. 3D).

Rs11024103 of PLEKHA7 and rs59766519 of C10orf53 Might Contribute to PACG by Affecting Enhancer Activity in the Iris

Significantly, two candidate variants influencing enhancer activity were located in the OCRs of adult human iris tissue (Fig. 4A). The first variant, rs59766519, was positioned approximately 2 kb upstream of the C10orf53 transcription start site and showed chromatin accessibility in both the iris and RPE (Fig. 4B). The G risk allele of rs59766519 exhibited a 43% increase in STARR-seq enhancer activity compared to the C non–risk allele. Thus, the increased PACG risk might be attributed to the upregulation of C10orf53 expression, potentially driven by the creation by the G allele of new binding sites for transcription factor such as Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9), early growth response 1 (EGR1), and KLF15.

The second variant, rs11024103, located within an intron of the PLEKHA7 gene, also displayed accessibility in both the iris and RPE cells (Fig. 4C). Chromatin conformation capture data from immortalized RPE cells39 revealed that this variant is in close proximity to the promoter of PLEKHA7. The risk allele C of rs11024103 increased STARR-seq enhancer activity by 3.5-fold in comparison to the non–risk allele, potentially due to the creation of binding motifs for transcription factors such as Sp1, Sp2, and zinc finger protein 148 (ZNF148).

PLEKHA7 was predominantly expressed in the posterior pigment epithelium cells of the iris, whereas C10orf53 exhibited relatively low expression levels but was detectable in the single-cell expression profiles of the iris (Figs. 4E, 4F).42 Notably, C10orf53 was undetectable in RNA-seq data using a standard sequencing depth of 6G per library in ARPE-19 cells, yet it was detectable via qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S5A).

To understand the knockdown effect of the two candidate genes, we performed siRNA-mediated gene knockdown experiments in ARPE-19 cells. qRT-PCR confirmed silencing efficiencies of 69% for PLEKHA7 and 58% for C10orf53. The silencing of these two candidate genes in ARPE-19 cells resulted in significant proportions of differentially expressed genes (6.7% in the PLEKHA7 knockdown experiment and 5.2% in the C10orf53 experiment) (Supplementary Figs. S5B, S5C). The downregulated genes were overrepresented in similar functional categories, with both primarily affecting cytoskeletal components (Figs. 4H, 4J). These results suggest that modulating PLEKHA7 and C10orf53 expression in the iris may underlie the PACG etiology through their effects on cytoskeletal components.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the genetic underpinnings of PACG by integrating ocular phenotypes from a large-scale, population-based study with ATAC-seq and allele-specific STARR-seq. Initially, no correlations were found between PACG susceptibility loci and the PACG significant phenotype, such as short AL and ACD. After adjusting for these unrelated phenotypes, we noted that a thicker and enlarged iris emerged as the independent phenotype associated with PACG. Consistently, we found significant enrichment of PACG GWAS variants in the OCRs of the human iris. Of these GWAS variants, 22 exhibited allele-specific activity in our high-throughput enhancer screening using STARR-seq experiments. Notably, two of these variants were located in regions of chromatin accessibility within the human iris, highlighting their potential to modulate transcriptional regulatory networks that are important in PACG. We found evidence suggesting that these variants might impact the expression of PLEKHA7 and C10orf53, with their downregulation affecting cytoskeletal organization. Overall, our results highlight the potentially critical role of the iris in PACG susceptibility and the effectiveness of high-throughput, massively parallel reporter assays, such as allele-specific STARR-seq, in developing hypotheses regarding the etiology of PACG (Fig. 5).

Summary plot.

Numerous studies have identified various risk factors influencing the onset or progression of PACG,7,18,58 yet none of these factors has been definitively linked to its genetic origins. The most prominent risk factors—short ACD and short AL—were not associated with PACG heritability, as evidenced by our meta-analyses. Their lack of genetic correlation suggests that these factors may not be as critical as traditionally thought. Historically, these two risk factors have been investigated in clinical settings due to the convenience of utilizing slit-lamp visualization and ultrasound measurement. Other risk factors had not been studied in detail until recent advancements in anterior segment OCT imaging over the past decade. Therefore, we shifted our focus toward other risk factors representing alternative mechanisms that might play a more substantial role in mediating the genetic effects in PACG.

Our study made significant strides in identifying the critical role of the iris in the genetic basis of PACG. By effectively isolating and removing confounding factors unrelated to currently distinguished GWAS signals, we first discovered an abnormal iris phenotype (thickened and enlarged) as an independent risk factor for PACG. Further support for this comes from our finding that PACG GWAS signals were highly enriched in the OCRs of the human iris. Variant-based heritability has been shown to be concentrated in the cis-regulatory regions of tissues implicated in diseases.59 Moreover, the strong heritability of iris phenotypes, including shape, size, and color,46,47,60 along with recently identified PACG GWAS loci related to iris morphology and pigmentation,61 reinforces the role of the iris in PACG. Based on these findings, we suggest that individuals with a family history of PACG might benefit from frequent and comprehensive iris screenings.

To unravel the genetic underpinnings of various complex traits and disorders, ATAC-seq and high-throughput screening of functional cis-regulatory variants have been utilized in conjunction.52,62–65 However, their joint application within the vision community remains unexplored. Although ATAC-seq and single-cell ATAC-seq (sc-ATAC-seq) have proven to be quite valuable in vision research,19,66–68 integrating these methods with high-throughput enhancer screening techniques, such as allele-specific STARR-seq, is crucial for directly assessing the biological impact of cis-regulatory variants. Such an integrated approach could greatly improve the identification of potential causal variants associated with heritable ocular diseases, including age-related macular degeneration, myopia, and open-angle glaucoma. In this study, we demonstrated the effectiveness of combining allele-specific STARR-seq with ATAC-seq to identify enhancer-modulating variants potentially underlying the etiology of PACG. Our methodology offers broad potential for application in studying other ocular diseases, as well.

Furthermore, our study revealed a similar correlation between the expression of PLEKHA7 or C10orf53 and cytoskeletal-related molecular function. These observations suggest that the effects of some PACG variants might converge on these molecular functions. Functional convergence of risk variants on specific pathways has also been observed for other complex diseases.69 The convergence on the same molecular function suggests that targeting cytoskeletal organization in the iris could be a viable strategy to prevent the onset of PACG.

The limitations of our analysis are as follows. First, although we used RPE cells as a proxy for iris tissue due to their similarity in gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles, this approach could have introduced bias when conducting the STARR-seq and using Hi-C interaction data from the hTERT RPE cell line to study enhancer–promoter contacts. Specifically, for Hi-C, we acknowledge that there may be iris-specific chromatin loops that could only be accurately captured using human iris tissue, which we were unable to obtain due to limited sample availability. Additionally, the translatability of allelic-specific STARR-seq results from in vitro assays in RPE cells to in vivo effects remains under investigation. The advent of adeno-associated-virus (AAV)–STARR-seq or AAV–massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA) technologies70,71 will allow us to further explore the translatability of cell model to in vivo conditions.

Second, the episomal plasmid-based STARR-seq was conducted outside of the endogenous genomic context; thus, further validation of the endogenous cis-regulatory activity of the potential functional SNPs identified in our study could have benefited from genome editing of these SNPs or the application of large serine recombinase–based technology to perform enhancer activity screening of sequences integrated into the genome.72

Third, incorporating PACG-affected iris tissue could offer additional insights to the current study. The PACG-specific OCRs could aid in identifying variants that induce OCR formation uniquely in the PACG context, thereby offering deeper insights into PACG pathogenesis.

Fourth, as the iris is comprised of a complex mix of cell types, bulk ATAC-seq of the iris may overlook cell-type–specific OCRs, especially when the cell type is underrepresented among total cells. Future research employing techniques such as single-cell ATAC-seq or single-cell STARR-seq could provide more insights into the cell-type–specific regulatory mechanisms in PACG.

The STARR-seq assay used to assess enhancer activity is highly sensitive and capable of identifying enhancers that might be masked by closed chromatin under baseline conditions. These STARR-seq enhancers may become active for specific cells or in disease-specific regulatory contexts. Therefore, in the current study, oligos that exhibited enhancer activity not located within OCRs of human iris tissue and enhancer-modulating variants not accessible within these OCRs might be accessible in a disease-specific or cell-type–specific context. To ensure a comprehensive assessment, we employed a high-throughput screening approach that included all candidate variants, thereby maximizing the utility of our study. Future work incorporating additional annotations, such as disease-specific ATAC-seq or single-cell ATAC-seq data, may further refine the prioritization of functional variants and enhance our understanding of their roles in disease.
