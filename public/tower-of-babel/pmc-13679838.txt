Early Oral Gastrografin Followed by Immediate CT for Precise Leak Localization and Surgical Planning After Total Laryngectomy
Authors: Seung Jo Jeong, Minsu Kwon, Yoon Se Lee, Young Ho Jung, Seung‐Ho Choi, Seung Hoon Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposePharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF) is the most common and clinically significant complication following total laryngectomy, often leading to delayed recovery and increased morbidity. Accurate and early detection of pharyngeal leak is critical; however, conventional imaging modalities such as contrast swallow studies and computed tomography (CT) have limitations in precise anatomical localization.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 117 patients who underwent total laryngectomy between January 2020 and December 2025. Patients were divided into two groups according to the postoperative imaging protocol: Gastrografin swallow followed by immediate CT (Gastrografin–CT group, n = 22) and conventional imaging (n = 95). Leak localization was assessed using a four‐point ordinal scale, and surgically actionable localization was defined as a score ≥ 2. Clinical outcomes and impact on management were also evaluated.ResultsThe Gastrografin–CT protocol demonstrated significantly improved leak localization compared with conventional imaging (mean localization score: 3.00 ± 0.00 vs. 1.62 ± 0.83, p = 0.01). Surgically actionable localization was achieved in all patients in the Gastrografin–CT group compared with 51.2% in the conventional group (p = 0.03). Leaks were identified on initial imaging in 100% of patients in the Gastrografin–CT group versus 82.9% in the conventional group. Although clinical outcomes, including time to oral feeding, complication rates, and hospital stay, did not significantly differ, the Gastrografin–CT protocol provided more precise anatomical delineation, facilitating targeted surgical planning.ConclusionPerforming CT immediately after an oral Gastrografin swallow significantly improves anatomical localization of pharyngeal leaks following total laryngectomy. By integrating leak detection and anatomical delineation in a single imaging session, this protocol enhances surgical decision‐making and may serve as a practical adjunct for postoperative evaluation and management of PCF.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Total laryngectomy remains an essential treatment option for advanced or recurrent laryngeal and hypopharyngeal malignancies, particularly in the salvage setting after chemoradiotherapy failure [1, 2, 3]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and reconstructive strategies, postoperative complications remain common, with overall complication rates reported to exceed 30% in some series [1, 4]. Among these, pharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF) is the most frequent and clinically significant complication following total laryngectomy [3, 5]. Reported incidence rates range from approximately 10%–30% in primary laryngectomy and may be substantially higher in salvage procedures [4, 5, 6]. PCF can lead to delayed oral feeding, prolonged hospitalization, wound infection, and occasionally life‐threatening complications such as carotid artery exposure or rupture [3, 7].

Early detection of pharyngeal dehiscence or leak is therefore critical in the postoperative management of patients undergoing total laryngectomy [3, 8]. Prompt identification of PCF has been shown to reduce surgical complexity and prevent severe complications while facilitating timely intervention [7, 8]. In many institutions, routine imaging studies are performed before initiation of oral feeding to assess the integrity of the neopharyngeal closure [6]. Esophagography using water‐soluble contrast agents has traditionally been used as a first‐line modality to detect contrast leakage [5, 6].

However, the diagnostic performance of contrast swallow studies is limited. Previous studies have reported variable sensitivity, with false‐negative findings occurring particularly in small or intermittent leaks [5, 9]. In addition, contrast swallow studies may demonstrate low positive predictive value and may overestimate clinically insignificant leaks, potentially complicating clinical decision‐making [9]. These limitations suggest that esophagography alone may be insufficient for reliable detection and precise localization of pharyngeal leaks.

Cross‐sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT) can provide detailed anatomical information regarding the surrounding soft tissues and postoperative spaces and is widely used in the evaluation of postoperative complications in head and neck surgery [2]. Nevertheless, CT performed without ingested contrast material frequently fails to clearly demonstrate the leakage tract or identify the precise site of dehiscence [10]. Previous reports have suggested that CT performed after oral contrast ingestion may improve visualization of pharyngeal fistulas by outlining the leakage pathway with contrast extravasation [11]. These observations indicate that combining swallow contrast studies with CT imaging may provide complementary diagnostic information by integrating leak detection and anatomical delineation.

Based on this concept, our institution adopted a protocol in which CT is performed immediately after an oral Gastrografin swallow study. Residual intraluminal contrast from the swallow examination may delineate the leakage tract on CT and facilitate precise anatomical localization of the defect. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the clinical utility of this early Gastrografin swallow followed by immediate CT protocol for postoperative leak evaluation after total laryngectomy, with particular focus on leak localization and surgically actionable imaging information.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent total laryngectomy at a single tertiary referral center between January 2020 and December 2025. Patients were consecutively enrolled in chronological order during the study period rather than selectively recruited. As part of the institutional postoperative protocol, patients routinely underwent imaging evaluation for pharyngeal leak prior to initiation of oral feeding, typically between postoperative days 7 and 14 following surgery. Patients without evidence of leak on imaging subsequently initiated oral feeding. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study (IRB No. 2025‐0762).

Patients were divided into two groups according to the postoperative imaging strategy used for leak assessment. In the Gastrografin–CT group, patients underwent an oral Gastrografin swallow study followed immediately by noncontrast neck CT. In the conventional imaging group, esophagography was performed as the standard initial postoperative examination for pharyngeal leakage. Additional CT was obtained separately when clinically indicated, rather than routinely or immediately following esophagography. Thus, all patients underwent esophagography, and the principal difference between the two groups was whether CT was performed immediately after the swallow study as part of an integrated imaging protocol. Clinical data including age, sex, prior radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (RT/CCRT), reconstruction method, and postoperative day of imaging were collected. Information regarding imaging findings, subsequent management decisions, and clinical outcomes was obtained through retrospective review of electronic medical records.

Imaging Protocol

In the Gastrografin–CT protocol, patients first underwent esophagography using oral Gastrografin as a water‐soluble contrast agent to evaluate for pharyngeal leak. Immediately after completion of the swallow study, patients were transferred to the CT suite and underwent noncontrast neck CT without additional contrast administration. Residual intraluminal Gastrografin from the swallow study allowed clear visualization of potential leakage tracts and facilitated anatomical localization of the leak site on CT imaging.

In the conventional imaging group, postoperative imaging evaluation was performed using standard approaches, including esophagography alone or CT imaging obtained independently without immediate preceding oral contrast ingestion.

Imaging Evaluation and Leak Localization Score

Postoperative imaging studies were independently reviewed by two head and neck surgeons (S.J.J. and S.H.H.), who were blinded to patients' clinical information, to assess the presence of pharyngeal dehiscence or leak and the degree of anatomical localization.

Leak localization was evaluated using a predefined four‐point ordinal scale reflecting the extent to which imaging findings could guide surgical localization of the leak site. A score of 0 indicated no identifiable leak or localization, while a score of 1 indicated a suspected leak without clear localization. A score of 2 corresponded to probable localization allowing regional identification of the leak site, and a score of 3 indicated precise localization enabling targeted surgical exploration.

Surgically actionable localization was defined as a localization score of ≥ 2, indicating that imaging findings provided sufficient anatomical information to guide targeted surgical planning. Imaging performance analysis was conducted among patients in whom pharyngeal dehiscence or leak was identified on postoperative imaging and whose management was consequently modified from the initially planned initiation of oral feeding.

Interobserver agreement for the Leak Localization Score was assessed using the exact agreement rate and linear‐ and quadratic‐weighted Cohen's kappa coefficients. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using bootstrap resampling.

Clinical Outcomes and Management Assessment

Postoperative management decisions following imaging evaluation were reviewed. Management change was defined as a deviation from the initially planned initiation of oral feeding based on postoperative imaging findings. Management changes were categorized as either conservative management, primarily involving delayed oral feeding and prolonged fasting, or surgical intervention, including controlled pharyngostoma. Clinical outcomes assessed included time to oral feeding, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay. Time to oral feeding and hospital stay were recorded in days.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between the Gastrografin–CT group and the conventional imaging group were performed using the Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Imaging Performance

Imaging performance was evaluated among patients in whom pharyngeal dehiscence or leak was detected on postoperative imaging, leading to a change from the initially planned oral feeding. The Gastrografin followed by CT protocol demonstrated significantly improved leak localization compared with conventional imaging. The mean localization score was significantly higher in the Gastrografin–CT group than in the conventional imaging group (3.00 ± 0.00 vs. 1.62 ± 0.83, p = 0.01).

Consistent with this finding, surgically actionable localization was achieved in all patients in the Gastrografin–CT group, compared with 51.2% in the conventional imaging group (100% vs. 51.2%, p = 0.03). Regarding the timing of detection, pharyngeal dehiscence or leak was identified on the initial imaging in all patients in the Gastrografin–CT group (100%) and in 82.9% of patients in the conventional imaging group. Delayed fistula following initially negative imaging occurred in 17.1% of patients in the conventional imaging group but was not observed in the Gastrografin–CT group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.57). Overall, the Gastrografin followed by CT protocol demonstrated superior imaging performance for leak localization (Table 2).

In one case, residual Gastrografin contrast from the preceding esophagography was clearly visualized on noncontrast CT, enabling precise localization of the pharyngeal leak site. The contrast material delineated the leakage tract, allowing accurate identification of the surgical entry point and facilitating targeted surgical planning (Figure 1). In contrast, another case showed that conventional esophagography failed to detect a leak, leading to the initiation of oral feeding. Subsequent CT performed due to neck swelling suggested the presence of a leak and allowed partial localization of the suspected dehiscence site, although the leakage tract was not clearly visualized. This highlights a potential limitation of esophagography alone in detecting early pharyngeal leaks(Figure 2). In a third case, esophagography demonstrated contrast leakage; however, the exact site of dehiscence could not be determined. A CT scan performed the following day without oral contrast also failed to localize the leak site, as no residual contrast material was present to delineate the leakage tract. This illustrates the limitation of delayed CT without ingested contrast for accurate localization (Figure 3).

Overall, these cases demonstrate that performing CT immediately after Gastrografin swallow improves anatomical delineation of leak sites and supports more precise surgical planning.

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 4. The median time to oral feeding was slightly shorter in the Gastrografin–CT group compared with the conventional imaging group (12.0 [IQR, 9.0–20.5] days vs. 14.0 [IQR, 12.0–22.0] days), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.45). Overall, clinical outcomes were similar between the two imaging strategies, although the Gastrografin–CT protocol showed a trend toward earlier oral feeding and shorter hospitalization (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that performing CT immediately after an oral Gastrografin swallow significantly improves anatomical localization of pharyngeal leaks following total laryngectomy. Compared with conventional imaging strategies, the Gastrografin–CT protocol showed superior localization performance, with significantly higher localization scores and a greater proportion of surgically actionable findings. Notably, in all patients with confirmed leak in the Gastrografin–CT group, imaging provided sufficient anatomical information to guide targeted surgical exploration, whereas conventional imaging frequently failed to identify the precise site of dehiscence.

Pharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF) remains the most common and clinically significant complication after total laryngectomy, with reported incidence ranging from approximately 10% to over 30%, and even higher in salvage settings [1, 3, 12, 13]. PCF is associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, delayed adjuvant therapy, and increased healthcare costs, underscoring the importance of early detection and accurate localization [5, 12]. In this context, optimal imaging strategies play a critical role not only in diagnosis but also in guiding subsequent management.

Esophagography has traditionally been used as a first‐line imaging modality for detecting postoperative leaks; however, its diagnostic performance is limited. Previous studies have reported low sensitivity, with rates as low as 26%, despite relatively high specificity [14]. Furthermore, contrast swallow studies may have limited positive predictive value and may either miss early or intermittent leaks or overestimate clinically irrelevant findings [9]. These limitations may lead to delayed diagnosis or inappropriate clinical decision‐making, particularly when imaging findings do not correlate with clinical suspicion.

Cross‐sectional imaging modalities such as CT provide superior anatomical detail and are useful for evaluating postoperative complications, including fistula, abscess, and soft tissue changes [2]. However, CT performed without intraluminal contrast may fail to demonstrate the precise leakage tract, limiting its utility for accurate localization [10]. Recent studies have suggested that combining oral contrast with advanced imaging modalities can significantly improve detection accuracy and anatomical delineation of leakage sites [11].

In this context, our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating that a simple modification of workflow—performing CT immediately after Gastrografin swallow—can effectively integrate the strengths of both modalities. This approach allows simultaneous detection and precise anatomical localization of the leak site, thereby improving surgical actionability compared with conventional sequential imaging strategies.

The improved localization performance observed with the Gastrografin–CT protocol likely results from the complementary strengths of contrast swallow studies and cross‐sectional imaging. As demonstrated in our illustrative cases (Figures 1, 2, 3), residual intraluminal contrast from preceding esophagography enabled clear visualization of the leakage tract on CT, allowing precise localization of the defect (Figure 1). In contrast, esophagography alone may fail to detect early or subtle leaks, as shown in Figure 2, where no leakage was identified despite the presence of a developing pharyngeal defect. Furthermore, delayed CT performed without ingested contrast may be insufficient for accurate localization, as illustrated in Figure 3, where the absence of residual contrast limited delineation of the leakage pathway. These findings highlight that while esophagography is effective for detecting contrast extravasation from the neopharynx, it provides limited spatial resolution regarding the exact location and extent of the defect, whereas CT offers high‐resolution cross‐sectional anatomical detail but may fail to demonstrate the leakage tract in the absence of intraluminal contrast [2, 14].

From a clinical perspective, accurate localization of the leak site is critical in the management of PCF. Early and precise identification of the defect may facilitate targeted surgical exploration and repair, potentially reducing surgical complexity and operative morbidity [3, 15]. Even when surgical intervention is already indicated, accurate identification of the dehiscence site and leakage pathway may provide valuable information for determining the surgical approach and extent of exploration.

In the present study, neither the overall management change rate nor the surgical intervention rate differed significantly between groups. However, the Gastrografin–CT group demonstrated significantly higher Leak Localization Scores and a greater proportion of Surgically Actionable Localization. These findings provide quantitative evidence that the combined protocol improves the anatomical information available for surgical planning, an advantage that may not be adequately reflected by management change or surgical intervention rates alone.

Nevertheless, these imaging‐based endpoints do not directly establish whether the surgical approach or extent of exploration was altered or whether surgical outcomes improved. Further studies evaluating concordance between imaging and operative findings, changes in operative planning, and surgical outcomes are needed to determine whether improved anatomical localization translates into measurable clinical benefits.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the small sample size of the Gastrografin–CT group and the unequal group sizes may have limited statistical power to detect differences in clinical outcomes. Third, imaging interpretation and localization scoring were performed retrospectively and may therefore be subject to observer bias. Furthermore, although surgically actionable localization was assessed as an imaging‐based endpoint, actual changes in operative planning and surgical outcomes were not separately evaluated. Although prior RT/CCRT and free flap reconstruction did not differ significantly between groups, other potential PCF risk factors were not comprehensively evaluated. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly given the small sample size of the Gastrografin–CT group.

Despite these limitations, several factors support the robustness of our findings. All patients underwent imaging according to a consistent institutional protocol, and leak localization was assessed using predefined criteria. Moreover, the observed differences in localization performance and surgical actionability were substantial and clinically meaningful. Taken together, these findings suggest that the Gastrografin–CT protocol represents a simple, practical, and effective imaging strategy for improving anatomical localization of pharyngeal leaks and may serve as a valuable adjunct in the postoperative management of patients undergoing total laryngectomy.

Conclusion

Esophagography followed immediately by CT improves the anatomical localization of postoperative pharyngeal leakage after total laryngectomy and provides a greater proportion of surgically actionable imaging findings. These improvements may provide valuable anatomical information for surgical planning, although their impact on actual operative decision‐making and surgical outcomes requires further investigation.
