Advancing quality evaluation and origin tracing of Hainan Camellia oleifera oil through Lipidomics and multidimensional analysis
Authors: Huan Huang, Pengyin Li, Rongli Li, Hongjian Zhang, Yan Tian, Lianhe Zheng, Qingsong Wang, Tian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Camellia oleifera oil is a high-value woody edible oil in China. To clarify origin-specific quality traits and formation mechanisms, this study systematically analyzed oil from four representative Hainan producing areas using a multidimensional approach, including physicochemical properties, functional components, fatty acids, volatiles and lipidomics. Multivariate statistics and KEGG pathway enrichment were applied to screen biomarkers and explore molecular mechanisms. Oils from different areas showed distinct characteristics, with Qiongzhong oil demonstrating optimal comprehensive quality. Core origin-discriminative biomarkers and four key metabolic pathways closely related to quality variation were identified, with altitude and soil pH as the primary environmental factors. This study fills the gap in tropical Camellia oil research and provides technical support for origin tracing and quality evaluation.

Introduction

Camellia oleifera oil (Camellia oleifera Abel.) is a woody edible oil unique to China (Xu et al., 2025). Rich in bioactive components such as oleic acid (>75%), polyphenols, sterols, and squalene, it possesses physiological functions including antioxidant properties and lipid regulation (Qin et al., 2024). Hailed as the “Eastern olive oil”, it holds broad application prospects in food, health supplements, and other sectors (Zhang et al., 2022). Hainan, a major Camellia oil production area in China's tropical region, possesses unique geographical and climatic conditions. These conditions create diverse ecological niches, providing differentiated growth environments for Camellia seeds (Kong et al., 2025). Consequently, Camellia seed oil quality may exhibit significant regional specificity.

In terms of research progress, Camellia oil quality evaluation has evolved from traditional physicochemical indicators (e.g., acid value, peroxide value) and fatty acid composition analysis to comprehensive characterization of functional compounds (e.g., polyphenols, tocopherols, β-sitosterol) and volatile aroma substances (Wang et al., 2017). Methodologically, analytical techniques have advanced from gas chromatography (GC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for single-component quantification to multi-omics approaches such as lipidomics and metabolomics, which enable systematic deciphering of molecular-level variations (Wei et al., 2022). Recent studies have increasingly focused on origin-specific quality traits, attempting to link environmental factors with metabolic profiles, though most remain limited to temperate production areas (Chang et al., 2025).

As consumer demand for “origin traceability” and “functionalization” of premium plant oils continues to rise, clarifying the quality characteristics and formation mechanisms of Camellia seed oil from different regions has become crucial for industry advancement (Fu et al., 2023). Current research on Camellia seed oil quality predominantly focuses on physicochemical properties, individual functional compounds, or fatty acid composition analysis (Qin et al., 2024). Systematic studies on tropical production areas remain scarce, particularly lacking in-depth investigations into the volatile aroma characteristics, lipid molecular composition, and environmental regulation mechanisms of Hainan-produced Camellia seed oil (Wang et al., 2022). Existing studies predominantly employ single-dimensional analytical methods, making it difficult to reveal the essence of regional differences at the molecular level (Shi et al., 2020). Furthermore, the association between environmental factors and quality indicators often remains at the level of simple correlation analysis, lacking integrated interpretation at the metabolic pathway level.

As a vital branch of metabolomics, lipidomics enables precise identification of lipid molecule types and concentration changes, providing robust technical support for deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying plant oil quality variations (Han, 2016). This study integrates multidimensional analyses of physicochemical properties, functional compounds, fatty acid composition, volatile compounds, and lipidomics to systematically characterize the quality traits of Camellia seed oil from four representative Hainan production areas (Wu et al., 2020). By screening region-specific biomarkers and integrating environmental factors, we aim to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying quality variations. This study seeks to fill the gap in research on regional differences in Hainan Camellia seed oil, providing technical methodologies and theoretical foundations for quality evaluation and origin tracing.

Camellia seed samples were collected from four representative production areas in Hainan Province, namely Qionghai, Qiongzhong, Chengmai, and Wuzhishan, during the same period of full seed maturity. Sample collection and preparation

Four counties in Hainan Province—Qionghai, Qiongzhong, Chengmai, and Wuzhishan—were selected as sampling areas. Qiongzhong and Wuzhishan are located in the central mountainous area with high altitude, low temperature and abundant rainfall; Qionghai is located in the eastern coastal plain with high temperature and sufficient precipitation; Chengmai is located in the western coastal area with relatively low rainfall and sandy soil. This gradient design can effectively isolate the effects of different environmental factors on the quality of Camellia oleifera oil. Sampling trees were chosen based on consistent growth vigor, tree age, and fruit development. Oil tea fruits were harvested from trees of similar age, balanced growth vigor, and comparable fruit characteristics.

Harvested Camellia oleifera fruits are dried at 60 °C to remove their shells, resulting in Camellia oleifera seeds with a moisture content of approximately 5%. After complete hull removal, a portion of the seeds was tested for kernel yield and crude fat content. The remaining seeds were crushed and cold-pressed using a screw press. The oil was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 10 min, then refrigerated for subsequent oil quality testing and analysis.

Physicochemical properties

The acid value, peroxide value, oil content, and kernel yield of the samples were determined following established international standards. Acid value was analyzed in accordance with AOCS Cd 3d-63, peroxide value was determined based on AOCS Cd 8b-90, and oil content was measured according to ISO 659. Kernel yield was assessed using a standard gravimetric method following the principle outlined in SN/T 0803.10–1999.

Functional compounds

Total phenolic content

Total phenolic content was determined with a slight modification of the method described by Ma et al. Briefly, 2.0 g of oil sample was extracted three times using 1 mL n-hexane and 2 mL 80% methanol to obtain the extract. For the color reaction, 0.5 mL of the extract was mixed with 1 mL water and 0.25 mL Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) reagent, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 3 min. After adding 0.75 mL of 1% sodium carbonate solution, the absorbance was measured at 765 nm following a 1-h reaction. Results were expressed as gallic acid equivalents per kilogram of oil (mg GAE/kg) (Kupina et al., 2018).

Flavonoid content

Flavonoid content was assayed using a method adapted from Ferreira et al., with the same extraction procedure as total phenols: 2.0 g of oil sample was triple-extracted with 1 mL n-hexane and 2 mL 80% methanol. The reaction system was prepared by sequentially adding 0.3 mL of 5% sodium nitrite, 0.3 mL of 10% aluminum chloride, and 2 mL of 1 mol/L sodium hydroxide to 1 mL of the extract. After incubating in the dark for 10 min, absorbance was measured at 510 nm, and results were expressed as rutin equivalents per kilogram of oil (mg RE/kg) (Ferreira et al., 2024).

Squalene and vitamin E content

Squalene content was determined by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) according to the method described in ISO 15086-2:2021 for the determination of squalene in animal and vegetable fats and oils. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol) content was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography with fluorescence detection (HPLC–FLD), following the procedure outlined in AOAC Official Method 2012.04.

β-sitosterol content

β-Sitosterol was quantified by gas chromatography. Oil samples were first saponified with 2 mol/L KOH-ethanol solution at 60 °C, followed by extraction with n-hexane. The extract was derivatized and injected into a TG-5SILMS column for separation, with detection performed using a flame ionization detector (FID) set at 320 °C. Quantification was carried out using an external standard method (Elhady et al., 2022).

Volatile compounds

Following the method described by Liang et al. (Liang et al., 2022) with minor modifications. The headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) procedure was performed as follows: Accurately weigh 5.0 g of Camellia oil sample into a 20 mL headspace vial, add 10 μL of 2-octanol (100 μg/mL, internal standard), and seal the vial with a PTFE‑silicone septum. The vial was equilibrated in a 40 °C constant temperature water bath for 30 min with a stirring speed of 500 r/min. Then a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was inserted into the headspace of the vial to extract the volatile compounds for 40 min at 40 °C. After extraction, the fiber was immediately inserted into the GC injection port and desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min with a split ratio of 10:1 for subsequent analysis. Gas chromatography conditions: Temperature program: initial temperature 60 °C, held for 5 min; ramped at 3 °C/min to 180 °C, held for 1 min; ramped at 30 °C/min to 260 °C, held for 5 min. Carrier gas: high-purity nitrogen at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Mass spectrometry conditions: Scan range 45–600 amu; ion source temperature 300 °C; transfer line temperature 270 °C. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing experimental mass spectra with the NIST 17 mass spectral library, and linear retention indices (LRIs) were calculated based on C7–C40 homologous alkenes. The final concentration of volatile compounds was calculated based on the ratio of target compound peak area to internal standard peak area, expressed in μg/g.

Fatty acid composition

Fatty Acid Composition: Determined according to ISO 12966. Gas Chromatography Conditions: TR-FAME column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm); temperature program: initial temperature 100 °C, hold for 13 min, then increase at 10 °C/min to 180 °C. Hold at 180 °C for 6 min, then increase to 200 °C at 1 °C/min and hold for 20 min. Increase to 230 °C at 4 °C/min and hold for 10.5 min. Split ratio: 40:1. Detector and injection port temperature: 250 °C. Injection volume: 1 μL.

Lipidomics analysis

Sample preparation

Accurately weigh 0.1 g of Camellia oil sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, add 200 μL of pre-chilled 75% methanol, and grind with steel balls for 3 min. After vortexing for 1 min, the sample was sonicated on ice for 3 min. Then, 30 μL of methanol and 600 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) were added to the tube, and the mixture was shaken at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 160 μL of ultrapure water was added, vortexed thoroughly to mix, and left to stand at room temperature for 30 min for phase separation. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, then the upper organic supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and dried under nitrogen. The dried extract was resuspended in 500 μL of acetonitrile-isopropanol (CH₃CN-IPA, 1:1, v/v) mixture, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was injected for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatographic conditions

Column: BEH C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, USA); mobile phase A was water (containing 0.1% formic acid), mobile phase B was acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1) (containing 0. v1% formic acid); The gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0–2 min, 30% B; 2–10 min, 30% B linear increased to 90% B; 10–12 min, 90% B maintained; 12–13 min, 90% B linear decreased to 30% B; 13–15 min, 30% B maintained for column equilibration. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, injection volume was 20 μL, and column temperature was 40 °C.

Mass spectrometry conditions

Tandem mass-to-mass scanning mode was employed. Electrospray ionization capillary voltage, injection voltage, and collision voltage were set at 1.0 kV, 40 V, and 6 eV, respectively. Ion source temperature and desolvation oven temperature were maintained at 120 °C and 500 °C, respectively. Carrier gas flow rate: 900 L/h. Mass spectrum scanning range: 50–1000 m/z. Resolution: 30,000.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD, n = 3). One-way ANOVA combined with Duncan's multiple range test was used to assess intergroup differences (p < 0.05). Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using SIMCA 16.0 software (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) to identify differentially expressed lipids (VIP > 1 and p < 0.05). R 4.3.0 software (ggplot2 package) was used for heatmap generation, correlation analysis, and visualization, while MetaboAnalyst 5.0 software performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes (10 μL) of all experimental samples to represent the average composition of the analyzed samples. The pooled QC sample was injected once at the beginning of the analytical run, and then injected every 10 experimental samples to monitor the stability of the UHPLC-MS/MS system throughout the analysis. A solvent blank was injected between the QC sample and the experimental sample to avoid carryover. The instrument stability was validated by the following criteria: total ion current (TIC) overlap of all QC samples >85%, and the relative standard deviation (RSD) of the peak area of lipid molecules in QC samples <5%. Representative TIC chromatograms of QC samples in both positive and negative ion modes are shown in Fig. S1. The high overlap of these profiles confirms the stability of the instrument throughout the analysis. The fitting parameters of the PLS-DA model are summarized in Table S1.

Results and discussion

Differences in physicochemical properties among Camellia oleifera oil from different producing areas

The physicochemical properties of Camellia seed oil from the four production areas showed significant differences (Table 2). The acid value of oil from Qiongzhong (0.27 ± 0.00 mg KOH/g) was significantly lower than that of other areas (p < 0.05), indicating optimal oil freshness (Yu et al., 2022). The Wuzhishan region exhibited the highest peroxide value (3.46 ± 0.03 mmol/kg), potentially linked to accelerated oil oxidation rates in high-altitude environments (Li et al., 2024). Qiongzhong demonstrated the highest oil content (47.23 ± 0.52%) and kernel yield (56.08 ± 1.63%), closely associated with the abundant nutrient supply from its fertile red soil; The Chengmai region exhibited the lowest kernel yield (48.94 ± 1.32%), potentially influenced by soil texture. Differences in volatile compounds among Camellia oleifera oil from different producing areas

A total of 42 volatile compounds were identified using Headspace Solid-Phase Microextraction-Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), including aldehydes (5 types), terpenes (5 types), alcohols (4 types), esters (4 types), and others (24 types). The Chengmai region exhibited the highest total volatile compound content, with d-limonene reaching 100.76 ± 1.35 μg/g. Characterized by distinct citrus and mint aromas, it serves as the region's signature flavor component. The Wuzhishan region is rich in isocitral (5.77 ± 0.01 μg/g) and 1-pentanol (10.12 ± 0.49 μg/g), forming a complex aroma profile of woody and grassy notes. Qionghai region exhibits higher pentanal (37.26 ± 2.93 μg/g) and methoxyacetic acid (40.20 ± 0.14 μg/g) levels. Pentanal contributes almond and malt aromas, while methoxyacetic acid imparts a distinctive oily, buttery note. Differences in fatty acid composition among Camellia oleifera oil from different producing areas

The unsaturated fatty acid (UFA) content of Camellia seed oil from all four production areas ranged between 85.83% and 87.85%, with oleic acid (C18:1) as the predominant component, consistent with the typical high-oleic characteristic of Camellia seed oil (Table 4). The Chengmai region exhibited the highest oleic acid content (81.87 ± 0.02%) and the lowest linoleic acid content (5.01 ± 0.00%). This may correlate with the region's lower annual precipitation, where water stress potentially inhibits fatty acid desaturase (FAD2) activity, thereby reducing linoleic acid synthesis (Gao et al., 2022). The Wuzhishan region exhibited the highest linoleic acid content (9.71 ± 0.01%), where temperature fluctuations at high altitudes may enhance FAD2 activity and increase polyunsaturated fatty acid levels (Ye et al., 2024). The Qiongzhong region had the highest stearic acid content (2.97 ± 0.01%), while the Qionghai region exhibited higher palmitic acid content (10.69 ± 0.04%) than the Chengmai region (9.39 ± 0.03%). Lipidomic characteristics of Camellia oleifera oil and origin-related variations

Lipid classification and relative abundance distribution

Through lipidomic analysis of Camellia oil from four representative production areas in Hainan (Qionghai, Qiongzhong, Chengmai, and Wuzhishan), a total of 839 lipid compounds were identified, covering five major classes and 20 subclasses (Fig. 2). Glycerolipids are the most abundant class, accounting for 50.8% of the total lipids (426 compounds), mainly consisting of 389 triglyceride (TG) subtypes and 37 diacylglycerol (DG) subtypes, which form the nutritional and energetic core of Camellia oil. Monoglycerides (MG), a minor subcategory within glycerolipids (6 compounds), contribute to the oil's emulsification properties (Li et al., 2022). The second most abundant class is glycerophospholipids, with 215 compounds representing 25.6% of the total, including phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidic acid (PA) among other subclasses—these lipids play critical roles in maintaining oil stability and functional activity (Zhong et al., 2023). Sphingolipids (61 compounds, 7.3%) are dominated by hexosylceramides (Hexosylceramide, e.g., Hex1Cer) and sphingosine (SPH), whose subtype distribution is closely linked to soil pH, reflecting the adaptive response of Camellia oil to environmental conditions. Fatty acyls (13 compounds, 1.5%) and sterol lipids (12 compounds, 1.4%) are present in smaller quantities: fatty acyls include short-chain and medium-chain fatty acid derivatives that serve as potential precursors of volatile aroma substances, while sterol lipids are led by β-sitosterol, enhancing the oil's health benefits and oxidative stability. The remaining 102 compounds (12.4%) are classified as other lipids, encompassing functional components such as squalene and vitamin E, as well as minor lipid derivatives that complement the oil's nutritional profile (Zhong et al., 2023).

Across all four production areas, the abundance order of lipid classes is consistently glycerolipids > glycerophospholipids > sphingolipids > other lipids > sterol lipids > fatty acyls, an inherent characteristic of Camellia oil that reflects its specificity as a woody edible oil (Li et al., 2022). Notably, while the overall class distribution is conserved, intra-class variations—such as the molecular diversity of TGs (389 subtypes) and the differential distribution of sphingolipid subtypes—are the key drivers of origin-specific quality differences. These variations lay the foundation for subsequent screening of origin-related biomarkers and analysis of environmental regulation mechanisms, providing a systematic lipidomic basis for deciphering the molecular essence of regional quality differences in Hainan Camellia oil (Deng et al., 2023).

Origin-specific lipid subclasses and their distribution patterns

Lipid subclasses in Camellia oil from four production areas exhibited distinct origin-specific distribution patterns, as revealed by hierarchical clustering and OPLS-DA. Fig. 3 illustrates structural variations of lipid subclasses across regions: Triacylglycerol (TG) dominated in all areas (38.73%–39.77%), with the highest proportion in Chengmai (39.77%) and the lowest in Wuzhishan (38.73%). Other subclasses (e.g., diacylglycerol, DG) also showed regionally differentiated proportions, laying the foundation for origin-specific lipid profiles. Fig. 4 reveals origin-specific metabolic networks: Regions with distinct environments (e.g., Wuzhishan vs Chengmai) showed more negative correlations, while geographically adjacent regions (e.g., Qionghai vs Qiongzhong) exhibited predominantly positive correlations (Deng et al., 2025). Within TG, Chengmai was enriched in oleic acid-rich species (e.g., TG(18:1/18:1/18:1)) (linked to FAD2 inhibition by moderate water stress), while Wuzhishan contained more linoleic acid-rich TGs (e.g., TG(18:1/18:2/16:0)) (linked to FAD2 activation by high-altitude temperature fluctuations) (Lin et al., 2018). For glycerophospholipids, Qionghai had higher phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, inhibiting lipid peroxidation) and Qiongzhong had higher phosphatidic acid (PA, participating in antioxidant signaling) (Park et al., 2021). Qionghai's higher phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) content is linked to its role in inhibiting lipid peroxidation by scavenging reactive oxygen species and reducing oxidative damage, which supports antioxidant defense and cellular protection. Screening and identification of differential lipid biomarkers

An OPLS-DA model was employed to analyze the lipidomics data. Using VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 as screening criteria, 32 differentially expressed lipids were identified. The detailed information of these differential lipids, including lipid class, VIP value, P-value, fold change and regulation trend in each pairwise comparison, is presented in Table S2. Further validation yielded 9 core biomarkers (AUC > 0.89) capable of precisely distinguishing Camellia oil origins (Table 5) (Wei et al., 2025). Specific markers for the Chengmai region included TG (18:1/18:1/18:1) (AUC = 0.96) and D -limonene (AUC = 0.98), with d-limonene showing specific enrichment (100.76 ± 1.35 μg/g) in Chengmai, serving as a signature flavor biomarker for this region; The specific markers for the Wuzhishan region are TG (18:1/18:2/16:0) (AUC = 0.94) and Hex1Cer (d14:0/18:1) (AUC = 0.90), associated with high linoleic acid content and acid soil adaptation, respectively (Dong et al., 2024). Squalene (AUC = 0.93), a characteristic marker of high-altitude environments, exhibited the highest concentration in the Wuzhishan region. The specific markers for the Qiongzhong region were β-linalool (AUC = 0.95) and PA (19:0/18:2) (AUC = 0.89), with β-linalool detected exclusively in Qiongzhong (0.86 ± 0.01 μg/g) and PA correlated with the region's low acid value (0.27 ± 0.00 mg KOH/g). The specific markers for the Qionghai region were PE (18:1/18:1) (AUC = 0.92) and valeraldehyde (AUC = 0.90), with PE closely associated with the lowest peroxide value in Qionghai. Glycerophospholipid metabolism (hsa00564) is a universal core pathway, significantly enriched across all comparisons with p-values ranging from 0.005 to 0.04 and enrichment ratios of 0.01 to 0.40. It involves the most differential lipids, as indicated by larger bubble sizes. In Qionghai, upregulated phosphatidylethanolamine synthesis inhibits lipid peroxidation, explaining the region's lowest peroxide value. Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) in Camellia oleifera oil exhibits strong antioxidant properties by scavenging free radicals, which interrupts the lipid peroxidation chain reaction and inhibits the formation of primary lipid peroxides, thereby significantly reducing the peroxide value of the oil (Zhong et al., 2023). Qionghai's neutral soil (pH 6.5) favors this synthesis. In Qiongzhong, enhanced phospholipase D activity converts phosphatidylcholine to phosphatidic acid, activating antioxidant signaling and promoting total phenolic accumulation, facilitated by moderate precipitation (1650 mm). This pathway is consistently enriched in high-altitude versus low-altitude comparisons, highlighting its role in mediating altitude-driven lipid differences (Yang et al., 2024).

Sphingolipid metabolism (hsa00600, p < 0.05) is functionally supported by phosphatidylinositol signaling system enrichment in three comparisons (p 0.01–0.015). Acidic soils in Wuzhishan and Qiongzhong upregulate hexosylceramide synthesis, enhancing lipid membrane stability for adaptation to high altitude or high precipitation. Neutral soils in Chengmai and Qionghai boost sphingosine kinase activity, converting sphingosine to 1-phosphosphingosine to regulate sterol synthesis. Soil pH directly influences sphingosine synthase activity, while intense UV radiation at Wuzhishan's high altitude further promotes sphingolipid accumulation to counter membrane damage. Glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor biosynthesis enriched in Wuzhishan versus Qionghai comparisons (p < 0.01) complements this metabolic network (Mitsuta et al., 2025).

Fatty acid elongation (hsa00062, p < 0.01) is validated by glycerolipid metabolism enrichment across all comparisons and fatty acid metabolism-related pathways in Qionghai versus Chengmai comparisons (p 0.01–0.04). In Chengmai, upregulated fatty acid elongase activity promotes oleic acid synthesis and accumulation of oleic acid-rich triglycerides. Low temperatures at Wuzhishan's high altitude inhibit this activity, favoring linoleic acid synthesis and related triglyceride accumulation (Wei et al., 2025).

Terpenoid biosynthesis (hsa00900, p < 0.01) is unique to Chengmai. Upregulated terpene synthase activity drives substantial d-limonene accumulation, presumably induced by high potassium content in local soils (Karunanithi & Zerbe, 2019). Its region-specific regulation is corroborated by distinct fatty acid metabolism enrichment in Qionghai versus Chengmai comparisons.

In summary, the four core pathways together with altitude (path coefficient 0.35) and soil pH (path coefficient 0.29) shape the origin-specific lipidomic characteristics of Hainan Camellia oil. Glycerophospholipid metabolism and glycerolipid metabolism are universal core pathways, while comparison-specific pathways reflect environmental specificity (Xie et al., 2025). These findings provide a comprehensive molecular explanation for quality differences, laying a foundation for origin tracing and quality regulation.

Correlation between environmental factors and quality indices of Camellia oleifera oil

Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant associations between environmental factors and Camellia seed oil quality indicators. Altitude showed a significant positive correlation with squalene content (r = 0.86, p < 0.01) and vitamin E content (r = 0.77, p < 0.01) (Wei et al., 2025). Intense ultraviolet radiation at high altitudes activated squalene synthase and tocopherol cyclase activity, promoting the accumulation of these components (Gu et al., 2025). Soil pH showed a significant positive correlation with flavonoid content (r = 0.82, p < 0.01) and a significant negative correlation with sphingolipid content (r = −0.76, p < 0.01). Acidic soils inhibit flavonoid synthesis while favoring sphingolipid accumulation (Long et al., 2022). Annual precipitation showed a significant positive correlation with total phenolic content (r = 0.69, p < 0.01) and a significant negative correlation with oleic acid content (r = −0.71, p < 0.01). Adequate precipitation promotes phenolic compound synthesis but inhibits oleic acid accumulation. Structural equation modeling further confirmed that elevation (path coefficient = 0.35) and soil pH (path coefficient = 0.29) are the primary environmental drivers influencing Camellia seed oil quality (Gao et al., 2022).

Conclusion

This study systematically characterized the quality differentiation and underlying regulatory mechanisms of Camellia oleifera oil from four representative production areas in Hainan, using an integrated strategy combining physicochemical property determination, functional component quantification, fatty acid and volatile compound profiling, and UHPLC-MS/MS-based lipidomics analysis. Distinct region-specific quality profiles were observed among the four production areas; notably, Camellia oleifera oil from Qiongzhong showed the optimal comprehensive quality based on entropy weight method integrating multiple key quality indicators, with outstanding freshness and antioxidant capacity. Lipidomics analysis screened a panel of core lipid biomarkers with high origin-discriminative ability, and KEGG enrichment analysis elucidated four key metabolic pathways closely associated with the regional quality variation, which were further found to be predominantly related to two key environmental factors: altitude and soil pH.

Rigorous experimental design and statistical validation strategies were implemented to ensure the reliability of multivariate statistical analysis and biomarker discovery. All biological replicates were collected from independent core planting plots in each region with consistent tree age, growth vigor and fruit maturity; strict statistical validation including nested cross-validation, 200-time permutation tests, and AUC-based discriminative ability evaluation were performed for OPLS-DA model construction and biomarker screening, fully ensuring the credibility of our results based on the available experimental data.

This study fills the research gap in systematic quality evaluation of tropical Camellia oleifera oil, provides a comprehensive molecular basis for the origin traceability and quality control of Camellia oleifera oil, and offers practical guidance for region-specific precise cultivation and high-value utilization of Camellia oleifera resources in tropical areas. Future studies will expand the sample size and sampling range across multiple harvest years to further verify the universality of the screened biomarkers, and deeply decipher the regulatory network of environmental factors on the quality formation of Camellia oleifera oil.
