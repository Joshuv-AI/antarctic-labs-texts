Contextual effects of monetary incentives on acceptance of unfair offers in the ultimatum game
Authors: Peipei Jin, Qian Chang, Chengxi Ding, Jinying Ouyang, Yinuo Xu, Zhuoyu Li, Wuji Lin, Ruixiang Gao, Lei Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Responders in the Ultimatum Game (UG) often reject unfair offers, yet previous studies remain inconsistent about when monetary incentives attenuate such rejections. To clarify this issue, we conducted four complementary experiments focusing on responders’ acceptance decisions while varying incentive realism, gain range, task structure, and participant background. Experiment 1 used a hypothetical one-shot questionnaire and did not support the predicted interaction pattern. Experiment 2 introduced real monetary incentives and revealed main effects of fairness and responder gain, but still no interaction. Experiment 3 substantially expanded the gain range in a repeated virtual UG and showed that gain-related increases in acceptance were stronger under lower-fairness offers than under higher-fairness offers. Experiment 4, conducted under a fixed very unfair offer and real monetary rewards, showed that gain-related changes in acceptance were markedly steeper among college students than among working adults. Across studies, gain-related acceptance patterns depended on task context, gain range, and participant background. These findings suggest that fairness concerns and monetary incentives jointly shape UG acceptance, and that high gains can attenuate unfairness-based rejection under some conditions.

Introduction

From the perspective of standard economic theory, if individual utility depends solely on one’s own monetary payoff, responders in the Ultimatum Game (UG) should accept any non-zero offer. However, a substantial body of research has consistently shown that actual decision making in the UG deviates from this prediction of the “economic man”: proposers typically refrain from making extremely self-serving offers, and responders often reject clearly unfair low offers6,7,16,18,27. At the responder level, low offers are frequently rejected on the grounds of unfairness, even when doing so requires individuals to forgo attainable gains2,8,29. These findings suggest that decision making in the UG is not driven exclusively by personal monetary utility; fairness preferences and inequity aversion also constitute important behavioral foundations10,20,31. Although fairness preferences exert a stable influence in the UG, under what conditions do monetary gains become sufficiently important to weaken responders’ fairness-based rejection? The existing literature has not yet provided a unified answer to this question.

It should be noted that rejection of unfair offers in the UG does not necessarily arise from a single psychological motive. Existing research suggests that rejection of low offers may reflect concern for fairness itself, but may also involve strategic consideration of the material consequences of accepting a low payoff15,35. Individuals with different social preference types also show systematic differences in proposer and responder behavior, indicating that monetary gain and fairness concerns are not two isolated forces, but may jointly enter the decision process in different ways19,33.

Based on this conclusion, researchers have increasingly focused on a core question: which exerts greater influence on UG decisions, monetary gain or fairness concern? Forsythe et al.12 conducted two comparative experiments, one with real monetary rewards and one without material incentives. They found that in both experiments, proposers’ offers were concentrated between 30% and 50% (78% in the incentive condition and 79% in the no-incentive condition), whereas the average rejection rates of responders were 7% and 17%, respectively. This finding suggests that responders are more likely to reject unfair offers when such rejection carries no economic cost. A subsequent question is how decisions on both sides of the game change as the total stake increases. Using a questionnaire design, Tompkinson and Bethwaite32 compared decisions under total stakes of $10 and $10,000 and found that, as the total stake increased, proposers offered smaller proportions (i.e., fairness declined), while responders also lowered their minimum acceptable proportion. In contrast, Munier and Zaharia25, who manipulated total stake across multiple levels, reported the opposite pattern: proposers’ offer proportions remained unchanged as the total stake increased (i.e., fairness remained stable), whereas only responders’ minimum acceptance threshold declined.

These inconsistent findings point to a central limitation. In many cases, it is difficult to isolate the independent role of one variable while strictly controlling the other, which in turn makes it hard to determine whether UG participants are more concerned with fairness or with monetary gain. A more precise approach therefore requires focusing on two core variables: responders’ personal gain and their perception of fairness. In this context, one point deserves particular emphasis. Previous studies have often used total stake size as the indicator of stake magnitude. Yet an increase in the total stake does not necessarily mean that the responder’s own payoff is large enough to change behavior. Under highly unfair splits, even a large total amount may still leave the responder with a rather limited personal payoff, making it difficult to directly test whether high gains weaken unfairness-based rejection. Put differently, the key issue is not the total size of the endowment itself, but whether the responder’s absolute individual payoff has reached a level sufficient to alter behavioral tendencies when facing unfair offers.

Whether gains are sufficient to alter unfairness-based rejection is reflected not only in differences in behavioral outcomes, but also in the way individuals weigh fairness against personal gain during the decision process. Process-oriented research suggests that rejection of unfairness does not necessarily stem from a single motive; rather, it may reflect concern for fairness itself, aversion to unequal outcomes, or evaluation of the material consequences of accepting a low payoff19,33,35. Dual-process perspectives further propose that rejection of unfair offers may more often reflect a rapid, emotionally loaded response, whereas accepting an unfair offer may require more deliberate evaluation of one’s own material gain; however, current evidence does not support a single unified process model for all fairness decisions17. In the UG, eye-tracking studies have also shown that both attention to one’s own payoff and comparison of the two parties’ payoffs influence final acceptance decisions, indicating that self-interest evaluation and fairness evaluation are not mutually exclusive, but dynamically interact during the decision process36. At the same time, individuals with higher cognitive reflection are more likely to accept unfair offers, suggesting that the influence of monetary gain on decision making may be enhanced when task conditions more fully engage calculation of one’s own payoff3. Although the present study does not directly examine the underlying neural mechanisms of the UG, this process-oriented literature offers important insights into the gain–fairness trade-off: when task conditions more strongly reinforce the material significance of personal gain, the influence of monetary gain on decision making may correspondingly increase.

Likewise, the relative influence of fairness and gain under high-stakes conditions remains unresolved in the literature. One line of research has shown that responders’ rejection behavior remains relatively stable even when monetary rewards increase substantially, suggesting that fairness preferences do not automatically disappear as stakes rise5,30. Another line of research, however, indicates that when unfair offers occur with sufficient frequency and responders are faced with sufficiently large absolute gains, their tendency to accept unfair offers increases significantly; under extremely high stakes, their behavior may even move toward a more gain-maximizing pattern1,22,26. These findings further suggest that the effect of gain on the acceptance of unfair offers is not a simple or stable main effect, but is more likely to depend on the experimental design and the specific context.

Later studies have examined in greater detail the boundary conditions under which responders reject unfair offers. For instance, economic status may affect individuals’ sensitivity to distributive unfairness9, and uncertainty may change how individuals balance fairness concerns against potential gains23. Taken together, this body of work suggests that responders’ reactions to unfair offers are not determined by any single factor, but may instead be jointly shaped by gain magnitude, task format, and participant background. Although previous research has begun to consider the interaction between gain and fairness in the UG4, most studies still focus on relative offer proportions or fixed reward levels, and only rarely investigate the combined effects of large differences in absolute gain, incentive realism, and participants’ broad economic background. Consequently, it remains unclear under what conditions monetary incentives exert sufficient behavioral force to alter responders’ willingness to accept unfair offers.

In summary, the long-standing inconsistency in previous findings may arise not simply from differences in samples or methodology, but more fundamentally from the fact that the trade-off between gain and fairness is itself constrained by multiple boundary conditions, including task setting, reward magnitude, and participant background. Against this background, the present study addresses a more specific question: under what conditions does responders’ personal gain exert a stronger influence on their acceptance of unfair allocations? More specifically, we examine whether increased gain raises responders’ willingness to accept unfair offers under certain conditions, and whether this pattern varies as a function of incentive realism, task structure, and participants’ economic background.

Based on the literature and research gap outlined above, the present study follows three main design principles. First, we focus on responders and treat responders’ absolute personal gain as the key manipulated variable, in order to distinguish more clearly the relative roles of monetary gain and fairness perception. Second, by varying incentive realism and task structure, we examine whether the gain–fairness trade-off changes as the material consequences of decisions become more salient and as individuals are repeatedly exposed to the task. Third, we include groups differing in broad economic background in order to test whether the subjective economic utility of the same absolute gain moderates acceptance of unfair offers.

To this end, we conducted four experiments (see Table 1) to determine the level of responders’ personal gain required to effectively weaken unfairness-based rejection, and to test whether this pattern remains stable across different task settings and participant groups. Experiment 1 used a one-shot hypothetical questionnaire design with a relatively narrow gain range to examine basic patterns of individual decision making. Experiment 2 retained the same factorial design logic but introduced real monetary incentives to test whether actual economic consequences alter the balance between gain and fairness when other conditions are tightly controlled. Experiment 3 employed a repeated virtual UG with a substantially expanded gain range to examine whether large increases in responders’ gain would increase acceptance of unfair offers. Experiment 4 fixed the offer at a highly unfair ratio and, under real incentive conditions, examined whether groups differing in broad economic background and income independence would show differential behavioral responses to the same absolute gain because of differences in its subjective economic utility.

Taken together, the four experiments provide a relatively systematic account of the conditions under which, and the groups for whom, responders’ gain more strongly increases acceptance of unfair offers.

Experiment 1

Purpose

This study examined whether responders’ acceptance rates in a hypothetical, unincentivized Ultimatum Game varied as a function of fairness, responder gain, and their interaction. We expected that acceptance would differ across combinations of fairness and gain, such that lower-fairness offers would be less likely to be accepted when responder gain was low than when it was high.

Methods

Participants

A total of 116 college students (40 females, Mage=24.4, SDage=1.43) participated in this UG as responders and were divided randomly into four groups (consisting of 29 students each); each group was randomly assigned to one of the following four experimental conditions. All participants signed the informed consent form before the experiment began.

Design and materials

As shown in Table 2, the experiment employed a 2 (fairness: relatively higher, 30%; relatively lower, 20%) × 2 (responder gain: high, 20 yuan; low, 2 yuan) between-subjects design. The two fairness levels were selected with reference to Ultimatum Game findings in Chinese samples, where offers around 20% are commonly treated as clearly disadvantageous and more likely to be rejected, whereas offers around 30% are closer to the lower boundary of what may be perceived as comparatively more acceptable (e.g.,11,24,34. We therefore treated 30% and 20% as representing a contrast in relative fairness, rather than a strict fair-versus-unfair dichotomy. The two gain levels were selected to create a clear difference in responder gain within a simple questionnaire-based setting, while maintaining a procedure that was brief and easy for participants to understand.

The materials took the form of questionnaires, of which there were four versions in total, differing only in responder gain and fairness level. In the first version, fairness was set at 30% and responder gain at 20 yuan; in the second version, fairness was set at 30% and responder gain at 2 yuan; in the third version, fairness was set at 20% and responder gain at 20 yuan; and in the fourth version, fairness was set at 20% and responder gain at 2 yuan.

Procedure

Participants were asked to indicate their willingness to accept the offer using an online questionnaire distributed via Wenjuanxing, a Chinese online questionnaire survey platform resembling Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, which offers high-quality sample collection services. For example, in the first version of the questionnaire, the participants were faced with the following situation: “Suppose that we offer you 67 yuan to share with a stranger. One of you, chosen by drawing lots, has the right to decide how to distribute the money. The person chosen can propose any distribution plan that they want, but the other person can decide whether to accept or reject it. In case of acceptance, each person will share the 67 yuan in accordance with the distribution plan; in case of rejection, neither of you will receive a penny.” After pressing the “continue” button for lottery, the participants were introduced to the result: “Now, the result of the lottery is that the other person has been chosen to propose a distribution plan, and you can decide whether to accept it or not.” Next, after waiting for a minute, the participants were presented with the distribution scheme and asked to choose acceptance or rejection— “The distribution plan that he proposes is 47 yuan for him and 20 yuan for you. Will you accept or reject it?” Each participant in this experiment completed only one version of the questionnaire, and each questionnaire contained only one question. After completing the questionnaire, participants did not receive any money regardless of whether they chose to accept or reject the distribution, a fact of which participants were aware prior to the study.

Statistical analysis

If the responder accepted the offer, the response was coded as 1, whereas if the responder rejected the offer, the response was coded as 0. In accordance with the research goal, we focused mainly on whether absolute monetary gains would impact responders’ acceptance as well as whether this impact would vary across different levels of fairness, which should be indicated by a 2 × 2 analysis of variance (ANOVA) with no repeated measures. Age and gender were reported descriptively as sample characteristics.

Results

The acceptance rates associated with the four experimental conditions are displayed in Fig. 1a, and the corresponding condition-wise counts and acceptance rates are reported in Table 2. Descriptively, acceptance rates were 89.66% in the relatively higher-fairness/high-gain condition, 75.86% in the relatively higher-fairness/low-gain condition, 65.52% in the relatively lower-fairness/high-gain condition, and 75.86% in the relatively lower-fairness/low-gain condition. Sensitive power analysis showed that our sample size had 80% power to detect an effect size of 0.26 in Cohen’s f (α = 0.05). The ANOVA results showed that the main effect of responder gain was not significant, F (1, 112) = 0.049, p = .826, partial η² < 0.001. The main effect of fairness was also not significant, F (1, 112) = 2.382, p = .126, partial η² = 0.021. The interaction between fairness and responder gain was likewise not significant, F (1, 112) = 2.382, p = .126, partial η² = 0.021.

Discussion

The results were in contrast with our hypothesis, the reasons for which might be as follows. First, since the UG experiment was conducted in a virtual form using the questionnaire and the participants did not receive any real monetary return, economic gains did not play their proposed incentivizing role. Subjects may consider obtaining money based on decisions as a low-probability event and view the allocation as hypothetical or a small gamble3. Accordingly, participants made decisions mainly on the basis of fairness. Second, the highest monetary return in this experiment was only 20 yuan, which was not sufficiently high to stimulate the participants. Compared to real money, hypothetical allocations are more likely to be rejected; and compared to large allocation amounts, smaller allocations are more likely to be rejected28. A third possibility concerns the fairness manipulation itself. The contrast between 30% and 20% may have represented only a relatively modest difference in perceived fairness, especially because 30% is near the lower boundary of offers that may be regarded as more acceptable rather than unequivocally fair. This may also have weakened the likelihood of observing a clear interaction pattern in Experiment 1.

Therefore, new experiments should be designed to exclude the impacts of these three possibilities. Experiment 2 aimed to explore the first possibility by focusing on a more authentic experimental setting in which participants were actually able to receive the money after playing the UG.

Experiment 2

Purpose

This study examined whether responders’ acceptance rates in a real-incentive Ultimatum Game varied as a function of fairness, responder gain, and their interaction. Relative to Experiment 1, the purpose of Experiment 2 was to test whether introducing actual monetary consequences would strengthen the behavioral effects of gain and fairness. We expected that acceptance would differ across combinations of fairness and gain, while also testing whether the gain effect would vary across fairness levels under real incentives.

Methods

Participants

A total of 159 college students participated in this UG. Although we focused only on the responders, to make the experiment more authentic, 32 of the participants were randomly assigned to be proposers, while the remaining 127 students (97 females, Mage=21.93, SDage=1.58) were randomly assigned to be responders. All the participants volunteered and consented to participate in the experiment, and as a reward for their participation, they received what they obtained in the UG game in addition to 5 yuan as the basic level of compensation. Thus, the final return for the participants depended on their decisions in the UG. All participants signed the informed consent form before the experiment began.

Design and materials

This UG experiment featured a 2 (fairness: high, 35%; low, 25%) × 2 (absolute monetary gain: high, 20 yuan; low, 2 yuan) between-subjects design and was conducted using a computer (see Table 2 as well). The high fairness level (35%) is adjusted based on Experiment 1 and references to Chinese workplace UG studies9, which found that offers around 35% are perceived as fair by most Chinese participants (especially college students) in real-money incentive scenarios. This is slightly higher than the 30% in Experiment 1 to better distinguish fair and unfair perceptions, while the low fairness level (25%) is set to be between the 20% unfair threshold and high fairness level to test the sensitivity of fairness perception. The four experimental conditions were randomly assigned to the responders by the computer.

Procedure

All the participants played the UG only once. The UG experiment was conducted in batches, with ten participants included in each batch. These ten participants were randomly divided into two groups (including five participants each) and were taken to two different places. Subsequently, participants were told that they would form a pair with someone in another group to play an online UG and that their role of proposer or responder was assigned randomly by the computer. But in fact, participants in both groups did not play online games with each other but were instead selected as responders based on computer settings in most cases. This deception was implemented to strengthen participants’ belief that they were interacting with a real proposer, thereby increasing the perceived realism of the UG scenario, which was pre-approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (as noted in the Institutional Review Board Statement). The deception was limited to the ‘existence of real proposers’ and did not involve misleading participants about monetary rewards—all participants who accepted offers received the promised monetary gains, and those who rejected received the basic compensation, ensuring no harm to participants’ interests. However, to increase the credibility of the game, one of the five players in each group was assigned to be the proposer and exclusive of data analysis. The remaining four people were all assigned to act as responders randomly under one of the four experimental conditions (see also Table 2 for the number of participants in each condition). Before beginning the game, the participants were informed of the rules of the game and subsequently asked to answer four questions about the rules of the UG to determine whether they had understood them. After beginning the game, the computer would randomly match another player with the participant and randomly assign roles to the two players, as this is what the participants were told. Thereafter, the computer would indicate the total amount of monetary reward, which could be one of four possibilities: 6 yuan (low gain but high fairness), 8 yuan (low gain and low fairness), 57 yuan (high gain and high fairness), and 80 yuan (high gain but low fairness). Subsequently, the participants who were assigned as responders waited for the distribution scheme given by the “proposer” and then decided whether to accept or reject it. Finally, the responders who accepted the offer actually received their gains from the UG in addition to another 5 yuan as basic remuneration, i.e., 7 yuan or 25 yuan in total, while the responders who rejected the offer and the proposers received only 5 yuan. After the experiment, all participants (including proposers and responders) were given a debriefing document and a one-on-one explanation by the experimenter. The debriefing included: (1) disclosing the deception (i.e., ‘the proposers you interacted with were not real participants, and the distribution schemes were pre-set by the computer’); (2) explaining the purpose of the deception (to ensure the authenticity of decision-making); (3) answering participants’ questions about the experiment; and (4) providing contact information for the research team for follow-up inquiries. No participants reported discomfort after the debriefing, and all agreed to retain their data for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis conducted for this experiment was the same as that conducted for Experiment 1.

Results

The acceptance rates are displayed in Fig. 1b, and the corresponding condition-wise counts and acceptance rates are reported in Table 2. Descriptively, acceptance rates were 90.32% in the relatively higher-fairness/high-gain condition, 77.42% in the relatively higher-fairness/low-gain condition, 79.31% in the relatively lower-fairness/high-gain condition, and 52.78% in the relatively lower-fairness/low-gain condition. Sensitive power analysis showed that our sample size had a power of 80% to detect the effect size of 0.25 in Cohen’s f (α = 0.05). The ANOVA results demonstrated that the main effects of absolute monetary gains (F (1,123) = 6.902, p = .010, partial η2 = 0.053) and fairness (F (1,123) = 5.642, p = .019, partial η2 = 0.044) were both statistically significant, whereas the interaction between these two factors was nonsignificant (F (1,123) = 0.824, p = .366). This result indicated that as absolute monetary gains increased, responders became more inclined to accept offers despite their low fairness.

Discussion

Unlike the results of Experiment 1, in this study, the participants were offered a real monetary return after the UG. Accordingly, this experiment found significant impacts of absolute monetary gains on responders’ acceptance. Therefore, the first possibility, i.e., that Experiment 1 did not find a significant main effect of monetary gains due to the inauthentic experimental setting, was confirmed.

However, this study did not find a significant interaction between absolute monetary gains and fairness, thus indicating that the influence of absolute monetary gains on responders’ acceptance did not vary across different levels of fairness, which was not in line with our hypothesis that responders’ decisions would be affected by fairness only within a certain range of economic gains. The most likely reason for this lack of verification is that the range of manipulated monetary gains was not sufficiently large, leading to little change in the role of fairness. Previous research has also found that the money effect in the Ultimatum Game is not very pronounced; although the total amount increased, the behavior of the players changed only slightly13.

As the second possibility for explaining the nonsignificant results of Experiment 2 that the monetary simulation was not high enough had not yet been explored, Experiment 3 aimed to examine how large variations in absolute monetary gains and fairness influence responders’ acceptance behaviors. Due to budgetary reasons, Experiment 3 employed a repeated-measures design; that is, each participant played multiple rounds of the virtual UG, during which participants were not provided with a corresponding monetary return.

Experiment 3

Purpose

This study aimed to examine how a wide range of responder gains was associated with acceptance patterns across multiple fairness levels in a repeated virtual Ultimatum Game. We expected that gain-related changes in acceptance would differ across fairness levels, such that increases in responder gain would be more strongly associated with acceptance under lower-fairness offers than under higher-fairness offers.

Methods

Participants

A total of 56 college students (42 females, Mage=20.50, SDage=1.50) volunteered and consented to participate in this experiment, all of whom received 10 yuan as compensation for their time. All participants signed the informed consent form before the experiment began.

Design and materials

The experiment employed a two-factor within-subject design. The two factors were absolute monetary gains and fairness. Absolute monetary gains ranged across seven levels: 1 yuan, 10 yuan, 25 yuan, 50 yuan, 500 yuan, 1000 yuan, and 12,500 yuan. Fairness ranged across eight levels: 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 35%, 40%, and 45%. All monetary amounts in this experiment were hypothetical and presented within a computerized virtual UG task.

Procedure

All participants played 56 rounds of the UG on a computer. To maintain attention throughout the task, we adopted a procedure commonly used in social cognitive neuroscience research (e.g.,31, in which a series of neutral faces was presented as putative game partners. All face images were taken from the Chinese Facial Affective Picture System14. They were neutral Chinese faces, half male and half female, with moderate attractiveness, and were used to avoid introducing salient social cues that might systematically bias decisions. Before the game began, participants were informed of the rules of the UG and answered two questions to confirm their understanding. Their role as responders was to decide, in each round, whether to accept the proposal presented by the computer. Each round unfolded as shown in Fig. 2: a fixation point appeared first, followed by a neutral face, the total amount to be divided, and finally the proposed allocation, at which point participants made an accept/reject decision.

Flow chart of the procedure in Experiment 3.

Statistical analysis

Acceptance was coded as 1 and rejection as 0. The primary aim of Experiment 3 was to test a specific pattern prediction: whether responder gain would be more strongly associated with acceptance under lower-fairness offers than under higher-fairness offers. To assess this pattern directly, we estimated two logistic regression models separately for higher-fairness offers (greater than 30%) and lower-fairness offers (less than 30%). This analytic strategy was used to test whether gain-related variation in acceptance was concentrated in the lower-fairness range, which was the central pattern of interest in this experiment. Age and gender were included as descriptive participant characteristics in these models because all participants completed the same repeated task, allowing these participant-level variables to be entered without changing the within-subject structure of the experimental manipulations.

Results

The acceptance rates across responder-gain and fairness combinations are shown in Fig. 3. Overall, acceptance rates were high and comparatively stable under higher-fairness offers, whereas under lower-fairness offers they increased more strongly as responder gain became larger. To test this pattern formally, we estimated two logistic regression models separately for higher-fairness offers (greater than 30%) and lower-fairness offers (less than 30%) (Table 3). Under higher-fairness conditions, fairness significantly predicted acceptance, whereas responder gain did not show a reliable effect. Under lower-fairness conditions, both fairness and responder gain significantly predicted acceptance. Thus, the association between responder gain and acceptance was stronger in the lower-fairness range than in the higher-fairness range. Age and gender were included in the logistic models as auxiliary background terms rather than as theoretically central predictors. Because the purpose of Experiment 3 was to examine whether gain-related acceptance patterns differed across fairness ranges, the demographic coefficients were not interpreted substantively in the present manuscript.

Discussion

Experiment 3 provided pattern-level evidence that the association between responder gain and acceptance differed across fairness levels in a repeated virtual Ultimatum Game. In particular, increases in responder gain were more strongly associated with acceptance under lower-fairness offers than under higher-fairness offers. This result suggests that even in a virtual, unincentivized setting, sufficiently large gain variation can influence responders’ decisions, although the strength of this influence depends on fairness level.

Because Experiment 3 used repeated hypothetical interactions and was not designed to assess trial-by-trial learning, adaptation, or response-time processes, it provides evidence about acceptance patterns rather than about the underlying process dynamics. In addition, the results do not support the conclusion that fairness ceases to matter altogether under very high gains. Indeed, even when hypothetical gains were extremely large, many participants still rejected very low-fairness offers.

One plausible interpretation is that, in a virtual UG setting without direct monetary consequences, the motivational pull of large gains may remain weaker than in a real-incentive context, whereas concern about unfairness may be comparatively amplified. Because Experiments 1–3 all involved college-student samples, Experiment 4 was designed to test whether a related gain pattern would also appear under real monetary incentives in a comparison between college students and employees.

Experiment 4

Purpose

This study aimed to examine whether the gain-related acceptance pattern observed in Experiment 3 would also appear in a more incentive-realistic setting in which participants could receive actual monetary gains. In addition, it compared two pre-defined participant groups that differed in broad economic background and income-independence status: college students without independent income and employees with independent income. Rather than treating this experiment as a fine-grained individual-level socioeconomic-status survey, we used this group contrast to test whether the same absolute gains would be associated with different acceptance patterns across two ecologically meaningful groups. We expected that, under a fixed very unfair offer (10%), acceptance rates would increase more sharply across gain levels among college students than among employees.

Methods

Participants

A total of 253 college students (102 females, Mage = 19.55, SDage = 1.76) and 687 employees (346 females, Mage = 33.03, SDage = 7.03) participated in this experiment. All participants volunteered and provided informed consent. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of the eight experimental conditions and completed only one Ultimatum Game decision. Participants received a basic payment of 5 yuan plus any gain obtained from the game.

Design and materials

The experiment employed a 2 × 8 between-subjects design. The two factors were participant identity (college student vs. employee) and responder gain. Responder gain varied across eight levels: 0.1 yuan, 0.2 yuan, 0.5 yuan, 10 yuan, 20 yuan, 30 yuan, 40 yuan, and 50 yuan. Fairness was fixed at 10%, representing a very unfair offer. Accordingly, the total amount to be distributed was 1 yuan, 2 yuan, 5 yuan, 100 yuan, 200 yuan, 300 yuan, 400 yuan, and 500 yuan, respectively. Procedure

This UG experiment was conducted online via Wenjuanxing in a questionnaire-based format. The experimental procedures were as follows. First, after informed consent, participants provided basic demographic information and were then randomly assigned by the system to one of the eight gain conditions. Before the game began, the participants were informed of the rules of the UG and told that the proposer and responder roles as well as the total amount of money available in the game would be assigned randomly by the computer; in addition, participants were informed that this game would allow them to obtain actual money. Taking the highest level of absolute monetary gains as an example, the instructions were as follows: “Thank you for taking time out of your busy schedule to participate in this study. You will be compensated with a basic award of 5 yuan plus the money you gain in the game. The money in the game is real money, and 500 yuan will be split between you and a stranger. Hereafter, one of you, decided at random, will decide to divide the 500 based on a distribution plan, and the other participant will decide whether to accept or reject the proposed distribution. If the proposed distribution is accepted by the other person, the 500 yuan will be divided in accordance with the distribution scheme; if the distribution is rejected, neither participant will receive the money”. After participants were presented with these instructions, they were directed to click to access the “next page” and to draw lots to determine who would act as the proposer. Subsequently, the result of the “random” draw indicated that “the other side proposes the allocation”, which was followed by “checking the allocation”. Finally, the participants decided whether to accept the proposed allocation (“450 yuan for the proposer and 50 yuan for you. Will you accept or reject it?”). If the participants accepted the offer, they received 50 yuan plus 5 yuan, i.e., 55 yuan in total; if the participants refused the offer, they received only 5 yuan in total.

Statistical analysis

If the responder accepted the offer, the response was coded as 1; if the responder rejected the offer, the response was coded as 0. Because Experiment 4 was designed as a between-subjects comparison between two pre-defined participant groups across eight gain conditions, the primary analysis focused on the main effects of participant identity and responder gain, as well as their interaction, using ANOVA on acceptance rates. By design, participant identity (college students vs. employees) was treated as the focal grouping factor, rather than decomposed into a full set of individual-level socioeconomic indicators. This analytic choice was consistent with the aim of the experiment, namely to examine broad group-level differences in gain-related acceptance patterns rather than to estimate a covariate-adjusted predictive model.

Results

The condition-wise sample sizes, accepted counts, rejected counts, and acceptance rates are reported in Table 4, and the acceptance-rate trends are illustrated in Fig. 4. Descriptively, acceptance rates in the college-student group rose from 16.13% at the lowest gain level to 88.57% at the 40-yuan condition, whereas acceptance rates in the employee group remained comparatively stable across conditions, ranging from 37.18% to 44.44%.

A 2 × 8 ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of participant identity, F (1, 924) = 38.908, p < .001, partial η² = 0.040, a significant main effect of responder gain, F (7, 924) = 9.883, p < .001, partial η² = 0.070, and a significant participant identity × responder gain interaction, F (7, 924) = 8.750, p < .001, partial η² = 0.062. These results indicate that the relationship between gain and acceptance differed markedly between the two groups. In particular, acceptance rates increased sharply across gain levels among college students, whereas the gain-related change was much weaker among employees.

Discussion

Experiment 4 revealed a clear group difference in how acceptance rates varied across gain levels. Relative to employees, college students showed a much steeper increase in acceptance as gains became larger. This pattern suggests that the relationship between gain and acceptance differed across the two groups.

In the present study, student versus employee identity was treated as a broad, ecologically meaningful grouping variable related to economic background and income-independence status, rather than as a fine-grained individual-level indicator of socioeconomic status. The observed group difference therefore should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of economic status alone, because participant identity may also capture age, life stage, work experience, and other correlated background characteristics.

What Experiment 4 more directly demonstrates is that, under a fixed very unfair offer, the same absolute gain was associated with different acceptance patterns in these two groups. This finding is consistent with the possibility that the same gain carried different subjective value for college students and employees. Future work could test this interpretation more precisely by measuring individual-level disposable income, subjective financial pressure, and related background variables within each group.

General discussion

Overall, the four experiments reported in the present study are best understood as a systematic investigation of a boundary-condition question: under what conditions do monetary incentives more strongly alter responders’ willingness to accept unfair offers. The Ultimatum Game literature has long shown that responders do not mechanically maximize monetary payoff. When confronted with unfair offers, rejection is often interpreted as a response to inequality and reciprocal fairness, a pattern consistent with models of inequity aversion and fairness preference10,20,31. In this respect, the main contribution of the present research is not to “prove” any single theory, but to clarify more precisely that gain-related acceptance patterns vary as a function of experimental setting, gain range, and participant background.

Taken together, the four experiments reveal a broadly consistent pattern. Experiment 1, conducted in a hypothetical questionnaire setting without real monetary incentives, did not support the predicted interaction pattern. Experiment 2, conducted with real monetary rewards, showed that both fairness and gain influenced acceptance behavior, although the predicted interaction again failed to emerge. Experiment 3 substantially expanded the gain range in a repeated virtual Ultimatum Game and showed that the association between gain and acceptance was stronger under lower-fairness offers than under higher-fairness offers. Experiment 4 further showed that, under a fixed very unfair offer and real monetary rewards, gain-related changes in acceptance differed markedly between college students and working adults. This overall pattern fits the broader picture in the Ultimatum Game literature, in which stake effects are not observed uniformly. On the one hand, some studies have reported significant stake effects under designs involving larger stakes and more frequent low offers1. On the other hand, meta-analyses and cross-study reviews point to substantial heterogeneity in Ultimatum Game behavior across cultural contexts, experimental settings, and specific manipulations21,27. The present study, therefore, should not be read as a strict isolation test of any single design dimension, such as “authenticity,” but rather as a systematic comparison of how multiple contextual factors jointly shape acceptance patterns.

From a theoretical perspective, the present findings are more consistent with a context-sensitive trade-off account than with any simple reduction of responder behavior to either “pure monetary utility pursuit” or “pure fairness preference.” In the present data, fairness and gain do not operate as mutually exclusive motives; instead, they jointly enter the decision process, and their relative weight shifts across contexts. This interpretation is more compatible with the inequity-aversion framework represented by Fehr and Schmidt10: individuals are neither concerned solely with their own payoff nor guided by fairness alone under all conditions. Rather, fairness concerns and gain motivation can coexist, with their relative strength varying across situations. A more appropriate conclusion from the present study, therefore, is not that fairness disappears under high gains, but that under some conditions, larger gains reduce people’s tendency to reject unfair offers; this reduction, however, does not imply that fairness concerns are completely eliminated. Put differently, the core value of the present research lies in providing clearer pattern-level evidence about when monetary incentives are more likely to outweigh fairness concerns.

Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted primarily as marginal, condition-level acceptance patterns across the experimental settings, rather than as a covariate-adjusted individual-level prediction model of acceptance decisions.

Limitations and future directions

The present research has several limitations. First, all four experiments focused on behavioral acceptance outcomes and therefore cannot directly reveal the cognitive or neural mechanisms underlying the trade-off between fairness and gain. Prior Ultimatum Game research has shown that process-level indicators and neural evidence can help to clarify more precisely how unfairness rejection and interest-based trade-offs emerge during decision making20,31. Future research could therefore build on the strengths of the present designs while incorporating process-level measures such as response time, eye tracking, neuroimaging, or brain stimulation to examine how this trade-off unfolds over the course of decision making.

Second, although the four experiments were intentionally designed to vary in setting and task structure in order to examine the relationship between gain and fairness under different conditions, this also means that the cross-experiment comparisons do not constitute a clean test of any single factor. In addition to incentive realism, fairness thresholds, gain ranges, one-shot versus repeated structures, and participant pools also varied across studies. Accordingly, the present paper should not be read as a strict isolation test of any single design feature, but rather as a comprehensive examination of how multiple experimental conditions jointly shape acceptance patterns.

Third, Experiment 3 adopted a repeated virtual-game design, but it was not intended to address trial-by-trial learning, behavioral adaptation, or response-time dynamics. Its contribution is therefore more appropriately understood as pattern-level evidence rather than a process-level account.

Fourth, Experiment 4 relied on a between-group comparison of college students and working adults to represent differences in broad economic background. This design was useful for testing whether the same absolute gains were associated with different behavioral patterns across two ecologically meaningful groups, but it cannot fully separate student/employee identity from age, life stage, work experience, or other related background characteristics. Future research could incorporate more fine-grained individual-level measures, such as disposable income, subjective financial pressure, and other demographic indicators, to examine this interpretation more precisely. Current research on stake effects, cultural differences, and heterogeneity across experimental settings also suggests that these boundary-condition questions are best addressed through systematic, multi-method designs that accumulate converging evidence1,21,27.

A final limitation concerns the scope of the statistical specifications used in the present study. The primary analyses focused on design-defined, condition-level acceptance-rate patterns rather than on individual-level predictive models. Accordingly, demographic variables such as age and gender were not treated as central theoretical predictors in the main analyses. Although this approach was aligned with the present aim of describing how acceptance patterns varied across experimentally defined fairness, gain, and group conditions, future large-sample studies could incorporate pre-planned covariate-adjusted specifications to further examine whether these context-dependent patterns remain robust after accounting for individual-level demographic characteristics.

Conclusion

The present study shows that responders’ acceptance behavior in the Ultimatum Game is not determined by any single factor, but is jointly influenced by fairness level and responder gain; moreover, the relative strength of these two influences varies across task contexts and participant backgrounds. Overall, gain-related acceptance patterns were shaped by the nature of the task (hypothetical/virtual vs. real-incentive settings), the range of gain variation, and broad group-level differences in economic background. The present study is more consistent with a context-sensitive trade-off account, according to which the relative influence of fairness concerns and monetary incentives shifts across experimental conditions.
