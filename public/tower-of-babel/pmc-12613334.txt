Top three priorities for artificial intelligence integration into emergency, critical, and perioperative medicine: an interdisciplinary clinical expert consensus
Authors: Beatrice Loriga, Francesco Baglivo, Valentina Bellini, Chiara Adembri, Jonathan Montomoli, Marco Cascella, Giacomo Diedenhofen, Luigi De Angelis, Nicola Gentili, Mattia Altini, Antonio Pastori, Raffaella Gaggeri, Elena Giovanna Bignami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly applied in emergency, critical, and perioperative medicine, yet its implementation remains limited and fragmented. Variability in digital maturity, governance, and clinical readiness continues to challenge large-scale adoption.MethodsA multidisciplinary expert consensus was conducted to identify key priorities for the safe and effective integration of AI in high-acuity settings. The consensus process included an independent literature review, group discussion, and blinded online voting. Priorities that reached at least 70% agreement on a 9-point Likert scale were considered consensual.ResultsThree priorities reached the predefined consensus threshold:Digitalization and sharing of healthcare data (92.3% agreement): Digitalize the Emergency, Critical, and Perioperative Department patient journey by adopting a shared standard structure for electronic medical records that is optimized for data sharing and interoperability.Efficacy and validation of AI models (93.4% agreement): Use only AI models that have demonstrated impact on patient outcomes, decision-making processes, or risk stratification validated through prospective studies or randomized clinical trials.AI education of healthcare professionals (100% agreement): Healthcare professionals must acquire a digital health literacy level appropriate for their specific role, with individuals with leadership and management roles having more in-depth knowledge.ConclusionsThe consensus identifies three strategic priorities to guide the integration of AI in high-acuity settings. Together, they outline a pragmatic roadmap for translating AI potential into safe and clinically meaningful practice.Keywords: AI, Artificial intelligence, Consensus, Critical Care, Digitalization, Digital literacy, Emergency Medicine, Healthcare, High-acuity care, Medicine, Perioperative Medicine

Introduction

Integrating artificial intelligence into high-acuity settings such as emergency departments, intensive care units, and perioperative care requires a strategic approach that aligns with the specific demands of these high-stakes and time-sensitive environments. Importantly, AI can enhance clinical workflows by streamlining the collection and analysis of health data, supporting decision-making, and potentially improving patient outcomes [1]. On the other hand, although AI is increasingly applied in emergency, critical, and perioperative care, its adoption remains uneven geographically. Therefore, while the potential of AI is widely recognized, its adoption in healthcare systems, such as Italy’s publicly funded system, remains limited and fragmented. In particular, the Italian healthcare system is regionally structured, with each of the 21 regions and autonomous provinces operating its own independent health service. As a result, implementation timelines, digital maturity levels, and governance models vary significantly across the country, making nationwide AI integration particularly complex.

Building on the recently published consensus [2], an intersocietal consensus, involving the Italian Society of Anesthesia, Analgesia, Resuscitation and Intensive Care (SIAARTI), the Italian Society of Artificial Intelligence in Medicine (SIIAM), and the Italian Society of Leadership and Management in Medicine (SIMM), proposes strategic priorities to guide the ethical and effective integration of digitalization. These priorities include the digitalization of clinical pathways, rigorous validation of AI tools, and investment in education and leadership development. Together, these actions aim to enable a coordinated and clinically meaningful transformation of care. Although rooted in the Italian context, these reflections address issues of broader European relevance, within the shared regulatory frameworks such as GDPR and the AI Act. By addressing priorities, healthcare institutions can promote AI adoption that enhances patient care, supporting clinical decision-making and optimizing resources.

Methods

We conducted a focus nominal group technique study to establish an expert consensus between the SIAARTI, the SIIAM, and the SIMM, with the core question being:

“What are the key priorities to integrate artificial intelligence safely and effectively into Italian high-acuity settings?”

The steering committee, composed of Prof. Elena Giovanna Bignami (President of SIAARTI), Dr. Luigi De Angelis (President of SIIAM), and Dr. Mattia Altini (President of SIMM), oversaw the process and identified the participants from the three scientific societies, selecting them based on their recognized clinical and scientific expertise in the field. The working groups included professionals with multidisciplinary backgrounds, such as anesthesiologists, hygienists, public health specialists, engineers, and healthcare management experts.

At the first online meeting, the methodology was defined. Each society was tasked with conducting an independent review of the literature within its area of competence. Although this was not a systematic review, all panel members could suggest articles they considered relevant. The review period lasted 2 months. Eligible publications included original articles, narrative and systematic reviews, meta-analyses, position papers, guidelines, experimental studies, and randomized controlled trials. Only English-language, peer-reviewed articles published in the past 10 years were considered.

The consensus process was organized in three phases. The first phase focused on sharing the literature findings, the second on critical definition and discussing potential priorities, and the third on voting and analyzing the results. The final statements were proposed based on the reviewed literature and the professional expertise of the panel members and were submitted for blinded online voting by all participants. Specifically, the vote was blinded for all participants except the first author, who collected the results.

Two rounds of online voting were planned. Agreement was expressed using an ordinal 9-point Likert scale, where 1–3 indicated disagreement (“inappropriate”), 4–6 indicated uncertainty, and 7–9 indicated agreement or support (“appropriate”). The three priorities that achieved at least 70% agreement within the two highest levels of the Likert scale were selected as final recommendations. A second round of voting was not required, as all statements reached consensus in the first round. Voting results are presented in tabular form in the Results section.

The recommendations represent the outcome of a consensus process and are based either on the evidence identified in the literature or, where such evidence was lacking, on the expert opinion of the panel. Discussions were conducted both synchronously (through dedicated videoconferences) and asynchronously (via email exchanges and shared documents). To limit the so-called bandwagon effect, each member was asked to express an independent judgment before each plenary discussion. The consensus process is outlined in Fig. 1.

Overall, the panel showed a very high level of consensus regarding the digitalization and sharing of healthcare data, with scores ranging from 6 to 9 and an agreement rate of 92.3%. For this priority, the median score was 9 (Q1 = 9; Q3 = 9), indicating uniformly strong support for this priority.

Similarly, the efficacy of AI models was rated highly by all participants, with scores between 7 and 9, a median of 8, and an agreement rate of 92.4%.

Unanimous agreement (100%) was achieved for the AI education of healthcare professionals, which received consistently high ratings (minimum = 8; median = 9; Q3 = 9).

In contrast, priorities related to real-time electronic health record (EHR) integration, stakeholder engagement, and infrastructure and resource investment received more variable ratings and lower levels of agreement (46.2% for each). For real-time EHR integration, scores ranged from 4 to 9, with a median of 6. Stakeholder engagement and infrastructure investment showed similar distributions, with median scores of 6 and interquartile ranges indicating mixed levels of support.

Consequently, the three priorities that achieved at least 70% agreement within the two highest levels of the Likert scale (i.e., Digitalization and sharing of healthcare data, Efficacy of AI models, and AI education of healthcare professionals) were selected as the final recommendations.

Priority 1: digitalization and sharing of healthcare data

The digitalization of clinical data and secure data sharing have become essential priorities in contemporary healthcare systems. Their importance extends beyond mere efficiency; they are pivotal to driving innovation, ensuring transparency, improving the quality of care, and safeguarding patient safety. These factors are particularly vital in high-acuity environments where timely and accurate clinical decisions can make the difference between life and death.

The value of data sharing

Sharing clinical data is crucial for advancing innovation and improving care, particularly in high-acuity settings [3]. Large language models (LLMs) can help convert unstructured data, such as PDFs and free text in Italy’s Electronic Health Record (EHR), into usable formats and support anonymization for secure sharing [4]. Federated learning enables collaborative AI training without transferring sensitive data, preserving privacy and improving generalizability. Combined with blockchain, a technology that allows data to be stored and shared securely, transparently, and immutably, these tools enhance trust, traceability, and support real-time decision-making systems like MySurgeryRisk [5, 6].

Barriers to implementation

Healthcare digitalization faces significant challenges, including clinicians’ resistance due to privacy concerns, workflow disruptions, and a lack of trust in new technologies, especially in high-pressure settings. These issues are intensified by legal and ethical uncertainties, such as data governance and the opaque nature of AI algorithms, which may further delay adoption [7, 8].

From a regulatory perspective, although the GDPR and the AI Act provide a common framework at the European level, their concrete implementation depends on national regulations that introduce significant variability. The Italian context, in particular, is characterized by high fragmentation of regional healthcare systems and the use of heterogeneous and non-interoperable electronic medical record (EMR) platforms. This fragmentation creates data silos, which hinder access to and integration of data across different healthcare settings. In emergencies, when a patient’s clinical history may be distributed across different hospitals, this lack of connectivity can severely hinder timely and effective decision-making [9]. These elements explain why data privacy management and the implementation of AI solutions require tailored approaches adapted to the peculiarities of healthcare systems.

Financial and organizational barriers also play a significant role. Implementing digital infrastructure requires not only significant capital investment but also strategic planning, workflow redesign, and the development of human resources. These requirements can be especially daunting for hospitals and health services operating under financial constraints or dealing with staff shortages [10].

Overcoming implementation challenges

Addressing these challenges requires a practical approach that involves organizational changes and staff engagement.

Data standardization is crucial: without shared formats, digital systems remain fragmented. In this context, Italy’s development of FSE 2.0 aims to unify healthcare data flows and ensure nationwide interoperability. At the European level, initiatives like the European Health Data Space and MyHealth@EU further promote cross-border data integration. Adopting standards such as HL7 FHIR and FAIR principles will help ensure secure, interoperable data exchange to support both patient care and research [11, 12].

Financial incentives and policy reforms are essential to support digital transformation in healthcare. Strategic investments, particularly in under-resourced settings, should prioritize infrastructure and reward innovation and quality improvements [9]. To address growing concerns about data privacy, emerging models such as federated learning and blockchain may offer secure and compliant data-sharing solutions. These technologies build trust and support adherence to evolving data protection regulations, such as those in the Italian healthcare framework [4, 5].

Critically, all digital initiatives must involve broad stakeholder engagement. Co-designing solutions with clinicians, administrators, patients, and developers ensures that technologies are user-friendly, clinically relevant, and socially acceptable. This collaborative approach increases adoption and promotes a culture of innovation within healthcare organizations [9].

The creation of Clinical AI Departments (CAIDs) within healthcare institutions offers a structured approach to managing AI integration [13]. These units support interoperability, standardization, real-time performance monitoring, and interdisciplinary collaboration, ensuring AI is deployed safely, equitably, and effectively. CAIDs must include not only IT engineers but also physicians and frontline healthcare professionals, whose involvement ensures clinical relevance, fosters adoption, and avoids the perception of externally imposed solutions. This interdisciplinary model reinforces AI as a practical support tool for clinical care. Additionally, CAIDs play a key role in ongoing model evaluation and recalibration, which is essential as patient characteristics and populations change over time.

Priority 2: efficacy of AI models

The need for clinically validated AI models

The integration of clinically validated AI models into healthcare is essential to safeguard patient safety and to ensure models deliver measurable clinical benefit. AI technologies have, in some cases, shown better performance than traditional methods, particularly in diagnostic accuracy, clinical outcome prediction, and resource optimization [14, 15]. Importantly, using AI models that have undergone rigorous validation, including prospective trials or randomized clinical studies, is crucial to reduce the risks of algorithmic bias and overfitting, which are the major barriers to safe and equitable clinical use [16].

Barriers to implementation

Despite their promise, validated AI tools still face many obstacles to their widespread adoption in clinical settings. Privacy concerns and intellectual property issues complicate the external validation of AI tools, as data sharing remains tightly regulated [14]. Additionally, AI models often exhibit inconsistent technical performance across clinical environments due to variations in data quality and local workflows [16, 17].

These models must be carefully tailored to the specific characteristics of each clinical setting to be truly usable and effective. This variability undermines clinician confidence and contributes to limited trust and acceptance among healthcare professionals [17]. Moreover, the inability to consistently verify the quality of input data raises legitimate concerns about the real-world effectiveness and accuracy of these models.

Further impediments include insufficient digital infrastructure, resource constraints, and a lack of skilled personnel to manage and operate AI systems, which are particularly evident in resource-limited or developing healthcare systems [18]. Organizational inertia and resistance to change also play a significant role, particularly when leadership fails to cultivate a culture of digital innovation [19].

Overcoming implementation challenges

Bringing clinically validated AI models into everyday healthcare practice, especially within the fast-paced environment of high-acuity settings, remains a complex challenge. To build credibility and trust in AI systems, implementing a data quality checklist and enhancing model transparency, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO), are foundational measures [20].

A key early requirement is the establishment of robust local validation infrastructures. Before any AI model can be safely deployed at scale, hospitals and health systems need tools and processes to assess how well these models perform within their specific clinical contexts. This local validation helps ensure that AI tools are not only accurate in theory but truly effective and reliable in practice [14].

Moreover, a clear regulatory framework is essential. Without standardized processes for certifying and approving AI models, both clinicians and patients may struggle to trust these technologies. In this context, the European AI Act and the Medical Device Regulation play a critical role in setting safety, transparency, and performance standards. Developing robust certification pathways aligned with these regulations helps ensure consistency across settings and support greater trust in the safe clinical use of AI [15].

Collaboration between clinicians, developers, researchers, and regulators is also crucial. These partnerships ensure that AI tools are not only technically sound but also tailored to real-world clinical needs and aligned with ethical standards [17, 21].

Ultimately, visionary leadership is crucial for driving and sustaining digital transformation in healthcare. Leaders must articulate a clear, strategic vision, secure the necessary resources, and foster an organizational culture that embraces innovation [22]. They play a critical role in addressing cultural resistance, guiding staff through change, and promoting a climate of continuous learning. By promoting iterative implementation (testing, learning, and adapting digital tools over time), they can support long-term integration of AI into clinical practice [23].

Priority 3: AI education of healthcare professionals

The role of education in empowerment

Empowering healthcare professionals through education is essential in the digital age. As AI transforms care delivery, clinicians need both technical skills and digital literacy to apply AI tools appropriately and safely in patient care [24]. In addition to existing frameworks like CoBaTrICE, AI training for perioperative, critical, and emergency medicine should include ongoing programs tailored to different professional roles. In line with the AI Act, these programs should also ensure traceability, documenting who was trained, by whom, on which tools, and at what competency level. Barriers to implementation

A major obstacle is the widespread lack of digital literacy, with skill levels varying by age, education, and geography [26]. AI training is inconsistently included in medical education and often lacks structured competency frameworks [27]. Additionally, training tends to focus on tool usage rather than integration into clinical workflows, especially problematic in specialties where AI is rapidly evolving but practical training is lacking [28].

Beyond educational gaps, there are structural and cultural challenges: fragmented regulations delay implementation, access to quality data is often limited by privacy laws, and financial constraints hinder system upgrades. Clinicians may also fear job loss, increased workloads, or feel overwhelmed by the complexity of AI systems, which can lead to resistance [29–32].

Overcoming implementation challenges

To overcome these challenges, healthcare institutions must invest in flexible, ongoing AI training programs and formally integrate AI education into medical and specialty training [33]. Curricula must evolve in tandem with technological advances, equipping professionals to assess and apply AI tools critically in clinical practice.

Leadership is equally critical. Leaders need both digital expertise and soft skills such as emotional intelligence and a strategic mindset to succeed. Such leadership profiles are key to driving AI adoption and aligning clinical and technological objectives [34] (Table 4).

Conclusion

As AI advances in healthcare, its integration into high-acuity settings has potential but faces complex challenges. This intersocietal position paper, developed by SIAARTI, SIIAM, and SIMM, proposes three strategic priorities to guide the adoption of AI within Italy’s public health system. First, the digitalization of the emergency patient journey through interoperable electronic records is essential. Without structured, shareable data, AI cannot provide reliable real-time insights in emergency, perioperative, and critical care. This shift involves technical, organizational, and cultural changes, as well as ethical considerations. Second, clinical validation is crucial. Only AI tools that show real-world benefits through rigorous prospective or randomized trials should be used. Validation should not be seen as a mere regulatory formality, but as a vital step to ensuring patient safety, clinical trust, and equity. Finally, AI adoption depends on healthcare professionals. Targeted education in AI and digital health is needed to empower healthcare professionals with the skills and understanding to use these technologies confidently. This can reduce resistance and promote a human-centered approach to care.

These priorities are deeply interconnected: reliable data underpins validation; effective use demands trained professionals; and adoption requires strong governance, leadership, and interoperability.

There is now an opportunity to move from a fragmented initiative to a coordinated system-wide adoption. High-acuity fields are fast, data-rich, and outcome-driven, and can lead this change. With strategic investment, collaboration, and leadership, AI can become a practical, everyday tool to support clinicians, improve care, and deliver timely, equitable health services.
