Chromatin remodelling: a driving force in reverse mechanotransduction
Authors: Julie Buisson, Florent Colin, Anna Labernadie, Morgane Rabineau, Dominique Vautier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChromatin serves as the dynamic carrier of genetic and epigenetic information in eukaryotic cells, playing a pivotal role in maintaining nuclear shape, mechanical stability, and cellular function. Aberrant nuclear morphology, often observed in mechanically stressed environments or diseases such as progeria and muscular dystrophy, correlates with nuclear dysfunction, DNA damage, and disrupted mechanotransduction. Chromatin exists in two main configurations – compacted heterochromatin and decompacted euchromatin – each regulating gene expression and cellular behaviour through epigenetic modifications. Histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases modulate chromatin compaction, while histone methylation introduces further regulatory complexity. Chromatin’s viscoelastic properties enable it to store and restore mechanical energy, acting as a mechanosensitive component within the nucleus. External forces propagate from the extracellular matrix through focal adhesions, the cytoskeleton, and the nuclear lamina to chromatin, forming a direct mechanotransduction pathway. However, the reverse pathway – how internal nuclear forces generated during chromatin remodelling influence the nuclear membrane, cytoskeleton, and cell adhesion – remains poorly understood. This review explores the role of chromatin as a tensegrity element, capable of generating mechanical forces through condensate formation. It examines evidence supporting chromatin decompaction as a regulator of reverse mechanotransduction and identifies potential mechanical partners involved in this process. Understanding these mechanisms may elucidate how chromatin dynamics contribute to cellular fate decisions and disease pathogenesis.KEYWORDS: Reverse mechanotransduction, heterochromatin/euchromatin remodelling, cellular mechanosensing, chromatin mechanics, chromatin force generation

Introduction

Chromatin serves as the physiological carrier of genetic and epigenetic information in eukaryotes. Under physiological condition, the chromatin architecture maintains the shape and mechanical stability of the nucleus in coordination with the lamins and the cytoskeleton. These nuclei display shape of healthy cell, enabling them and tissues to perform their specialized functions. However, aberrant nuclear shape is a diagnostic indicator of human diseases and can lead to nuclear dysfunction. Aberrantly shaped nuclei are found in mechanically demanding environments, such as muscle cells, in cardiac diseases associated with progeria, ageing, and dystrophy [1]. Deficient nuclear rigidity leads to DNA breaks and damage during migration through confined environments [2]. Abnormal nuclear mechanics can also disrupt mechanotransduction, leading to perturbation of transcription [3]. Mutations in LMNA, the gene encoding A-type lamins, cause laminopathies-diseases of striated muscle and other tissues. The cell shapes the nucleus through mechanotransduction pathways that sense and respond to extracellular signals, thereby regulating chromatin architecture by modulating its compaction and decompaction [4,5]. Chromatin is a mechanical component of the nucleus, characterized by two main configurations depending on its degree of compaction: i) compacted and genetically silent heterochromatin, localized at the nuclear periphery and tightly bound to the nuclear lamina [6] and ii) decompacted and actively transcribed euchromatin, more widely spaced between nucleosomes, homogenously localized within the nucleus and near nuclear pores. Chromatin is continuously remodelled between these two configurations, regulating cellular behaviour through epigenetic modifications. For example, the global architecture of chromatin undergoes changes during cell development and differentiation. In addition, embryonic stem cells are known to have a hyperdynamic, decompacted chromatin state [7]. Chromatin remodelling is driven by epigenetic histone modifications that regulate the wrapping and unwrapping of DNA around nucleosomes, the core of histone proteins. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone acetyltransferases (HATs) are enzymes with opposing effects that mediate a subtype of modifications, controlling chromatin architecture [8,9]. HAT enzymes catalyse the addition of acetyl groups to histones, promoting decompacted chromatin. CBP (CREB-binding protein) and p300 act as co-activators by recruiting HATs to gene promoters. HDAC enzymes remove acetyl groups, leading to compacted chromatin. Sin3 and N-CoR (Nuclear Receptor Co-Repressor) act as co-repressors, recruiting HDACs to gene promoters. Once the acetyl groups have been removed, histone lysine residues can undergo further modifications, such as methylation. Histone methylation is catalysed by enzymes called histone methyltransferases (HMTs). Unlike acetylation, methylation can have variable effects on gene expression, depending on the specific residue that is methylated and the number of methyl groups added. For example, methylation of histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) is often associated with transcriptional activation, while methylation of histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) is often associated with transcriptional repression. Other protein complexes that remodel nucleosomes via ATP hydrolysis [10–12] can change chromatin architecture. Variation in compaction is a highly dynamic process that corresponds to changes in chromatin stiffness [13] and nucleoplasm viscoelasticity [14]. The viscoelastic compliance of chromatin allows it to store and restore energy transmitted by both extracellular and internal nuclear forces [15]. In the direction of the extracellular environment to the nucleus, external forces applied via integrin propagate to focal adhesion proteins and F-actin (associated with myosin II), then from F-actin complex (nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton linker; nesprins and Sun1/2) to the nuclear lamina network at the nuclear membrane, and finally to the chromatin [16] (Figure 1). These mechanical components form a direct mechanotransduction pathway. However, less is known about the opposite direction: whether internal nuclear forces, generated by the nuclear machinery during chromatin decompaction, are transduced to the nuclear membrane. Ultimately, it remains unclear whether these forces are transmitted to focal contacts through the cytoskeleton, influencing cell adhesion and/or cell fate decisions. In this review, to address this point, chromatin is first defined as a mechanosensitive nuclear component. Specifically, it discusses how chromatin functions as a tensegrity component through its mechanical properties. It also explores how chromatin behaves like a spring, capable of storing and restoring mechanical energy. Particularly, how chromatin compaction generates mechanical forces through condensate formation, providing forces ranging from pico- to nanonewtons. The second part develops the works showing that chromatin decompaction regulates the reverse mechanotransduction pathway. The third part of this review discusses the potential mechanical partners that play key roles in the reverse mechanotransduction pathway.

Bidirectional transmission of mechanical forces between the nucleus and the plasma membrane. A. Chromatin conformation–driven forces: chromatin behaves as a dynamic, compressible, and elastic polymer that mechanically couples the nucleus to the cytoskeleton. Its viscoelastic properties enable it to absorb, buffer, and redistribute mechanical stresses, functioning as a tensegrity element. Under mechanical load, chromatin undergoes reversible compaction and decompaction, storing mechanical energy analogously to a spring. Release of this energy during relaxation generates outward forces on the nuclear envelope (NE). These chromatin-derived forces contribute to nuclear shape regulation and can propagate outward to influence focal adhesion (FA) remodeling via reverse mechanotransduction. In parallel, chromatin compaction increases nuclear stiffness, facilitating actomyosin-mediated nuclear pulling. B. Force transmission via LINC complexes: the LINC (Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton) complex provides a physical bridge between chromatin and the cytoskeleton by coupling SUN proteins at the inner nuclear membrane (INM) to KASH-domain proteins at the outer nuclear membrane (ONM). This architecture enables transmission of tensile, compressive, and shear forces across the nuclear envelope and supports bidirectional mechanotransduction. SUN2, in particular, regulates cytoskeletal tension and promotes FA assembly through activation of the small GTPase RhoA. At the ONM, nesprins connect to actin filaments directly and to microtubules and intermediate filaments indirectly, thereby linking the cytoskeleton to SUN proteins at the INM. C. Force transmission via focal adhesions: focal adhesions serve as primary sites of mechanical coupling between the extracellular matrix (ECM) and the cytoskeleton. In direct mechanotransduction, integrins transmit forces from the ECM to chromatin through FA complexes and the actin cytoskeleton. Talin and vinculin act as key mechanosensitive adaptors, linking β1-integrins to actin filaments. Chromatin remodeling feeds back on this system by modulating cytoskeletal organization and FA dynamics, including talin, vinculin, and FAK stabilization. In reverse mechanotransduction, chromatin-generated forces propagate outward to regulate integrin-mediated adhesion and signaling. Additionally, chromatin liquid–liquid phase separation promotes the formation of biomolecular condensates, which can generate mechanical stresses that further drive chromatin reorganization.

In this review, we define reverse mechanotransduction as a conceptual framework in which chromatin remodelling actively contributes to the generation or modulation of mechanical forces that propagate from the nucleus to the cytoskeleton and the cell surface. Although supported by numerous experimental observations, this mechanism remains emerging and not yet fully established.

Chromatin as a mechanosensitive nuclear component

The nuclear envelope, the lamina and the chromatin: tensegrity components due to their mechanical properties

Cells perform their specialized functions by adapting to environmental changes. Signal transduction pathways communicate directly with chromatin, the site of integration and interpretation of signals regulating modifications of transcriptional programs [17]. Chromatin is a dynamic structure, resembling a compressible and elastic polymer, which establishes a dual connection between the nucleus and the cytoskeleton [18]. These physical properties enable it to absorb shocks [19] from both external and internal nuclear forces [15], making chromatin a tensegrity element [20]. In response to those various forces, chromatin can generate and transmit strengths to the cell, which responds rapidly and reversibly [21]. Chromatin is a mechanosensitive nuclear component and a force-generation machinery for mechanotransduction, defining the mechanical properties of the nucleus [22–24]. A characteristic physical property of the nucleus is its rigidity (1–10kPa), the highest among all cellular components, about 10 times stiffer than the cytoplasm (0.1–1 kPa). The nucleus behaves like a viscoelastic material, composed of three major distinct elements: the nuclear envelope, the lamina, and the chromatin [25–28]. This nuclear viscoelastic behaviour arises from transient, yet strong interactions between chromatin and the nuclear lamina [29]. Compacted chromatin nuclei have significantly higher elastic stiffness compared to decompacted chromatin nuclei, which are more flexible [30–33]. At small strains (deformation of a few microns), the chromatin gel acts as a spring that resists stretching. The lamina contributes little as it bends easily. Longer deformations generate lamin A-based strain stiffening. Chromatin continues to resist the stretching of the nucleus [13]. Thus, the nuclear envelope, lamina and chromatin act together as components of an interdependent tensegrity system. This system gives the nucleus dynamic and adaptive mechanical properties that are essential for transmitting and integrating intracellular forces.

Chromatin: a spring capable of storing and restoring mechanical energy

Chromatin has recently been described as a spring-like structure that can resist and respond to mechanical forces, storing and restoring mechanical energy [31,34] and transmitting these forces across the cell. Nuclear shape is affected by external forces during cell adhesion or migration, as well as by internal cellular tensions resulting from cytoskeletal contractility. In response to these forces, chromatin can compact or decompact, storing mechanical energy. Once these forces cease to exist, the chromatin returns to its original conformation (releasing its stored energy), thus reproducing the behaviour of the spring [28]. These viscoelastic properties allow chromatin to deform under the effect of a force and to return to its initial shape to a certain extent when the force is removed, making chromatin an elastic load-bearing system [27]. When chromatin is highly elastic, it can transmit local forces over long distances [15]. Consequently, chromatin remodelling could propagate mechanical signals throughout the cell, influencing nuclear organization, cell adhesion, and migration dynamics [35]. During confined migration through narrow pores in dense ECM, the nucleus undergoes high-strain deformation. In such cases, viscoelastic chromatin fibres are the dominant mechanical component (with a stiffness of approximately 0.2–1 kPa) [34,36,37]. The elastic modulus of chromatin fibres are spatially heterogeneous, facilitating DNA-related biochemical processes such as transcription, replication, and DNA repair [36,38,39]. These mechanical properties provide physical support for the dynamic binding of lamina and nuclear envelope proteins to the cytoskeleton [40]. Thus, this physical support makes chromatin more than just a passive DNA storage unit; it becomes an actively regulated entity ruled by power-law rheology [27,41]. Chromatin is also continuously mobile. Its mobility is driven by mechanical forces originating from both within the nucleus and from the viscoelastic resistance characteristic of polymer reptation [42–44]. Segment or domain of chromatin can move slowly, referred to as ‘slow chromatin’, in structurally confined environments, with fluctuations ranging from 1 to 2 µm. It can also move rapidly, known as ‘fast chromatin’, during chromatin remodelling processes when exposed to lower physical constraints [45,46]. This chromatin state is deformed by dilation strains [47], , exhibiting more tensile strain than shear strain.

Mechanical forces induced by chromatin compaction

The mechanisms of chromatin compaction formation include: i) liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of chromatin and chromatin-associated proteins such as HP1α (Heterochromatin Protein 1), ii) neutralization of chromatin’s electrostatic charges by the histone H1 linker, and iii) transcriptional silencing processes [48–50]. The LLPS mechanism facilitates the formation of biomolecular condensates (compacted chromatin), generating significant mechanical forces that drive chromatin rearrangement. For example, the transition from a locally stiffer to a softer chromatin state induced by DNA damage or transcriptional activation can promote the nucleation of DNA droplets and the growth of DNA repair condensates [51]. Initially, droplet growth repels chromatin through repulsive interactions. Subsequently, droplet fusion occurs to minimize surface energy, bringing together chromatin regions that were initially distant and localized in separate droplets, thereby creating attractive interactions [38,52]. The chromatin polymer can also mechanically suppress droplet coalescence and maturation, effectively controlling the number, size and positioning of condensates [53]. Furthermore, histone acetylation leads to LLPS dissolution, while depletion of HP1 - which is strongly involved in chromatin compaction – increases chromatin mobility [54]. Consequently, chromatin becomes less compact and more dynamic, moving more freely within the nucleus. The interaction between LLPS and chromatin generates mechanical forces that drive chromatin remodelling, enabling the transition between diffuse and condensed states. This process is crucial for understanding how chromatin stores and releases mechanical energy in a controlled manner during cellular processes.

Chromatin: a piconewtons to nanonewtons force generator

The paradox of nuclear stiffness

Chromatin remodelling generates internal forces within the nucleus and transmits them to the nuclear envelope, thereby modulating nuclear rigidity. In direct mechanotransduction, chromatin stretching is inversely proportional to cell stiffness in response to external forces [55]. These data suggest that in reverse mechanotransduction, nuclear stiffness is reduced by 35–50% due to chromatin decompaction with epigenetic remodellers such as Tricostatin A (TSA), 3-Deazaneplanocin A (DZNep), or Valproic Acid (VPA). In contrast, nuclear stiffness is increased by 40% with chromatin compaction induced by Methylstat [25,52,56–58]. However, Zhao and co-workers showed that acute chromatin decompaction with TSA stiffens the nucleus. This effect is attributed to increased nuclear volume and enhanced nuclear import, including partial recruitment of lamina proteins to the periphery and contributing to an initial stiffening of the nucleus. Even before chromatin is fully decompacted, the stretching of the nuclear envelope and LINC complexes may activate the RhoA/ROCK or YAP/TAZ signalling pathways. An increase in nuclear pressure would lead to an increase in actomyosin tension and reinforce focal adhesions via the Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) pathway. Although the temporal coupling between these mechanical phases and downstream signalling remains to be fully elucidated, once decompaction has stabilized, chromatin reorganization increases the mobility of loci and dilutes lamin A/C, thereby reducing nuclear stiffness [59], which is consistent with the more flexible state of the nucleus observed during the reverse mechanotransduction process. Furthermore, chromatin exhibits intrinsic dynamic remodelling even in the absence of external stimuli, as shown by FRET-FCS experiments revealing that the first 8 base pairs of nucleosomal DNA spontaneously unwrap 30% of the time, with unwrapping and rewrapping time constants of 3 ms and 1 ms, respectively [60]. Additionally, driving chromatin from compaction to decompaction makes the nuclear interior more viscous and deformable [60–63].

Chromatin remodelling stores and releases mechanical energy, generating internal cellular forces

Mechanical energy stored and released during chromatin remodelling acts as a source of internal force. For example, during ATP-dependent remodelling, complexes like SWI/SNF apply tension along chromatin fibres, transmitting forces to the cytoskeleton via the LINC complex, thereby modulating nuclear shape and facilitating confined cell migration [64]. In parallel, chromatin compaction via phase separation such as HP1α-mediated compacted chromatin formation, produces surface tension that reshapes the nucleus and affects DNA accessibility, impacting mechanosensitive transcription [65,66]. These internal forces integrate chromatin into a mechanical component in mechanotransduction, influencing migration, adhesion, and gene expression. These forces typically range from piconewtons (pN) to nanonewtons (nN), posing significant challenges for accurate measurements within cells. Techniques for assessing nuclear mechanical properties are commonly used methods for characterizing the viscoelasticity properties of the nucleus. These approaches allow the application of controlled mechanical forces to chromatin and the measurement of resulting deformations. Micropipette deformation assays reveal Young’s modulus of 1–5 kPa for chromatin fibres [25]. Chromatin plays a critical role in the mechanical resistance of the nucleus when subjected to small deformations. Nuclear volume is modulated by chromatin compaction due to its viscoelastic properties [67]. Thus, altering chromatin state directly affects this short-extension regime (small-scale deformations or extensions of chromatin) [37]. For instance, more compact chromatin may be less deformable and more resistant to mechanical stress, while less compact chromatin may be more deformable and adaptable to mechanical forces. Nuclear force can be quantified by the transition from the short-extension regime (<30% or 3 µm strain) to the long-extension force regime (>30% strain), where the nucleus exhibits 1.5- to 2-fold greater resistance to deformation. These force vs. extension lines provide a force per unit length spring constant in nN/μm, corresponding to an elastic modulus from 0.2 to 1 kPa.

Visualization and quantification of chromatin dynamics

Advances in fluorescence-based techniques, such as fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS), have enabled the visualization and quantification of chromatin dynamics [68] under force, from the 30 nm chromatin fibre to the 10 nm nucleosome. Single-molecule force spectroscopy (SFCS) of a stretched single chromatin fibre with an optical tweezer has shown that a pulling force of 5–6 pN is needed to decompact the chromatin [69]. Similarly, a pulling force of 4 pN is required to induce nucleosome disruption [70]. In contrast, another study demonstrated that a force of 15–20 pN is needed to unwrap the nucleosome and reversibly rewrap it in 10 ms [63]. In addition, various other technologies and techniques have been developed to quantify chromatin forces, such as direct micromanipulations of chromatin using tools such as atomic force microscopy (AFM) or fluidic microscopy (FluidFM) [71,72]. These tools enable controlled mechanical forces to be applied to chromatin, and the resulting deformations to be measured [73]. The functional consequences of a global change in the spatial organization of chromatin manifest at a nanometric scale, typically ranging from 10 to 100 nm [7]. For example, stress applied via magnetic beads on the cell surface transmits force to chromatin within 10 milliseconds, followed by gene upregulation [74]. This suggests that genes are activated directly by chromatin stretching, without the involvement of relay molecules from the cytoplasmic biochemical signalling cascades. This finding highlights the critical role of local mechanical stress in exposing specific chromatin domains and directly facilitating gene transcription.

Computational modelling of chromatin mechanics

Statistical mechanics models provide quantitative descriptions of individual chromatin fibre extension in response to force, offering a theoretical framework for understanding chromatin mechanics [75,76]. Two main approaches of computational modelling of chromatin mechanics are: 1) Polymer simulation models recapitulate the structure, mechanics, and dynamic behaviour of chromatin by representing it as a semi-flexible, crosslinked polymer (with decompacted chromatin being more flexible and compacted chromatin more rigid), contained within a polymer shell (the nuclear lamina). Transcriptional activity is modelled by motor activity, such as RNA polymerase II, that unwinds the chromatin [57,77]. For instance, a strong interaction reflects a compact structure, while a weak interaction represents the more open state of the decompacted chromatin. 2) A model representing chromatin by a chain of flexible beads (representing a portion of 10 kb DNA) in Euclidean space can quantify distance or interaction frequencies between chromatin and other nuclear components [78]. Thus, combining those computational approaches with chromatin imaging or biophysical techniques will enable a deeper understanding of how chromatin architecture mechanically contributes to nuclear mechanotransduction.

Reverse mechanotransduction: concept and experimental evidence

Concept of reverse mechanotransduction

Reverse mechanotransduction refers to the inside-out transmission of mechanical signals originating from the nucleus, such as those arising from chromatin remodelling, and propagating outward to influence cytoskeletal organization, cell–cell junctions, extracellular matrix interactions, and ultimately tissue-level behaviours such as tension and morphogenesis. Rather than replacing classical mechanotransduction (outside-in signalling from the extracellular matrix to the nucleus), reverse mechanotransduction complements it, establishing a bidirectional mechanical dialogue between the cell interior and its environment. This bidirectional coupling is essential for (i) maintaining mechanical homoeostasis by balancing internal and external forces to preserve tissue integrity, (ii) enabling adaptive responses that allow cells to probe and remodel their environment during processes such as development or wound healing, and (iii) preventing pathological dysregulation, as disruptions in this balance – observed in diseases such as cancer or laminopathies – can lead to altered cellular mechanics and function. As discussed in Section 3.3, reverse mechanotransduction may rely on canonical mechanotransduction pathways, including the LINC complex, which could mediate the propagation of nuclear-derived mechanical signals towards the cytoskeleton.

Experimental evidence linking chromatin remodelling to cell mechanics

Chromatin decompaction correlates with focal adhesion structuration from the nucleus to the cell surface

Chromatin decompaction has been linked to the organization of focal adhesions and the formation of actin filaments in SW480 cells derived from human colon cancer [5]. This phenomenon has been described when chromatin decompaction is induced either by (1) the mechanical properties of the substrate or (2) by histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition. Specifically, chromatin decompaction was observed with tricostatin A (TSA), a widely used HDAC inhibitor, in SW480 cells adhered to soft hydrogels [5], , as well as with valproic acid (VPA), another HDAC inhibitor, in mesenchymal stem cells cultured on soft gels [79].

These observations led us to propose, for the first time, the concept of reverse mechanotransduction, defined here as a new model in which chromatin remodelling generates mechanical forces that are transmitted from the nucleus to the cytoskeleton and the cell surface. Although these data demonstrate a correlation between HDAC inhibitor-induced chromatin decompaction and the structuring of focal adhesions and actin filaments, a potential limitation is that HDAC inhibitors may also activate transcriptional programs independently of chromatin mechanics. In particular, mechanosensitive pathways such as YAP/TAZ signalling, known to regulate cytoskeletal organization and focal adhesion dynamics and control the expression of genes such as CTGF and CYR61 [80,81] may contribute to the observed effects.

Chromatin remodelling and global cellular mechanics

Recent work demonstrates that chromatin organization can directly affect global mechanical properties of the cell, such as plasma membrane tension and cell volume. Specifically, a process involving intense physical deformation, chromatin decompaction was found to increase membrane tension and cell volume, independently of transcriptional changes [82]. This mechanical role of chromatin structure is a fascinating aspect of our research, extending to the cellular and extracellular domains. The emerging concept of reverse mechanotransduction redefines our understanding of mechanotransduction by proposing that chromatin could act both as a receptor and transmitter of mechanical signals, influencing cell behaviour (e.g. cell adhesion and cell migration). Additional observations are consistent with this view. During carcinoma progression, large fluctuations in cell density increase nuclear size and rigidity, heterochromatin redistribution, and remodelling of the perinuclear actin architecture. It generates chronic strains, DNA damage, and nuclear envelope ruptures, activating DNA response gene program, including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) – stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signalling, and triggering transcriptional rewiring [83]. These mechanical stresses suggest that chromatin reorganization and nuclear swelling may contribute to a chromatin-triggered mechanical cascade.

Direct mechanical manipulation of chromatin

The concept of reverse mechanotransduction is further supported by studies demonstrating that chromatin can be mechanically stretched by local stress on the cell surface [25] using a three-dimensional magnetic bead [46], , upregulating transcription. Furthermore, when specific genomic loci are mechanically manipulated using piconewton-scale magnetic forces, they display micrometre-scale displacements inside the nucleus, revealing a fluid-like chromatin environment that transiently resists and redirects force transmission [84]. Taken together, these results highlight the mechanical plasticity of chromatin and support the hypothesis that chromatin remodelling contributes to the propagation of intracellular forces. However, it remains to be demonstrated whether this propagation occurs through direct mechanical transmission from chromatin to the cell surface.

Chromatin remodelling and cytoskeletal force transmission

Chromatin remodelling is associated with changes in cell migration and cytoskeletal organization, potentially through alterations in nuclear mechanics and force transmission [85]. For example, inducing chromatin decompaction and inhibiting F-actin drastically reduces migration, indicating that force transmission relies on the F-actin network [86]. In tenocytes, chromatin compaction induced by the demethylase inhibitor GSK-JA or HATi ANA inhibits tenomodulin (Tnmd) expression, a tendon-specific transmembrane glycoprotein that promotes tenocyte proliferation and migration, even under cyclic uniaxial stretching conditions. This suggests that force transmission is hindered by chromatin compaction. In contrast, chromatin decompaction reactivates Tnmd expression and enhances migration [87], , revealing that decompaction facilitates force transmission from chromatin to the cell surface. Similarly, chromatin compaction by HATi ANA or SA2D (ATP-dependent chromatin remodeller) disrupts F-actin and focal adhesions (FAs), abrogating force transmission. In contrast, our team showed that driving chromatin decompaction by removing the ANA histones inhibitors restores these structures and re-establishes force transmission [35]. In parallel, chromatin decompaction induced by VPA increases levels of F-actin, FAK, paxillin, and vinculin in FA complexes, enhancing adhesion to the extracellular matrix (ECM) [79]. Taken together, these results show that chromatin remodelling is tightly linked to cytoskeletal dynamics and the organization of focal adhesions. Chromatin decompaction also affects mechano-adaptation under fluid stress. For example, TSA-induced decompaction accelerates endothelial cell actin alignment under shear stress, while methylstat-induced compaction inhibits this alignment [88]. Similarly, TSA softens the nucleus and enhances fibroblast migration, whereas chromatin compaction stiffens the nucleus and reduces migration [89].

These observations are consistent with the hypothesis of reverse mechanotransduction, according to which chromatin decompaction may facilitate coupling between the nucleus and the cytoskeleton and, consequently, promote actin reorganization and adhesion dynamics. It remains difficult, however, to determine whether these effects result from direct transmission of mechanical force via chromatin or from indirect mechanisms, such as changes in nuclear deformability or transcriptional regulation.

Mechanistic framework and current limitations

The concept of reverse mechanotransduction is further supported by studies from Stephens et al., revealing that the cell possesses a native chromatin regulatory pathway sensing the extracellular environment, preserving nuclear morphology [22]. This study highlights the role of chromatin as an active regulator of nuclear mechanics rather than a passive structural component.

More broadly, external mechanical stimuli activate mechanotransduction through mechanosensitive ion channels in the plasma membrane [90–92] leading to chromatin compaction and heterochromatin formation [93–95]. These findings support the existence of bidirectional mechanical feedback between the cell surface and the nucleus. In this context, changes in chromatin organization may not only result from external forces but could also contribute to cellular mechanical responses.

In addition, emerging evidence underscores the bidirectional nature of mechanical feedback pathways [96] emphasizing the active role of chromatin structure alterations in the mechanical influence of the nuclear environment. For example, exposure to altered mechanical environments, such as shifts in extracellular matrix stiffness or tension, not only reorganizes chromatin architecture but also contributes to the establishment of mechanical memory that persists after the mechanical stimulus is removed, shaping phenotypic plasticity and future responses to mechanical cues. This mechanomemory effect reflects how chromatin structure alterations can actively modulate downstream cellular functions in response to past mechanical loading [97].

Future studies should adopt a multidimensional approach to dissociate the mechanical and transcriptional mechanisms i) Genetic perturbations (for exemple CRISPR targeting HDAC1/2, SUN1/2, or YAP) to isolate specific pathways; ii) Pharmacological decoupling (for example combining TSA with YAP or ROCK inhibitors, or using hexanediol to induce decompaction without HDAC inhibition) to dissociate chromatin mechanics from signalling cascades; III) Live-time cellular imaging of chromatin dynamics, actin, and focal adhesions to establish temporal hierarchies and test predictive models of force transmission. These experiments will be essential to determine whether chromatin-induced mechanical forces directly remodel the cytoskeleton, or whether parallel transcriptional programs (e.g. YAP/MRTF-A activation) are responsible for the observed phenotypic changes.

Chromatin remodelling as a potential generator of transcription-independent nuclear internal force

A central question in the context of reverse mechanotransduction is whether chromatin remodelling can generate mechanical effects independently of transcriptional activity. Chromatin remodelling determines DNA accessibility to the transcription machinery, activating the expression of genes. However, accumulating evidence suggests that chromatin organization may also influence cellular behaviour through mechanical effects that are, at least in part, independent of transcription. In this context, chromatin remodelling may contribute to the generation or modulation of internal nuclear forces, although the extent of this contribution remains to be fully established. Indeed, transcription upregulation is sensitive to the magnitude of chromatin stretching [46], , indicating a link between mechanical deformation and gene regulation. However, previous studies have revealed that the transcription machinery is not always the only parameter determining cell fate, as demonstrated by the effects of histone-modifying drugs. For example, histone-modifying drugs can induce significant changes in chromatin organization and cellular behaviour, even when transcriptional outcomes are not proportionally altered [10,98]. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) increase histone acetylation, leading to chromatin decompaction and a more accessible chromatin state.

In contrast, histone methyltransferase inhibitors target enzymes that add methyl groups to lysine or arginine residues on histone proteins. H3K4me3 and K3K36me3 methylations are associated with open chromatin and promote transcription. H3K9me3 (recruitment of heterochromatin protein 1 compacting DNA into heterochromatin) and K3K27me3 (modification of the PRC2 complex with the enzyme EZH2) compact chromatin and block transcription. While these modifications have a significant impact on transcription, they also alter chromatin organization at a higher level, which can have mechanical consequences. Importantly, several studies suggest that transcriptional modulation does not substantially alter global chromatin architecture or nuclear mechanical properties. In particular, inhibition of RNA polymerase II has been shown to have limited effects on chromatin [22,35,99] and minimal impact on the overall balance of mechanical forces between the nucleus and the actin cytoskeleton [55]. These observations support the idea that chromatin remodelling can occur independently of transcriptional activity and may contribute to cellular mechanics through structural changes. Moreover, it has been shown that the acidic peptide DEL (DELQRASIDP) causes chromatin decompaction and relocation of loci towards the nuclear centre without inducing significant gene expression (since the DEL peptide does not recruit DNA polymerase II to target promoters). The DEL peptide acts by modifying the structure of chromatin through interaction with histones. Chromatin becomes more accessible without triggering transcription [82]. Taken together, these findings suggest that chromatin remodelling can modulate nuclear organization and potentially influence cellular mechanical behaviour independently of transcription. However, whether these structural changes are sufficient to generate and transmit mechanical forces beyond the nucleus remains an open question. This distinction is essential for differentiating mechanical contributions of chromatin from transcription-driven effects in mechanobiology.

Chromatin compaction and cell migration: a complex relationship

The contrasting results reported in several studies highlight the complexity of the relationship between chromatin state and migration. TSA-induced chromatin decompaction softens the nucleus, increasing deformability and facilitating migration [5]. Other work reveals that decompaction induced by TSA or EZH2 inhibitors like GSK126, can decrease migration speed by altering nuclear polarity and disrupting the nucleus-centrosome axis required for directional migration [8]. In addition, EZH2 inhibition affects not only chromatin structure but also transcriptional programs (e.g. repression of pro-migratory genes like SNAIL/TWIST) [100]. In collective cell sheet migration, intercellular force coordination predominates over nuclear deformability. In individual migration (isolated cells in 2D/3D), nuclear softness becomes limiting, enabling passage through confined matrix pores [101]. The impact of chromatin remodelling also depends strongly on the mode of migration. In collective migration, intercellular force coordination overcomes nuclear deformability, whereas in individual 2D or 3D migration, nuclear softness becomes a key limiting factor enabling cells to pass through confined matrix pores [101]. Recent work by Zhao et al. further refines this view by demonstrating that chromatin compaction during confined migration dynamically reshapes nuclear condensates [102], , suggesting that chromatin organization is actively remodelled in response to mechanical constraints. This study highlights that chromatin is not merely a passive structural element but participates in adaptive nuclear reorganization during migration under physical confinement.

Another explanation is that chromatin decompaction strengthens the actin cytoskeleton and enhances cell-substrate adhesion, which could increase anchoring while reducing migration speed. In this context, chromatin remodelling may regulate the balance between adhesion and motility rather than directly determining force transmission. This could also lead to increased actomyosin tension as part of a broader mechanical feedback response [36,41]. In the context of reverse mechanotransduction, we therefore hypothesize that chromatin decompaction could, in certain contexts, strengthen the coupling between the nucleus and the cytoskeleton, thereby contributing to a more dynamic actomyosin network. The attachment of the nucleus to the actin cytoskeleton plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular tension. Actin contraction, generated by activated myosin II motors sliding actin filaments, produces forces that are transmitted to both the apical surface and the basal nucleus [18]. The loss of the nucleus by mechanical enucleation decreases overall cell contractility and mechanotransduction [103]. Furthermore, actin contraction controls nuclear blebbing and shape independently of nucleus confinement [104]. Chromatin decompaction is required for cell contractility [35] and migration [103]. For example, TSA-induced chromatin decompaction softens the nucleus, making it more elastic and facilitating the passage of cell through narrow spaces of the ECM, and to fill porous scaffolds in tissue engineering applications [7,105]. Additionally, recent studies have shown that chromatin decompaction is crucial for maintaining nuclear elasticity and facilitating cellular movements in constrained environments [106]. Interestingly, other studies have shown that chromatin compaction induced by wound-healing assay, which leads to a smaller and stiffer nucleus, helps the actomyosin cytoskeleton to pull the nucleus, facilitating cell migration in confined spaces [107,108]. Overall, these findings suggest that chromatin state does not exert a unidirectional effect on migration but instead tunes distinct mechanical migration strategies. In this context, chromatin remodelling may modulate the balance between nuclear deformability, cytoskeletal tension, and adhesion dynamics. While these observations are consistent with the reverse mechanotransduction hypothesis, direct evidence of force transmission from chromatin to the cytoskeleton remains limited, and alternative mechanisms, including changes in nuclear mechanics and transcriptional regulation, must be carefully considered.

Alternative indirect pathway via the β-Catenin/Wnt signaling

Chromatin remodelling also influences the β-Catenin/Wnt pathway, which regulates cell adhesion and migration by modulating β-Catenin localization and cytoskeletal dynamics [109]. EZH2 and PCR2 are critical for chromatin compaction and myogenic differentiation via the Wnt pathway [47,110]. Disruption of this pathway, e.g. using emerin-null myogenic progenitors [111], , suggests that the transmission of force from the nucleus to the Wnt pathway involves emerin, a nuclear envelope protein. Inducing chromatin decompaction with TSA on a soft substrate leads to the active β-Catenin translocation into the nucleus, while inducing chromatin compaction with HATi Pu139 reduces β-Catenin levels, affecting processes like osteogenic differentiation. Furthermore, lamin A/C regulates the nuclear localization of β-Catenin via emerin, suggesting a synergistic role of chromatin conformation, lamina, and emerin in Wnt pathway. These results point to a reverse mechanotransduction pathway where chromatin remodelling influences the Wnt pathway through lamina A/C and emerin, offering an alternative to the force transmission pathways involving the LINC complex, F-actin, and RhoA.

Despite growing evidence, several limitations remain. Most studies rely on indirect readouts of nuclear mechanics or cytoskeletal organization, and direct measurements of force generation by chromatin are still limited. Furthermore, it remains difficult to distinguish mechanical effects from transcriptional or biochemical signals. Overall, while numerous studies report correlations between chromatin organization and cellular mechanical behaviour, direct evidence of force transmission from chromatin to the cell surface remains limited, and alternative explanations must be considered.

Nucleus deformation and bleb formation

A recent study also found that transcription inhibition suppressed nuclear bleb formation and stabilization, as well as bleb-based nuclear ruptures, but had no effect on the state of histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) modification or chromatin stiffness [57]. Nucleus deformation can lead to the formation of nuclear blebs (temporary nuclear protrusion), reflecting the cell’s response to mechanical cues. Chromatin is a recognized determinant in maintaining normal nuclear shape and play a role in nuclear blebs formation [112–114]. For example, abnormal chromatin compaction can generate localized forces that deform the nuclear envelope, resulting in blebs. Interestingly, while inhibiting transcription suppresses blebbing, it does not affect nuclear stiffness, major histone modifications, or nuclear deformation [38,115,116]. Furthermore, inducing chromatin decompaction with histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), such as TSA, VPA or DZNep, increases nuclear blebs independently of transcription and lamina activity. It is important to note that bleb composition is independent of histone modifications or lamina level [58]. These findings suggest that chromatin itself may exert forces to push the nuclear envelope outward, independently from other nuclear components. This hypothesis was recently verified by the experimental compaction and decompaction of chromatin in the presence of 5,6-dichloro-1β-D-ribofuranosylbenzimidazole (DRB). DRB is a transcription inhibitor that blocks the phosphorylation of the C-terminal domain of the large RNA polymerase II subunit. Remodelling of chromatin when transcription machinery is inhibited for 24 h by DRB does not prevent the assembly of focal adhesions (FAs). These results therefore suggest that, during chromatin decompaction, cells use proteins that have been depolymerized during the compaction process to reassemble the focal adhesions, rather than using newly synthesized cytoskeletal proteins [35].

Additionally, studies have shown that chromatin rapidly senses cellular physical confinement in the following manner. Cells perceive the size of the substrate via focal adhesions (FAs) and the RhoA/ROCK and YAP/TAZ pathways. On large substrates, cells spread out to their maximum extent and exhibit high contractility. In contrast, cell contractility is low on small substrates. On large substrates, G-actin polymerizes into F-actin, which allows the nuclear translocation of MKL and the activation of pro-contractile genes. On small substrates, however, F-actin polymerization is weak and MKL remains in the cytoplasm while HDAC3 translocate into the nucleus. The nuclear enzyme HDAC3 deacetylates histones (e.g. H3K9ac), resulting in chromatin compaction, reduced nuclear volume and increased nuclear rigidity. Consequently, compacted chromatin and the rigid nucleus restrict the transmission of mechanical forces, thereby reducing contractility further [117]. Chromatin is engaged in feedback control through internal forces, offering a new ‘inside-out’ perspective on its role in reverse mechanotransduction [17,97].

In the next chapter, we identify the mechanical partners involved in the propagation of internal nuclear force from chromatin architecture to focal adhesions.

Key mechanical partners in the reverse mechanotransduction pathway

To integrate these elements into a unified framework, we next examine how key mechanical components, including the nuclear envelope, LINC complex, cytoskeleton, and integrins, may mediate the propagation of chromatin-derived forces towards the cell surface.

Nuclear envelope proteins transmit internal forces across the cell

The nuclear envelope (NE) is a dynamic scaffold connecting chromatin to the cytoskeleton [38,118,119]. NE is composed of nuclear pore complexes, lamins that provide structural support, and the LINC complex such as SUN and KASH domain proteins (described in the chapter LINC complex below). During chromatin remodelling, the EN must adapt to changes in nuclear volume and nuclear pressure, modifications in nuclear rigidity, and the redistribution of cytoskeletal forces to and from the nucleus in order to preserve cellular functionality. The internal pressure of the nucleus is balanced by forces arising from both the cytoskeleton and chromatin remodelling [120,121]. The local stiffening of the cell nucleus enhances interactions between the cytoskeleton and the nucleoskeleton under tension, facilitating the efficient transmission of tensile forces in both directions [122]. Moreover, the spring-like behaviour of chromatin during its transition from compaction to decompaction exerts mechanical forces by pushing the NE outward, thus altering the shape and the rigidity of the nucleus [22,31]. Conversely, chromatin compaction pulls the NE [123,124], , which may resist or store mechanical forces. This chromatin remodelling can also affect the conformation of NE proteins and their ability to transmit signals throughout the cell via the cytoskeleton.

The lamina and the nuclear rigidity

In most cell types, compacted chromatin (heterochromatin) is restricted to an irregular rim located at the nuclear periphery, tightly bound to the nuclear lamina via lamina-associated domains (LADs). Compacted chromatin is also located around the nucleolus as well as in patches throughout the nucleoplasm, while decompacted chromatin (euchromatin) fills up the majority of the nucleus and is found close to the nuclear pore complex (NPCs) [38,125]. In direct mechanotransduction, the nuclear lamina contributes to nuclear rigidity and plays a major role in force transmission, influencing chromatin dynamics [126,127]. However, studies have shown that chromatin mechanics dominate the regulation of nuclear rigidity, rather than the nuclear lamina [112]. The degree of chromatin compaction directly influences the mechanical properties of the nucleus, including its rigidity and resistance to deformation [37]. For example, chromatin decompaction by HDACi or HMTi (histone methyltransferase inhibitors) disrupts its interaction with LADs, softens the nucleus and increases nuclear volume without affecting nuclear lamina levels [128,129]. This confirms the central role of chromatin in maintaining nuclear integrity and mechanical stability. Consistently, chromatin decompaction by TSA and lamin A/C deletion enhances cell migration independently of the force transmission mediated by lamin A/C, suggesting that chromatin remodelling may be the key player in this phenomenon. Mechanistically, the transition from a compacted to a decompacted state disconnects chromatin from LADs, weakening the structural support provided by the nuclear lamina. This results in a softer nuclear rigidity and a displacement of decompacted chromatin towards the nuclear interior. Subsequently, the decompacted chromatin accumulated in the nucleus could exert internal forces, physically pushing outward on the NE without interacting with nuclear lamins and LADs [61]. Conversely, chromatin remodelling from a decompacted to a compacted state would reconnect to the LADs, stiffening the nucleus by exerting a pulling force to reduce nuclear volume [12].

In reverse mechanotransduction, force transmission from chromatin remodelling to the cell surface may involve nuclear components other than the nuclear lamina. Furthermore, chromatin mobility itself could exert direct pressure on the NE without an intermediary. However, distinguishing direct force generation from indirect effects mediated by changes in nuclear mechanics remains challenging.

In pathological contexts such as laminopathies, where the nuclear lamina is compromised, force transmission is not abolished but reconfigured. For instance, Leong et al. explored a targeted genetic approach. Using CRISPR, they modified the KASH domain of Nesprin-1, a key component of the LINC (Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton) complex, in cardiomyocytes from mice carrying LMNA mutations. Since Nesprin-1 is the primary anchor of the NE for microtubular components, its loss leads to the delocalization of microtubular proteins from the nucleus. However, disruption of Nesprin-1 attenuates LMNA-related cardiac pathology through a reduction in nuclear microtubular activity [130]. This indicates that force transmission by microtubules may be dysregulated rather than compensatory in this context. However, the effect of inducing chromatin remodelling on NL or other NE components has been poorly studied [131]. Further investigations are needed to confirm these hypotheses.

The LINC complex

The LInker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton (LINC) complex is a physical element that could transmit the internal forces generated by chromatin decompaction itself outward to the nuclear envelope. The LINC complex physically connects the nucleoskeleton to the cytoskeleton through the NE, mediating force transmission and regulating chromatin organization [19,126]. For example, in direct mechanotransduction, the LINC complex transmits tensile, compressive, and shear forces to chromatin [132]. The LINC complex links chromatin via nuclear lamina to SUN proteins (Sad1 and UNC-84) on the inner nuclear membrane (INM), while connecting to the cytoskeleton via KASH proteins (Klarsicht, ANC-1, and Syne Homology), such as nesprins, anchored on the outer nuclear membrane (ONM) [123,133,134] (Figure 1). Nesprins bind to SUN protein trimers, creating a mechanical link between ONM and INM. This mechanical link transmits forces between the chromatin and the cytoskeleton [135]. Disruption of the LINC complex alters force transmission and disrupts the actin cytoskeleton, demonstrating its key role in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity [136]. Chromatin structure might also regulate the assembly of LINC complex proteins and their ability to transmit signals across the NE. LINC complexes containing INM Sun2 protein promote focal adhesion assembly by activating the small GTPase RhoA. However, in cells lacking Sun2, focal adhesions fail to assemble despite the presence of SRF/Mk11 transcription factor/coactivator complex. These results suggest that LINC complexes regulate RhoA activity through a transcription-independent mechanism. Additionally, the INM Sun1 protein antagonizes LINC Sun2 complexes, inhibiting RhoA activation and focal adhesion assembly [137]. Hieda and co-workers demonstrated that SUN1 interacts with proteins of the LINC complex, which physically connects the nucleus to actin filaments. They revealed that signals initiated in the nucleus (e.g. changes in nuclear regulation or chromatin remodelling) can be transmitted via SUN1 and the LINC complex to modulate cytoskeletal dynamics and the maturation of focal adhesions [136]. This represents an ‘inside-out’ signalling pathway identified in this review as a reverse mechanotransduction pathway. Consistently, recent work has shown that SUN1/2 proteins regulate macrophage polarization by modulating nuclear size and stiffness, highlighting how LINC-dependent changes in nuclear mechanics can influence downstream cellular behaviour [138]. These findings further support the idea that nuclear mechanical states, potentially influenced by chromatin remodelling, can contribute to inside-out signalling pathways. However, a disturbance of SUN1 could alter nuclear rigidity, thereby affecting the sensitivity of integrins to external forces and, consequently, the maturation of focal adhesions via a parallel signalling pathway. The LINC complex is composed of SUN proteins located in the inner nuclear membrane and KASH (Klarsicht/ANC-1/Syne-1 homology) proteins located in the outer nuclear membrane. SUN proteins interact with KASH proteins to form a bridge that spans the nuclear envelope. This complex physically connects the nuclear lamina and chromatin to the cytoskeleton, facilitating the transmission of mechanical forces between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. At the ONM, nesprins bind directly with the actin and indirectly with microtubules and intermediate filaments, linking the cytoskeleton to the SUN proteins in INM [139,140]. Enucleation or depletion of LINC complex components disconnects the nucleus from the cytoskeleton, leading to changes in the perinuclear organization of actin stress fibres [141]. In summary, these results suggest that chromatin remodelling alters the structure and function of proteins in the LINC complex, modifying the mechanical properties of the nucleus and the transmission of mechanical signals throughout the cell. Alternatively, the force generated by the decompacted chromatin could directly push the nuclear envelope outwards without the intervention of the LINC complex.

Cytoskeleton, a dynamic link between chromatin and focal adhesion

Bidirectional mechanical communication between chromatin and the cytoskeleton has been demonstrated. This bidirectional mechanical communication results in a dynamic feedback loop, stabilizing and enhancing the actomyosin cytoskeleton, ultimately stimulating cellular contractility. The contractile forces exerted by the cytoskeleton induce chromatin remodelling in the nucleus. Elastic restoring forces are thus generated at the chromatin level. These nuclear forces, in turn, alter the actin cytoskeleton, and may deform the ECM. Finally, this bidirectional communication transmits mechanical signals from the cell surface to the nucleus, and reciprocally from the nucleus to the cell surface. This phenomenon illustrates the adaptive mechanoresponse of chromatin to mechanical forces. The principal network in nuclear mechanotransduction consists of the actin cytoskeleton (F-actin), myosin II and the small GTPase RhoA. The actin cytoskeleton provides rapid transmission (seconds to minutes) of mechanical signals within cells, regulating migration, division and morphological changes [142,143]. Chromatin anchors to focal adhesions (FAs) via the LINC complex and the F-actin network, contributing to mechanical force generation and cytoskeletal organization. This connection remains vital for F-actin organization, FAs and cell-cell adhesion [38,135]. Yang and co-workers demonstrated that intercellular junctions exhibit a viscoelastic response dependent on the rate of deformation, meaning their strength and ability to reorganize are rate-sensitive [144]. The observed synchronized junction failures at high deformation rates could be explained by the limited capacity of junctions to absorb and dissipate mechanical energy. In this context, we propose that rapid chromatin remodelling may generate forces that exceed this threshold, leading to abrupt, coordinated junction rupture. If multiple cells undergo simultaneous rapid chromatin remodelling, for example, in response to a global stimulus, these forces could become synchronized, potentially leading to collective junction failures. While direct experimental evidence is currently lacking, recent studies have shown that changes in chromatin conformation can generate forces sufficient to deform the nucleus and cytoskeleton [97], [145] supporting the idea that chromatin can act as an internal mechanical driver. This raises the possibility that chromatin tension could be transmitted to cell-cell junctions and propagated to neighbouring cells. Such force transmission across junctions enables tissue-scale mechanical coordination, which is essential in processes such as morphogenesis and cell differentiation. Therefore, a local tension could affect both the originating cell and its surrounding cellular environment via mechanical signals. Studies in embryonic development have shown that gradients of mechanical tension correlate with spatial patterns of cell differentiation [146]. In addition, chromatin remodelling stabilizes the cytoskeleton and dynamics of talin, vinculin and FAK at FAs [19,25,88]. Changes in the organization of these FAs can modify nuclear and chromatin architecture [147,148], indicating that chromatin forces generated by chromatin remodelling regulate the maturation and dynamics of FAs [149,150]. Disruption of nuclear mechasnosensing, by disconnecting the nucleus from the cytoskeleton, upregulates HDAC. Blocking HDAC activity with TSA rescue histone acetylation, nuclear area and stress fibre formation FAs [151]. These results suggest that chromatin remodelling contributes to stress fibre formation via a reverse mechanotransduction pathway. Chromatin decompaction by inhibitors TSA, SAHA or tubustatin A, also increases tubulin acetylation, promoting cell elasticity and reducing stress fibre turnover [152]. Similarly, in acute liver failure (ALF), the HDAC6 inhibitor ACY1215 increases F-actin and vinculin levels, suggesting the contribution of chromatin remodelling to cytoskeletal stabilization [153]. Moreover, inhibitor cytochalasin B reduces F-actin and vinculin in ALF pretreated with ACY1215, confirming the contribution of chromatin decompaction to the dynamics of these cytoskeletal elements. These observations suggest a strong correlation between chromatin state and cytoskeletal organization, although direct mechanical coupling remains to be demonstrated.

The small GTPase RhoA

In direct mechanotransduction, the GTPase RhoA, a key effector of integrin signalling, orchestrates the actin cytoskeleton dynamic, cell adhesion and migration and actively participates in force transmission, thereby regulating the chromatin organization. RhoA achieves its regulatory roles by cycling between an inactive GDP-bound state and an active GTP-bound state, interacting with various effectors and regulatory proteins. Confinement of the cell below the size of the nucleus causes nuclear deformation, increasing nuclear membrane tension and opening ion channels, which releases Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum. Increased Ca2+ concentrations and nuclear membrane tension favour the association of the calcium-dependent phospholipase cPLA2 with INM, where cPLA2 produces arachidonic acid. Arachidonic acid facilitates RhoA activation and myosin II recruitment to the cell cortex, inducing cell migration. These processes constitute a reverse transduction mechanism with RhoA as key regulator [154] as demonstrated by Buisson et al. They showed that after chromatin compaction to decompaction, RhoA inhibition by CT04 collapses the cells, adopting a round shaped morphology with a reduced adhesion strength [35].

Integrins: linking the cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix

In direct mechanotransduction, integrins relay force transmission from ECM to chromatin via FAs and the actin cytoskeleton. Talin and vinculin are the main FA connectors between integrins and actin fibres, binding β1-Integrin and actin together [155]. In reverse mechanotransduction, integrins are essential for cell adhesion, linking the cytoskeleton to the ECM and transmitting signals bidirectionally [26,156]. Recent studies have shown that artificial chromatin decompaction using HDAC1-3 inhibitors (e.g. MS-275) profoundly impacts the expression of adhesion molecules, cytoskeleton regulators, and ECM proteins in mesothelial cells. This promotes epithelial-like morphology and modulates α5β1 integrin activity [156,157]. Induction of chromatin decompaction by MS-275 disrupts α5β1-integrin activity in tumour cells by downregulating actin cytoskeleton regulators such as Talin-1 and α-Actinin 1. These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that HDACi, such as TSA, induce the expression of integrin α4, β2, and β6 subunits in hepatocyte cell lines [158,159]. To summarize, a possible scenario of reverse mechanotransduction transmitting mechanical signals from the nucleus to the extracellular matrix involves the following multi-step pathway: 1) Changes in chromatin tension or nuclear rigidity activate SUN1/2 proteins in the inner nuclear membrane. 2) SUN1/2 connects to Nesprins (outer nuclear membrane), bridging the nucleus to the actin cytoskeleton (LIN complex).3) Forces propagate through actin fibres and RhoA, modulated by myosin contraction and stabilized by focal adhesions (FAs) (talin, vinculin). 4) Activated integrins (β1-integrin) transmit forces to the ECM, reinforcing adhesion and enabling dynamic cellular responses (migration, mechanosensing).

Physiopathological impact of chromatin remodeling

Interestingly, the effects of chromatin remodelling appear to differ between cancerous and healthy cells, reflecting the complexity of reverse mechanotransduction signalling. In cancer cells, chromatin decompaction often inhibits force transmission, whereas in healthy cells it promotes it. This disparity accentuates the cell-type-specific nature of chromatin remodelling effects. HDACi, such as TSA and MS-275, known for their anti-cancer properties, can induce different effects depending on the cellular context [106,160–162]. For example, while HDACi such as TSA, sodium butyrate (NaB), VPA, or MS-275 promote the proliferation and differentiation of healthy osteoblasts [163], , they inhibit cell proliferation in cancer cells [164]. HDAC1/2 inhibition also affects cell adhesion by altering the actin cytoskeleton polymerization and reducing the expression of actin and integrin regulators, such as Talin 1, which is crucial for focal adhesion formation and integrin activation by linking the actin cytoskeleton to integrin receptors. This deficiency decreases β1 integrin activation and disrupts extracellular secretion and distribution of fibronectin 1, highlighting a pathway from chromatin remodelling to actin cytoskeleton, FAs, β1 integrin, ECM organization and, ultimately, the adhesion of unhealthy cells. These observations suggest that chromatin remodelling plays a central role in regulating interactions through the talin-integrin signalling pathway, which is essential for cell adhesion, shape and migration. Chromatin remodelling may influence this pathway by transmitting mechanical forces from chromatin to the actin cytoskeleton, then to talin, integrins and finally to the ECM. The contrasting effects of TSA on tumour and healthy cells highlight the cell-type specificity of the impact of chromatin remodelling, reinforcing the concept of reverse mechanotransduction. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these differential effects, and to explore the therapeutic potential of targeting chromatin remodellers in various cellular contexts.

Conclusion

This review introduces the new concept of reverse mechanotransduction, describing how chromatin remodelling generates and transmits mechanical forces that influence cellular behaviour. We suggest that: 1) Chromatin acts as a spring and serves as an active mechanical epicentre, generating and transmitting mechanical forces within the cell. 2) The transmission of force from the chromatin to the cell surface, named reverse mechanotransduction, challenges the unidirectional view of direct mechanotransduction by suggesting that chromatin can initiate mechanical signals. 3) Chromatin remodelling directly impacts nuclear stiffness, cytoskeletal dynamics, and focal adhesion formation, mechanically influencing cellular behaviour independently of transcriptional activity. 4) Chromatin remodelling regulates cellular processes such as migration and adhesion through reverse mechanotransduction, involving mechanosensors such as LINC complex, F-actin cytoskeleton, focal adhesions, and integrins. These observations strongly support the idea of a bidirectional mechanotransduction between the nucleus and cytoskeleton, where chromatin responds to external mechanical forces through remodelling. Then, according to its degree of compaction, chromatin either stores the forces by pulling the nuclear envelope to protect the genome or generates internal forces that are then transmitted back to the cytoskeleton and cell surface, influencing cellular mechanics. Future research will need to clarify mechanosensors and pathways involved in reverse mechanotransduction, particularly how chromatin remodelling affects force transmission to nuclear envelope proteins, microtubules, or intermediate filaments, which remain little explored. Advances in biophysical technologies, such as AFM and magnetic tweezers, will be essential to quantification in real time the internal forces generated by chromatin remodelling. Additionally, the study of reverse mechanotransduction in different cell types could reveal its role in various biological processes, such as development, tissue regeneration, and disease progression. This knowledge guides the development of innovative therapeutic strategies for the treatment of diseases or regenerative therapies. For example, designing biomaterials with specific topographies or rigidities that deliver chromatin remodellers in a controlled manner could promote tissue repair. Overall, integrating reverse mechanotransduction with direct mechanotransduction will lead to a better understanding of cellular mechanics and chromatin mechanobiology, particularly how cells integrate external and internal mechanical forces to regulate their behaviour. Together, these observations suggest that chromatin may act both as a mechanical integrator and as a potential driver of intracellular force transmission, although the extent and mechanisms of this contribution remain to be fully elucidated.
