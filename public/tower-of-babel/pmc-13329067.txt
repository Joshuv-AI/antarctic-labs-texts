Training that sticks: sustaining knowledge and communication excellence in geriatric oncology through a multidisciplinary training program
Authors: Patricia A Parker, Koshy Alexander, Smita C Banerjee, Yesne Alici, Christian Nelson, Elizabeth Schofield, Nessa Coyle, Andrew J Roth, Ruth Manna, Javier Gonzalez, Beatriz Korc-Grodzicki, Mary R Janevic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe number of older adults with cancer, those who require complex needs and care, continues to increase. With limited learning opportunities, the healthcare workforce is unprepared to meet this growing need. This study examined the long-term impact of Geriatric Oncology: Cognition and Communication (Geri Onc CC), a multidisciplinary program developed and implemented to address this gap in care.MethodsMultidisciplinary healthcare providers (HCPs) were recruited from across the United States and internationally to participate virtually or in person at a comprehensive cancer center in the Northeast United States. The program included education about geriatric syndromes, unique and complex issues related to older adults with cancer, and tailored communication strategies. Knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy were assessed pre- and post-training and at 6- and 12-month follow-ups. Skill utilization and implementation practices were evaluated at 6- and 12-month follow-ups only.Results282 HCPs participated. Initial gains in knowledge and self-efficacy were maintained at both 6 and 12 months post-training (p values <.01). More than 70% indicated using all skill categories “somewhat more” or “much more” at both follow-up periods. Most HCPs implemented changes in their clinical practice, teaching, and/or research based on what they learned.Discussion and ImplicationsGeri Onc CC has demonstrated a sustained, positive impact in many areas, including knowledge and self-efficacy. It also led to greater usage of the skills learned and changes in clinical care, teaching, and research. The program addresses a critical gap in education and training of the workforce caring for older adults with cancer.Keywords: Interdisciplinary education, Evaluation, Cognition, Clinician–patient communication

Geri Onc CC is an evidence-based program to enhance knowledge and communication skills of healthcare team members who treat older adults with cancer. Offering an innovative opportunity in an interactive, supportive learning environment, it combines didactic knowledge-based content, case-based discussions, and interactive exercises, and addresses geriatric syndromes, unique and complex issues in treating older adults with cancer, and effective communication with them and their caregivers. Its key strength is its long-term follow-up evaluation and demonstration of its sustained impact. By preparing the healthcare workforce to meet the needs of this growing population, Geri Onc CC enhances patient- and family-centered care.

Background and objectives

The number of individuals with cancer continues to grow. Fifty-nine percent of people diagnosed with cancer in the United States are 65 years or older.1 Global estimates suggest that rates of cancer in older individuals will continue to rise.2

Managing cancer in older adults can be extraordinarily complex. Numerous factors, such as cognitive decline, frailty, comorbidities, polypharmacy, and sensory impairment, often co-exist with cancer and need to be considered to provide optimal cancer care.3,4 Even though there are growing numbers of older adults with cancer, and they have many unique care considerations, there are few geriatric specialists. Most patients receive care from other types of oncology healthcare providers (HCPs). Yet, most HCPs do not receive training in how to effectively support older patients in their oncology care.5,6

For older patients with cancer, their function, cognition, psychosocial issues, caregiver-related issues, and other geriatric syndromes need to be understood and considered by the oncology team. The integration of geriatric teaching into oncology should include not only oncologists but all members of the multidisciplinary healthcare team. Oncology nurses, advanced practice providers, psychiatrists, social workers, pharmacists, occupational therapists, and other members of the health care team who interact with older patients regularly need to receive geriatric oncology education throughout their training continuum.7 There have been some initiatives to enhance HCPs’ knowledge and skills in geriatric oncology through the integration of relevant content into oncology fellowship programs8 as well as through stand-alone trainings.9,10 A few programs have included some form of program evaluation that have demonstrated some beneficial effects, including learner satisfaction and increased knowledge and confidence, although sample sizes have been small.9,11–13 Overall, however, it is clear that there are insufficient opportunities for HCPs to receive specialized training in how to best care for and communicate with older adults with cancer.14 Additionally, programs have rarely examined whether any positive effects of the program persist and how it gets integrated into HCPs’ practices.

To meet this need, we developed a training program, Geriatric Oncology: Cognition and Communication (Geri Onc CC). Briefly, Geri Onc CC is a 2-day virtual or in-person training followed by six brief, collaborative videoconference activities over a 12-month period. The training provides education about geriatric syndromes, unique and complex issues for older individuals with cancer, and how to most effectively communicate with them and their caregivers.

We previously established the efficacy of the Geri Onc CC program in terms of participant satisfaction with the course, increased self-efficacy, greater knowledge of geriatric oncology, and significant uptake of general and geriatric-specific communication skills immediately after participating in the 2-day training.15 However, whether these positive results persist is not yet known. Accordingly, the goal of this article was to examine the long-term (6- and 12-month) impact of the Geri Onc CC Training Program. Specifically, we examined HCPs’ knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy, reported use of communication skills, whether they made changes to clinical care, teaching, and/or research after participating in the program, and the extent to which participants shared their learning with others.

Research design and Methods

Geriatric oncology cognition and communication training program

Details on the development, format, and content of Geri Onc CC are described elsewhere.15,16 Briefly, Geri Onc CC is a training program for multi-disciplinary HCPs that aims to improve knowledge and communication skills when caring for older adults with cancer and their loved ones. The study was conducted at a comprehensive cancer center in the northeast. Participants were recruited nationally and internationally through a variety of organizations. We conducted most of the training virtually; one was conducted in person at the investigators’ institution. The main training lasts 2 days. On the first day of training, HCPs learn about topics such as delirium, depression, dementia, and decision-making capacity. On the second day, they participate in three communication skills training modules (Geriatrics 101, Cognitive Syndromes, Shared Decision Making with patients and families) in which participants put into practice what they learn on the first day of the training with standardized patients (i.e., professionally trained actors who portray patients and caregivers).17–20 These modules include both didactic content and tailored small group skill rehearsal with standardized patients and caregivers. HCPs then participate in six brief collaborative videoconferencing activities over a 12-month period. Each videoconferencing activity was 1-hour and was led by program faculty. They were bi-monthly and consisted of didactics, case-based learning, and discussion related to cognition, nutrition, polypharmacy, Geriatric Assessment, cultural considerations, and resources available for individuals with cognitive impairment. It also provided HCPs with opportunities to discuss challenging cases or issues they have encountered in their own clinical settings. Participants received continuing education credits for their participation in the training.

This project was determined to be exempt from review by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (X19-044).

Participants and procedures

HCPs were recruited through several marketing methods, including e-mail, social media, our institution’s Continuing Medical Education office, listservs, and through oncology, internal medicine, geriatrics, and psycho-oncology. Interested HCPs completed an online application about their experience and interest in participating in the training and were selected and prioritized based on their work with older adults with cancer, their disciplines, the settings in which they worked, and their location, with the goal of having diverse cohorts (approximately 20 HCPs per cohort). Eligibility requirements included the ability to speak, read, and understand English proficiently to participate in study activities. All study requirements/assessments were described in the application, and verbal consent was provided prior to their participation in study activities.

Before the training, post-training, and 6 and 12 months later, HCPs completed assessments about their self-efficacy, knowledge about geriatric oncology, and attitude toward older adults. At the 6- and 12-month follow-ups, participants also completed measures of self-reported communication skill utilization in practice settings, measures of implementation, interpersonal communication about training content, and preferences for additional training.

Measures

Measures relevant to the current article are described below:

Demographic and work characteristics

Participants were asked to provide their race/ethnicity, gender, discipline, academic degree, experience, and interest in working with older adults, and work settings.

Self-efficacy

The self-efficacy measure was developed by the authors and used in previous studies of communication skills training,20–24 adapted to the specific content of this training. It has been shown to have good internal consistency reliability in previous studies. HCPs rated their perceived ability to complete the tasks of each module on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree). It was completed at all four assessment points with a summary score calculated as the mean across items (range = 1–5).

Knowledge about geriatric oncology

Knowledge was assessed with 10 multiple-choice questions that were based on the specific course content. Our team members reviewed these items for clarity, and they were pretested on a group of five HCPs. It was completed at all four assessment points with a sum (range = 0–10) used for analysis.

Attitude toward older adults

The UCLA Geriatric Attitudes Scale25,26 measured attitude toward older adults with 14 items, each on a scale from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree, with higher scores indicating a more favorable attitude toward older adults. It was completed at all four assessment points, and a sum score (range = 14–70) was calculated.

Self-reported communication skill utilization in practice setting

Participants completed an assessment of their use of each of five categories of communication skills taught in the course (Agenda Setting, Checking, Questioning, Information Organization, and Empathic Communication) with their last 5 patients using a 5-point Likert Scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always). This measure was only completed at 6- and 12-month follow-ups.

Interpersonal communication about the training content

Interpersonal communication about the Geri Onc CC training was evaluated by one item: “After attending the Geri Onc CC training, have you talked to anyone about what you learned in the training?” If they responded yes, they were asked with whom they shared information (colleague/team within same discipline, colleague/team from another discipline, supervisor, trainee/student, at grand rounds/department meeting or similar setting, or other). This measure was completed at 6- and 12-month follow-ups only.

Measure of implementation

We asked participants to indicate whether they had made any clinical, teaching, or research changes after participating in Geri Onc CC . Response options included “yes, sought out additional information,” “yes, I have implemented new information,” “no, my current practice already reflected activity recommendations,” or “no.” If they indicated yes, they were asked how they have incorporated what they have learned into each of the three areas (open-ended response). If they indicated no, they were asked to respond whether they had encountered any barriers from a list of seven barriers (e.g., time barrier, cost barrier, patient barrier). This measure was only completed at 6- and 12-month follow-ups.

Preferences for additional training

Participants were asked whether they were interested in receiving additional training in geriatric oncology, whether they wanted to train others, and whether they would be interested in participating in a train-the-trainer program. This measure was only completed at 6- and 12-month follow-ups.

Data analysis

Participants’ demographic and professional characteristics, along with pre-training communication challenges and previous training, are described using summary statistics. Participants with assessments completed through 12 months were compared to non-completers on pre-training characteristics and measures using a series of independent samples t-tests and Chi-square tests. Summary scores for self-efficacy, knowledge, and attitude were summarized at pre-training and each longitudinal timepoint (i.e., post-training, 6- and 12-month follow-up), with longitudinal means compared to pre-training using a series of paired samples t-tests using all pairwise complete data. Effect sizes are presented as Cohen’s d. In addition to raw p-values, all findings were assessed using the Hochberg step-up adjustment27 to control type I error among multiple comparisons. Summary scores for self-efficacy, knowledge, and attitude were summarized at pre-training and each longitudinal timepoint (i.e., post-training, 6- and 12-month follow-up), with longitudinal means compared to pre-training using a series of paired samples t-tests using all pairwise complete data. Self-reported skill changes by skill type (e.g., Agenda Setting), communication about training, further information seeking, integration of principles and implementation, and training preferences are all summarized separately for 6- and 12-month follow-up. Statistical analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC).

Results

Two-hundred eighty-two HCPs participated in the training. Participants were 87% female, 66% were white, and 67% were non-Hispanic/Latino. Participants were from more than 12 professions, with social work (24%), physicians (20%), nurse practitioners (13%), and nurses (12%) being the most common. They were from 37 states and 19 countries. They worked in more than 11 types of settings, with comprehensive cancer centers (41%), community hospitals/clinics (21%), and academic medical centers (17%) being the most frequent. Eight percent of the HCPs had been caring for older adults with cancer for less than a year, 33% for 1–5 years, 24% for 6–10 years, 15% for 11–15 years, and 19% for 16 or more years. Twenty-five percent of the HCPs reported that they had previously completed some type of communication skills training, 92% reported that they at least occasionally experienced communication challenges with their patients, and 43% reported that they have communication challenges with patients at least weekly. All 282 participants completed the training; 99% (n = 280) completed the pre-training questionnaire, 96% (n = 270) completed the post-training questionnaire, 66% (n = 188) completed the 6-month follow-up questionnaire, and 65% (n = 188) completed the 12-month questionnaire. Those who completed 12-month questionnaires did not differ significantly from non-completers on gender, race, ethnicity, clinical area, age distribution of patients, work title, years of experience with older adult cancer patients, previous communication skills training, or employment setting.

Self-efficacy and knowledge, but not attitudes, increased following training. Mean self-efficacy for caring for and communicating with older adults with cancer increased from 3.3 (SD = 0.7) at pre-training by over 1-point at post-training (M = 4.5, SD = 0.5; (d = 1.86, p < .001), with the improvement remaining significantly higher than pre-training at both 6 months (d = 1.60, p < .001) and 12 months (d = 1.62, p < .001). Scores for knowledge about aspects of geriatric oncology had smaller, but still significant increases, from a mean of 6.1 (SD = 1.6) at pre-training, to 6.7 (SD = 1.5) at post (d = 0.40, p < .001), with sustained effects at 6 months (d = 0.23, p = .002) and 12 months (d = 0.27, p < .001). At pre-training, the mean score for attitudes toward older adults was 57.3 (SD = 5.3), with no significant differences observed at any longitudinal time points. Completers did not differ significantly from non-completers on baseline self-efficacy, knowledge, or attitudes. All significant findings held after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

When asked the extent to which participants used the communication skills learned and practiced during the training, more than 70% reported using all categories of skills “somewhat more” or “much more” at both 6-month (74%) and 12-month (76%) follow-ups (Figure 1). In fact, compared to before the training, 91% of participants indicated they were using agenda-setting skills more than before the training, 93% reported using more information organization skills, and 95% reported using more checking skills 12 months later.

Self-reported skill change.

When asked if they had talked to others about the training, 92% reported they had done so at the 6-month follow-up and 94% at the 12-month follow-up. When asked who they had talked to about the Geri Onc CC program, 80% (6-month follow-up) and 83% (12-month follow-up) indicated they had talked to a colleague at their hospital, and 39% (6-months) and 41% (12-months) reported they had talked to colleagues at other institutions.

HCPs indicated that participating in the Geri Onc CC training encouraged them to seek out additional information/learning and made changes to their clinical care, teaching, and research activities. At the 6-month follow-up, 23% of HCPs reported that they sought out additional information, and 53% implemented changes to clinical care based on their learning. There were similar percentages at 12 months: 19% sought out additional information, and 63% reported implementing information learned into clinical practice. Examples of how they incorporated the training into their clinical practice included structuring a family case conference to follow the shared decision making module, feeling more comfortable with leading conversations about shared decision-making, incorporating the skill of agenda setting when working with patients, performing parts of the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment in their practice, now screen all adults 65 and older before receiving chemotherapy, and asking more questions to check understanding.

At the 6-month follow-up, 16% reported they sought out additional information, and 45% reported implementing the skills and knowledge into their teaching. At the 12-month follow-up, 18% reported they sought out additional information, and 43% reported they made changes to their teaching because of their participation in the training. Examples of how the program influenced teaching included discussing the concepts of fit, vulnerable, and frail older adults with students and colleagues, integrating geriatric principles into formal coursework, incorporating principles of capacity assessment into teaching, and educating colleagues about using the confusion assessment method and the need to assess cognition and functioning.

Twelve percent reported making changes to their research activities by seeking out more information, 11% made implementation changes at the 6-month follow-up, and 10% indicated that they had sought out more information for their research at the 12-month follow-up, and 12% reported they had implemented changes to their research as a result of what they learned in Anonymized for Review. Some examples of how the program influenced participants’ research included incorporating cognitive screening tools in research projects, developing projects focusing on the psychiatric aspects of care in older adults with cancer, and plans to develop a project on decision-making capacity in older adults with cancer. For HCPs who did not make changes, lack of time was the most frequently cited barrier.

Most participants were interested in receiving more training (93% at 6 months and 92% at 12 months), training others (62% at 6 months and 57% at 12 months), and attending a train-the-trainer program (62% at 6 months and 58% at 12 months) (Table 2).

Discussion and implications

Findings from the current study indicate that increases in knowledge and self-efficacy identified immediately following the Geri Onc CC intensive 2-day training15 persisted 1 year later. Attitudes did not change immediately after training nor at the 6- and 12-month follow-ups. Participants sought out this training opportunity, and attitudes toward older adults were already positive, so this finding is likely due to a ceiling effect. Further, HCPs reported continuing to utilize the learned communication skills 6- and 12-month post-training, and the vast majority made changes to their clinical practice, teaching, and research activities as a result of their participation in Geri Onc CC . We hypothesize that these lasting effects may have been due in part to the immersive and interactive nature of the training. Another indicator of the value of the program is that more than 90% of participants shared information learned in the program with colleagues at both their institutions and at other institutions. Although other studies have demonstrated initial efficacy of training in geriatric oncology content, this study demonstrated the sustained impact, something rarely seen in other educational programs.14 There is significant interest in wanting to expand and further disseminate the training. This is very important given the low numbers of HCPs trained in geriatric oncology, despite the growing number of older adults with cancer.28 More than half of the participants indicated that they have made changes to clinical care and teaching following their participation in Geri Onc CC. Despite the great interest that participants have in implementing changes regarding how older adults with cancer are cared for in their home institutions, leadership support is necessary for many of these changes. Therefore, there is potential for even broader implementation of knowledge and skills related to caring for older adults with cancer. Since we have received funding to conduct Geri Onc CC, for another 5 years, we are adding content that directly addresses ways to approach institutional challenges, including leadership buy-in and priorities.

This training responds to the need to equip the healthcare workforce to care for older adults with cancer and their caregivers.29–33 The Geri Onc CC training curriculum included most topics of geriatric syndromes and domains of the geriatric assessment, as well as tailored strategies and communication skills for working with this population. Notably, more than a third of the participants had already been caring for older adults for 11 or more years. We believe that the fact that even very experienced clinicians sought out this training and that we have demonstrated changes in key outcomes reflects the quality and applicability of our program across all levels of experience.

Some limitations of the study should be noted. Although we collected follow-up data up to 12 months after the training, which is a strength, due to the design of the study, we only assessed self-reported measures at these follow-up assessments. Future work should examine the transfer of skills to the clinical setting and the impact of the training on patient and family outcomes. A limitation of this project is the lack of a priori criteria to define program success. It is also important to note that we utilized a pre-post design for this study. A randomized design with a comparison group would further demonstrate its efficacy. Participants sought out this training and were therefore highly motivated to engage in this program. This may limit generalizability to the broader oncology workforce. Further, though completion of follow-up data was not associated with pre-training measures, it is possible that those who were more satisfied or for whom gains were retained longer after training were more likely to be included in the analyses of longitudinal timepoints. Finally, participants were from a variety of settings and disciplines, and specific roles differed, and aspects of the course content may have been more applicable to some participants than others. The didactic content was designed to be relevant to an interdisciplinary audience; therefore, it did not maximize discipline-specific content. We encouraged HCPs to engage in a discussion about how the content was relevant to them and to ask specific questions related to their role or setting. For the communication skills portion, we were able to tailor the scenarios and skills rehearsal to individual learners’ specific roles.

There are very few opportunities for HCPs to obtain specialized training on how to care for older adults with cancer. The Geri Onc CC course provides an evidence-based, interactive training that includes didactic knowledge-based content, case-based discussions, and interactive exercises. It provides an innovative opportunity for all members of the healthcare team to learn in an interactive and supportive environment designed to enhance knowledge and communication skills. Currently, this training is only available through this training grant. Given the initial success, our goal is to examine implementation approaches so that we can offer it more widely to other HCPs.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the Geri Onc CC program continued to have a beneficial impact on HCPs even 12 months after the training, as measured by sustained improvement in HCPs’ self-efficacy, improved knowledge about geriatric oncology, and the use of communication skills tailored to the needs of this population. This training fills an important gap in cancer care for older adults with cancer and their caregivers and has the potential to enhance patient- and family-centered care.
