Implantation of autologous peripheral nerve tissue to the nucleus basalis of Meynert in patients with Parkinson’s disease during deep brain stimulation surgery: a 2-year report of safety, feasibility, and clinical response
Authors: Sheliza Ali, Jorge E. Quintero, Mallory Richert, Lisa Koehl, Amelia J. Anderson-Mooney, Julie A. Gurwell, Katherine S. Manning, Saad Hulou, Thomas Hines, John T. Slevin, Greg A. Gerhardt, Frederick A. Schmitt, Craig G. van Horne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In BriefResearchers assessed whether placing regenerative cells into the nucleus basalis of Meynert during deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery could be performed safely and according to plan. The procedure was safe and workable, and the surgical experience helped improve targeting of this brain region. This study shows that combining cell therapy with DBS is possible, helping advance new experimental treatments that both stimulate and repair brain function.

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity resulting from dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra (SN). In addition to motor features, cognitive impairment is common and significantly affects quality of life.1,2 Neuronal degeneration also occurs in the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), a key cholinergic structure implicated in cognition, and NBM degeneration predicts cognitive decline in PD.3–6 Cognitive deficits in PD are multifactorial, commonly affecting executive function, processing speed, language, visuospatial abilities, and memory; up to 26% of patients without dementia meet criteria for mild cognitive impairment.7,8 Current treatments (pharmacological therapy, nonpharmacological interventions, and deep brain stimulation [DBS]) are symptomatic, and no disease-modifying therapies are available, particularly for cognitive decline.

Early intervention for PD is challenging because motor diagnosis occurs after substantial SN cell loss,9 although residual viable neurons might remain amenable to neuroprotective strategies.10 Emerging approaches aim to address both symptoms and underlying pathology through gene- and cell-based therapies, including growth factors, stem cells, fetal tissue, and autologous peripheral nerve tissue (PNT).11–14 Autologous PNT is proposed to provide neuroprotective and regenerative support. Following injury, PNT expresses neurotrophic and angiogenic factors, including nerve growth factor, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor, and vascular endothelial growth factor.15 Previous studies have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of implanting PNT into the SN during DBS surgery with encouraging preliminary clinical findings.16–19

While SN targeting aims to preserve dopaminergic neurons and mitigate motor progression, the NBM represents a potential target for cognitive intervention. Prior studies have delivered nerve growth factor–expressing grafts to the basal forebrain in Alzheimer’s disease, including direct NBM targeting.20,21 Our group has reported long-term survival and innervation of PNT grafts implanted into either the SN or NBM in patients with PD undergoing DBS.22 The NBM is one of 4 cholinergic nuclei found in the basal forebrain with projections primarily to the cortex.23 In PD, the NBM shows neuronal loss and Lewy body pathology24 that is closely associated with attentional and executive dysfunction.6 Given its role in PD symptoms and its widespread cortical projections, the NBM represents a key and accessible target for interventions aimed at modifying the progression of nonmotor symptoms.

Building on prior SN implantation studies with PNT16–18 and evidence supporting NBM modulation for cognitive benefit,4,25,26 we aimed to evaluate the 2-year safety and feasibility of unilateral PNT implantation to the NBM in participants with PD undergoing globus pallidus internus (GPi) DBS. Secondary outcomes included motor and cognitive measures. We hypothesized that cognitive outcomes following GPi DBS plus NBM PNT implantation would be comparable with those observed after GPi DBS alone, indicating no additional cognitive risk beyond expected post-DBS changes.27

Methods

This single-center open-label study was designed to determine the feasibility and safety of PNT implantation into the NBM of participants undergoing DBS surgery, including long-term clinical safety of the combined treatment. The study procedures were approved by the University of Kentucky Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion in the study. A single surgeon performed all implants.

Participants

Seven participants (5 male; mean age 69 years, range 64–72 years) with PD underwent bilateral DBS electrode implantation to the GPi plus a unilateral (contralateral to the most affected side of their motor symptoms) PNT regenerating sural nerve graft to the NBM (hereafter referred to as DBS-Plus), as described by Quintero et al.17 All participants were right-handed and White, with a mean education duration of 15.7 years (range 12–20 years). Participants were considered for study enrollment based on the following inclusion criteria: undergoing DBS of the STN or GPi, age 40–75 years, able to give informed consent, positive response to carbidopa-levodopa, no significant cognitive deficit per a formal neuropsychological examination, and ability to tolerate the surgical procedure. Study exclusion criteria included any condition that made DBS of the GPi an inappropriate intervention for the participant and previous PD surgery or intracranial surgery.

Surgical Intervention

DBS-Plus was performed in two stages as previously described.17 Briefly, stage I involved implantation of the pulse generator and lead extensions as standard-of-care DBS treatment followed by transection of the sural nerve at the lateral aspect of the ankle and left in situ in preparation for implantation during stage II. Stage II involved implantation of stimulating electrodes into the designated targets as standard-of-care DBS treatment followed by harvesting the distal portion of the transected sural nerve, preparing fascicles and loading into a custom-made guide tube, and implanting the tissue unilaterally into the NBM. Stage II of the procedure occurred a mean of 6 days (range 3–14 days) after stage I, and although PNT implantation occurred during the same surgical session as DBS electrode implantation, it temporally followed the standard DBS procedure. With the use of autologous tissue, participants were not administered immunosuppressants.

Safety and Feasibility

Safety evaluations began following stage I of the surgical procedure. Outpatient postoperative monitoring occurred at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. Adverse events, including any noted in clinical reports/visits and patient record reviews, were recorded and submitted to the Data and Safety Monitoring Board of the University of Kentucky. Adverse events were categorized as mild, moderate, or severe, as well as either related to the study intervention or not.

Analysis of PNT Placement

Preoperative MRI (MPRAGE and susceptibility-weighted imaging) studies were used in conjunction with intraoperative microelectrode recordings (Supplementary Information) to localize the NBM target for implantation. Postoperative MRI and CT were used to determine implant trajectories and placement with the use of anatomical structures overlaid on MR/CT image sets (Brainlab). On postoperative imaging, placements that showed contact with the created NBM object were deemed to be on target.

Clinical Outcomes

Neuropsychological Assessment

All participants completed baseline and follow-up neuropsychological evaluations, which involved a clinical interview with the participant and a reliable informant, cognitive and behavioral test administration and scoring, and interpretation of test scores. Tests were administered and scored by a licensed psychological associate under the supervision of a licensed clinical neuropsychologist (Table 1). Baseline neuropsychological testing was conducted while participants were receiving PD medication, and follow-up assessments were conducted while participants received both PD medication and DBS therapy. The clinical interview and interpretation of test scores were completed by a licensed clinical neuropsychologist.

Outcomes from the baseline neuropsychological assessment were used as part of the presurgical standard of care to determine eligibility for DBS surgery (e.g., assessing for dementia). As part of this first-in-human research protocol, neuropsychological testing was repeated for the first participants at approximately 6 months (mean 5.8 months, SD 0.7 months) after surgery. A third neuropsychological assessment was completed approximately 24 months (mean 24.0 months, SD 1.2 months) after surgery. A neurocognitive diagnosis was provided based on the outcome of each neuropsychological assessment by using criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5).28

Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale

The Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)29 was administered at baseline and 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month postoperative time points following the DBS-Plus procedure. Part III of the UPDRS, the motor examination, was administered on and off antiparkinsonian medication at baseline, and on and off medication and stimulation at follow-up. In the practically defined off state, participants were off antiparkinsonian therapy for at least 12 hours. The UPDRS examination also included the modified Hoehn and Yahr scale and Schwab and England scale.

Non-Motor Symptoms Scale

The Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS)30 is a 30-item self-report questionnaire that assesses severity and frequency of symptoms in 9 domains of nonmotor symptoms in PD (cardiovascular, sleep, mood/cognition, perceptual problems/hallucinations, attention/memory, gastrointestinal, urinary, sexual function, and miscellaneous). The NMSS was administered at baseline and 24 months.

Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire

The 8-item Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-8)31 is a self-report questionnaire that assesses quality of life using a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always) to indicate frequency of problems. This was administered at baseline and 24 months.

Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose

The levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) as an indicator of the dosage of PD-related medications was calculated for each participant using the methodology of the standardized guidelines provided by Tomlinson et al.32

Statistical Analysis

For neuropsychological test scores, the Z-score change was calculated by domain (Table 1). Then, the Z-score change within each domain was compared with a meta-analytically derived effect size of estimated mean decline associated with DBS surgery targeting the GPi in patients with PD.27 For domains with positive effect sizes (i.e., indicating improvement due to DBS), we compared the observed z-score change with 0 because we were interested in determining whether the intervention caused reductions in cognitive functioning, as opposed to less improvement than typically observed among prior research samples.

Clinical outcomes for the UPDRS, NMSS, and PDQ-8 were interpreted qualitatively. The scores of the UPDRS (parts I, II, III, and IV), modified Hoehn and Yahr scale, NMSS, and PDQ-8 are interpreted as a higher score representing more severe PD symptoms. A lower percentage on the Schwab and England scale represents reduced independence. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0) and JMP (version 14.0, SAS Institute), with p < 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Results

Study Feasibility and Safety

All participants (n = 7) received bilateral DBS electrodes targeting the GPi and all underwent the implantation of PNT at the time of their planned stage II DBS surgery (Fig. 1). Although the PNT was not treated with contrast material to make it radiopaque, we used postoperative MRI analysis to interpret that 4 placements were on target, and 3 implants were either superior or lateral to the planned target (Fig. 2). From a study-compliance perspective, all participants completed the 6- and 12-month visits (Table 2), and 5 of 7 completed the 24-month visit because 2 participants died of unrelated causes (complications following hip fracture and pneumonia, respectively). There were no serious adverse events related to the study intervention and the only study-related adverse event was related to sural nerve biopsy, with numbness on the side ipsilateral to the nerve biopsy. This adverse event profile is similar to that of our previously reported findings in participants who received PNT implants to the SN.18,19

Clinical Response

Neurocognitive Diagnosis

At baseline, all participants met DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for mild neurocognitive disorder, representing a mild decline of cognition from estimated premorbid functioning without significant impact on independent completion of activities of daily living. At the 6-month follow-up, 3 participants’ neuropsychological performance and everyday functioning progressed to mild dementia, suggesting progressive cognitive decline that interfered with independence. Of those 3 participants, 2 were diagnosed with PD dementia and 1 with mixed dementia presentation secondary to combined PD and vascular disease. At the 24-month follow-up, the remaining 5 participants included 4 with mild neurocognitive disorder and 1 with mild dementia (i.e., major neurocognitive disorder, mild severity).

Neuropsychological Assessment

Two score changes were calculated by taking the difference between z-scores at 2 time points: baseline versus 6 months and baseline versus 24 months (Fig. 3A). Both score changes were compared with published effect sizes of post-DBS cognitive change27 using 1-sample t-tests. The analysis was notably underpowered due to small sample size, thus effect sizes were interpreted based on magnitude. The effect sizes indicated small to medium effects of DBS-Plus on cognition for most domains.

The most pronounced change based on the 6-month score change was in the visuospatial domain, with a medium to large effect size (d = −0.81) (Table 3); however, this domain was based on different neuropsychological measures than that of the comparison sample whereas all other domains overlapped with the comparison sample.27 Notably, this effect was nonsignificant and did not hold at the 24-month postoperative assessment (d = −0.29) (Table 4). UPDRS, NMSS, PDQ-8, and LEDD Outcomes

UPDRS part I (Mood, Behavior, and Mentation) and part II (Activities of Daily Living) scores remained relatively stable between baseline and the 24-month follow-up. Two participants had a single missing item for the UPDRS part III in the off state, and missing data were imputed using a mean substitution for that item across all participants at the corresponding time point. There was minimal change in the UPDRS part III on-state score (reduced mean score) 6 months after surgery compared with baseline, and a minimal to moderate increase in the mean score at 12 months that remained grossly unchanged until 24 months after surgery. Off-state part III scores were a mean of 36.4 (SD 8.8) at baseline and 39.8 (SD 15.4) at 24 months (Table 5 and Fig. 3B).

UPDRS part IV (Complications of Therapy) scores showed a change from baseline (mean 9.1, SD 3.5) compared with the 24-month follow-up (mean 5.0, SD 3.3); importantly, there were no cases of additional dyskinesia. The modified Hoehn and Yahr staging remained relatively stable, ranging from stage 2 (bilateral disease without impairment of balance) to stage 3 (mild to moderate bilateral disease with some postural instability and physical independence) throughout 24 months. Both the NMSS and PDQ-8 scores remained relatively stable at baseline compared with 24 months postoperatively. The greatest change on the NMSS was in the urinary domain, which assesses urgency, frequency, and nocturia (Table 5). Finally, the mean LEDD values at baseline and 24 months after surgery were 475 mg (SD 231 mg) and 499 mg (SD 499 mg), respectively.

Discussion

This is the first study to assess the safety, feasibility, and clinical responses of PNT implantation to the NBM during GPi DBS surgery in patients with PD. Previous publications have outlined the safety and feasibility of PNT grafts targeting the SN during DBS.16,18,19 We found that unilateral implantation of PNT to the NBM was a safe and feasible procedure. There were no serious adverse events associated with the study intervention, and the 2 participant deaths during the study period were due to unrelated causes. From a feasibility standpoint, all participants were able to receive the PNT implant without interference to their standard-of-care DBS therapy. Additionally, all participants were able to complete the 12-month and 24-month study visits, excluding the 2 participants who died before the 24-month visit.

Although we used intraoperative microelectrode targeting to aid in localizing NBM cholinergic neurons, postoperative imaging analysis showed 4 implants were on target and 3 were off target. Target accuracy was determined using automated software–based object segmentation, a method with recognized technical limitations. An additional limitation relates to the PNT itself, which was not treated with contrast material to make it radiopaque. Consequently, the implant location must be inferred from the delivery cannula tract rather than direct visualization of the implanted tissue, introducing further uncertainty in target verification. Targeting the NBM remains technically challenging because its anatomical boundaries are not readily visualized with conventional imaging. Therefore, accurate localization depends on careful identification of adjacent anatomical landmarks. The NBM is generally situated inferior to the globus pallidus externus and GPi, superior to the anterolateral aspect of the optic tract, and lateral to the anterior commissure. Accordingly, delineation of the inferior borders of the GPi and globus pallidus externus, the superior border of the optic tract, and the lateral border of the anterior commissure is essential to approximate the NBM location. Reliable identification of these structures requires high-resolution imaging. Preoperative high-resolution T2-weighted 3T MRI (along with MPRAGE and FGATIR [fast gray matter acquisition T1-weighted inversion recovery]) enables improved visualization of these landmarks and might enhance targeting accuracy. In earlier participants, high-resolution imaging was not available, which likely limited targeting precision. The current availability of high-resolution MRI represents a meaningful methodological improvement to facilitate more consistent and anatomically accurate NBM targeting. Since this trial, our imaging protocols and anatomical object overlay have advanced, and we would expect improved on-target delivery in future studies.

Neuropsychological outcomes data were collected over the 2-year follow-up. Neurocognitive diagnoses and specific neuropsychological changes observed following the DBS-Plus procedure did not differ from those reported in other studies of cognitive outcomes after standard-of-care DBS, although the neurocognitive batteries and cohorts differed from ours.27 At the 6-month assessment, 3 participants had progressed from mild cognitive changes to mild dementia. Although reports of conversion to dementia in patients receiving DBS to the GPi are limited, cognitive outcomes between DBS to the GPi and STN are comparable, and the incidence of dementia after subthalamic nucleus DBS is reported to be 25% over 3 years after surgery.33 Cognitive changes are not unusual in PD, with approximately 25% of patients with PD without dementia having mild cognitive impairment and estimates increasing up to 50% at 5 years following diagnosis.7,8 There is also greater risk of dementia in PD, with cumulative prevalence ranging 17%–83% and increased prevalence at later stages of the disease.7,8 Thus, in our sample, the addition of PNT to the NBM did not substantially increase the risk for cognitive decline and dementia beyond rates typically observed in late-stage PD. Given the small number of participants and nature of the study intervention (unilateral delivery of PNT), we did not identify a correlation between clinical response and implant target localization. We are currently investigating whether placing PNT near the target is sufficient for cell-survival factors to become available to surrounding neurons and cells.

Our findings also suggest that PNT delivery to the NBM during GPi DBS showed some improvement in the domains of processing speed and attention at the 24-month follow-up, with moderate to large effect sizes compared with typical changes seen in a sample of patients with PD who underwent GPi DBS alone.27 However, these findings were not statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size of our pilot study. The NBM has been discussed as a potential target for DBS in dementia secondary to both PD and Alzheimer’s disease.34 One pilot study found that, whereas DBS to the NBM was determined to be safe and tolerated in patients with PD dementia, improvements were noted on psychiatric measures but not cognitive measures. However, the sample was small, lacked a control group, and was solely comprised of patients whose cognitive state had already progressed to dementia.35 Yet, at least one human case study of DBS to the NBM in Alzheimer’s disease found promising effects,25 and a study in nonhuman primates showed some evidence of cognitive improvement with intermittent stimulation.4,24 These studies, along with our reported outcomes in the current study, support the need for additional research in this area.

We have previously used the off-state UPDRS part III score to observe underlying progression/symptoms of PD, and calculated a mean 9-point decrease in scores (open label) after 2 years in participants who received PNT implantation to the SN.17 In the current study, we targeted the NBM, an area not associated with motor symptoms, and we report a mean 3-point higher part III score (open label) for participants after 2 years, which is in line with the reported motor progression of 3 points per year in persons with PD.36 This result indicates that PNT support might have target-specific clinical effects. Conversely, we found slightly worsening scores for the UPDRS part I, UPDRS part II, UPDRS part III (on state), Schwab and England, and PDQ-8, with slight improvement in the overall NMSS score. The small sample size and small score changes are difficult to interpret but seem to agree with the worsening motor scores and diminishment in activities of daily living. A larger study will be necessary to explore these issues.

The limitations of this study included the small sample size and off-target implants, which can be overcome in future research involving larger samples, as well as recent advancements in imaging protocols and anatomical object overlay to improve on-target implantation. Future studies would also benefit from the addition of control groups that include patients with PD undergoing DBS without grafting and those receiving best medical management without DBS.

Conclusions

The current study outlines the safety, feasibility, and clinical outcomes of implanting autologous PNT grafts into the NBM in patients with PD undergoing GPi DBS surgery. Our findings indicate that the procedure is safe and feasible without increased risk of decline in cognition or motor functioning. We targeted the NBM, an area not associated with motor symptoms, and the mild progression of motor and quality of life ratings suggest that PNT support might have target-specific clinical effects. Overall, these findings are encouraging and support the need for further research in this area.
