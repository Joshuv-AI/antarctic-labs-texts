Local constancy of reduction type and related invariants for curves in p p -adic families
Jakab Schrettner

Abstract
We investigate the behaviour of the reduction type and related invariants of curves in families of curves over a discretely valued field. By a family, we will mean a set of curves obtained by perturbing the coefficients of the defining equations. We will show that the reduction type in these families is locally constant in the topology induced by the valuation. We also derive local constancy results for some related invariants, such as the Tamagawa number, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer ‘fudge factor’ and the Galois representation.



1
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to investigate curves in families over a discretely valued field
K
, mainly with respect to their reduction types and related invariants. By a family we will mean curves obtainable from each other by small perturbations in the coefficients of the equations defining the corresponding curves.

In particular we will prove the following results. In each case we let
K
be a discretely valued field, with valuation ring
R
, which is assumed to be excellent (a mild condition that is satisfied for example if
R
has characteristic zero, or is complete), with uniformiser
\pi\in R
and residue field
k=R/\pi R
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\mathrm{char}\,K\not=2
. Let
C/K
be a hyperelliptic curve given by

C:\,y^{2}=f(x)

where
f\in R[x]
. Suppose
\widetilde{f}\in R[x]
is separable, of the same degree as
f
,
f-\widetilde{f}\in\pi^{N}R[x]
, and we take the hyperelliptic curve
C^{\prime}
given by

C^{\prime}:\,y^{2}=\widetilde{f}(x).

Then for
N
sufficiently large (depending on
f
),
C
and
C^{\prime}
have regular models with the same special fibre.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
\mathrm{char}\,K\not=2
. Let
C/K
be a bihyperelliptic curve, i.e. one given by the affine model

C:\,\begin{cases}y^{2}=f(x)\\
z^{2}=g(x)\end{cases}

for polynomials
f,g\in R[x]
such that

has no repeated roots. Suppose
\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g}
are of the same degree as
f,g
with
\widetilde{f}\widetilde{g}
separable,
f-\widetilde{f},g-\widetilde{g}\in\pi^{N}R[x]
, and we consider the bihyperelliptic curve

C^{\prime}:\,\begin{cases}y^{2}=\widetilde{f}(x)\\
z^{2}=\widetilde{g}(x)\end{cases}.

Then for
N
sufficiently large (depending on

),
C
and
C^{\prime}
have regular models with the same special fibre.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
C/K
be a smooth projective curve embedded in

as a complete intersection, i.e. given by the vanishing of

C:\{F_{1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n})=\ldots=F_{n-1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n})=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}

with

homogeneous. Suppose
\widetilde{F}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{F}_{n-1}
are homogeneous polynomials of the same degree as the

, with
F_{i}-\widetilde{F}_{i}\in\pi^{N}R[X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}]

C^{\prime}:\{\widetilde{F}_{1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n})=\ldots=\widetilde{F}_{n-1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n})=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}

have regular models with the same special fibre.

All of these will be special cases of Theorem
5.3
, which applies to curves presented as complete intersections inside some ambient space. In each case, we perturb the coefficients of the equations defining the curve; if the perturbations are sufficiently small, this yields a curve with the same reduction type. One may regard this as saying that the reduction type is
locally constant
in these families.

As a consequence, any invariant that can be read off of the special fibre of a regular model is also locally constant: this includes invariants such as the Tamagawa number (the number of
k
-points of the component group of the Jacobian), the index (smallest positive degree of a
K
-rational divisor) and the deficiency (whether
C
has a
K
-rational divisor of degree
g-1
). In particular we have

Theorem 1.4

(= Theorem
5.7
)
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be smooth projective curves in a family over
K
as in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
or Theorem
1.3
, defined by equations whose difference is divisible by
\pi^{N}
. Then for sufficiently large

, the following hold:

1.

if

is locally soluble (i.e. has a

is locally soluble.

2.

C
and
C^{\prime}
have the same Néron component group and Tamagawa number.

3.

Suppose

is perfect. Then
C
and
C^{\prime}
have the same index. If
C
and
C^{\prime}
are geometrically connected, then one is deficient if and only if the other is.

We also obtain similar local constancy results for some more related invariants, such as the so-called BSD fudge factor (which is a local factor appearing in the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, see Definition
6.1
), and the Galois representation associated to the curves. In particular, we get the following:

.

Let

and

•

•

(Theorem

) if

Remark 1.6
.

The BSD fudge factor is computed with reference to a basis of differentials on the curve. In the theorem above, we have to take a common basis of differentials on
C
and
C^{\prime}
, by which we mean bases that arise as the restriction of a common set of differentials on the ambient space in which both
C
and
C^{\prime}
are embedded. (In the language of Section
6
, they are both good for
S
, for a set of differentials
S
on the ambient space. See Definition
6.8
). For a more specific statement for hyperelliptic curves, see Corollary
6.16
.

The motivation for these results is twofold. First, it enables us to perform certain ‘global-to-local’ arguments. Consider a smooth projective curve
C
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Suppose we’d like to show something about a local invariant of
C
which is known in cases of ‘sufficiently good reduction’. By a small perturbation of the coefficients of the equations defining
C
, we may obtain a curve
C^{\prime}
defined over the global field
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus we may consider the reduction of
C^{\prime}
at all places
q\not=p
as well. Assuming that
C^{\prime}
has ‘sufficiently good reduction’ away from
p
, we know our desired result holds at these places. One can then argue using a product formula or other local-global result that the result must hold at
p
. Our result guarantees that if the perturbation was sufficiently small,
C
will have the same local invariants as
C^{\prime}
, meaning that the result holds for
C
as well. Such an argument can be found e.g. in
[
DM23
, Section 11]
to approximate curves over local fields with curves defined over global fields, in order to study Jacobians of genus 2 curves.

The second motivation is computational: suppose one would like to compute the reduction type of a curve given by explicit equations in a computer algebra system. Our results imply that for this purpose, it suffices to know the coefficients of the defining equations up to some finite precision, which might make the computation easier. In fact, a result such as ours is necessary to compute reduction types over fields such as
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
by a computer: as the computer can only store the coefficients defining our curve up to finite precision, local constancy ensures that this precision is enough to obtain the correct result.

For elliptic curves in Weierstrass form, local constancy of various invariants follows from Tate’s algorithm
[
Tat75
]
(see also the exposition in
[
Sil94
, Chapter IV, §9]
). It follows from the description of the algorithm that a sufficiently small perturbation in the Weierstrass equation coefficients doesn’t change the special fibre of the minimal regular model, and also other invariants such as the Tamagawa number, valuation of the minimal discriminant, and conductor exponent.

Liu
[
Liu25
]
describes a similar algorithm for resolving the singularities of double covers of regular surfaces, giving explicit equations for the resulting surfaces. In particular, he produces an explicit algorithm, similar to Tate’s algorithm, for finding a desingularization of Weierstrass models of hyperelliptic curves. This implies a local constancy result similar to ours in these cases.

For hyperelliptic curves
C:y^{2}=f(x)
, Dokchitser, Dokchitser, Maistret and Morgan
[
DDMM22
, Theorem 1.29]

show, using the machinery of cluster pictures, that various invariants of
C
are locally constant as functions of the coefficients of
f
, including the special fibre of the minimal regular model when
C
is semistable and the residue characteristic
p
is odd. This was extended to curves with tame potentially semistable reduction by Faraggi and Nowell
[
FN20
]
.

Kisin
[
Kis99
]
proves a result in a similar vein as ours, about the local constancy of Galois representations in
p
-adic families.

Our results generalise these, in that they apply to any smooth projective curve that is presented as a local complete intersection (vanishing locus of
n
equations in
n+1
variables) inside some ambient space. Moreover, we have no restrictions on the residue characteristic, and no restriction on the reduction type of our curve such as semistability. However our results only give information about the special fibre of some regular model. We will not attempt to extend these results to the special fibre of the minimal regular model or the minimal normal crossings model.

1.1
Layout

In Section
2
, we formally define what we mean by families of curves, or equivalently by a small perturbation of the coefficients defining a curve. This is the notion of
N
-closeness. We also define the related notion of formal
N
-closeness, and establish some properties of these.

In Section
3
, we show that models that are
N
-close are also formally close, by constructing appropriate formal automorphisms of the models of these curves. The results in these section follow closely the work of Cutkosy and Srinivasan
[
CS93
]
on the local equivalence of singularities, adapted to work in the setting of working over a discrete valuation ring rather than a field.

In Section
4
, we show that formal closeness of curves persists through blowups, by constructing formal automorphisms on blowups of models. In Section
5
we use this and the results of the preceding section, along with known results on resolutions of singularities, to show our result on the local constancy of reduction type in families.

In Section
6
we give some background on the BSD fudge factor, and prove its local constancy in families, while in Section
7
we prove the local constancy of Galois representations.

1.2
Notation and conventions

We will work in the following general setup: we let
R
be an excellent discrete valuation ring, with field of fractions
K
, residue field
k
and uniformiser
\pi
. In Section
6
, we will also fix an absolute value
|\cdot|:K\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
compatible with the valuation.

The scheme
\mathrm{Spec}\,R
has a closed point
s
, and a generic point
\eta
. If
\mathcal{X}
is an
R
-scheme, the fibre above
s
is the special fibre
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, and the fibre above
\eta
is the generic fibre
\mathcal{X}_{\eta}=\mathcal{X}_{K}
. We will always denote
R
-schemes by curly letters, and their generic fibres by the corresponding regular letter, i.e.
R
-schemes
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{X}
have generic fibres
\mathcal{X}_{K}=X,\mathcal{C}_{K}=C
.

Recall that a
model
of a (smooth, projective) curve

over
K
is an integral, proper, flat
R
-scheme
\mathcal{C}
with generic fibre isomorphic to
C
.

The hat notation
\widehat{(\cdot)}
will always denote
\pi
-adic completion of an
R
-algebra.

All rings and schemes will be assumed to be Noetherian, and all
R
-schemes are assumed to be of finite type.

2
Families of curves

We first formalise our notion of
p
-adic families, or more precisely the notion of two curves related to each other by a small perturbation of the defining equations. This is done by the notion of
N
-closeness, which is defined for given models of these curves:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
be
R
-schemes, both closed subschemes of an
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
of codimension
r
. Also let
N\geq 1
be an integer. We say that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are
N
-close
if there is an affine open cover
\{\mathcal{X}_{i}\}_{i}
of
\mathcal{X}
such that for each
i
,

\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Y}=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}=V(\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{r})

such that
r=\mathrm{codim}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X})
is the codimension of
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{X}
, and
f_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{i}\in\pi^{N}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{X}_{i})
.

Let
C,C^{\prime}
be smooth projective curves over
K
with respective models
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, which are both embedded in a regular
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
. If
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are
N
-close, then by slight abuse of language we will also say that the generic fibres
C
and
C^{\prime}
are
N
-close.

This encompasses the situation in Theorem
1.1
(and also
1.2
,
1.3
).

Example 2.2
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and
C
be the hyperelliptic curve of genus
g
given by

C:\;y^{2}=f(x)

This in fact means that
C
is glued together from two affine pieces

\displaystyle C_{1}:\;

\displaystyle\{y^{2}=f(x)\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}_{x,y}\qquad\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle C_{2}:\;

\displaystyle\{v^{2}=u^{2g+2}f(1/u)\}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}_{u,v}

along the maps
u=\frac{1}{x},v=\frac{y}{x^{g+1}}
. Similarly if
C^{\prime}
is the hyperelliptic curve

C^{\prime}:\;y^{2}=\widetilde{f}(x),

then it is similarly glued together from two affine pieces
C^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}_{x,y}
and
C^{\prime}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}_{u,v}
. Note that both curves are embedded in the surface obtained by gluing the two planes
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{x,y}
and
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{u,v}
together via the maps above.

If, without loss of generality, we take
f,\widetilde{f}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]
, then all the same equations define
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-schemes, i.e.
C
has a model
\mathcal{C}
glued together from two affine pieces

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x))\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x,y]

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}_{p}[u,v]/(v^{2}-u^{2g+2}f(1/u))\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{2}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}_{p}[u,v]

again along the maps
u=\frac{1}{x},v=\frac{y}{x^{g+1}}
. Similarly
C^{\prime}
has a model
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
glued from similar pieces. Both models are contained in the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
obtained by gluing
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
along the above maps.

It’s easy to verify that
C
and
C^{\prime}
are
N
-close if
f-\widetilde{f}\in p^{N}\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]
because e.g.

\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{X}_{1}=V(y^{2}-f(x))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{X}_{1}=V(y^{2}-\widetilde{f}(x))

and
(y^{2}-f(x))-(y^{2}-\widetilde{f}(x))=f(x)-\widetilde{f}(x)\in p^{N}\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x,y]
. A similar relation holds in the other chart.

We turn to establishing some properties of
N
-closeness.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be
R
-schemes.

1.

The condition of
N
-closeness gets stronger with increasing
N
: if
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are
N
-close, then they are also
M
-close for any
M\leq N
.

2.

If
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are 1-close, then they have the same special fibre (as subschemes of
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, i.e. they are the same
k
-scheme).

3.

Suppose
L/K
is an extension of discretely valued fields, with valuation rings
R=\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{L}
. If
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are
N
-close, then

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}

are also
N
-close. Hence
N
-closenes is stable under base change.

Proof.

(1) and (2) are clear from the definitions. For (3), if
\mathrm{Spec}\,A=U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is an affine open, then
U_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}=\mathrm{Spec}\,(A\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}\mathcal{O}_{L})
is an affine open on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}
. By
N
-closeness we have that

\displaystyle U\cap\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})

\displaystyle U\cap\mathcal{C^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=V(\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{r})

with
f_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{i}\in\pi^{N}A
. Clearly it’s also true that

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}

\displaystyle=V(f_{1}\otimes 1,\ldots,f_{r}\otimes 1)

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}

\displaystyle=V(\widetilde{f}_{1}\otimes 1,\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{r}\otimes 1)

and we have that
f_{i}\otimes 1-\widetilde{f}_{i}\otimes 1\in\pi^{N}(A\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}\mathcal{O}_{L})
.
∎

Next, we define the related notion of formal
N
-closeness. This means that locally on the ambient space
\mathcal{X}
, there are formal automorphisms taking
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, which are congruent to the identity modulo

. By a formal automorphism, we mean an automorphism of the

-adic completions.

In what follows,
\widehat{(\cdot)}
denotes

-adic completion.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
be
R
-schemes, both closed subschemes of a regular integral
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is covered by affine open subschemes
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}
such that
\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}
and
\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I^{\prime}_{i}
. Let
N\geq 1
be an integer. We say that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are
formally
N
-close
if there are

1.

integral
R
-algebras
C_{i}
with surjections
p_{i}:C_{i}\twoheadrightarrow A_{i}
,

2.

automorphisms
\sigma_{i}:\widehat{C_{i}}\to\widehat{C_{i}}

satisfying the following properties:

•

for each
i
, if
p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i})=J_{i}
and
p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i}^{\prime})=J_{i}^{\prime}
then we have

\sigma_{i}(\widehat{J_{i}})=\widehat{J^{\prime}_{i}}

•

for each

,

\sigma_{i}\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N}.

We can also establish some properties of formal
N
-closeness:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be
R
-schemes.

1.

The condition of formal
N
-closeness also gets stronger with increasing
N
: if
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are formally
N
-close, then they are also formally
M
-close for any
M\leq N
.

2.

Using the notation of the above definition, we must have
I_{i}+\pi^{N}A_{i}=I_{i}^{\prime}+\pi^{N}A_{i}
. In particular if
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are formally 1-close, then they have the same special fibre (again as closed subschemes of

).

Proof.

(1) is clear from the definition. For (2), the claim is equivalent to
J_{i}+\pi^{N}C_{i}=J_{i}^{\prime}+\pi^{N}C_{i}
, and this is true because both ideals are

∎

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that both
N
-closeness and formal
N
-closeness for
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
implies that
\mathcal{X}
has an affine open cover
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}
such that

\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}^{\prime}

for ideals
I_{i},I_{i}^{\prime}
with
I_{i}+(\pi^{N})=I_{i}^{\prime}+(\pi^{N})
.

We have the following property of closeness and formal
N
-closeness as they relate to regularity:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be 2-close or formally 2-close. Then if
x\in\mathcal{Y}_{s}
is a point (hence also
x\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{s}
), and
\mathcal{Y}
is regular at
x
, then
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is also regular at
x
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}
be the maximal ideals of the local rings
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},x},\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},x},\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},x}
respectively. Because
x
is on the special fibre,
\pi\in\mathfrak{n}
. Both 2-closeness and formal 2-closeness implies that there are ideals
I,I^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{n}
such that
I+(\pi^{2})=I^{\prime}+(\pi^{2})
and

\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},x}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},x}/I\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},x}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},x}/I^{\prime}

and moreover we have that

\dim\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},x}=\dim\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},x}=\dim\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},x}-r

for some integer
r\geq 1
. Hence, since
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},x}
is regular, it suffices to prove that

\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}}/(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}})^{2}\cong\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}/(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}})^{2}

as
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},x}
-modules (since then they have the same dimension).

However we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}}}{(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\cong\frac{\mathfrak{n}+I}{\mathfrak{n}^{2}+I}=\frac{\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{n}^{2}+I}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}}{(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\cong\frac{\mathfrak{n}+I^{\prime}}{\mathfrak{n}^{2}+I^{\prime}}=\frac{\mathfrak{n}}{\mathfrak{n}^{2}+I^{\prime}}

and
I+(\pi^{2})=I^{\prime}+(\pi^{2})
implies that the denominators are equal, proving the claim.
∎

3
Formal automorphisms of models

In this section, let
\mathcal{Y}
be an
R
-scheme with smooth generic fibre (such as a model of a smooth projective curve), embedded in some regular

-scheme
\mathcal{X}
. We would like to show that if an
R
-scheme
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is

-close to

, then it’s also formally close. A precise statement is as follows:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be an
R
-scheme with smooth generic fibre, embedded in some regular
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be an
R
-scheme that is
N

. Then there is a positive integer

, then
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are formally

-close.

Remark 3.2
.

As we will see from the proof, the integer

\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X}
and also the cover realising the
N
-closeness of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is regular,
\mathcal{X}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R
is a local complete intersection morphism
[
Liu02
, Example 6.3.18]
. Hence (by shrinking the
\mathcal{X}_{i}
if necessary), we can assume that each
A_{i}
is a quotient of a polynomial ring over
R
, i.e. there are surjections

p_{i}:C_{i}=R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}}]\twoheadrightarrow A_{i},

such that
K_{i}=\ker p_{i}
is generated by a regular sequence. We will show the above theorem by constructing automorphisms of the
\widehat{C_{i}}
.

Take an
R
-scheme
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
that is
N
-close to
\mathcal{Y}
. Then by assumption we have that

\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}^{\prime}

where
I_{i}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
and
I_{i}^{\prime}=(\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{r})
such that
f_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{i}\in\pi^{N}A_{i}
. In other words, both
I_{i}
and
I_{i}^{\prime}
are generated by regular sequences.

Now since the kernel of
p_{i}:C_{i}\twoheadrightarrow A_{i}
is also generated by a regular sequence, so are the preimages
J_{i}=p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i})
and
J_{i}^{\prime}=p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i}^{\prime})
. Hence we are reduced to proving the following claim, a version of
[
CS93
, Theorem 5.1]
:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
C=R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over
R
, and let
J,J^{\prime}\subseteq C
be ideals of height
m
, such that

J=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})\qquad\text{and}\qquad J^{\prime}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})

with
f_{i}-g_{i}\in\pi^{N}C
. Suppose the
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{Spec}\,C/J
has smooth generic fibre. Then there is an integer
d\geq 1
such that if
N\geq d
, then there is an automorphism
\sigma
of
\widehat{C}
such that
\sigma(\widehat{J})=\widehat{J^{\prime}}
and
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N-d}
.

Proof.

The proof that follows is very similar to the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
CS93
]
, but adapted to the case of working over a discrete valuation ring
R
rather than a field
k
. We give the full proof for completeness. We take
N\gg 0
, specifying further throughout the proof.

Let
W=\left(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)
be the
m\times n
Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives of the
f_{i}
. We let
\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{p}\}
be exactly the
m
-element subsets of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, so that
p=\binom{n}{m}
. Also set
W(\tau_{\alpha})
to be the
m\times m
submatrix of
W
consisting of the rows of
W
indexed by the elements of
\tau_{\alpha}
. We let
m_{\alpha}=\det W(\tau_{\alpha})
, and
L=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{p})
be the matrix generated by the
m\times m
minors of the Jacobian.

Since
\mathcal{X}
has smooth generic fibre, the ideal
L+(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
contains some power
\pi^{f}
of
\pi
. So we can write

\pi^{f}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}f_{j}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}d_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}

For some
c_{j},d_{\alpha}\in C
. Then
g_{i}-f_{i}\in\pi^{N}C
means that we can write for each
i
,

\displaystyle g_{i}-f_{i}

\displaystyle=\pi^{N-2f}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}f_{j}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}d_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{ij}f_{i}\mod{\pi^{N-2f}L^{2}}

where
c_{ij}\in\pi^{N-2f}C
, and we take
N\geq 2f
. We therefore have

G\equiv(I+C)F\mod\pi^{N-2f}L^{2}

where
G
is the column vector
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
, similarly for
F
,
C
is the
m\times m
matrix
(c_{ij})
, and
I
is the
m\times m
identity matrix. Now since
c_{ij}\in\pi^{N-2f}C
, the matrix
I+C
is invertible over
\widehat{C}
, taking
N\geq 2f+1
. So we may replace
G
by

G^{\prime}=(I+C)^{-1}G,

since the entries
g_{i}^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
generate the same ideal of
\widehat{C}
as the
g_{i}
. Moreover the above relation gives us that
g_{i}^{\prime}\equiv f_{i}\mod{\pi^{N-2f}L^{2}}
.

Hence we may WLOG assume that
f_{i}\equiv g_{i}\mod{\pi^{N-2f}L^{2}}
. This means that we can write

g_{i}=f_{i}+\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{p}d^{i}_{\alpha\beta}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}

for some
d^{i}_{\alpha\beta}\in\pi^{N-2f}C
. If we let
D_{\alpha\beta}
be the
m\times 1
matrix

D_{\alpha\beta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}d^{1}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\vdots\\
d^{m}_{\alpha\beta}\end{array}\right]

then the previous equation becomes

G=F+\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{p}D_{\alpha\beta}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}.

Now construct some matrices as follows: let
B_{\tau_{\alpha}}
be the
n\times m
matrix whose rows corresponding to elements of
\tau_{\alpha}
are the rows of
\mathrm{adj}(W(\tau_{\alpha}))
in the same order, and the remaining rows are zero. This is done so that the resulting
n\times mp
matrix

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}B_{\tau_{1}}&\ldots&B_{\tau_{p}}\end{array}\right]

Satisfies

WA=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}m_{1}I&\ldots&m_{p}I\\
\end{array}\right]

where
I
is the
m\times m
identity matrix. Now consider the
mp\times 1
column matrix

T=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}T_{1}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr T_{p}\end{array}\right]

where
T_{\alpha}
is the
m\times 1
column matrix

(T_{\alpha})_{i}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{p}a^{i}_{\alpha\beta}m_{\beta}

for some indeterminates
a^{i}_{\alpha\beta}\in\widehat{C}
. Also set

AT=H=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}h_{1}(a^{i}_{\alpha\beta})\\
\vdots\\
h_{n}(a^{i}_{\alpha\beta})\end{array}\right]

Using this notation, we have that

WH=WAT=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}m_{1}I&\ldots&m_{p}I\\
\end{array}\right]T=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{p}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a^{1}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\vdots\\
a^{m}_{\alpha\beta}\end{array}\right]m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}.

Now we try to find the automorphism
\widehat{C}\to\widehat{C}
taking
f_{i}
to
g_{i}
. The automorphism will be of the form
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}+u_{i}
for some
u_{i}\in\widehat{C}
. So we would like to have
f_{i}(x+u)=g_{i}
. We have the Taylor expansion

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(X+U)\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(X+U)\end{array}\right]=F+W\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}\end{array}\right]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{1}_{ij}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda^{m}_{ij}\\
\end{array}\right]u_{i}u_{j}

for some
\lambda_{ij}^{k}\in\widehat{C}
. Hence showing the existence of such a
U
amounts to solving the system of equations

G=F+W\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}\end{array}\right]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{1}_{ij}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda^{m}_{ij}\end{array}\right]u_{i}u_{j}

for the
u_{i}
. We will set
u_{i}=h_{i}=h_{i}(a^{k}_{\alpha\beta})
, and try to solve the resulting system for the variables
a^{k}_{\alpha\beta}
. Thus we have

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}\end{array}\right]=H=AT

and hence the equation we need to solve is

G=F+WAT+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{1}_{ij}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda^{m}_{ij}\end{array}\right]h_{i}h_{j}.

Now we can express
WAT
by
(\dagger\dagger)
, and we have that each
h_{i}
is a linear combination of terms
a^{k}_{\alpha\beta}m_{\gamma}
, so this equation is equivalent to

G=F+\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{p}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a^{1}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\vdots\\
a^{m}_{\alpha\beta}\end{array}\right]m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}\sum_{\alpha,\ldots,\zeta=1}^{p}M^{kl}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta\epsilon\zeta}a^{k}_{\alpha\beta}a^{l}_{\gamma\delta}m_{\epsilon}m_{\zeta}

where the
M^{kl}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta\epsilon\zeta}
are
m\times 1
column vectors with entries in
\widehat{C}
.

By
(\dagger)
, it suffices to solve the system

D_{\alpha\beta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a^{1}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\vdots\\
a^{m}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\end{array}\right]+\sum_{\gamma,\delta,\epsilon,\zeta=1}^{p}M^{kl}_{\gamma\delta\epsilon\zeta\alpha\beta}a^{k}_{\gamma\delta}a^{l}_{\epsilon\zeta}

for the variables
a^{k}_{\alpha\beta}
. This is equivalent to the system

d^{i}_{\alpha\beta}=a^{i}_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}\sum_{\alpha,\ldots,\zeta=1}^{p}(M^{kl}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta\epsilon\zeta})_{i}a^{k}_{\alpha\beta}a^{l}_{\gamma\delta}m_{\epsilon}m_{\zeta}

This system of equations has a solution by Hensel’s lemma (Theorem
A.1
): indeed,
a^{i}_{\alpha\beta}=0
is an approximate solution because
d^{i}_{\alpha\beta}\in\pi^{N-2f}C
, and the matrix of partial derivaties of the above polynomials evaluated at zero is the identity matrix. So we have a solution with
a^{i}_{\alpha\beta}\in\pi^{N-2f}\widehat{C}
.

We thus have a map
\sigma:\widehat{C}\to\widehat{C}
such that
\sigma(f_{i})=g_{i}
. It is moreover given by
\sigma(x_{i})=x_{i}+u_{i}
, where
u_{i}\equiv 0\mod\pi^{N-2f}
, which implies that
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N-2f}
. If
N\geq 2f+2
, then we get
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{2}
, which implies that it’s an automorphism by
[
CS93
, Lemma 5.0]
. This proves our claim, with
d=2f+2
.

∎

Using this, the proof of Theorem
3.1
is easy.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The
N
-closeness gives us a cover by affine open sets
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}
such that

\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A_{i}/I_{i}^{\prime}

where
I_{i}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
and
I_{i}^{\prime}=(\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{r})
are generated by regular sequences, and
f_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{i}\in\pi^{N}A_{i}
.

By refining this cover we can assume that for each
i
, we have a surjection
p_{i}:C_{i}\twoheadrightarrow A_{i}
with
C_{i}
a polynomial ring over
R
, and the kernel of this projection is generated by a regular sequence (because
\mathcal{X}
is regular, so
\mathcal{X}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R
is a local complete intersection). Moreover by quasi-compactness of
\mathcal{X}
we may take this cover to be finite. Put again
J_{i}=p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i})
and
J_{i}^{\prime}=p_{i}^{-1}(I_{i}^{\prime})
.

By Proposition
3.3
, for each
i
there is an integer
d_{i}
such that if
N\geq d_{i}
, then there exists an automorphism
\sigma_{i}
of
\widehat{C_{i}}
such that
\sigma_{i}(\widehat{J_{i}})=\widehat{J_{i}^{\prime}}
and
\sigma_{i}\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N-d_{i}}
. Hence if we take
d=\max_{i}d_{i}
, then for
N\geq d
it follows that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are formally
(N-d)
-close.
∎

4
Automorphisms of blowups

One can obtain regular models of a curve by starting from an arbitrary model, and resolving its singularities through blowups (see Theorem
5.1
). Our aim in this section will be to show that formal closeness of models is preserved under blowups in some sense, so that this process gives us formally close regular models of curves. First we review the relevant properties of blowups.

4.1
Blowups

Let
X
be a scheme, and
D\subseteq X
be a closed subscheme. The blowup of
X
along
D
is a scheme
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X
along with a morphism
p:\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X\to X
. We call
D
the centre of the blowup. We will not describe the construction of the blowup in detail, but just note the properties relevant for us, following §8.1 of
[
Liu02
]
.

The blowup
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X
can be computed locally, i.e if
U\subseteq X
is an open set, and
p:\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X\to X
is the blowup, then

\mathrm{Bl}_{D\cap U}U\cong p^{-1}(U).

Thus it suffices to compute blowups of affine schemes. So suppose
X=\mathrm{Spec}\,A
, and
D=\mathrm{Spec}\,A/I
for an ideal
I\subseteq A
. In this case we will also use the notation
\mathrm{Bl}_{\mathrm{Spec}\,A/I}\mathrm{Spec}\,A=\mathrm{Bl}_{I}A
. Pick generators
I=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{r})
for
I
. Then the blowup
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X
is the union of affine subschemes
\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
with

B_{i}=\frac{A[T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}]/(h_{i}T_{1}-h_{1},\ldots,h_{i}T_{r}-h_{r})}{\text{$h_{i}^{\infty}$-torsion}}.

(By
h_{i}^{\infty}
-torsion we mean any
x
such that
h_{i}^{e}x=0
for some
e\geq 0
). If
A
is an integral domain and
h_{i}\not=0
, we can also express this as

B_{i}=A\left[\frac{h_{1}}{h_{i}},\ldots,\frac{h_{r}}{h_{i}}\right]\subseteq\Frac(A).

Now suppose that
Y\subseteq X
is a closed subscheme, containing the centre of the blowup
D
. Then
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}Y
is naturally a closed subscheme of
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X
. In the affine case
X=\mathrm{Spec}\,A
integral,
Y=\mathrm{Spec}\,A/J
and
D=\mathrm{Spec}\,A/I
with
J\subseteq I
, we have that

\mathrm{Bl}_{D}Y\cap\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}=\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}/J_{i}

where
J_{i}
is the
strict transform
of
J
in
B_{i}
, defined as

J_{i}=\{x\in B_{i}\mid\exists e\geq 0,\;h_{i}^{e}x\in JB_{i}\}.

Lastly, blowing up a regular scheme at a regular centre produces a regular scheme, i.e. if
X
is regular, and
D\not=X
is a regular closed subscheme, then
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}X
is regular.

4.2
Constructing automorphisms of blowups

In this subsection, we aim to show that formal
N
-closeness is preserved under blowups, in the following sense:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be an
R
-scheme embedded inside a regular integral
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
, and let
D\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}
be a regular closed irreducible subscheme. Then if
N\geq 3
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is formally
N
-close to
\mathcal{Y}
, then the blowups
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are formally
(N-1)
-close in
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

We essentially follow the proof of
[
CS93
, Theorem 5.1]
, again adapted to our setting of working over a discrete valuation ring.

By formal
N
-closeness we have
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{s}
, so
D\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{s}
as well, and the blowups make sense. We also have that
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{X}
is regular, since
D
and
\mathcal{X}
are regular.

By properties of blowups, we may work locally, so we can assume that
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A
, with
\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A/I
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Spec}\,A/I^{\prime}
, and that there is a surjection
p:C\twoheadrightarrow A
with
p^{-1}(I)=J
,
p^{-1}(I^{\prime})=J^{\prime}
, and an automorphism
\sigma:\widehat{C}\to\widehat{C}
such that
\sigma(\widehat{J})=\widehat{J^{\prime}}
and
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N}
.

Then the closed set
D
corresponds to a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq A
with
\pi\in\mathfrak{p}
. We let
\mathfrak{p}=(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r})
with
h_{i}\not=0
. The blowup
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{X}
is covered by affine patches
\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}
, where

B_{i}=A\left[\frac{h_{0}}{h_{i}},\ldots,\frac{h_{r}}{h_{i}}\right].

We have
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y},\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{X}
, and moreover

\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}=V(I_{i})

where
I_{i}
is the strict transform of
I
in
B_{i}
.
Similarly
\mathrm{Bl}_{D}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cap\mathrm{Spec}\,B_{i}
is given by the vanishing of the strict transform
I_{i}^{\prime}
.

It suffices to prove that for each
i
, there exists a surjection
q_{i}:C_{i}\twoheadrightarrow B_{i}
with
q_{i}^{-1}(I_{i})=J_{i},q_{i}^{-1}(I_{i}^{\prime})=J_{i}^{\prime}
, and an automorphism
\sigma_{i}:\widehat{C_{i}}\to\widehat{C_{i}}
with
\sigma_{i}(\widehat{J_{i}})=\widehat{J^{\prime}_{i}}
. We prove this for
i=0
, for simplicity we let
h_{0}=h
and
B_{0}=B
, so
\mathfrak{p}=(h,h_{1},\ldots,h_{r})
and

B=A\left[\frac{h_{1}}{h},\ldots,\frac{h_{r}}{h}\right].

The strict transforms of
I
and
I^{\prime}
inside
B
are denoted by
I_{0},I_{0}^{\prime}
respectively.

Pick
h^{\prime},h_{i}^{\prime}\in C
such that
p(h^{\prime})=h
and
p(h_{i}^{\prime})=h_{i}
, and moreover let

\ker p=(h_{r+1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{t}^{\prime})

for some
t\geq r+1
. Then we have

p^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})=(h^{\prime},h_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{t}^{\prime})

Now we set

C_{0}=C\left[\frac{h_{1}^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}},\ldots,\frac{h_{t}^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\right]

Which is the coordinate ring of one of the charts on the blowup
\mathrm{Bl}_{p^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})}C
. The strict transform of
\ker p\subseteq C
in
C_{0}
is the ideal

L=\{x\in C_{0}\,\mid\,\exists e\geq 0,h^{\prime e}x\in\ker p\}=\left(\frac{h_{r+1}^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}},\ldots,\frac{h_{t}^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\right)

and
C_{0}/L\cong B
. Let
q:C_{0}\twoheadrightarrow B
be the corresponding surjection.

Denote by
J_{0},J_{0}^{\prime}
the strict transforms of
J,J^{\prime}
inside
C_{0}
. One easily sees that
J_{0}=q^{-1}(I_{0})
and
J_{0}^{\prime}=q^{-1}(I_{0}^{\prime})
as well (they both cut out the blowup of
A/I=C/J
along the ideal
\mathfrak{p}
). It remains to construct an automorphism
\sigma_{0}:\widehat{C_{0}}\to\widehat{C_{0}}
such that
\sigma(\widehat{J_{0}})=\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
and
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N-1}
.

The automorphism
\sigma:\widehat{C}\to\widehat{C}
satisfies
\sigma\equiv\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N}
, so there is some
x\in\widehat{C}
such that

\sigma(h^{\prime})=h^{\prime}+\pi^{N}x

but since
\pi\in\mathfrak{p}
, in
\widehat{C_{0}}
we can write
\pi=h^{\prime}\tau
for some
\tau\in\widehat{C_{0}}
. Then we have

\sigma(h^{\prime})=h^{\prime}(1+y)

for some
y\in\pi^{N-1}\widehat{C_{0}}
. By a similar argument we can write

\sigma(h_{i}^{\prime})=h_{i}^{\prime}+h^{\prime}y_{i}

for some
y_{i}\in\pi^{N-1}\widehat{C_{0}}
.

Now define
\sigma_{0}:C_{0}\to\widehat{C_{0}}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}|_{C}

\displaystyle=\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\left(\frac{h_{i}^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{i}^{\prime}/h^{\prime}+y_{i}}{1+y}

One easily checks that this is well-defined:

•

since
y\in\pi^{N-1}\widehat{C_{0}}
,
1+y
is a unit (as
N\geq 2
)

•

we have
\sigma_{0}(h^{\prime})\sigma_{0}(h_{i}^{\prime}/h^{\prime})=\sigma_{0}(h_{i}^{\prime})
by
(\star)
.

•

\widehat{C_{0}}
has no
\sigma_{0}(h^{\prime})=h^{\prime}(1+y)
-torsion, since it has no
h^{\prime}
-torsion.

Moreover, since
\sigma_{0}(\pi)=\pi
, it extends to a map
\sigma_{0}:\widehat{C_{0}}\to\widehat{C_{0}}
. It is easily seen from the definition that
\sigma_{0}\cong\mathrm{id}\mod\pi^{N-1}
. Hence if
N\geq 3
, then this map is also an automorphism, by
[
CS93
, Lemma 5.0]
.

It remains to check that
\sigma_{0}(\widehat{J_{0}})=\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
. Pick
x\in J_{0}
, then there is some
e\geq 0
such that

h^{\prime e}x\in J

Then we know that
\sigma(h^{\prime e}x)\in\widehat{J^{\prime}}
, which is the
\pi
-adic completion of
J^{\prime}
. So for all
n\geq 0
, there exists some
z\in J^{\prime}
such that

\sigma(h^{\prime e}x)-z\in\pi^{n}\widehat{C}

We can also write, passing to
\widehat{C_{0}}
,

\sigma(h^{\prime e}x)=\sigma_{0}(h^{\prime e}x)=\sigma_{0}(h^{\prime})^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)=h^{\prime e}(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)

So we have that

h^{\prime e}(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)-z\in\pi^{N}\widehat{C_{0}}

Meaning that there is an
a\in\widehat{C}
such that

h^{\prime e}(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)+\pi^{N}a=z

As before, we can write
\pi=h^{\prime}\tau
in
\widehat{C_{0}}
, so that if
n\geq e
we can write

h^{\prime e}((1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)+\pi^{n-e}\tau^{e}a)=z\in J^{\prime}

Which means by the definition of strict transform that

(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)+\pi^{N-e}\tau^{e}a\in J_{0}^{\prime}

Since such a relation holds for all
n\geq e
, we can conclude that
(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)
is contained in the
\pi
-adic closure of
J_{0}^{\prime}
, i.e.

(1+y)^{e}\sigma_{0}(x)\in\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}

and since
1+y
is a unit, we get
\sigma_{0}(x)\in\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
too.

Hence we have shown that
\sigma_{0}(J_{0})\subseteq\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
. By
\pi
-adic continuity, we also get
\sigma_{0}(\widehat{J_{0}})\subseteq\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
. A similar argument with
\sigma_{0}^{-1}
in place of
\sigma_{0}
shows the opposite inclusion, so we get that
\sigma_{0}(\widehat{J_{0}})=\widehat{J_{0}^{\prime}}
as claimed. ∎

5
Reduction types of curves in families

In this section, we apply our results from previous sections to models of curves.

5.1
Local constancy of reduction type

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over
K
, with a model
\mathcal{C}
, and suppose
\mathcal{C}
is embedded in a regular integral
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
as a local complete intersection, i.e. it’s locally given by
r=\mathrm{codim}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{X})
equations. One can find a regular model of
C
by a sequence of blowups as follows:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a model of a smooth projective curve
C/K
. Assume
R
is excellent. Then there is a sequence of morphisms

\mathcal{C}_{reg}=\mathcal{C}_{n}\to\ldots\to\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{C}

such that
\mathcal{C}_{reg}
is regular, and for each
i
, the map
\mathcal{C}_{i+1}\to\mathcal{C}_{i}
is the blowup of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
in some irreducible regular centre
D_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
, such that
D_{i}
is contained in the special fibre of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
CJS13
, Theorem 0.1]
.
∎

So take such a sequence of blowups in centres
D_{i}
. Also inductively put
\mathcal{X}_{i+1}=\mathrm{Bl}_{D_{i}}\mathcal{X}_{i}
so that
\mathcal{C}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{i}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

There is an integer
a\geq 1
such that if
N\geq a
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a model a smooth projective curve
C^{\prime}
that is
N
-close to
\mathcal{C}
, then one can perform a sequence of blowups

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}\to\ldots\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

in centres
D_{i}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
is regular and formally
(N-a)
-close to
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, there is a
d\geq 1
such that if
N\geq d
, then
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are formally
(N-d)
-close. We let
a=d+n+2
, and claim this works.

If
N\geq a
, then by Theorem
3.1
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are formally
(N-d)
-close. Thus they have the same special fibre, and
D_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{s}
, so it makes sense to blow up
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
in this centre. Now applying Theorem
4.1
, we get that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}
are formally
(N-d-1)
-close. Iterating this argument, we get that for each
i
,
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{i}
are formally
(N-d-i)
-close.

Hence
\mathcal{C}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
are formally
(N-d-n)
-close. Now since
N-d-n\geq 2
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is regular, we have by Proposition
2.7
that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
is regular at all points on the special fibre. Since its generic fibre is regular (by assumption it’s a model of a smooth curve over
K
), we obtain that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
is regular. We also know that it’s formally
(N-a)
-close to
\mathcal{C}_{n}
as claimed.
∎

We can apply this to get our main result:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
R
be an excellent discrete valuation ring, and
K
its field of fractions. Let
C/K
be a smooth projective curve, and
\mathcal{C}
a given model of it, presented as a local complete intersection inside a regular integral
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
. Then there is an integer
a\geq 1
such that if
N\geq a
and
C^{\prime}
is a smooth projective curve
N
-close to
C
, then
C
and
C^{\prime}
have regular models with the same special fibre.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2
,
C
and
C^{\prime}
have regular models which are formally 1-close for
N
sufficiently large. These models have the same special fibre if
N\geq 1
, by Proposition
2.5
(2).
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Note that as in Theorem
3.1
, the value of
a
in this theorem depends not only on the curve
C
, but also on the open cover realising the
N
-closeness to
C^{\prime}
.

5.2
Tamagawa number, index, deficiency

We now show the local constancy of various invariants that can be read off of the special fibre. The first invariant is the Tamagawa number, which is one of the local invariants appearing in the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over
K
, and let
J
be its Jacobian. The Néron model
\mathcal{J}
of
J
decomposes as

0\to\mathcal{J}^{0}\to\mathcal{J}\to\varphi_{\mathcal{J}}\to 0

where
\mathcal{J}^{0}
is the identity component, and
\varphi_{\mathcal{J}}
is the component group. The
Tamagawa number
of
C
is
c(C)=|\varphi_{\mathcal{J}}(k)|
, the number of
k
-points of the component group.

We also look at the index and the deficiency. When our curve arises via base change from a number field, the deficiency controls the size of the 2-primary part of its Tate-Shafarevich group modulo squares, see
[
PS99
, Corollary 12]
. See also the exposition in
[
DGKM24
, §5.1]
.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over
K
. Its
index

I(C)
, is the smallest positive degree of a
K
-rational divisor on
C
. If
C
is geometrically connected, we say that
C
is
deficient
if it has no
K
-rational divisor of degree
g-1
, i.e. if
I(C)
does not divide
g-1
.

We will say that a curve
C
over
K
is
locally soluble
if it has a
K
-rational point.

Our result on the local constancy of reduction type yields the following:

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be smooth projective curves which are
N
-close. Then, for sufficiently large
N
, the following hold:

1.

if
R
is Henselian,
C
is locally soluble if and only if
C^{\prime}
is locally soluble.

2.

C
and
C^{\prime}
have the same Néron component group, and hence the same Tamagawa number.

3.

If
k
is perfect, then
C
and
C^{\prime}
have the same index. If both
C
and
C^{\prime}
are geometrically connected, then
C
is deficient if and only if
C^{\prime}
is deficient.

Proof.

In all cases, it suffices to take
N
sufficiently large so that
C
and
C^{\prime}
have regular models with the same special fibre, which is possible by Theorem
5.3
.

For local solubility, note that if
C
has a regular model
\mathcal{C}
, then
C(K)=\mathcal{C}(R)
by properness of
\mathcal{C}
. Because
\mathcal{C}
is regular, each section lands in the smooth locus
\mathcal{C}^{0}
of the map
\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R
, see
[
Sil94
, Corollary 4.4(b)]
, so
\mathcal{C}(R)=\mathcal{C}^{0}(R)
. Now since
R
is Henselian, the reduction map

\mathcal{C}(R)=\mathcal{C}^{0}(R)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}(k)

is surjective,
[
Liu02
, Corollary 6.2.13]
. Hence
C(K)
is non-empty if and only if
\mathcal{C}_{s}(k)
is non-empty, i.e. local solubility can be read off of the special fibre of a regular model.

For the Néron component group and Tamagawa number, this can also be read off of the special fibre of a regular model, see
[
BL99
, Theorem 1.1]
.

The same is true of the index: let
\mathcal{C}
is a regular model of
C
, and
\mathcal{C}_{s}
has components
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n}
, with multiplicities
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
. Also set
e_{i}=[\overline{k}\cap K(\Gamma_{i}):k]
, and let
I^{\prime}=\mathrm{gcd}_{i}\{d_{i}e_{i}\}
. Then
[
BL99
, Corollary 1.3]
implies that
I^{\prime}\mid I(C)
, and
[
CS96
, Théorème 3.1]
implies that
I(C)\mid I^{\prime}
. (Note that in
[
BL99
]
the authors use the quantity
r_{i}=[k^{s}\cap K(\Gamma_{i}):k]
where
k^{s}
is a separable closure, in place of our
e_{i}
. The
k
perfect assumption is necessary to ensure that these are equal.)
Hence
I(C)=I^{\prime}
depends only on
\mathcal{C}_{s}
. Since deficiency depends on whether the index divides
g-1
where
g
is the genus, we also get local constancy of the deficiency.

∎

6
The BSD fudge factor

The BSD fudge factor is a local factor appearing in the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. Let
X
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
J=\mathrm{Jac}(X)
is its Jacobian. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture asserts that the
L
-function
L(J,s)
has an analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
, its order of vanishing at
s=1
is the Mordell-Weil rank of
J
, and its leading coefficient is given by

\frac{|\Sha_{J}|\cdot\textrm{Reg}_{J}}{|J(\mathbb{Q})|\cdot|J^{\vee}(\mathbb{Q})|}\cdot\Omega_{J}\cdot\prod_{p}c_{p}\left|\frac{\omega}{\omega^{0}}\right|_{p}

where
\Omega_{J}
is the real period,
c_{p}
is the Tamagawa number at the prime
p
, and
\left|\frac{\omega}{\omega^{0}}\right|_{p}
is the
fudge factor
at
p
.

Both the real period and the fudge factor may be expressed in terms of differentials on the curve and its regular models. Firstly we have the isomorphism between global sections of differentials on
X
and
J
:

\Omega^{1}_{X/\mathbb{Q}}(X)\cong\Omega^{1}_{J/\mathbb{Q}}(J)

(see
[
Mil86
, Proposition 2.2]
). Choosing a basis of differentials
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}
on
X
, i.e. a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of the left-hand side above, we can express the real period as

\Omega_{J}=\left|\int_{J(\mathbb{R})}\omega_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{g}\right|.

Now choose a prime
p
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a regular model of
X
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and
\mathcal{J}
the Néron model of
J
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then we have an isomorphism between differentials on
\mathcal{J}
and global sections of the canonical sheaf
\omega_{\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}
:

\omega_{\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{X})\cong\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{J}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{J})

which are moreover compatible with the natural identifications
\omega_{\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{X})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}\cong\Omega^{1}_{X/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(X)
and
\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{J}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{J})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}\cong\Omega^{1}_{J/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(J)
, see
[
Bom22
, Lemma 9]
.

Now
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-basis of
\Omega^{1}_{X/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(X)
. Moreover
\omega_{\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{X/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(X)
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-lattice of full rank. If we let
\omega^{0}_{1},\ldots,\omega^{0}_{g}
be a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-basis of
\omega_{\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(\mathcal{X})
, then there is a unique
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that

\lambda\cdot\omega^{0}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega^{0}_{g}=\omega_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{g}

inside
\Omega^{g}_{J/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(J)
. The
p
-adic valuation of
\lambda
doesn’t depend on the basis
\omega^{0}_{i}
chosen, and the fudge factor at
p
is

\left|\frac{\omega}{\omega^{0}}\right|_{p}=|\lambda|_{p}.

This measures how far the wedge product of our chosen basis of differentials
\omega_{i}
is from being a Néron differential, i.e. one that is regular along the special fibre of the Néron model of
J
at
p
.

We record this as a definition in more generality, working over
R
. Choose and fix an absolute value
|\cdot|:K\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
compatible with the valuation.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over
K
of genus
g
, and let
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}
be a
K
-basis of
\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a regular model of
C
, and let
\omega^{0}_{1},\ldots,\omega^{0}_{g}
be an
R
-basis of the
R
-lattice
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})
. There is a unique
\lambda\in K
such that

\lambda\cdot\omega^{0}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega^{0}_{g}=\omega_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{g}

inside
\bigwedge^{g}\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
. The
BSD fudge factor
of
C
computed with respect to
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}
is the absolute value

\left|\frac{\omega}{\omega^{0}}\right|=|\lambda|.

Remark 6.2
.

As before, this is well-defined because the absolute value of
\lambda
doesn’t depend on the choice of basis
\omega^{0}_{i}
chosen. It also doesn’t depend on our choice of regular model, as the image of the map
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})\to\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
is independent of the regular model
\mathcal{C}
.

Our goal in this section will be to prove a local constancy result for the BSD fudge factor.

6.1
The canonical sheaf

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a regular model of the smooth projective curve
C
. We collect the relevant facts about the canonical sheaf, drawing on §6.3-6.4 of
[
Liu02
]
.

Definition 6.3
.

The
canonical sheaf
of
\mathcal{C}
over
R
, denoted
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
, is an invertible sheaf on
\mathcal{C}
defined as follows: suppose
i:\mathcal{C}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is a regular immersion into a smooth
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
, then

\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}=\det(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{X}})\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}}i^{*}(\det(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{X}/R})).

Here
\det
denotes the top exterior power of a locally free sheaf, and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{X}}
is the normal sheaf, i.e. if
\mathcal{I}
is the sheaf of ideals defining
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{X}}=i^{*}(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^{2})^{\vee}
.

Note that such an embedding
i
always exists for
\mathcal{C}
a regular model, because
\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R
is a local complete intersection morphism, and in fact we may take
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{A}^{n}_{R}

[
Liu02
, Example 6.3.18]
. The definition of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
is independent of the choice of
i
by
[
Liu02
, Lemma 6.4.5]
.

We can express the canonical sheaf locally as follows: take an affine open set
U=\mathrm{Spec}\,A\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, and suppose
A=R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I
, where
I
is an ideal generated by a regular sequence
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
. This corresponds to a regular immersion
i:U\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{n}_{R}
. The normal sheaf is given by

\mathcal{N}_{U/\mathbb{A}^{n}}=(I/I^{2})^{\vee}

where
I/I^{2}
is free of rank
n-1
over
A
, with basis
\overline{F}_{1},\ldots,\overline{F}_{n-1}
, the images of the
F_{i}
. Moreover
\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n}/R}
is free of rank
n
, generated by
dT_{1},\ldots,dT_{n}
. So we have that
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}|_{U}=\omega_{U/R}=\widetilde{\omega}_{A/R}
, where
\omega_{A/R}
is the
A
-module

\omega_{A/R}=\det(I/I^{2})^{\vee}\otimes_{A}(\Omega^{n}_{\mathbb{A}^{n}/R}\otimes_{R[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]}A)

which is free of rank 1, with basis
(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((dT_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dT_{n})\otimes 1)
.

We have a natural map

c:\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{C}/R}\to\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}

which is an isomorphism on the smooth locus of
\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R
, in particular on the generic fibre
[
Liu02
, Corollary 6.4.13]
. Locally it is given as follows: if
U=\mathrm{Spec}\,A\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is an affine open set as above, then this corresponds to a map

\displaystyle\Omega^{1}_{A/R}

\displaystyle\to\omega_{A/R}

\displaystyle dt_{j}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((dF_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dF_{n-1}\wedge dT_{j})\otimes 1)

where
t_{j}
is the image of
T_{j}
in
A
.

Taking sections over the generic fibre,
c
induces an isomorphism

\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})\otimes_{R}K=\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(C)\cong\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)

which means that
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})
is an
R
-lattice of full rank in the
K
-vector space
\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
. We will call this the lattice of
regular differentials
inside
\omega^{1}_{K/C}(C)
.

We can characterize the set of regular differentials as the set of differentials which are regular along each component of the special fibre. We expand this in more detail: if
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{s}
is a component of the special fibre, then the generic point
\xi
of
\Gamma
is a codimension 1 point of
\mathcal{C}
, hence
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}
is a DVR (as
\mathcal{C}
is regular). There is a corresponding valuation
v_{\xi}:K(\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
on the function field. Now if
\tau:\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}\mid_{U}\cong\mathcal{O}_{U}
is any trivialization of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
over an open set
U
containing
\xi
, then we have an induced map

\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R,\eta}\underset{\tau_{\eta}}{\cong}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\eta}=K(\mathcal{C})

with the first isomorphism coming from the trivialization.

Definition 6.4
.

We say that a differential
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}_{C/K,\eta}
is
regular along
\Gamma
if applying
v_{\xi}
to the image of
\alpha
under the map

\Omega^{1}_{C/K,\eta}\underset{c_{\eta}}{\xrightarrow{\sim}}\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R,\eta}\underset{\tau_{\eta}}{\xrightarrow{\sim}}K(\mathcal{C})

yields a nonnegative number.

Remark 6.5
.

This is independent of the choice of trivialization
\tau
, as choosing another trivialization changes the isomorphism by a unit in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}^{\times}
.

We then have the following characterization of the image of the restriction map
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})\to\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
:

Theorem 6.6
.

The image of the map
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})\to\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
consists of the differentials
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
that are regular along all components of the special fibre
\mathcal{C}_{s}
.

Remark 6.7
.

This statement is not really specific to our case, it holds in general for invertible sheaves on normal schemes.

Proof.

First suppose that
\alpha\in\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}(\mathcal{C})
. If
\xi
is the generic point of any component of the special fibre, then
\alpha\in\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R,\xi}
, which is sent by any trivialization to an element of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}
. Hence
\alpha
is regular along any component of the special fibre.

Conversely suppose
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
is regular along all components of the special fibre. Then for any generic point
\xi
of a component, there is an element
\beta\in\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R,\xi}
which has the same germ at
\eta
as
\alpha
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is integral, the restriction maps of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
are injective, so
\alpha
extends to a section of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
over an open set containing
\xi
. Applying this to all components,
\alpha
extends to a section of
\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R}
over an open set
U
containing
C
and the generic points of each irreducible component of
\mathcal{C}_{s}
. Hence
\mathcal{C}\setminus U
is a closed set of codimension
\geq 2
, and since
\mathcal{C}
is regular, hence normal,
\alpha
extends to a global section of the canonical sheaf as claimed.
∎

6.2
Local constancy of the fudge factor

Take the following setup: let
\mathcal{C}
be a (not necessarily regular) model of a smooth projective curve, embedded in a regular integral
R
-scheme
\mathcal{X}
, with generic fibre
\mathcal{X}_{\eta}=X
. Suppose
S=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}\}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
is a set of differentials.

Definition 6.8
.

Say that the model
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
good for
S
if the following conditions are met:

•

the generic fibre
C
of
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth projective curve of genus
g
,

•

we have
S\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{X/K,\eta_{C}}
where
\eta_{C}
is the generic point of
C
,

•

the natural restriction map
\Omega^{1}_{X/K,\eta_{C}}\twoheadrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{C/K,\eta_{C}}=\Omega^{1}_{K(C)/K}
maps
S
to a
K
-basis of the space of global sections
\Omega^{1}_{K/C}(K)\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(C)/K}
.

In this situation we denote the images of
\omega_{i}
in
\Omega^{1}_{K/C}(C)
by
\omega_{i}|_{C}
.

Example 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the
R
-scheme glued from
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathrm{Spec}\,R[x,y]
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}=\mathrm{Spec}\,R[u,v]
along the maps
u=\frac{1}{x},v=\frac{y}{x^{g+1}}
as in Example
2.2
, and let
S=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}\}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
be the set of differentials

\omega_{i}=x^{i-1}\frac{dx}{y}.

If
f\in R[x]
is separable of degree
2g+1
or
2g+2
, then the Weierstrass equation

y^{2}=f(x)

defines a model
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of a hyperelliptic curve
C
, again as in Example
2.2
. Then
S
is good for
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, we have that

\omega_{i}|_{C}=x^{i-1}\frac{dx}{y}\in\Omega^{1}_{K(C)/K}.

It is well known that these form a
K
-basis of the global sections
\Omega^{1}_{K/C}(C)
. Note that we may think of both
\omega_{i}
and
\omega_{i}|_{C}
as ‘the differential
x^{i-1}\frac{dx}{y}
’, however the former is though of as an element of
\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
and the latter as an element of
\Omega^{1}_{K(C)/K}
.

Now suppose
\mathcal{C}
is good for
S
and consider the
K
-vector space
V\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
spanned by
S
. This is a
g
-dimensional space (since the
\omega_{i}|_{C}
are linearly indepenedent, so are the
\omega_{i}
). There is an obvious isomorphism

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega_{i}|_{C}

We will use this to transfer regular differentials into

.

In what follows, we will derive a condition for a differential

, in case

Theorem 6.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be regular. With notation as above, the differential

\omega=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cdot dt_{j}}{c}\in V

where
a_{j},c\in A
and

\Gamma
(i.e.

v_{I}\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\overline{u_{I,j}}}{c}\right)\geq 0

where

is the normalized valuation on

associated to the discrete valuation ring
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}
, and

is the unique element of

and
\overline{u_{I,j}}
is its image in
\Frac(A/I)=\Frac(B/J)

Proof.

The canonical sheaf over

, where

\omega_{I}=\det(J/J^{2})^{\vee}\otimes_{B/J}(\Omega^{n}_{B/R}\otimes_{B}B/J),

which is free of rank 1 over

, and we have an explicit isomorphism

\displaystyle B/J

\displaystyle\cong\omega_{I}

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\mapsto(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((dT_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dT_{n})\otimes 1).

This is a trivialization of the canonical sheaf on

We also have that the canonical sheaf restricted to the generic fibre is equal to the sheaf of differentials. This is encoded by the natural map

\displaystyle c:\Omega^{1}_{(A/I)/R}

\displaystyle\to\omega_{I}

\displaystyle dt_{j}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((dF_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dF_{n-1}\wedge dT_{j})\otimes 1).

This map becomes an isomorphism upon tensoring with

(i.e. over the generic fibre), and hence also when tensoring with

(i.e. on the stalk at the generic point of
\mathcal{C}

To find the differentials in

that are regular along

\Omega^{1}_{K(C)/K}\xrightarrow{\sim}\omega_{\mathcal{C}/R,\eta_{C}}\xrightarrow{\sim}K(C)

\Omega^{1}_{\Frac(A/I)/K}\cong\omega_{I}\otimes_{A/I}\Frac(A/I)\cong\Frac(A/I)

where the first map is the above isomorphism, and the second map comes from the trivialization. One checks easily that the map is given by

\displaystyle dt_{j}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((dF_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dF_{r}\wedge dT_{j})\otimes 1)=

\displaystyle=(\overline{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\overline{F}_{n-1})^{\vee}\otimes((u_{I,j}dT_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dT_{n})\otimes 1)

Hence the composite map sends

and

is regular along

if the

-valuation of the latter is nonnegative, as claimed.
∎

Now take another regular model of a smooth projective curve
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that is also good for

, and is also

\mathcal{C}
for some

. Locally on

, we have

. We can also write
A/I^{\prime}=B/J^{\prime}
, where

is generated by a regular sequence

.

We can repeat the above argument with

replacing

, so similarly a generic differential

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cdot dt_{j}}{c}\in\Omega^{1}_{A_{I^{\prime}}/K}

where

and

, is regular along

if and only if

v_{I^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\overline{u_{I^{\prime},j}}}{c}\right)\geq 0

where

is the valuation associated to the DVR
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\xi}
, and

We will need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be
N
-close or formally
N
-close regular models of smooth projective curves, both good for
S
. With notation as above, the following hold:

(1)

We have
v_{I}(\pi)=v_{I^{\prime}}(\pi)=d
where
d
is the multiplicity of
\Gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{s}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{s}
.

(2)

We have
u_{I,j}\equiv u_{I^{\prime},j}\mod\pi^{N-1}
.

(3)

For any
x\in A
, if we denote
\widetilde{x}
its image in
A/I
and
\widehat{x}
its image in
A/I^{\prime}
, then
v_{I}(\widetilde{x})=n
and
N\geq\max\{2,n+1\}
guarantees that
v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{x})=n
too.

(4)

For any
\omega\in V
, there is an integer
d\geq 1
(depending on
\omega
) such that if
N\geq d
then

\omega|_{C}\text{ is regular along $\Gamma$}\iff\omega|_{C^{\prime}}\text{ is regular along $\Gamma$},

or in other words

\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\iff\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\Gamma}.

Proof.

(1)
This is
[
Liu02
, Exercise 8.3.3(a)]
.

(2)
Since
\Omega^{n}_{B/R}
is free of rank 1 over
B
with basis
dT_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dT_{n}
, it suffices to show that

(u_{I,j}-u_{I^{\prime},j})dT_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dT_{n}=dF_{1}\wedge\ldots dF_{n-1}\wedge dT_{j}-d\widetilde{F}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge d\widetilde{F}_{n-1}\wedge dT_{j}\in\pi^{N-1}\Omega^{n}_{B/R}.

However, we have
J+\pi^{N}B=J^{\prime}+\pi^{N}B
(this follows from both
N
-closeness and formal
N
-closeness), so that
\widetilde{F}_{i}=F_{i}+\pi^{N}G_{i}
. Hence

d\widetilde{F}_{i}=dF_{i}+d(\pi^{N}G_{i})\equiv dF_{i}\mod\pi^{N-1}

by the Leibniz rule, which implies the claim.

(3)
Let
\mathfrak{n}
be the maximal ideal of the regular local ring
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},\xi}
. We have
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},\xi}/I
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X},\xi}/I^{\prime}
. Because
I+(\pi^{N})=I^{\prime}+(\pi^{N})
and
N\geq 2
, we know that
I
and
I^{\prime}
have the same image in the cotangent space
\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. Since both
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\xi}
are 1-dimensional, we can choose
t\in\mathfrak{n}
such that the class of
t
and the image of
I
generate
\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. Then the class of
t
is a uniformiser in both
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\xi}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\xi}
. By shrinking the open set
U=\mathrm{Spec}\,A
we may assume that
t
is defined over
U
.

Now for an element
x\in A
, we have that
v_{I}(\widetilde{x})\geq m\iff x\in I+(t^{m})
and
v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{x})\geq m\iff x\in I^{\prime}+(t^{m})
. By
(1)
we have
\pi\in I+(t^{d})
and
\pi\in I^{\prime}+(t^{d})
too. Now since
I+(\pi^{N})=I^{\prime}+(\pi^{N})
, we must have
I+(t^{m})=I^{\prime}+(t^{m})
as long as
m\leq Nd
.

Hence if we have
v_{I}(\widetilde{x})=n
and
N\geq n+1
, then
x\in I+(t^{n})=I^{\prime}+(t^{n})
and
x\not\in I+(t^{n+1})=I^{\prime}+(t^{n+1})
, which implies that
v_{f^{\prime}}(\widehat{x})=n
also.

(4)
Express
\omega
as

\omega=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cdot dt_{j}}{c}

with
a_{j},c\in A
and
c\not\in I
. By
(3)
we have that for
N
sufficiently large,
v_{I}(\widetilde{c})=v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{c})
and in particular
c\not\in I^{\prime}
.

Now pick
N
large enough such that
x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}u_{I,j}
and
c
have the same valuation in
A/I
and
A/I^{\prime}
, and also such that
N\geq 1+v_{I}(\widetilde{c})
. Also set

y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}u_{I^{\prime},j}.

Then we have that
x\equiv y\mod\pi^{N-1}
by
(2)
. If
\omega|_{C}
is regular along
\Gamma
, then we have that

v_{I}\left(\frac{\widetilde{x}}{\widetilde{c}}\right)\geq 0.

We can also write

\frac{\widehat{y}}{\widehat{c}}=\frac{\widehat{x}}{\widehat{c}}+\pi^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{z}}{\widehat{c}}

for some
z\in A
. By assumption we have that
v_{I}(\widetilde{x})=v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{x})
and
v_{I}(\widetilde{c})=v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{c})
. We also have
N\geq 1+v_{I}(\widetilde{c})=1+v_{I^{\prime}}(\widehat{c})
, so
v_{I^{\prime}}(\pi^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{z}}{\widehat{c}})\geq 0
as well. This means
v_{I^{\prime}}(\frac{\widehat{y}}{\widehat{c}})\geq 0
, and
\omega|_{C^{\prime}}
is regular along
\Gamma
.

If
\omega
is not regular along
\Gamma
, the same argument shows that
v_{I^{\prime}}(\frac{\widehat{x}}{\widehat{c}})=v_{I}(\frac{\widetilde{x}}{\widetilde{c}})<0
and
v_{I^{\prime}}(\pi^{N-1}\frac{\widehat{z}}{\widehat{c}})\geq 0
, so therefore we get that
v_{I^{\prime}}(\frac{\widehat{y}}{\widehat{c}})<0
and therefore
\omega|_{C^{\prime}}
is not regular along
\Gamma
.
∎

We can now turn to showing local constancy of the lattice of regular differentials, and hence of the fudge factor.

Theorem 6.14
.

Suppose
k
is finite. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a regular model of a smooth projective curve that is good for the set
S=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}\}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
of differentials. There is an integer
n\geq 1
such that if
N\geq n
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is another regular model of a smooth projective curve that is good for
S
, and is
N
-close or formally
N
-close to
\mathcal{C}
, then
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}=W_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\Gamma}
. In particular the BSD fudge factors of the generic fibres
C,C^{\prime}
, computed with respect to the bases of differentials
\omega_{i}|_{C},\omega_{i}|_{C^{\prime}}
are equal.

Proof.

Theorem
6.6
just states that

W_{\mathcal{C}}=\bigcap_{\Gamma}W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}

where the intersection is over all components
\Gamma
of
\mathcal{C}_{s}
.

If
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
are 1-close or formally 1-close, then they have the same special fibre, and hence in this case it suffices to show that

W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}=W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}

for all components
\Gamma
of
\mathcal{C}_{s}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{s}
.

Now let
B
be an
R
-basis of the lattice
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\subseteq V
, and let
B^{\prime}
be a set of coset representatives of the quotient
\pi^{-1}W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}/W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
. Then
B^{\prime}
is a finite set because
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\cong R^{g}
implies that

\pi^{-1}W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}/W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\cong\pi^{-1}R^{g}/R^{g}\cong k^{g}

and we assumed that
k
is finite.

By part
(4)
of Lemma
6.13
, picking
N
sufficiently large ensures that any element
\omega\in B\cup B^{\prime}
satisfies

\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\iff\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\Gamma}.

We claim that for such large
N
(and if
N\geq 1
), we have
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}=W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}
. On the one hand both of these are
R
-lattices, and
B\subseteq W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}
so we have
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\subseteq W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}
. On the other hand if
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\subsetneq W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}
, then there must be an
\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}\setminus W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
such that
\pi\omega\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
. Then there is some
\omega^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
such that

\omega-\omega^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}\subseteq W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}

by the definition of
B^{\prime}
. Hence
\omega^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\Gamma}
too. By our choice of
N
we then have
\omega^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
too. So
\omega^{\prime},\omega-\omega^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
but
\omega\not\in W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}
, a contradiction. Hence
W_{\mathcal{C},\Gamma}=W_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\Gamma}
as claimed.

The fudge factor of
C
with respect to the basis
\omega_{i}|_{C}
can be computed from the lattice
W_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq V
. Specifically, if
\omega_{1}^{0},\ldots,\omega_{g}^{0}
is an
R
-basis of
W_{\mathcal{C}}
, then the fudge factor is
|\lambda|
where
\lambda\in K
satisfies

\lambda\cdot\omega_{1}^{0}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{g}^{0}=\omega_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{g}.

Indeed this is just the definition of the fudge factor, transferred through the restriction isomorphism
V\cong\Omega^{1}_{C/K}(C)
. Similarly the fudge factor of
C^{\prime}
computed with respect to the
\omega_{i}|_{C^{\prime}}
is computed from
W_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
. The local constancy of the fudge factor then follows.
∎

Combining this with our results on resolution of singularities by blowups, we can remove the assumption that our models are regular:

Theorem 6.15
.

Suppose
k
is finite. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a model of a smooth projective curve that is good for the set
S=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{g}\}\subseteq\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}
of differentials. There is an integer
n\geq 1
such that if
N\geq n
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is another model of a smooth projective curve that is good for
S
, and is
N
-close to
\mathcal{C}
, then the BSD fudge factors of
C
and
C^{\prime}
, computed with respect to the bases of differentials
\omega_{i}|_{C},\omega_{i}|_{C^{\prime}}
respectively, are equal.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
there is a sequence of blowups

\mathcal{C}_{n}\to\ldots\to\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{C}

in regular centres
D_{i}
on the special fibre, such that
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is regular. By Theorem
5.2
, there is an
a\geq 1
such that if
N\geq a
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
N
-close to
\mathcal{C}
, then we can perform a sequence of blowups

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}\to\ldots\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

in centres
D_{i}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{n}
is regular and formally
(N-a)
-close to
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

Putting
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{i+1}=\mathrm{Bl}_{D_{i}}\mathcal{X}_{i}
so that
\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{i}
. Since all the blowups are centred on the special fibre, the generic fibres, are unchanged, and in particular we have the identification on differentials

\Omega^{1}_{K(X_{i})/K}\cong\Omega^{1}_{K(X)/K}.

So we can regard
S
as a set of differentials on the
\mathcal{X}_{i}
for any

. Since
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are good for

, so are
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{i}
for any

(N-a)

.
∎

Corollary 6.16

We know that the curves

7
Galois representations

In this section we prove a local constancy result for Galois representations in families of curves. The precise result is the following:

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
K
be a local field of characteristic 0. Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be
N
-close models of smooth projective curves
C,C^{\prime}
over
K
, of genus
g\geq 2
. Assume that
\mathcal{X}
is smooth over
\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{O}_{K}
. There is an integer
d\geq 1
such that if
N\geq d
, then the Galois representations

H^{1}_{\acute{e}t}(C_{\overline{K}},\mathbb{Q}_{l})\qquad\text{and}\qquad H^{1}_{\acute{e}t}(C^{\prime}_{\overline{K}},\mathbb{Q}_{l})

are isomorphic (here
l\not=p
is a prime).

Proof.

The proof uses a result of Tim and Vladimir Dokchitser
[
DD25
, Theorem 2(iii)]
, saying that the
G_{K}
-representation
H^{1}_{\acute{e}t}(C_{\overline{K}},\mathbb{Q}_{l})
is uniquely determined by point counts
\#\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{L}(\mathbb{F}_{L})
, for a finite list of extensions
L/K
. Here
\mathcal{C}_{L}
is a regular model of
C_{L}
over
L
, with special fibre
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
, and
\mathbb{F}_{L}
is the residue field of
L
.

Hence it suffices to show that for any fixed finite extension
L/K
,
C_{L}
and
C^{\prime}_{L}
have regular models with the same number of points on their special fibre. We have that
C_{L}
and
C^{\prime}_{L}
are
N
-close, since
N
-closeness is preserved under base extension by Proposition
2.3
(3). Note also that since
\mathcal{X}
is smooth over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{O}_{L}}
is regular. Hence by Theorem
5.3
, for
N
sufficiently large, we have that
C_{L}
and
C^{\prime}_{L}
have regular models with the same special fibre. In particular these regular models have the same number of
\mathbb{F}_{L}
-points on their special fibre, proving the claim.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

In
[
DD25
]
, Theorem 2(iii) is phrased in terms of the minimal regular model
\mathcal{C}_{L}
of
C
over
L
. However, the proof goes through even if we replace
\mathcal{C}_{L}
by an arbitrary regular model. Indeed, the proof relies on the isomorphism

H^{1}_{\acute{e}t}(C_{\overline{L}},\mathbb{Q}_{l})^{I_{L}}\cong H^{1}_{\acute{e}t}((\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{L})_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{L}},\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{L}),

and the Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula to express the Frobenius traces on étale cohomology as point counts. Both of these hold in the case of
\mathcal{C}_{L}
a proper regular model.

As an easy corollary, we also obtain the following:

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
K
be a local field of characteristic 0. Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be
N
-close models of smooth projective curves
C,C^{\prime}
over
K
, of genus
g\geq 2
. For
N
sufficiently large, the curves
C
and
C^{\prime}
have the same local Euler factor, local root number and conductor exponent.

Proof.

All the listed invariants are functions of the Galois representation, hence the claim follows by Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Appendix A
Appendix: Hensel’s lemma

In one of our proofs, we shall need the following version of Hensel’s lemma, for
\pi
-adically complete non-local rings.

Theorem A.1

(Hensel’s lemma)
.

Let
A
be a ring, complete with respect to an ideal
I
. Suppose that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in A[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are
n
polynomials in
n
variables, and suppose we have
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
such that

f_{i}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in I^{d}\text{ for each }i

for some integer
d\geq 1
, and that the
n\times n
matrix

J=\left(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}

is invertible over
A
. Then there exist
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in A
such that
b_{i}\equiv a_{i}\mod I^{d}
and

f_{i}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=0\text{ for each }i.

Proof.

Define the function
F
on
A^{n}
by

F\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}t_{1}\\
\vdots\\
t_{n}\end{array}\right]\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\\
\vdots\\
f_{n}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\end{array}\right].

We change variables as follows: let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in A[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be polynomials such that for
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
we have

F(J^{-1}(X+\mathbf{a}))=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}g_{1}(X)\\
\vdots\\
g_{n}(X)\end{array}\right]=G(X).

Then by assumption
g_{i}(0,\ldots,0)\in I^{d}
for all
i
, and the matrix
(\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(0,\ldots,0))
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. If we can find
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}=(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{n}^{\prime})
such that
g_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{\prime})=0
for all
i
, and
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in I^{d}A^{n}
, then
\mathbf{b}=J\mathbf{b}^{\prime}+\mathbf{a}
satisfies the conditions of the theorem.

Hence we may WLOG assume that
\mathbf{a}=(0,\ldots,0)
and that
J
is the identity matrix.

We define the sequence
\mathbf{a}_{m}
of elements of
A^{n}
by the recurrence

\mathbf{a}_{0}=\mathbf{a}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{a}_{m+1}=\mathbf{a}_{m}-F(\mathbf{a}_{m}).

We claim that
\mathbf{a}_{i}
converges to
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
which satisfies the requirements of the theorem.

It is clear that for all
m
,
\mathbf{a}_{m}\in I^{d}A^{n}
. We can show by induction that
\mathbf{a}_{m}\equiv\mathbf{a}_{m+1}\mod I^{d+m}
for all
m\geq 0
. For
m=0
, this is true by assumption (as
F(\mathbf{a})\in I^{d}A^{n}
). Now suppose
m\geq 1
and the claim holds for all integers up to
m
. We can put

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{m+1}-\mathbf{a}_{m}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{a}_{m}-F(\mathbf{a}_{m}))-(\mathbf{a}_{m-1}-F(\mathbf{a}_{m-1}))=

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{a}_{m}-\mathbf{a}_{m-1})-(F(\mathbf{a}_{m})-F(\mathbf{a}_{m-1}))

We will use the Taylor expansion

F(X)=F(0)+X+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}R_{ij},

which holds for some
R_{ij}\in A^{n}
(remember
J
is the identity). Plugging in
\mathbf{a}_{m}
and
\mathbf{a}_{m-1}
, and using
\mathbf{a}_{m}\equiv\mathbf{a}_{m-1}\mod I^{d+m-1}
we get that

F(\mathbf{a}_{m})-F(\mathbf{a}_{m-1})\equiv\mathbf{a}_{m}-\mathbf{a}_{m-1}\mod I^{2(d+m-1)}

and since
2(d+m-1)\geq d+m
, we get that
\mathbf{a}_{m+1}-\mathbf{a}_{m}\in I^{d+m}
.

Hence since
A
is
I
-adically complete, we get a limit
\mathbf{b}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbf{a}_{m}
. Since
\mathbf{a}_{m}\in I^{d}A^{n}
for all
m
, we have
\mathbf{b}\in I^{d}A^{n}
, and taking the limit of
F(\mathbf{a}_{m})=\mathbf{a}_{m}-\mathbf{a}_{m+1}
as
m\to\infty
we get that
F(\mathbf{b})=0
, as desired.
∎
