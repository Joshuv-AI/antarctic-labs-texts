Effects of multimodal mobile health interventions on cardiovascular risk factor management in patients with diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yan Zhang, Xingqi Cao, LiJuan Huang, Yue Zhu, Liying Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDiabetes mellitus significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). While mobile health (mHealth) interventions show promise, there is limited evidence on the efficacy of multimodal approaches for managing comprehensive CVD risk factors. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of multimodal mHealth interventions in managing CVD risk factors in patients with diabetes.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs), reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.Data sourcesMEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library and CINAHL were searched for RCTs published from January 2010 to December 2024.Eligibility criteriaRCTs involving adults (≥18 years) with diabetes who received multimodal mHealth interventions (incorporating at least three components such as mobile apps, remote monitoring and SMS reminders) for ≥3 months, compared with standard care, were included.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers screened records, extracted data and assessed the risk of bias using the original Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) tool. Perform effect size pooling using R V.4.4.3 and report the corresponding results, taking into account the observed heterogeneity.ResultsOut of 2730 screened records, 17 RCTs (n=2946 participants) met the inclusion criteria. Multimodal mHealth interventions significantly reduced haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (weighted mean difference (WMD) = −0.38%, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.24; p<0.0001), fasting glucose (WMD=−14.10 mg/dL, 95% CI −20.89 to −7.30; p<0.0001), systolic blood pressure (WMD=−1.30 mm Hg, 95% CI −2.12 to −0.47; p=0.0021), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (WMD=−6.07 mg/dL, 95% CI −9.45 to −2.68; p=0.0004), total cholesterol (WMD=−5.40 mg/dL, 95% CI −9.85 to −0.93; p=0.0177) and triglycerides (WMD=−6.50 mg/dL, 95% CI −12.65 to −0.35; p=0.0383). Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has emerged as a global public health challenge, characterised by a persistently escalating prevalence. Projections indicate that the global diabetic population will surge to 783 million by 2045. Diabetes is not only an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) but is also closely linked to the pathogenesis and progression of CVD.1 2 Studies reveal that 68% of patients with diabetes ultimately succumb to CVD-related complications.3 Moreover, hypertension, dyslipidaemia or overweight/obesity—common comorbidities at the time of diabetes diagnosis—further amplify CVD risks.4 These multimorbidities not only precipitate severe events such as myocardial infarction and stroke but also markedly impair patients’ quality of life and exacerbate socioeconomic burdens.5

Comprehensive management of key modifiable CVD risk factors (including blood glucose, blood pressure, lipids, body weight/body mass index (BMI), physical activity and smoking status) has been proven effective in delaying the progression of atherosclerotic CVD.6 However, a significant gap persists between clinical practice and guideline recommendations: widespread issues include insufficient disease awareness, poor adherence and challenges in sustaining long-term lifestyle modifications among patients.7 8 Traditional management models, reliant on frequent outpatient visits, are effective yet cost-prohibitive, while information dissemination through conventional media remains limited. Consequently, the exploration of cost-effective and scalable novel management strategies to enhance patients’ self-management capabilities and optimise risk factor control has become an urgent clinical priority.9 10

Internet-based healthcare offers innovative pathways for chronic disease management through telemedicine and digital tools. Among these, mobile health (mHealth)—integrating remote monitoring, behavioural interventions and medication reminders—transcends temporal and spatial constraints, fostering enhanced patient–provider interactions.11 12 Evidence demonstrates that smartphone-based self-management tools significantly improve medication adherence and glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes.13 Nevertheless, existing studies predominantly focus on isolated parameters, with most characterised by small sample sizes and high heterogeneity in conclusions.14 Although Haider et al conducted a systematic review of mHealth interventions prior to 2019, the past 5 years have witnessed rapid advancements in this field, necessitating the integration of emerging evidence.15

This study aims to systematically evaluate the synergistic effects of multimodal mHealth interventions on the control of key CVD risk factors (such as blood pressure, lipids, body weight/BMI, physical activity and smoking status) in patients with diabetes through a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis. By synthesising updated evidence and quantifying effect sizes, this research seeks to provide robust evidence for optimising diabetes care models in community settings, thereby advancing clinical practice toward more efficient and intelligent paradigms.

Methods

The present study employed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis 2020 guidelines to ensure a comprehensive and systematic approach to our review.16 The protocol for this review has been registered in the PROSPERO database under the following ID: CRD420251050970.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across five databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science (via Clarivate), Embase (via Elsevier), Cochrane Library (via Wiley), CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), covering publications from January 2010 to December 2024. Search terms included “internet”, “electronic”, “information”, “network”, “platform”, “telemedicine”, “WeChat”, “mobile”, “app”, “management”, “diabetes mellitus” and “cardiovascular risk factors” combined with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. Additional studies were identified through reference tracking and manual searches of grey literature to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Selection criteria

The articles had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) Randomised controlled trials (RCTs); (2) Participants aged ≥18 years with diabetes mellitus; (3) Interventions incorporating ≥3 multimodal components (eg, mobile apps, SMS reminders, remote glucose monitoring, video consultations, wearable devices) lasting ≥3 months; (4) Either assessed one or more CVD risk factors; (5) Comparator groups receiving standard care. Exclusion criteria: Non-RCTs, paediatric studies and non-English full-text articles. The English-language restriction was applied for practical reasons, as accurate translation and critical appraisal of non-English studies were beyond the available resources; we acknowledge this as a potential limitation, though the core research in this field is predominantly published in English.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Online supplemental table 1 presents the detailed retrieval strategy. Two independent researchers (YZha and XC) extracted data on first author, publication year, study design, sample size, intervention details, follow-up duration and outcomes (eg, haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose, systolic blood pressure (SBP)/diastolic blood pressure (DBP), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, smoking status, physical activity, adverse events). During the literature screening process for this systematic review, databases including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science (via Clarivate), Embase (via Elsevier), Cochrane Library (via Wiley) and CINAHL (via EBSCOhost) were initially searched, yielding 2730 records which were imported into the EndNote V.21 reference management system. Subsequently, the ‘Find Duplicates’ function within EndNote was employed to remove 1162 duplicate records, leaving 1568 records for further screening. Subsequently, a preliminary screening was conducted by reviewing titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 1525 ineligible studies. The remaining 43 studies advanced to the full-text assessment stage. During full-text assessment, we downloaded and thoroughly reviewed all 43 articles. Based on inclusion criteria, 22 were excluded for the following reasons: inappropriate study design,5 unsuitable intervention,6 irrelevant study population,5 conference abstracts3 and review articles.3 Ultimately, 17 studies were included for subsequent meta-analysis. Discrepancies were resolved through third-party arbitration. Methodological quality was assessed using the original Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) tool across seven domains: (1) Random sequence generation; (2) Allocation concealment; (3) Blinding of participants and personnel; (4) Blinding of outcome assessment; (5) Incomplete outcome data; (6) Selective reporting; and (7) Other biases.17 Studies were classified as ‘low risk’, ‘high risk’ or ‘unclear risk’ of bias.

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using R software V.4.4.3. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the Q test and quantified with the I² statistic. A fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was low (p>0.1, I² <50%), while a random-effects model was used when heterogeneity was high.

When analysis results exhibit significant differences and high heterogeneity (I² >50%), we employ meta-regression analysis to investigate the potential influence of covariates (risk of bias, trial follow-up duration, age, male gender, geographical region) on outcome measures. Specifically, we use the metareg() function within the matafor package for regression analysis, employing the restricted maximum likelihood method to fit a mixed-effects model. We first performed univariate meta-regression analyses. When a single outcome measure was significantly influenced by multiple covariates, we included these covariates in multivariate meta-regression analyses. For missing data, we use multiple imputation methods, selecting data points that most closely resemble the original data distribution for analysis.

This study summarised findings using corresponding effect sizes and their 95% CIs. For continuous variables, mean differences (MD) were used for pooling, with effect size pooling conducted using the inverse variance weighting method. For dichotomous variables, ORs served as effect measures. To provide more robust estimates in scenarios involving sparse event data, we employed the Mantel-Haenszel method based on generalised linear mixed models for pooling.

Results

A total of 2730 articles were initially retrieved through database searches. After screening titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review based on predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria and evaluation of intervention/control measures, 17 studies involving 2946 patients were ultimately included.1834 Figure 1 displays a flowchart of the study selection process. Risk of bias assessment

All 17 studies were multicentre RCTs conducted internationally. Each study reported baseline characteristics and explicitly mentioned randomisation. The randomisation protocols were clearly described, and blinding of data collectors and analysts was implemented across all studies. Complete outcome data were reported, with reasons for missing data thoroughly documented. Risk of bias assessment using the original Cochrane RoB tool indicated low risk across all domains except performance bias (due to inherent unblinding in behavioural interventions). The overall methodological quality was robust. Methodological quality assessment results are presented in figures2 3.

Meta-analysis results

Glycaemic control: Multimodal mHealth interventions significantly reduced HbA1c levels (weighted mean difference (WMD) = −0.38%, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.24, p<0.0001) under high heterogeneity (I²=60.6%, p=0.0006), analysed using a random-effects model (online supplemental figure 1). Similarly, fasting blood glucose decreased substantially (WMD = −14.10 mg/dL, 95% CI −20.89 to −7.30, p<0.0001) with minimal heterogeneity (I²=0%, p=0.7802), supporting a fixed-effect model (online supplemental figure 2).

Blood pressure: Interventions significantly lowered SBP (WMD = −1.30 mm Hg, 95% CI −2.12 to −0.47, p=0.0021), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=46%, p=0.0265) justifying a fixed-effect model (online supplemental figure 3). In contrast, DBP showed no statistically significant difference between groups (WMD=0.16 mm Hg, 95% CI −0.43 to 0.75, p=0.5919), analysed via fixed-effect model due to moderate heterogeneity (I²=31.2%, p=0.1264) (online supplemental figure 4).

Lipid profile: Significant improvements were observed in LDL-C (WMD=−6.07 mg/dL, 95% CI −9.45 to −2.68, p=0.0004; random-effects model, I²=61.8%, p=0.0004) (online supplemental figure 5), total cholesterol (WMD = −5.39 mg/dL, 95% CI −9.85 to −0.93, p=0.0177; random-effects model, I²=57%, p=0.0043) (online supplemental figure 6) and triglycerides (WMD = −6.50 mg/dL, 95% CI −12.65 to −0.35, p=0.0383; fixed-effect model, I²=0%, p=0.7549) (online supplemental figure 7). However, HDL-C levels remained unchanged (WMD = −0.26 mg/dL, 95% CI −1.76 to 1.24, p=0.7333; random-effects model, I²=50.9%, p=0.0147) (online supplemental figure 8).

Anthropometric and behavioural outcomes: BMI did not show any significant differences (WMD = −0.47 kg/m², 95% CI −1.18 to 0.24, p=0.1933; random-effects model, I²=75.3%, p<0.0001) (online supplemental figure 9). Physical activity participation significantly increased in the intervention group (OR=3.41, 95% CI 1.16 to 10.05, p=0.0259; random-effects model, I²=83.3%, p=0.0004) (online supplemental figure 10). Conversely, smoking cessation rates showed no significant effect (OR=1.03, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.51, p=0.8909; fixed-effect model, I²=0%, p=0.8909) (online supplemental figure 11).

Adverse events: No significant intergroup difference (OR=0.89; 95% CI 0.61 to 1.28; p=0.5210). Homogeneous results (I²=0%; p=0.8204) justified a fixed-effect model (online supplemental figure 12).

Meta-regression results

HbA1c, LDL-C, total cholesterol and physical activity participation met the criteria for meta-regression. Moreover, SBP is closely associated with CVD; hence, we conducted additional analyses. The analysis showed that intervention duration was a significant predictor of HbA1c (β=0.0115, 95% CI 0.0009 to 0.0220, p=0.033). This model accounted for approximately 21.1% of the observed heterogeneity (adjusted R² = 21.07%). However, significant residual heterogeneity remained (residual I² = 51.24%, p=0.004), suggesting that other unmeasured factors may have influenced the results (for the relationship between specific intervention timing and HbA1c, please refer to figure 4). Publication bias

Funnel plots were generated to assess potential publication bias for outcomes reported in ≥10 studies, as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. This threshold ensures adequate statistical power for visual and statistical symmetry tests. Among the 12 parameters analysed in this meta-analysis, eight key outcomes met this criterion: HbA₁c (17 studies, Egger: p=0.4131), SBP (15 studies, Egger: p=0.4256), BMI (11 studies, Egger: p=0.3758), LDL-C (17 studies, Egger: p=0.9811), DBP (14 studies, Egger: p=0.5856), total cholesterol (14 studies, Egger: p=0.1540), HDL-C (14 studies, Egger: p=0.3057) and triglyceride (13 studies, Egger: p=0.0014). The results of the Egger test indicate that, except for triglycerides, all other outcomes showed p values >0.05, suggesting a low probability of publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our primary findings and identify potential outlier studies exerting disproportionate influence, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out method. No single trial had a disproportionate impact on the pooled effect estimates for the other outcome measures, nor did any trial alter the statistical significance of these outcomes.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of multimodal mHealth interventions in managing CVD risk factors in patients with diabetes. The systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that mHealth interventions can significantly improve key CVD risk factors in patients with diabetes, such as LDL, SBP, cholesterol and triglycerides, at a statistically significant level. However, we must interpret these findings carefully in terms of their clinical significance.

The average reduction in SBP was 1.30 mm Hg, along with moderate decreases in LDL-C and triglycerides. When considering any single measure in isolation, the clinical significance may appear limited. However, cardiovascular risk management is inherently a multidimensional strategy, and the ultimate benefit arises from the synergistic improvement of all risk factors. Our study reveals that the value of multimodal mHealth interventions lies in their ability to simultaneously and modestly optimise the entire spectrum of risk factors, rather than achieving dramatic breakthroughs in a single measure. This broad improvement, combined with the low-cost and scalable nature of mHealth, underscores its unique clinical value.

Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses based on the core functions of the multimodal mHealth intervention, providing further context for interpreting these results. Our subgroup analysis indicates that the overall averages may mask significant heterogeneity in the effects of different intervention models on certain outcome measures. The specific design of the intervention model appears to be a key factor determining its clinical effectiveness.

Regarding SBP, although the overall reduction of −1.30 mmHg is smaller than the expected effect of single-drug therapies in traditional hypertension management, this result should be reconsidered in the context of mHealth interventions. First, the population included in this study was not entirely composed of patients with uncontrolled hypertension, and the baseline differences in blood pressure levels limited the average reduction. More importantly, our subgroup analysis revealed that interventions centred on ‘reminders and communication support’ showed the most consistent blood pressure-lowering trend. This type of intervention addresses core barriers in blood pressure management by improving medication adherence and promoting regular self-monitoring. Considering mHealth as a behavioural support tool, its 1–2 mm Hg reduction in blood pressure is comparable to the effects of some lifestyle interventions (eg, salt restriction).35 Moreover, when combined with pharmacotherapy, such a reduction may be sufficient to achieve a meaningful reduction in vascular event risk at the population level.

Regarding LDL-C reduction (−6.07 mg/dL), this represents a moderate improvement. However, subgroup analysis provided deeper insights. The ‘reminders and communication support’ and ‘structured self-management’ models achieved reductions of −6.63 mg/dL and −14.47 mg/dL, respectively. For each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C, the relative risk of major cardiovascular events decreases by approximately 21% to 25%.36 Thus, the ‘reminders and communication support’ model is estimated to reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events by 3.6% to 4.2%, while the ‘structured self-management’ model is expected to lower the risk by 7.8% to 9.3%. These findings suggest that targeted mHealth intervention models can lead to clinically significant, non-negligible risk reductions, particularly when used as primary prevention or in combination with statin therapy, potentially offering cumulative benefits to patients.

Regarding the modest reduction in triglycerides (−6.83 mg/dL), its clinical significance should indeed be considered in light of the intervention type. Notably, in the ‘reminders and communication support’ subgroup, we observed a significantly larger reduction (−22.58 mg/dL). This larger decrease is more likely attributed to the comprehensive effects of the intervention on lifestyle factors (such as diet, exercise and alcohol consumption), which are key determinants of triglyceride levels. Therefore, the dilution of the overall effect suggests that future interventions aiming to effectively manage triglycerides should focus on explicitly integrating and strengthening behavioural changes targeting nutrition and physical activity, rather than relying solely on general health reminders.

The multimodal mHealth strategy significantly improved blood glucose control, achieving clinically meaningful reductions in HbA1c and fasting blood glucose levels. These improvements align with the ability of the technology to provide real-time physiological monitoring and medication adherence support. For example, integrated blood glucose and blood pressure tracking, along with automatic alert functions, facilitate early intervention before the indicators exceed critical thresholds. This may explain why the intervention outperformed standard care. Conversely, neutral effects on DBP and HDL-C suggest physiological or design limitations. Diastolic pressure may be less responsive to behavioural interventions due to stronger autonomic regulation, while HDL-C elevation often requires pharmacological modulation. The null finding in smoking cessation further highlights the ‘intention-action gap’ in digital health—where knowledge dissemination alone is insufficient without addressing nicotine addiction mechanisms.37 38 Paradoxically, our meta-regression revealed that longer intervention durations were significantly associated with diminished HbA1c reductions. This suggests that the initial benefits of mHealth interventions may be subject to diminishing returns over time, potentially due to behavioural fatigue and waning patient adherence. Consequently, perpetual intensive intervention may not represent an optimal long-term strategy.

The most compelling evidence arises from the interaction between physical activity and BMI. mHealth interventions drove substantial increases in physical activity participation, which directly mediated weight control effects. This synergy operates through three primary mechanisms: app-based goal-setting (eg, step targets and exercise reminders) that foster habit formation; wearable-generated progress visualisation (eg, calorie expenditure data) reinforcing behavioural persistence39 40, and social accountability enabled by peer comparison features in network-integrated platforms.23 41 Critically, this dual benefit addresses a fundamental challenge in diabetes management: sustainable lifestyle modification. While traditional approaches struggle with long-term adherence, mHealth’s capacity for continuous engagement leverages a virtuous cycle wherein activity-induced weight loss improves insulin sensitivity, subsequently enhancing exercise capacity and further reinforcing behavioural change.

While the robustness of our findings is evident, three key limitations merit thoughtful consideration. First, performance bias stemming from inherent unblinding in behavioural trials remains a methodological constraint, potentially amplifying observed effect sizes. Second, significant generalisability gaps persist for elderly and low-literacy populations, as current interventions often presume digital fluency and cognitive capacities not universally present. Third, although we have endeavoured to explore potential heterogeneity through meta-regression, unidentified heterogeneity remains.

These limitations crystallise critical priorities for future investigation. To address resistant clinical outcomes, next-generation interventions should integrate targeted adjuncts such as pharmacotherapy modules (eg, embedding varenicline protocols within smoking cessation apps) and combine HDL-C exercise prescriptions with polygenic risk scoring to personalise cardiovascular prevention. Equity-focused implementation must become paramount, necessitating voice-based interfaces for low-literacy users and rigorous validation of simplified wearables in geriatric cohorts to bridge the digital divide. Furthermore, advancing the field requires concerted standardisation efforts: establishing core component checklists through initiatives like the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) - mHealth extension and defining evidence-based minimum intervention durations to ensure sustained efficacy. Collectively, these directions address current evidence gaps while accelerating the translation of mHealth innovations into equitable, generalisable clinical practice.42 43

Conclusion

Multimodal mHealth interventions effectively optimise key cardiovascular risk factors in patients with diabetes. Meta-analysis confirms significant improvements in glycaemic control (HbA1c, fasting glucose), SBP, lipid profiles (LDL-C, total cholesterol, triglycerides) and physical activity levels. In contrast, these interventions yielded no significant benefits for DBP, HDL-C, body mass index or smoking cessation. The treatment effects were heterogeneous and demonstrated a potential tendency to wane over time. This evidence positions mHealth as a valuable adjunct to conventional care, yet future work must prioritise intervention optimisation, equitable access and validation of long-term benefits to enable widespread clinical implementation.
