Multiscale replay: A robust algorithm for stochastic variational inequalities with a Markovian buffer


Abstract
† † ⋄ Tianjiao Li was at Georgia Tech when most of this work was performed. We introduce the Multiscale Experience Replay (MER) algorithm for solving a class of stochastic variational inequalities (VIs) in settings where samples are generated from a Markov chain and we have access to a memory buffer to store them. Rather than uniformly sampling from the buffer, MER utilizes a multi-scale sampling scheme to emulate the behavior of VI algorithms designed for independent and identically distributed samples, overcoming bias in the de facto serial scheme and thereby accelerating convergence. Notably, unlike standard sample-skipping variants of serial algorithms, MER is robust in that it achieves this acceleration in iteration complexity whenever possible, and without requiring knowledge of the mixing time of the Markov chain. We also discuss applications of MER, particularly in policy evaluation with temporal difference learning and in training generalized linear models with dependent data.

Introduction

Variational inequalities (VIs) provide a general mathematical framework for modeling a wide range of problems in various domains, such as game theory, economics, operator theory, as well as modern machine learning
(
10
;
59
;
2
;
11
;
3
;
21
)
. An appealing aspect is that they allow for a unified treatment of both optimization and equilibrium problems.

Given a closed convex feasible region
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an operator
F:X\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the VI
problem is to find an
x^{*}\in X
such that

\displaystyle\langle F(x^{*}),x-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0.\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }x\in X.

(1)

Throughout this paper, we assume the existence of a solution
x^{*}
to problem (
1
).
We also assume that
F
is an
L
-Lipschitz continuous operator, i.e.,
for some
L>0
,

\displaystyle\|F(x_{1})-F(x_{2})\|\leq L\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }x_{1},x_{2}\in X.

(2)

The notation
\|\cdot\|
refers to the Euclidean norm throughout this paper, unless stated otherwise.

Additionally, we assume
F
to be a generalized strongly monotone operator, i.e., for some
\mu>0
,

\displaystyle\langle F(x),x-x^{*}\rangle\geq\mu\|x-x^{*}\|^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }x\in X.

(3)

Clearly, condition (
3
) holds if
F
is strongly monotone, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle F(x_{1})-F(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\geq\mu\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }x_{1},x_{2}\in X.

Note that our assumed generalized strong monotonicity condition (
3
) is weaker than global strong monotonicity, but already guarantees that
x^{*}
satisfying Ineq. (
1
) is unique.

The wide range of applications of VIs has motivated extensive algorithmic studies in the past few decades. Classical algorithms for monotone VIs include (but are not limited to) the gradient projection method
(

, the extragradient method
(

, and the proximal point algorithm

(

1.1
Stochastic VIs with dependent data

In this work, we operate in the latter stochastic setting where only inexact information about the operator
F
is available.
Specifically, we assume the existence of a stochastic oracle, which, when given any query point
x\in X
, generates a stochastic estimator
\widetilde{F}(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\xi\sim\pi
is a random variable defined in some probability space
\Xi
. The estimator satisfies the property that at any fixed point
x
, it is marginally unbiased, in that

\displaystyle F(x)={\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi)]=\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\widetilde{F}(x,\xi)d\pi(\xi).

(4)

A typical assumption in the stochastic optimization literature is that we have access to a stream of samples
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},...)
that are drawn i.i.d. from
\pi
. However, this assumption is violated in important real-world scenarios such as Markov decision processes (MDPs) and reinforcement learning (RL)
(
64
)
.
In such settings it is common to confront the more challenging
Markovian noise
setting, where
(\xi_{t}:t=1,2,\ldots)
is a Markov process defined on the state space
\Xi
, and
\pi
denotes the unique stationary distribution of this process.
Stochastic VIs with Markovian noise have recently garnered significant attention across applications in machine learning, statistics, optimization, and control, where data often exhibits serial correlations
(
23
;
62
;
4
;
41
)
. The key challenge in this setting is that sample correlations lead to
biased
estimators of the operator
F
. Concretely, letting
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by the history
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{t-1})
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\neq F(x)
. The conditional bias
{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-F(x)
introduces challenges for developing fast-converging algorithms.

Having said that, stochastic optimization with dependent data has seen significant study. While we cannot hope to cover this vast literature, we refer the reader to
18
and references therein for a discussion of classical developments.
Specifically relevant to VIs with Markovian noise is the literature on policy evaluation in reinforcement learning, which is a fundamental and extensively researched problem.
Here, the most popular algorithm is called temporal difference (TD) learning, and can be viewed as serial stochastic approximation with a suitably constructed VI. The algorithm was first introduced by
65
and theoretical analysis of TD learning has seen significant progress over the years.
66
provided one of the earliest results, proving the asymptotic convergence of TD learning with function approximation. Other classical results on asymptotic convergence include those by
8
and
9
, who used an ordinary differential equation (ODE) framework to analyze the long-term behavior of TD learning. More recently,
7
established finite-time performance guarantees for TD learning.
Other notable contributions include works by
63
;
13
;
19
;
42
;
36
;
68
.

Broadly speaking, guarantees in this literature decompose the error
\|x_{T}-x^{*}\|^{2}
at iteration
T
into two components: One component, which tracks the progress of the algorithm even in the absence of noise, is called the deterministic error and typically decays exponentially in
T
; there have been various methods proposed to accelerate the exponent in this convergence in terms of how it depends on the conditioning of the problem (see, e.g.,
37
and references therein). The other component, called the stochastic error, captures how stochasticity influences convergence—this is typically the slower error term in noisy settings and forms the focus of this paper. With this context in mind, let us more concretely describe two representative results in this literature in order to motivate our paper.

On the one hand, for the problem of solving (projected) linear fixed point equations—which is a special case of VIs with a linear operator—
45
recently analyzed the classical serial stochastic approximation algorithm on Markovian data with iterate averaging. They demonstrated a convergence rate of

\sigma^{2}
is a suitably defined noise level in the problem,
\bar{\tau}
is the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain and

is the total number of iterations; since the algorithm proceeds serially, this is also the total number of samples processed. On the other hand,
32
introduced the conditional temporal difference (CTD) algorithm for solving the stochastic VI problem with Markovian data. They tackled the problem of data correlation via
skipping

However, CTD requires prior knowledge of the Markov chain’s mixing properties to decide how many samples to skip per iteration; unfortunately, perfect knowledge of these properties is rarely available a priori in real-world applications. 1.2
Memory buffers and experience replay

In this paper, we make use of an additional resource that is typically available in practice and could be helpful in designing algorithms that converge quickly without knowledge of mixing properties, which is a
memory buffer
.
Specifically, suppose that we can store the samples in a buffer of size
B
, where we start the algorithm using the vector of previously collected samples

\displaystyle\xi^{B}=(\xi_{t})_{t=1}^{B},

(5)

and update this buffer as (and if) we gather more samples. Serial stochastic approximation algorithms such as TD simply process all samples in the buffer in causal order, while skipping-based algorithms such as CTD aim to process every
\overline{\tau}
samples in the buffer.
Can we instead strategically select samples from the buffer at each iteration of the algorithm? Note that since these samples are stored, the algorithm has the flexibility to process entries of the buffer in any order.

Memory buffers are commonly used in reinforcement learning (RL), and a very popular algorithm that arises from this paradigm is called experience replay (ER), which was initially introduced by
38
. Broadly speaking, most ER algorithms process the buffer by sampling data points uniformly at random,
and these techniques have gained widespread popularity for breaking temporal dependencies in data when training RL agents that gather samples on the fly
(
43
;
67
;
70
;
20
;
34
)
.
Despite its empirical success, the theoretical understanding of ER in iterative methods is still in its infancy
(
48
;
47
;
33
;
35
, see, e.g.)
. For example,
47
derived sample complexity bounds for Q-learning using the super-martingale structure of “reverse experience replay”.
33
proposed an algorithm using reverse experience memory to address linear system identification problems, while
35
provided regret bounds for regularized policy iteration with replay buffers in the context of average-reward MDPs. Additionally,
16
offered insights into the stochastic properties of the replay buffer, establishing the asymptotic convergence of actor-critic algorithms in RL.

While ER has been largely confined to settings in RL, these ideas have not been more broadly adopted in stochastic optimization. Furthermore, the question of
how
to optimally sample entries of the buffer in order to achieve optimal rates of convergence has not been thoroughly investigated to our knowledge. This paper aims to fill this important gap in the context of developing VI algorithms, and to apply these insights to specific examples of VIs (including in RL).

1.3
Contributions and organization

Our main contribution is to propose and analyze a new algorithm, which we call
multiscale experience replay
(MER), for stochastic optimization in VIs with Markovian data when we have access to a memory buffer.
In more detail:

1.

Our algorithm involves a careful sampling scheme to select entries of the memory buffer with respect to which algorithmic updates are performed. Specifically, the algorithm operates in epochs, and within each epoch, the iterates are updated using samples separated by a replay gap that decreases over epochs. Importantly, the implementation of this scheme does not require any information about the mixing time of the Markov chain. Under standard assumptions, we prove iteration complexity bounds on the algorithm that show acceleration comparable to skipping samples optimally, as in the CTD algorithm.

2.

In addition to establishing upper bounds on the error, we also show that in its initial epochs, our algorithm
emulates
the behavior of the analogous iteration run on i.i.d. data provided the chain mixes in
1
-Wasserstein distance and the stochastic operator is appropriately regular. While it has been informally argued that experience replay enjoys such an advantage, we rigorously establish a two-sided bound showing that the error of our algorithm is tracked by its analog run on i.i.d. samples.

3.

Finally, we apply MER to generalized linear models as well as the policy evaluation problem in RL. For both problems, we show convergence rates that are comparable to state of the art guarantees but now with an algorithm that is agnostic to the mixing time of the chain. Analogous results regarding emulation of the i.i.d. setting are also established.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide some illustrative examples of VIs that we focus on in the paper. Section
3
introduces the MER algorithm for VIs. In Section
4
, we present our assumptions and main results in the general setting. In Section
5
, we discuss consequences of these results for our illustrative examples. In Section
6
, we present numerical experiments that corroborate our theoretical results, and Section
7
collects proofs of all our claims. We conclude the paper with a discussion of open problems in Section
8
.

Recall from our earlier set-up that we are interested in finding a point
x^{*}
in a closed convex feasible region
X

2.1
Generalized linear models (GLMs)

Consider the following generalized linear regression model — also called the single-index model —
where we have an unknown signal
x^{*}\in X
and observations are generated according to

\displaystyle y_{t}=f(a_{t}^{\top}x^{*})+v_{t}.

(6)

Here
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the
t
-th sample of covariates,
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the corresponding response, and the scalar
v_{t}
denotes unobserved zero-mean i.i.d. noise such that
{\mathbb{E}}[v_{t}^{2}]=\sigma_{v}^{2}
. Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz continuous and strongly monotone link function satisfying, for all
x,y
in the domain of
f
, the pair of inequalities
|f(x)-f(y)|/|x-y|\leq L_{f}
and
(f(x)-f(y))(x-y)\geq\mu_{f}(x-y)^{2}
. Monotone GLMs model several applications spanning data analysis and signal recovery
(
28
;
39
;
55
;
24
;
71
;
22
, see, e.g.)
.

Now suppose that the covariates
(a_{t})_{t=1}^{B}
are generated from an ergodic Markov chain with finite mixing time such that the
a_{t}
’s remain bounded almost surely, i.e.,
\|a_{t}\|\leq D_{a}
a.s. for all
t
. Moreover, suppose that
a_{1}
is drawn from the stationary distribution
\pi
of the chain, and let
\Lambda:={\mathbb{E}}[a_{t}a_{t}^{\top}]
denote the stationary covariance satisfying
\Lambda\succcurlyeq\kappa I
for some
\kappa>0
.

For each sample covariate
a_{t}
, the corresponding observation
y_{t}
is obtained using the GLM model from Eq. (
6
). To pose the signal recovery problem in our framework, note that the
t
-th sample is given by the tuple
\xi_{t}=(a_{t},y_{t})
, and our Markovian buffer collects
B
samples
(a_{t},y_{t})_{t=1}^{B}
. Our goal is to estimate the underlying signal
x^{*}
using samples from this buffer.
In order to solve for
x^{*}
, we formulate a stochastic VI problem with the operators defined as

\displaystyle F(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[af(a^{T}x)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[af(a^{T}x^{*})\right]\text{ and }\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t})=a_{t}f(a_{t}^{T}x)-a_{t}y_{t}.

(7)

By definition, note that

F(x^{*})=0
, which implies that the VI condition in Ineq. (
1
) is satisfied.

Verifying conditions on operator
F
.

Note that

\displaystyle\|F(x)-F(y)\|

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[af(a^{T}x)-af(a^{T}y)\right]\|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[\|a\||f(a^{T}x)-f(a^{T}y)|\right]

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}L_{f}{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[\|a\|\|a^{T}(x-y)\|\right]

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(ii)}}{\leq}L_{f}{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[\|a\|^{2}\right]\|x-y\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}\cdot D_{a}^{2}\cdot\|x-y\|,

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows from the Lipschitzness of the link function
f
and step
{\sf(ii)}
follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Thus, the operator
F
is Lipschitz with parameter
L_{f}\cdot D_{a}^{2}
.

We also have

\displaystyle\langle F(x),x-x^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[\langle af(a^{T}x)-af(a^{T}x^{*}),x-x^{*}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[(f(a^{T}x)-f(a^{T}x^{*})a^{T}(x-x^{*}))\right]

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\geq}\mu_{f}{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}\left[(a^{T}(x-x^{*}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mu_{f}(x-x^{*})^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim\pi}[aa^{T}](x-x^{*})\overset{{\sf(ii)}}{\geq}\mu_{f}\cdot\kappa\cdot\|x-x^{*}\|^{2},

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows from the strong monotonicity of
f
and step
{\sf(ii)}
follows from the assumption
\Lambda\succcurlyeq\kappa I
. Thus, the operator
F
is generalized strongly monotone with parameter
\mu_{f}\cdot\kappa
.

2.2
Policy evaluation in reinforcement learning

Policy evaluation is formulated using a Markov reward process (MRP), described by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},P,R,\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the state space,
P
is the transition matrix,
R
is the reward function and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor; see, e.g.,
58
;
6
for more details and background. At each time step, the system moves from the current state
s\in\mathcal{S}
to some new state
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
with probability
P(s,s^{\prime})
while the agent receives a reward of
R(s,s^{\prime})
corresponding to this transition. The expected instantaneous reward for state
s
is defined as
r(s)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}R(s,s^{\prime})P(s,s^{\prime})
. The value function of a state is defined as the infinite-horizon discounted reward received starting in that particular state

\displaystyle v^{*}(s)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},s_{t+1})|s_{0}=s\right],

and the goal of policy evaluation is to solve for
v^{*}
.
Letting
D=|\mathcal{S}|
denote the number of states, the value function
v^{*}
and the reward function
r
are
D
-dimensional vectors of reals, and the following Bellman equation is satisfied

\displaystyle v^{*}=r+\gamma Pv^{*}.

(8)

We assume that the Markov chain is ergodic, thus there is a unique stationary distribution
\pi
with strictly positive entries satisfying
\pi P=\pi
.
Let
\Pi:=\text{diag}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},...,\pi_{D})\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
.

In modern variants of this problem with large state spaces,
it is common to seek approximate solutions for the Bellman Eq. (
8
). Specifically, we choose a
d
-dimensional subspace, defined as
\mathbb{S}:=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{d}\}
for
d
linearly independent basis vectors and attempt to solve for
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{S}
through the

projected
Bellman equation, which can be written in matrix-vector notation as follows.

Defining the matrix
\Psi:=[\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{d}]^{T}
, the equation reads

\displaystyle\Psi\Pi(\bar{v}-r-\gamma P\bar{v})=0,

and since
\bar{v}:=\Psi^{T}\bar{\theta}
for some
\overline{\theta}
, the projected Bellman equation can be equivalently written in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle\Psi\Pi\Psi^{T}\bar{\theta}=\Psi\Pi r+\Psi\Pi\gamma P\Psi^{T}\bar{\theta}.

(9)

For simplicity, we define a set of orthonormal basis vectors for the subspace
\mathbb{S}
, i.e.,
\Phi\equiv[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}]:=Q^{-1/2}\Psi
where
Q_{i,j}:=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle
. Denote
\lambda_{\max}(Q)
and
\lambda_{\min}(Q)
as the maximum and the minimum eigenvalues of the matrix
Q
, respectively. We can solve the projected Bellman Eq. (
9
) by viewing it as a VI with the following operators:

\displaystyle F(\theta)=\Psi\Pi(\Psi^{T}\theta-r-\gamma P\Psi^{T}\theta)\text{ and }\widetilde{F}(\theta,\xi_{k})=\left(\left\langle\psi(s_{k}),\theta\right\rangle-R(s_{k},s_{k+1})-\gamma\left\langle\psi(s_{k+1}),\theta\right\rangle\right)\psi(s_{k}).

From the definition of the operator
F
above, we observe that for
\bar{\theta}
, which is the solution to the projected Bellman Eq. (
9
),
F(\bar{\theta})=0
. This means that the VI condition in Ineq. (
1
) is satisfied for
\bar{\theta}
.

In the Markovian setting, we are given a trajectory from the Markov reward process

(s_{t},s_{t+1},R(s_{t},s_{t+1}))_{t\geq 0}
, where
s_{0}
is drawn from the stationary distribution.
From these, we form a Markovian buffer containing samples

\displaystyle\xi^{B}=(\xi_{t}=(\psi(s_{t}),\psi(s_{t+1}),R(s_{t},s_{t+1})))_{t=1}^{B}.

(10)

Our goal is to estimate
\bar{\theta}
using samples from the Markovian buffer.

Verifying conditions on operator
F
.

It can be readily verified that
F
is a
(1+\gamma)\cdot\lambda_{max}(Q)
-Lipschitz continuous and
(1-\gamma)\cdot\lambda_{min}(Q)
-strongly monotone operator
(
37
, see, e.g.)
.

3
Multiscale experience replay

Having provided examples that motivate our work, we now introduce our main algorithm for solving stochastic VIs with a Markovian buffer.
As a stepping stone to our procedure, recall that standard stochastic approximation (SA) with sample-skipping (termed CTD in
32
) is given by

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\eta\langle\widetilde{F}(x_{t},\xi_{t\bar{\tau}}),x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}\right\},\text{ for }t=1,2,\ldots.

(11)

This update uses samples separated by
\bar{\tau}
steps, a value that should ideally be chosen proportional to the mixing time of the Markov chain. However, as previously mentioned and illustrated in Figure
1
, misspecifying the skipping parameter
\bar{\tau}
can lead to both statistical and computational inefficiencies.

To resolve this issue, we propose the following multiscale experience replay (MER) algorithm.
Given a memory buffer of size
B
, the MER algorithm judiciously chooses elements of this buffer by keeping the “active” samples used in the SA updates as far apart as possible. The method is formally presented as Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Multiscale Experience Replay

Input
: Memory Buffer
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{B}\}
, Total number of epochs
K
.

for

k=1,\ldots,K

do

Re-initialize the variable
{x_{1}^{(k)}}\in X
independently of the past.

Define the replay gap
\tau_{k}=\frac{B}{2^{k}}
and the sample size used in
k
-th epoch
T_{k}=2^{k}
.

for

t=1,\ldots,T_{k}

do

Using the sample
\xi_{t\tau_{k}}
, perform one step of SA:

\displaystyle{x_{t+1}^{(k)}}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\eta_{k}\langle\widetilde{F}({x_{t}^{(k)}},\xi_{t\tau_{k}}),x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x-{x_{t}^{(k)}}\|^{2}.

(12)

Delete the sample
\xi_{t\tau_{k}}
and append the new sample to the end of the buffer.

end

for

end

for

Operationally, the memory buffer is utilized over multiple passes (epochs), where we re-initialize the iterate at the beginning of each epoch. In epoch
k
, the replay gap, defined as
\tau_{k}=B/2^{k}
, sets the separation between samples used to update the iterate within that epoch. The total number of samples used in the
k
-th epoch is thus
T_{k}=2^{k}
.
The replay gap
\tau_{k}
progressively decreases the scale at which the algorithm operates. If
\tau_{k}=\bar{\tau}
for some
k
, then the algorithm executes the skipped SA update (
11
) in that epoch. This multiscale process continues until the final epoch recovers the standard serial SA update, which proceeds without any sample skipping.

Note that as stated, Algorithm
1
is designed for the online setting where we continue to observe new samples
\xi_{B+1},\xi_{B+2},\ldots
as we run iterations and wish to store them in the buffer. At each iteration, the algorithm processes the current sample and removes it from the buffer, appending a newly observed sample to the end of the buffer. This algorithm can be easily adapted to an offline setting where no new samples are available by omitting the step that deletes the current sample. In this case, the buffer remains static, allowing the same data points to be reused across multiple epochs. All our results also extend to the static setting,

4
Theoretical results

In this section, we present convergence guarantees for the MER method proposed in Algorithm
1
.
We start by introducing and discussing the assumptions that underlie our analysis.

4.1
Assumptions and shorthand notation

We begin by making a mild assumption regarding the optimal solution
x^{*}
.

Assumption 1
.

The optimal solution
x^{*}
lies in an
\ell_{2}
ball of known radius
D/2
, i.e.
x^{*}\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(D/2)
. Thus, we initialize iterates at the beginning of each epoch within this ball independently of the past, so that
{x_{1}^{(k)}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(D/2)
.

We now make the following mild assumptions regarding the stochastic operator
\widetilde{F}
, and we will verify in Section
5
that these assumptions hold in the two specific examples that we introduced in Section
2
.

Assumption 2
.

The stochastic operator
\widetilde{F}
is a Lipschitz continuous map in its first argument, i.e., there exists a
\widetilde{L}_{1}>0
, such that for any
\xi\in\Xi
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{F}(x_{1},\xi)-\widetilde{F}(x_{2},\xi)\|\leq\widetilde{L}_{1}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|.

(13)

Assumption 3

(Variance of stochastic operator)
.

There exist positive scalars
\sigma,\zeta\geq 0
such that for every point
x\in X
and pair of natural numbers
t^{\prime}\geq t
, we have with probability
1
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\big\|\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t^{\prime}})-{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t^{\prime}})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big\|^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}.

(14)

To motivate our next two assumptions, note that if
\xi_{t}
is drawn from the stationary distribution independently of
x
, then the stochastic operator
\widetilde{F}
is an unbiased estimator of
F
, satisfying
{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t^{\prime}})]=F(x)
. However, since the noise is Markovian in our setting, the stochastic operator is biased conditionally on the past, i.e., for
t^{\prime}\geq t
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t^{\prime}})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\neq F(x)
.
Our next two assumptions ensure that the Markov chain mixes fast enough so that the bias at the optimal point
x^{*}
can be properly controlled, and the conditional bias of the difference of two operator evaluations is also controlled.

Assumption 4

(Bias at the optimal solution)
.

There exist constants
C_{M}>0
and
\rho_{1}\in(0,1)
such that for every pair of natural numbers
t^{\prime}\geq t
, the following inequality holds with probability 1:

\displaystyle\|F(x^{*})-{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x^{*},\xi_{t^{\prime}})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\|\leq C_{M}\cdot\rho_{1}^{t^{\prime}-t}.

(15)

Assumption 5

(Bias of difference)
.

There exist constants
C_{B}>0
and
\rho_{2}\in(0,1)
such that for every pair of points
x,y\in X
and pair of natural numbers
t^{\prime}\geq t
, the following holds with probability
1
:

\displaystyle\|F(x)-{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{t^{\prime}})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-F(y)+{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{F}(y,\xi_{t^{\prime}})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\|\leq C_{B}\cdot\rho_{2}^{t^{\prime}-t}\cdot\|x-y\|.

(16)

Given their close connection to the mixing properties of the Markov chain, we refer to
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
in the above assumptions as
mixing parameters
.
We define
\rho\coloneqq\max\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}\in(0,1)
, so that both Assumption
4
and Assumption
5
are satisfied with the common mixing parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
. We also define

\displaystyle C\coloneqq\frac{C_{M}}{40}+C_{B},

(17)

that combines the multiplicative mixing constants from Assumption
4
and Assumption
5
.

Having stated our assumptions, we proceed to define some additional notation to facilitate the presentation of our main theorem and subsequent discussions.
We start by defining the following time scale parameter
\tau_{M}
, which characterizes the number of time steps needed for the bias of the operator
\widetilde{F}
to be sufficiently reduced,

\displaystyle\tau_{M}\coloneqq\frac{\log({18C}/{\mu})}{\log({1}/{\rho})}.

(18)

This parameter is related to how fast the Markov chain mixes, and also involves the strong monotonicity scalar
\mu
. Throughout the paper, we refer to
\tau_{M}
as the “effective mixing time”.
We also define

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\coloneqq\frac{\tau_{M}}{\tau_{k}}.

(19)

Note that the parameter
\alpha_{k}
evolves with
k
monotonically. To develop intuition, it is useful to divide this evolution into the following two stages:

•

(
\tau_{k}>\tau_{M}
) When the replay gap is greater than the effective mixing time, we have
\alpha_{k}<1
. Roughly speaking, the samples used in such an epoch
k
should be thought of as nearly i.i.d. due to the mixing conditions in Assumption
4
and Assumption
5
.

•

(
\tau_{k}\leq\tau_{M}
) When the replay gap is less than the effective mixing time, we have
\alpha_{k}\geq 1
. The samples in this case are inevitably correlated, and this correlation becomes stronger as
\tau_{k}
decreases.

Therefore, the parameter
\alpha_{k}
encapsulates the shift from (almost) independent to correlated samples as the epochs advance.

To define our final pieces of notation, recall the parameter
D
from Assumption
1
and define

\displaystyle\bar{L}\coloneqq L+\widetilde{L}_{1},\;M\coloneqq\max\left\{2+\frac{4C}{\mu},\frac{40C_{M}}{\mu}\right\}

\displaystyle p_{k}\coloneqq 1+(\log T_{k})^{-1}\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mu^{2}M\max\{1,D^{2}\}}{3(6\sigma^{2}+4\|F(x^{*})\|^{2})}\right).

(20)

These parameters will simplify notation in our theorem statements. Operationally, the parameter
\bar{L}
combines the Lipschitz constants of the operators
F
and
\widetilde{F}
.

4.2
Convergence guarantees for MER

With the assumptions stated and parameters defined in Section
4.1
, we are in a position to provide convergence guarantees for the MER method.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
5
hold. If the buffer size satisfies the lower bound

B\geq\frac{32p_{1}\tau_{M}(\zeta^{2}+16\bar{L}^{2})}{3\mu^{2}}\log\left(\frac{32p_{1}\tau_{M}(\zeta^{2}+16\bar{L}^{2})}{3\mu^{2}}\right)
and we have the step-size schedule

\displaystyle\eta_{k}\coloneqq\min\left\{\frac{3\mu}{16(\zeta^{2}+16\bar{L}^{2})},\frac{p_{k}\log T_{k}}{\mu T_{k}}\right\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,K,

(21)

then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|{x_{T_{k}+1}^{(k)}}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq M\left(1+\frac{3\mu^{2}}{8\left(\alpha_{k}+1\right)(\zeta^{2}+16\bar{L}^{2})}\right)^{-T_{k}}\left(D^{2}+1\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{20C_{M}\rho^{\tau_{M}+\tau_{k}-1}}{\mu}\left(1+\frac{3}{128\left(\alpha_{k}+1\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{3(2+p_{k}\log T_{k})}{\mu^{2}T_{k}}\left(\alpha_{k}+1\right)(6\sigma^{2}+25C_{M}^{2}\rho^{2(\tau_{k}-1)}+4\|F(x^{*})\|^{2}).

(22)

See Section
7.2
for the detailed proof of this theorem.

A few remarks are in order. First, note that in order to set the step-size schedule in Eq. (
21
), we require access to some parameters
2
2

2

There is some dependence on the pair
(C,C_{M})
, but these quantities are not as critical for mixing as
\rho
and
\tau_{M}
.
that depend on the population operator
F
, but crucially not on
\rho
or
\tau_{M}
, which define the mixing properties of the chain. This stands in stark contrast to other algorithms in this literature

, and reveals a key advantage of having access to a memory buffer as in our setting.

Let us next discuss the error terms in the guarantee (
22
). The first term on the RHS of Ineq. (
22
), which converges linearly, corresponds to the deterministic error of the MER method – this term would exist even in the absence of stochasticity in the problem. It is likely that the dependence on the condition number
\bar{L}/\mu
in this term can be improved by applying the operator extrapolation technique proposed in
31
;
32
; as mentioned before, obtaining optimal accelerated convergence of the deterministic error is beyond the scope of our paper.
The second term on the RHS of Ineq. (

) is due to the bias at the optimal point
x^{*}
.
This term is typically negligible since it decays exponentially with
\tau_{M}
.

As mentioned earlier, our focus is on the last term in the RHS of Ineq. (

), which is a bound on the stochastic error. Specifically,
when

is at least of the order
T_{k}\tau_{M}

this stochastic error term simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{3(2+p\log T_{k})}{\mu^{2}T_{k}}\left(6\sigma^{2}+4\|F(x^{*})\|^{2}\right)\right),

Conversely, when
\alpha_{k}\gg 1

\alpha_{k}
, depending on the ratio of the effective mixing time and the replay gap. However, note that we always have
\alpha_{k}\leq\tau_{M}

, MER outperforms the standard Markovian SA in

\tau_{k}

in the last epoch when the replay gap satisfies
\tau_{k}=1
.

4.3
Tracking the i.i.d. error

In the previous section, we demonstrated that when the replay gap
\tau_{k}
is larger than the effective mixing time
\tau_{M}
, the MER algorithm has a rate of convergence resembling that of i.i.d. sampling with
T_{k}
samples. While encouraging, this is simply a comparison of upper bounds and leaves open the question of whether the i.i.d. behavior is actually emulated by MER.
In this section, we analyze if and how the error of the MER algorithm — especially in the initial epochs — tracks the error of running simple SA on an i.i.d. sample sequence.

Before stating such a guarantee, we require some additional definitions and assumptions. Begin by defining the Wasserstein distance on a metric space,
(\Xi,r)
. For a pair
(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
of probability distributions on
\Xi
, let
\mathcal{P}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
denote the space for all possible couplings of
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
. For any
q\geq 1
, the Wasserstein-
q
distance between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{q,r}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\left\{\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}r(x,y)^{q}d\gamma(x,y)\right\}^{1/q}.

(24)

We take the metric
r
to be the Euclidean distance throughout this paper, unless stated otherwise.
We now make the assumption that the chain mixes fast in Wasserstein distance.

Assumption 6

(Wasserstein mixing)
.

With the effective mixing time
\tau_{M}
defined in Eq. (
18
), for any
x,y\in X
, there exists universal positive constant
c_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(\delta_{x}P^{\tau_{M}},\delta_{y}P^{\tau_{M}})\leq c_{0}\|x-y\|,

(25)

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the distribution that places all its mass on
x
and recall that
P
is the transition matrix of the Markov chain.

We also require an additional Lipschitzness assumption on the second argument of the stochastic operator
\widetilde{F}
.

Assumption 7
.

The stochastic operator
\widetilde{F}
is a Lipschitz continuous map in its second argument, i.e., there exists a positive constant
\widetilde{L}_{2}
, such that for any
x\in X
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{1})-\widetilde{F}(x,\xi_{2})\|\leq\widetilde{L}_{2}\|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\|.

(26)

In the initial epochs, the replay gap
\tau_{k}
is large. Concretely, to state our guarantee below, suppose that for some
\beta>1
we have
\tau_{k}=\beta\tau_{M}
.
Let
x_{T+1}
denote the iterate obtained after applying
T
-steps of the MER update of Eq. (
12
) at epoch
k
, starting from some initialization
x_{1}
. In particular, conditional on
x_{1}
, the randomness in
x_{T+1}
arises purely from the Markovian samples
(\xi_{k\beta\tau_{M}})_{k=1}^{T}
used in the updates. To compare this iterate with an i.i.d. analogue, define
\widetilde{x}_{T+1}
as the iterate obtained by starting from the same initialization
x_{1}
and applying
T
-steps of the update

\widetilde{x}_{t+1}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\eta\langle\widetilde{F}(\widetilde{x}_{t},\widetilde{\xi}_{t\beta\tau_{M}}),x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{x}_{t}-x\|^{2}\right\},\text{ for }t=1,2,\ldots,

where
(\widetilde{\xi}_{k\beta\tau_{M}})_{k=1}^{T}
denotes an i.i.d. sequence of samples drawn from the stationary distribution
\pi
. Let us define the errors in the two cases as
\Delta_{T+1}\coloneqq x_{T+1}-x^{*}
and
\widetilde{\Delta}_{T+1}\coloneqq\widetilde{x}_{T+1}-x^{*}
, respectively.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
,
6
and
7
hold. If the step-size parameter is chosen to satisfy
\eta\leq\frac{1}{\widetilde{L}_{1}T}
, then

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\|\Delta_{T+1}\|-{\mathbb{E}}\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{T+1}\|\right|\leq 3c_{0}\cdot\frac{\widetilde{L}_{2}}{\widetilde{L}_{1}}\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot e^{-\beta}.

(27)

Consequently, if
\beta=\log T^{3/2}
, then the difference between the algorithmic errors is lower order and satisfies

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\|\Delta_{T+1}\|-{\mathbb{E}}\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{T+1}\|\right|\leq\frac{3c_{0}\widetilde{L}_{2}}{T\widetilde{L}_{1}}.

(28)

See Section
7.4
for a proof of this theorem.

Qualitatively, Theorem
2
demonstrates that when the replay gap
\tau_{k}
is sufficiently large (specifically, when
\beta
is large), the error of our MER algorithm on a Markovian data buffer closely resembles the error of standard SA on an i.i.d. sample sequence. This finding directly confirms the core benefit sought by any experience replay strategy—it is able to emulate i.i.d. behavior even with dependent data. As a consequence, as long as the SA algorithm on an i.i.d. sequence achieves a faster convergence rate than the worst-case Markovian bound, the MER algorithm will replicate this faster rate provided the epoch number
k
is not too large.

5
Consequences for specific examples

Having established our general results, we now specialize them to the examples of GLMs and policy evaluation discussed in Section
2
. In these examples, it is common to assume that the Markov chain mixes in total variation (TV) distance. The TV distance between a pair
(\mu^{\prime},\mu)
of probability distributions on
\Xi
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}|d\mu(\xi)-d\mu^{\prime}(\xi)|.

(29)

For an ergodic Markov chain with unique stationary distribution
\pi
, we denote the conditional distribution of the random variable
\xi_{t^{\prime}}\in\Xi
at time step
t^{\prime}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
as
P_{t-1}^{t^{\prime}}
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{t}
. A commonly used TV mixing assumption is the following:

Assumption 8
.

For
t^{\prime}\geq t
, there exists positive constants
C_{TV}
and
\rho_{TV}\in(0,1)
such that we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,P_{t-1}^{t^{\prime}})\leq C_{TV}\cdot\rho_{TV}^{t^{\prime}-t}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{w.p. }1.

5.1
Generalized linear models

Revisiting the GLM example from Section
2.1
, our objective is to estimate the underlying signal
x^{*}
using the given Markovian buffer
\xi^{B}
. Theorem
1
yields the following corollary for the GLM model.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. In addition assume that the Markov chain generating observations
(a_{t},y_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is geometrically mixing in the total variation metric so that it satisfies Assumption
8
. Choosing step-sizes prescribed by Theorem
1
,
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|{x_{T_{k}+1}^{(k)}}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq M\left(1+\frac{3\mu_{f}^{2}\kappa^{2}}{\left(\alpha_{k}+1\right)8(\zeta^{2}+64L_{f}^{2}D_{a}^{4})}\right)^{-T_{k}}\left(D^{2}+1\right)+\frac{18(2+p_{k}\log T_{k})}{\mu_{f}^{2}\kappa^{2}T_{k}}\left(\alpha_{k}+1\right)\sigma^{2}.

Here
\sigma^{2}=6D_{a}^{2}\sigma_{v}^{2}
and
\zeta^{2}=12L_{f}^{2}D_{a}^{4}
.

We prove Corollary
1
in Section
7.5
.

Comparing with prior results, we note that the deterministic error term in the bound converges at a linear rate, which matches the lower bound established in
48
[Theorem 1]. The dependence on the effective mixing time
\tau_{M}
(through
\alpha_{k}
) is information-theoretically optimal for this term.
In terms of dependence on the dimension of the problem
d
, we observe that the parameter
\sigma^{2}
typically scales linearly with

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d\sigma_{d}^{2}}{T_{k}}\left(\frac{\tau_{M}}{\tau_{k}}+1\right)\right),

where

represents the dimension-independent noise level of the problem
(c.f.
48
, Theorem 3)
.
Also, Corollary
1
implies that to find an
\epsilon

k

{\mathbb{E}}\|{x_{T_{k}+1}^{(k)}}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon

\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left(\frac{(\alpha_{k}+1)\bar{L}^{2}}{\mu_{f}^{2}}\log\frac{(D^{2}+1)}{\epsilon},\frac{\sigma^{2}(\alpha_{k}+1)}{\epsilon\mu_{f}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right).

This iteration complexity bound is nearly optimal, up to a logarithmic factor, in terms of dependence on
\epsilon

(
32
)
.

We now turn to stating how well the MER algorithm tracks the i.i.d. error for the GLM model. We persist with the previous notation:
\Delta_{T+1}
denotes the error of running
T
steps of the MER algorithm on Markovian samples with replay gap
\tau_{k}=\beta\cdot\tau_{M}
for
\beta=\log T^{3/2}

denotes the error of running
T
steps of standard SA on i.i.d. samples.

We additionally assume that
X
has an
\ell_{2}
diameter of at most
D_{x}
and we have a bounded observation space, i.e.,

(30)

Suppose that assumptions stated above hold. Additionally, suppose that the Markov chain generating observations

6
Numerical results

In this section, we report numerical experiments for the MER algorithm, comparing its performance to standard serial SA and SA with sample-skipping. We consider both the logistic regression problem and the policy evaluation problem. We also include results for the SA algorithm on i.i.d. data to assess how well each method can emulate the i.i.d. setting.

6.1
Policy evaluation with Markovian noise

We first consider the policy evaluation problem, where we compare the MER algorithm against TD and CTD, which are the analogs of SA for this setting without and with skipping, respectively.
We consider Markov reward processes with a parameter
m
that controls the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain, while the discount factor is fixed at
\gamma=0.8
for all runs. Given a state space
\mathcal{S}

1

Feature vectors are defined as

\phi(s)=\mathbf{e}_{s\wedge d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\mathbf{e}_{i}

|\mathcal{S}|=30
and

\frac{\|\overline{\theta}_{T_{k}}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}

\overline{\theta}_{T_{k}}

\theta^{*}
is the optimal solution, and

is the initialization.

7
Proofs

In this section, we present proofs of all our main results.

We note that while the MER algorithm utilizes a dynamic buffer where samples are deleted and appended (as seen in Algorithm
1
), the theoretical convergence analysis for a static buffer case extends directly to this setting. The key justification lies in the
epoch-wise re-initialization
of the iterate
x_{1}^{(k)}
. At the start of each epoch
k
, the variable is initialized independently of its previous trajectory. This mechanism ensures that for the duration of the
k
-th epoch, the algorithm effectively operates on a fixed snapshot of the memory buffer. In the dynamic buffer, the samples used are even further apart, thus the performance guarantees derived under the static buffer assumption hold for each epoch individually. Consequently, the convergence guarantees established herein for the static case remain globally valid for the dynamic buffer implementation of Algorithm
1
.

in policy evaluation is Lipschitz in its second argument with the Lipschitz constant
\widetilde{L}_{2}=\sqrt{3}\left((2+\gamma)\cdot D_{\psi}\cdot D_{\theta}+\bar{R}\right)
.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
6
and thus of Corollary
4
.

8
Discussion

We introduced the MER algorithm, a novel approach for solving stochastic VIs with Markovian data when we have access to a memory buffer.
We provided an upper bound on its stochastic error, showing a faster decay than the serial method based on vanilla stochastic approximation. In addition, we provided a two-sided expectation error bound with respect to the i.i.d. setting to demonstrate that MER effectively mitigates the challenges posed by data dependence, allowing the algorithm to mimic and closely track the performance of stochastic approximation methods designed for i.i.d. samples. A key advantage of our approach is that it achieves these properties without requiring prior knowledge of the Markov chain’s mixing time, a common but often restrictive requirement in related work. We also provided numerical results to corroborate the effectiveness of the MER algorithm in solving practical problems such as signal estimation in GLMs and policy evaluation in RL. More broadly, the MER algorithm can be viewed as a decorrelation device that provides theoretical justification for the experience replay heuristic. We believe this device could be leveraged to solve broader classes of problems involving dependent data.

Our work suggests several salient directions for future research, and we mention some concrete technical questions below. First, the current MER algorithm only achieves a suboptimal deterministic error. It would be interesting to incorporate acceleration techniques for variational inequalities (e.g.,
31
;
32
) to match the deterministic error lower bound
(
52
)
. Second, though order-optimal, the stochastic error of the MER method can be potentially sharpened beyond worst-case guarantees. For example, instance-dependent lower bounds are known for solving policy evaluation and more general fixed-point equations
(
37
;
46
)
, and these lower bounds have been achieved by stochastic approximation methods with variance reduction
(
37
)
as well as sample average approximation
(
57
)
. An interesting direction for future work is to develop instance-dependent information-theoretic lower bounds for the Markovian setting with a memory buffer, and to design efficient MER-type algorithms that achieve these bounds.
