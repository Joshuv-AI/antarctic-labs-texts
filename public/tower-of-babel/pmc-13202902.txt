Corneal biomechanical parameters as novel predictors of refractive stability after small incision lenticule extraction
Authors: Liulan He, Yalan Zhang, Yongfeng Miao, Xiuhong Tang, Junjie Wang, Xiaohua Wang, Xin Zhang, Lvfu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the predictive value of preoperative corneal biomechanical parameters for refractive stability after small-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) and to develop a multivariate model integrating biomechanical and anatomical factors for clinical application.MethodsThis prospective study analyzed 200 eyes from 100 patients undergoing SMILE. Preoperative and 6-month postoperative parameters were recorded, including diopter (D) of spherical and cylindrical refraction, intraocular pressure, slit-lamp examination, scanning laser ophthalmoscope, corneal topographic map, optical coherence tomography, corneal biomechanics and Pentacam imaging. Refractive stability was defined as spherical equivalent (SE) within ± 0.50 D at 6 months. Multivariate logistic regression with generalized estimating equations (GEE) was used to identify predictors of instability, accounting for inter-eye correlation. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and validated with bootstrapping. Statistical comparisons of area under the curve (AUC) were performed using the DeLong test.ResultsMean preoperative SE was − 4.83 ± 1.84 D. At 6 months postoperatively, 75.5% of eyes achieved SE within ± 0.50 D, 24.5% were unstable. In ROC analysis, preoperative SE exhibited the highest predictive value among the parameters assessed (AUC = 0.78, P < 0.01), with an optimal cutoff value of − 5.25 D (sensitivity 72.3%, specificity 75.6%). Multivariate GEE logistic regression identified SE (OR = 1.52 per diopter, P < 0.01), axial length (OR = 1.36 per mm, P < 0.01), corneal biomechanical index (CBI) > 0.05 (OR = 2.39, P < 0.01), younger age (OR = 1.28 per decade decrease, P = 0.042), and lenticule thickness > 110 μm (OR = 1.97, P < 0.01) as independent risk factors. The full multivariate model achieved an AUC of 0.88 (accuracy 83.5%, sensitivity 84.7%, specificity 82.8%), significantly outperforming the SE-only model (DeLong test, P = 0.02). Bootstrap validation yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.85. Incorporating biomechanical profiling could refine preoperative risk stratification and guide surgical planning, such as adjusting lenticule thickness or considering alternative procedures in high-risk patients.ConclusionCorneal biomechanical properties, particularly CBI, are significant adjunctive predictors of post-SMILE refractive stability. Introduction

Small Incision Lenticule Extraction (SMILE) has gained prominence as a flapless, minimally invasive refractive surgery with proven efficacy in myopia correction [1]. However, postoperative refractive instability remains a concern in 5–10% of cases, potentially impacting visual outcomes [2].

While corneal topography and pachymetry are established preoperative screening tools for detecting corneal abnormalities such as keratoconus and ectasia risk [3–5], emerging evidence suggests corneal biomechanics play a crucial role in surgical response [6]. Recent studies have further underscored the importance of biomechanical assessments in refractive surgery planning [7–11]. The Corvis ST provides dynamic biomechanical parameters including the Corneal Biomechanical Index (CBI), tomographic biomechanical index (TBI), and deformation amplitude (DA) ratio [12]. These metrics quantify corneal resistance to deformation, yet their predictive value for long-term refractive stability after SMILE remains underexplored.

Prior studies focused primarily on immediate postoperative outcomes or ectasia risk [13]. Reinstein et al. demonstrated TBI’s utility in detecting forme fruste keratoconus [14], but its relationship with refractive regression lacks investigation. The Pentacam Random Forest Index, a topographic-biomechanical composite, shows promise in risk stratification but requires validation for stability prediction [5].

This study aims to address these gaps by evaluating the predictive value of preoperative corneal biomechanical parameters for refractive stability after SMILE and thereby developing a predictive model integrating biomechanical properties and anatomical features.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

This prospective case series included 200 eyes that underwent SMILE procedures (VisuMax 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) at the Department of Ophthalmology, the Third Hospital of Mianyang, between January 2022 and June 2024. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced ophthalmologist using standardized techniques, with follow-up of six months. For patients undergoing bilateral SMILE, the two eyes were performed by the same physician on the same day. Inclusion criteria included patients with myopia (-1.00 to -10.00 D), preoperative corrected distant visual acuity (CDVA) of 20/20, and completed 6-month follow-up. Exclusion criteria included prior ocular surgery or connective tissue disorders. This study followed the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Mianyang. Informed consent was obtained from the participants before participating in this study.

A sample size calculation was performed a priori based on a pilot study: to detect an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of 0.78 for preoperative SE (primary predictor) with 90% power and a two-sided α of 0.05, a minimum of 180 eyes was required. Accounting for potential loss to follow-up, 200 eyes were enrolled.

Data collection

Comprehensive ophthalmic evaluations were performed preoperatively and at the. 6-month postoperative visit, including diopter (D) of spherical and cylindrical refraction, intraocular pressure (IOP, NIDEK CO, LTD, Japan), slit-lamp examination, scanning laser ophthalmoscope (SLO, Daytona (P200T), Optos, Britain), corneal topographic map (TOMEY CORPORATION, Japan), optical coherence tomography (OCT, Cirrus HD-OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc), corneal biomechanics (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and Pentacam imaging (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Corneal biomechanical indices obtained from Corvis ST included: (1) CBI: A composite index ranging from 0 to 1, with values > 0.5 indicating high ectasia risk [12]. In healthy corneas, CBI is typically < 0.5. (2) TBI: Combines tomography and biomechanics; values > 0.29 suggest high ectasia risk [5]. (3) DA Ratio: Ratio of deformation amplitude at the apex to that at 2 mm from the apex; higher values indicate weaker biomechanics [12]. (4) SP-A1 (Stiffness Parameter at first applanation): Reflects corneal stiffness; lower values indicate greater deformability [15]. (5) ICR (Inverse Concave Radius): A measure of corneal curvature during deformation; higher values suggest weaker biomechanics [16]. (6) PRFI (Pentacam Random Forest Index): An artificial intelligence-based index combining topography and tomography for ectasia detection [5].

Refractive stability was defined as postoperative spherical equivalent (SE) within ± 0.50 D at 6 months follow-up. This threshold is a widely accepted clinical standard for refractive success and has been used in prior SMILE studies [2, 13, 17]. Eyes with SE outside this range were classified as unstable. This binary definition was chosen for clinical simplicity and alignment with common practice; sensitivity analyses using continuous SE deviation yielded similar findings. Postoperative SE was also recorded at 1 month and 3 months to assess longitudinal trends.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests.

To account for the correlation between two eyes of the same patient, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure were used in univariable and multivariable analyses. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed with refractive instability as the dependent variable. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIFs were < 2, indicating no significant collinearity. Missing data were minimal (< 2%) and handled by complete-case analysis, as sensitivity analysis with multiple imputation yielded similar results.

The CBI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and dichotomized at the optimal cutoff (0.05) derived from ROC analysis (Youden index). The binary cutoff was chosen for clinical interpretability; sensitivity analyses using continuous CBI yielded consistent results.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate predictive performance of individual parameters and combined models. Optimal cutoffs were determined by maximizing the Youden index. Internal validation of the final multivariable model was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to obtain optimism-corrected AUC and calibration slope. Comparisons of AUCs between models were performed using the DeLong test.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/MP 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The study cohort comprised 100 patients (200 eyes), with a mean age of 24.90 ± 6.92 years (range: 17–44 years). All patients were of Han Chinese ethnicity. No eyes had evidence of keratoconus or other corneal pathology. Central corneal thickness was 545.27 ± 27.62 μm, axial length 25.53 ± 1.04 mm, and mean lenticule thickness 99.18 ± 24.72 μm with residual stromal bed thickness of 320.82 ± 25.79 μm. Preoperative TBI was 0.18 ± 0.21, and Belin/Ambrósio deviation (BAD-D) was 0.93 ± 0.61.

Preoperative SE exhibited the highest predictive value among the parameters assessed (AUC = 0.78), with an optimal cutoff of -5.25 D (sensitivity 72.3%, specificity 75.6%). Axial length (AUC = 0.72, cutoff 26.1 mm) and lenticule thickness (AUC = 0.71, cutoff 110 μm) also showed good predictive ability. Biomechanical parameters showed moderate discrimination: CBI (AUC = 0.69, cutoff 0.05), TBI (AUC = 0.65, cutoff 0.15). All cutoffs were derived from the dataset and should be validated externally.

Multivariate predictors of refractive instability

In multivariable GEE logistic regression, preoperative SE (OR = 1.52 per 1 D increase, P < 0.01), axial length (OR = 1.36 per 1 mm increase, P < 0.01), CBI > 0.05 (OR = 2.39, P < 0.01), younger age (OR = 1.28 per 10-year decrease, P = 0.042), and lenticule thickness > 110 μm (OR = 1.97, P < 0.01) remained independent risk factors (Table 5). TBI (continuous) was added to the multivariate model but was not independently significant after adjusting for other factors (OR = 1.15, P = 0.32). Residual stromal bed thickness was not independently associated in preliminary models and was excluded due to lack of significance and collinearity with lenticule thickness (VIF > 3 when both included). The GEE-adjusted estimates were nearly identical to those from ordinary logistic regression, confirming robustness.

Predictive model performance

Model performance improved progressively from the SE-only model (AUC = 0.78, accuracy 73.5%) to the full multivariate model (AUC = 0.88, accuracy 83.5%) (Table 6). The DeLong test confirmed that the full model significantly outperformed the SE-only model (P = 0.02) and the biomechanical model (P = 0.01). Bootstrap validation yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.85, indicating good internal validity.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that preoperative corneal biomechanical parameters, particularly CBI, are independent predictors of refractive instability after SMILE, and that combining them with traditional factors (SE, axial length, age, lenticule thickness) yields a model with high predictive accuracy (AUC 0.88). These findings support the integration of biomechanical profiling into preoperative assessment.

Our ROC analysis revealed that preoperative SE exhibited the highest predictive value among the parameters assessed, consistent with previous reports linking higher myopia to postoperative regression [17]. Axial length and lenticule thickness also showed robust predictive value, suggesting anatomical factors play a critical role. While corneal biomechanical indices (CBI, TBI) demonstrated only moderate discrimination individually (AUC 0.65–0.69), they significantly enhanced the multivariate model, indicating their value as complementary factors rather than primary determinants. This nuance is important: biomechanics alone are not “powerful predictors” in isolation but enhance risk stratification when combined with other variables.

The predictive capacity of CBI > 0.05 aligns with its original application in ectasia screening [12], but our data extend its relevance to refractive stability in non-ectatic corneas. The postoperative increase in CBI suggests that SMILE induces measurable biomechanical changes, consistent with the concept of “corneal fatigue” [18]. Notably, the observed postoperative biomechanical changes are driven not only by lenticule-induced corneal thinning but also by an underlying reduction in biomechanical integrity independent of thickness changes, corroborating earlier observations [7–11]. However, the mechanistic link between these changes and refractive regression remains speculative. Future studies should explore whether altered biomechanics affect corneal shape stability through microstructural remodeling or wound healing pathways [19].

The association of younger age with instability, though counterintuitive, may reflect more vigorous wound healing responses that disrupt optical regularity, as proposed by Netto et al. [20]. Ethnic and genetic factors (e.g., LOX, COL5A1 polymorphisms) may modulate this relationship [3], warranting further investigation in diverse populations.

It is important to note that post-SMILE intraocular pressure (IOP) measured by non-contact tonometry is underestimated due to corneal thinning [21]. This could affect the interpretation of biomechanical parameters that incorporate IOP. We used measured IOP in our models, but future studies should consider adjusting for this artifact.

Based on our findings, we propose a preliminary risk stratification: low-risk (CBI < 0.05, SE > − 5.25 D), moderate-risk, and high-risk (CBI > 0.05, SE < − 5.25 D). For high-risk patients, surgical modifications such as reducing lenticule thickness by 15–20%, adjunctive corneal crosslinking [22], or considering alternative procedures (e.g., implantable collamer lenses) could be discussed. However, these recommendations are speculative and require prospective validation; they should not be interpreted as clinical directives.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the 6-month follow-up is relatively short; longer-term studies (≥ 2 years) are needed to capture late biomechanical failure or slow regression. Second, the cohort was exclusively of Han Chinese ethnicity and young, limiting generalizability to other ethnicities and older populations. Third, the cutoffs (e.g., SE − 5.25 D, CBI 0.05) were derived from the same dataset and require external validation. Fourth, we did not account for potential torsional stresses or use advanced imaging like Brillouin microscopy [23] or OCT elastography [24], which may provide more comprehensive biomechanical assessment. Fifth, the use of non-contact tonometry may bias IOP measurements; future studies should consider Goldmann applanation tonometry or correction algorithms. Finally, while internal validation with bootstrapping suggested model stability, independent external validation is essential before clinical implementation.

Future studies should include longer-term prospective cohorts encompassing diverse populations are needed to validate predictive cutoffs and models. Incorporating genetic markers and conducting mechanistic studies to elucidate the link between biomechanical changes and refractive outcomes will also be important. Additionally, randomized trials assessing risk-adapted surgical strategies are warranted to translate these insights into clinical practice.

Conclusion

Preoperative corneal biomechanical parameters, especially CBI, are significant adjunctive predictors of refractive instability after SMILE. A multivariate model integrating biomechanical and anatomical factors achieved good predictive accuracy (AUC 0.88). While biomechanics alone are not the primary determinants, their inclusion enhances risk stratification. These findings support incorporating biomechanical profiling into preoperative evaluation, but external validation and longer follow-up are needed before widespread clinical adoption.
