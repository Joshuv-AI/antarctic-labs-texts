Target‐site mutations and phenotypic failure in Myzus persicae against pyrethroids and carbamates in central Europe
Authors: Jitka Stara, Jan Hubert, Pavla Pabiskova, Frantisek Kocourek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe green peach aphid, Myzus persicae, is a major pest and virus vector in central European agriculture. Following the neonicotinoid seed treatment ban and under climate warming, its populations have increased, raising concerns about insecticide resistance.RESULTSThis study assessed the phenotypic resistance and underlying target‐site mutations in Czech populations of M. persicae from oilseed rape between 2022 and 2024. Bioassays with cypermethrin, pirimicarb, and acetamiprid revealed extreme and increasing pyrethroid resistance, whereas susceptibility to pirimicarb and acetamiprid remained high or improved compared to earlier periods. Molecular screening of 140 field‐collected aphids identified four sodium channel mutations (L1014F, M918L, M918T, L932F) and the ace2 mutation S431F. Survival under insecticide selection strongly highlighted the functional role of specific mutations: the M918L mutation was the most prevalent among cypermethrin survivors (68.75%), confirming it as the primary mechanism of pyrethroid target‐site resistance in these populations. Conversely, the S431F mutation reached complete fixation (100%) in pirimicarb survivors, serving as a definitive marker for carbamate resistance and indicating a high risk of rapid resistance development upon intensive use. Significant negative correlations between M918L and L1014F/L932F suggest divergent resistance lineages.CONCLUSIONNo clear geographical patterns were observed, indicating that resistance is driven by agricultural practices rather than regional factors. These findings highlight the rapid evolution of pyrethroid resistance and underscore the need for integrated pest management, including molecular monitoring and careful insecticide rotation, to sustain control of M. persicae in Czechia. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

The economic importance of Myzus persicae (Sulzer) lies both in its polyphagy, with a host range of over 1015 plant species worldwide, and in its role as an effective vector of plant viruses. 1 In central Europe, it attacks a few key crops, including oilseed rape, sugar beet, potatoes, and brassica vegetables. 2 In Czechia, M. persicae is the primary vector for several economically critical plant viruses, such as, Potato Virus Y (PVY) (affecting potatoes), Beet Yellows Virus (BYV) (affecting sugar beet), and Turnip Mosaic Virus (TuMV) (affecting Brassica crops, including oilseed rape). 3 Overall, this species can transmit up to 180 phytopathogenic viruses. 1 In addition to the indirect damage caused by viral infections, there is also direct damage caused by sucking, which has led to significant economic losses. 4

Long‐term suction trap data reveal a pivotal shift in M. persicae populations in Czechia after 2013, with a significant increase in abundance compared to the previous decade. 5 , 6 This rise has amplified its role as a key virus vector in potato and other crops. The trend is attributed to a synergy of two factors: climate warming and the aphid's expansion into oilseed rape crops following the neonicotinoid seed treatment ban. The decline in protection has been most pronounced in rapeseed, where corresponding population increases have been documented across multiple European regions. 7 , 8

A direct measure against the spread of viruses is the application of insecticides against M. persicae. However, their frequent use has led to the selection of resistance in this pest to several classes of insecticides, including pyrethroids, 9 , 10 , 11 carbamates, 12 , 13 and neonicotinoids. 14 , 15 , 16 This problem is also becoming increasingly relevant in Czechia, where populations of M. persicae resistant to pyrethroids and carbamates have recently been reported. 17

Pyrethroid resistance in M. persicae is primarily mediated by target‐site mutations in the voltage‐gated sodium channel (para‐type). Two key mutations, L1014F (kdr) and M918T (super‐kdr), confer reduced neuronal sensitivity, with the M918T mutation typically enhancing the resistance level conferred by L1014F. Notably, the M918T mutation has never been reported in the absence of the kdr (L1014F) mutation. 9 , 18 These mutations are widespread at high frequencies in populations across Europe, including the United Kingdom (UK), France, Spain, Italy, Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands. 19 Further genetic diversity has been identified with the discovery of additional mutations, such as M918L (super‐kdr‐like) and L932F. 10 , 17 , 20 , 21 In contrast to M918T, the M918L mutation can confer a resistant phenotype even in the absence of the kdr mutation (L1014F). 20 The co‐occurrence of M918T and M918L on different alleles within a single individual has also been documented. 10 Although the presence of resistance genes in M. persicae populations has been investigated in several studies, 10 , 20 , 21 the effect of specific mutations on the level of aphid resistance to pesticides remains largely unknown. Some studies suggest that the M918L mutation confers greater resistance to pyrethroids than the L1014F mutation alone or in combination with the M918T mutation. 20 However, there is insufficient knowledge of the correlation between the occurrence of mutations and the level of resistance in bioassays.

Target‐site resistance to carbamates, such as pirimicarb, in M. persicae is conferred by a modification of acetylcholinesterase. This mechanism is primarily driven by the S431F mutation in the ace2 gene, which causes a structural change in the enzyme, reducing its sensitivity to the insecticide. 12 , 22 The ace2 allele carrying the S431F mutation has been reported at high frequencies in field populations across numerous European countries, including UK, France, Spain, Italy, Belgium, and the Netherlands. 15 This widespread prevalence underscores the significance of this resistance mechanism in field populations.

Neonicotinoid resistance in M. persicae is primarily mediated by two established mechanisms: the overexpression of the CYP6CY3 P450 monooxygenase and the R81T target‐site mutation. This resistance has been documented in populations across Europe, including Greece, France, and Italy. 16 , 23 , 24 Recently, the spectrum of resistance mutations was expanded by the identification of a second target‐site mutation, V101I, in Chinese strains. 25

The principal aims of this study are to: (i) quantify the prevalence of pyrethroid, carbamate and neonicotinoid resistance in Czech field populations of M. persicae from oilseed rape using diagnostic bioassays between 2022 and 2024; (ii) determine the spatiotemporal dynamics of this resistance across different regions of Czechia; (iii) establish the natural incidence and frequency of known resistance mutations (i.e., kdr, super‐kdr, super‐kdr‐like, MACE, and others) in these populations; (iv) evaluate the relationship between phenotypic resistance (from bioassays) and the genotypic profile of the aphids; (v) assess the frequency of resistance mutations in survivors of insecticide selection pressure during bioassays.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection

A total of 28 local populations of M. persicae were collected from commercial oilseed rape (Brassica napus subsp. napus) crops in various regions of Czechia between September and October 2022, 2023, and 2024 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The localities of collection represent the main regions in Czechia where winter oilseed rape is cultivated. Approximately 100 parthenogenetic female adults and nymphs were collected from oilseed rape plants in each locality. The samples were collected from commercial fields in which either no pesticides had been used or one or two applications of pyrethroids had been made, to combat Psylliodes chrysocephala. The aphids were collected from three sites in the field (three different areas, each approximately 24 m apart). Each local population was kept separately in a controlled environmental chamber at 20 °C, with 75% relative humidity and a photoperiod of 16 h:8 h (light/dark). The aphids were reared on kohlrabi plants (Brassica oleracea convar. gongylodes) and the parthenogenetic progeny (after three generations) were used for the bioassays.

Dose response bioassay

Insecticides

The sensitivity of M. persicae populations collected in 2022–2024 was evaluated using formulated products of cypermethrin, pirimicarb and acetamiprid (Table 2). The recommended field application doses of the earlier‐mentioned insecticides were used to determine the concentrations employed in the bioassays, under the assumption that 400 L of water would be used per hectare. Four concentrations of each insecticide diluted with water were used to generate data for estimating the dose–response curves (Table 2).

Leaf dip method

The IRAC leaf dip test no. 019 26 was used to evaluate the susceptibility of adult M. persicae to insecticides. Bioassays were conducted on M. persicae populations collected from oilseed rape between 2022 and 2024. Kohlrabi plant (cv. Gongylodes) leaf discs were cut, immersed in a 100 mL solution of insecticide for 10 s, dried on filter paper and placed, top side down, in plastic cups containing cooled agar (Agar Agar 700; Hages Ltd, Boskovice, Czechia), with a drop of water on the surface to help the leaf discs adhere. Using a smooth brush, 20–30 adult aphids were transferred to each leaf. The exact number of aphids transferred to each leaf was documented. Three replicate leaves per concentration were used. The cups were then sealed with breathable lids and placed in a controlled environment chamber at 20 °C with 60% relative humidity and a 16 h:8 h light/dark cycle. The laboratory efficacy of the active ingredients was expressed as aphid mortality after 72 h. The M. persicae alive individuals obtained from the bioassays were stored at −20 °C for further molecular analysis, which were carried out between 2023 and 2025.

Detection of mutations associated with resistance to pyrethroids and carbamates

Between 2023 and 2025, the total DNA of 270 M. persicae individuals was extracted, that is, 80 individuals that survived the field‐recommended dose (FRD) of cypermethrin (16 populations from 2022 and 2023), 50 individuals that survived FRD of pirimicarb (ten populations from 2022 and 2023), and 140 untreated individuals (28 populations from 2022–2024). Five individuals per population were analyzed separately. This sample size was chosen to enable a broad geographic survey of resistance mutation presence across multiple populations, rather than to obtain precise allele frequency estimates at the individual population level. DNA was extracted using a 10% Chelex solution. Briefly, 20 μL of deionized water was added to a test tube and the specimen was homogenized using a sterile pipette. Then, 150 μL of Chelex and 5 μL of proteinase K in concentration of 20 mg mL−1 were added. The specimens were boiled at 60 °C for 2 h and then at 95 °C for 15 min to inactivate the proteinase K, after which they were used as templates for polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The DNA samples were stored at −20 °C. DNA of individuals that survived FRD of cypermethrin was used to detect the M918L, M918T, L932F and L1014F mutations. DNA of individuals that survived FRD of pirimicarb was used to detect the occurrence of S431F mutation and DNA of untreated individuals was used to detect natural occurrence of all the mentioned mutations related to resistance to pyrethroids and carbamates.

DNA sequencing (Sanger method) of para gene of paratype voltage‐gated sodium channel was used to evaluate kdr and super‐kdr single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in one step. We sequenced PCR products from 190 M. persicae individuals, comprising 140 untreated field‐collected aphids (2022–2024) and 50 survivors of cypermethrin FRD bioassays (2022–2023). Fragments were amplified according to instructions for PCR using primer pairs Aph1 and Aph16. 27

We screened for the pirimicarb resistance‐associated mutation S431F in two cohorts of M. persicae: 140 untreated field‐collected individuals (2022–2024) and 50 individuals that survived a pirimicarb FRD bioassay (2022–2023). Fragments 382 bp were amplified by PCR as previously described. 28 The presence of S431F mutation was detected by restriction enzyme Sspl (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania). To amplify the 382 bp fragment hiding the S431F mutation, we used the allele non‐specific forward (5′MpS431F) and reverse (3′MpS431F) primers and the PCR protocol outlined by Kwon et al. 28 After PCR in a C1000 Touch thermocycler (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA), restriction was performed. The unmutated CAATATT is cleaved by the restriction endonuclease SspI AAT/ATT (Fast digest SspI restriction enzyme). The reaction product (10 μL) was added to the digestion mixture (20 μL) and incubated at 37 °C (SspI) for 5 min. 11 The digestion mixture consisted of the restriction enzyme (0.5 unit μL−1) and a 1× concentration of the supplied buffer. The digestion products, as well as a ZipRuller DNA ladder express 2100 bp (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) that was used as the size standard, were separated by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels. Gels were stained in SERVA DNA Stain Clear G (Heidelberg, Germany) and imaged using a G:BOX (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). We sequenced selected individuals (ten classified as mutant and ten as wild‐type based on restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis). In all cases, the RFLP results were consistent with the sequencing data: individuals with the undigested fragment (382 bp) carried the S431F mutation, while those showing digested fragments carried the wild‐type allele. This validation step confirmed the reliability of the RFLP method for detecting the S431F mutation in our study.

Data analyses

Raw data are presented in Supporting Information, Tables S4–S7. The data from bioassays were analyzed by probit analysis using a dose‐effect analysis in R software 4.4.1. 29 The probit model was calculated for every insecticide. The following R packages were used: ggplot2 30 for graphical output, dplyr 31 for data manipulation, and MASS 32 for probit analysis and median lethal concentration (LC50) estimation. The insecticide concentration (LOG10) population and year and their interactions were the factors, and mortality was dependent variable (binomial distribution). The LC50 values, that is, concentrations required to kill 50% of populations, were fitted with a 95% confidence limit from the model. The mortality data in most cases covered a sufficient range of 5–95% at the tested concentrations. The goodness‐of‐fit of the probit model to the observed dose–response data was evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) test. For each population and each insecticide, we compared the observed number of dead individuals with the number expected under the fitted probit model across the tested concentration levels. In our analysis, all reported LC50 values (for populations where mortality at the highest tested concentration exceeded 50%) were based on models with a non‐significant Pearson's χ 2 test (P > 0.05). For populations where mortality at the highest tested concentration remained below 50%, LC50 could not be reliably estimated and these populations were excluded from quantitative LC50‐based comparisons, though they were included in qualitative resistance classification. For baseline populations where mortality at the lowest tested concentration exceeded 50%, LC50 values were estimated by extrapolation below the tested concentration range using the fitted probit model. Such extrapolation is statistically valid when the dose–response model provides a good fit for the data in the observed range.

Resistance ratios (RRs) were calculated as the LC50 of each population divided by the lowest LC50 (most susceptible baseline) for the corresponding insecticide. Due to the absence of a long‐term laboratory susceptible reference strain of M. persicae in Czechia, RRs were calculated using the most susceptible field population from the study period as a baseline. This approach is widely accepted and has been published in several peer‐reviewed studies. 33 , 34 RR served as a continuous measure for temporal trends, while categories were created as discrete variables for genotype association studies. Both approaches are complementary, providing deeper insight into resistance dynamics in M. persicae populations. All bioassays were conducted with the same formulated products to ensure consistency in potential formulation effects across all tested populations. The baseline LC50 values were: 0.38 mg L−1 for cypermethrin (Ceska Lipa in 2023), 0.23 mg L−1 for pirimicarb (Prostejov in 2023), and 0.05 mg L−1 for acetamiprid (Ivanovice na Hane in 2024).

Myzus persicae populations were classified into three resistance levels per insecticide (C1–C3, P1–P3, and A1–A3 for cypermethrin, pirimicarb, and acetamiprid, respectively) using LC50‐based thresholds. Group classification (C1–C3, P1–P3, A1–A3) was used for qualitative categorization to analyze relationships between phenotypic resistance and genotype (mutation presence). Discrete categories (e.g., ‘highly resistant’) allow statistical testing of mutation frequency differences among resistance levels. The classification for cypermethrin was low resistance (C1; LC50 0.1–5.0 mg L−1), resistance (C2; LC50 5.0–300.0 mg L−1), and high resistance (C3; LC50 > 50 000 mg L−1). The classification for pirimicarb was: high sensitivity (P1; LC50 less than 1.0 mg L−1), sensitivity (P2: LC50 1.0–20.0 mg L−1), and low sensitivity/resistance (P3; LC50 more than 20.0 mg L−1). The classification for acetamiprid was high sensitivity (A1; LC50 less than 0.5 mg L−1), sensitivity (A2: LC50 0.5–5.0 mg L−1), and low sensitivity (A3; LC50 more than 5.0 mg L−1). These classifications were added into the new probit models and evaluated.

Mann–Whitney analysis (included in the standard R installation) was used to compare log‐scaled RRs from populations in 2022–2024 and in 2018–2022 period (data from Stara et al. 17 ). To analyze the relationship between RR and lethality at FRD in 2022–2024 and 2018–2022 periods, the lethality was plotted over log‐scaled RR from all populations for each pesticide. A linear model was used to fit the data as follows: y = ax − b, where x is the log‐scaled RR, y is the lethality at recommended active ingredient rate, a is the slope, and b is the y‐intercept.

Contingency tables and χ 2 analyses were used to determine the frequency of resistance mutations in M. persicae samples from different populations. Contingency tables and χ 2 analysis were also used to analyze the association between mutations and M. persicae resistance to pyrethroids and carbamates in samples from bioassays. Resistance index (RI) was calculated using the standard formula: RI = mutation frequency in pesticide group/mutation frequency in control group, where pesticide group consisted of samples from bioassays with cypermethrin or pirimicarb and control group consisted of samples without the bioassay.

Spearman correlations test was used to analyze correlation between occurrence of target‐site mutations followed by permutation test to evaluate the significance of the results.

RESULTS

Toxicity of insecticides to M. persicae populations

Efficacy of cypermethrin, pirimicarb, and acetamiprid against M. persicae populations varied between 2022 and 2024. Control mortality remained low (0–12.5%). For cypermethrin, mortality at the FRD was generally low and declined markedly from 2023 to 2024, with mean mortality falling to 19.22%. Sensitivity to pirimicarb was high in 2023–2024, reaching 100% mortality in most populations, while mortality did not reach 100% in any population during 2022 (range: 87.8 to < 100%). In contrast, M. persicae populations were consistently susceptible to acetamiprid across all years, with mortality exceeding 90% in all cases (Table 3).

The full model explained substantial variation in the response to cypermethrin. Population was the strongest predictor (χ 2 = 1782.8, df = 24, P < 0.001), followed by its interaction with log_conc (χ 2 = 571.7, df = 24, P < 0.001). Year (χ 2 = 353.8, df = 2, P < 0.001) and the year × log_conc interaction (χ 2 = 237.1, df = 2, P < 0.001) were also significant, while the main effect of log_conc was significant but comparatively smaller (χ 2 = 145.7, df = 1, P < 0.001). We acknowledge that for highly resistant populations (e.g., those with mortality at the highest concentration < 50%), future studies may benefit from exploring a broader concentration range, although such an approach was beyond the scope of the present work.

Pirimicarb LC50 values varied within a narrower range (0.23–86.78 mg L−1, Table S1b). The full model explained substantial variation in the response to pirimicarb. The strongest predictor was population (χ 2 = 1285.2, df = 24, P < 0.001), followed by its interaction with log_conc (χ 2 = 531.8, df = 24, P < 0.001). Year (χ 2 = 187.4, df = 2, P < 0.001) and the year × log_conc interaction (χ 2 = 71.0, df = 2, P < 0.001) were also significant, while the main effect of log_conc was significant but comparatively smaller (χ 2 = 463.8, df = 1, P < 0.001).

Acetamiprid exhibited the highest stability (0.05–8.55 mg L−1, Table S1c). The full model explained substantial variation in the response to acetamiprid. All predictors were statistically significant. The strongest predictors were log_conc (χ 2 = 515.86, df = 1, P < 0.001) and population (χ 2 = 523.02, df = 24, P < 0.001), which had comparable magnitudes of association. Year was also significant (χ 2 = 90.09, df = 2, P < 0.001). Both interaction terms were significant: year × log_conc (χ 2 = 14.97, df = 2, P < 0.001) and population × log_conc (χ 2 = 234.40, df = 24, P < 0.001). These results demonstrate that the effect of log_conc on the response is conditional upon both year and population, with the population interaction being substantially larger in magnitude.

The results of models calculated for each pesticide demonstrate that the effect of log_conc on the response is conditional upon both year and population.

For cypermethrin, using the FRD of 25 mg L−1 as a benchmark, 22% of populations exhibited low resistance (Group C1), 39% were resistant (Group C2, LC50 > FRD), and 39% were highly resistant (Group C3). A notable temporal pattern was observed, with the highly resistant Group C3 consisting predominantly of populations sampled in 2024, whereas Group C2 was primarily composed of populations from 2022. For pirimicarb, susceptibility was distributed as highly sensitive (37%), sensitive (41%), and low‐sensitivity/resistant (22%) populations (Table S2b). The LC50 values for the low‐sensitivity group (28–87 mg L−1) fell within an ambiguous range that precluded definitive discrimination between low inherent sensitivity and true resistance. For acetamiprid, most populations (79%) were moderately sensitive, with smaller proportions classified as highly sensitive (7%) or low‐sensitive (14%) (Table S2c). In contrast to cypermethrin, no clear temporal associations were evident for the susceptibility groups of pirimicarb or acetamiprid. Furthermore, the group distributions for all three insecticides showed no clear correlation with the regional origin of the populations (Fig. 1).

Temporal and spatial patterns of insecticide resistance

Resistance to cypermethrin showed extreme inter‐population variation, with RRs ranging from 2.63 (Zdar in 2024) to 203 429.82 (Tasovice in 2022) (Table S1a). No statistically significant difference was found between the mean log RR for 2018–2020 (1.96 ± 0.82 standard deviation (SD); data from Stara et al. 17 ) and 2022–2024 (3.40 ± 2.11 SD) (P = 0.092). However, the higher mean in the latter period suggests a possible trend toward increased resistance, largely driven by the extreme cases from 2024.

For pirimicarb, RRs ranged from 1.52 (Plzen and Vsesulov in 2024) to 377.30 (Sezemice in 2022) (Table S1b), with historical data indicating substantially higher values in 2002 than in 2023–2024. A statistically significant decrease in resistance was observed between periods. The mean log RR was significantly higher in 2018–2020 (2.981 ± 0.841 SD; data from Stara et al. 17 ) than in 2022–2024 (1.391 ± 0.856 SD) (P = 0.0003), corresponding to an approximately 39‐fold reduction in the relative risk of resistance.

Acetamiprid RRs ranged from 2.60 (Znojmo in 2024) to 139.00 (Tasovice in 2022) and were evenly distributed between 2022 and 2024 (Table S1c). Resistance levels in the recent period (2022–2024; mean log RR = 1.576 ± 0.534 SD) were substantially lower than those observed in the historical period (2018–2020; mean log RR = 2.610 ± 0.353 SD). This difference was statistically significant, as confirmed by both a Welch's t‐test (P = 0.0026) and a Mann–Whitney U test (P = 0.002). This result indicates decrease of tolerance of M. persicae populations to acetamiprid in 2022–2024 period.

Linear regression analysis revealed a strong and highly significant negative correlation between the cypermethrin RR (logRR) and insecticide‐induced mortality during both study periods (see Fig. 5). During the 2018–2020 period, mortality decreased sharply as resistance increased, according to the equation y = 111.6 − 31.5x (coefficient of determination (R 2) = 0.729, P < 0.001). A similarly strong and significant relationship was observed for the 2022–2024 period, described by the equation y = 89.9 − 18.9x (R 2 = 0.738, P < 0.001). A clear temporal shift was evident in the slope of the regression lines. The slope for the 2018–2020 period (−31.5) was substantially steeper than that for the 2022–2024 period (−18.9). This flattening of the dose–response relationship suggests that higher levels of resistance have become established in the M. persicae population, meaning that incremental increases in the RR now result in smaller reductions in field efficacy than in the earlier period.

Target‐site resistance mutations in field populations of M. persicae

Sequencing of the para sodium channel gene in 140 individual M. persicae from 28 field populations identified four heterozygous SNPs, each leading to an amino acid substitution associated with pyrethroid resistance: L1014F (kdr), M918L (super‐kdr‐like), M918T (super‐kdr), and L932F. The overall mutation frequencies of mutant aphids across all individuals were: M918L (55.71%), L1014F (48.57%), L932F (37.86%), and M918T (10.71%) (Table 5). All tested aphids carried at least one mutation, which occurred in four distinct genotypic patterns: M918L alone; L932F + L1014F; M918T + L1014F; and M918T + M918L + L1014F (Table S3a). Notably, the M918L substitution was the most common, followed by L1014F, indicating a widespread prevalence of these two resistance mutations in the surveyed populations.

Correlation and linkage between target‐site mutations in field populations of M. persicae

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant pairwise associations among the four resistance‐related mutations (Fig. 8). A strong positive correlation was observed between the L932F and L1014F mutations (rₛ = 0.80). In contrast, strong negative correlations were found between M918L and L1014F (rₛ = −0.92) as well as between M918L and L932F (rₛ = −0.87). These results suggest that the M918L mutation may displace the L1014F and L932F mutations, possibly due to a higher fitness advantage in carriers. Weaker positive correlations were detected for M918T with both L1014F (rₛ = 0.46) and L932F (rₛ = 0.46), while the association between M918T and M918L was weakly negative (rₛ = −0.35). No substantial correlation was observed between M918T and S431F (rₛ = 0.20). These patterns suggest that M918L tends to occur independently of L1014F and L932F, possibly reflecting a competitive fitness relationship, whereas L932F and L1014F frequently co‐occur in the same individuals.

Association of mutations with insecticide survival in M. persicae after laboratory selection

All the analyzed M. persicae individuals who survived cypermethrin FRD in the bioassay in 2022 and 2023 had one of the following mutations: M918T, M918L, L932F or L1014F. The M918L mutation was the most common occurring in 68.75% individuals. This was followed by the L1014F mutation in 28.75% individuals and the L932F mutation in 23.75% individuals. M918T occurred in only 7.5% individuals (Tables 5 and S3b).

Selection with cypermethrin in the bioassay led to change in the frequency of kdr mutations. While in the natural population the most common mutations were M918L (55.71%) and L1014F (48.57%), in individuals surviving the FRD bioassay the frequency of M918L increased to 68.75%. In contrast, the occurrence of L1014F, L932F, and M918T mutations in survivors decreased to 28.75%, 23.75%, and 7.50%, respectively. These results suggest that the M918L mutation is the main mechanism of cypermethrin target resistance in the tested populations.

The frequency of the S431F mutation differed markedly between natural populations and laboratory selection. It was present in 27.14% of field‐sampled individuals but reached complete fixation (100%) among survivors of the pirimicarb FRD bioassay (Tables 5 and S3c). This is direct evidence of strong selective pressure.

Analysis of target‐site mutations in individuals surviving the FRD of cypermethrin or pirimicarb in the 2022 and 2023 bioassays revealed a strong and specific association between the S431F mutation and resistance (Fig. 9). This mutation showed a highly significant positive association with a RI of 3.68 (P < 0.001).

Association of target‐site mutations M918T, M918L, L932F, L1014F and S431F with Myzus persicae resistance to cypermethrin and pirimicarb.

The M918L mutation was also significantly associated with an increased RI (RI = 3.37, P < 0.001). By contrast, the L932F, L1014F and M918T mutations did not show a significant association with resistance in this assay (RI = 1.1, 0.8 and 0.5, respectively) (Fig. 9).

DISCUSSION

History of monitoring and controlling resistance in M. persicae in Czechia

Comprehensive monitoring of M. persicae resistance to cypermethrin (pyrethroid), pirimicarb (carbamate), and acetamiprid (neonicotinoid) has been conducted in Czechia since 2018. Following initial results from 2018–2021, 17 this article presents findings from the second stage (2022–2024). Bioassays made it possible to evaluate trends in sensitivity to acetamiprid. This phase also involved expanding genetic screening to include the natural occurrence of target‐site mutations in relation to pyrethroids and carbamates, enabling the analysis of mutation associations.

Between 2018 and 2024, significant shifts in insecticide use influenced resistance development. Pyrethroid resistance persisted, driven by applications against spring rapeseed pests, virus vectors on potatoes and cereals, and other crops. While targeted use against M. persicae on winter rapeseed nearly ceased, overall application intensity of pyrethroids remained high. In contrast, carbamate selection pressure was minimal, with no use recorded between 2022 and 2024. Acetamiprid selection pressure in autumn rapeseed remained low compared to previous neonicotinoids, partly due to its limited efficacy against the main pest, Psylliodes chrysocephala. Low M. persicae prevalence in 2023–2024 further reduced applications of neonicotinoids. During 2023–2024, rapeseed protection involved flonicamid, acetamiprid, flupyradifurone (mainly as seed treatment), and pyrethroids. Spirotetramat was used on other crops until its registration ended in 2025, while diamide use (e.g., cyantraniliprole) increased significantly from 2022. The expansion of the insecticide spectrum, including substances effective against resistant populations, has been a key factor shaping M. persicae resistance evolution in Czechia.

Development of resistance of M. persicae to cypermethrin

Bioassay monitoring of field populations from rapeseed between 2018 and 2024 confirmed a high and widespread resistance to pyrethroids. Pyrethroid resistance in M. persicae persists even though targeted applications against this aphid on winter oilseed rape have nearly ceased. This is likely due to the continued intensive use of pyrethroids against other arthropod pests in the same crops, such as Psylliodes chrysocephala in oilseed rape, as well as in other crops like potatoes and cereals, where pyrethroids are applied against various pest species. These applications impose indirect selection pressure on M. persicae populations, contributing to the maintenance and further spread of resistance mechanisms even in the absence of direct targeting.

Average mortality following FRDs fell below 20% in 2023–2024. The LC50 value for cypermethrin showed extreme variability, exceeding 999.9 mg L−1 for 32% of populations. While the rate of resistance development has slowed since 2022, the number of highly resistant populations has increased. This high resistance correlates with a high frequency (69% to 8%) of four different para sodium channel mutations found in survivors after FRD application.

To document ongoing resistance evolution, a wide range of LC50 values for cypermethrin was recorded, indicating significant inter‐population variability. Between 2018 and 2024, LC50 values varied from 0.38 mg L−1 to > 999.99 mg L−1. Similarly broad variability was reported for bifenthrin. 35 However, a key distinction emerged: while the most resistant clones in the cited study were homozygotes (R/R) for kdr and super‐kdr genotypes, our study found only heterozygous (S/R) individuals for these mutations in resistant field populations, despite comparable resistance levels.

Comparisons of resistance between geographical regions are feasible using directly comparable LC50 values from standardized bioassays. However, comparing published RRs is problematic due to inconsistent baseline LC50 values from differing ‘most sensitive’ reference populations. For instance, the LC50 of 0.38 mg L−1 identified as the most sensitive in our field study is 43 times lower than a value (16.5 mg L−1) reported elsewhere for pirimicarb. 28 In this publication, RR for cypermethrin was calculated using our most sensitive field LC50 (0.38 mg L−1 from Ceska Lipa in 2023), and historical data were recalculated accordingly. This differs from Stara et al., 17 who used an LC50 of 0.19 mg L−1 from an Australian laboratory population. 36

A limitation of this study is that for the most susceptible populations used as baselines for RR calculation, the LC50 values fell below the lowest tested concentration. The probit analysis allows extrapolation in such cases when mortality at the lowest concentration exceeds 50%. The future monitoring will be done to include a broader concentration range to directly estimate LC50 values for tested populations. The use of a field‐derived susceptible baseline rather than a laboratory reference strain may underestimate absolute resistance levels compared to studies using susceptible laboratory clones, 37 however it still provides a valid relative comparison of resistance among populations within the region and over time.

Development resistance of M. persicae to pirimicarb

Monitoring of field populations from rapeseed between 2018 and 2024 indicates a decrease in resistance to pirimicarb. Average mortality following FRDs increased from 91% in 2020 to 98% in 2024. The LC50 showed low variability, ranging from 0.23 to 86.8 mg L−1. Since 2021, field populations have become more sensitive to pirimicarb, with high sensitivity observed in 2023 and 2024.

The detected resistance level does not fully correspond to the occurrence of the S431F mutation in local populations, found in 27% of individuals. This suggests the phenotypic expression of resistance is lower than the mutation frequency implies. However, the S431F mutation was present in all individuals surviving the FRD in bioassays. A synergistic effect between pirimicarb and piperonyl butoxide (PBO) observed in some populations between 2018 and 2020 17 indicates that phenotypic resistance likely results from a combination of target‐site mutations and metabolic resistance. These factors may have contributed to the reappearance of sensitivity between 2020 and 2024.

In this publication, the RRs for pirimicarb were determined using the lowest LC50 value of 0.23 mg L−1 from the most sensitive field population (Prostejov in 2020–2024). Historical LC50 data (2018–2020) were recalculated using this same baseline. This differs from the original publication, 17 which used an LC50 of 16.5 mg L−1 from a sensitive South Korean laboratory population. 28

Sensitivity of M. persicae to acetamiprid

Bioassay monitoring of 28 field populations between 2018 and 2024 revealed no high‐level resistance to acetamiprid, as mortality at the FRD exceeded 90% in all populations. However, RRs showed considerable inter‐population variability, with 13 populations exhibiting RR values exceeding 50 (range: 2.60–139.00). While these RR values indicate reduced sensitivity compared to the most susceptible baseline population, they do not reach levels that would compromise field efficacy, as all populations remained highly susceptible at the FRD (mortality > 90%). This suggests that acetamiprid tolerance in Czech M. persicae populations is evolving, warranting continued monitoring. LC50 values ranged from 0.05 to 8.55 mg L−1.

Monitoring confirmed that reduced sensitivity to acetamiprid is a notable evolutionary trait. Even the least sensitive populations had LC50 values far below the FRD. This reduced sensitivity in M. persicae parallels that described for Rhopalosiphum padi to pyrethroids, observed in Australia, 38 Ireland, 39 and Germany. 40 UK studies 41 show R. padi populations with varying sensitivity, where the least sensitive were 32 times less sensitive than the most sensitive. Insect tolerance to insecticides often involves reduced sensitivity prior to full resistance selection. The tolerance of insects to insecticides is mostly explained by the reduced sensitivity of the pest to the active ingredient of the insecticide, or to the entire group, with the same mechanism of tolerance expression, for cases before or after resistance selection. In addition to genetic resistance, insect populations can develop reduced sensitivity (tolerance) to insecticides. This is often a metabolic‐based mechanism whereby the insect sequesters or detoxifies the active ingredient, and it is the first indication that resistance to insecticides is developing. 42

Diversity and distribution of target‐site mutations

Molecular analysis has confirmed that target‐site mutations are a primary mechanism conferring resistance to pyrethroids (e.g., L1014F, M918L, M918T, L932F in the para gene) and carbamates (S431F in the ace1 gene) in European populations of M. persicae, 15 , 20 a finding now confirmed in Czech populations (Table 5). In our 2022–2024 samples from 28 field populations, we detected four distinct genotypic patterns: M918L alone; L932F + L1014F; M918T + L1014F; and M918T + M918L + L1014F. The M918L mutation was the most prevalent, consistent with its earlier dominance in 2018–2020 Czech samples. 17 The high frequency of M918L and L1014F correlates with observed phenotypic resistance to cypermethrin, and M918L was notably the most prevalent mutation among cypermethrin survivors, confirming its central role in pyrethroid resistance. In contrast to the previous Czech study, we detected the M918T + L1014F combination for the first time in the 2022–2024 period. The analysis of five individuals per population provides a limited basis for precise estimation of allele frequencies in this study. Therefore, future studies focusing on the detailed characterization of resistance dynamics in specific populations should include larger sample sizes per site to obtain more accurate frequency estimates and better capture intra‐population genetic diversity. The methods limited to decide whether mutations such as 918T/L occur on the same DNA molecule (cis) or on different molecules (trans) remains unknown. The clonal isolation followed by Sanger sequencing will be necessary to directly resolve the haplotype configuration and fully elucidate the functional impact of these co‐occurring mutations. 9

The prevalence of resistance mutations varies significantly across Europe. In southern and western Europe, the L1014F mutation is widespread and frequently co‐occurs with M918T, forming a haplotype linked to high‐level pyrethroid resistance in populations from Italy, Greece, Belgium, and Spain. 10 , 11 , 43 A similar strong linkage exists between L1014F and L932F in regions such as France. 20 Our data confirm these positive correlations, confirming the persistence of these linked haplotypes. In contrast, the M918L (super‐kdr‐like) mutation exhibits a distinct and often mutually exclusive pattern. First identified in the UK, 15 it is consistently reported not to occur with L1014F. 10 , 19 Our findings of strong negative correlations between M918L and both L1014F and L932F support this genetic incompatibility. This suggests the evolution of at least two divergent pyrethroid‐resistance lineages in Europe: one centred on the L1014F + M918T/L932F complex and another on M918L. The strong negative correlations suggest possible competitive displacement or fitness trade‐offs, which may influence resistance dynamics under different insecticide regimes. The M918L mutation confers greater resistance to pyrethroids (types I and II) than L1014F alone or combined with M918T, potentially at a lower fitness cost. 10 , 20 , 35

All field‐collected individuals carrying target‐site mutations were heterozygous (S/R), with no homozygotes detected, consistent with previous reports on M. persicae field populations. 21 , 27 Direct comparisons of resistance levels between populations with different mutation combinations should therefore be interpreted with caution, as phenotypic expression may be influenced by allelic configuration and differential gene expression.

Additionally, monoallelic expression could contribute to variable resistance phenotypes even among individuals sharing the same genotype, particularly for mutations such as M918L. 35 Although we did not directly investigate this, the observed variability in phenotypic resistance suggests that regulatory mechanisms, including differential allelic expression, may play a role and need to be screened in the future.

Importantly, we found no clear regional pattern in the distribution of resistance in Czechia for any insecticide, suggesting that resistance is widespread and likely driven by agricultural practices rather than localized factors.

Variability in the evolution of target‐site resistance

The carbamate‐resistance mutation S431F shows more variable associations. Although earlier studies documented linkage between S431F and M918L, 20 , 43 we observed only a weak association. This discrepancy may reflect regional differences in selection pressure, host plant influence, or population genetic background. The S431F mutation also occurs independently, indicating that carbamate resistance can evolve separately from pyrethroid resistance. The S431F mutation was strongly associated with pirimicarb resistance, reaching fixation in survivors of laboratory bioassays (Table 5). This mutation serves as a clear molecular marker for carbamate resistance and its detection in over one‐quarter of field‐sampled individuals indicates a tangible risk of resistance development if pirimicarb is used intensively. The lack of temporal trends in pirimicarb and acetamiprid resistance groupings further indicates that resistance to these compounds in Czechia is not currently increasing at the population level, though vigilant monitoring remains essential.

Beyond geographic variation, the host plant on which M. persicae populations reside is a critical, and often deterministic, factor shaping the frequency and combination of target‐site resistance mutations. A clear dichotomy is observed between the primary woody host, peach (Prunus persica), and various herbaceous secondary hosts (e.g., oilseed rape, potato, sugar beet). Peach orchards, which often receive intensive insecticide treatments, act as reservoirs for the most diverse and potent resistance haplotypes. Notably, the L1014F + M918T combination, conferring high‐level pyrethroid resistance, is strongly associated with clones with holocyclic development originating from peach. 10 , 19 This environment appears to maintain a broad genetic pool of resistance. Our data proved the occurrence of L1014F and M918T also in M. persicae samples from oilseed rape.

Populations on annual crops like oilseed rape frequently exhibit a distinct resistance profile dominated by the M918L (super‐kdr‐like) mutation, which is often found in the absence of L1014F. 15 , 20 This pattern may result from a founder effect, where clones with the M918L genotype successfully colonized this crop system and proliferated, coupled with crop‐specific insecticide selection pressures that differ from those in orchards. The strong negative correlations we observed between M918L and the peach‐associated mutations (L1014F, L932F) further underscore this host‐based genetic divergence.

The evolution of resistance in aphid is modulated by the life cycle. 14 Myzus persicae can undergo both sexual (holocyclic) and asexual (anholocyclic) reproduction. 44 In temperate regions, the holocycle of M. persicae, involving sexual reproduction and overwintering of eggs, occurs alongside a mixture of holocyclic and anholocyclic clones. The existence of sexual reproduction increases diversity and allows the appearance of new combinations of mutations, while parthenogenesis can allow the rapid multiplication of a huge number of individuals carrying the selected combinations of mutations. Myzus persicae populations in Czechia exhibit both holocyclic and anholocyclic life cycles. The proportions of each type vary from year to year, depending on weather conditions. Holocyclic populations overwinter as eggs on primary hosts such as Prunus persica, while anholocyclic populations overwinter parthenogenetically on secondary hosts such as oilseed rape.

Interestingly, the S431F mutation itself does not show a strong, exclusive association with any particular host plant. 19 Its presence across peach, oilseed rape, and other crops indicates that selection for carbamate resistance is widespread and can evolve independently within populations associated with different host plants, although M. persicae remains a highly polyphagous species capable of host alternation. This broad distribution underscores the pervasive historical use of carbamate insecticides.

Phenotypic expression of resistance in M. persicae may be influenced by mutations at the target site or metabolic resistance. 15 Phenotypic resistance to acetamiprid may be preceded by the gradual development of tolerance. As our research shows, resistance to acetamiprid has not been observed in the phenotype after selection pressure induced by neonicotinoids since 2018. In the evolution of resistance in M. persicae, the expression of resistance in the phenotype may be influenced by other mechanisms. Panini et al. 35 revealed a mechanism of insecticide resistance resulting from transposon‐mediated insertion mutagenesis of a genetically dominant insecticide‐sensitive allele, which allows the activation of previously unavailable recessive resistance alleles.

Conclusions and management implications

This study documents a concerning escalation in pyrethroid resistance among M. persicae populations in Czechia. Our results showed that the S431F and M918L mutations significantly increase the level of resistance in the phenotype after pyrethroid selection. No resistance to acetamiprid was observed, and susceptibility to pirimicarb has improved in recent years, likely due to reduced field use. The absence of clear geographical patterns indicates that resistance evolution is primarily driven by agricultural practices rather than regional factors. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated pest management strategies that incorporate molecular monitoring, insecticide rotation, and the promotion of non‐chemical controls to mitigate resistance development and preserve the efficacy of existing insecticides. Future work should focus on understanding the fitness costs of resistance mutations and evaluating the impact of insecticide use patterns on resistance selection in the field.
