Gap-graded soil erosion modes predicted by µ-CT informed graded erosion model and multi-criteria assessment
Authors: Bin Zhu, Yu-Fei Xie, Dai-Rong Su, Jia-Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates internal erosion in gap-graded gravelly soils, focusing on the critical fine content transition zone (30–35%). By integrating µ-CT imaging, geometric criteria analysis, and a novel predictive model based on the graded erosion principle, we demonstrate that fine particle content governs the transition between suffusion and piping erosion modes. Experimental results reveal that exceeding the 30–35% threshold shifts the soil fabric from a stable coarse-grained skeleton to an “over-filled” structure, where coarse particles float within a fine-dominated matrix, drastically increasing piping susceptibility. A multi-criteria assessment framework validates the limitations of traditional geometric criteria (e.g., Cu, Kenney & Lau) for transitional soils, while the grading entropy criterion offers enhanced robustness. The proposed graded erosion model successfully predicts particle size distribution evolution toward stable Fuller limits and porosity changes by incorporating particle-size-sensitive erosion rates with physical thresholds. This integrated methodology advances the understanding and prediction of internal erosion in gap-graded soils, supporting the design of resilient geotechnical structures and improving infrastructure risk assessment.

Introduction

In engineering practice, erosion problems of granular soil typically encompass two main concerns: Seepage Stability Evaluation and Erosion Mode Prediction. The former assesses water seepage and its effects on soil structure. Factors like permeability, pore pressure, and fine particles influence soil stability, helping identify risks like suffusion or suffosion (piping), In this study, suffosion and piping are considered synonymous terms, representing the same type of erosion hazard.which weaken soil and cause instability1. The latter predicts erosion types based on soil conditions, such as suffusion (transportation of fine particles) or suffosion (formation of piping channels and sinkhole). In the Xiacun Town case2, groundwater under intense rainfall or pumping conditions developed high hydraulic gradients, continuously eroded fine particles, and formed seepage channels, ultimately leading to soil erosion and ground collapse.Accurate prediction is vital for designing structures that resist erosional damage and ensure long-term stability.

The earliest and most enduring geometric criterion, which is based on the geometric shape of the gravel soil gradation curve, is an empirical evaluation method. Due to its intuition and practicality, these methods continue to serve as the primary approaches for assessing the seepage stability of gravel soils. Gravel soils with gap gradation, which are susceptible to seepage erosion, can experience a transition in erosion modes due to the influence of their fine particle content, specifically from suffusion to piping erosion, accompanied by changes in permeability3,4. Previous studies have indicated that a fine grain fraction of 30% (GAQSIQ-CHN, 2022)5 is a critical state for gap-graded gravel soils. Beyond this threshold, coarse grains cannot form a dominate fabric or skeleton and tend to “float” within the finer grains, which limit is up to 35% fraction defined by Kenney and Lau (1985)6. Vaughan (2006)7 described this as an ‘‘over-filled’’ structure. However, this process requires a higher hydraulic gradient. Here, increased pore pressure results in a loss of shear strength. Zhong et al.8 found through their research that both specimen size and hydraulic loading history affect the critical hydraulic gradient, with the critical hydraulic gradient decreasing as the specimen length (seepage path) increases. Li et al.9 examined how hydraulic gradient, confining pressure, and initial density influence internal erosion and post-erosion behavior of gap-graded soils.In contrast, suffusion erosion occurs with lower hydraulic gradient (smaller than 1.0) and typically happens in what is considered a stable grading soil10. Therefore, the content of fine grains plays a crucial role in determining the erosion mode and internal stability of granular soil11. A fine particle content between 30% and 35% represents a critical and sensitive transition zone where the erosion mode shifts from suffusion to piping5,6. Recent studies on suffusion in gap-graded soils show that the erosion process can be divided into distinct stages and that soils with transitional microstructures are more sensitive to stress conditions12, while triaxial tests indicate that preferential flow paths dominate suffusion kinetics under constant mean effective stress, with a limited role of stress deviator13.

In recent years, the development of coupled Discrete Element Method and Computational Fluid Dynamics numerical simulations1,12,13 and micro-observation techniques such as tomography14,15 have provided new approaches for studying the hydrodynamic mechanisms and microstructural evolution of erosion at the particle scale16., However, from the perspective of geometric criteria, traditional methods struggle to precisely delineate the mesoscale processes of pore pressure or fluid flow variations with changing fine particle content. They also face challenges in directly observing the dynamic evolution of how fine particles gradually participate in the internal structural action of soils under near-critical conditions in large-scale engineering, particularly within the critical transition zone of fine particle content (30%–35%). Existing gradation-based criteria exhibit limitations and inconsistencies when evaluating the internal stability of gravelly soils18. While emerging approaches, such as entropy-based criteria19,20 and energy-based erosion theory21, attempt to provide more unified quantitative assessments, a deeper understanding is still required regarding the direct link between the microscopic mechanisms governing erosion mode transition and the corresponding macroscopic hydraulic responses within this critical fine-content transition zone.

From a geometric criterion perspective, it is challenging to precisely visualize how pore pressure or fluid flow changes with varying fine particle content. Moreover, directly observing variations in pore pressure on a large engineering scale, especially near critical state—specifically in the range of fine particle content from 30% to 35% where fine particles are believed to gradually participate in the internal structural action of granular soil—is challenging. Currently, an effective approach is to observe these changes directly at the pore scale using tomography techniques such as Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Computerized X-ray Tomography (CT), and other advanced technologies. These methods offer new avenues for studying the internal structure of granular soils22,23. By measuring the geometry structure of micro-pores and particles in µ-CT images, researchers can obtain the macroscopic hydraulic behaviors of sandstone by mechanic simulation, which is also applicable to the seepage erosion evaluation. Nguyen et al. showed through X-ray CT tests that piping induces heterogeneous evolution of the microstructure and physical properties of granular soils24,25.

This study employs µ-CT imaging to examine the hydraulic behavior of gap-graded gravel soils under near-critical conditions. Three distinct groups of granular soil specimens were analyzed, with incremental increases in fine-grain content and specific exclusions of particle sizes. First, a criterion integrated evaluation index is proposed mainly based on graded erosion principle. It innovatively links µ-CT microstructural information with macroscopic hydraulic responses and particle gradation evolution, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods that rely on single indicators, (e.g., Uniformity coefficient, Kenney & Lau index, optimal fine particle content) and the incompatibility in criteria for assessing the internal instability of gravelly soils, which has been a persistent challenge.The proposed model can predict the evolution of gradation and porosity during erosion and is cross-validated with multiple geometric criteria, achieving a more systematic assessment of erosion stability and modes such as Kenney and Lau. Second, the Micro-Mechanism of Erosion Mode Transition is researched derived from hydraulic criteria. It elucidates how fine-particle content dominates the flow intensity indirectly controlling the erosion mode by influencing the coarse-particle skeleton structure and quantitatively analyzes the high sensitivity of parameters such as hydraulic conductivity, deepening the understanding of the transition mechanism and the critical conditions.

This study aims to elucidate, through CT imaging analysis, the transformation laws of erosion modes and their corresponding imaging characteristics in gravelly soil subjected to progressive particle erosion within critical gradation ranges. The rationality and effectiveness of the methodology will be validated against geometric criteria. Furthermore, an evaluation method will be established to correlate microscopic imaging features with the permeability effects and erosion modes of gravelly soil.

Materials

Preparation of specimens

Three distinct groups of granular soil specimens were prepared and remoulded, each containing varying proportions of fine particles—30%, 32%, and 34%. These specimens were carefully packed into cylindrical Acrylic containers, each measuring 35 mm in height and diameter. They represented typical characteristics of gap-graded gravel soils, with specific particle size ranges deliberately omitted: 1–2 mm, 0.5–1.5 mm, and 0.25–0.5 mm. To capture detailed internal structures, high-resolution 3D X-ray microimaging was performed using the Zeiss Xradia 510 Versa system. This advanced imaging technology generated voxel datasets for each group, producing volumetric data sets of 1004 × 1014 × 1024 voxels. So, the imaging resolution achieved was 35 µm per voxel, ensuring comprehensive visualization and analysis of the soil specimens at the microscale. These datasets are crucial for studying the distribution and interaction of fine particles within the granular matrix, particularly in relation to the hydraulic behavior and permeability of gap-graded gravel soils near critical conditions. The principle of real-time X-ray CT scanning devices is shown in Fig. 1.

To thoroughly investigate the internal erosion behavior of gap-graded gravelly soils approaching critical conditions, we employed micro-computed tomography (µ-CT) imaging and processing techniques. These included region of interest (ROI) extraction, watershed threshold segmentation, coarse and fine particle statistics, and annotation methods to process the acquired CT scan images of soil samples (Fig. 2). These techniques enable high-resolution extraction and detailed analysis of the soil’s internal structure. The study utilized three sets of granular soil samples with varying fine particle contents, strictly controlled within the range of 30% to 35%.

The samples were categorized based on the absence of certain particle sizes. The particle sizes missing from each sample fell into three specific ranges: 1–2 mm, 0.5–1 mm, and 0.25–0.5 mm. By identifying and documenting these missing sizes, we were able to classify the samples into distinct groups.

However, while the Cu-based criterion offers certain reference value for macroscale erosion mode assessment, it has notable limitations in evaluating internal stability. These limitations are mainly reflected in the following aspects: First, as a global gradation parameter, Cu fails to accurately capture the mechanical interaction between fine particle fillings and the coarse-grained skeleton in gap-graded soils. Second, the method does not adequately account for the influence of fine particle content—even with the same Cu value, soils with different fine contents may show significant differences in internal stability and erosion mechanisms. Finally, microstructural characteristics (e.g., particle arrangement, pore connectivity) play a decisive role in internal stability, yet Cu cannot reflect such structural information. For instance, in the case shown in Fig. 2, where coarse particles are “floating” within a fine-grained matrix, the Cu value may exceed 20, but due to the lack of a stable coarse skeleton, the erosion mode is more likely to be internal instability-induced suffosion (structural collapse due to intensified seepage erosion) rather than stable suffusion (mere transport of fines). Therefore, in practical engineering applications, it is essential to incorporate multidimensional indicators such as fine particle content and pore structure parameters to establish a more systematic evaluation framework for the internal stability of gravelly soils. Advanced watershed threshold segmentation technology was applied to conduct a more detailed segmentation of three types of gap-graded gravel soil samples (Fig. 4), identifying large pores, small pores, matrix particles, and sporadically distributed mineral particles. This segmentation method allows for a clearer distinction of the microstructural characteristics of gravel soils, particularly the porosity and particle distribution, which are crucial for understanding the material’s erosional behavior under different environmental conditions28. In practical engineering, sieve analysis can obtain the gradation curves of gravelly soils, thereby providing rapid assessment indicators such as the coefficient of uniformity (Cu)4,29,30. However, through detailed image analysis of the soil microstructure, engineers can more accurately predict seepage-induced erosion modes. From a visual analysis perspective: Both Type - and Type - specimens exhibit distinctly unstable gradation characteristics, with significantly high fine content and localized occurrences of coarse particles being “floating” within the fine matrix. These specimens demonstrate clear suffosion susceptibility, though relatively high hydraulic gradients are required to trigger such erosion. In contrast, while Type - specimens maintain relatively stable overall gradation curves, they still possess potential suffusion risks even under low hydraulic gradients.

Theorietical background

Graded erosion principle for gravel soils

For some erodible gravel soils with wider or gap-graded particle sizes, there exists a principle known as graded internal erosion. This process involves the selective erosion of finer grain sizes, which occurs at specific critical hydraulic gradients. Essentially, the finer the particles, the lower the hydraulic gradient required to mobilize and transport them within the range of granular, non-cohesive soils (e.g., sands and silts). This trend reverses for cohesive clay particles, where electrochemical binding forces significantly increase the resistance to erosion, often requiring higher hydraulic gradients or prior structural breakdown for transport to occur.This principle highlights how different soil particle sizes behave differently under flowing water, influencing erosion patterns accordingly4,17,45. Actually, during the erosion process of gravel soil, the value of d0 continually changes, altering the erosion limit SIG (%) for each state. Thus, an implicit procedure constantly monitors these changes. Once if fine grains are not completely eroded and the erosion conditions persist, the erosion process continues to guide the PSD curve to a stable state. To facilitate this analysis, a computer subroutine (Granular_soil_PSD) has been developed in MATLAB to plot the PSD curve for any given state.

Grading State Index IG

The changes in soil particle gradation can occur through two main mechanisms: first, particle breakage leads to an increase in fine particles, and second, the slow transport of fine particles leads to a decrease in fine particles. These changes can affect the fundamental properties of the soil, particularly the characteristics of its critical state33. Obviously, the issue of graded erosion in gap-graded gravel soil discussed in this paper pertains to the latter mechanism. In the process of internal erosion, the Fuller curve generally served as a limiting grading to determine the ultimate eroded state of the granular soil with a particle size distribution.

Muir and Maeda (2008)33propose a grading state index to conveniently describe the changing grading of the soil, along with the rules governing how this index changes with compression or erosion, as well as its impact on the soil’s mechanical properties. A definition of the grading state index, IG, in a semi-logarithmic gradation plot is derived by comparing the areas under the current particle size distribution and under a limiting distribution, i.e. Fuller limit (Fig. 6a). The IG34 ranges from 0 to 1, reflecting the transition of soil grading from a uniform particle size to a limiting grading, which may potentially exhibit fractal characteristics35.

It is important to emphasize that Eq. 8 leads to two primary implications. The first implication is that erosion occurs without threshold of hydraulic gradient below which the process does not occur. In soils with a well-graded particle distribution, particularly those with fine components within the silt range, this assumption is reasonable, as even a minimal hydraulic gradient can induce erosion of the finest particles. However, a different behavior may be anticipated in more uniform coarse soils, where a specific threshold for the hydraulic gradient, beyond which erosion occurs, can be identified38.

The second implication is that, if sufficient time is provided, complete erosion will occur, meaning the process will entirely remove the fine particles (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{m}$$\end{document} → m0 as t → ∞). However, this second limitation is unlikely to be realistic in engineering practice. In fact, when the gradient values are within those typically encountered in practical applications, the theoretical time required for complete erosion becomes exceedingly long (hundreds or thousands of years), thus losing its engineering significance.

Erosion mode identification: A grading entropy framework

The grading entropy-based diagram has emerged as a significant tool for predicting the internal erosion stability of soils, becoming a focal point of research in recent years. By calculating the base entropy and entropy increment of the soil particle distribution, this approach facilitates the prediction of the system’s thermodynamic state and the degree of disorder. These metrics provide insights into the internal erosional stability of the soil, as well as potential erosion mode and their evolutionary direction20,44. The fundamental principles underlying this methodology have been proposed and applied in various representative cases, with a detailed exposition provided in the Appendix A.

Figure 9a shows that all of -specimen (L, M, or H) are unstable during the first two stages of erosion, indicating a vulnerability to erosion or failure in the initial phase. However, by State 2, these groups stabilize, meaning they reach a condition where they no longer experience significant destabilization or erosion during this phase. The erosion mode in this phase is piping, where water flows through internal voids or channels, further degrading the soil. This piping process continues until it reaches Zone II, where the erosion behavior stabilizes, and the specimens show resistance to further internal erosion. In the Zone II, the fine particles are fulfilling the voids between coarse particles and participate in the interaction among the structural skeleton.

It is worth to note that the A-value for relative base entropy in the -specimen (L) lies just to the right of the 2/3 line, signaling critical stability. Comparing this to Fig. 8a, the grading curve aligns with the upper limit of the Fuller curve (n = 0.5). Meanwhile, the final stable grading curve corresponds to the Fuller curve (n = 0.6), suggesting that two erosion phases are required, as outlined by the graded internal erosion analysis. The results from the graded erosion calculation closely match those derived from the entropy-based diagram, with only slight discrepancies.

Figure 9b presents more conclusive results, showing that each of the -specimen reaches stability (2/3 line) after one erosion phase (two states), except for the -specimen (H), which undergoes two erosion phases (three states). These results, compared with the corresponding DSP diagram (Fig. 7d and f), align with the upper limit of the Fuller curve (n = 0.5). This suggests that the Fuller curve’s criterion (n = 0.6) for gravel soil erosion stability may be somewhat conservative, as it overestimates the number of erosion phases needed for stability in some cases.

Erosion mode evaluation: a Kenney & Lau framework

Further assessment of the erosion stability has been carried out by integrating other methodologies, such as the one proposed by Kenney and Lau (1985)6, which is based on the geometric characteristics of the fine-grain part of the DSP curve. In Fig. 12a, the curves of F versus H’ (mass fraction between a specified grain size D and 4D) are plotted, along with the unstable boundary defined by Kenney and Lau’s criterion (shadowed area). According to this criterion, soil instability is indicated if the F versus H’ curve lies partially or entirely within the shadowed region. However, relying solely on the F versus H’ curve for accurate judgment may not always be sufficient. A more reliable interpretation involves considering the value of (H’/F)min, which is more informative. Figure 12b, a dual diagram corresponding to Fig. 12a, offers a more precise evaluation by referencing the position of the (H’/F)min value.

From Fig. 12a, it can be seen that for and -specimens, the F values in the range of 10% to 30% lie within the shadowed area. In Fig. 12b, it can also be observed that they have almost the same (H′/F)min, both of which are located within the shadowed area. Based on this result, it can be concluded that internal erosion may cause instability in these specimens. The behavior of -specimen is consistent with previous evaluations. However, -specimen differs from the optimal fine particle method in terms of erosion mode, as the latter follows a transformation mode. The reason for this is believed to be related to the density of the fine particles, which has had a certain impact and requires further assessment. Nonetheless, it can be reasonably inferred that -specimen tends to transition toward a piping erosion mode that could destabilize the structure.

The -specimen, although within the shadowed area in the F value range of 5% to 28%, can still be judged as critically stable. This conclusion can be further confirmed through the (H′/F)min value on the F ~ H′/F curve in Fig. 12b, where the values of this sample are located near the stability boundary. Based on its (H′/F)min value, the sample can be classified as critically stable. In this version, the optimal filling space for fine particles, ϕₒ, is directly related to the grain size distribution characteristics of the coarse fraction. However, the assumption of uniform coarse grains is inherently ambiguous and highly sensitive, making it difficult to establish a definitive interpretation. This assumption is not only closely tied to the compaction characteristics of coarse and fine particles in gravelly soils but may also be influenced by the material properties of the grains themselves. For instance, a study by Slangen & Fannin(2017)44 demonstrated significant differences in the sensitivity to various erosion modes between glass beads and natural soil particles.

The improved version of Equation B3 (GAQSIQ-CHN, 2022)5 simplifies the calculation of ϕₒ by setting it to a fixed empirical value of 0.30. This change essentially transforms the criterion into an empirical assessment. It is these theoretical and empirical uncertainties that lead to the ambiguous classification of the critically positioned Sample Group in this study.

Discussion

Based on the comprehensive experimental and analytical results presented, the following key points warrant in-depth discussion regarding the internal erosion behavior of gap-graded gravelly soils, particularly within the critical fine content transition zone (30–35%).

Interplay between fine particle content and erosion mode transition

The experimental data robustly confirm that fine particle content is the predominant factor governing the transition between suffusion and suffosion (piping) erosion modes. For specimens in Groups and (with higher initial uniformity coefficients, Cu > 20), the erosion process initiated as suffusion. However, as the fine content approached and exceeded approximately 30–35%, a shift towards suffosion was observed. This transition is mechanistically linked to the structural role of fines. Below the critical threshold, fines primarily fill voids within a stable coarse-grained skeleton. As the fine content increases, this skeleton becomes progressively “over-filled“7, leading to a state where coarse particles “float” within the fine matrix. This transition fundamentally alters the soil’s fabric from a coarse-dominated skeleton to a fine-dominated matrix, drastically reducing its shear strength. The -specimen group, with its inherently stable gradation (Cu < 10), consistently exhibited suffusion behavior without transitioning to suffosion, underscoring the stability provided by a well-interlocked coarse skeleton.

Validation and limitations of geometric criteria

This study provides a critical evaluation of various geometric criteria for erosion assessment. The results generally align with the Kenney & Lau6 and grading entropy criteria, which successfully identified the instability of - and -specimens and the stability of -specimens. The grading entropy framework, in particular, offered a thermodynamic perspective, effectively mapping the evolution of specimens towards stability as fines were eroded.

However, significant discrepancies emerged with the optimal fine-grain content criterion, especially for the critically positioned -specimen group. The high sensitivity of this criterion to the assumed porosity of the coarse skeleton (ϕc or ϕ0) highlights a major limitation. The theoretical calculation is highly dependent on an idealized characterization of the coarse skeleton’s porosity, which is influenced by grain shape, packing, and compaction—factors not fully captured by gradation curves alone. This underscores that while geometric criteria are valuable for initial screening, their accuracy can be compromised for soils in transitional states.

The role of µ-CT imaging in elucidating micro-mechanisms

The application of µ-CT imaging was pivotal in bridging the gap between macroscopic hydraulic behavior and microscopic soil fabric. The visualizations directly revealed the “floating” coarse grains in - and -specimens with high fine content, providing tangible evidence for the “over-filled” fabric concept. This microstructural insight explains why these specimens required higher hydraulic gradients to initiate erosion but were then prone to more catastrophic suffosion failure. For the -specimen, the images confirmed a stable coarse skeleton with fines acting as void fillers. The ability to segment and quantify pores and particles paves the way for future quantitative correlations between microstructural parameters and macroscopic erosion susceptibility.

Implications of the graded erosion principle and predictive model

The proposed graded erosion principle and its mathematical formulation offer a dynamic framework for predicting the evolution of soil properties during erosion. The model’s success in forecasting the progression of PSD curves towards a stable Fuller limit and the concomitant increase in porosity is a significant step forward. By incorporating a time- and gradient-dependent erosion rate that is sensitive to particle size, the improved model addresses a key limitation of previous empirical models that lacked a physical erosion threshold. This allows for a more realistic simulation of the erosion process. The prediction that - and -specimens required multiple erosion phases to reach stability, while -specimens remained stable, aligns well with physical expectations.

Limitations and future research

Regarding the surface morphological characteristics of particles, Didier Marot et al43. investigated the influence of coarse particle angularity on internal seepage erosion in cohesive sandy soils. They found that increased angularity could enhance erosion resistance by up to five times and reduce hydraulic conductivity. As suggested by Slangen and Fannin (2017)44, future research should incorporate the effects of particle shape and mineralogy. Additionally, the sensitivity of the parameter ϕ₀ in the optimal fine-content criterion warrants experimental validation across various soil types. Finally, integrating real-time monitoring with µ-CT imaging holds the potential to bridge the gap between laboratory models and field-scale erosion dynamics.

It is important to note that the graded erosion model proposed in this study is a phenomenological model. Its core contribution lies in utilizing microstructural information to predict the evolution of macroscopic soil properties (such as gradation and porosity) with varying fine particle content, and in achieving cross-validation with multiple traditional geometric criteria. It is not designed to simulate the fluid-solid interaction mechanisms at the particle scale (as revealed, for example, by CFD-DEM approaches). This clarification defines the positioning of our model within the current research framework. Its strength lies in establishing a bridge between microscopic observations and macroscopic engineering assessments, while its simplification of fundamental mechanical processes points the way for future development of multi-scale models that integrate microscopic mechanisms with macroscopic responses.

It should be noted that the validation of the multi-criteria integrated evaluation framework established in this study is primarily based on a series of carefully designed laboratory tests and numerical model cases. Although these cases are sufficient to clearly reveal the controlling influence of fine particle content on the transition of erosion modes within the critical transition zone and to verify the logical consistency of the proposed method, the number of analyzed specimens remains relatively limited. Future research is necessary to further validate and refine the universality and robustness of this framework across a broader range of soil types and under more complex stress paths and hydraulic conditions.

In conclusion, the transition of erosion modes in gap-graded gravelly soils is a complex process predominantly controlled by fine particle content. Integrating µ-CT imaging with the proposed graded erosion principle provides a powerful, microstructurally-informed framework for understanding and predicting internal erosion behavior, ultimately contributing to the construction of more resilient geotechnical structures.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated internal erosion in gap-graded gravelly soils, focusing on the critical fine content transition zone (30–35%). Through integrated µ-CT imaging, geometric criteria analysis, and a newly developed predictive model, key conclusions emerge:

(1) When the fine particle content exceeds 30–35%, the transition of erosion modes is dominated by the controlled morphology of the soil structure. In this study, the proposed hierarchical erosion model, within a multi-scale integrated framework, successfully predicts the evolution of gradation toward the Fuller stability limit and the associated porosity changes, thereby providing a robust and innovative approach to elucidating and forecasting the critical relationship between erosion modes and structural evolution. (2) The study validates a multi-criteria assessment framework. While traditional geometric criteria (Cu, Kenney & Lau) provide useful screening, they show limitations for transitional soils. The grading entropy criterion offers more robust stability evaluation, demonstrating that no single method suffices for comprehensive assessment.

(3) The proposed graded erosion model successfully predicts PSD evolution toward stable Fuller limits and porosity changes. By incorporating particle-size-sensitive, time-dependent erosion rates with physical thresholds, it overcomes previous limitations and provides a realistic framework for forecasting long-term soil evolution.

This research establishes that erosion modes directly reflect fine-content-controlled soil fabric. The integration of advanced imaging, multi-faceted analysis, and physically-based modeling significantly enhances erosion prediction capabilities, contributing to more resilient geotechnical structures. Future work should focus on model refinement and quantitative µ-CT analysis to strengthen micro-macro correlations.
