Zaxinone mimics boost growth and productivity of wheat under normal and low-nutrient regimes
Authors: Arshad Jalal, Mohamed Salem, Muhammad Jamil, Lamis Berqdar, Kit Xi Liew, Yagiz Alagoz, Abdugaffor Ablazov, Pei-Yu Lin, Amer Alrudayan, Marcelo Carvalho Minhoto Teixeira Filho, Tadao Asami, Salim Al-Babili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing demand for sustainable agriculture requires innovative strategies to reduce reliance on synthetic fertilizers without compromising crop growth and productivity. Plant-derived biostimulants are among the transformative strategies for enhancing nutrient use efficiency, crop resilience and productivity across diverse ecosystems. Therefore, this study investigated the potential of two novel naturally-inspired growth stimulants, MiZax3 and MiZax5, to enhance wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) growth and yield under reduced NPK fertilizer across multiple growth systems, including hydroponic, greenhouse, and field conditions.ResultsIn hydroponic systems, application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 at 5 µM with 50% NPK significantly improved root length, crown root development, and fresh and dry plant biomass, achieving performance comparable to full NPK fertilization. Greenhouse trials further revealed that MiZax3 and MiZax5 with 50% NPK remarkably increased growth and yield attributes, often surpassing 100% NPK fertilization. Notably, the number of grains spike− 1 and grain weight were increased by 25–34% and 36–45%, respectively, compared with full fertilization. Field experiments validated these findings, MiZax3 and MiZax5 with 50% NPK treatments promoting vegetative growth, enhancing photosynthetic performance, and increasing grain yield by 8–11% over full-strength NPK alone. Moreover, metabolite profiling revealed that MiZax application boosts carbon and nitrogen assimilation associated with increasing free sugars and growth-related amino acids. When applied with 50% NPK, MiZax further amplified sugar availability and amino acid accumulation while modulating central carbon metabolism, demonstrating synergistic enhancement of metabolic efficiency.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings demonstrate that MiZax functions both independently and integrated with reduced fertilization to optimize metabolic allocation toward growth-supporting pathways and nutrient-use efficiency, highlighting their potential as sustainable biostimulants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08895-y.Keywords: Biostimulants, MiZax, Zaxinone, wheat yield, NPK fertilizer, Sustainable agriculture

Introduction

Food demand is increasing at an unprecedented rate due to rapid global population growth, projected to reach 10 billion by 2050, and the intensified ongoing climate change [1]. These demographic pressures further threaten global food security by increasing reliance on high-input agricultural practices that are often neither economically viable for resource-poor farmers nor environmentally sustainable [2]. Among major cereal crops, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one the primary drivers in global food security, serving as a staple crop for over 35% of the global population and contributing over 20% of daily caloric intake [3]. Its global importance stems from wide adaptability across diverse agro-climatic regions [4], making it the world’s second most cultivated cereal crop, grown across an estimated area of 200 million hectares [5, 6]. Ensuring higher wheat productivity, therefore remains a critical priority for sustaining global food security, particularly in regions with poor soil conditions.

The arid and semi-arid regions with inadequate plant growth media intensify threats to food security by hindering root establishment, nutrient uptake, and water retention [7]. Soil texture is primarily determined by parent material that plays a crucial role in stabilizing soil organic matter through organo-mineral interactions [8]. Under these challenging conditions, synthetic fertilizers remain essential to modern agriculture, primarily to replenish depleted macronutrients (N, P, K) critical for plant growth [9, 10]. However, the overuse of these fertilizers raises significant concerns like degrading soil fertility, polluting water resources, and accelerating greenhouse gas emissions [11]. In addition, limited resources such as phosphate rock introduce another uncertainty regarding the long-term sustainability relying on conventional fertilizers [12].

Numerous technological and agronomic strategies are being explored to tackle these challenges and enhance crop productivity while reducing overreliance on synthetic inputs [13]. In particular, biostimulants have garnered attention as promising tools for improving plant health, nutrient uptake, and stress tolerance through bioactive compounds that influence plant metabolic processes [14, 15]. Biostimulants can be applied in various ways, such as seed treatments, soil amendments, or foliar sprays, which increase the yield and quality of different crops by stimulating plant growth and resilience [16, 17]. Among the emerging biostimulants, zaxinone, a naturally derived apocarotenoid essential for the normal growth of rice, synthesized by the enzyme zaxinone synthase (ZAS), has gained significant attention for its role in promoting root development, optimizing sugar metabolism, and modulating hormone balance [18–21]. However, the large-scale use of zaxinone is limited due to its restricted natural availability and complex organic synthesis. To overcome this challenge, we developed two easily synthesized zaxinone analogs, MiZax3 and MiZax5, which demonstrated comparable biological activities and are therefore suitable for broad application in agricultural cropping systems, showing efficacy in both hydroponic and soil-grown wild-type rice [22]. These analogs improved growth, yield, and quality in various horticultural crops, including capsicum, potato, saffron, and strawberry, under greenhouse and open-field conditions [23–26]. In addition, MiZax improved growth, yield and quality of various horticultural crops at 5–10 µM, at much lower concentrations than the widely used commercial biostimulant humic acid (1–1.5 g L⁻¹), highlighting its efficacy compared with conventional biostimulants [23].

The inadequate plant growth conditions are considered one of the devastating agricultural challenges for food security, with pronounced impacts in arid and semi-arid agroecosystems [27]. Therefore, we hypothesized that amending the nutrient-poor medium integrated with NPK fertilizers and MiZax application would enhance primary metabolites and nutrient use efficiency that led to better plant growth and yield of wheat. In this context, the main objective of this study was to investigate the potential use of zaxinone mimics, MiZax3 and MiZax5, as novel biostimulants, in combination with NPK fertilizers, on the growth, yield, primary metabolites and nutrients content of wheat under different growth mediums, addressing challenges to sustainable agricultural practices and food security.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and growth conditions

Experiments were conducted in three growth conditions: (1) environmentally controlled hydroponic system, (2) greenhouse conditions, and (3) an open field at the Agricultural Research Field Site of the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST; 22°18’08.6″N, 39°06’40.0″E), Saudi Arabia (Fig. S1).

Experimental design

The experiments under hydroponic and greenhouse growth conditions were conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD), while a randomized complete block design (RCBD) was implemented for the open field.

Hydroponic experiment

Local wheat seeds were treated with 30% (v/v) commercial bleach for 10 min and rinsed 10 times with sterile water in a laminar flow cabinet. The sterilized seeds were then germinated on ½ MS (0.4% agarose) medium and incubated for 24 h in the dark at 30 °C. Afterward, the seeds were transferred to a growth chamber where the temperature was maintained at 28 °C during the day and 22 °C at night, with a photoperiod of 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness. The photon irradiance was set at 500 µmol m− 2 s− 1, and the air relative humidity was kept at 70%. Subsequently, the 7-day-old seedlings were cultivated hydroponically into 50 mL black falcon tubes for 4 weeks in Hoagland’s nutrient solution, which contains 5.6 mM NH4NO3, 0.8 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.8 mM K2SO4, 0.18 mM FeSO4·7H2O, 0.02 mM Na2EDTA·2H2O, 1.6 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.8 mM KNO3, 0.4 mM K2HPO4.3H2O, 0.023 mM H3BO3, 0.0045 mM MnCl2·4H2O, 0.0003 mM CuSO4·5H2O, 0.0015 mM ZnCl2, and 0.0001 mM Na2MoO4·2H2O, adhering to the previously described protocol [21]. Treatments were added by suspending the reagents in acetone before adding them to the Hoagland solution (pH: 5.7). The treatments consisted of: T1 = Control (without MiZax and NPK), T2 = MiZax3 (5.0 µM), T3 = MiZax5 (5.0 µM), T4 = NPK 100%, T5 = NPK 100% + MiZax3 (5.0 µM), T6 = NPK 100% + MiZax5 (5.0 µM), T7 = NPK 50%, T8 = NPK 50% + MiZax3 (5.0 µM), T9 = NPK 50% + MiZax5 (5.0 µM), T10 = NPK 25%, T11 = NPK 25% + MiZax3 (5.0 µM), and T12 = NPK 25% + MiZax5 (5.0 µM). The treatments corresponded to the amounts of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) in the Hoagland solution; 1.0 mL of NPK L− 1 of water correspond to 100% NPK, 0.5 mL of NPK L− 1 of water correspond to 50% NPK, and 0.25 mL of NPK L− 1 of water correspond to 25% NPK. The nutrient solution was replenished twice a week with the treatments of 5.0 µM MiZax3 and MiZax5 (solved in 0.05% acetone). The concentration of MiZax was chosen based on the previous studies [24, 25, 28]. For each treatment, a total of 8 biological replicates (n = 8) were harvested after four weeks, and phenotype for crown root, root and shoot length, plant fresh and dry biomass.

Greenhouse experiment

Uniform-sized seeds of the local wheat variety were directly sown in 4 L plastic pots filled with Stender soil in a greenhouse under specific conditions (temperature: 28 °C day/25°C night; photoperiod: 12 h light/12 h dark; light intensity: ~800 µmol m−2s− 1; and relative humidity: 70%). Each pot was initially planted with four seeds and allowed to germinate in the same environment. Uniform seedlings of the same age (approximately 2 weeks old, at the 2–3 true leaf stage) were selected for growth, while the extras were removed to maintain one plant per pot. The plants were irrigated with tap water twice a week. The treatments comprised MiZax foliar spray, MiZax3 (5 µM), MiZax5 (5 µM), reduced fertilizer doses (recommended dose-100%, half of the recommended dose-50%, and a quarter of the recommended dose-25%), along with a control containing acetone. The stock solution of MiZax3 and − 5 was prepared by suspending the active ingredient in acetone, creating a 10 mM suspension, and storing it at -20 °C for future foliar spray. Before each foliar application, the stock solutions (5 µM) were freshly prepared by diluting the stock to the required concentration with water. The foliar sprays were applied using a handheld sprayer to ensure full leaf canopy coverage. MiZax3 and − 5 were sprayed eight times with a week interval during the crop growth cycle, with the first spray conducted two weeks after sowing. Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) were applied from fertilizer sources such as urea (N-P2O5-K2O: 46-0-0), diammonium phosphate (DAP: N-P2O5-K2O: 18-46-0), and sulfate of potassium (SOP: N-P2O5-K2O: 0-0-51 + 18% sulfur) in three splits (planting, tillering, and heading initiation). Fertilization was performed at the 100% dose (N-P-K ratios of 120 kg ha− 1, 40 kg ha− 1, and 100 kg ha− 1), 50% of the recommended dose (N-P-K ratios of 60 kg ha− 1, 20 kg ha− 1, and 50 kg ha− 1), and 25% of the recommended dose (N-P-K ratios of 30 kg ha− 1, 10 kg ha− 1, and 25 kg ha− 1). Each treatment was replicated six times. The pots were shuffled every week till the grain filling stage, to maintain randomization and even distribution of the light. For each treatment, fertilizers were applied to the pot surface and immediately irrigated to ensue consistent nutrient delivery.

Open field experiment

Experimental field characterization

An open-field experiment was conducted during the winter growing season at the Agricultural Research Field Site, KAUST, located on the shores of the Red Sea, Saudi Arabia. The experimental site is characterized by an arid climate with an average annual rainfall of < 100 mm and an average relative humidity of 60–70%. The mean temperature ranged between 18 °C and 30 °C during the wheat-growing season.

Before sowing, the experimental field was plowed twice, and random soil samples (0–25 cm depth) were collected from across the field. The samples were properly mixed to make a homogenous composite soil sample that was analyzed for the chemical characterizations and soil type [29], summarized in Table 1.

Experimental design and treatments

The field experiment was designed using randomized complete blocks (RCBD) in a 3 ⋅ 4 factorial schemes with four replicates. The treatments included three MiZax foliar sprays (control-without MiZax, MiZax3, and MiZax5 at a concentration of 5 µM each) and four rates of NPK fertilization: 0.0%, the full recommended dose (100%), half of the recommended dose (50%), and quarter of the recommended dose (25%). The treatments followed the same protocol as the greenhouse experiment. The MiZax treatments involved suspending MiZax3 and MiZax5 (from the 10 mM stock) in one liter of water and applying it with a handheld sprayer for each plot. The NPK treatments at the 100% dose (N-P-K ratios of 120 kg ha− 1, 40 kg ha− 1, and 100 kg ha− 1), 50% of the recommended dose (N-P-K ratios of 60 kg ha− 1, 20 kg ha− 1, and 50 kg ha− 1) and 25% of the recommended dose (N-P-K ratios of 30 kg ha− 1, 10 kg ha− 1, and 25 kg ha− 1) were manually applied to the surface of each plot without incorporation. The amounts of N, P, and K for each dose were evenly split into three split applications: during plantation, during the tillering stage, and at heading initiation.

Crop management

The regionally adapted wheat variety (Saudi local variety; provided by Prof. Magdi A. A. Mousa, Department of Arid Land Agriculture, King Abdulaziz University, Saudi Arabia) was sown manually on November 18, 2023. The seeds were planted in rows spaced 30 cm apart within a 2 m × 1 m plot, leaving a 0.7 m buffer zone between plots to minimize spray drift and fertilizer overlap. All plots received consistent irrigation with fresh water through a subsurface drip irrigation system. Standard drip irrigation was supplied with fresh water via a subsurface drip irrigation system (Rain Bird LD-06-12-1000 Landscape drip, operating at 3.4 L h⁻¹ per dripper), scheduled for 10 min daily until flowering and then adjusted to 15 min every other day until harvest, based on the crop’s requirements and local climate conditions. Weeds were managed manually. All other agronomic practices were standardized across all treatments. In each plot, the four central rows were harvested manually on February 27, 2024, and placed into 50 kg plastic bags. The collected spikes were threshed using an electric thresher in separately labeled paper bags and weighed on an electronic balance.

Plant sampling and evaluations

Growth and yield

Plant growth traits, including plant height, number of tillers plant⁻¹, and plant biomass of wheat, were recorded according to standard protocols. Plant height was measured with the help of a measuring tape from the soil surface to the tip of the topmost fully opened leaf. Tillers were counted manually in each treatment. Plants were harvested at the ground surface and oven-dried at 65 °C for 72 h to determine the plant dry weight, using a digital balance. Spike length was measured, and spikelets spike⁻¹ were counted. A hundred-grain weight was determined by counting 100 grains and weighing with a digital balance. In greenhouse conditions, grains from each spike were individually harvested and weighed for grain weight plant⁻¹. In field conditions, four central rows were harvested from each plot and quantified for shoot biomass in kg ha⁻¹. The harvested plants were dried, mechanically threshed, and weighed, then converted the values into kg ha⁻¹. Leaf chlorophyll quantification

Chlorophyll was extracted from frozen leaf tissue following a modified protocol [31]. Equal amounts of tissue (25 mg) were placed in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes, ground to a fine powder with 2 mm metal beads and mixed with 1 mL of 80% acetone. Samples were vortexed for 30 s, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and centrifuged at 4,282 × g for 5 min. A 200 µL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance at 645 nm, 663 nm, and 470 nm was measured using a Tecan Infinite M1000 Pro microplate reader [32, 33].

Leaf nutritional analysis

Twenty leaves were collected at the heading stage of the wheat. The leaves were dried for 72 h in a forced-air oven at 65 °C. The samples were ground in a stainless-steel Wiley knife mill by passing through a 10-mm-mesh sieve, stored in labeled plastic bags, and determined for the concentration of N, P, and K using a semi-micro Kjeldahl sulfuric digestion method [34].

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis

Leaves from each treatment were harvested and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were subsequently freeze-dried for 24 h and finely ground to a homogeneous powder. For metabolite extraction, 10 mg of dried material was extracted with 1 mL of methyl-tert-butyl ether (MTBE)/ methanol (3:1, v/v). The extracts were vortex-mixed for 30 s and incubated on a shaker at 4 °C for 30 min. Phase separation was induced by the addition of 0.7 mL of water/methanol (3:1, v/v), followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C [35]. Aliquots (100 µL) of the lower polar phase were transferred to fresh microcentrifuge tubes and dried under vacuum using a SpeedVac concentrator. Dried extracts were derivatized by methoximation with 30 µL of methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg mL⁻¹ in pyridine), vortexed briefly, and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. Silylation was then performed by adding 70 µL of MSTFA containing n-alkane standards, followed by vortexing and incubation for 30 min at 37 °C. The derivatized samples were transferred to GC vials, and 1 µL was injected for analysis. GC–MS analysis was conducted using a gas chromatograph coupled to an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, GC Orbitrap GC-MS/MS). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL min⁻¹. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a DB-5 MS capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). The injector temperature was maintained at 280 °C, while the transfer line and ion source were set to 300 °C. The oven temperature program was initiated at 70 °C with a 2-min hold, ramped at 8 °C min⁻¹ to 220 °C, then increased at 16 °C min⁻¹ to a final temperature of 325 °C, which was held for 10 min. Mass spectra were acquired in full-scan mode over an m/z range of 35–700. Data processing and metabolite annotation were performed using MS-DIAL version 4.2 [36].

Statistical analysis

The Two-way ANOVA (F-test) was used to analyze the growth and yield data of wheat obtained from hydroponics, greenhouse, and open-field conditions. The statistical differences were determined by two-way ANOVA with a probability level of p < 0.05, using Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Pearson correlation and principal component analysis (PCA), using FactoMineR and factoextra packages in R-studio version 2024.04.2 + 764, were performed to identify the influence of treatments on growth and yield of wheat and the correlation between the studied traits [37]. The graphs were made using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1. Metabolite abundance was represented via heatmaps generated in Multiple Experiment Viewer (MeV_4_9_0) [38].

Results

MiZax improve the wheat growth at reduced level of NPK fertilizer under hydroponic conditions

The hydroponic system demonstrated that combining MiZax3 or MiZax5 at 5 µM across various concentrations of the nutrient solution significantly enhanced the initial growth of wheat (Table S1; Fig. 1). The number of crown roots was increased with application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 at the concentration of 5 µM at half-strength nutrient solution compared to the control (Fig. 1A, B). These treatments were observed with 79% and 80% higher number of crown roots as compared to control (Table S1). Similarly, longer roots were observed with integrated application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 under half-strength nutrient solution, compared control (Fig. 1A, C). The treatments with MiZax3 and MiZax5 at half-strength nutrient solution were observed with an increase of 113% and 105% in root length of wheat seedlings, respectively (Table S1).

In addition, MiZax3 and MiZax5 at half-strength nutrient solution resulted in longer shoots compared to control (Fig. 1A, D). Likewise, the fresh plant biomass of hydroponic wheat was significantly enhanced with the combined application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 with half-strength nutrient solution , which was statistically comparable to the treatments with MiZax3 + full-strength nutrient solution relative to the control (Fig. 1A, E). The treatments involving MiZax3 and MiZax5 at 50% nutrient solution exhibited greatest plant dry biomass (Fig.1A, F), with an increase of ∼61 and 50% as compared to control, respectively (Table S1).

MiZax improve the growth and yield of wheat at reduced level of NPK fertilizer under greenhouse conditions

The various levels of NPK fertilizers combined with MiZax3 and MiZax5 at a concentration of 5 µM positively influenced the growth of wheat under greenhouse conditions (Fig. 2A). We observed that the interaction between various levels of NPK fertilizers and biostimulants was significant in plant height (Fig. 2B) and productive tillers (Fig. 2C) of wheat. The treatments of MiZax3 and MiZax5 with 50% NPK fertilizer increased plant height (39%) as compared to the control (Fig. 2B; Table S2). The shortest plants were observed in control treatments. Also, the treatments of MiZax3 and MiZax5 with 50% fertilizer increased the number of productive tillers by 141% and 127%, respectively as compared to control, which were even more than in the fully fertilized treatments (Fig. 2C; Table S2).

Growth and yield traits

Field study demonstrated that the foliar spray of MiZax in combination with soil-applied NPK fertilizer significantly enhanced wheat growth attributes (Fig. 4A; Table S4). We observed a significant interaction between MiZax and fertilizer levels for the plant height, number of total tillers, and plant dry biomass of wheat (Fig. 4B-D). Under 50% reduced NPK, MiZax3 and MiZax5 increased plant height to 72 and 70 cm, and the number of tillers (166 and 158 tillers m− 2), compared to control treatments (Table S4). In addition, plant dry biomass was increased by 47% and 41% with application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 with 50% NPK, respectively, compared to control (Fig. 4D; Table S4).

The application of MiZax and NPK enhanced leaf chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, whereas their interactions were not significant (Table 3). GC-MS metabolic profiling reveals MiZax-driven modulation of primary metabolites alone and in combination with NPK

Metabolomic profiling revealed significant changes in wheat leaves, treated with MiZax3 and MiZax5 at 5 µM concentration (Fig. 7). MiZax treatment resulted in a consistent increase in free sugars, particularly glucose and fructose, relative to control plants. Concomitantly, several amino acids closely associated with nitrogen assimilation and growth, including glutamine, arginine, glycine, and alanine, were elevated. Moderate increases were also observed in branched-chain (e.g., leucine and valine) and aromatic amino acids (e.g., tyrosine), indicating a broad stimulation of amino acid metabolism. In contrast to controls, MiZax treatment increased phosphorylated sugars, including glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate, reflecting enhanced carbon assimilation, supporting biosynthetic processes. Notably, galactinol level was reduced under MiZax, consistent with decreased allocation toward stress-related raffinose family oligosaccharide. Additionally, β-alanine levels declined, suggesting a potential decreased investment in stress-related amino acid, further emphasizing the growth-promoting metabolic processes induced by MiZax. Consistently, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between MiZax-treated and control samples. This multivariate distinction underscores MiZax-driven reprogramming of primary metabolites. While application of 50% NPK alone produced limited changes in soluble sugars and amino acids, the combination of MiZax with 50% NPK resulted in a markedly stronger metabolic change (Fig. S2). This treatment further increased the accumulation of glucose, fructose, and sucrose, alongside pronounced elevations in amino acids, particularly asparagine, arginine, glycine, valine, and alanine. Additionally, the observed increase in fumarate upon treatment with MiZax combined with 50% NPK suggests an induction of TCA cycle intermediates toward energy production, possibly supporting enhanced growth and stress adaptation, even in the context of partial NPK nutrition. Overall, the combined MiZax and reduced NPK treatment produced a metabolic profile distinct from NPK alone, characterized by efficient carbon utilization and enhanced growth-related metabolite pools.

Discussion

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the main global cereal crops, providing a major share of calories and protein for over one-third of the global population [3]. Sustaining its productivity is critical for global food security but remains challenged due to low soil fertility, limited nutrient availability, and the rising costs and environmental impacts associated with intensive fertilizer use [39]. These constraints are prominent in arid and semi-arid regions, where soils are poor in organic matter and deficient in essential macronutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) [39, 40]. The soil of the current experimental site is classified as sandy loam and characterized by a relatively high pH, low electrical conductivity, low organic matter, and nutrient deficiency, particularly with nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (Table 1). These unfavorable characteristics make the soil less productive. Therefore, sustaining crop productivity under such marginal conditions, particularly in dryland agriculture, requires integration of fertilizers and plant growth regulators/biostimulants.

Biostimulants are substances and microbes that can enhance plant growth, nutrient use efficiency, and tolerance to abiotic stress [41]. They are broadly categorized in inorganic substances such as zinc oxide and silicon, microbes, and other bioactive compounds and natural substances including amino acids, chitosan, seaweed extracts, humic substances [9, 42]. Despite the positive effect on plant performance and productivity [43], large-scale field application of many biostimulants remains constrained by their complex commercial formulations, product-specific modes of action, high application doses, labor-intensive practices, and associated application costs [44–46]. Humic substances and seaweed extracts are among the most widely used biostimulants in agriculture, influencing chelation, nutrient cycling, and disease suppression to improve plant growth and soil health [47, 48]. Soil application of humic acid at 400 g kg− 1 soil and foliar spray of zinc oxide at 100 mg L− 1 improved soil health, and growth and productivity of wheat under nutrient-poor soil [49]. Similarly, several studies reported that application of humic acid ranging from 100 kg to 1000 kg ha− 1 increased plant growth and yield of various horticultural and cereal crops [50–53]. However, MiZax sustained its growth-promoting and stress-tolerance enhancing effects at much lower amounts 6–12 g ha− 1 across controlled and field conditions, highlighting its suitability for large-scale agricultural application [22, 54, 55].

The unique ability of zaxinone mimics, MiZax3 and MiZax5, in the present study, to maintain better crop growth and high productivity under reduced fertilizers sets them apart from other biostimulants across different growth mediums, highlighting their novel role in sustainable crop production [54]. MiZax showed consistent or even better effects at much low concentration compared to commercially available biostimulants, like humic acid, in term of growth promotion and enhancing productivity of different horticultural crops under field conditions [24]. Hence, our hydroponic system revealed that the co-application of 50% reduced fertilizers with MiZax3 and MiZax5 significantly increased root length, crown root development, shoot length, and fresh and dry biomass of wheat, comparable to full fertilization (Fig. 1). Zaxinone and its mimics have been reported to increase crown root length and root biomass of rice [20, 22]. The present results align with previous studies demonstrating that biostimulants can enhance root growth and nutrient use efficiency, particularly under suboptimal nutrient conditions [17, 56].

Similarly, the interaction between MiZax3, MiZax5, and NPK fertilizers under greenhouse and field conditions significantly influenced plant height, productive tillers, grains spike− 1, and grain weight plant− 1 and grain yield (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). The treatments with 50% fertilization combined with MiZax3 and MiZax5 produced taller plants and higher grain yields, comparable to those achieved with 100% fertilization. This might be due to the mode of action of zaxinone mimics, MiZax, which regulate phytohormones such as root sugar metabolism, and modulation of strigolactones and cytokinin content [18], also stimulate plant biological activities such as Comamonadaceae, Acidibacter, and Devosia [57]. All these mechanism and activities support the function of zaxinone and its mimics as nutrient efficiency enhancers rather than direct nutrient substitutes [21, 25]. Our results also demonstrated that the foliar spray of MiZax3 and MiZax5 enhanced chlorophyll and carotenoid contents in the leaves of wheat under the field conditions (Table 3). This is consistent with previous findings that overexpression of the Zaxinone Synthase (ZAS) gene in rice could enhance photosynthetic efficiency, thereby promoting overall plant growth [18, 21]. Moreover, it has reported previously that biotechnological manipulation of carotenoid metabolism can enhance the total carotenoid content and improve the agronomic composition of various crop species [58, 59].

Besides this, the positive correlation between the growth and yield traits (Fig. S3), further endorsed the effect of the combined application of MiZax and reduced NPK fertilizer, establishing better growth that contributes to higher grain yield and agronomic efficiency (Fig. 5C, D). In addition, the reduction in unfilled grains and the increase in 100-grain-weight under MiZax treatments (Table 4), indicate better grain fill and resource allocation that might be due to higher nutrient uptake and physiological efficiency [21, 60]. These results are consistent with the previous evidence suggesting that biostimulants, particularly MiZax, can improve crop resilience and yield stability under controlled and field conditions [23, 24, 28]. The increase in plant dry biomass and spike length with MiZax3 and MiZax5 further supports their role in enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and assimilate partitioning, as reported in earlier studies [21, 28]. These observations support our hypothesis that exogenous application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 contribute to improved plant performance even under nutrient-limited conditions.

Metabolism is a central process required for the uptake and utilization of energy and nutrients to ensure the survival, reproduction, growth, and development of living organisms. Our field study investigated the effect of MiZax3 and MiZax5 on the leaf N, P, and K acquisition (Fig. 6A-C) and primary metabolites (Fig. 7). It has been reported that application of fertilizer may influence the metabolic profile of plants, and elucidate how different nutrient levels modulate metabolic pathways, which are essential for sustainable agriculture and higher crop yields [54, 61]. The accumulation of free sugars and amino acids provides readily available energy and carbon skeletons for biosynthetic processes supporting plant growth. Further, the observed decline in galactinol and β-alanine under MiZax treatment suggests reduced investment in stress-associated reserves, reinforcing the conclusion that MiZax promotes growth-oriented metabolites. When combined with 50% NPK, MiZax enhances the integration of carbon and nitrogen metabolism, improving efficient utilization of nutrients. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MiZax application under reduced NPK input enhances central metabolic activity in wheat, primarily by reinforcing the TCA cycle and sugar as well as amino acid metabolism. Such metabolic reprogramming may contribute to improved growth and stress resilience under limited nutrient conditions.

The findings of this study have significant implications for sustainable agriculture, particularly in the context of reducing fertilizer inputs while maintaining crop productivity. The ability of MiZax3 and MiZax5 to enhance wheat growth and yield under reduced fertilization aligns with global efforts to minimize the environmental impact of intensive farming practices. By improving plant growth and crop resilience, these biostimulants offer a promising strategy for addressing the dual challenges of food security and environmental sustainability.

Conclusion

MiZax are natural-based synthetic mimics that consistently promote plant growth across laboratory, greenhouse, and field conditions. Our results demonstrate that the foliar application of MiZax3 and MiZax5 at 5 µM enhanced wheat growth, photosynthetic performance, and grain yield of wheat under reduced NPK fertilization. These findings highlight MiZax as an effective and practical strategy for sustaining crop productivity while lowering fertilizer inputs. The ability of these MiZax to maintain high grain yield under 50% less fertilizer highlights their potential in minimizing the environmental footprint of agriculture while ensuring food security. Moreover, this study provides compelling evidence that the MiZax application at the agronomic level induces a coordinated reprogramming of primary metabolism characterized by increased free sugars as well as growth-promoting amino acids. The synergistic interaction between MiZax and 50% NPK fertilizer improves nutrient-use efficiency while reducing reliance on full fertilizer inputs. These findings confirm the growth-promoting effects of MiZax and support its potential application as a biostimulant for sustainable crop productivity. Future research will focus on elucidating the molecular and physiological mechanisms of MiZax and exploring their integration with other sustainable agricultural practices, such as conservation agriculture and integrated nutrient management, to optimize their application and maximize long-term agronomic benefits. We will further validate the effect of MiZax under different cropping conditions, such as diverse soil types, climatic zones, agronomic systems and multiple genotypes.
