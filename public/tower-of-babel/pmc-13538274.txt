High Rates of Return to Soccer After Primary Hip Arthroscopic Labral Repair for Femoroacetabular Impingement Syndrome
Authors: Akeem Henry, Louis Kang, John‐Rudolph Smith, Malik Dancy, Gavin Ward, Bruce A Levy, Mario Hevesi, Aaron J Krych, Kelechi R Okoroha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate return‐to‐sport (RTS) outcomes and patient‐reported outcomes in soccer players after primary hip arthroscopic labral repair for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome.MethodsAll patients undergoing primary hip arthroscopy with labral repair for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome from 2011 to 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients who had a minimum 2‐year follow‐up, played soccer, and had no prior hip surgery were included. Patient‐reported outcome measures and RTS were collected at the final follow‐up. Logistic regression was used to assess associations between potential covariates (e.g., age, sex, and level of play) and RTS.ResultsForty‐eight hips in 41 patients (21 men [51%]) with a mean age at surgery of 21.9 years (range 13‐41 years) were included. Thirty‐two out of the 41 patients (78%) played at either the recreational or high school level. After removing those who did not return for nonmedical reasons, 29 out of 36 (81%) patients were able to return to soccer. Among patients with available patient‐reported outcome measures, significantly greater postoperative scores were seen in the RTS group for Hip Outcome Score‐Activities of Daily Living (95 vs 85) and Hip Outcome Score‐Sport (88 vs 71, both P < .05). Soccer players younger than 25 years had significantly higher odds of RTS compared with those aged 25 years or older (odds ratio = 8.8, P = .006). Five hips (10%) underwent revision at the final follow‐up.ConclusionsSoccer players undergoing primary hip arthroscopic labral repair for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome reported an 81% RTS rate at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up, with most returning at the same or higher level of play. At a mean follow‐up of 5.5 years, most patients achieved Patient Acceptable Symptom State thresholds. Younger age was associated with a higher likelihood of return to sport.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, therapeutic retrospective case series.

Femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is a common cause of hip pain among athletes. 1 , 2 FAIS can result from morphologic abnormalities in the proximal femoral head (cam type), acetabulum (pincer type), or a combination of both. 3 Abnormal contact between the femoral head and the acetabulum can result in labral tears and cartilage damage, causing pain and functional impairment. 4 , 5 Additionally, FAIS can limit hip range of motion, altering biomechanics and potentially leading to muscle strains, bursitis, or tendinitis around the hip and low back. 6 Cam‐type deformities develop during adolescence and can affect high‐level athletes. 2 Gerhardt et al. conducted an epidemiological study of hip abnormalities in elite soccer players and found cam lesions in 68% of male players and 50% of female players. Pincer lesions were present in 27% of men and 10% of women. 7 The demanding nature of soccer places unique stresses on the hip, including repetitive high‐velocity kicking, which may exacerbate FAIS‐related pathology and increase the likelihood of requiring surgical management to restore hip function and alleviate pain. 8 , 9 , 10

Hip arthroscopy has become the standard minimally invasive procedure to treat FAIS, showing improvement in patient satisfaction and return to sport. 11 , 12 Prior studies in soccer players have reported favorable outcomes, but these cohorts included mixed surgical techniques rather than exclusively labral repair. 13 , 14 , 15 Marom et al. evaluated 87 competitive soccer players and reported a 75% return‐to‐sport (RTS) rate, with 49% returning to the same or higher level. 14 Another study by Ortiz‐Declet et al. assessed 34 amateur soccer players, finding that 27 (79%) returned to sport, with 19 (70%) competing at the same or higher level compared with preoperative. 15 Notably, none of these cohorts consisted entirely of patients undergoing labral repair, highlighting the need for soccer‐specific outcome data in a fully labral repair cohort.

Although RTS rates in soccer players have been documented after hip arthroscopy, data specifically focusing on soccer cohorts undergoing primary labral repair remain limited. Although many athletes report good results from their surgery, the rate of RTS depends on multiple variables including the athlete's age, level of play, degree of injury, and concomitant injuries, and desire to return. 8 , 16 The purpose of this study was to evaluate RTS outcomes and patient‐reported outcomes in soccer players after primary hip arthroscopic labral repair for FAIS. We hypothesized that most patients would achieve RTS and report satisfactory postoperative hip function, as defined by meeting the Patient Acceptable Symptomatic State (PASS) thresholds.

METHODS

Study Design

This study was a retrospective cohort study from a prospectively collected hip arthroscopy outcomes database. After institutional review board approval (IRB: 8‐002259), a large database containing surgical patients undergoing hip arthroscopy was queried to identify patients who underwent primary hip arthroscopy at a single institution from 2011 to 2022. Eligible patients included those who consented for inclusion, underwent primary hip arthroscopy with labral repair and cam/pincer resections for FAIS, played soccer perioperatively, and had a minimum 2‐year follow‐up period. Patients were excluded if they had prior hip surgery on the symptomatic hip, a history of hip dysplasia, conditions affecting hip morphology (e.g., Legg‐Calvé‐Perthes, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, and post‐traumatic changes), underwent concomitant periacetabular osteotomy, or declined participation. Hip dysplasia was defined radiographically using the lateral center‐edge angle (LCEA), with an LCEA ≤ 20° considered dysplastic and excluded from the study. Patients with borderline acetabular coverage (LCEA 21°‐25°) were included, consistent with commonly used definitions in the hip arthroscopy literature; however, they did not comprise a large portion of the cohort.

Surgical Indications

Patients were indicated for primary hip arthroscopy with labral repair for the treatment of symptomatic FAIS with associated labral pathology. Indications for surgery included persistent hip or groin pain refractory to nonoperative management, positive findings on physical examination consistent with FAIS, and evidence of labral pathology on MRI, in the setting of preserved joint space and absence of advanced degenerative changes.

All patients underwent a trial of nonoperative management prior to surgery, typically consisting of activity modification, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medications, and a structured physical therapy program for a minimum of 3 months. The decision to proceed with surgical intervention was made by the treating surgeon based on persistent symptoms, functional limitation, physical examination findings, and imaging characteristics, with the goal of improving pain, function, and RTS potential.

Surgical Technique

Most cases were performed by 3 experienced staff surgeons (K.R.O., B.A.L., A.J.K.). Standard hip arthroscopy practice was employed, starting with positioning the patient on a modified traction table with a well‐padded perineal post and distraction of the appropriate lower extremity per our institution's normal procedure. Operative procedures can be found in Table 1. Diagnostic arthroscopy was conducted in all patients after establishing anterolateral and mid anterior portals. Patients underwent either interportal or T‐capsulotomy, at the surgeon's discretion. Pincer and cam decompression were carried out as needed using an arthroscopic burr. Primary capsular repair was generally performed after capsulotomy unless otherwise specified per protocol. All patients underwent arthroscopic labral repair with suture anchors.

All patients followed a standardized postoperative hip arthroscopy rehabilitation protocol. Patients were partial weight‐bearing for the first 2 weeks, followed by progressive weight‐bearing and range of motion as tolerated. Rehabilitation emphasized restoration of pain‐free range of motion and strength, with initiation of running and plyometric activities at approximately 12 weeks postoperatively. RTS progression was guided by objective functional criteria, including ≥75% limb symmetry prior to soccer‐specific activities and ≥90% symmetry for clearance to full return to soccer.

Data Collection

Patient's demographics, operative characteristics, and relevant postoperative rehabilitation data were obtained from electronic medical record review. Any complications or reoperations related to their primary hip arthroscopy were also collected. Radiographic parameters were measured based on preoperative hip radiographs to include LCEA, Tonnis angle, and alpha angle. Additionally, coxa profunda and protrusio acetabuli were both recorded when present.

Patients were contacted at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) tools or by phone call to collect outcome data. This included sport‐specific data (e.g., level of play), RTS parameters (e.g., if they RTS and at what level did they return), and patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs). To accurately attribute reasons for not returning to sport, patients who did not RTS were asked whether hip‐related limitations prevented their return. If hip issues were not reported as the reason, patients were further asked to specify alternative reasons (e.g., graduation, changing priorities, or unrelated injuries). PROMs were selected based on their previous validation in cohorts undergoing hip arthroscopy. 17 , 18 , 19 The Hip Outcome Score (HOS) including Activities of Daily Living (HOS‐ADL) and Sport (HOS‐Sport) subscales, modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Tegner activity scale, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain both at rest and with use, VAS 5‐point scale for global rating of change, and surgery satisfaction (on a 10‐point scale) were the collected PROMs. The PASS thresholds of 74 for mHHS, 87 for HOS‐ADL, and 75 for HOS‐Sport were used based on previously published literature. 19 , 20 , 21

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables such as age, body mass index, or LCEA were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables such as sex, level of play, or VAS 5‐point scale were reported as counts and percentage of the entire cohort. Comparative statistics used t‐test for normally distributed data, whereas the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare non‐normally distributed data. Similarly, χ 2 test for independence and Fisher's exact test were used for normal and non‐normal categorical data, respectively. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Comparative PROM analysis was performed between athletes who successfully RTS and those who did not. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with bilateral hips was performed to assess the robustness of PROMs comparisons.

Logistic regression analysis was performed with consideration of the sample size to maximize statistical power. The model evaluated RTS as a binary outcome, assessing each potential predictor separately to estimate its association with RTS, producing odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each variable. Sensitivity analysis including only unilateral hips was also performed to evaluate for differences from the larger cohort. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and a P value of less than .05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Group Demographics

Among 3520 hips in 3003 patients who underwent hip arthroscopy, 57 patients (68 hips) underwent hip arthroscopy with a history of soccer participation. After applying inclusion criteria, 41 patients (48 hips) were included in the study (Figure 1). The cohort consisted of 21 men and 20 women, with a mean age of 21.9 ± 7.2 years (range, 13‐41) and a mean body mass index of 25.9 ± 5.0 kg/m2. Thirty‐two out of the 41 patients (78%) played at either the recreational or high school level, with 7 college players and 2 professional players. Of those who reported position, midfielder (48%) was the most common position affected. The mean LCEA was 30.6° ± 6.1° (95% CI: 29.2‐32.9), the mean Tonnis angle was 6.6° ± 3.8° (95% CI: 5.4‐7.9), and the mean alpha angle was 63.2° ± 8.3° (95% CI: 60.6‐65.8). Regarding Tonnis grade, 29 hips (60%) were grade 0 and 19 (40%) were grade 1 preoperatively. Nine out of 48 hips (19%) showed coxa profunda (Table 1). Forty‐one hips (85%) underwent interportal capsulotomy, and 7 (15%) underwent T‐capsulotomy. Forty‐eight hips underwent femoroplasty (100%), whereas 30 underwent acetabuloplasty (63%). Primary capsular repair was performed in 46 of the 48 hips (96%). The patients who did not undergo capsular repair included a 38‐year‐old male with a tight hip and a 26‐year‐old female who was enrolled in a Capsular Repair Randomized Controlled Trial (CREST) and was randomized to noncapsular repair group.

Disaggregation of patient demographics by sex showed a significantly younger mean age (19.7 ± 5.4 vs 25.7 ± 7.9 years, P = .002) and lower mean body mass index (24.8 ± 4.6 vs 27.7 ± 4.7 kg/m2, P = .024) in female soccer players compared with men. No differences existed among any radiographic parameters.

Return to Soccer

Twenty‐nine out of 41 patients (71%) returned to sport after their primary hip arthroscopy. Of the 12 patients who did not return to soccer, 5 did not return for nonmedical reasons (e.g., graduating and changing priorities). After removing these 5 patients, 29 out of 36 (81%) were able to return after undergoing surgery. Of the remaining 7 patients who did not RTS, 4 (11%) cited hip limitations and 3 (8%) dealt with additional injuries unrelated to the hip that prevented them from returning (Table 2). Among those who successfully returned to sport, 28 of 29 (97%) returned at the same or higher level than preoperatively. The majority competed at the high school (n = 14) or recreational level (n = 7), followed by collegiate (n = 6) and professional players (n = 2). Among those who did not return, most were recreational (n = 6) or high school players (n = 4), with fewer collegiate athletes (n = 2) and no professional players. Eight patients (22%) were still actively playing soccer at a mean follow‐up of 4.0 ± 2.3 years.

Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures

PROMs were successfully collected for 30 out of the 41 (73%) patients. The mean follow‐up was 5.5 ± 2.9 years. Mean HOS‐ADL, HOS‐Sport, and mHHS scores at final follow‐up were 92.1 ± 11.7 (95% CI: 88.1‐96.1), 82.2 ± 20.4 (95% CI: 75.1‐89.3), and 85.1 ± 12.9 (95% CI: 80.7‐89.5), respectively. At the final follow‐up, a substantial proportion of patients achieved the PASS thresholds: 21/28 (75%) for HOS‐ADL, 20/27 (74%) for HOS‐Sport, and 22/28 (79%) for mHHS. Of those with complete PROMs, 75% reported their hip was “slightly better” or “much better” than before surgery. Fifteen percent reported no improvement compared with before surgery. Mean VAS pain at rest and with use were 1.1 ± 1.8 (95% CI: 0.5‐1.7) and 2.2 ± 2.3 (95% CI: 1.4‐3.0), respectively. Overall surgery satisfaction on a scale from not at all satisfied (0) to most satisfied (10) was 7.9 ± 2.8 (95% CI: 6.9‐8.8, Table 4).

Complications and Reoperations

Six hips (13%) went on to reoperation at a mean of 1.8 ± 1.4 years after primary hip arthroscopy. One hip (2%) had a subsequent ipsilateral periacetabular osteotomy procedure. Five hips (10%) underwent revision arthroscopy at a mean of 2.0 ± 1.4 years postoperatively (range, 1.0‐4.4), with all 5 patients undergoing revision labral repair (3 residual cam lesion, 1 residual pincer, and 1 isolated labral tear that was repaired along with lysis of adhesions). There were no perioperative complications.

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this study was that 81% of soccer players successfully returned to sport after primary hip arthroscopic labral repair at a minimum of 2 years, with a mean follow‐up of 5.5 years. When including players who did not return for nonmedical reasons (e.g., graduation and changing priorities), the overall RTS rate was 71%. Among those who successfully returned to sport, 28 of 29 (97%) returned at the same or higher level than preoperatively. Predictors of RTS suggested that younger age and player position may influence the likelihood of returning to soccer.

The RTS rate observed in this cohort of soccer players is comparable to that reported in previous studies. Locks et al. reported a 96% RTS rate among 24 professional soccer players with a mean RTS time of 9.2 months, and Ortiz‐Declet et al. found a return to soccer rate of 79% among 34 amateur male and female soccer players, both after hip arthroscopy for FAIS. 13 , 15 More recently, Marom et al. examined 87 competitive soccer players at a mean 6‐year follow‐up and found that 75% returned to soccer after hip arthroscopy. 14 , 22 The discrepancy in the return rates could be explained by the level of play. A systematic review by Casartelli et al. showed that higher levels of sports participation correlate with the likelihood of RTS, and overall, 87% of athletes including all levels (recreational, collegiate, and professional) return to sport posthip surgery. 22 Our cohort, consisting primarily of recreational and high school soccer players, exhibited an RTS rate of 81% at a mean of 5.5‐year follow‐up. Few studies exist on mid‐ to long‐term outcomes of soccer players after arthroscopic treatment for FAIS, and additional studies are required to fully understand RTS rates, recognizing that long‐term participation is influenced by factors beyond hip function, such as career changes, other injuries, and changing priorities.

Patient‐reported outcomes can provide quantitative data on long‐term outcomes and, to our knowledge, have only been evaluated in 1 prior soccer‐specific study. Ortiz‐Declet et al. found significant improvements in all PROMs documented (mHHS, Nonarthritic Hip Score, HOS‐Sport, and VAS pain) at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up compared with preoperative. They also reported final follow‐up averages of 87.9 for mHHS, 89.6 for Nonarthritic Hip Score, 81.9 for HOS‐Sport, 81.8 for International Hip Outcome Tool, 1.6 for VAS pain, and 8.3 for patient satisfaction. 15 These values correspond to the results of our study: mHHS of 85.1, HOS‐Sport of 82.2, VAS pain of 1.1, and patient satisfaction of 7.9. Ortiz‐Declet et al. included amateur soccer players in their cohort, matching closely with our study that contained 79% recreational or high school players. Notably, 21% of the patients in their study underwent labral debridement or labral reconstruction, whereas our cohort only included those who had labral repair. They reported a similar comparison of PROMs between those who did and did not RTS and found significantly higher HOS‐Sport and International Hip Outcome Tool scores in the RTS group. 15 We similarly found significantly higher scores in HOS‐ADL, HOS‐Sport, and Tegner activity scale in the RTS group at a mean follow‐up of 5.5 years.

Predictors of return to soccer after hip arthroscopy have been examined by Marom et al. and Locks et al., and they showed that in all‐comers undergoing hip arthroscopy, significantly improved outcomes are seen in younger patients and men. 6 , 12 In competitive soccer players, Marom et al. conducted multivariable logistic regression analysis and found that female players and older‐aged players were less likely to return to soccer. They reported that for every 1 year gained in age, the odds of returning to soccer decreased by 10.5%. 14 Locks et al. reported that in professional soccer players, age was negatively correlated with time to RTS. 13 Our logistic regression did not show a significant difference regarding sex, but it did indicate significantly higher odds (OR 8.8) of return to soccer in those under the age of 25 compared with older athletes. Our analysis also revealed lower odds (OR 0.1) of RTS in midfielders when compared with defenders, suggesting that positional demands and other soccer‐specific factors may also play a role in RTS after arthroscopic treatment for FAIS.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. Preoperative PROMs were not collected for most of this cohort, and thus, no quantifiable pre‐ versus postoperative comparisons were able to be made. Furthermore, the retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias, because patients with positive outcomes may have been more likely to respond to outcome surveys. The 11‐year study period may introduce temporal bias, because hip arthroscopy techniques and outcomes have evolved, and the variable timing of follow‐up increases the potential for recall bias, particularly regarding sport‐specific outcomes. The involvement of multiple surgeons, along with differences in follow‐up duration, surgical techniques, nonoperative management, and rehabilitation protocols, may introduce variability and limit standardization; however, inclusion of multiple experienced surgeons also enhances the generalizability of our findings to broader clinical practice.

CONCLUSIONS

Soccer players undergoing primary hip arthroscopic labral repair for FAIS reported an 81% RTS rate at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up, with most returning at the same or higher level of play. At a mean follow‐up of 5.5 years, most patients achieved PASS thresholds. Younger age was associated with a higher likelihood of return to sport.
