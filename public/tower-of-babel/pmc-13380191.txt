Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome Associated With Malignant Hypertension Presenting With Pulmonary-Renal Syndrome-like Symptoms
Authors: Hiroyuki Okawa, Yukihiro Wada, Noritoshi Kato, Mayuko Hanyuda, Ryota Uchitubo, Tetsuya Abe, Kazuhiro Takeuchi, Yasushi Nagaba, Shoichi Maruyama, Yasuo Takeuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Both atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and malignant hypertension (MHT) are causes of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and their coexistence has been reported. We describe a 22-year-old man who exhibited severe hypertension, pulmonary hemorrhage, and acute kidney injury. Laboratory evaluation revealed thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and severe kidney dysfunction, without evidence of retinal hemorrhage or renal artery stenosis. Given suspicion for MHT-related thrombotic microangiopathy or immune-mediated pulmonary-renal syndrome, antihypertensive therapy, continuous hemodiafiltration, corticosteroids, and plasma exchange were initiated. However, autoimmune serologic testing was negative, von Willebrand factor protease (ADAMTS13) activity was preserved without inhibitor, and stool cultures were negative, leading to discontinuation of plasma exchange and tapering of corticosteroids. Thereafter, hematologic abnormalities transiently improved, but kidney dysfunction persisted, and pulmonary hemorrhage and cytopenia recurred with a marked reduction in complement C3 despite stabilized blood pressure reduction. Plasma exchange was resumed, and ravulizumab was initiated. Kidney biopsy demonstrated ischemic glomerular changes without immune deposits and marked narrowing of the interlobar arteries. Although genetic analysis identified variants in complement-related genes, definitive causative abnormalities remained undetermined. The patient was discharged with recovered kidney function, normalization of complement proteins, and sustained hematologic remission. Collectively, this clinical course is compatible with aHUS. We consider that MHT might trigger overt aHUS with pulmonary-renal syndrome-like features.

Introduction

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) are disorders caused by endothelial injury and defined by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and pathologic evidence of microvascular injury or thrombosis.1 TMAs are classified into atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, Shiga toxin-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome, and secondary TMAs associated with specific diseases or conditions.2

aHUS, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.5 per million individuals per year, represents a prototype complement-mediated disease, with inherited or acquired defects in the alternative complement pathway.1 Recently, ravulizumab, a long-acting C5 inhibitor, has emerged as a first-line therapy in Japan, markedly reducing the need for kidney replacement therapy and lowering treatment burden.3

Malignant hypertension (MHT) is characterized by a marked elevation in blood pressure (BP).4 Retinopathy with hemorrhages and left ventricular hypertrophy are commonly observed in MHT.4 Acute kidney injury also frequently complicates MHT, substantially influencing prognosis.5 The prevalence of TMA in MHT has been reported to be ∼30%.6 Most patients with renal insufficiency due to MHT-associated secondary TMA (MHT-TMA) are refractory to therapy and initiate kidney replacement therapy.4

Various conditions, including infections, pregnancy, kidney transplantation, and MHT, can trigger the onset of aHUS.7 Severe hypertension is frequently observed in aHUS and is considered a secondary consequence of renal ischemia with subsequent activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.7,8 Because aHUS and MHT share clinicopathologic features,8 distinguishing aHUS from MHT-TMA is clinically challenging but crucial when severe hypertension and TMA occur concurrently. Herein, we describe a rare and clinically informative case of aHUS presenting with MHT and an acute pulmonary-renal syndrome-like manifestation.

Case Report

A 22-year-old man was admitted with severe hypertension, dyspnea, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and kidney dysfunction. He had mildly elevated BP in high school but did not seek medical care, and urinalysis was normal. Twenty days before admission, he experienced a transient low-grade fever. Five days before, dyspnea and epistaxis appeared and progressively worsened. At another hospital, laboratory tests showed 6.7 g/dL hemoglobin, 90,000/μL platelet count, and 17.6 mg/dL serum creatinine (Scr) concentration, prompting referral to our hospital. His family history was notable for hypertension and cerebral infarction in his mother and maternal grandfather. There was no history of smoking, alcohol abuse, or medication use, including illicit drugs.

On admission, his consciousness was clear. Other vital signs were body temperature 36.6 °C, heart rate 109 beats/min (regular), BP 210/110 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 100% on 3 L/min of oxygen. Physical examination revealed localized ecchymosis over the left clavicle and fine crackles in both lower lungs. Fundoscopic examination showed hard exudates. Abdominal tenderness, leg edema, or neurologic deficits were not detected. Laboratory findings are summarized in Table 1. Urinalysis demonstrated nephrotic range proteinuria without significant hematuria. Complete blood count showed anemia (hemoglobin 7.8 g/dL) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 92,000/μL). Haptoglobin was markedly decreased, and lactate dehydrogenase was elevated. Coagulation studies were unremarkable. Serum biochemistry revealed severe kidney dysfunction (Scr 17.7 mg/dL). Antinuclear antibody, anti-Scl-70 antibody, and viral serologies for hepatitis B and C viruses and human immunodeficiency virus were negative. Complement levels were normal, whereas plasma renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system components were elevated. No clinical features suggested homocystinuria. Chest computed tomography revealed bilateral infiltrative shadows (Fig 1A-C). Echocardiography showed concentric left ventricular hypertrophy (posterior wall thickness 15.7 mm) and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (55%). On admission, intravenous nicardipine and continuous hemodiafiltration were initiated for severe hypertension and acute kidney injury, respectively (Fig 2). On hospital day 2, methylprednisolone pulse therapy was started for pulmonary-renal syndrome-like manifestations, with suspected antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis or anti-glomerular basement membrane disease. Concurrently, TMA lesion was considered based on laboratory findings. In addition to immunosuppressive therapy, plasma exchange was performed from hospital day 3 to 5. On hospital day 5, immunologic tests, including antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody and anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies, were negative. Preserved ADAMTS13 (von Willebrand factor protease) activity without its inhibitor, together with negative stool cultures, further excluded the possibility of immune-thrombocytopenic purpura and Shiga toxin-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome. Therefore, glucocorticoids were tapered, and plasma exchange was discontinued. Accordingly, MHT-TMA was considered most likely, and BP control was intensified. With stabilization of respiratory status and improvement in laboratory findings, the patient was switched from continuous hemodiafiltration to intermittent hemodialysis on hospital day 6. On hospital day 14, the patient developed acute respiratory failure with severe hypertension and thrombocytopenia, prompting reinitiation of continuous hemodiafiltration with mechanical ventilation. Given concern for aHUS, laboratory and genetic evaluations were performed, revealing elevated lactate dehydrogenase, markedly reduced C3, and C4 decreased to the lower normal limit. These findings indicated recurrent TMA despite stabilized BP reduction. Plasma exchange was repeated on hospital days 19 and 21, followed by ravulizumab administration on day 25. Thereafter, hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia gradually resolved, with increased urine output and improved kidney function. Hemodialysis was discontinued on hospital day 28, lactate dehydrogenase and C3 normalized, and BP remained well controlled (Fig 2).

A kidney biopsy performed on hospital day 24 yielded 30 glomeruli. Light microscopy revealed diffuse glomerular collapse, segmental sclerosis, occasional fibrin thrombi in glomerulus, and focal double contours of the glomerular basement membrane (Fig 3A-E). Arterioles and interlobular arteries demonstrated fibrin thrombi and edematous intimal thickening (Fig 3F-H). Immunofluorescence analysis showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy demonstrated wrinkling of the glomerular basement membrane and no organized glomerular deposits. The patient was discharged on hospital day 48. Four months after discharge, a targeted sequencing panel covering major complement-related genes identified missense variants in THBD (NM_000361.3:c.1208G>A, p.Arg403Lys) and CFB (NM_001710.6:c.221G>A, p.Arg74His). Anti-complement factor H antibody levels were not elevated. Soluble C5b-9 was not measured, unfortunately, due to institutional limitations. Thirteen months later, he continues ravulizumab every 8 weeks, with stable kidney function (Scr 4.3 mg/dL), preserved hematologic parameters, and well-controlled BP.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing aHUS from MHT-TMA. Recurrent TMA activity despite stabilized BP reduction, young age at onset, and response to ravulizumab supported a diagnosis of aHUS associated with MHT. A marked reduction in C3 during the clinical course, compared with the mild decrease in C4, indicated alternative pathway activation. Features of essential hypertension rather than aHUS,2 such as male sex, age >45 years, rapid TMA resolution, absence of glomerular thrombi, and no requirement for dialysis, were all inconsistent with this case. Moreover, this case underscores the limits of conventional therapy in complement-driven TMA and supports considering complement inhibition in presumed MHT-TMA with relapse, hypocomplementemia, or extrarenal involvement.

At admission, the presence of left ventricular hypertrophy without hypocomplementemia led us to initially suspect MHT-TMA, despite the absence of typical hypertensive retinopathy. Left ventricular hypertrophy suggested sustained hypertension over a prolonged period. Recent reports indicate that MHT can be diagnosed even without retinopathy.9 However, the subsequent clinical course was not compatible with MHT-TMA alone. Notably, TMA activity recurred despite stabilized BP reduction, arguing against MHT as the sole cause of ongoing endothelial injury. Previous studies have shown that MHT is a relatively common complication in patients with aHUS.4,8 In a retrospective cohort of 55 patients with aHUS, 34.5% were diagnosed with MHT.8 Cavero et al4 further reported that aHUS was the underlying cause of TMA in 24 of 40 patients (60%) who initially presented with MHT. Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated that a substantial proportion of patients with hypertensive emergency and renal TMA have abnormalities in complement regulation.2,10,11 These findings suggest that MHT and aHUS are not mutually exclusive but rather represent a disease spectrum. Uncontrolled hypertension may influence both diseases as a consequence and a trigger of complement-mediated endothelial injury. Consistent with this concept, renal pathology in our case showed chronic TMA lesions likely related to long-standing hypertension, with superimposed acute lesions. This pattern suggests that complement dysregulation in aHUS might contribute to acute activity lesion, with MHT and aHUS interacting to exacerbate vascular injury.

One notable feature of this case was diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) complicating aHUS. DAH is extremely rare in aHUS, with only 2 cases reported to date.12,13 In contrast, several cases of DAH associated with MHT have been described.14,15 In this case, transbronchial lung biopsy showed hemosiderin-laden macrophages but no microthrombi, arguing against a direct pathogenic link between aHUS and DAH. However, since aHUS can precipitate MHT and MHT itself could cause DAH, an indirect contribution of aHUS to DAH development via severe hypertension cannot be excluded.

Several genetic abnormalities have been implicated in aHUS16; meanwhile, identifiable pathogenic variants are absent in approximately 50% of cases.17 In this case, although variants in THBD and CFB were identified, their nonnegligible frequency in the general population precludes definitive genetic proof.18,19 Nonetheless, given the clinical course and exhaustive exclusion of other secondary TMAs, these variants probably functioned as predisposing factors with incomplete penetrance rather than as direct causative mutations. Accordingly, although persistent kidney dysfunction (Scr ∼4.0 mg/dL) warranted long-term ravulizumab therapy to prevent irreversible damage, the absence of high-risk variants, such as in CFH, suggests that discontinuation of ravulizumab could be considered20 through shared decision making once clinical stability is ensured in this patient.

In conclusion, this rare case highlights the importance of identifying aHUS in MHT-induced TMA to enable timely treatment and prevent initiation of kidney replacement therapy.
