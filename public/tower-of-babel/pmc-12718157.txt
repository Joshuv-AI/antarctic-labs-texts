A model, mixed-species urinary catheter biofilm derived from spinal cord injury patients
Authors: Parisa Noorian, Kailey Hamann, M Mozammel Hoque, Gustavo Espinoza-Vergara, Joyce To, Dominic Leo, Priyadarshini Chari, Gerard Weber, Obaydullah Marial, Julie Pryor, Iain G Duggin, Bonsan Bonne Lee, Scott A Rice, Diane McDougald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplex multispecies biofilms consistently colonise urinary catheters, causing persistent asymptomatic bacteriuria and frequent symptomatic episodes in long-term catheterized individuals. Simple single-species models often fail to capture the complexities of mixed-species interactions and lab-based organisms may not reflect the genomic diversity found in real-world infections. Additionally, growth under flow conditions promotes robust, complex-biofilm structures. Therefore, to reflect the dynamics of in vivo infections, biofilm samples from clinical indwelling catheters of spinal cord injury (SCI) participants colonised by 5–10 species were used to establish polymicrobial macro-fluidic models, in catheters. This resulted in final models of 2–4 species biofilms.Metagenomic techniques using short-read Illumina and long-read Oxford Nanopore sequencing was used to assess the taxonomic composition, in vivo to in vitro biofilms diversity shifts, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis and complete metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). In silico analysis revealed a high number of varied antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factors and biofilm associated factors present in these biofilms.Antibiotic resistance testing using our models highlighted the drastic differences between planktonic bacteria, single-species and multispecies biofilms. While single-species biofilms show considerably increased tolerance to antibiotics compared to their planktonic counterparts, this resistance is even greater in multispecies biofilms. Under flow conditions, all species in the multispecies biofilm showed increased resistance, unlike static conditions where only most did.Models developed and characterised in this study are expected to facilitate testing of effective strategies to prevent and treat catheter-associated infections by enabling more accurate analysis of biofilm inhibition, disruption and microbial interactions.Keywords: Multi-species biofilm, In vitro model, Urinary catheter, Spinal cord injury, Bacteriuria, Urinary tract infections

Introduction

Urinary catheters are commonly used when the bladder cannot empty naturally. The insertion of a catheter provides an abiotic surface free of the host's innate defence system, creating a suitable niche for bacterial colonisation. This often leads to catheter associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI), which are hard to diagnose and even harder to treat [1]. CAUTI have potentially life-threatening consequences in part due to selection for antibiotic resistant strains [2,3] and formation of single- or multi-species biofilms. Prolonged catheterization increases likelihood of biofilm formation, symptomatic infection and greater bladder flora diversity [4]. The thickness of the biofilm from clinical urinary catheters varies from patchy monolayers of cells to extensive biofilms up to 0.5 mm. Such biofilm sometimes cover the entire catheter length reaching bacterial load of 5 × 109 colony-forming units (CFU) cm-2 [5].

Multi-species bacterial communities exhibit social behaviours including altered virulence, production of secondary metabolites, horizontal gene transfer, colonisation potential and persistence, biofilm development as well as increased stress resistance such as antimicrobial compound resistance [6,7]. Biofilm-associated pathogens have a much higher survival rate in comparison with their planktonic counterparts against stresses such as nutrition deprivation, extremes in salinity, pH, temperature, UV, chlorine, antimicrobial agents and phagocytosis by immune cells by one or more mechanisms [8]. Multispecies biofilms exhibit even greater resistance to these stresses compared to single-species biofilms [[8], [9], [10]]. When interventions alter the microbial community population, they can shift competitive dynamics, resource availability, and niche occupation. For instance, in a static, synthetic multi-species biofilm, low nutrient conditions and sub-lethal concentrations of an alcohol-based disinfectant increased overall biofilm biomass while reduction of the dominant taxa lead to an increase in antimicrobial-resistant minor taxa [11].

Interactions within multispecies biofilms are highly dynamic, with outcomes varying by species composition. Previous dual species studies have shown inhibitory and supportive effects of these relationships. In dual species biofilms, Escherichia coli cell numbers decreased when co-cultured with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, while P. aeruginosa seemed to benefit from the interaction [12]. In another study pre-colonisation of catheters by cocultures of E. coli with non-pathogenic species, such as Delftia tsuruhatensis or Achromobacter xylosoxidans promoted adhesion of E. coli [13]. Klebsiella pneumoniae formed biofilms in co-incubation with E. coli or Enterococcus faecalis, but co-inoculation with P. mirabilis impaired its biofilm formation. E. coli did not persist in biofilms during co-incubation with K. pneumoniae but was not affected by E. faecalis. E. faecalis surface attachment was partially inhibited by E. coli but not by K. pneumoniae [14]. These differences highlight the nuanced and species-specific nature of microbial interactions within catheter-associated biofilms.

Various static, microwell and microfluidic-based biofilm formation systems are widely used. Each system has benefits and disadvantages but regardless of the system, in vitro biofilm models often show high variability between results of different systems, preventing reliable comparison of treated biofilms to their controls [15,16]. To design an in vitro model representative of real-life patients, there are many important factors to be considered, including the substratum material, growth conditions, microbial community, as well as the physiological and immunological responses of the host [17]. This discrepancy in models arises due to differences in surface properties, the absence of biological interactions, surface uniformity, variations in hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity, lack of shear forces, insufficient antimicrobial defences, and altered biofilm architecture. Due to these challenges, it is currently impractical to control all aspects of the bladder environment in a single model.

Our approach focuses on achieving consistent outcomes under the flow of artificial urine inside a urinary catheter using clinically relevant bacterial isolates. For this purpose, bacterial communities isolated from spinal cord injury (SCI) participants were used to establish a repeatable multispecies biofilm on the intraluminal surface of catheters. Here, we used synthetic urine medium, which imitates nutrient conditions within the bladder better than nutrient rich media and can be consistently produced in large volumes required for macro-fluidic systems. High throughput sequencing and metagenomic analysis were used to study the development of the community composition at each step as well as to identify the specific genes, e.g. antimicrobial genes and virulence genes, associated with those community members.

Here, we also used our models to evaluate the efficacy of existing treatments against complex biofilm-associated infections, providing valuable data to improve clinical practices. Our multispecies biofilms are relevant to clinical bacterial samples and can serve as a robust platform for experiments with novel antimicrobial compounds, biofilm-disrupting agents, and alternative therapeutic strategies in a controlled and reproducible environment. This would further allow us to investigate the basic principles of microbial interactions, biofilm formation, and gene expression within a clinically relevant context.

Methods

Participant's natural biofilm community

The samples for this project were obtained as part of a multi-centre observational longitudinal study, 3PU (ACTRN12622000613707). Biofilm collection was performed as part of the patient's routine care or catheter change due to suspected UTI by their care team and only samples that would otherwise be discarded were collected over 18 months. The used catheters were explanted by trained health personnel. A 5 cm section of the bladder end of the catheter was cut using sterile scissors and collected in a sterile container containing 5 ml of sterile saline. After the new catheter were installed, fresh catch urine was collected from newly installed catheter in a sterile container. Both containers were transported to the research team for analysis. Biofilms were collected by cutting the catheter length wise and then physically detaching the biofilm using a cell scraper. The catheter pieces were then submerged in saline and cells were extracted by vortexing briefly, sonicating in the ultrasonic cleaner, Power Sonic 420 (Thermoline) on the medium setting for 1 min, followed by vortexing. Here, 1 ml of catheter cell suspension was centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min. The supernatants were discarded and the cell pellets from the catheter were stored at −20 °C for DNA extraction. The remaining catheter cell suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min and cell pellets were frozen at −80 °C in glycerol.

Establishing in vitro urinary catheter associated multi-species biofilms

Mixed species bacterial culture inputs were prepared using six biofilm samples selected at random from a pool of routine catheter changes stored in 25 % glycerol. Samples were inoculated in complete composite Synthetic Human Urine (ccSHU) [18] consisting of 100 mM NaCl, 17.0 mM Na2SO4, 280 mM Urea, 38.0 mM KCl, 4.0 mM CaCl2, 9.0 mM Creatinine, 3.4 mM Na3C6H5O7, 20.0 mM NH4Cl, 3.2 mM MgSO4, 0.18 mM Na2C2O4, 3.6 mM NaH2PO4, 6.5 mM Na2HPO4, 16.0 mM KH2PO4, 0.6 mM C5H4N4O3, 13.5 mM NaHCO3, 13.2 mM MgCl2, 1.1 mM C3H6O3, 0.005 mM FeSO4 supplemented with 0.1 % (v/v) casamino acids, 0.2 % (v/v) yeast extract [18], 0.5 % Glucose (27.75 mM) and 0.5 % Foetal calf serum (FBS). The amount of Glucose is elevated compared to healthy urine and is more comparable to those with diabetes or transient glycosuria and was done to support microbial growth dynamics in the model [19]. Yeast extract and FBS are not components of native urine; however, these were added consistently across all experiments to enhance microbial viability and ensure reproducible biofilm development, particularly for fastidious species. The pH was adjusted to 6.2 and sterilized by filtration through a 0.2 μm filter before use. Samples were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C without shaking.

Releen Male Inline Catheters 18G, 10 cc, Silicone 40 cm (Coloplast) were aseptically cut into 10 cm lengths pieces. All catheter pieces were coated in 500 μg mL-1 Fibrinogen from human plasma (Sigma) overnight at 4 °C. Masterflex® peristaltic pumps (Cole-Parmer) with Masterflex® L/S® Multichannel Cartridge Pump Head with Reduced Pulsation for Microbore 2-Stop Tubing, 12 Channel, 8 Roller was used for flow delivery into catheters. All media flasks, connectors, pump and waste lines were autoclaved prior to use except for Masterflex® L/S® 2-Stop Precision Pump Tubing, Platinum-Cured Silicone which were sterilized using 0.25 % Bleach solution for 1 h before being washed for 1 h using sterile distilled water. Pump lines were placed in inflow bottles containing ccSHU and then connected to Masterflex tubing, using straight connectors. The Masterflex tubing ran through the cartridge pump that had a steady flow rate of 50 μL min-1. This was connected using straight connectors to another segment of pump line that attached on to the 10 cm length piece of catheter and were connected to waste lines ending in waste flasks (Fig. 1- A). Initially, ccSHU was allowed to run for 2 h to prime the system. Each sample of mixed species bacterial culture input was diluted to OD600 = 0.001 in 1 mL of ccSHU. The flow was paused and each end of the catheter was blocked to minimise the chance of backflow. Each culture input was loaded into a 1 mL syringe attached to a 26G needle and directly injected into the catheter piece and sealed using silicone. To allow cell attachment, the flow was restarted 2 h after injection. The system was then allowed to run with flow of 50 μl min-1 for 4 d at 37 °C, after which the inflow medium was replaced with saline and allowed to run for 2 h to remove planktonic bacteria. The catheter section was then removed and the extra liquid was drained. Each section was sliced horizontally in half using a sterile scalpel blade. The internal biofilm was recovered using a cell scraper and the pieces were submerged in saline and cells were extracted by vortexing briefly, sonicating in the ultrasonic cleaner, Power Sonic 420 (Thermoline) on medium for 1 min and then vortexing again. A portion of the extracted biofilm was kept at −20 °C for total DNA extraction. A portion of the extracted biofilm was plated on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates to monitor the diversity of the biofilms based on chromogenic appearance of the colonies. Isolates from these plates were stored in 25 % glycerol at −80 °C and used as bacterial culture input for the next passages of biofilm flow (G1-G3). This process was repeated three times.

Whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from biofilm samples using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and quantity of the isolated DNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The DNA samples were resuspended in TE buffer and stored at −20 °C before further analysis. Genomic library preparation for all short-read sequencing was using the Hackflex library preparation method [20] and sequenced on NovaSeq PE150. The reads ranged from 81.5 to 4 0.2 M reads. Samples explanted from participants were heavily contaminated with human genomic DNA. An Miseq sequencer was used to estimate the amount of human gDNA contamination and therefore, heavily contaminated samples were adjusted and sequenced deeper while samples that were from pure bacterial culture were shallow sequenced.

The G4 Input of models C-1 and C-5 mixed species bacterial culture was produced by inoculation of the of second passage (G3) biofilms output and incubation for 48 h at 37 °C static. Genomic DNA was extracted using MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA was then quantified using Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and NanoDrop One. Agilent 4200 TapeStation system and Agilent Femto Pulse systems were used for analysis of high-molecular-weight fragment size. DNALigation Sequencing Kit V14 (SQK-LSK114) and its recommended protocol for making sequencing libraries was used. Each library was then run on one PromethION Flow Cell separately. Nanopore sequencing signals were processed using MinKNOW 22.12.5 and base-called using Guppy 6.4.6+ae70e8f on the PromethION tower using the GPUs. This resulted in a total of 99,064,035,191 bp from which 78,941,083,703 bp (79.69 %) passed with quality ≥ Q9 and N50 of 6,432 bp for model C-1 and total bases 61,720,670,275 bp from which 48,685,922,888 bp (78.88 %) passed with quality ≥ Q9 and N50 6,230 bp for model C-5.

The quality of the pair-end short reads was checked with FastQC [21], trimming and adaptor cut was performed by fastp set at default [22,23]. Nanopore long reads were trimmed based on quality (in priority ≥90 %) and length (12 kbp) and adaptor cut was performed by Fitlong [24].

Metagenomic sequencing, and recovery and assessment of microbial populations

Short reads were used to determine the taxonomic classification using Kraken2-2.1.3 [25]. The ready to use standard plus Refeq protozoa & fungi database (77 GB) available from the Langmead lab website (https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2; 09-10-2023 version) was used. Minimum hit groups and confidence were set to 4 and 0.3, respectively. Followed by Braken V2.9 for a taxonomic abundance estimation and Pavian for visualization [26,27]. All samples were heavily contaminated with human DNA. Here, the percentages are reported after filtering out human reads using Pavian Homo Sapiens option as well as the removal of all phage reads.

Genomes of the single isolates were assembled using SPAdes v.3.15.5 genome assembler [28]. Aviary's [29] Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) recovery workflow was used to co-assemble the short and long reads alongside MAG recovery, binning and annotation. Various different tools were used, including NanoPack and NanoPack2 [30,31], fastp [22,23], Flye [32], Racon [33], Pilon [34], metaSPAdes [35], Unicycler [36], Minimap2 [37], samstools [38], CONCOCT [39], VAMB [40], MetaBAT 1 and MetaBAT 2 [41,42], DAS Tool [43], MaxBin 2.0 [44], SemiBin 2 [45], Rosella [46], CheckM and CheckM2 [47,48], eggNOG mapper 2 [49] and GTDB-Tk 2 [50]. Briefly, hybrid assembly of short and long reads was performed using workflow “assemble.” The assemblies were manually checked using MetaQUAST [51] and Bandage [52]. Genomes of each community were then recovered using workflow “recover.” Quality of MAGs was checked using CheckM2 version 1.0.2. Taxonomy information of MAGs was determined using GTDB-Tk 2.3.3 and taxonomy of the single isolates was determined using GTDB-Tk 2.4.0. ABRicate V1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/ABRicate) all databases update on November 04, 2023 was used to screen for AMR genes with the NCBI AMRFinderPlus [53], The Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [54] and virulence factors in The virulence factor database (VFDB) [55] and E. coli virulence factors (E.coli-VF) [56].

Metaphlan 4.1.1 [57] and Strainphlan 4.1 [58] were used to track individual strains across samples and determine whether there was strain specific divergence or the presence of a multi-strain species complex. The MetaPhlAn mpa_vJun23_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202307 was used to map reads to marker sequences.

The breseq v0.38.3 pipeline [59] was used to compare the single isolates of same species from each model that showed different phenotype. Briefly, prokka 1.14.6 [60] was used to annotate each genome. The annotated isolates were used as reference by breseq pipeline to identify for mutations and gdtools program were used for comparison between samples. Breseq's polymorphism mode was used to detect genetic variants within the passages [61].

Antibacterial testing

Models C-1 and C-5, were used for the antibacterial testing. Single bacterial isolates were selected by using colony morphology and colour on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates. Single isolates were grown overnight in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) (BD BACTO™) broth at 37 °C, 200 rpm. The day of the test, cultures were diluted and regrown to mid-log phase to absorbance 0.5 at 600nm. Antibiotic discs were placed on Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA) plates using antimicrobial susceptibility disc dispenser (Oxoid™) followed by overnight incubation for 18 h at 37 °C. The antibiotic disks (Oxoid™) used in this study included: Amikacin 30 μg, Ampicillin Sulbactam 20 μg, Ampicillin 25 μg, Cefotaxime 30 μg, Ceftazidime 30 μg, Ceftriaxone 30 μg, Chloramphenicol 30 μg, Ciprofloxacin 5 μg, Gentamicin 10 μg, Imipenem 10 μg, Kanamycin 30 μg, Meropenem 10 μg, Nalidixic Acid 30 μg, Neomycin 30 μg, Netilmicin 30 μg, Rifampicin 30 μg, Spectinomycin 25 μg, Streptomycin 25 μg, Sulfamethoxazole 100 μg, Tetracycline 30 μg, Tobramycin 10 μg, Trimethoprim 5 μg [62].

The antimicrobial susceptibility of planktonic cells was performed using a resazurin-based microdilution assay [63]. Briefly, overnight cultures of each strain or the established mixed model G4 input were grown overnight in ccSHU and adjusted to an OD600 = 1.0. Each strain was then diluted 1000-fold in ccSHU. Each sample was then inoculated in a 96 well microtiter plate with different concentrations of the selected antibiotics. Each plate was incubated at 37 °C, static for 20 h. After the incubation period, 5 μl of 1 mg mL-1 Resazurin (Sigma) was added to each well incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Results were quantified using fluorescence spectrophotometer excitation at 575 nm and an emission at 585 nm using Spark® microplate reader (Tecan). A positive control of isolates in ccSHU with no antibiotics and a negative control of antibiotics without bacteria was used in this assay.

Releen Male Inline Catheters 18G, 10 cc, Silicone 40 cm (Coloplast) were aseptically cut into 15 mm lengths pieces. The inflation line was removed using a sterile scalpel blade and each piece was fixed to the lid of a 48 well plate (Fig. 1-B). Overnight cultures of each isolate were grown in BHI broth at 37 °C, 200 rpm. Mixed biofilm samples were grown in ccSHU for 48 h at 37 °C without shaking. Each culture was then washed in SHU and adjusted to an OD600 = 0.1. The cultures were diluted 100-fold in ccSHU and inoculated in 48 well plates with modified lids. Each plate was incubated at 37°c, static for 48 h and the medium was refreshed every 12 h. Fresh ccSHU with or without gentamicin for 500 μg ml-1 was subsequently added for a further 24 h and the medium was again refreshed after a further 12 h. The catheter pieces were then washed in saline, 9 g L-1 NaCl2 before detachment of each catheter from plate. The biofilm was scraped off and the pieces were submerged in saline and cells were extracted by vortexing and ultrasonic cleaner. The bacterial biomass was then enumerated using CFU on BHI agar for single isolates and CHROMagar™ Orientation for multispecies samples.

To test multi-species biofilm antimicrobial susceptibility under flow conditions, 4 d biofilms were established on 10 cm length catheter pieces. Each condition was tested in 3 separate lines for each repeat and the experiments were repeated as 3 independent experiments. Two timepoints and concentrations were used to test antibiotic resistance. Doses of 80 mg of gentamicin per 20 ml of saline instillation for 15 min are frequently used as a bladder irrigation technique [64]. To test the effectiveness of the antibiotic without the help of the physical force of the wash, saline or gentamicin 4 mg ml-1 were injected gently using a syringe to the lines containing 4 d old biofilms. Upmost care was used to inject very gently to eliminate the disruption of biofilm via physical force. After 15 min, the lines were washed for 2 h with saline only and all of the liquid was gently drained from them. The catheter section was then removed and sliced horizontally in half using sterile scalpel blade. The internal biofilm was scrapped and the pieces were submerged in saline and cells were extracted by vortexing and ultrasonic cleaner. The bacterial biomass was then enumerated using CFU on CHROMagar™ Orientation. Furthermore, the Input bottles for the rest of the lines were switched to fresh ccSHU with or without gentamicin for 500 μg ml-1 for another 24 h under the same flow conditions. After this all lines were washed for 2 h with saline only, and all the liquid was drained gently from them. The remaining attached cells were then removed as described above and quantified.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, La Jolla California, USA (www.graphpad.com). Data that did not follow Gaussian distribution as determined by analysing the frequency distribution graphs was natural log transformed. Two-tailed Student's t-tests were used to compare means between experimental samples and controls. For experiments including multiple samples, two-way ANOVAs were used for the analysis, and Sidak's Multiple ComparisonTest provided the post-hoc comparisons of means when appropriate. Data organization, visualization and formatting were conducted using the R package “tidyverse”, “ggplot2” and “ggtree” [65,66].

Results

Establishment of an in vitro multi-species biofilm from clinical biofilms

Our main goal was to establish a stable, in vitro multi-species biofilm community on an intraluminal catheter derived from clinical catheter-associated flora and closely define the community's features. For this purpose, bacterial communities from the indwelling catheters of six SCI patients with neurogenic bladders were selected. The participants were at least five years post-initial injury and at least 2 months post last symptomatic CAUTI. All samples were explanted as part of the participant's routine catheter change and were considered asymptomatic bacteriuria (Table 1).

C-1 was mainly colonised by E. coli (31.95 %), Veillonella parvula (20.42 %), Lactobacillus paragasseri (13 %), Lactobacillus gasseri (9 %), Bifidobacterium dentium (6 %), K. pneumoniae (3.41 %), Streptococcus agalactiae (2.69 %), Streptococcus anginosus (1.36 %), P. aeruginosa (1.21 %) and Haemophilus parainfluenzae (1 %). The normally aerotolerant anaerobes or microaerophilic Lactobacillus spp. and B. dentium, which originally compromised 28 % of the total reads, did not thrive in the input cultures under the conditions used (Fig. 2). Interestingly, P. aeruginosa and H. parainfluenzae were also completely lost from first in vitro Input growth, while V. parvula and B. dentium could not be detected after the first biofilm formation (G1_1_Out) and second passages of biofilm formation (G1_2_Out), respectively. The final community abundance of the C-1 biofilm model shifted to E. coli (71.77 %) and K. pneumoniae (24.81 %) at higher abundance (>1 % of reads) and S. agalactiae (0.16 %), K. quasipneumoniae (0.11 %) and K. variicola (0.04 %) at lower abundance (<1 % of reads). Even though S. agalactiae was at low abundance, 0.16 % of the community, it was readily recovered by cultivation on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates alongside E. coli and Klebsiella spp.

C-2 was mainly colonised by 9 bacterial species, V. parvula (38.37 %), E. coli (24.89 %), S. agalactiae (13.15 %), B. dentium (10 %), Actinotignum schaalii (5 %), P. aeruginosa (2 %), H. parainfluenzae (2 %) and S. anginosus (1 %). After 3 passages of biofilm formation, the community abundance shifted so that only E. coli and E. faecalis remained at 90.32 % and 9.50 % of the total reads, respectively. Both species were recovered on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates.

The community abundance of C-4 changed to P. aeruginosa and E. faecalis after the first in vitro growth. Whereas P. aeruginosa and E. faecalis compromised 88.34 % and 11.01 % of the total reads, respectively and were recovered on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates.

C-5 was colonised mainly by Actinotignum schaalii (32.4 %), V. parvula (29.16 %), K. pneumoniae (19.94 %), E. faecalis (10.59 %), P. aeruginosa (3.55 %), S. agalactiae (1.95 %) and Campylobacter curvus (1.18 %). On the original catheter E. coli was only 0.46 % of the reads and reached 13.53 % of the reads by G3_5_Out. P. aeruginosa also increased from 3.55 % in the patient's catheter sample to 76.48 % of the reads in G3_5_Out. K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis, P. aeruginosa and E. coli were recovered on CHROMagar™ Orientation plates.

C-6 was colonised by mainly S. aureus (54.28 %), P. aeruginosa (21.87 %), Klebsiella michiganensis (10.22 %), Serratia marcescens (8.43 %) and K. pneumoniae (3.33 %). The biofilm extracted from the second passage of biofilm formation (G2-6-out) showed that the community shifted to P. aeruginosa (40.78 %), E. coli (20.48 %), S. marcescens (16.89 %), P. mirabilis (13.1 %) and K. pneumoniae (8.28 %). P. mirabilis was initially detected as 0.00053 % on the patient's catheter but increased to 13.1 % by G2-6-out. Similarly, E. coli was found at 0.02 % and rose to 20.48 % by G2-6-out. The third passage of planktonic input (G3-6-In) did not grow in ccSHU and no detectable DNA was recovered.

Models C-1, C-3 and C-6 were mixed at a ratio of 1:1:1 (G1_Mix_Out and G2_Mix_In) containing K. pneumoniae (43.62 %), P. aeruginosa (29.14 %), P. mirabilis (19.53 %), E. coli (5.95 %) and S. agalactiae (1.45 %). No biofilm was formed from inoculation of the G2_Mix_In and no DNA was recovered.

In summary, biofilms of models C-1, C-2 and C-4 developed in vitro were primarily compromised of two major species, while biofilm of model C-5 had four major species. Whereas, biofilms of models C-3, C-6 and mixture of C-1, C-3, C-6 could not be established in the laboratory.

Models C-1 (two highly abundant species E. coli and K. pneumoniae as well as one low-abundant species S. agalactiae) and C-5 (4 highly abundant species K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis, P. aeruginosa and E. coli) were selected for further analysis and were sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) ultradeep long read metagenome sequencing on a nanopore PromethION as well as short read Illumina shotgun sequencing. Hybrid short and long read assembly was used for generating metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Both sets of MAGs were of high quality: >90 % complete, <5 % contaminated (Supplementary Data 2). In model C-1, E. coli and K. pneumoniae were completed at 100 % and S. agalactiae was completed at 92.49 %. For model C-5, K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis and E. coli were completed at 100 % and P. aeruginosa was completed at 96.35 %, as calculated by CkeckM2. GTDB-Tk results confirmed the species assignment.

Detecting mutations in the community and determining presence of different strains of the same species in multispecies communities using metagenomic analyses presents significant challenges due to the complexity of microbial communities and the high degree of genetic similarity within strains. To detect potential changes within each passage when in vitro biofilms were being established, we focused on the four prominent species found in the models: E. coli, K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis, and P. aeruginosa. The changes in the dominant strains between models was determined by utilising the StrainPhlAn pipeline with its default settings, which selects the allele with more than 80 % dominance [67]. In all of the models, the dominant strains remained consistent through in vitro passages and group together on the phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Data 3).

Toxin production and secretion systems are also well represented. Genes such as hlyA-D (hemolysin), cnf1 (cytotoxic necrotizing factor), sat (autotransporter toxin), and vat (hemoglobin protease) suggest multiple mechanisms for host tissue damage and immune modulation. Capsule synthesis genes (cpsA-C, kpsD, M, T) and motility-related genes (flg, fli, motA, B) further enhance the virulence profile, supporting immune evasion and dissemination within the host.

We decided to focus on the aminoglycoside gentamicin for the remainder of this study. Greater antibiotic resistance in biofilms

Gentamicin, specifically as an intravesical instillation, is a treatment for CAUTI in patients with neurogenic bladders and is recommended based on studies in which participants who underwent the antimicrobial instillation reported a good response with a reduction of symptomatic CAUTI [73,74]. In many other cases, gentamicin is delivered by IV/IM, especially if sepsis is a concern. Gentamicin can also be given as intravesical instillations at 4 mg ml-1 for 15 min or intramuscular and intravenous injections against CAUTIS [75]. Following 80–120 mg gentamicin IV or IM, the bulk of the gentamicin is excreted in the urine within the first 24 h after administration and varies from 3 to 600 μg ml -1 [76,77]. Therefore, the effect of gentamicin on biofilms was tested at either 4 mg ml-1 for 15 min or at 500 μg ml -1for 24 h. To compare the effectiveness of gentamicin against isolates in biofilms, we established biofilms using two different systems, biofilms grown on catheter pieces fixed to the lid of multi-well plates or under flow intraluminal catheters pieces. To quantify the culturable cells in each biofilm, CHROMagar™ Orientation plates were utilised.

Discussion

Biofilms on urinary catheters often present as complex, multispecies communities that can complicate treatment and management of catheter-associated infections (CAUTIs) [78]. Pathogens associated with CAUTIs often exhibit higher antibiotic resistance compared to those in non-catheter UTIs [79]. This is on top of the inherent resistance characteristics of biofilms.

One of the primary challenges in creating a multispecies bacterial model is accurately replicating the microbial communities that inhabit the human body and understanding the relationships among these organisms. To address this challenge, we took a novel approach that differed from previous studies that typically select isolates and artificially mix them in the lab. Instead, we started with pre-existing biofilm communities extracted from SCI patients who were asymptomatic and initially colonised by 5–10 species (Table 1). This allowed us to stabilise and cultivate this community under controlled laboratory conditions which resulted in final models of 2–4 species (Fig. 1). This method intended to maintain a more natural relationships among the bacteria, leading to a more representative model for testing.

Specific growth conditions can introduce selection bias in in vitro cultures both planktonically and on catheters leading to loss of some genus of the bacteria. Anaerobic/microaerophilic bacteria (e.g., V. parvula, Lactobacillus spp., B. dentium and H. parainfluenzae) were consistently lost or reduced in early biofilm formation, likely due to oxygen exposure and nutrient limitations in artificial media. Fastidious organisms frequently require specialised media, cofactors, growth factors, low nutrient conditions, or specific redox potentials that differ from media that favour fast growers or aerobes. A single medium rarely meets all requirements simultaneously. Aerobes consume oxygen rapidly and can create steep, small-scale oxygen gradients in the mixed biofilm. Obligate anaerobes need anoxic microenvironments and are killed or stressed by even low levels of O2. Creating stable, reproducible oxygen profiles (both spatial and temporal) in a single “one-pot” culture is technically difficult [80,81].

Importantly, selection bias extends beyond species-specific biofilm adaptability and fitness, particularly due to the transition from catheter-derived biofilms to planktonically grown input cultures, which introduces a significant shift in community structure from the outset. Taking all of this into account, E. coli emerged as dominant in several models C-1, C-2 and C-5, K. pneumoniae become one of the major species in models C-1 and C-5 and E. faecalis in models C-2, C-4 and C-5. P. aeruginosa also became dominant in models C-4 and C-5, consistent with its known biofilm-forming ability and competitiveness [82,83]. S. aureus was initially dominant in C-4 and C-6 but declined sharply or disappeared entirely. In vitro culture conditions here significantly alter catheter biofilm composition, favouring fast-growing aerobes taxa. While this model does not fully replicate the complexity of patient biofilms, it remains a valuable tool for investigating interspecies interactions under controlled conditions.

In the C-2 catheter, E. faecalis was initially below the sequencing detection limit, but with successive passages its abundance rose to 9.5 % of total reads. One explanation could be due to increase of E. coli from 24.89 % to 90 % by the final passage which assisted E. faecalis growth. Previous co-culture experiments using paired isolates from the same patients showed E. coli markedly enhanced E. faecalis growth in artificial urine medium, whereas E. faecalis grows poorly in monoculture [84].

Under our culture conditions, the C-3 community did not grow, and the C-6 community failed after two consecutive passages. P. mirabilis was detected in both, with C-3 starting at a relatively high abundance (13.1 %), while in C-6 it was initially rare (0.00053 %) but increased markedly over two passages to 13.1 % before the community ceased to grow. P. mirabilis has previously been shown to exert negative effects on other pathogens in competition experiments [85]. Additional studies are needed to elucidate these interactions and confirm the role of specific pathogens in changing the previously established communities.

Models C-1, C-3, and C-6 were mixed at equal ratios with the aim of studying community stability, taxonomic composition, and dynamics across different human hosts, and to compare these with the outcomes of their respective single-host models. The outcome in a complex community is not simply the sum of its parts. However, this mixed community was not stable and in similar fashion to its individual parts, C-3 and C-6, P. mirabilis increased from 2.2 % to 19.5 % before the community ceased to grow.

Each model contained genetically distinct strains of the four dominant species, highlighting strain-level diversity across models. But in each model, there were no changes in the dominant strains in each in vitro passage (Supplementary Data 3). Although it is possible for point mutations to happen in vitro, especially for bacterial species with higher mutation rates. The P. aeruginosa yellow-green pigmented (strain 5-6) was isolated in the model but we could not detect its presence in the original catheter community by culture methods screening for the yellow-green pigment phenotype. Breseq's polymorphism flag revealed that point mutations in genes appeared early, during the first passage and persisted throughout subsequent passages. In contrast, several other mutations emerged in intermediate planktonic or biofilm passages but later disappeared from the community. Most SNPs listed in Table 3 arose much later, predominantly during the final passage (Data not shown). The underlying drivers of these mutations and their persistence within the community, warrant further investigation, particularly in relation to interspecies interactions and spatial organisation. These aspects should be the focus of future studies.

P. aeruginosa strain 5-6 showed slight increase in biomass under gentamicin pressure whereas the non-pigmented strain 5-10 showed slight decrease as result of gentamicin treatment (Fig. 4-B). Several regulatory genes and metabolic pathways in this strain carried non-synonymous SNPs. For example, sensor protein RstB, part of the two-component RstA/RstB signal transduction systems, positively regulates the efflux pump MexEF-OprN, enzymes involved in anaerobic nitrate respiration, and pyoverdine biosynthesis [86]. These mutations contribute another layer of complexity to the multispecies biofilm.

The antibiotic assessments were designed with several objectives. First, we aimed to demonstrate the outcomes that clinicians might observe when using the standard clinical pathology disk diffusion method for each isolate. This is important, as many isolates may be under-reported as sensitive to antibiotics in context of biofilm infections [[87], [88], [89]]. Furthermore, pathology data for multi-species urine samples is often limited, as traditional urine cultures struggle to accurately identify and quantify multiple pathogens simultaneously [90,91]. This means that if either E. coli or K. pneumoniae from C-1 or K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa from C-2 were to be identified they will be reported in clinical pathology as sensitive to gentamicin (Table 5–A).

Second, we collected planktonic antibiotic susceptibility profiles in ccSHU, which can be used for direct comparison with those obtained from biofilms grown in ccSHU under both static conditions (static multi well plates) and dynamic flow conditions. S. agalactiae strain 1-1 from model C-1 showed a MIC of 64 μg ml-1, whereas in model C-5 E. coli strain 5-1 showed a MIC of 512 μg ml-1and E. faecalis strain 5-3 a MIC 128 μg ml-1. All other isolates were sensitive to Gentamicin treatment in planktonic conditions. Interestingly, the MIC of both models are much lower in planktonic conditions C-1 is 16 μg ml-1 and C-5 is 128 μg ml-1. It seems that the assumption that the MIC of the planktonic multispecies communities, which consist of varying percentages of different species, should match the highest individual MIC oversimplifies the dynamics of a multispecies community and currently, we do not know the mechanism behind this effect.

While ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole are commonly prescribed for UTIs [92], we deliberately chose gentamicin due to its clinical relevance in our patient cohort. Gentamicin is frequently used as a last-resort treatment, administered both systemically and locally, including intravesical instillation and catheter irrigation, where high concentrations are delivered directly to the bladder [93]. This dual use allowed us to model both systemic and localised treatment scenarios, as illustrated in Fig. 5A and C.

Protective effects against antibiotics in multispecies settings has been previously shown by de Vos et al. [85], who showed that polymicrobial communities can collectively withstand antibiotics through mechanisms such as extracellular enzyme activity, detoxification, or local environmental modification. Leading to resistance levels that cannot be explained by single-species biofilm resistance alone.

In single-species C-1 biofilms, S. agalactiae showed minimal reduction loss (<1 log), E. coli biomass decreased by 3 logs, and K. pneumoniae was completely eradicated (8 logs reduction). However, in the mixed-species C-1 biofilm, in both static and flow conditions E. coli and K. pneumoniae biomass dropped by less than 1 log, clear evidence of interspecies protection and that this protection is greater in the biofilm than in planktonic culture.

Similarly, in model C-5, E. coli exhibited resistance to gentamicin under all tested conditions, likely due to the presence of the aac(3)-IId gene. E. faecalis, which showed a 1.5 log reduction in single-species biofilms, remained unaffected in both static and flow mixed biofilms. P. aeruginosa displayed consistent tolerance across mixed static, and mixed flow conditions, with only a 1 log reduction similar to P. aeruginosa strain 5-10. K. pneumoniae was significantly reduced in single-species biofilms (6 log reduction), and this effect persisted under static mixed conditions, but under flow, the reduction was limited to just 1 log, highlighting the protective effect of the flow. Differences between shear forces shaping biofilm architecture and hydrodynamic conditions changing nutrient gradients and localised microenvironments could be some of factors behind this increase in gentamicin resistance between static and flow conditions [94].

However, the simplicity and reproducibility static multi well plates with biofilm growing on catheter pieces fixed to the lid of the plate as pegs allowed us to test all single isolates and mixed biofilm models. The biofilm under macro-fluidic model is burdened by labour intensive and high costs but the architecture, structure and arrangement of bacteria within biofilm community is heavily implicated in antibiotic resistance [95]. In this work we clearly showed that under flow conditions, all species within the multispecies biofilm demonstrated heightened resistance, highlighting biofilm integrity and cooperative defence mechanisms. While the static conditions failed to fully replicate this resistance in all species such in case of K. pneumoniae.

Overall, this work has simulated simplified clinical polymicrobial biofilm environments, to improve strategies for managing and mitigating microbial infections caused by biofilms. Future efforts to develop antibiofilm strategies need to consider the intricate nature of polymicrobial biofilms, including their inherent resilience and resistance to treatment. It's also essential to test these approaches in appropriate models to ensure their effectiveness.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The samples for this project were obtained as part of a multi-centre observational longitudinal study 3PU (ACTRN12622000613707). The ethics approval for all study protocols were obtained from the Northern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) 2021/ETH00329 as well as Site Specific Assessment (SSA) 2021/STE00542 by Royal North Shore Hospital governance, 2021/STE00541 by Prince of Wales Hospital governance and Royal Rehab governance dated 29/9/2022. Participants were adults, age ≥18 years, with stable SCI and stable neurogenic bladder management technique for at least 4 weeks before start of the study. Participants agreed to have their urinary system microbial community and the microbiome DNA from the catheter and urine to be extracted and stored long-term. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and samples were de-identified by the assigning of an arbitrary subject number. Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) was used to capture demographic data as well as sample information.
