A functional atlas of transposon-encoded products and their integration into host networks
Authors: Carles Borredá, Pol Vendrell-Mir, Basile Leduque, Mireia Bueno Merino, Céline Oury, Nathalie Glab, Vincent Colot, Leandro Quadrana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transposable elements (TEs) are pervasive genomic components that propagate via self-encoded factors, yet the nature, regulation, and function of these factors remain largely unresolved. Here, we integrated extensive long- and short-read transcriptome data, regulatory network analyses, deep proteomics, and structural predictions to construct a comprehensive atlas of TE products in Arabidopsis. We show that TE expression is embedded within host regulatory circuits, with DNA methylation and transcription factors jointly shaping TE transcriptional activity. Proteomic analyses confirm the production of over a hundred of high-confidence TE-encoded proteins, and structure-guided analyses of the transcript-informed TE proteome predict previously uncharacterized structural folds, multimerization capacity, and host protein interaction potential. Structural alignments further uncover cryptic homologies between TE-encoded proteins and host factors, including cases of domestications and co-options. Together, our study reveals the functional integration of TEs into cellular pathways and underscores the role of TEs as active drivers of genome function and innovation.

Introduction

Transposable elements (TEs) are ubiquitous DNA sequences that move and self-propagate across the genome. Despite the threat they pose for genome integrity, TEs are also major drivers of evolution. For instance, through so-called “domestication”, TEs have enabled the emergence of proteins with important cellular functions, including the repurposing of TE transposases to give rise to the RAG recombinases in jawed vertebrates1 or the transcription factors FHY3 and FAR1 that modulate light signaling in plants2. In addition, TEs have played major roles in the evolution of eukaryotic chromosomes, including the formation of telomeres and centromeres3. Despite the extensive knowledge regarding the contribution of TEs to cellular2 and chromosome functions at the macroevolutionary scale, our understanding of the products encoded by TEs remains limited.

TEs achieve mobilization by encoding at least one core enzyme involved in the process through DNA or RNA sequence recognition. Class I retrotransposons move via an RNA intermediate and rely on a reverse transcriptase and endonuclease, whereas Class II DNA transposons use a DNA intermediate often mobilized by a “cut-and-paste” mechanism catalyzed by transposases. In addition to these highly conserved core enzymes, TEs can encode a wide variety of accessory proteins or protein domains4,5. These factors, often lineage-specific, are critical for TE propagation, for example by modulating integration preferences or counteracting silencing responses through interactions with host cellular pathways4–6. Analogous to accessory genes in a pangenome, which are variably present across strains and play crucial roles in local adaptation7, accessory TE factors and their interplay with host proteins may contribute to shaping the TE’s niche specificity. However, their diversity, function, and interactions with host proteins are largely unknown. This knowledge gap is largely due to the lack of reliable functional annotations of TEs. In fact, most TE sequences in a genome are thought to be fossilized copies of once active elements and typically have lost all coding potential8–10. In addition, robust epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation in plants and mammals, suppress the expression of transcriptionally competent TEs11, which complicates the identification of those that may still be active as well as of the genes they contain and the proteins they encode. Furthermore, because of their repetitive nature, analyzing TEs and their transcripts with short-read sequencing technology is inherently challenging9.

Here, by leveraging extensive long-read transcriptomic data from A. thaliana plants defective in multiple layers of epigenetic silencing and exposed to various environmental stresses, along with ribosome profiling, proteomics, and protein structure-guided searches, we constructed a comprehensive atlas of TE-genes and products. Our results demonstrate the deep integration of TEs into the host’s regulatory, transcriptional, interactome, and evolutionary networks. The resource, methodological framework, and biological insights presented here pave the way for similar studies in other organisms.

Results

Transposable elements transcriptome

The Arabidopsis reference genome (Col-0 release TAIR10) contains about 32,000 annotated TE sequences belonging to 326 families12. A vast proportion of these annotations correspond to ancestral TE copies that have degenerated to different degrees and presumably lost any mobilization activity8. As a first step to identify transcriptionally active TEs and the products they encode, we generated Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) long-read cDNA sequencing data from plants defective in the chromatin remodeler DDM1 (Fig. 1A), which is required for DNA methylation-dependent silencing of TEs13. We also analyzed plants combining the ddm1 mutation with mutations in the RDR2 or RDR6 genes, which are involved in the siRNA-mediated transcriptional and post-transcriptional silencing of TEs, respectively14. In total, we obtained more than 9 million reads, with a median read length of 1500 nucleotides. Several Arabidopsis TEs have been shown to be induced by environmental stresses15,16. To broaden the spectrum of transcriptionally reactivated TEs, we generated additional ONT-based long-read cDNA sequencing data for ddm1, ddm1rdr2 and ddm1rdr6 mutants subjected to heat stress or to the bacterial elicitor flagellin, which mimics bacterial infection16. High-confidence transcript isoforms were identified using the Full-Length Alternative Isoform analysis of RNA (FLAIR) bioinformatic tool17 (Fig. 1A, B). In total, we assembled 65,985 transcript isoforms, spanning 19,514 genes not overlapping TE sequences (hereafter referred to as cellular genes) and 1791 TEs (Fig. 1C). 98.7% of all exon-exon junctions identified by our approach over cellular genes were validated by Illumina short reads, and 97.4% displayed canonical GT-AG splice sites. Based on the position and strand of the poly-A tail in the ONT long reads, the orientation of 98.8% of all cellular gene-derived isoforms was determined, matching the reference orientation in 97.8% of cases. Altogether, these results establish the high quality of the obtained transcript annotation (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

We next characterized the 5732 transcripts (8.3% of all the annotated transcripts) generated exclusively by TEs (Supplementary Data 1), as opposed to chimeric transcripts that span both TEs and cellular genes18,19, (Fig. 1C). The majority originates from Ty3/Gypsy LTR retrotransposons, followed by EN/SPM and MuDR DNA transposons, as well as Ty1/Copia LTR retrotransposons (Fig. 1D). For specific superfamilies, particularly the EN/SPM DNA transposons, DNA methylation loss in control conditions was sufficient to induce the expression of most of the TEs detected in our transcriptome assembly (Fig. 1D). In contrast, for the Ty1/Copia, Ty3/Gypsy and MuDR superfamilies, a larger proportion of elements was expressed exclusively when epigenetically compromised plants are subject to biotic or abiotic stresses, indicating that the environment plays a more prominent role in the regulation of these type of TEs (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 3). The combination of high-depth long-read transcriptomics on various epigenetic mutants with strong effect on TE control, together with environmental stresses allowed us to detect expression for the greatest number of Arabidopsis TEs so far, with over 60% of the TE-transcript isoforms detected here being absent from previous splicing-aware, transcriptome-guided, or predicted TE annotations18,20 (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 2B, C).

To assess the specificity of our TE-transcript annotation, we assessed the presence of high-quality RNA-seq short reads spanning exon-exon junctions. As for cellular genes, 96.4% of spliced introns within our long-read transcriptome TE annotation were validated by short-read data, compared to only 72.2% for previous annotations (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Most (86.6%) TE-introns contain GT-AG donor-acceptor splicing sites (Supplementary Fig. 2E), indicating that TE-derived primary transcripts are typically processed by canonical splicing. Expressed TEs are much longer and with higher homology to their corresponding Repbase consensus sequence21 than non-expressed TEs (Fig. 1F), indicating that TE’s age and completeness is a good proxy for their transcriptional competence.

TEs often produce multiple proteins, encoded either by distinct TE-genes22,23, by alternative splicing24 or post-translational processing of immature proteins25. To explore these mechanisms systematically, we quantified both the number of non-overlapping TE-genes fully contained within TEs and their transcript isoforms, determining orientation in each case. In total, we identified 2422 TE-genes and substantial variability in gene architectures across TE families and superfamilies. For instance, MuDR DNA transposons typically harbor two to three TE-genes, with some elements containing up to six (Fig. 1G). In contrast, Arabidopsis EN/SPM3 and EN/SPM7 DNA transposons possess a single TE-gene, generating distinct transcripts through alternative splicing (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 4), structurally resembling the maize En/Spm transposon24. However, while En/Spm alternative transcript isoforms produce two distinct proteins (TnpD transposase and TnpA accessory factor), we identified at least four Arabidopsis EN/SPM families (SPM2, 5, 6, and 9) that encode putative TnpD and TnpA proteins via two separate, non-overlapping genes arranged head-to-tail in tandem (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, we identified three likely non-autonomous families, EN/SPM1, 1A, and 4, that contain only a single gene, related to TnpA (Supplementary Fig. 5). Collectively, our findings highlight the rapid evolutionary turnover in TE coding strategies, demonstrating that closely related TE families have transitioned between alternative splicing and split-gene architectures to encode functionally related proteins.

Similar to cellular genes, TE-genes generally produce one or a few transcript isoforms, with the notable exception of EN/SPM and LINE elements (Fig. 1G). Specifically, AT3G32230 (EN/SPM2) and AT2G10250 (ATLINE1_6) TE-genes generate over 37 distinct transcript isoforms each. Nonetheless, fewer than four isoforms account for over 95% of steady-state mRNAs in all cases (Fig. 1H). Taken together, these observations underscore the diverse mechanisms TEs employ to encode the multiple functions crucial for their propagation.

Intertwined regulatory networks of TEs and cellular genes

We next leveraged our high-quality annotation of TE-genes to investigate their transcriptional regulation using 375 publicly available RNA-seq datasets that encompass 21 different plant tissues and 19 growth conditions of wild-type Arabidopsis plants (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6A). On the basis of 3270 TE-genes, including 848 putative TE-genes annotated in silico in TAIR10 but not present by our transcriptomic-informed de novo annotation, and 25,664 cellular genes robustly expressed in at least one sample, we reconstructed the transcriptional network. Overall, 75% of TE-genes and 82.4% of cellular genes grouped into 32 co-expressed clusters (Supplementary Data 3 and 4), and these were associated with diverse biological functions (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Fig. 6B). Remarkably, TE-genes were distributed across most clusters, and their co-expressed cellular genes were typically not adjacent (>1 Kbp), especially for TE-genes carried by full-length TEs (Supplementary Fig. 6C). To identify cis-regulatory elements that may underlie the TE-gene regulatory networks, we reanalyzed binding sites for 529 Arabidopsis transcription factors (TFs), as measured by in vitro DNA affinity purification and sequencing (DAP-seq)26. In total, we identified over 4.5 million TF binding sites (TFBS) within DAP-seq peaks (Supplementary Fig. 6D and Supplementary Data 5). LTR retrotransposons frequently carry TFBS upstream of TSS, consistent with LTR sequences playing regulatory roles27. In contrast, TFBSs in DNA transposons were more often found downstream of the TSS (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that regulation of Class II TEs may rely preferentially on downstream regulatory sequences. Thus, structural differences between TE classes influence the spatial distribution of TFBS.

In addition to these global patterns, analyses of individual TF families revealed idiosyncratic distributions (Fig. 2C). Specifically, while GeBP TFBS were overrepresented upstream of the TSS in both cellular and TE-genes, C2C2-GATA and AP2-EREBP binding sites were instead enriched downstream of TSSs. Thus, our results indicate that the reported tendency of specific TFBSs to accumulate on only one side of the TSS28 is conserved between cellular and TE-genes.

The enrichment of TFBSs near the TSS of TE-genes, along with our observations of coexpression with cellular genes, prompted us to investigate the role of TFs in TE regulatory networks. We analyzed the expression profiles of TFs and TE-genes containing binding sites for these TFs and identified 1129 co-expressed TF–TE pairs. TFBSs for these co-expressed pairs were more prominantly enriched near the TSS (Supplementary Fig. 6E), supporting their functional relevance. Collectively, these findings show that TE expression is tightly integrated into the host’s transcriptional network and that different TE-genes have evolved distinct transcriptional regulations linked to specific cis-regulatory sequences.

DNA methylation-sensitive TFs shape the transcriptional network of TEs

We next explored the influence of DNA methylation on the transcriptional network of TEs and cellular genes. To this end, we obtained RNA-seq data for nine developmental stages and reproductive tissues of ddm1 plants, as well as seedlings of ddm1, ddm1rdr2 and ddm1rdr6 mutants subjected to biotic and abiotic stresses. Network reconstruction yielded 78 groups of co-expressed TE and cellular genes (Fig. 2D). Although more diffuse, clusters of co-expressed features in wild-type and epigenetically compromised datasets retained a significant degree of similarity, with Jaccard indices far higher than expected by random associations (Fig. 2E; KS test P < 1 × 10−100). Importantly, the distribution of Jaccard indices for TE-genes was significantly shifted toward lower values compared to cellular genes (KS test P = 7.1 × 10−36), indicating that loss of DNA methylation exerts a stronger rewiring effect on the transcriptional networks of TE-genes than on those of cellular genes (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, Jaccard similarity declines as TE integrity increases (Supplementary Fig. 8A), consistent with younger TEs being more deeply embedded in DNA methylation-sensitive regulatory networks than older, degenerated elements.

A notable example of DNA methylation-sensitive modulation involves the widespread GATC regulatory motif, which is enriched downstream of TSSs (Fig. 2C) and has been shown to enhance gene transcription levels28. While the effect of GATC motif accumulation on cellular gene expression is similar between wild-type and ddm1 plants (0.16), it is only associated with TE expression in the ddm1 mutant (0.16 vs 0.02, Fig. 2F), indicating that TEs exploit the GATC dose-dependent transcriptional regulation to modulate their expression in a DNA methylation-dependent manner.

Beyond the widespread influence of GATC motifs, DNA methylation may also shape TE expression networks by modulating TF binding. To explore this possibility, we reanalyzed DAP-seq data for 343 Arabidopsis TFs generated on both native genomic DNA (retaining endogenous DNA methylation) and PCR-amplified DNA (effectively methylation-free)26. Differential binding between these two substrates thus provides an in vitro measure of DNA methylation sensitivity of TFs. Using this approach, we found that TE-genes whose coexpression network is most altered upon methylation loss (lowest wild-type vs ddm1 Jaccard index) are significantly more enriched on DNA methylation-sensitive TFBS than those with highly conserved coexpression network (highest Jaccard index) (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Fig. 8A). Notably, this association was observed for TE-genes but not for cellular genes, and it was stronger for full-length than truncated TEs (Supplementary Fig. 8B), indicating that younger, intact elements are more prominently regulated through methylation-sensitive TFs. A striking example of this pattern is the heat shock factor HSFA2, previously shown to regulate the LTR-retrotransposon ATCOPIA78/ONSEN29. Coexpression between HSFA2 and ATCOPIA78/ONSEN was much higher in hypomethylated than wild-type backgrounds (Fig. 2H), suggesting a modulatory role of DNA methylation in HSFA2-mediated ATCOPIA78/ONSEN expression. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed HSFA2 in wild-type and ddm1 mutant plants, which exhibit hypomethylation of its cognate TFBS over ATCOPIA78/ONSEN copies (Supplementary Fig. 8C). Expression analyses of three independent HSFA2-overexpressing and control lines show significant upregulation of ATCOPIA78/ONSEN exclusively in ddm1 (Fig. 2I), establishing a direct modulatory role of DNA methylation on the HSFA2-mediated induction of this retrotransposon. Together, these results indicate that the DNA methylation sensitivity of TFs configures the wiring of TEs into host regulatory networks.

Transposable elements proteome

We next set out to determine the extent to which expressed TEs encode proteins. To address this, we first explored the presence of open reading frames (ORFs) within the functionally annotated set of TE transcripts. Out of 5732 TE transcripts, 5046 encode peptides of at least 50 amino acids (Supplementary Data 1), spanning 1904 TE-genes. Approximately one third of the TE-genes (686 out of 1904) produce at least one transcript potentially encoding proteins with at least one conserved domain (CD), as identified in the CDD database (Supplementary Fig. 9). To assess whether the transcripts produced by the 1904 TE-genes with ORFs are engaged in translation, we interrogated ribosome footprint profiling (Ribo-seq) datasets from wild-type and ddm1 plants30. Ribo-seq coverage varied greatly among TE-genes, with most of them showing low or no coverage and some showing levels comparable to those of cellular genes (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Further analysis indicated that this distribution is compounded by a mix of truncated TEs with low Ribo-seq coverage, and full-length TE copies with high translational efficiency (Fig. 3B). To directly confirm the expression of TE-encoded proteins, we performed MS-based data-dependent and independent acquisition (DDA and DIA, respectively) proteomics on both wild-type and ddm1 plants (Fig. 3A). In addition, we integrated these data with previously published proteomic datasets spanning multiple Arabidopsis tissues and developmental stages31. High-confidence peptides (q-value < 0.001) were matched against the Arabidopsis reference proteome and our curated TE-gene annotation (Supplementary Data 6 and 7). In total, we identified peptides matching 243 TE proteins (Supplementary Data 8), 135 of which were supported by two or more independent peptides and considered as high-confidence proteins for subsequent analyses (Supplementary Fig. 11A and Supplementary Data 9). Almost all high-confidence TE proteins (115 out of 135) were exclusively detected in ddm1 plants and restricted to transcriptionally active TEs, further supporting bona fide TE-encoded proteins, as opposed to peptide misassignment (Supplementary Fig. 11B). These high-confidence TE proteins spanned diverse classes and functional categories of TEs and TE-encoded proteins, including GAG proteins produced by Ty1/Copia LTR-retrotransposons or the TnpA-like protein encoded by EN/SPM DNA transposons (Figs. 3D, E), among others (Supplementary Fig. 11C). Comparison with Ribo-seq data showed that both cellular and TE-encoded proteins with high ribosome occupancy were more likely to be detected by LC-MS (Fig. 3C), establishing that full-length expressed TEs can be actively translated in Arabidopsis. Interestingly, ribosome profiling and proteomics together reveal a two-step attenuation of TE protein output. Roughly 92% of expressed cellular genes, 78% of expressed TE-genes from full‑length elements, and only ~50% from truncated TEs carry ribosome footprints in ddm1 (Fig. 3F). This gradient suggests that truncated TEs encounter the strongest barrier already at the level of translation initiation and/or elongation, whereas most intact elements still efficiently recruit ribosomes. Among the ribosome‑bound transcripts, however, peptide recovery falls to 41% for cellular genes, 15.2% for full‑length TEs, and just 2.9% for truncated elements (Fig. 3G), suggesting an additional post-ribosomal constraint on TE protein output. Importantly, the reduced detection of TE proteins relative to cellular genes is not explained by differences in transcript abundance (Supplementary Fig. 10E).

Translational repression by siRNAs has been reported in Arabidopsis32, but whether this mechanism affects TEs, which are specifically targeted by RDR2- and RDR6-dependent siRNAs14, remains unknown. To investigate the potential role of these siRNAs in limiting TE protein accumulation, we performed high-depth DIA proteomics on ddm1, ddm1rdr2, and ddm1rdr6 mutant plants. On average, we identified approximately 60 and 67 TE-encoded proteins per sample in ddm1 and in the double mutants, respectively (Fig. 3H). Combining the proteins identified in single and double mutants increased the total number of high-confidence proteins from 135 to 139, in agreement with the predominant role of DDM1 as a major orchestrator of TE transcriptional regulation (Fig. 1). Moreover, TE protein abundance relative to transcript levels in ddm1 was nearly identical in the double mutants (Fig. 3I), indicating that RDR2- or RDR6-dependent siRNAs do not play a major role in translational or post-translational repression of TE expression. Altogether, these results revealed that TE protein production is constrained at the post-translational level, most severely for truncated copies but also for intact elements, and that this constraint is largely independent of siRNA-based mechanisms.

A hidden diversity of TE-encoded protein domains

We next set out to explore the functions of TE-encoded proteins. Given their rapid evolution, sequence homology alone often fails to uncover distant relationships or latent functions. Indeed, we found that only one third of the predicted ORFs contained recognizable conserved domains (Fig. 3A). In contrast, protein structures are expected to retain homology over much longer evolutionary timescales, making it possible to detect functional and evolutionary relationships even when sequence similarity is lost33. Because TE-encoded proteins are critically underrepresented in protein structure databases, including the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database34, we modeled the structures of the 6835 proteins predicted to be encoded by the 4965 ORF-containing TE-transcript isoforms using AlphaFold2 (AF2)35. More than half of these protein sequences (56.1%) had high or very high-confidence structural predictions (pLDDT > 70) (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Comparison between the predicted and experimentally resolved structure of the VANDAL21-encoded VANC protein36, not used for AF2 training, shows a strong match (Supplementary Fig. 12B), further supporting the robustness of the structural modeling. On the basis of predicted alignment errors and inter-residue distances in the 3D structures, we identified 4,466 globular structural domains (SDs), as well as numerous intrinsically disordered regions.

We next performed both structural and sequence-based alignments between TE-encoded proteins and cellular proteins. Only 17% of TE proteins share >50% sequence identity with any cellular protein, whereas a structural match can be found for 84% of them (Fig. 4A). By contrast, 65% of cellular proteins show sequence matches to other cellular proteins. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) of pairwise structural similarity between TE and cellular proteins revealed dozens of clusters (Fig. 4B). Notably, some clusters of TE-encoded proteins included a few cellular proteins too, suggesting potential evolutionary or functional relationships. As a first validation of our approach, we identified a cluster containing MuDR-encoded transposase structural domains alongside the transcription factors FHY3 and FAR1 (Fig. 4B, C), consistent with previous evidence that these transcription factors originated from the domestication of a MuDR transposase2.

Beyond this validation, we identified a cluster of structurally similar Ty3/Gypsy-encoded proteases that includes the cellular protein DNA-DAMAGE INDUCIBLE 1 (DDI1), a highly conserved eukaryotic ubiquitin-dependent protease involved in cell cycle regulation37,38, along with a family of uncharacterized cellular proteases (Fig. 4B, D). The high structural similarity, together with the phylogenetic placement of these uncharacterized cellular proteases as an internal branch within Ty3/Gypsy proteases (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. 13), suggests that they originated from the domestication of a retrotransposon protease.

Our analyses also revealed structural homology between MuDR-encoded VANC proteins and the cellular factor MAINTENANCE OF MERISTEMS (MAIN) (Fig. 4B, E), a chromatin regulator involved in TE silencing39. Since MAIN predates the emergence of VANC proteins39,40, which are dicot-specific, our results suggest that VANC anti-silencing factors probably evolved via the capture and repurposing of MAIN-like proteins. We also identified a previously unrecognized domain encoded by ATLANTYS LTR retrotransposons with strong structural homology to the meiotic-specific Asynaptic 2 (ASY2) protein (Fig. 4B, F), which is implicated in the formation of the meiotic chromosome axis via coiled-coil oligomerization41,42. In addition, the globular SDs at the C-terminal region of EN/SPM3-encoded TnpA resemble the small β-barrel-like NDX-B domain of the plant-specific heterochromatin regulator NODULIN HOMEOBOX (Fig. 4B, G), which controls DNA methylation over TEs43. Given that NODULIN HOMEOBOX is shared among land plants (Supplementary Fig. 14), whereas TnpA-like domains are only found in flowering plants, our findings suggest that EN/SPMs co-opted and repurposed this conserved heterochromatin regulator. Together, these data reveal a highly modular TE proteome in which ~75% of proteins retain structural relatives despite significant sequence divergence. Structure‑guided searches therefore unlock the identification of cryptic evolutionary links, including new cases of domestication and host‑to‑TE co-option that are invisible to primary sequence surveys.

Structural modeling of TE multiprotein complexes

TE mobilization typically involves multiprotein complexes44; however, the extent of TE-encoded protein–protein interactions (PPIs) is largely unknown. To address this question, we used AlphaFold-Multimer to model homo- and heterodimers45. We focused on 18 TE-encoded proteins from 11 full‑length elements in our transcript-informed TE annotation, spanning both Class I and Class II superfamilies (Supplementary Data 10). These TEs were selected because they are strongly expressed in epigenetic mutants and have independent evidence of ongoing mobility, consistent with functional TE-encoded transposition machinery, either from prior experimental mobilization assays46 or inferred transposition in natural populations47. While predicted heterodimers were relatively rare (14 out of 153 possible interactions), two-thirds (13 out of 18) of the analyzed TE proteins formed homodimers (Fig. 5A). This proportion is significantly higher than that obtained for 1 M sets of 18 random cellular proteins (Monte Carlo P = 0.0264 versus cellular sets), suggesting a higher homodimerization potential for TE-encoded proteins compared to cellular proteins. Further characterization of homodimerization domains revealed that the integrase of the Ty3/Gypsy retrotransposon ATGP3 (ATGP3:AT3TE16035) has a large interacting surface involving scattered residues (Supplementary Fig. 15A), consistent with previous observations48. Similarly, the protease encoded by Ty1/Copia ATCOPIA93 (ATCOPIA93:AT5TE20395) displayed a large interaction surface formed by scattered residues (Supplementary Fig. 15B). We next assessed the potential of TE-encoded proteins to assemble into higher-order homomers. To this end, we modeled the 3D structures of homomers containing up to six subunits and evaluated their interaction potential using the interface predicted template modeling score (ipTM)49. Strikingly, several TE-encoded proteins that showed no dimerization potential were nevertheless assembled in silico into tetramers–hexamers (Fig. 5B). For example, GAG proteins formed stable pentamers and hexamers (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 16), a configuration paralleling the resolved icosahedral shells of Ty1/Copia VLPs50. Moreover, we found that VANC and the poorly characterized Athila domain encoded by ATHILA LTR-retrotransposons form compact pentamers via interactions through extended coiled-coil domains (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 16D, E). Although establishing higher-order homomeric conformations from structural predictions is challenging and the use of different approaches may differ, these results underscore the potential of TE-encoded proteins to assemble into higher-order homomeric complexes.

Having mapped global self‑assembly, we next asked whether TE proteins also build mixed complexes inside a single element. Notably, the EN/SPM3-encoded TnpA-like protein homodimerizes (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 17A, B), and shows heterodimerization potential with its cognate transposase, TnpD (Fig. 5A). The homodimerization and heterodimerization propensities of TnpAs and TnpDs suggest they may assemble into a larger protein complex. We modeled the 3D structures of complexes with variable numbers of TnpA (A) and TnpD (D) combinations (Fig. 5D) and used the ipTM metric to determine the most likely stoichiometries51. We found high ipTM for the A4:D2 configuration in which TnpA dimers interact with distinct subunits of the TnpD dimer (Fig. 5E). This configuration is reminiscent of transpososome structures observed in bacterial transposons such as Tn752, where the accessory proteins TnsC and TnsD bind target DNA to guide and regulate the transposase. Consistently, we identified a positively charged pocket in the globular domain of EN/SPM3-encoded TnpA (Supplementary Fig. 17A, C), indicative of DNA-binding53. Hence, the model suggests that DNA binding by TnpA may potentially bridge distant DNA molecules while positioning TnpD transposases at the element’s terminal inverted repeats to form a nucleoprotein complex (Fig. 5F), offering a structural framework for the En/Spm transpososome. Altogether, these structural predictions indicate that several TE-encoded proteins can form higher-order complexes, providing powerful working hypotheses for future structural and biochemical exploration.

TE-host PPI networks revealed by structure-based screening

Given the modularity and functional complexity revealed by TE protein structures, we next investigated how these proteins may interact with cellular proteins. We performed AlphaFold-Multimer PPI screens54,55 for 16 proteins encoded by distinct types of Class I and Class II TEs against 4164 host-encoded cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins involved in structural and regulatory cellular functions (Supplementary Data 11). To validate the method, we benchmarked 1596 experimentally supported Arabidopsis heterodimers and 1596 random Arabidopsis protein pairs (Fig. 6A). This benchmarking revealed that an ipTM > 0.42 achieved 55% sensitivity at 95% specificity (Supplementary Fig. 18A, B). In addition to ipTM, we implemented another metric, ipSAE, which integrates multiple PPI features to achieve higher specificity and sensitivity56. Using a mean ipSAE threshold across models of 0.1, we achieved 60.7% sensitivity at 95% specificity (Fig. 6B). Applying this last cutoff to the 66,624 TE-host binary combinations, we identified 707 predicted high-confidence PPIs (Fig. 6C). Network analysis uncovered 63 host “hubs” that interact with ≥2 TE proteins, doubling the random expectation (Fig. 6D, E). Gene‑ontology enrichment of host hub proteins showed a striking bias toward nuclear‑import pathways (Fig. 6F), with several TE factors predicted to interact with these proteins. Similar enrichments were found with more stringent ipSAE thresholds or using ipTM, suggesting robust predictions (Supplementary Fig. 18). Our analyses uncovered additional pathway-specific enrichment patterns (Fig. 6F). At least five TE-encoded proteins interact with host proteins implicated in transcriptional regulation. Across the interactome, the number of PPIs varied widely among TE-encoded proteins, ranging from 1 (LINE12 ORF2) to 192 (VANDAL21 VANB) (Fig. 6C). In some cases, TE-host PPI interphases overlap with homodimerization domains identified on TE proteins (Fig. 5), raising the possibility of non-specific or competing interactions (Supplementary Fig. 19). Strikingly, accessory proteins (VANB, VANC, GAG, Athila) consistently showed far more host contacts than core catalytic enzymes (transposase, integrase, reverse transcriptase)(Fig. 6D, G). This difference persisted across ipSAE or ipTM thresholds (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. 18E) and could not be accounted for by differences in protein length, structural prediction confidence, or homodimerization capacity (Supplementary Fig. 20). Instead, our results suggest that accessory TE proteins may have a higher intrinsic tendency than core catalytic proteins to form PPIs with host proteins.

Variation in the number of PPIs may stem, in part, from structural properties. Specifically, protein surface accessibility and the density of unfavorable protein–water interfaces have been linked to a higher propensity for PPIs57. To explore these possibilities, we measured surface-to-volume ratios (protein roughness) and backbone H-bond solvent accessibility in core catalytic and accessory TE-encoded proteins. Both metrics were significantly higher in accessory TE proteins than in core proteins (Fig. 6H, I), supporting the notion that a rougher protein surface and greater solvent accessibility facilitate PPI formation.

These observations prompted us to ask how accessory TE proteins evolved structural properties that favor PPIs. Accumulation of slightly deleterious mutations, while not disrupting protein function, can nonetheless erode the protein surface or protein–water interface, promoting the evolution of PPIs57. Under relaxed purifying selection, such mutations are expected to accumulate more frequently, increasing the propensity of proteins to engage in new PPIs58. To test this hypothesis, we calculated and compared the ratio of non-synonymous-to-synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) for core and accessory TE proteins. This analysis revealed that accessory TE proteins exhibited significantly higher dN/dS ratios than core proteins (Fig. 6J), indicating a relaxation of purifying selection and a faster evolutionary rate. Moreover, dN/dS ratios of TE proteins correlate positively with their number of PPIs (Pearson one-tailed test, P = 0.014). Altogether, our analyses suggest that relaxed selective pressure on accessory TE proteins could have facilitated the emergence of interactions with host proteins, driving the diversification of TE-host relationships.

Discussion

Despite being major components of most eukaryotic genomes, TEs and their encoded products remain poorly characterized at the functional level. Here, by integrating deep long-read transcriptomics and proteomics from Arabidopsis plants defective in multiple layers of epigenetic silencing and subjected to environmental stresses, we built a comprehensive atlas of TE-genes and products. Our analysis provides a first integrated functional characterization of the large and diverse repertoire of transcriptionally active TEs present in a single genome.

A key finding of our study is that TE expression is deeply embedded within the host’s regulatory landscape, with most TE-genes displaying developmental or environmental specificities. In mammals, retrotransposons undergo widespread reactivation during zygotic genome activation19, and prior studies in Arabidopsis reported upregulation of TEs in pollen59. We found no evidence for global TE reactivation in any Arabidopsis developmental stage, including pollen, or stress condition, neither at the transcript (Supplementary Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 10B, C, F) nor at the protein level (Supplementary Fig. 10D). Instead, we identified distinct TE subsets with divergent transcriptional programs, suggesting that reactivation occurs in specific cellular niches rather than in a single tissue. This contrast with mammals may stem from fundamental differences in germline specification: in plants, the germline is not set aside early in development but arises late from meristematic stem cells, allowing transposition throughout the life cycle to generate heritable insertions.

Consistent with its central role in TE suppression60, transcriptional network reconstructions in wild-type and epigenetically compromised mutant backgrounds revealed that DNA methylation exerts a stronger influence on the transcriptional network of TE-genes than on that of cellular genes, especially for young TEs. As a case in point, we show that HSFA2-induced reactivation of the heat-sensitive retrotransposon ATCOPIA78/ONSEN is sensitive to DNA methylation. In addition, we detected dose-dependent modulation of TE expression levels via the accumulation of GATC motifs28, and we show that this modulation is also contingent on DNA methylation.

An important finding of our work is that numerous TEs are not only transcriptionally active but translated into measurable proteins, contributing to the cellular proteome. Notably, we observed a steep drop-off in ribosome association among truncated TEs, and a further decline from ribosome occupancy to peptide detection in full-length TEs. These patterns reveal two additional layers of repression: strong translational exclusion of truncated TEs, likely due to ribosome stalling30,61, and post-translational suppression of proteins encoded by full-length TEs. Importantly, these constraints are not alleviated in ddm1rdr2 or ddm1rdr6 mutant backgrounds, indicating that siRNA-dependent pathways appear to play little role in translational and post-translational suppression of TEs. The involvement of other mechanisms, such as proteasome-mediated degradation or subcellular sequestration of TE proteins62–64, warrants further investigation.

Structural modeling substantially expanded the known functional and evolutionary landscape of TE proteins, uncovering numerous cryptic homologies undetectable through sequence similarity alone. We traced the origin of the MuDR-encoded anti-silencing protein VANC40,65, which likely resulted from the co-option of a cellular protein related to the chromatin regulator MAIN39. We also identify a family of uncharacterized cellular proteases that evolved through domestication of a Ty3/Gypsy retrotransposon. Other striking examples include co-option of the meiotic protein ASY2 and the NDX-B domain into accessory proteins encoded by ATLANTYS LTR retrotransposons and EN/SPM DNA transposons, respectively.

A recurring theme in our findings is the modularity of TE-encoded products, both in their discrete domain architectures and multimerization capacity. Modularity likely facilitates their functional diversification while maintaining compact coding capacity, a key constraint for TEs given the selective pressure against large genomic cargo66. It also provides a mechanistic basis for the evolutionary plasticity of TE proteins, as modularity facilitates domain shuffling, multimerization, and eventual co-option into host regulatory systems5. By acting as combinatorial platforms, modular TE proteins may thus enhance the functional repertoire of TEs while facilitating their integration into host cellular pathways.

Finally, we systematically mapped in silico the interactome between diverse TE-encoded proteins and host proteins. Benchmarking AlphaFold-Multimer predictions against experimentally validated Arabidopsis protein complexes enabled us to confidently identify hundreds of potential interactions between TE proteins and host proteins, particularly those involved in nuclear trafficking and cell cycle control. Accessory TE proteins consistently displayed broader PPI networks than core TE enzymes, a difference we found associated with higher surface roughness and solvent-exposed hydrogen bonds, two structural traits known to favor PPIs57. From an evolutionary standpoint, solvent accessibility has been proposed to augment as a consequence of relaxed purifying selection, fueling a non-adaptive expansion of interactome complexity58. In line with this scenario, accessory TE proteins showed higher dN/dS ratios than core proteins and a positive correlation between evolutionary rate and PPI number. Moreover, our findings show a much wider structural and functional landscape of accessory than core TE proteins, punctuated by frequent co-options and repurposing for cellular activities. It is possible that their rapid turnover, frequent acquisition of host domains, and structural adaptability play a major role in the diversification of TE-host relationships. Similar mechanisms may operate broadly across host-virus interactions67, with potential implications for antiviral therapeutic strategies. We note that while these predictions provide a valuable hypothesis-generating framework, future experimental validation, including yeast two-hybrid screens or co-immunoprecipitation assays, will be essential to confirm the in vivo relevance of these interactions

In summary, our work demonstrates that TEs are not merely parasitic or passive genomic passengers but active contributors to the molecular and evolutionary dynamics of their host. The extensive integration of TEs into transcriptional and protein networks, coupled with the discovery of cryptic protein domains and host interactions, underscores their multifaceted roles in genome function. Our functional atlas not only expands our knowledge of TE biology but also lays a conceptual and methodological foundation for similar studies across the tree of life.

Methods

Plant materials

A. thaliana Col-0, ddm1-213, ddm1rdr2 and ddm1rdr6 were grown in long-days (16 h:8 h light:dark) at 23 °C. ddm1rdr2 and ddm1rdr6 plants were obtained by crossing naive heterozygous ddm1 plants (i.e., plants produced after repeated backcrossing of an initial ddm1 parent) with heterozygous rdr2 or rdr6, followed by selfing and selection of F2 double mutants.

To generate HSF2A overexpressing lines, the HSF2A (AT2G26150.1) CDS was cloned into the ALLIGATOR2 vector68 to generate the 35S::HA-HSFA2 construct. Col-0 and ddm1-2 (G4) plants were transformed via floral dip using Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. T2 seeds from three independent transgenic lines (carrying or not the transgene) were selected based on fluorescence and grown in half-strength MS medium (½ MS) under long-day conditions at 23 °C. Seven-day-old seedlings of 35S::HA-HSFA2 Col-0 plants and ddm1-2 (G6), and their corresponding transgene-free controls, were collected in bulk for RNA extraction.

To generate transcriptomic data from different tissues of methylation-deficient mutants, ddm1-2 (G4) plants were grown under long-day conditions until flowering. Flowers were dissected following the developmental stages69. The collected samples included flower buds prior to stage 3, flower buds at stages 5–7 (with sepals removed), pollen sacs at stages 9–11, pollen sacs at stage 13, carpels at stages 9–11, carpels at stage 13, and fertilized carpels at stage 15. Dissected material was immediately frozen in dry ice and kept at −80 until use.

To generate transcriptomic data from methylation-deficient mutants subjected to biotic and abiotic stresses, plants were grown in ½ MS under long-day conditions at 20 °C:18 °C. Heat shock treatments were performed by transferring 10-day-old seedlings to 6 °C under long-day conditions for 24 h, then to 37 °C for 24 h under long-day conditions. Flagellin treatments were performed by transferring 10-day-old seedlings to ½ MS liquid medium (0.1% Agar) containing 1 μM of flagellin 22 peptide (QRLSTGSRINSAKDDAAGLQIA) for 48 h. In both cases, plants were collected immediately after treatment and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

For total protein extraction, three plates containing ~1000 Col-0, ddm1rdr2, and ddm1rdr6 seedlings and four plates containing ~1000 ddm1-2 (G4) seedlings were grown in ½ MS medium under long-day conditions for 7 days until collection and flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen.

RNA extraction and expression analyses

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of rosette leaves using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant Kit (Macherey-Nagel). 1 µg of total RNA was used to construct poly-A cDNA libraries for sequencing (paired-end 150-nucleotide reads) by BGI Tech Solutions. In the case of the material collected from dissected ddm1-2 flowers, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Scientific).

Library preparation and Nanopore sequencing were performed as previously described70. Briefly, 10 ng of total RNA obtained from ddm1, ddm1rdr2 and ddm1rdr6 plants was amplified and converted into cDNA using SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA kit (Clontech). About 17 fmol of cDNA was used for library preparation using the PCR Barcoding kit (SQK-PBK004 kit, ONT) and cleaned up with 0.6× Agencourt Ampure XP beads. About 2 fmol of the purified product was amplified during 18 cycles, with a 17-min elongation step, to introduce barcodes. Samples were multiplexed in equimolar quantities to obtain 20 fmol of cDNA, and the rapid adapter ligation step was performed.

Multiplexed libraries were loaded according to the manufacturer’s instructions on an R9.4.1 flow cell (ONT) for plants grown under control conditions, or R10.4.1 (ONT) for plants subjected to either heat shock or flagellin treatment. Standard 72-h sequencing was performed on the MinION MkIB or Promethion P2 instrument. MinKNOW software was used for basecalling the raw sequencing data produced using versions 19.12.5 and 24.11.10 for the R9.4.1 and the R10.4.1 flow cells, respectively.

To evaluate the role of DNA methylation in HSFA2-mediated reactivation of ONSEN, total RNA was extracted using the Nucleospin RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel) from seedlings of three 35S::HSFA2 independent transgenic lines in ddm1 and wild-type background, as well as three independent non-transgenic lines derived from the same floral dip. 1 μg of RNA was used for RT using SuperScript II (Invitrogen). RT–qPCRs were performed in triplicate using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master mix (Roche) and run in a LightCycler 480 System (Roche). RT–qPCR expression levels for ONSEN and HSFA2 were obtained using specific primers (ATCOPIA78F TTCCGCTCCAAGCTTCTTGT, ATCOPIA78R TTAACGGTGCCAAGATCCCC, HSFA2F TGGAGGAAGAAACGGTGACG, and HSFA2R CCTCAGCTACAAGCACACCA) and normalized to PP2A housekeeping gene expression using the 2–∆∆Ct method and primers previously reported71 (PPA2F TATCGATGACGATTCTTCGTGCAG, PPA2R GCTTGGTCGACTATCGGAATGAGAG). Primers were designed against the ORF of the studied gene or the TE using Primer-Blast72 and synthesized by Eurofins.

Annotation of TE-transcripts isoforms

Transcript annotation was performed using the FLAIR pipeline17. Splicing junctions based on short-read sequencing data were extracted using ‘junctions_from_sam.py’ script and used to correct ONT long reads using ‘flair.py correct’ script. Transcript isoforms were then detected using the ‘flair.py collapse’ script, and transcripts supported by at least five long reads were retained. Exon-exon junctions were validated using publicly available short-read RNA-seq data. Transcript annotations containing at least one junction not supported by a minimum of 5 reads in total were considered to be low-confidence annotations and were not included in downstream analyses.

Transcripts were assigned to TAIR10 genes or TEs as follows. Transcripts sharing an exon-exon junction, displaying a reciprocal coverage of over 90%, or having over 75% of their length covered with a TAIR10 gene or TE, were assigned. Overlapping transcript isoforms (reciprocal coverage >90%) were bundled together to the same TAIR10 entry. Whenever a transcript could be assigned to two or more TAIR10 annotations, it was assigned to that with the highest overlap. Transcripts overlapping two or more genes in over 50% of the transcript length were annotated as fusions. Non-overlapping (reciprocal coverage below 50%) transcript annotations assigned to the same TAIR10 annotation were defined as independent TE-genes. Any remaining transcript was considered to be cryptic. To generate a new compound annotation, all cellular genes from TAIR10 were first used to build the backbone annotation. Then, TE-genes annotated as above in methylation-defective plants not subject to stress were included. New TE-genes annotated exclusively in plants subject to stress were then added, and finally all TAIR10-exclusive TE annotations were imported. To assign a strand to each annotation, the nucleotide content of soft-clipped bases from long-read alignments was calculated for the first 10, 15, 20, and 25 clipped bases. Reads with a downstream frequency of A or an upstream frequency of T over 50% on any of these windows were assigned to the forward and reverse strand, respectively, with all other reads being considered uninformative. A strand was assigned for each cellular or TE-gene only when all the informative reads linked to it displayed the same orientation.

Transcriptomic analysis

Read counts from all transcriptomic datasets were generated for all the features in the combined TAIR10+TE-gene annotation using featureCounts73, assigning fractional counts to multimapping and overlapping reads (-O -M --fraction). Cellular and TE-genes were considered as expressed and retained for further analyses if they satisfied both of the following criteria in at least one sample: (i) raw counts >10 reads and (ii) TPM > 0.5. Read counts were normalized by sequencing depth and transformed using the vst function of DESeq274, analyzing separately data produced from methylation-compromised mutants and wild-type plants. Batch effects for different sequencing experiments were controlled using the R package ComBat.

For coexpression network analysis, two independent signed weighted gene coexpression networks were built using WGCNA75, corresponding to wild-type or epigenetically compromised backgrounds described above. The β coefficients used were 10 and 17 for the wild-type and epigenetically compromised networks, respectively. Coexpression clusters were defined using the function blockwiseModules, and GO term enrichment for each cluster was performed using the R package clusterProfiler76. For visualization, the complete topological overlap matrix was pruned to keep only the top 5 and 10 edges (for mutant and wild-type networks, respectively) with the highest weight for each gene and plotted using Cytoscape77. For each cellular and TE-gene, the Jaccard similarity index was calculated based on the top 1000 most correlated genes in each of these networks.

DAP-seq data analysis

The detection of DAP-seq peaks, indicative of TF binding, was performed with GEM78, arguments --k_min 6 --kmax 20 --k_seqs 600 --k_neg_dinu_shuffle --t 5. TFBS within DAP-seq peaks were detected with FIMO79 using the corresponding TF motifs26 with a reciprocal coverage over 80%. Only DAP-seq peaks containing cognate TF binding motifs were included in further analyses. Relative distance between TFBS and the closest TSS was measured for each TE-gene and cellular gene from the center of the DAP-seq peak, considering only TFBS included within TE boundaries. To determine methylation sensitivity for the TFBS, all TFBS within annotated genes and 1000 bp surrounding them were analyzed. TFs binding naked and native (methylated) DNA were extracted from a previous study26. DNA methylation sensitivity of TF was calculated based on the differential occupancy of TFs on amplified DNA (i.e., no DNA methylation) vs native DNA (i.e., with endogenous DNA methylation) measured by DAP-seq. Significance of differences was assessed by performing independent Mann–Whitney U tests.

GATC motifs were defined as any of the six top-effect 6-mers previously identifed28 in either forward or reverse orientation, and their total abundance 500 bp downstream of promoters of both TE and cellular genes was estimated. Effect size was defined as the slope in a linear regression between GATC motif abundance and normalized RNA-seq read counts (vst).

Protein extraction

Total protein extraction was performed as described before31. Approximately 100 mg of 10-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder with a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The powder was suspended in 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid in acetone and precipitated overnight at –20 °C. Pellets were washed twice with ice-cold acetone, air-dried, and resuspended in urea digestion buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) for 1 h). Extracts were cleared by centrifugation (20,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay. Samples were then stored at −20 °C.

For proteolysis, 100 µg of proteins per sample were diluted fourfold with 50 mM triethyl-ammonium bicarbonate to reduce urea to ≤2 M, and sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) was added at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:20 (w/w). For extra high-depth DIA proteomics, protein digestion was performed using a mix of Trypsin and LysC to increase the digestion efficiency and diversity of the library. Digestion proceeded for 4 h at 37 °C with gentle shaking. Peptides were recovered by centrifugation through 10 kDa molecular-weight-cut-off ultrafiltration devices (12,000 × g, 15 min, 20 °C), rinsed once with 100 µL 0.5 M TEAB, and the combined filtrates were lyophilized.

DDA proteomics

DDA Quantitative Proteomics was performed by BGI Tech Solutions. Dried peptides were dissolved in mobile phase A (2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), clarified (20,000 × g, 10 min), and loaded onto a Thermo UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system. After trapping and desalting, peptides were separated on a self-packed C18 column (75 µm ID × 25 cm, 3 µm, 100 Å) at 300 nL/min using the following gradient of mobile phase B (98% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid): 0–5 min, 5% B; 5–45 min, 5% → 25% B; 45–50 min, 25% → 35% B; 50–52 min, 35% → 80% B; 52–54 min, 80% B; 54–60 min, 5% B for re-equilibration. The column outlet was coupled directly to the mass spectrometer via a nano-ESI source.

Peptides were ionized at +2 kV and analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos operating in DDA mode. Full-scan MS¹ spectra were acquired from m/z 350–1500 at 60,000 resolution (at m/z 200) with an automatic-gain-control target of 1 × 10⁵ charges. The most intense 30 precursors (charge 2+–6+, intensity >2 × 10⁴) were selected for HCD fragmentation (normalized collision energy 30%), and MS² spectra were recorded in the Orbitrap at 15,000 resolution with an AGC target of 2 × 10⁴. The MS² first mass was fixed at m/z 100, and dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s to avoid repeated sampling of the same ion.

DIA high-depth proteomics

DIA high-depth quantitative proteomics was performed by BGI Tech Solutions. Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid (FA) in water), clarified (20,000 × g, 10 min), and 1 µg was injected onto a Vanquish Neo UHPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) fitted with an EASY-Spray C18 column (150 µm × 15 cm, 2 µm, 100 Å). Peptides were eluted at 2.5 µL/min using an 8 min stepped gradient: 4–25% mobile phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% FA) over 0–4 min; 25–35% B over 4–5.8 min; 35–99% B over 5.8–6.2 min (flow increased to 3 µL/min); hold at 99% B for 0.7 min before re-equilibration. The column outlet was coupled directly to the mass spectrometer via the integrated EASY-Spray nano-ESI source (spray voltage 1.8 kV).

An additional high-depth DIA quantitative proteomics run was performed on proteins extracted from ddm1 plants, essentially as described above with the following modifications. Peptides were eluted at a varying flow rate starting on 2.5 µL/min using a 24 min stepped gradient instead of 8 min: 4–5% mobile phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% FA) over 0–0.5 min with flow rate decreased to 2 µL/min; 5–8.5% B over 0.5–0.9 min, flow rate 2–0.8 µL/min; 8.5–25% B over 0.9–14.2 min, flow rate 0.8 µL/min; 25–35% B over 14.2–21.1 min, flow rate 0.8 µL/min; 35–55% B over 21.1–21.9 min, flow rate 0.8–2 µL/min; 55–99% B over 21.5–21.9 min, flow rate 2–2.5 µL/min; hold at 99% B for 0.8 min before re-equilibration.

In both cases, DIA was performed on a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer using the following settings. Peptide ions were generated at +1.8 kV and full-scan MS1 spectra were recorded from m/z 380–980 at 240,000 resolution (at m/z 200) with an automatic-gain-control (AGC) target of 5 × 105 charges (500%) and a maximum injection time (MIT) of 5 ms. The 600 m/z range was divided into 300 sequential 2 m/z isolation windows. Each window was fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD, normalized collision energy 25%), and MS2 spectra were acquired in the Astral analyzer at 60,000 resolution with an AGC target of 5 × 105 and a 3 ms MIT.

Proteomic data analysis

Raw proteomic data was analyzed using DIA-NN 2.3.080, providing a custom protein database that included the TAIR10 proteome, proteins derived from our functional TE annotation, and all the peptides derived from 6-frame translation of transposable elements annotated in TAIR10 for which no evidence of transcriptions was found in our data (used for false positive rate estimation).

DIA-NN was run first in library generation mode and then in analysis mode, and the resulting dataset was filtered downstream; the specific running parameters and filtering thresholds can be found in Supplementary Data 6, and included a conservative q-value cutoff of 0.001 at the protein-group level. The set of high-confidence proteins was defined as those supported by a minimum of two independent peptides in the same sample. In the case of TE proteins. To ensure TE protein specificity, we removed any peptide sequence that matched both TE-encoded and non-TE (cellular) protein groups. This produced a set of high-confidence proteins per sample, with some proteins being high confidence in some samples but not in others. Only high-confidence proteins were used for downstream analysis and comparisons. TE protein detection and quantification were based exclusively on TE-specific peptides.

High-throughput sequence alignments

All short-read transcriptomic datasets analyzed in this work81–97(Supplementary Data 2), either generated here or downloaded from public repositories, were mapped against the TAIR10 genome using STAR98 with options --outFilterMatchNmin 30 --alignIntronMax 10,000 --alignSJoverhangMin 3, removing PCR duplicates using Picard.

Four Ribo-seq datasets generated from wild-type and ddm1 mutant plants were retrieved from Ref.30. Raw reads were mapped using STAR with options --outFilterMultimapNmax 50 --alignIntronMax 10,000 --outSAMprimaryFlag AllBestScore. The read depth over exons on each strand and the proportion of covered bases on exons were calculated using samtools depth.

DNA affinity purification and sequencing (DAP-seq) data obtained in Arabidopsis26 for 529 TFs were processed using a modified version of the bioinformatics pipeline implemented before. Specifically, reads mapping to repetitive sequences were also considered to detect binding over TEs. Reads were mapped on the TAIR10 genome using Bowtie2 v.2.3.299, and PCR duplicates were removed using Picard.

Long reads from ddm1, ddm1rdr2, and ddm1rdr6 plants, either grown under control conditions or subject to stress, were mapped on the Arabidopsis reference genome TAIR10 using minimap v2.11-r797100 with the following options -ax splice -G 30k -t 12.

Conserved domain prediction of TE-encoded proteins

For each annotated transcript isoform, all putative ORFs were detected using NCBI’s standalone ORFfinder v0.4.3, restricting the start codon exclusively to ATG and requiring the encoded proteins to be at least 50 residues long. The first and the longest ORFs were selected for each transcript for subsequent analyses. Conserved domains were predicted using rpsblast 2.13 with the parameters -seg no -comp_based_stats 1 -e-value 1e-5, querying the CDD Database 3.20101. Results were parsed using rpsbproc 0.5.

Structural domain prediction of TE-encoded proteins

Protein structures from all TE ORFs predicted in transcripts from mutant plants grown under control conditions were modeled using the local implementation of ColabFold 1.4102, selecting only the top-scoring model. Predicted structures for the Uniprot Reference proteome of Arabidopsis UP000006548 were downloaded from https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/download. In both TE protein structures and the Reference Arabidopsis Proteome, SDs were defined as groups of at least 50 subsequent amino acids with a maximum intra-group PAE of 10 Å.

TE-encoded SDs were compared using foldseek search103 (--cov-mode 0 --exhaustive-search 1 -e inf) and clustered applying an LDDT threshold of 90% and a reciprocal coverage threshold of 90%, effectively restricting clusters to domains with structural similarity along their complete structure. SDs were also aligned to RCSB, AlphaFold2-modeled Swissprot databases (downloaded using foldseek utilities), and the Uniprot proteome of Arabidopsis using foldseek search. To capture distant homologs, the e-value threshold was set to 0.01, and only hits spanning over 80% of the TE-encoded SD were retrieved to avoid the inclusion of false positives produced by short, spurious hits (--cov-mode 2 -c 0.8 -e 0.01). Each cluster was assigned a function based on the best-scoring structural hits and presence of conserved domains. LDDT-based distances were chosen as a computationally efficient103, confidence-aware proxy of TM-scores104 to organize the millions of models tested. Clusters without consistent hits were aligned using ChimeraX’s matchmaker to clusters with hits, and reassigned to the matching cluster when having an alignment over 66% of their length with an RMSD below 3 Å. Key structural homology assignments highlighted in the Results were validated using TM-scores computed with USalign.

For UMAP representation, all Arabidopsis SDs from either TEs or the Uniprot proteome were aligned using foldseek search (--exhaustive-search 1 -e inf) and the UMAP was performed over the matrix of structural distances, defined as 1-lDDT. Categorization into specific TE proteins was performed based on the structural functional assignment above. For each TE-encoded SD, only the 25 most similar cellular protein SDs were included on the UMAP representation.

EN/SPM proteins and aspartic protease phylogeny

All EN/SPM-derived proteins longer than 200 amino acids were aligned with maize TnpD and TnpA proteins (GeneBank accessions AAG17043 and AAG17044, respectively) using the MAFFT 7.508105 E-INS-i algorithm. A maximum likelihood tree was built using FastTree 2.1.11106, and each protein was identified as either TnpA or TnpD according to their phylogenetic distance to the maize proteins. Short or truncated isoforms encoding these genes were classified as TnpA- or TnpD- encoding by aligning their protein products with the products of other members of the same EN/SPM family but were excluded from the phylogenetic tree.

For the aspartic protease phylogeny, all TE SDs with structural homology to aspartic proteases and all TAIR10 SDs formed an isolated cluster neighboring that of TE proteases. SD sequences, together with human and yeast DDI genes (UniProt accessions Q8WTU0 and P40087, respectively), were aligned and a phylogenetic tree was built as described above.

Structure-based multimerization and DNA-binding potential prediction

Protein dimers and higher-order multimers were modeled using AlphaFold-Multimer45 as implemented in ColabFold 1.4102, and run on a server with two NVLink interconnect RTXA6000. Given the computational cost of structural prediction on large proteins and protein complexes102, a representative set of 16 TE-encoded proteins was selected for this analysis. Dimerization potential was first determined based on interatomic distance and structural prediction quality. Specifically, residues with inter-chain pLDDT > 33 and interatomic distance between β-carbons (α-carbon in the case of glycine) <8 Å were considered as interacting. Protein pairs were considered to interact if they shared at least 10 close contact residues. Then the mean and the standard deviation of ipTM scores across models were used to estimate interaction potential. For multimers with a total length over 5000 amino acids, only three models and five models were run.

AlphaFold2-Multimer screens

Interaction potential between the representative set of 16 TE-encoded proteins and cellular proteins was performed using AlphaFold2-Multimer45. Specifically, proteins linked to primary and secondary metabolisms, located in chloroplasts, mitochondria, membranes, or extracellular compartments were excluded. In addition, proteins with GO terms associated with the cell cycle, DNA damage repair and binding, chromatin organization, and epigenetic regulation were retained. Structures of the 4164 cellular gene proteins fulfilling these criteria vs. the 16 TE-encoded proteins (66,624 pairs) were predicted using 3 models and a single recycle per model.

A set of interacting Arabidopsis protein pairs was retrieved from the Arabidopsis Interactome 2.0 (available at the TAIR website), selecting those with direct interaction evidence supported by yeast two-hybrid assays, resulting in 1596 protein pairs in the interacting set. To generate a set of random protein pairs comparable with the interacting set, 1596 protein pairs were randomly sampled from the pool of proteins forming the interacting set, excluding pairs already present in the interacting set.

Potentially interacting pairs were defined using two independent metrics: the interface PTM (iPTM) provided directly from AlphaFold2-Multimer, and the interaction prediction Score from Aligned Errors (ipSAE)56. The thresholds to consider robust interactors were selected based on our true and random sets to have a specificity above 95% and a sensitivity of at least 50%, and equal 0.42 for iPTM and 0.1 for ipSAE. Interacting residues were calculated as described above.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

The Spm3-TnpA open reading frame was synthesized (Twist Bioscience), cloned into the pTwist ENTR backbone, and subsequently transferred into the pDEST-AD-X and pDEST-BD-X destination vectors107 via LR recombination to generate GAL4 activation domain (AD) and DNA-binding domain (BD) fusions. The resulting constructs were co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain PJ69-4A. Co-transformants were selected on synthetic medium -Leu-Trp (–LW) plates, and protein–protein interactions were assessed based on growth on synthetic medium -Leu-Trp-His (–LWH) plates.

Prediction of DNA-binding potential and protein rugosity

To identify putative DNA-binding pockets, positively charged protein surfaces were identified using the APBS algorithm implemented in ChimeraX and manually annotated along the amino acid protein sequence. DNA-binding residues were confirmed using GraphSite53. Protein volume, area, and solvent-accessible area were calculated using the ChimeraX command “measure”. Independent Mann–Whitney U tests were used to test the significant differences between core and accessory proteins.

Calculation of dN/dS

TE protein sequences from each superfamily were grouped according to their function and aligned using the MAFFT E-INS-i algorithm. Protein alignments were converted into codon alignments by combining them with their corresponding cDNA using the EMBL tool pal2nal108. dN and dS were calculated using the NG86 model109 between all protein pairs with at least 10 aligned codons. Statistical tests were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
