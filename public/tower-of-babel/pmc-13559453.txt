Risk-stratified simplification of intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy for pleural infection
Authors: Woon Hean Keenan Chong, Dipika Agrawal
Published: 2026

We thank Xiang et al. for their thoughtful comments on our systematic review and meta-analysis comparing concurrent versus sequential intrapleural administration of fibrinolytic therapy and DNase for pleural infection (1,2). We agree that the central question should not be whether intrapleural therapy can be simplified for all patients with pleural infection, but rather how simplification can be applied safely, pragmatically, and selectively across heterogeneous clinical phenotypes. Our meta-analysis found no statistically significant difference between concurrent and sequential administration in treatment success, mortality, need for surgery, hospital length of stay, chest tube duration, pleural drainage volume, pain, or bleeding requiring transfusion. However, we emphasized that these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the evidence base remains limited, observational, and heterogeneous. The pooled estimates should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of universal equivalence.

We agree with Xiang et al. that pleural infection is not a single uniform disease state. Patients differ substantially in baseline clinical severity, radiographic complexity, timing of intervention, microbiology, comorbidity burden, anticoagulant or antiplatelet exposure, renal function, frailty, and surgical fitness. These factors may influence both the likelihood of response to intrapleural therapy and the risk of complications. Therefore, a simplified concurrent approach may be reasonable in selected patients in whom the expected benefit of reduced procedural complexity, fewer drain accesses, improved workflow, and earlier administration outweighs the theoretical pharmacological or bleeding concerns. However, patients with extensive loculation, high bleeding risk, delayed presentation, severe systemic illness, or a high likelihood of requiring surgical escalation may require individualized decision-making rather than protocol-driven simplification.

The issue of bleeding risk deserves particular attention. In our review, pooled bleeding events requiring transfusion did not differ significantly between concurrent and sequential regimens. However, heterogeneity was high, and anticoagulant use, antiplatelet exposure, coagulation parameters, malignancy, and renal impairment,—factors associated with greater bleeding risk—as well as the RAPID (renal, age, purulence, infection source, and dietary factors) score, were under-reported across studies (3). This limits the ability to determine whether certain high-risk subgroups may be more vulnerable to bleeding with one administration strategy over another. We therefore agree that future studies should prospectively capture bleeding risk factors and apply standardized definitions of clinically significant hemorrhage.

We also agree that radiographic disease burden and severity scores should be incorporated more systematically. The RAPID score has prognostic value in pleural infection and may provide a useful framework for risk stratification (4). Similarly, thoracic ultrasound and computed tomography findings, including septation or loculation, pleural contrast enhancement, residual pleural collection after drainage, and drain position, may help characterize disease complexity, guide drainage adequacy, and inform risk stratification or escalation decisions, although their ability to predict response to intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy specifically remains insufficiently defined (5). Unfortunately, these variables were under-reported in the available comparative studies, which precluded meaningful subgroup or meta-regression analyses in our review.

An important next step is therefore not simply to conduct larger trials comparing concurrent and sequential regimens, but to design studies that integrate risk stratification from the outset. A future pragmatic non-inferiority trial could evaluate concurrent administration against sequential therapy while prespecifying subgroup analyses by RAPID score, bleeding risk, radiographic complexity, timing from chest tube insertion to therapy initiation, and dose strategy. Such a design would better determine whether concurrent therapy is broadly non-inferior or preferentially suitable for defined lower-risk or operationally appropriate populations. We also wish to emphasize that simplification should not be interpreted as de-escalation of clinical vigilance. Intrapleural therapy remains part of a broader pleural infection pathway that includes timely antibiotics, adequate image-guided drainage, early reassessment, surgical consultation when appropriate, and multidisciplinary decision-making. Concurrent administration may simplify drug delivery, but it should not replace careful patient selection, monitoring, and escalation planning.

In summary, we concur with Xiang et al. that the future of intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy should move toward risk-stratified implementation. The current evidence suggests that concurrent administration may be a practical alternative to sequential therapy in selected settings, but it should not yet be regarded as universally applicable to all patients with pleural infection. Further prospective studies should determine not only whether simplified administration is safe and effective, but also which patient cohorts are most likely to benefit from it.
