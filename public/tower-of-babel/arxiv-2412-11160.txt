1.
Introduction

As a powerful tool and method, random walks have found broad applications in various aspects. Frequently cited examples include image segmentation
(
Grady, 2006
)
, randomized algorithm design
(
Sarwate and Dimakis, 2012
)
, collaborative recommendation
(
Fouss et al., 2007
)
, community detection
(
Lambiotte et al., 2014
)
, among others. A fundamental quantity related to random walks is hitting time
(
Lovász, 1993
;
Sheng and Zhang, 2019
)
, also called first-passage time
(
Condamin et al., 2007
)
. For a random walk on a graph, the hitting time
H_{ij}
from a vertex
i
to another vertex
j
is the expected time for the walker starting from
i
to visit
j
for the first time. Hitting time is related to many problems and has been successfully applied to diverse areas, such as Hanoi problem with random move
(
Wu et al., 2011
;
Qi et al., 2020
)
, query suggestion
(
Mei et al., 2008
)
, and clustering algorithm
(
Chen et al., 2008
)
.

Except for the intrinsic interest of hitting time itself and its direct applications, many other relevant quantities related to random walks are encoded in or expressed in terms of this crucial quantity, for example, absorbing random-walk centrality
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
(or Markov centrality
(
White and Smyth, 2003
)
), Kemeny constant
(
Hunter, 2014
)
, cover time
(
Bloznelis et al., 2022
;
Xu and Zhang, 2023
)
, and random detour time
(
Boley et al., 2011
;
Ranjan and Zhang, 2013
)
. As the name implies, the absorbing random-walk centrality is a measure for the importance of vertices on a graph. For a vertex
j
, its absorbing random-walk centrality
H_{j}
is defined by
H_{j}=\sum_{i}\rho(i)H_{ij}
, where
\rho(\cdot)
is the starting probability distribution over all vertices. In the context of economic networks, each sector is represented by a vertex, while the flow of economic activity between sectors is modeled as weighted edges. To identify key sectors in a national economy, Blöchl
et al.

(
Blöchl et al., 2011
)
selects the vertex with minimum absorbing random-walk centrality. This approach leverages absorbing random-walk centrality to reveal significant structural aspects of diverse national economies, which is a crucial task in data mining. Different from the shortest-path based centrality measures, random-walk based centrality metrics include the contributions from essentially all paths
(
Newman, 2005
)
, and thus have a better discriminating power.

For random walks on a graph with
n
vertices, the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
is defined as the expected time for the walker starting from one vertex to second vertex selected randomly from the graph according to the stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}=\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\cdots,\pi_{n}\right)^{\top}
of the random walk, that is,
\mathcal{K}=\sum_{j}\pi_{j}H_{ij}
. The Kemeny constant has also found a wealth of applications in different fields
(
Xia and Zhang, 2024a
)
. It can be utilized to gauge the efficiency of user navigation through the World Wide Web (WWW)
(
Levene and Loizou, 2002
)
. Moreover, the Kemeny constant is related to the mixing rate of an irreducible Markov chain
(
Levin et al., 2009
)
, by regarding it as the expected time to mixing of the Markov chain
(
Hunter, 2006
)
. Recently, the Kemeny constant has been applied to measure the efficiency of robotic surveillance in network environments
(
Patel et al., 2015
)
and to characterize the noise robustness of a class of protocols for formation control
(
Jadbabaie and Olshevsky, 2019
)
.

Motivations
.
Despite the wide range of applications of the absorbing random-walk centrality and the Kemeny constant, it is a computational challenge to obtain their exact values. By definition, both the absorbing random-walk centrality and the Kemeny constant are a partial average of some hitting times.
However, the exact value of hitting time between any pair of vertices in a graph involves all eigenvalues and eigenvectors of (normalized) Laplacian matrix associated with the graph
(
Lovász, 1993
;
Lin and Zhang, 2013
)
, the time complexity of which is cubic on the vertex number. Thus, for large realistic networks with millions of vertices, we cannot obtain their absorbing random-walk centrality and the Kemeny constant by resorting this straightforward method for computing hitting time. It is then of theoretical and practical interest to seek for alternative approximate approaches that scale to large networks.

Moreover, in various practical applications, many problems cannot be reduced to determining the importance of individual nodes, but are essentially to find a group of
k
nodes that is the most important among all node groups, each containing exactly
k
nodes. For example, how to place resources on a fixed number of
k
peers in P2P networks, so that they are easily accessed by others
(
Gkantsidis et al., 2006
)
. Again for instance, in the field of wireless networks, sensor placement involves selecting an optimal subset of vertices to place sensors to sample physical signals
(
Krause et al., 2008
;
Ranieri et al., 2014
)
, such as radiation or temperature. Finally, in the context of point cloud sampling
(
Dinesh et al., 2020
;
Chen et al., 2017
)
, it requires selecting a representative subset of points to preserve the geometric features of reconstruction. These problems reveal an inherent connection between crucial node identification and data mining.

To solve the problem of identifying crucial nodes, the hitting time of node groups is utilized in many previous studies. For instance, the group hitting time can be regarded as the transmission cost of sensor groups in a wireless sensor network
(
Mo et al., 2020
)
. Under the capacity constraint
k
, the minimization of transmission cost is transformed into minimizing the group hitting time. In addition, the group hitting time can be interpreted as the purchase probability of items in a recommendation system
(
Yin et al., 2012
;
Johnson and Ng, 2017
)
, and the recommendation problem naturally leads to the minimization of group hitting time. Recently, the absorbing random-walk centrality for an individual vertex
(
White and Smyth, 2003
)
has been extended to a group of vertices
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
. Moreover, the problem of choosing
k
most important vertices was proposed, and an approximated algorithm was developed to solve the problem in cubic running time, which is not applicable to large networks. Thus, it is of great significance to propose a hitting time based centrality for vertex groups and design fast algorithm to find the most important
k
vertices. For instance, this fast algorithm can be analogously applied in
(
Blöchl et al., 2011
)
to identify crucial sector groups in a national economy, potentially informing policy decisions and economic analyses.

Contributions
.
In a preliminary version
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
of this paper, we studied the absorbing random-walk centrality

for an individual vertex and Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
. We focused on a special absorbing random-walk centrality
H_{j}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}H_{ij}
, where the starting probability distribution
\rho(\cdot)
is the stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\cdots,\pi_{n})^{\top}
of the random walk, and we call
H_{j}
walk centrality. The walk centrality

has received considerable attention
(
Tejedor et al., 2009
;
Beveridge, 2009
;
Beveridge, 2016
)
. We first expressed

in terms of

for all vertices, and further expressed
H_{j}
and
\mathcal{K}
in terms of quadratic forms of pseudoinverse of the Laplacian matrix. We then proposed a fast algorithm

to compute approximate

for all vertices in
V
and
\mathcal{K}
for the whole graph in nearly linear time on the number of edges, based on the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma
(
Achlioptas, 2001
)
and the Laplacian solver
(
Spielman and Teng, 2004
;
Spielman, 2010
;
Koutis et al., 2011
;
Livne and Brandt, 2012
;
Cohen et al., 2014
;
Kyng and Sachdeva, 2016
)
. Finally, we experimentally demonstrated that
ApproxHK
is accurate and is significantly faster than the direct exact computation of related quantities according to their definitions. The results from this preliminary version are primarily presented in Sections
3
,
4
, and
8.2
. The key contributions of this preliminary version are summarized as follows.

•

We express the absorbing random-walk centrality
H_{j}
and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
in terms of quadratic forms of pseudoinverse of the Laplacian matrix.

•

We propose an approximation algorithm called
ApproxHK
to compute
H_{j}
and
\mathcal{K}
in nearly linear time on the number of graph edges.

•

Numerical experiments reveal the accuracy and efficiency of
ApproxHK
.

In the preliminary work
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
, we focus solely on the mean hitting time for individual vertices. Our current study extends the walk centrality
H_{j}
of individual vertices by proposing group walk centrality (GWC) for vertex groups. In a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
with
n=|V|
vertices and
m=|E|
edges, the GWC
H(S)
of a group
S\subseteq V
is defined as
H(S)=\sum_{i\in V\setminus S}\pi_{i}H_{iS}
, where
H_{iS}
is the expected time taken by a random walker starting from vertex
i
to visit any vertex in
S
for the first time. We establish a connection between
H(S)
and two other quantities related to hitting times. We show that
H(\cdot)
is a monotonic and supermodular set function. We then consider an NP-hard optimization problem MinGWC: how to find an optimal set
S^{*}
of
k\ll n
vertices such that
H(S^{*})
is minimized. We devise two greedy algorithms,
DeterMinGWC
and
ApproxMinGWC
to approximately solve this problem. The former has a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e})
approximation factor and
O(n^{3})
running time, while the latter has a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e}-\epsilon)
approximation factor for any error parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
\widetilde{O}(km\epsilon^{-2})
running time, where
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
notation hides the
{\rm poly}\log
factors. Finally, extensive experiment results demonstrate that both
DeterMinGWC
and
ApproxMinGWC
are accurate, while
ApproxMinGWC
is more efficient and is scalable to massive networks with millions of vertices. The results from our current presentation are detailed predominantly in Sections
5
,
6
,
7
, and
8.3
. The key contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We propose a vertex group centrality called Group Walk Centrality (GWC) based on the group hitting time.

•

We introduce the GWC minimization problem, proving its NP-hardness.

•

Due to the monotonicity and supermodularity of GWC, we develop an efficient greedy algorithm called
ApproxMinGWC
based on
ApproxHK
to approximately solve the GWC minimization problem.

•

Extensive experiment results demonstrate that both
ApproxHK
and
ApproxMinGWC
are accurate and scalable, which can be applied to networks with millions of vertices.

Preliminaries

In this section, we give a brief introduction to some notations, as well as some basic concepts about graphs, such as Laplacian matrix, resistance distance, random walks, hitting times, and some quantities derived from hitting times.

Notations

We use
\mathbb{R}
to denote the set of real number, and we use normal lowercase letters such as
a,b,c
to represent scalars in
\mathbb{R}
. We denote vectors with bold lowercase letters such as
\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}

\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}
. For the convenience of representing specific elements in vectors and matrices, we use
a_{i}
to represent the
{i}^{\rm{th}}
element of vector
\bm{a}
, and use
\bm{A}_{[i,j]}
to represent the entry at position
(i,j)
in matrix
\bm{A}
. We use
\bm{A}_{[i,:]}
and
\bm{A}_{[:,j]}
to denote, respectively, the
{i}^{\rm{th}}
row and
{j}^{\rm{th}}
column of matrix
\bm{A}
. We write sets in subscripts to denote subvectors and submatrices. For example,
\bm{A}_{[S,j]}
represents the submatrix of
\bm{A}
, which includes those matrix elements with row indices in
S
and column index as
j
. Similarly,
\bm{a}_{-S}
represents the subvector of
\bm{a}
obtained from
\bm{a}
by removing elements with indices in set
S
, and
\bm{A}_{-S}
represents the submatrix of
\bm{A}
obtained from
\bm{a}
by removing elements with row indices and column indices in set
S
. Note that the subscript takes precedence over the superscript, thus
\bm{A}_{-S}^{-1}
denotes the inverse of
\bm{A}_{-S}
rather than the submatrix of
\bm{A}^{-1}
. We use
\bm{1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to denote a vector of
n
dimensions with all elements being
1
. Sometimes we skip subscripts if there is no ambiguity. For a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, its Frobenius norm is
\left\|\bm{A}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}\right)}
.

Definition 2.1 (
\epsilon
-approximation).

Let
x
and
\tilde{x}
be positive scalars, with

as the error parameter such that
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We refer to

.

2.2.
Graph and Laplacian Matrix

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
denote a connected undirected weighted graph or network, where
V
is the set of vertices,
E
is the set of edges, and
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the positive edge weight function, with
w_{e}
being the weight for edge
e
. Then, there are total
n=\left|V\right|
vertices and
m=|E|
edges in graph
\mathcal{G}
. Let
w_{\max}
and
w_{\min}
denote the maximum edge weight and minimum edge weight, respectively. Namely,
w_{\max}=\max_{e\in E}w_{e}
and
w_{\min}=\min_{e\in E}w_{e}
.

Mathematically, the topological and weighted properties of a graph
\mathcal{G}
are encoded in its generalized adjacency matrix
\bm{A}
with the entry
a_{ij}
denoting the adjacency relation between vertices
i
and
j
. If vertices
i
and
j
are linked to each other by an edge, then we denote this edge as
e=\left(i,j\right)
, with
a_{ij}=a_{ji}=w_{e}>0
. If vertices
i
and
j
are not adjacent,
a_{ij}=a_{ji}=0
. In a weighted graph
\mathcal{G}
, the degree
d_{i}
of a vertex
i
is defined by
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}

(
Barrat et al., 2004
)
, where we denote the maximal vertex degree as
d_{\max}=\max\left\{d_{i}|i\in V\right\}
.
The diagonal degree vector of graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined to be
\bm{d}=\left(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{n}\right)^{\top}
, while the diagonal degree matrix of graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined to be
{\bm{D}}={\rm diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
, and the Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
is
{\bm{L}}={\bm{D}}-{\bm{A}}
. Thus,
\bm{L}
is a symmetric diagonally dominant (SDD) matrix. Moreover, for
any nonempty proper subset
S
of vertex set
V
,
\bm{L}_{-S}
is a symmetric diagonally dominant M-matrix (SDDM).

Let
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|\times\left|V\right|}
be the incidence matrix of graph
\mathcal{G}
. For each edge
e
with two end vertices
i
and
j
, a direction is assigned arbitrarily. Let
\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}
be the row of matrix
\bm{B}
associated with edge
e
. Then the element
b_{eu}
at row corresponding to edge
e
and column corresponding to vertex
u
is defined as follows:
b_{eu}=1
if vertex
u
is the tail of edge
e
,
b_{eu}=-1
if vertex
u
is the head of edge
e
, and
b_{eu}=0
otherwise. Let
\bm{e}_{u}
be the
u
-th canonical basis of the space
\mathbb{R}^{\left|V\right|}
, then for an edge
e
connecting two vertices
i
and
j
,
\bm{b}_{e}
can also be recast as
\bm{b}_{e}=\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j}
. Let
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|\times|E|}
be a diagonal matrix with every diagonal entry corresponding to the weight
w_{e}
of an edge
e\in E
. Then the Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
of graph
\mathcal{G}
can be written as
\bm{L}=\bm{B}^{T}\bm{W}\bm{B}=\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}
.

The Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. All its eigenvalues are non-negative, with a unique zero eigenvalue. Let
0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}
be the
n
eigenvalues of
\bm{L}
, and let
u_{i}
,
i={1,2,\dots,n}
, be their corresponding mutually orthogonal unit eigenvectors. Then,
\bm{L}
has the following spectral decomposition:
\bm{L}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}
. It is easy to verify that
\lambda_{n}\leq nw_{\max}

(
Li and Schild, 2018
)
.
Since
\bm{L}
is not invertible, we use
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
to denote its pseudoinverse, which can be written as
\bm{L}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}
. Let
\bm{J}
denote the matrix with all entries being ones. Then the pseudoinverse
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
can also be recast as
\left(\bm{L}+\frac{1}{n}\bm{J}\right)^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{J}

(
Ghosh et al., 2008
)
. Note that for a general symmetric matrix, it shares the same null space as its Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
(
Ben-Israel and Greville, 1974
)
.
Since the null space of null of
{\bm{L}}
is
\bm{1}
, it turns out that
{\bm{L}}\bm{1}={\bm{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Note that for an invertible matrix, its pseudoinverse is exactly its inverse.

2.3.
Electrical Network and Resistance Distance

For an arbitrary graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
, we can define its corresponding electrical network
\bar{\mathcal{G}}=(V,E,r)
, which is obtained from
\mathcal{G}
by considering edges as resistors and considering vertices as junctions between resistors
(
Doyle and Snell, 1984
)
. The resistor of an associated edge
e
is
r_{e}=w_{e}^{-1}
. For graph
\mathcal{G}
, the resistance distance
R_{ij}
between two vertices
i
and
j
is defined as the effective resistance between
i
and
j
in the corresponding electrical network
\bar{\mathcal{G}}

(
Klein and Randić, 1993
)
, which is equal to the potential difference between
i
and
j
when a unit current enters one vertex and leaves the other one.

For graph
\mathcal{G}
, the resistance distance
R_{ij}
between two vertices
i
and
j
can be expressed in terms of the elements of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
as
(
Klein and Randić, 1993
)
:

(1)

R_{ij}={\bm{L}}_{[i,i]}^{\dagger}+{\bm{L}}_{[j,j]}^{\dagger}-2{\bm{L}}_{[i,j]}^{\dagger}.

Define
\bm{R}
as the
n\times n
resistance matrix of graph
\mathcal{G}
, whose entry
R_{[i,j]}
at row
i
and column
j
represents the resistance distance
R_{ij}
between vertices
i
and
j
.

Lemma 2.2.

(
Tetali, 1991
)

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a simple connected graph with
n
vertices. Then the sum of weight times resistance distance over all pairs of adjacent vertices in
\mathcal{G}
satisfies

\sum_{\left(i,j\right)\in E}w_{ij}R_{ij}=n-1.

Similarly, We can also define the resistance distance
R_{iS}
between a vertex
i
and a group
S
of vertices. In the electrical network
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
corresponding to graph
\mathcal{G}
, if all the vertices in group
S
are grounded, the voltage of all vertices in set
S
is always zero.
Then the resistance distance
R_{iS}
between vertex
i
and vertex group
S
is defined as the voltage of
i
when a unit current enters
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
at vertex
i
and leaves
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
at vertices in
S
. Let
\mathscr{P}_{iS}
denote a simple path connecting vertex
i
and an arbitrary vertex in
S
. Then, the following relation holds:

(2)

R_{iS}\leq\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathscr{P}_{iS}}w_{ab}^{-1}\,.

2.4.
Random Walk on a Graph

For a connected weighted graph
\mathcal{G}
with
n
vertices, the classical random walk on
\mathcal{G}
can be described by the transition matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. At any time step, the walker located at vertex
i
moves to vertex
j
with probability
{\bm{P}_{[i,j]}=d_{i}^{-1}\bm{A}_{[i,j]}}
.
It is easy to verify that

(3)

\bm{P}=\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}.

If
\mathcal{G}
is finite and non-bipartite, the random walk has a unique stationary distribution
(
Lin and Zhang, 2013
)

(4)

\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\cdots,\pi_{n})^{\top}=\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d},\frac{d_{2}}{d},\cdots,\frac{d_{n}}{d}\right)^{\top},

where
d
is the sum of degrees over all vertices, namely
d=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}
.

A fundamental quantity for random walks is hitting time
(
Lovász, 1993
;
Condamin et al., 2007
)
. The hitting time
H_{ij}
from vertex
i
to vertex
j
, is the expected number of jumps for a walker starting from
i
to visit
j
for the first time.
In other words, if we denote the time steps for a walker starting from
i
to first reach
j
as the random variable
T_{ij}
, then we have
H_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{ij}\right]
.
There is an intimate relationship between hitting time and resistance distance
(
Tetali, 1991
)
.

Lemma 2.3.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a connected weighted graph with resistance matrix
\bm{R}
. Let
H_{ij}
be the hitting time from vertex
i
to vertex
j
. Then,

(5)

H_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}(R_{ij}+R_{jz}-R_{iz}).

Moreover, hitting time is also related to the fundamental matrix under the non-absorbing random walk model.

Lemma 2.4 (
(
Boley et al., 2011
)
).

For the non-absorbing random walk model, let
\bm{F}^{*}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\bm{1}\bm{\pi}^{\top})^{-1}\bm{\Pi}^{-1}
denote the fundamental matrix, where
\bm{\Pi}
is defined as
\textrm{diag}(\bm{\pi})
. Then, the hitting time
H_{ij}
can be represented in terms of the elements of matrix
\bm{F}^{*}
as

H_{ij}=\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}-\bm{F}^{*}_{[i,j]}.

A lot of interesting quantities can be derived from hitting times. Here we only consider three quantities, the absorbing random-walk centrality
(
White and Smyth, 2003
;
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
, the Kemeny constant
(
Xia and Zhang, 2024b
)
, and the random detour time
(
Boley et al., 2011
;
Ranjan and Zhang, 2013
)
.

For a vertex
j
in graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
, its absorbing random-walk centrality
H_{j}
is defined as
H_{j}=\sum_{i}\rho(i)H_{ij}
, where
\rho(\cdot)
is the starting probability distribution over all vertices in
V
. By definition,
H_{j}
is a weighted average of hitting times to vertex
j
. The smaller the value of
H_{j}
, the more important the vertex
j
. The random-walk based centrality has an obvious advantage over those shortest-path based centrality measures
(
Newman, 2005
)
. In
(
White and Smyth, 2003
;
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
, the uniform distribution for
\rho(\cdot)
is considered for the starting vertices. In this paper, we concentrate on a natural choice of
\rho(\cdot)
by selecting the starting vertex from the stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
. In this case,
H_{j}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}H_{ij}
, which has been much studied
(
Tejedor et al., 2009
;
Beveridge, 2009
;
Beveridge, 2016
)
. In the following text, we call
H_{j}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}H_{ij}

walk centrality
for short.

Another quantity we are concerned with is the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
. For a graph
\mathcal{G}
, its Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
is defined as the expected steps for a walker starting from vertex
i
to vertex
j
selected randomly from the vertex set
V
, according to the stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
. That is,
\mathcal{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}H_{ij}
. The Kemeny constant has been used to measure the user navigation efficiency through the WWW
(
Levene and Loizou, 2002
)
and robotic surveillance efficiency in network environments
(
Patel et al., 2015
)
. It can also measure the mixing rate of random walks
(
Levin et al., 2009
)
.

Most quantities for random walks on graph
\mathcal{G}
are determined by the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the normalized Laplacian matrix
(
Chung, 1997
)
,
{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{L}{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, of
\mathcal{G}
, including the walk centrality and Kemeny constant. By definition,
{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{L}{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is a real, symmetric, semi-definitive matrix. Let
0=\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}\leq\sigma_{3}\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{n}
be the
n
eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian matrix
{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{L}{\bm{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. And let
\bm{\psi}_{1}
,
\bm{\psi}_{2}
,
\bm{\psi}_{3}
,
\ldots
,
\bm{\psi}_{n}
be their corresponding mutually orthogonal eigenvectors of unit length, where
\bm{\psi}_{i}=(\psi_{i1},\psi_{i2},\ldots,\psi_{in})^{\top}
. Then
(
Lovász, 1993
;
Beveridge, 2016
)
,

(6)

H_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}H_{ij}=\frac{d}{d_{j}}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\psi_{kj}^{2}

and

(7)

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\,H_{ij}=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\,.

Equations (
6
) and (
7
) provide exact computation for the walk centrality and Kemeny constant, respectively. However, both formulas are expressed in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the normalized Laplacian, the computation complexity for which scale as
O(n^{3})
. Thus, direct computation for
H_{j}
and
\mathcal{K}
using spectral method appears to be prohibitive for large networks, and is infeasible to those realistic networks with millions of vertices.

3.
Connections between Walk Centrality and Kemeny Constant

Although both the walk centrality
H_{j}
and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
have attracted much attention from the scientific community, the relation between them for a general graph
\mathcal{G}
is still lacking.
In this section, we establish an explicit relation between the walk centrality
H_{j}
and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
.
We then express both quantities in terms of quadratic forms of the pseudoinverse
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
of graph Laplacian
\bm{L}
, which is helpful to address their computational challenges.

First, we show that the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
can be expressed in a linear combination of the walk centrality
H_{j}
for all vertices in
\mathcal{G}
, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted graph. Then, its Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
and walk centrality
H_{j}
obey the following relation:

(8)

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}H_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{d_{j}}{d}H_{j}.

Proof.

From (
7
), the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
is independent of the starting vertex
i
. Define
\mathcal{K}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\,H_{ij}
. Then
\mathcal{K}_{i}=\mathcal{K}_{j}
holds for any pair of vertices
i
and
j
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\,H_{ij}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\,H_{ij}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{d_{j}}{d}H_{j},

which establishes the lemma.
∎

After obtaining the relation governing the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
and walk centrality
H_{j}
, we continue to express them in terms of quadratic forms of matrix
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
.

Lemma 3.2.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted graph with Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
. Then, the walk centrality
H_{j}
and Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
can be represented in terms of quadratic forms of the pseudoinverse
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
of matrix
\bm{L}
as:

(9)

H_{j}=d(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi})

and

(10)

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi}).

Proof.

We first prove (
9
). Inserting (
5
) and (
1
) into (
6
) leads to

(11)

H_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}(R_{ij}+R_{jz}-R_{iz})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}\left({\bm{L}}_{[j,j]}^{\dagger}-{\bm{L}}_{[i,j]}^{\dagger}-{\bm{L}}_{[j,z]}^{\dagger}+{\bm{L}}_{[i,z]}^{\dagger}\right)

The four terms in the brackets of (
11
) can be sequentially calculated as follows:

(12)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}{\bm{L}}_{[j,j]}^{\dagger}=d^{2}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{e}_{j}\,,

(13)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}{\bm{L}}_{[i,j]}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}{\bm{L}}_{[j,z]}^{\dagger}=d^{2}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\pi}\,,

and

(14)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sum_{z=1}^{n}d_{z}{\bm{L}}_{[i,z]}^{\dagger}=d^{2}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\pi}\,.

Plugging (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
) into (
11
), we obtain

(15)

H_{j}=d(\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{e}_{j}-2\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\pi}+\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\pi})=d(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{\pi}).

Substituting (
15
) into (
8
) gives (
10
).
∎

4.
Fast Approximation Algorithm for Walk Centrality and Kemeny Constant

In the preceding section, we reduce the problem of computing
H_{j}
and
\mathcal{K}
to evaluating the quadratic forms
(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})
,
u=1,2,\ldots,n
, of matrix
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
.
However, this involves computing the pseudoinverse of
\bm{L}
, the straightforward computation for which still has a complexity of
O(n^{3})
, making it infeasible to huge networks. Here, we present an algorithm to compute an approximation of
H_{u}
for all
u\in V
and
\mathcal{K}
in nearly linear time with respect to the number of edges, which has a strict theoretical guarantee with high probability.

Let
C(u)=(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})
, which can be written in an Euclidian norm as

(16)

\begin{split}C(u)&=(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})=(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\\
&=(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})=\|\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|^{2}.\end{split}

This in fact equals the square of the distance between vectors
\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{e}_{u}
and
\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{\pi}
, which can be evaluated by the Johnson-Lindenstraus
Lemma (JL Lemma)
(
Achlioptas, 2001
)
.

Lemma 4.1 (JL Lemma
(
Achlioptas, 2001
)
).

Given
n
fixed vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\epsilon>0
, let

\bm{Q}_{k\times d}
,
k\geq 24\log n/\epsilon^{2}
, be a matrix with each entry equal to
1/\sqrt{k}
or
-1/\sqrt{k}
with the same probability
1/2
. Then with probability at least
1-1/n
,

\left\|\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Q}\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{Q}\bm{v}_{j}\right\|^{2}

for all all pairs
i,j\leq n
.

Lemma
4.1
indicates that, the pairwise distances
\|\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\|^{2}
(
i,j=1,2,\ldots,n
) are almost preserved if we project the
n
vectors
\bm{v}_{i}
(
i=1,2,\ldots,n
) into a lower-dimensional space, spanned
by
O(\log n)
random vectors.

In order to compute
C(u)
, we use Lemma
4.1
to reduce the dimension. Let
\bm{Q}
be a
k\times m
random projection matrix. Then
\|\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|
is a good approximation for
\|\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|
. Here we can use sparse matrix multiplication to compute
\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}
. However, computing
\bm{Z}=\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}
directly involves inverting
\bm{L}+\frac{1}{n}\bm{J}
. We avoid this by solving the system of equations
\bm{L}\bm{z}_{i}=\bm{q}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, where
\bm{z}^{\top}_{i}
and
\bm{q}^{\top}_{i}
are, respectively, the
i
-th row of
\bm{Z}
and
\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}
. For the convenience of description, in the sequel we use the notation
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
to hide
\mathrm{poly}\log
factors. By using Laplacian solvers
(
Spielman and Teng, 2004
;
Spielman, 2010
;
Koutis et al., 2011
;
Livne and Brandt, 2012
;
Cohen et al., 2014
;
Kyng and Sachdeva, 2016
)
,
\bm{z}^{\top}_{i}
can be efficiently approximated. We here use the solver from
(
Cohen et al., 2014
)
, the performance of which is characterized in Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 4.2 (SDD Solver
(
Cohen et al., 2014
;
Kyng and Sachdeva, 2016
)
).

There is an algorithm
\bm{x}=\mathtt{Solver}(\bm{L},\bm{y},\delta)
which
takes an SDDM matrix or a Laplacian
\bm{L}
,
a column vector
\bm{y}
, and an error
parameter
\delta>0
, and returns a column vector
\bm{x}
satisfying
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{x}=0
and

\|\bm{x}-\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{y}\|_{\bm{L}}\leq\delta\|\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{y}\|_{\bm{L}},

where
\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{\bm{L}}=\sqrt{\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{L}\bm{y}}
.
The algorithm runs in expected time
\widetilde{O}\left(m\log(1/\delta)\right)
.

By Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
, one can approximate
C(u)
arbitrarily well.

Lemma 4.3.

Given an approximation factor
\epsilon\leq 1/2
and a
k\times n
matrix
\bm{Z}
that satisfies

C(u)\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Z}\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right)\right\|^{2},

for any vertex
u\in V
and

\left\|\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{v}_{v}\right)\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Z}\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{v}_{v}\right)\right\|^{2}

for any pair of vertices
u,v\in V
.
Let
\bm{z}_{i}
be the
i
-th row of
\bm{Z}
and let
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}
be an approximation of
\bm{z}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,2,...,k\}
, satisfying

(17)

\|\bm{z}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i}\|_{\bm{L}}\leq\delta\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{\bm{L}},

where

(18)

\delta\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}\frac{d-d_{u}}{d}\sqrt{\frac{(1-\epsilon)w_{\min}}{(1+\epsilon)n^{4}w_{\max}}}.

Then for any vertex
u
belonging to
V
,

(19)

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)^{2}C(u)\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|^{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)^{2}C(u),

where
\widetilde{\bm{Z}}=[\tilde{\bm{z}}_{1},\tilde{\bm{z}}_{2},...,\tilde{\bm{z}}_{k}]^{\top}
.

Proof.

To prove (
19
), it is sufficient to show that for an arbitrary vertex
u
,

(20)

\begin{split}\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}-\|{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})}\|^{2}\right|&=\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|-\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\right|\times\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|+\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\right|\\
&\leq\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{9}\right)\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2},\end{split}

which is obeyed if

(21)

\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|-\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|.

This can be understood from the following arguments. On the one hand, if

\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}-\|{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})}\|^{2}\right|\leq\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{9}\right)\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}
, then

\left(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{3}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{9}\right)\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}\leq\|{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})}\|^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{9}\right)\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2},

which, combining with
\epsilon\leq 1/2
and the assumption that
C(u)\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Z}\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right)\right\|^{2}
, leads to Eq. (
19
).
On the other hand, if Eq. (
21
) is true, we have
\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\leq(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|
. Thus,

\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|+\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\right|\leq(2+\frac{\epsilon}{3})\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|,

which results in Eq. (
20
).

We now prove that (
21
) holds true. By applying triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|-\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|=\frac{1}{d}\left\|\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}^{2}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v})\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v})\right\|^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to the fact that
d=\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}\geq\sqrt{\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}^{2}}
.

Since we only consider connected graphs, there exists a simple path
\mathcal{P}_{v}
between
u
and any other vertex
v
. Applying the triangle inequality along path
\mathcal{P}_{v}
, the square of the last sum term in the above equation can be evaluated as:

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathcal{P}_{v}}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})\right\|\right)^{2}\leq n\sum_{v=1}^{n}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathcal{P}_{v}}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})\right\|^{2}=n^{2}\left\|(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})\bm{B}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=n^{2}\left\|\bm{B}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{w_{\min}}\left\|\bm{W}^{1/2}\bm{B}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,.

The last term can be bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{w_{\min}}\left\|\bm{W}^{1/2}\bm{B}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{w_{\min}}\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{B}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{w_{\min}}\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})\bm{L}(\bm{Z}-\widetilde{\bm{Z}})^{\top}\right)=\frac{n^{2}}{w_{\min}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\bm{z}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{L}(\bm{z}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{z}}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}\delta^{2}}{w_{\min}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}\bm{z}_{i}=\frac{n^{2}\delta^{2}}{w_{\min}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{Z}\bm{L}\bm{Z}^{\top}\right)=\frac{n^{2}\delta^{2}}{w_{\min}}\left\|\bm{W}^{1/2}\bm{B}\bm{Z}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,,

where the inequality follows from (
17
) and the last term can be further evaluated by Lemma
2.2
as

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}\delta^{2}}{w_{\min}}\left\|\bm{W}^{1/2}\bm{B}\bm{Z}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}\delta^{2}}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}\|\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{B}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{a}-\bm{e}_{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in E}w_{ab}R_{ab}=\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}(n-1)(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}},

where
w_{ab}
represents the weight of the edge with endvertices
a
and
b
.

In addition,
\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}
can also be bounded by

\begin{split}\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|^{2}&\geq(1-\epsilon)C(u)=(1-\epsilon)(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\\
&\geq(1-\epsilon)\lambda_{n}^{-1}\left\|\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right\|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)(nw_{\max})^{-1}\frac{(d-d_{u})^{2}}{d^{2}}.\end{split}

In the above equation, the first inequation follows due to the following reason. Note that
\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}
is orthogonal to vector
\bm{1}
, which is an eigenvector of
\bm{L}^{\dagger}
corresponding to the
0
eigenvalue. Hence,
(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\geq\lambda_{n}^{-1}\left\|\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right\|^{2}
always holds true.

Thus, we have

\frac{\left|\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|-\left\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|\right|}{\left\|\bm{Z}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\right\|}\leq\delta\left(\frac{n^{2}(n-1)(1+\epsilon)}{w_{\min}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{nw_{\max}}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}\frac{d}{d-d_{u}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3},

where
\delta
is given by (
18
).
∎

Lemma
4.3
leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4.

There is a
\widetilde{O}(m\log{c}/\epsilon^{2})
time algorithm, which inputs a scalar
0<\epsilon<1
and a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
where
c=\frac{w_{\max}}{w_{\min}}
, and returns a
(24\log n/\epsilon^{2})\times n
matrix
\widetilde{\bm{Z}}
such that with probability at least
1-1/n
,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)^{2}C(u)\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{Z}}(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi})\|^{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)^{2}C(u)

for any vertex
u\in V
.

Based on Theorem
4.4
, we present an algorithm
ApproxHK
to approximately compute the walk centrality
H_{u}
for all vertices
u\in V
and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}

5.
Group Walk Centrality

In this section, we extend the walk centrality
H_{j}
to the case of a group
S
of vertices, denoted as
H(S)
and called Group Walk Centrality (GWC), which measures the relative importance of the vertex group
S
. We first give the definition of GWC. Then, we provide an examination of GWC, by establishing a connection between GWC and random detour time and the Kemeny constant. Finally, we show that as a set function
H(\cdot)
, GWC is monotonic and supermodular.

5.1.
Definition and Expression

We utilize absorbing random walks with multiple traps
(
Zhang et al., 2012
)
to define GWC. For random walks on a connected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with vertices in set
S
being traps (absorbing vertices), the hitting time (absorbing time)
H_{iS}
from vertex
i
to vertex group
S
is the expected number of steps for a walker starting from
i
to visit any vertex of
S
for the first time. If we denote the time steps for a walker starting from
i
to first reach an arbitrary vertex in
S
as random variable
T_{iS}
, then we have
H_{iS}=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{iS}\right]
. Based on the hitting time
H_{iS}
to a vertex group
S
, we define the GWC of a set of vertices.

Definition 5.1 (Group Walk Centrality, GWC).

For a given vertex group
S
in a connected undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, its GWC
H(S)
is defined as the expected time for a random walker starting from a vertex chosen from
\mathcal{G}
according to the stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
, to reach any vertex in
S
. Mathematically,
H(S)
is expressed as follows:

H(S)=\sum_{i\in V}\pi_{i}H_{iS}.

Note that for the case that set
S
contains only one vertex
j
, GWC
H(S)
reduces to walk centrality
H_{j}
. If
H(S)
is larger, then the vertices in
S
are not easily accessible, which means that the vertices in
S
are more peripheral, and are thus less important; if
H(S)
is smaller, then the vertices in
S
are more important.

We now give an expression for
H(S)
. For random walks in graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with absorbing vertex group
S
and transition matrix
\bm{P}
, the fundamental matrix
\bm{F}
is
(
Zhang et al., 2012
)

(22)

\bm{F}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\bm{P}_{-S}^{l}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{-S})^{-1}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P})_{-S}^{-1}.

From (
22
), we can see that the entry
\bm{F}_{[i,j]}
of matrix
\bm{F}
is the expected number of times a random walker starting from vertex
i
visits vertex
j
before being absorbed by vertices in
H(S)
. Then,
H(S)
can be expressed as

H(S)=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{F}\bm{1}=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}(\bm{I}-\bm{P})_{-S}^{-1}\bm{1}.

By using (
3
),
H(S)
can be further written as

(23)

H(S)=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}\right)_{-S}^{-1}\bm{1}=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}\right)_{-S}^{-1}\bm{D}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{D}_{-S}\bm{1}=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}.

Since
\bm{L}_{-S}
is an SDDM matrix, according to (
23
) and Lemma
4.2
, we can approximately compute GWC of any given vertex group
S
by calling
\mathtt{Solver}\left(\bm{L}_{-S},\bm{d}_{-S},\delta\right)
. We omit the detail of this approximation algorithm, since it simply utilizes Lemma
4.2
.

5.2.
Connection with Other Quantities of Random Walks

In this subsection, we establish a link between GWC
H(S)
in graph
\mathcal{G}
and some other quantities related to hitting times for random walks without traps in
\mathcal{G}
.

We first introduce a notion of random detour walk
i\rightarrow S\rightarrow j
. It is a random walk starting from a vertex
i
, that must visit at least a vertex in set
S
before it arrives at the target vertex
j
and stops. The group random detour time
D_{ij}(S)
for the random detour walk
i\rightarrow S\rightarrow j
is defined as the expected time for the walker starting from vertex
i
to visit at least a vertex in group
S
, and then reaches vertex
j
for the first time. Note that when set
S
includes only one vertex
k
,
i\rightarrow S\rightarrow j
and
D_{ij}(S)
are reduced to the corresponding notion and quantity previously proposed in
(
Ranjan and Zhang, 2013
;
Boley et al., 2011
)
for a single vertex. By definition, for a single vertex
k
, the random detour walk for
i\rightarrow k\rightarrow j
is equal to
H_{ik}+H_{kj}-H_{ij}
, which is defined based on the following triangular inequality for hitting time
H_{ij}\leq H_{ik}+H_{kj}
of all
1\leq i,j,k\leq n

(
Hunter, 2005
)
. An intuitive interpretation of the random detour time
H_{ik}+H_{kj}-H_{ij}
is that the number of transition steps
H_{ij}

from one vertex
i
to another vertex
j
can be expected to increase when a forced passage through a third vertex
k
is prescribed.

For an absorbing random walk in graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with vertices in set
S
being absorbing ones, we denote the probability that a walker starting from vertex
i
first reaches vertex
u
in absorbing group
S
as the
{(i,u)}^{\rm{th}}
entry of matrix
\bm{P}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\left|S\right|}
. Then,
D_{ij}(S)
can be expressed as

(24)

D_{ij}(S)=H_{iS}+\sum_{k=1}^{\left|S\right|}\bm{P}^{\prime}_{[i,k]}H_{kj}.

This expression shows that if
D_{ij}(S)
is larger, then it is difficult for a walker to reach vertices in
S
, which indicates that vertices in
S
are relatively less significant in the overall network. Thus,
D_{ij}(S)
is consistent with the quantity
H_{iS}
. This enlightens us to explore the relation between
H(S)
and related quantities about hitting times, such as
D_{ij}(S)
. Theorem
5.2
establishes a link governing GWC
H(S)
, group random detour time
D_{ij}(S)
, and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
.

Theorem 5.2.

For an arbitary vertex group
S\subseteq V
in a connected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with
n
vertices,

(25)

H(S)+\mathcal{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}D_{ij}(S).

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.4
, the hitting time can be represented in terms of
\bm{F}^{*}
. Thus, the right-hand side of (
25
) can be rewritten as

(26)

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}D_{ij}(S)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}H_{iS}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\left|S\right|}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\bm{P}^{\prime}_{[i,k]}H_{kj}\\
&=H(S)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\prime}\left(\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}\bm{1}-\bm{F}^{*}_{[S,j]}\right)\\
&=H(S)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\prime}\bm{1}-\bm{\pi}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\prime}\bm{F}^{*}_{[S,:]}\bm{\pi}\\
&=H(S)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}-1,\end{split}

where the final equality is due to the fact that
\bm{P}^{\prime}\bm{1}=\bm{1}
and
\bm{F}^{*}\bm{\pi}=\bm{1}

(
Boley et al., 2011
)
.

On the other hand, we can rewrite the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
as

(27)

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}H_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\left(\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}-\bm{F}^{*}_{[i,j]}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}\bm{F}^{*}_{[j,j]}-1.

Combining (
26
) and (
27
) completes the proof.
∎

Since the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
is only related to the structure of graph
\mathcal{G}
, (
25
) indicates that there exists only a constant difference between GWC
H(S)
and a weighted sum of the group random detour time
D_{ij}(S)
over all pairs of vertices. Thus, from the perspective of decimating power of vertex group,
GWC
H(S)
is equivalent to weighted sum of the group random detour times. In this sense, Theorem
5.2
deepens the understanding of the importance of vertex group
S
from a different perspective.

5.3.
Monotonic and Supermodular Properties

In this subsection, we show that the set function
H(\cdot)
has some desirable properties. For this purpose, we first introduce the definitions of monotone and supermodular set functions. For simplicity, we use
S+u
to represent
S\cup\left\{u\right\}
.

Definition 5.3 (Monotonicity).

A set function
f:2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is monotonic if and only if the inequality
f(X)\geq f(Y)
holds for two arbitrary nonempty sets
X
and
Y
satisfying
X\subseteq Y\subseteq V
.

Definition 5.4 (Supermodularity).

A set function
f:2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is supermodular if and only if the inequality
f(X)-f(X+u)\geq f(Y)-f(Y+u)
holds for two arbitrary nonempty sets
X
and
Y
satisfying
\forall X\subseteq Y\subseteq V,u\in V\setminus Y
.

Next we demonstrate that the set function
H(\cdot)
is monotonically decreasing and supermodular.

Theorem 5.5.

Let
S
be a nonempty set of vertices of a connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
. Then, for any vertex
u\in V\setminus S
,

H(S)\geq H(S+u).

Proof.

By Definition
5.1
, when a random walker arrives at a vertex in
S
, it must have arrived at vertices in
S+u
. Therefore,
H_{vS}\geq H_{v\left(S+u\right)}
holds for any vertex
v\in V
, which concludes our proof.
∎

Theorem 5.6.

Let
S
and
T
be two nonempty sets of vertices of a connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
obeying
S\subseteq T\subsetneq V
. Then, for any vertex
u\in V\setminus T
,

H(S)-H(S+u)\geq H(T)-H(T+u).

Proof.

By definition of absorbing time for random walks with traps,
H(S)-H(S+u)
can be represented as

(28)

\begin{split}H(S)-H(S+u)&=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[T_{vS}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{v\left(S+u\right)}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[T_{vS}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{T_{vS},T_{vu}\right\}\right]\right).\end{split}

We next represent
\min\left\{T_{vS},T_{vu}\right\}
in terms of the indicator function. For a subset
A
of the probability space, we denote
\chi\left(A\right)
as the indicator function of the event
A
. Concretely,
\chi\left(A\right)
is defined as

\chi\left(A\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{event }A\text{ holds true;}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}.

Then,
\min\left\{T_{vS},T_{vu}\right\}
can be recast as

\min\left\{T_{vS},T_{vu}\right\}=T_{vS}\chi\left(\left\{T_{vS}\leq T_{vu}\right\}\right)+T_{vu}\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vS}\right\}\right),

inserting which into (
28
) and considering the linearity of the mean, one obtains

H(S)-H(S+u)=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\mathbb{E}\left[(T_{vS}-T_{vu})\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vS}\right\}\right)\right].

Similarly, we have

H(T)-H(T+u)=\sum_{v\in V}\bm{\pi}_{v}\mathbb{E}\left[(T_{vT}-T_{vu})\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vT}\right\}\right)\right].

As mentioned in proof of Theorem
5.5
,
T_{vS}\geq T_{vT}
holds for any vertex
v\in V
.
Thus, when
T_{vu}<T_{vT}
holds true, the relation
T_{vu}<T_{vS}
also holds. Then,
\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vS}\right\}\right)\geq\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vT}\right\}\right)
holds for any vertex
v\in V
.
Combining these two inequalities, one obtains

(T_{vS}-T_{vu})\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vS}\right\}\right)\geq(T_{vT}-T_{vu})\chi\left(\left\{T_{vu}<T_{vT}\right\}\right)

for any vertex
v\in V
. This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Minimizing Group Walk Centrality Subject to Cardinality Constraint

In this section, we define and study the problem of minimizing the GWC
H(S)
by optimally selecting a fixed number
k=\left|S\right|
vertices constituting set.

6.1.
Problem Formulation

As shown above,
H(S)
is a monotonically decreasing set function, since the addition of any vertex to set
S
will lead to a decrease of the quantity
H(S)
. Then, we naturally raise the following problem called MinGWC: How to optimally choose a set
S
of
k=\left|S\right|
vertices in graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, so that the quantity
H(S)
is minimized. Mathematically, the optimization problem subject to a cardinality constraint can be formally stated as follows.

Problem 1 (
Min
imization of
G
roup
W
alk
C
entrality, MinGWC).

Given a connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
with
n
vertices,
m
edges and an integer
k\ll n
, the goal is to find a vertex group
S^{*}\subseteq V
such that the GWC
H(S^{*})
is minimized. In other words,

S^{*}=\argmin_{S\subseteq V,\left|S\right|=k}H(S).

Since the problem MinGWC is inherently combinatorial, we can naturally resort to a naïve solution by exhausting all
\tbinom{n}{k}
cases of vertex selection. For each of the possible cases that
k
vertices are selected to form set
S
, we calculate the quantity
H(S)
corresponding to the
k
vertices in set
S
and output the optimal solution
S^{*}
with the minimum group walk centrality. Evidently, this method fails when
n
or
k
is slightly large since it has an exponential time complexity
O(\tbinom{n}{k}n^{3})
, assuming that inverting an
n\times n
matrix needs
O(n^{3})
time. Below we will show that the MinGWC problem is in fact NP-hard.

6.2.
Hardness of the MinGWC Problem

In this section, we prove that the MinGWC problem is NP-hard, by giving a reduction from the vertex cover problem for 3-regular graphs, the vertices of which have identical degree 3. For a 3-regular graph, the vertex cover problem is NP-complete
(
Fricke et al., 1998
)
, the decision version of which is stated below.

Problem 2.

Given a connected 3-regular graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k
, the vertex cover problem is to determine whether exists a subset
S\subseteq V
of
\left|S\right|\leq k
vertices, such that every edge in
E
is incident with at least one vertex in
S
, i.e.,
S
is a vertex cover of
G=(V,E)
.

We continue to give a decision version of the MinGWC problem for a 3-regular graph.

Problem 3.

Given a connected 3-regular graph
G=(V,E)
with every edge having a unit weight, an integer
k
, and a threshold
(n-k)/n
, the MinGWC problem asks whether exists a subset
S\subseteq V
of
\left|S\right|\leq k
vertices, such that
H(S)\geq(n-k)/n
.

Theorem 6.1.

The MinGWC problem is NP-hard.

Proof.

We give a reduction from the above vertex cover problem in a 3-regular graph
G=(V,E)
to the decision version of the problem of group walk centrality. Specifically, we will prove that for any nonempty set
S\subseteq V
of
\left|S\right|=k
vertices,
H(S)\geq(n-k)/n
, where the equality holds if and only if
S
is a vertex cover of
\mathcal{G}
.

We first prove that if
S
is a vertex cover of
\mathcal{G}
, then
H(S)=(n-k)/n
. In the case that
S
is a vertex cover of
\mathcal{G}
, there is no edge between vertices in
V\setminus S
. Then (
23
) is simplified as

H(S)=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{D}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{1}.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is a 3-regular graph,
\bm{\pi}=\begin{pmatrix}1/n&\cdots&1/n\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
.
Thus,
H(S)=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{1}=(n-k)/n
.

We continue to demonstrate that if
S
is not a vertex cover of
\mathcal{G}
, then
H(S)>(n-k)/n
. Note that for the case that
S
is not a vertex cover, for a vertex
u\in S
and any vertex
v\in V\setminus S
,
H_{uS}=0
and
H_{vS}\geq 1
always hold. Moreover, there exists at least one edge
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
in edge set
E
that is not covered by
S
. Thus, for a walker starting from vertex
u^{\prime}
, it moves to vertex
v^{\prime}
with a probability of at least
\frac{1}{d_{\max}}>\frac{1}{n}
, then it continue walking until being absorbed by vertices in
S
. For this case the absorbing time
H_{u^{\prime}S}
is bounded as

H_{u^{\prime}S}>\big(1-\frac{1}{d_{\max}}\big)\times 1+\frac{1}{d_{\max}}\times 2=\big(1+\frac{1}{d_{\max}}\big)>1+\frac{1}{n}.

Similarly, we obtain
H_{v^{\prime}S}>1+\frac{1}{n}
.

Let
S^{\prime}
denote
S\cup\left\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\right\}
. Then we are able to get a lower bound of GWC
H(S)
as

\displaystyle H(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V\setminus S^{\prime}}\pi_{v}H_{vS}+\pi_{u^{\prime}}H_{u^{\prime}S}+\pi_{v^{\prime}}H_{v^{\prime}S}>\bm{\pi}_{-S^{\prime}}\bm{1}+\pi_{u^{\prime}}\big(1+\frac{1}{n}\big)+\pi_{v^{\prime}}\big(1+\frac{1}{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{1}+\frac{\pi_{u^{\prime}}+\pi_{v^{\prime}}}{n}>\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{1}=(n-k)/n.

This finishes the proof.
∎

6.3.
A Simple Greedy Algorithm

Since the MinGWC problem is NP-hard, it is nearly impossible to design a polynomial time algorithm to solve it. One often resorts to greedy algorithm. Due to the proved fact that the objective function
H(\cdot)
of the MinGWC problem is monotonic and supermodular, a naïve greedy strategy for vertex selection yields an approximated solution with a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e})
approximation factor
(
Nemhauser et al., 1978
)
. Initially, we set
S
to be empty, then
k
vertices from set
V\setminus S
are added to
S
iteratively. During the first iteration, the vertex
u
with the minimum walk centrality
H_{u}
is chosen to be added to
S
. Then, in each iteration step
i>1
, the vertex
u
in the set
V\setminus S
of candidate vertices is selected, which has the maximum marginal gain
\Delta(u,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}H(S)-H(S+u)
. The algorithm terminates when
k
vertices are chosen to be added to set
S
.

At each iteration of the naïve greedy algorithm, one needs computing the maximum marginal gain
\Delta(u,S)
for every candidate vertex in set
V\setminus S
. As shown in (
23
), a straightforward calculation of
\Delta(u,S)
for every vertex
u
requires inverting a matrix, which needs
O((n-\left|S\right|)^{3})
time. Thus, for the
(O(n-\left|S\right|))
candidate vertices, the computation time at each iteration is
O((n-\left|S\right|)^{4})
. Hence, the total computation complexity of the naïve greedy algorithm is
O(kn^{4})
since
\left|S\right|\ll n
.

In order to further decrease the time complexity of the naïve greedy algorithm, we provide an expression for
\Delta(u,S)
.

Lemma 6.2.

Given a connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
, the nonempty vertex group
S
, and the Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
, for any vertex
u\in V\setminus S
,
\Delta(u,S)
can be alternatively expressed as

(29)

\Delta(u,S)=H(S)-H(S+u)=\frac{\left(\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}\right)^{2}}{d\left(\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right)}.

Proof.

For vertex
u\in V\setminus S
, we denote the
{u}^{\rm{th}}
column of submatrix
\bm{L}_{-S}
as
\begin{pmatrix}d_{u}\\
-\bm{a}\end{pmatrix}
. After properly adjusting the vertex labels, matrix
\bm{L}_{-S}
can be rewritten in block form as

\bm{L}_{-S}=\begin{pmatrix}d_{u}&-\bm{a}^{\top}\\
-\bm{a}&\bm{A}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{A}
denotes
\bm{L}_{-(S+u)}
.
According to the properties of block matrices, we get

\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}t&t\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\\
t\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{a}&\bm{A}^{-1}+t\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

where
t={\left(d_{u}-\bm{a}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{-1}\right)}^{-1}
.
Therefore, when
S\neq\varnothing
,
\Delta(u,S)
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\Delta(u,S)&=H(S)-H(S+u)=\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}-\bm{\pi}_{-(S+u)}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-(S+u)}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-(S+u)}\\
&=\frac{1}{t}\left(t\bm{\pi}_{u}+t\bm{\pi}_{-(S+u)}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{a}\right)\left(td_{u}+t\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-(S+u)}\right)\\
&=\frac{\left(\bm{\pi}_{-S}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right)\left(\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}\right)}{\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}}=\frac{\left(\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}\right)^{2}}{d\left(\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}\right)},\end{split}

finishing the proof.
∎

Lemma
6.2
demonstrate that for any empty set
S
if
\bm{L}_{-S}
is already computed, by using (
29
) we can calculate
\Delta(u,S)
for any vertex
u\in V\setminus S
in
O((n-\left|S\right|)^{2})
time.
This avoids directly inverting the matrix
\bm{L}_{-(S+u)}
, which needs time
O((n-\left|S\right|+1)^{3})
. When
S=\varnothing
, (
29
) becomes invalid. For this case, we use the result
H_{u}=d\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right)^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\left(\bm{e}_{u}-\bm{\pi}\right)
in Lemma
3.2
to compute the walk centrality
H_{u}
for every vertex
u\in V
, and add the vertex
u
with the smallest
H_{u}
to
S
. Based on Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
6.2
, we propose a simple greedy algorithm
DeterMinGWC
(\mathcal{G},k)
to solve the MinGWC problem, which is outlined in Algorithm
2
. The algorithm first computes
H_{u}
for every vertex
u\in V
in time
O(n^{3})
. Then, it works in
k-1
rounds. In each round, it evaluates
\Delta(u,S)
for all the
(n-\left|S\right|)
vertices in set
V\setminus S
(Line 6) in
O((n-\left|S\right|)^{3})
time. Therefore, the whole running time of Algorithm
2
is
O(kn^{3})
, much lower than the time complexity
O(kn^{4})
of the naïve greedy algorithm.

Algorithm 2

DeterMinGWC
(\mathcal{G},k)

Input :

A connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
;

\bm{d}

,

3

In addition to good efficiency, Algorithm

is very effective since it has a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e})
approximation factor, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.3.

The algorithm
S_{k}
=
DeterMinGWC
(\mathcal{G},k)
takes a connected weighted undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
and a positive integer
1\geq k\ll n
, and returns a group
S_{k}
of
k
vertices.
The return vertex group
S
satisfies

where

u^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\argmin_{u\in V}H(\left\{u\right\})

and

S^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\argmin_{\left|S\right|=k}H(S).

Proof.

According to the supermodularity of
H(\cdot)
,

H(S_{i})-H(S_{i+1})\geq\frac{1}{k}\left(H(S_{i})-H(S^{*})\right)

holds for any positive integer
i
, which indicates

H(S_{i+1})-H(S^{*})\leq\big(1-\frac{1}{k}\big)\left(H(S_{i})-H(S^{*})\right).

Then, we further obtain

H(S_{k})-H(S^{*})\leq\big(1-\frac{1}{k}\big)^{k-1}\left(H(S_{1})-H(S^{*})\right)\leq\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e}\left(H(S_{1})-H(S^{*})\right),

which, together with the fact that
S_{1}=\left\{u^{*}\right\}
, completes the proof.
∎

7.
Fast Greedy Algorithm for the MinGWC Problem

Although Algorithm
2
is much faster than the naïve greedy algorithm, it still cannot handle large networks since its time complexity is
O(kn^{3})
. As shown above, the key steps for Algorithm
2
are to compute the walk centrality
H_{u}
for every vertex
u\in V
and the marginal gain
\Delta(u,S)
for every vertex
u\in(V\setminus S)
. In this section, we provide efficient approximations for
H_{u}
and
\Delta(u,S)
. For
H_{u}
, it can be efficiently evaluated by using Algorithm
1
. For
\Delta(u,S)
, we can alternatively approximate the two quantities
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}
and
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}
in the numerator and denominator of (
29
), respectively.

In the rest of this section, we first approximate
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}
and
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{u}
. Then, we present an error-guaranteed approximation to
\Delta(u,S)
, based on which we further propose a fast approximation algorithm to the MinGWC problem with
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e}-\epsilon)
approximation ratio and time complexity
\tilde{O}(km\epsilon^{-2})
for any error parameter
0<\epsilon<1
.

7.1.
Approximation of the Quantity
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{d}_{-S}

We first approximate the quantity
\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}
for any node
i\in V\setminus S
. To this end, we introduce the following two lemmas.

Lemma 7.1.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted undirected graph with Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
. Then, for an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq V
of vertices and an arbitrary vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-\left|S\right|}
, the relation
v_{i}^{2}\leq nw_{\min}^{-1}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{L}_{-S}}
holds.

Proof.

As shown in Section
2.2
, Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
can be written as
\bm{L}=\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{B}
. Similarly, as an SDDM matrix,
\bm{L}_{-S}
can be decomposed as
\bm{L}_{-S}=\bm{L}^{\prime}+\bm{Z}=\bm{B}^{\prime\top}\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{B}^{\prime}+\bm{Z}
, where
\bm{L}^{\prime}
denotes the Laplacian matrix of a graph, and
\bm{Z}
is a diagonal matrix with every diagonal entry larger than 0. In order to prove the lemma, we distinguish two cases:
\bm{Z}_{[i,i]}\neq 0
and
\bm{Z}_{[i,i]}=0
.

For the first case
\bm{Z}_{[i,i]}\neq 0
, we have
\bm{Z}_{[i,i]}\geq w_{\min}
. Then, it is apparent that
v_{i}^{2}\leq nw_{\min}^{-1}\left\|\bm{v}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{L}_{-S}}
. For the second case
\bm{Z}_{[i,i]}=0
, there must exist a vertex
j
satisfying
\bm{Z}_{[j,j]}\geq w_{\min}
in a component of the resultant graph containing vertex
i
, which is obtained from
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
after removing vertices in
S
. Let
\mathscr{P}_{ij}
denotes a simple path connecting vertex
i
and vertex
j
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{v}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{L}_{-S}}

\displaystyle\geq w_{\min}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathscr{P}_{ij}}(v_{a}-v_{b})^{2}+w_{\min}v_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq w_{\min}n^{-1}\left(\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathscr{P}_{ij}}(v_{a}-v_{b})+v_{j}\right)^{2}\geq w_{\min}n^{-1}v_{i}^{2},

which completes our proof.
∎

Lemma 7.2.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted undirected graph with
n
vertices and the minimum edge weight
w_{\min}
, and let
\bm{L}
be the Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
. Then, for any nonempty set
S\subseteq V
,

\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\right)\leq n^{2}w_{\min}^{-1}.

Proof.

According to the result in
(
Clark and Poovendran, 2011
)
, the diagonal entry
\left(\bm{L}_{-S}\right)_{[u,u]}
of
\bm{L}_{-S}
is exactly the resistance distance
R_{uS}
between vertex
u
and vertex group
S
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is connected, the length of an arbitrary simple path connecting vertex
u
any vertex in
S
is no more than
n
.
Making use of (
2
), we have

\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{iS}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathscr{P}_{iS}}w_{ab}^{-1}\leq n^{2}w_{\min}^{-1},

which finishes the proof.
∎

With the above two lemmas, we can approximate
\bm{e}_{u}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}
. For simplicity of the following proofs, define
\bm{x}_{i}
as
\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}
for any vertex
i\in V\setminus S
. By definition,
\bm{x}_{i}
represents the trapping time of a random walker starting at vertex
i
and being absorbed by vertices in
S
. Then, it follows that
\bm{x}_{i}\geq 1
. Since
\bm{L}_{-S}
is an SDDM matrix, we can estimate
\bm{x}_{i}
by applying Lemma
4.2
, which avoids computing the inverse of matrix
\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}
.

Lemma 7.3.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted undirected graph with the Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
. Let
S
be a nonempty subset of vertex set
V
, let
\epsilon
an error parameter in
(0,1)
, and let
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\mathtt{Solver}(\bm{L}_{-S},\bm{d}_{-S},\delta_{1})
, where
\delta_{1}=\frac{w_{\min}\epsilon}{7n^{3}w_{\max}}
. Then, for any vertex
i\in V\setminus S
,

(30)

\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}\approx_{\epsilon/7}\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}.

Proof.

Combining Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
7.1
,
(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i})^{2}
is bounded as

\displaystyle(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq nw_{\min}^{-1}\left\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{L}_{-S}}\leq nw_{\min}^{-1}\delta_{1}^{2}\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{L}_{-S}}

\displaystyle\leq n^{4}w_{\min}^{-1}w_{\max}^{2}\delta_{1}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\right)\leq n^{6}w_{\min}^{-2}w_{\max}^{2}\delta_{1}^{2},

where the last inequality is due to Lemma
7.2
.
Considering
\bm{x}_{i}\geq 1
, we obtain

\frac{\left|\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}\right|}{\bm{x}_{i}}\leq n^{3}w_{\max}w_{\min}^{-1}\delta_{1}=\epsilon/7,

which leads to the desirable result.
∎

7.2.
Approximation of the Quantity
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}

We next estimate the quantity
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
in the denominator of (
29
). For this purpose,
we represent
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
in terms of an Euclidean norm as

(31)

\begin{split}\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}&=\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}(\bm{B}^{\prime\top}\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{B}^{\prime}+\bm{Z})\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}=\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{B}^{\prime\top}\bm{W}^{\prime}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{Z}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\\
&=\left\|\bm{W}^{\prime 1/2}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Z}^{1/2}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

Thus, the evaluation of
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
is reduced to computing the
\ell_{2}
-norm of two vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. However, exactly calculating
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}
by evaluating
\ell_{2}
-norms still has a high computational cost, since we still need to invert the matrix
\mathbf{L}_{-S}
. Although by utilizing Lemma
4.2
, one can approximate
\ell_{2}
-norms. Unfortunately, since the dimensions of matrices
\bm{W}^{\prime}
and
\bm{Z}
are, respectively,
O(m)
and
O(n)
, resulting in an unacceptable number of calls to the
\mathtt{Solver}
function. In the following text, by using Lemma
4.1
we will propose an approximation algorithm to estimate the two
\ell_{2}
-norms, which significantly lessens the computational cost.

In order to exploit Lemma
4.1
, we introduce two projection matrices
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
and
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
, where
q=r=\left\lceil 24\epsilon^{-2}\log n\right\rceil
. Each of the entry for
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
and
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
is
1/\sqrt{q}
or
1/\sqrt{q}
with identical probability
1/2
. Then, we have

(32)

\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{-S}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}=\left\|\bm{W}^{\prime 1/2}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Z}^{1/2}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\prime 1/2}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{R}\bm{Z}^{1/2}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}.

For the sake of simplicity, we denote
\bm{W}^{\prime 1/2}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}
and
\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{\prime 1/2}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}
as
\bm{X}
and
\tilde{\bm{X}}
, respectively. Moreover, we denote
\bm{Z}^{1/2}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}
and
\bm{R}\bm{Z}^{1/2}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}
as
\bm{Y}
and
\tilde{\bm{Y}}
, respectively.
Then (
32
) can be rephrased as

(33)

\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}=\left\|\bm{X}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}.

Making this expression, we can efficiently approximate the quantity
\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}
.

Lemma 7.4.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
be a connected weighted undirected graph with Laplacian matrix
\bm{L}
. Let
S
be a nonempty subset of vertex set
V
, and let
\epsilon
be an error parameter in
(0,1)
. Let
\overline{\bm{X}}
and
\overline{\bm{Y}}
denote
\bm{Q}\bm{W}^{1/2}\bm{B}
and
\bm{R}\bm{Z}^{1/2}
respectively. Let
\bm{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
and
\bm{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
be matrices such that
\bm{X}^{\prime}_{[i,:]}=\mathtt{Solver}(\bm{L}_{-S},\overline{\bm{X}}_{[i,:]},\delta_{2})
,
\bm{Y}^{\prime}_{[i,:]}=\mathtt{Solver}(\bm{L}_{-S},\overline{\bm{Y}}_{[i,:]},\delta_{2})
, where
q=r=\left\lceil 24(\epsilon/7)^{-1}\right\rceil\log n
,
\delta_{2}=\frac{w_{\min}\epsilon}{31n^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon/7}{2w_{\max}(1+\epsilon/7)}}
. Then, for any vertex
i\in V\setminus S
,

(34)

\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}=\left\|\bm{X}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\approx_{\epsilon/3}\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}.

Proof.

According to triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\tilde{\bm{X}}-\bm{X})\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\leq\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}-\bm{X}^{\prime}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{[j,:]}-\bm{X}^{\prime}_{[j,:]}\right\|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{q}nw_{\min}^{-1}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{[j,:]}-\bm{X}^{\prime}_{[j,:]}\right\|_{\bm{L}_{-S}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{q}nw_{\min}^{-1}\delta_{2}^{2}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{[j,:]}\right\|_{\bm{L}_{-S}}^{2}}\leq n\delta_{2}\sqrt{w_{\min}^{-1}}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\right\|_{F},

where the third inequality and the fourth inequality are due to Lemma
7.1
and Lemma
4.2
, respectively.

Considering
q=r=\left\lceil 24(\epsilon/7)^{-1}\right\rceil\log n
and Lemma
4.1
, we further obtain

\displaystyle n\delta_{2}\sqrt{w_{\min}^{-1}}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq n\delta_{2}\sqrt{w_{\min}^{-1}(1+\epsilon/7)\sum_{i\in V\setminus S}\left\|\bm{X}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq n\delta_{2}\sqrt{w_{\min}^{-1}(1+\epsilon/7)\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\right)}\leq n^{2}\delta_{2}w_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{1+\epsilon/7},

where the last inequality is due to Lemma
7.2
.
Similarly, for
\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|
, we have

(35)

\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|\leq n^{2}\delta_{2}w_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{1+\epsilon/7}.

On the other hand, according to Lemma
4.1
, we have

\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon/7)\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}\geq(1-\epsilon/7)n^{-2}w_{\max}^{-1}

where the last inequality is due to the scaling of
\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}_{-S}^{-1}\bm{d}_{-S}\geq 1
.

According to the Pigeonhole Principle, at least one element of
\left\{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|,\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right\}
is no less than
\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon/7}{2w_{\max}}}
.
Without loss of generality, we suppose
\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\geq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon/7}{2w_{\max}}}
. Then, we have

\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|}\leq n\delta_{2}w_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{2w_{\max}(1+\epsilon/7)}{1-\epsilon/7}}=\frac{\epsilon}{31}.

In addition, we obtain

\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|\left(\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|+\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right)}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{31}\left(2+\frac{\epsilon}{31}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{15},

which means

(36)

\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{15}.

Using (
35
), it is not difficult to obtain

\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|}\leq n\delta_{2}w_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{2w_{\max}(1+\epsilon/7)}{1-\epsilon/7}}=\frac{\epsilon}{31},

which indicates

(37)

\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right|\left(\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|+\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|\right)}{\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{31}\left(2+\frac{\epsilon}{31}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{15}.

Combining (
36
) and (
37
), we are finally able to get

\frac{\left|\left(\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right)-\left(\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right)\right|}{\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|+\left|\left\|\tilde{\bm{Y}}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right|}{\left\|\bm{X}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\bm{Y}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{7},

which, together with Lemma
4.1
, completes the proof.
∎

7.3.
Efficient Evaluation of Marginal Gain
\Delta(u,S)

Combining Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
, we propose an efficient algorithm called
ApproxDelta
approximating
\Delta(u,S)
for
S\neq\varnothing
. The outline of
ApproxDelta
is presented in Algorithm
3
. The
ApproxDelta
algorithm calls the
\mathtt{Solver}
in Lemma
7.4
for
q=\left\lceil 24(\epsilon/7)^{-2}\log n\right\rceil
times, resulting in a complexity of
\tilde{O}\left(m\epsilon^{-2}\right)
. The approximation guarantee for Algorithm
3
is provided in
Lemma
7.5
.

Algorithm 3

ApproxDelta
(\mathcal{G},S,\epsilon)

Input :

\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
: a connected weighted undirected graph;

S\subseteq V
: the absorbing vertex set;

\epsilon\in(0,1)
: an approximation parameter

Output :

\Delta(u,S)
: the margin for vertex
u\in V\setminus S

1

\bm{L}=
Laplacian of
\mathcal{G}
,
d_{u}=
the degree of
u
for all
u\in V
,
d=\sum_{u\in V}d_{u}

2

n=\left|V\right|,m=\left|E\right|

3

w_{\min}=\min\left\{w_{e}|e\in E\right\},w_{\max}=\max\left\{w_{e}|e\in E\right\}

4

\delta_{1}=\frac{w_{\min}\epsilon}{7n^{2}w_{\max}}
,
\delta_{2}=\frac{w_{\min}\epsilon}{31n^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon/7}{2w_{\max}(1+\epsilon/7)}}

5

\bm{x}^{\prime}=\mathtt{Solver}(\bm{L}_{-S},\bm{d}_{-S},\delta_{1})

6

Construct matrix
\bm{Q}_{q\times m}
and
\bm{R}_{q\times n}

The algorithm

takes a connected weighted undirected graph

Proof.

Considering Lemma
7.5
and the supermodular property of the objective function

, we are able to obtain that for any positive integer

To further demonstrate the performance of our proposed algorithm

9.
Related Work

As a fundamental concept in social network analysis and complex networks, network centrality has received considerable attention from the scientific community
(
Newman, 2010
)
. The value of centrality metrics can be used to rank nodes or edges in networks
(
Lü et al., 2016
)
, with an aim to identify important nodes or edges for different applications. In the past decades, a host of centrality measures were presented to describe and characterize the roles of nodes or edges in networks
(
White and Smyth, 2003
;
Boldi and Vigna, 2014
;
Benzi and Klymko, 2015
;
Bonchi et al., 2016
;
Li and Zhang, 2018
;
Yi et al., 2018
;
Bao et al., 2022
;
Bao and Zhang, 2021
)
. Among various centrality metrics, a class of popular ones are based on shortest paths, of which betweenness centrality
(
Brandes, 2001
;
Geisberger et al., 2008
)
and closeness centrality
(
Bavelas, 1948
;
Bavelas, 1950
)
are probably the two most popular ones. A main drawback of these two measures is that they only consider the shortest paths, neglecting contributions from other paths, even the second shortest paths. To overcome this flaw, betweenness centrality based on random walks
(
Newman, 2005
)
and Markov centrality
(
White and Smyth, 2003
)
were introduced, both of which display better discriminate power than their traditional shortest path based centrality metrics
(
Newman, 2005
;
Bergamini et al., 2016
)
, since they incorporate the contributions from all paths between different node pairs.

In addition to the measures of centrality for an individual vertex, many metrics for centrality of node group were presented, in order to measure the importance of a node group. Most previous node group centrality measures are based on graph structure
(
Xu et al., 2017
)
or dynamic processes
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
. For example, based on the shortest paths, group betweenness
(
Dolev et al., 2009
;
Yoshida, 2014
;
Mahmoody et al., 2016
)
and group closeness
(
Everett and Borgatti, 1999
;
Zhao et al., 2014
;
Bergamini et al., 2018
)
were proposed. Similar to the case of individual nodes, group betweenness and group closeness ignore contributions from non-shortest paths. To capture the contributions of all paths, motivated by the work of White and Smyth
(
White and Smyth, 2003
)
, absorbing random-walk group centrality was proposed
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
. An algorithm for minimizing the absorbing random-walk group centrality with a cardinality constraint was also proposed
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
. It has a
O(kn^{3})
, and is thus not applicable to large networks.
Our group walk centrality is different from that in
(
Mavroforakis et al., 2015
)
. Moreover, our fast algorithm
ApproxMinGWC
has a nearly-linear time complexity.

10.
Conclusion

The hitting time of random walks arises in many practical scenarios. However, the time cost of exactly computing hitting time is prohibitively expensive. In this paper, we studied a walk centrality
H_{j}
for a single vertex and Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
of a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,w)
with
n
vertices and
m
edges, both of which are actually weighted averages of hitting times and have found wide applications. We established a link between the two quantities, and reformulated them in terms of quadratic forms of the pseudoinverse of the Laplacian
\bm{L}
for graph
\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, we provided a randomized approximation algorithm
ApproxHK
with probabilistic guarantee, which computes the walk centrality
H_{j}
for all vertices
j\in V
and Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
in nearly linear time with respect to the edge number
m
.

We then introduced the group walk centrality for a group
H(S)
of vertices, which extends the notion of walk centrality of an individual vertex to multiple vertices in set
S
. We proved that as a set function,
H(\cdot)
is monotone and supermodular.
We established a link among
H(S)
, the Kemeny constant, and group random detour time, which deepens the understanding of the cruciality of node group
S
. Analogous to the property of the Kemeny constant, this connection is very interesting, whose interpretation presents an opportunity for future research. We then considered the NP-hard optimization problem MinGWC of finding the set
S^{*}
of
k\ll n
vertices, aiming to minimize
H(S^{*})
. We then developed two greedy approximation algorithms,
DeterMinGWC
and
ApproxMinGWC
, which solve the problem MinGWC by iteratively selected
k
vertices. The former obtains a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e})
approximation factor in time
O(n^{3})
; while the latter achieves a
(1-\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{1}{e}-\epsilon)
approximation ratio in time
\widetilde{O}(km\epsilon^{-2})
for any
0<\epsilon<1
.

Finally, we conducted extensive experiments on various real and model networks, in order to verify the performance of our proposed algorithms. The results show that algorithm
ApproxHK
is both efficient and accurate for approximating walk centrality of all individual vertices and the Kemeny constant
\mathcal{K}
. For the two greedy algorithms
DeterMinGWC
and
ApproxMinGWC
, both of them are very accurate, often returning almost optimal solutions. Particularly, the randomized algorithm
ApproxMinGWC
is scalable to large networks, which quickly returns good approximation solutions in networks with over a million vertices.
