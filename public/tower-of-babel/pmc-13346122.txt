How I do it: OA-PICA bypass and transcondylar approach for VA fusiform aneurysm

Introduction

Vertebral artery aneurysms account for approximately 2.0% of all intracranial aneurysms and are relatively uncommon [2]. Their treatment is challenging because preservation of the perforating arteries and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is mandatory, irrespective of whether a microsurgical or endovascular strategy is chosen. Although recent reports increasingly support the efficacy of endovascular therapy, microsurgical treatment remains indispensable, especially for PICA-involved aneurysms, in which an occipital artery (OA) –PICA bypass is often required to preserve PICA flow [3, 6]. Microsurgical techniques should therefore continue to be widely learned.

A major issue in achieving technical proficiency is the simplification and standardization of the procedure. Here, we describe in detail the techniques currently used at our institution.

Surgical anatomy

The caudal loop of the PICA is generally the most suitable recipient site for bypass surgery; however, it is reportedly absent or located at a high position in approximately 28% of cases [4, 5], making preoperative assessment essential.

PICA-involved vertebral artery aneurysms are typically located medial to cranial nerves IX–XII.

The mastoid emissary vein can serve as a landmark for the sigmoid sinus, and the anterior condylar emissary vein may indicate the position of the hypoglossal nerve. A well-developed posterior condylar emissary vein may cause significant venous bleeding during craniotomy, and its size should be assessed preoperatively.

The occipital artery, after passing through the longus capitis muscle, gives off muscular branches, then ascends between the splenius capitis and semispinalis capitis muscles. It enters the subcutaneous layer at the superior nuchal line to supply the occipital scalp [7].

Our technique

A reverse L-shaped skin incision is made posterior to the auricle, extending to a point located three-fifths of the distance between the mastoid tip and the occipital midline, with the asterion included in the skin flap. The intersection between the incision and the OA is identified preoperatively using Doppler ultrasonography (Figs. 2).

A skin incision extending from the postauricular region, incorporating the asterion, and ending at the point corresponding to three-fifths of the distance between the mastoid tip and the occipital midline. The quintile line: the distance between the mastoid tip and the occipital midline, dashed line: mastoid process, ★: asterion

The incision is initiated at the OA crossing point, and the OA is identified and preserved without transection to maintain blood flow and prevent thrombotic occlusion. The skin flap is elevated in the subfascial plane, avoiding scalp clips and using traction hemostasis to reduce postoperative wound complications [8].

At this stage, the OA lies deep to the trapezius and splenius capitis muscles. In our technique, the OA is identified from the muscle surface, bluntly dissected using instruments such as a needle holder, and the overlying muscle is incised while protecting the artery. This approach allows safe OA harvest down to its entry beneath the mastoid process. The incision line can also be incorporated into a z-shaped muscle reflection, enabling en bloc mobilization and straightforward reconstruction of the occipital muscle group during closure, thereby minimizing postoperative muscle atrophy (Figs 3, 4).

A z-shaped occipital muscle group incision line based on the course of the occipital artery

a Preoperative non-contrast head CT. b Non-contrast head CT at 1 year postoperatively demonstrating preserved thickness of the skin and muscle layers, without apparent atrophy

The occipital muscle group is detached from the occipital bone and reflected posteriorly while preserving OA flow.

A craniotomy is performed with two burr holes placed along the sigmoid sinus. The inner table over the sigmoid sinus is drilled to expose approximately half of the sinus, providing sufficient visualization toward the midline clivus.

Following craniotomy, the occipital muscle group is further detached, and the foramen magnum is opened. Foramen magnum decompression improves orientation during condylar drilling and facilitates OA–PICA bypass by extending the working space medially.

The extracranial VA is identified using Doppler ultrasonography and secured. Venous plexuses surrounding the V3 segment are meticulously controlled to obtain proximal VA control in case of aneurysm rupture.

Condylar drilling is performed anteriorly from the opened foramen magnum. Inferiorly, the hypoglossal canal represents the depth limit, while superiorly, drilling of the jugular tubercle to the same depth as the hypoglossal canal provides a reliable landmark. This constitutes our standard transcondylar approach.

The dura mater is opened from the medullary side, and cerebrospinal fluid is released by opening the cerebellomedullary cistern to facilitate safe dural opening. The PICA caudal loop is commonly selected as the recipient site, and an OA–PICA bypass is performed.

After bypass completion, further arachnoid dissection is carried out to expose the aneurysm. The aneurysm is trapped by clipping the proximal and distal VA segments and the PICA origin, with careful preservation of perforating arteries.

During dural closure, a collagen matrix dural substitute (DuraGen®, Integra LifeSciences, Plainsboro, NJ, USA) is placed in the subdural space, followed by dural suturing. Fibrin sealant is then applied, and an additional dural substitute is placed epidurally to prevent cerebrospinal fluid leakage. The occipital muscle group is sutured and returned to its original position to preserve cosmesis. Thorough irrigation is performed at each step of closure to reduce the risk of postoperative infection [1]. To prevent wound complications related to OA harvesting, subcutaneous sutures are placed at wider intervals, and skin closure is completed with staples to eliminate dead space.

Postoperatively, the head of the bed is elevated to 30 degrees until the patient is mobilized to prevent venous bleeding. Early mobilization is promoted if the routine head CT on postoperative day 1 shows no abnormalities. Aspirin is maintained for a minimum of 1 month, provided no complications arise.
